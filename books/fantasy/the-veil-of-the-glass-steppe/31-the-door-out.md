## 31. The Door Out

They came over the last rise of glass as the sun was setting behind them, which meant the light went ahead of them, and the plain lit up from horizon to horizon like a struck match held under the world.

Sebastian stopped walking. She had been walking for what felt like three days and one lifetime, and her boots had worn grooves in a surface that was not supposed to wear, and she was so tired that her own legs had become an abstract concept she occasionally consulted. But when she saw it — saw *it*, the actual thing, the Door Out at last after prophecies and ledgers and a war nobody remembered fighting — she forgot to be tired entirely.

It was not a door.

She had expected, somehow, a door. Wood, perhaps. Iron bands. A hinge with opinions. Something architectural, something the guild could have catalogued: *one door, slightly impossible, provenance unknown.* Instead there was only a seam — a line of light standing upright on the glass like the first crack in an egg, taller than a cathedral spire if cathedrals were honest about their ambitions, thinner than a hair if you looked at it straight on. It did not glow so much as *insist*. The air around it bent politely out of its way.

"That's it?" said Ilse.

Ilse was leaning on Sebastian's shoulder, because the Custodian blade had gone through her side two days ago near the Salt Gate and the Echoes' bandages — woven, impossibly, out of the smell of bread baking — could stop blood but could not hurry it. She had insisted on walking anyway. She had insisted with the particular ferocity of someone who has already decided the argument and is merely waiting for the other party to catch up.

"That's it," Sebastian said.

"It's smaller than I imagined."

"Everything true is."

Ilse made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been pain; with her it had always been both. "You've been saving that one since the customs house, haven't you."

"Four years," Sebastian admitted. "I work slowly."

* * *

The seam of light did nothing as they approached. It simply stood there, patient as a ledger, and Sebastian understood — with the deep professional recognition of a woman who had spent eleven years shelving objects that defied description — that this thing was old. Older than Vellmark. Older than borders, older than treaties, older than whatever frightened creature had first looked across an open plain and thought *mine*. The Door had been here before anyone needed to go anywhere. It was waiting now the way stone waits, the way grief waits.

When they were ten paces away, it spoke.

Not in words. Not in any voice she could have written down afterward. It spoke the way a scale speaks when you step onto it — a simple, total accounting of what stood before it. Sebastian felt herself weighed: the guild apprenticeship, the ledgers, the loneliness disguised as diligence, the night she'd found her own name in a book a century old. All of it read, all of it known, all of it found —

*sufficient.*

"Is it talking to you?" Ilse whispered.

"I think it's weighing us." Sebastian swallowed. "I think we're passing."

"And then?"

And then the Door asked its price.

* * *

She had known, of course. The Echoes had told her plainly enough, back in their village of folded light, when her grandmother's echo had taken her hands and said, *the Door demands a memory of equal weight to a country.* She had refused, stalled, bargained. She had spent three days preparing to be brave about losing her future — the life she would live after crossing, whatever shape it took, surrendered sight unseen. That had seemed, in the abstract, almost elegant. A trade of futures. The country got tomorrow; she gave up hers. Clean bookkeeping. Oren would have approved of the symmetry.

But standing before the seam of light, Sebastian understood at last that she had misread the terms. It was worse than elegance. It was worse than clean.

The Door did not want the life she would live after crossing.

It wanted the life she *would have lived* — every hour of it, unspooled forward from this exact moment, all the days that would never happen now because she was spending them here instead. And it wanted them paid out the only way memory pays: by taking, first, everything that had ever pointed toward them. Her childhood — because it had built the woman who would live those days. Her mother's voice, bought back at such cost at the Night Market, gone again. Oren's face. The smell of the archive. The guild's crooked stairs. The taste of burnt coffee she'd drunk every morning of her apprenticeship while pretending it was fine, because admitting it wasn't fine would have started conversations.

And Ilse.

The understanding arrived whole, terrible in its clarity, the way dawn arrives — gradually, and then all at once. She would pay with her past, not her future, because the future was only a rumor the past told about itself. She would walk through the Door and come out the other side a stranger wearing her own coat. She would open a country for people who belonged, and she would not belong anywhere, because belonging requires a *before*, and hers would be spent.

"Oh," Sebastian said aloud. Just that. It seemed important to say something, and honesty was the theme of the day.

"What?" Ilse's hand tightened on her arm. "What is it? Sebastian, what did it say?"

"It didn't say anything." Sebastian heard her own voice and was mildly surprised by how steady it was. "That's rather the point. It doesn't negotiate. I checked. Professionally." A small, hysterical part of her noted that she was making jokes, and that making jokes was what she did when the world ended, and that the world was currently ending at a rate of approximately one childhood per minute once she touched the light.

"You don't have to do this alone." Ilse turned to face her fully, wincing, refusing the wince. "There are other ways. The Echoes offered — your grandmother —"

"No." Sebastian said it gently, but she said it. "If I take her peace to buy my passage, then the country I found is founded on the same arithmetic the veil was. Trade someone else's remembering for my convenience. No." She looked at the seam of light, at the thin gold insistence of it, older than cruelty itself. "Besides, it isn't offering anymore. Look."

The Door had begun, very quietly, to open.

* * *

Through the widening seam she could see — not Vellmark as it was. Not the pre-war capital with its tram bells and its forty kinds of plum brandy, not the restored country Kessler dreamed of ruling with Custodian clipboards. She could see possibility itself, unformed, waiting: a plain that wanted to be meadows, a valley that wanted streets, air that wanted names in it. A blank page. The guild had spent a hundred years trading in lost things, and here at the end of everything was the largest blank space she had ever seen, and it was not empty. It was *expectant*.

Somewhere behind them, far off across the darkening glass, there was a sound like thunder learning to march. Kessler's retrieval squad. Perhaps an hour. Perhaps less. The glass would slow them — the Steppe reshaped itself around pursuit, and the Echoes were singing the old border songs to confuse the roads — but it would not stop them. Nothing would stop Kessler except a closed door, and the Door could only be held during the anniversary of the Veiling, and the anniversary was *now*, burning down like a candle in a draft.

"Sebastian." Ilse caught her face in both hands. Her palms were warm and one of them was shaking, and she did not pretend otherwise, which Sebastian loved her for, had always loved her for — the refusal to make courage look easy. "Listen to me. Whatever it takes. Whatever it costs. You tell me before you do it. You don't get to decide alone. That was the deal, remember, back at the festival we never attended. Everything shared."

"I remember," Sebastian said. "We danced badly."

"We danced *catastrophically*. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

"The difference matters," Sebastian agreed, and leaned her forehead against Ilse's, and let herself have exactly four seconds of ordinary human cowardice. Four seconds of wanting to run the other way, to find some cottage somewhere and grow tomatoes and never learn another secret as long as she lived. Then she breathed out, and the four seconds were spent, and she was Sebastian Winterbourne, last apprentice of the Guild of Impossible Things, and impossible things were, as it happened, her entire professional specialty.

"All right," she said. "Here's what it wants. It wants my past. All of it. Everything that points toward the life I won't live, paid in full, starting with the earliest entries." She smiled, and was distantly amazed to find the smile mostly genuine. "I'm told I keep excellent records. It seems fitting that I finally balance one."

Ilse went white. Whiter than the wound had made her. "No. No — there has to be —"

"There isn't. I've read the ledgers, love. I've read *all* the ledgers. Equal weight or no trade. A country weighs—" she gestured at everything, the horizon, the history, the half-remembered war — "this much. So does a whole life. It's the fairest exchange I've ever seen, actually. The veil cheated everyone it traded with. This thing doesn't cheat."

"Then I'll pay half. We'll split it, we'll —"

"With what? Your memories are Custodian property, Ilschen. They'd take you apart at the border of your own skull looking for them." Sebastian took her hands. "And even if they weren't — even if we could split it — you'd forget me, and I'd forget you, and we'd stand on either side of a new country being strangers who feel oddly sad around each other. I've read that story too. It's in the archive. Nobody likes the ending."

"So what's the ending here?" Ilse's voice cracked clean through. "You walk through and you don't know my name. You build a country and you don't know why your hands shake when you see dark hair. Is that the victory?"

Sebastian considered the question honestly, because Ilse deserved honesty more than comfort.

"It's the beginning," she said. "Of something. For everyone else." She squeezed her hands. "And for me — I don't know. Maybe I'll meet a woman at a customs post somewhere. Apparently I'm slow, but I get there eventually."

A sound escaped Ilse that was half sob and half furious laughter. "You're *unbelievable*."

"I'm a guild apprentice. Unbelievable is on the invoice."

* * *

Farther off now, unmistakable: the march of the squad, and beneath it a wrongness in the air, the sound of Kessler's machines unstitching the Steppe's careful confusions. Minutes. The Echoes had bought them minutes, singing themselves hoarse with old anthems, weaponizing nostalgia one last time.

Sebastian turned to the Door.

Up close, the light was warm. That surprised her most, somehow — after all the dread, all the vertigo, the watching-felt-from-the-prophecy, the whole long unraveling of home into forgery — that the thing asking everything of her should feel like standing near an oven on a winter morning. Like her mother's kitchen, said a voice in her that still had a mother's kitchen to reference. *Hurry,* said the rest of her, thinking of marching boots.

She understood, in these final moments of having a past to understand with, what 'the life after' truly meant. Not the years forfeit. Not the sacrifice in the abstract. It meant this: that some doors only open from the inside of a person, and you have to be willing to be the hinge. That love is not diminished by being forgotten; it is only unindexed. Somewhere in the new country's founding there would be a clause no one could source, an instinct toward mercy no one could cite, a woman named Sebastian Winterbourne who flinched pleasantly at the smell of burnt coffee without knowing why — and that would be her. That would be enough. That would have to be.

"Ilse Marchetti," she said clearly, so the words would be among the last ones stored, given priority placement, filed at the front. "I love you. I have since the customs house. I was going to tell you properly at the festival we never attended, but we danced catastrophically instead, and honestly that was better."

"I know," Ilse whispered. "I knew before you did. Custodians notice things. It's the whole job."

"Noticed this too, did you?"

"Every single day for four years." Ilse kissed her — brief, fierce, tasting of salt and bread-smell bandages and the end of the world — and then stepped back, holding on to her composure with both bleeding hands. "Go. Before I think of an argument good enough to stop you."

"You won't. You're bad at stopping things you believe in. It's why you defected."

"Go, Sebastian."

She went.

One step. The light took her ankle like warm water. Two steps. It rose past her knees, her waist, and inside the light she could feel the accounting begin — gentle, thorough, irreversible. The first thing to go was small: the name of her childhood street, dissolving like sugar in tea. Then the street itself, then the house, then —

Her mother's voice, singing.

She felt it lift out of her and did not try to hold it, because she had already learned this lesson once at the Night Market, paying guild wages for a single hour: some things you carry by letting them go. The song went into the Door, and the Door brightened, and somewhere in the widening seam a valley decided to be a valley.

Then Oren. His lucid mornings, his terrible handwriting, his confession by candlelight — *I signed it to save my brother, girl, and I'd sign it again, and that's the whole tragedy of men* — rising out of her like heat off summer stone. The guild's crooked stairs. The jar of borrowed thunder, which rattled once in her memory, indignant, and was spent. The key to a house that burned. The ledgers, thousands of them, every stolen recollection she had ever shelved, pouring out of her now in reverse order, restitution at last, the guild trading its entire inventory for the one thing it had always claimed to exist for.

The glass under the Door had begun, very faintly, to grow grass.

Behind her, Ilse was shouting her name — and Sebastian turned, because she was still, barely, still herself enough to turn — and saw Ilse running toward her through the light, wounded, defiant, tears cutting through the dust on her face, shouting words Sebastian suddenly could not parse. The sounds arrived lovely and meaningless, birdsong in a language she had almost finished forgetting.

Almost.

Because the last thing to go, the very last entry, the one the Door saved for the end the way a kind executioner saves the hood — was Ilse herself. Her name. The customs post. The dance. Four years of watching and being watched and choosing, over and over, in increments, treasonously, to stay.

Sebastian felt it begin to leave and did the only thing left to do, the impossible thing, the guild-trained thing:

She held on.

Not to keep it — nothing kept, nothing kept, the Door was patient and fair and absolutely immovable — but to slow it. To pay it out word by word, thread by thread, the way you feed a rope through your hands so it doesn't burn. And behind her, catching up, catching her, Ilse's arms came around her from outside the light, and Ilse began to speak — fast, urgent, telling their story aloud, feeding it back to her word by word as fast as it drained away, a hand-to-hand battle against forgetting fought with nothing but memory and grammar —

"— your name is Sebastian Winterbourne, mine is Ilse Marchetti, we met at the customs post at Veren Ford, you argued with me about a stamp, you were *wrong* about the stamp —"

The Door opened wider.

The grass grew.

And the first boots of Kessler's squad crested the far rise of glass.
