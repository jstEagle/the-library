## 32. What Sebastian Forgets

Ilse had been trained, once, to observe without intervening. It was the first doctrine they taught at the Custodian academy: *A keeper who touches the veil becomes part of it.* She had recited that sentence for twenty years the way other people recited prayers, and she had believed it right up until the moment she knelt on the glass Steppe with Sebastian Winterbourne's head in her lap and watched her begin to disappear.

The Door was a seam of light older than the country, standing in the middle of nothing, humming like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. And Sebastian stood before it with her palms open, the way she did everything—bravely, stubbornly, and with an expression that suggested she'd already thought of three objections and decided none of them mattered.

"No," Ilse had said. Not eloquently. Just: no, the way you say it when you have run out of arguments and are left only with the truth.

Sebastian had looked back over her shoulder at her, and her smile had been terrible and bright. "You knew this was the price."

"I knew there would be a price. I didn't know it would be *you*."

"Nobody ever knows it's them," Sebastian said. "That's rather the point of prices."

And then she had turned back to the Door, and the Door had begun to take.

* * *

It started small. That was the cruelty of it—there was no thunderclap, no rending of light. The first thing to go was a Tuesday.

Ilse saw it happen because she had spent years learning to read people the way auditors read ledgers, and something in Sebastian's face simply *blanked*—a flicker, a gap, like a page torn cleanly out of a book. Sebastian blinked twice.

"I lost—" she began, frowning. "There was a day. A gray day. I was eight, maybe nine, and someone made me soup." Her brow creased harder. "Why can't I remember the soup?"

"Because it's gone," Ilse said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "The Door took it."

"The Door took my *soup*?" Sebastian laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and for one absurd moment they were just two women kneeling on a frozen plain of glass, laughing about soup. Then Sebastian's hand drifted up to her own collarbone, where the guild's seal hung on its cord, and her laughter thinned. "How much of me is a bowl of soup, do you think? How many Tuesdays does a person have?"

"Don't count them," Ilse said. "Counting is how you lose things faster. You start holding each memory up to the light to check it's still there, and the checking wears them thin."

"And what do I do instead?"

What Ilse did instead was reach for her. She gathered Sebastian against her chest, one arm across her shoulders, one hand cradling the back of her head, and she put her mouth close to Sebastian's ear.

"You hold on to someone else's voice," she said. "I've read the Custodian field manuals, all of them, including the ones we weren't supposed to have. The veil works by subtraction—it takes a memory and then lets the silence around it spread. But silence can be interrupted. A story told aloud, in real time, word by word, fills the gaps faster than the veil can open them. It's not a cure. It's a *stall*. Like sandbags against a flood."

"How long will your sandbags hold?"

"Long enough," Ilse said, which was a lie, and from the look in Sebastian's eyes, a lie they both agreed to permit.

"Then talk," Sebastian said, and closed her eyes. "Tell me everything. Start wherever you like. Start at the customs house."

So Ilse started at the customs house.

* * *

"You were twenty-two," she said, "and you were arguing with a border clerk about a stamp."

Sebastian's breath caught. "Was I winning?"

"You were never going to win. The stamp said your grandmother's surname was spelled wrong, and you informed him—in that voice you use, the reasonable one, the one like a drawn curtain—that spelling a name wrongly was a small act of violence, and that violence, however small, was still violence, and that you would stand there until the paperwork reflected reality."

"That sounds exactly like me."

"It *was* exactly like you. I was behind you in the queue with a forged transit pass in my coat pocket, and I remember thinking two things. The first was that you were going to get us both detained. The second was that I had never in my life wanted to be detained by anyone so much."

Sebastian smiled with her eyes still shut. "Go on."

"The clerk gave in eventually. He always gives in; that's the secret of clerks. They fight until they're tired and then they pretend they were tired all along. He corrected the spelling, and you thanked him so graciously that he blushed, and you walked out of that office like a woman who had personally reorganized the borders of Europe. And you passed me on the steps, and you stopped, and you said—" Ilse paused, because saying the next part aloud, into Sebastian's hair, while the Door hummed and drank, felt like setting down something heavy after carrying it for years. "You said, 'Your papers are trembling. Are you all right?'"

"I didn't know you were Custodian."

"You didn't know anything about me. That was the miracle of it. You looked at a nervous stranger with forged papers and your only instinct was to ask if she was all right." Ilse swallowed. "I said I was fine. You said, 'Liar, but a polite one,' and you bought me coffee at the kiosk outside, and you talked for forty minutes about the guild's inventory—you told me about a jar of borrowed thunder, do you remember the jar?—"

"I—" Sebastian's face went smooth again. Empty. The flicker of erasure moved through her like weather. "Jar of what?"

"*Thunder,*" Ilse said quickly, pressing the word back into her like pressing a flower into a book. "Borrowed thunder. In a preserving jar, on the third shelf of the guild cellar, next to the key to a house that burned down in 1911. You loved that jar. You used to shake it very gently, once a season, just to hear it grumble. You said impossibilities needed exercise or they got sulky."

"Did I say that?" Sebastian murmured. "That does sound like something I'd say." And the shape of her came back—the particular tilt of her head, the wry line between her brows—and Ilse exhaled.

*Keep talking,* she told herself. *Word by word. Fill every gap before it opens.*

* * *

The toll came in waves. Ilse learned to read their approach: a stillness in Sebastian first, a kind of listening, as though she could hear the Door chewing somewhere beneath the world. Then the blankness. Then the panic—not loud, never loud; Sebastian's panics were quiet, dignified affairs—but a tightening around the eyes, a hand grasping at Ilse's sleeve like a swimmer at driftwood.

Her childhood went first, in broad swaths. The gray Tuesdays, then the whole gray year. Her mother's voice—which she had bought back an hour of at the Night Market, an hour Ilse had watched her treasure like a lit candle cupped in both hands—flickered and guttered. Sebastian wept through that one, silently, her face pressed to Ilse's shoulder, while Ilse described the market itself instead: the stalls of confiscated letters, the lullabies sold by weight, the vendor with the crooked hat who had traded fairly and wrapped the hour of her mother's voice in brown paper and string.

"You carried it home under your coat," Ilse said. "You wouldn't let me carry it. You said some things have to be carried by the person they belong to."

"Yes," Sebastian whispered. "Yes, I remember the weight of it. Paper and string." And the candle relit, briefly, in her face.

But the tide kept coming.

Somewhere past midnight—though on the Steppe there was no midnight, only a permanent dusk that the glass reflected back in colors that belonged to other skies—the Door reached for bigger things. Sebastian lost the layout of the guild. Lost the smell of Oren Voss's pipe tobacco. Lost, with a small wounded sound that Ilse would hear for the rest of her life, the name of her own mentor.

"He's called Oren," Ilse said. "Oren Voss. He forgets you now, most days, but on good mornings he remembers, and when he remembers he calls you 'the last apprentice' like a title he's proud of. You signed his ledgers in green ink because black ink was too final for you. He keeps every ledger. He has kept them for fifty years, even the ones that hurt him, especially the ones that hurt him."

"Green ink," Sebastian repeated. Her hand rose to touch her own fingers as if checking they existed. "Green ink."

"Green ink."

And then, in the deepest hour, the Door found Ilse herself.

She felt it before she understood it. Sebastian, half-dreaming against her chest, suddenly stiffened—looked up at her with the polite, distant courtesy of a stranger on a train.

"I'm sorry," Sebastian said carefully. "Do I know you? You seem—I feel as though I ought to know you."

The words went through Ilse like a blade finding the gap between ribs. Twenty years of Custodian discipline held her upright; twenty years of training not to intervene, not to flinch, not to become part of what she guarded—and here, finally, at the end of all of it, she broke every rule she'd ever learned and pulled Sebastian closer.

"Yes," she said. "You know me. Listen, and I'll prove it. Your name is Sebastian Winterbourne. You were born on both sides of a border that no longer officially exists, which makes you the only person alive who belongs to both erased halves of a country, which makes you the only person who could open this door, which is why you're opening it, which is why I'm here, which is why—" Her voice cracked and she let it. There was no more use pretending to composure; composure was for audits. "Which is why I love you. My name is Ilse Marchetti. I was a Custodian for twenty years and a coward for nineteen of them, and in the twentieth I burned my own file and walked out of the capital with the veil's master schedule sewn into my coat lining, and I did it for you, and I would do it again, and you know me, Sebastian, you *know* me—"

"Ilse," Sebastian breathed. The name arrived on her face like dawn arriving on a landscape—slowly, and then all at once. "Ilse Marchetti. You have a scar on your left thumb from slicing bread at a festival neither of us actually attended."

"A festival neither of us actually attended," Ilse confirmed, laughing and crying at once, because that was the thing they'd sheltered inside together, weeks ago, during the memory-storm—a borrowed recollection of lanterns and bad fiddle music and warm wine, a festival that had never happened except in the shape of their wanting it. "You stepped on my foot during the dancing and blamed the accordion player."

"I stand by that. It was absolutely the accordion player's fault." Sebastian's grip tightened on her wrist. "Don't stop. Whatever it costs you—don't stop telling it."

"It doesn't cost me anything."

"Liar," Sebastian said softly, "but a polite one."

* * *

Toward what might have been morning, the toll grew greedy. Names went. Small ones first—a neighbor, a teacher, a cousin—and then, horribly, Sebastian began reaching for her own.

"What's my—" she started once, and stopped, and her eyes went wide with a terror so pure it had shed all its dignity. "Ilse. What's my name. I had one. It was written somewhere. It was in a book, an old book, it was written a hundred years before I was born—"

"Sebastian," Ilse said, and said it again, and again, folding the name into her over and over the way you'd fold a blanket around someone shivering. "Sebastian Winterbourne. Last apprentice of the Guild of Impossible Things. Keeper of the jar of thunder. Writer in green ink. Arguer with clerks. Buyer of soup-adjacent Tuesdays. Belonger to both sides of the border. Founder-to-be of something new." She pressed her forehead to Sebastian's. "Say it back."

"Sebastian," Sebastian whispered. "Winterbourne." A pause, a struggle, the tide pulling. "Sebastian Winterbourne. That's—yes. Yes. It fits. It fits like a key."

"That's because it is one. You're a key, my love. You've been a key since before you knew what the lock looked like."

Outside them, far off across the glass plain, the air had begun to shudder. Ilse heard it before she allowed herself to interpret it: a deep rhythmic percussion, boots, hundreds of boots, and beneath them the flat mechanical song of Custodian retrieval engines warming up. Kessler had come, and he had not come quietly, and he had not come alone.

Sebastian heard it too. Half-erased, hollow-eyed, holding onto her own name like a rope over a cliff, she still managed a ghost of her old wry smile.

"They're early," she said.

"They're punctual," Ilse said grimly. "It's the one virtue they taught us."

"Then we haven't got long." Sebastian turned toward the seam of light. It was brighter now, fed on everything it had taken, drinking the shape of her life in slow silver sips. "Ilse. If I finish paying—if the toll takes everything—if I walk through that door and don't know why I walked through it—"

"Then I'll tell you," Ilse said. "Every day. Every morning, over breakfast, in whatever country we build on the other side. I'll tell you the soup story and the customs-house story and the accordion-player story until you're sick of them, and then I'll keep telling them. You are not going anywhere I can't follow with words."

Sebastian kissed her—brief, fierce, tasting of salt and cold glass and the particular desperation of people stealing time—and then turned to face the Door, and squared her shoulders, and lifted her empty hands.

And the Door, having waited a century for exactly this, opened wider—

—as the first rank of Kessler's soldiers crested the ridge of glass behind them, and the horizon filled with light that had nothing to do with dawn.
