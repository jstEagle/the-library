## 34. The Weight of a Country

The Door was screaming.

That is the only word for it. A seam of light older than the country it had guarded, and it was screaming—not with sound, exactly, but with the particular pressure a kettle makes just before the whistle, the whole world tightening toward a note that hadn't arrived yet. Kessler had forced it wide with a hundred borrowed memories and the weight of his own terrible certainty, and now it was trying to close on an army, and armies do not fit through keyholes.

Sebastian knelt on the glass of the Steppe, half-erased, and did the arithmetic of what remained of her.

It was not nothing. That surprised her. She had expected—when she had stepped forward, when she had begun to pay—to feel herself thinning like ink in rain, and there had been some of that, certainly. Her childhood was gone; she could feel the absence of it the way you feel a missing tooth, a smooth place where something used to catch. Her mother's voice, bought back at such cost at the Night Market, had drained out of her again like water through open fingers. Even her name felt loose in her mouth, a coat belonging to someone slightly larger.

But underneath all that, something held. Something small and stubborn and warm.

*Ilse*, said the thing that held. *The ledger labeled Zero. The first entry, written in full.*

They had taken her past. They had not yet taken her future, because she had not yet decided what it was.

"Sebastian!" Ilse's voice came from somewhere behind her, ragged with pain and distance. "Get away from it—it's pulling—"

"I know," Sebastian said, though she wasn't sure she did. She was kneeling perhaps ten paces from the seam of light, and the light had stopped being a line. It had become a mouth, or a wound, or—she thought suddenly, absurdly—a doorway in a house where nobody had lived for fifty years, standing open because no one had thought to shut it, waiting for someone to come home and decide what the room should be.

Kessler stood before the Door with his arms spread like a man presenting a cathedral. His retrieval squad poured past him in ranks, soldiers stepping onto the glass with the confidence of men who believed maps were promises. And the glass was eating them. Not killing them—the Steppe was never cruel, only honest—but each soldier who crossed left footprints full of his own remembered Sundays, and each footprint slowed him, and the Door drank the Sundays and grew wider and more frantic and more wrong, a river forced to run uphill.

"This country died of sentiment," Kessler called, and his voice carried across the plain with the flat clarity of a man reading from an official document. "I am giving it teeth. Stand aside, Winterbourne. Your guild is ash. Your prophecy is spent. History belongs to whoever arrives first."

"You're not arriving," Sebastian said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, and she heard Oren in it, the old man's lucid rasp: *an impossible thing is only impossible until someone does the paperwork.* "You're looting. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

She looked down at her hands. Half of her was missing, and what remained was lighter than she had ever been. That was the horror of it, and also—if she was honest, and the Steppe insisted on honesty—the temptation. Erasure was not pain. It was relief. It was setting down luggage after a very long walk. The Door's price was not cruelty; it was simply the market rate for countries, and countries are heavy.

Behind her, Ilse dragged herself across the glass, one hand pressed to her side where the Custodian blade had opened her. Behind *them*, scattered across the plain like candles in wind, the Echoes stood their ground—her grandmother among them, luminous and unbowed, singing the old borders while Custodian nostalgia-traps snapped shut around soldiers and left them weeping in loops of harvest festivals and first snows.

And the Door screamed on, glutted and starving at once, because it had been fed a hundred years of endings and never once been offered a beginning.

*Oh*, thought Sebastian Winterbourne, who had spent her whole invisible life cataloguing other people's impossibilities, and who understood, in the last clear moment of her old self, that she had been apprenticed to this exact errand since the day she first shelved a jar of borrowed thunder.

*You're not supposed to give it the past. You're supposed to give it something it hasn't got.*

* * *

"Grandmother," she said, and the Echo turned—or the woman who claimed to be her grandmother, who might have been, who had earned the word either way by standing between Sebastian and a squad of armed men singing lullabies in a language the treaty had outlawed.

"Child." The Echo's light flickered. She was spending herself; Sebastian could see it, the way you can see a candle guttering. "Whatever you mean to do, do it quickly. The Door cannot hold both his army and its grief much longer."

"The price," Sebastian said. "A memory equal in weight to a country. I've been paying it wrong. We've all been paying it wrong. The Accord, the Veiling, Kessler—we keep trying to hand it *backwards*. Old Vellmark. The borders as they were. But it doesn't want what we remember. It wants—" She faltered, because the idea was enormous and she was half a person, and enormous ideas need whole people.

"It wants what you will remember," her grandmother finished softly.

"A future." Sebastian laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "The impossible thing. Fifty years of guild ledgers and the answer was in the founding charter the whole time. We trade in what's lost. Nobody ever tried trading in what isn't *yet*."

"And what would you give it?" the Echo asked. "You've already paid your childhood. Your mother's voice. What's left that weighs enough?"

Sebastian turned. Ilse had reached her—of course she had; Ilse Marchetti had crossed a border war, an order of Custodians, and a field of weaponized nostalgia to reach her, a few more yards of glass was nothing—and she knelt there bleeding and furious and beautiful, and Sebastian understood the shape of the payment before she understood how to say it.

"The life after," she said. "That's what the Door asked for. Not my past. My *after*. Every year I'd have lived on the far side of it. Every morning. Every ordinary Tuesday." She took Ilse's hand, careful of the wound. "Every version of this, grown old."

"No," Ilse said immediately, the way you'd say *no* to a knife coming at your throat. "No, absolutely not, you've paid enough, you can barely remember—"

"That's rather the point." Sebastian smiled at her. Gentle humor had always been their dialect; even now, especially now. "You once falsified eleven years of reports about me. The least I can do is falsify one about our retirement."

"Sebastian—"

"There's a cottage in it," Sebastian went on, and her voice began to shake, because she could see it, the Door could see it, the whole plain could see it: the after, laid out in inventory, item by item, the way she'd once catalogued a jar of thunder and a key to a burned house. "There's a garden Ilse will pretend not to care about and then secretly reorganize by blooming season. There's a dog we'll argue about naming. There's a shelf of ledgers, and every winter we add a volume, and every spring we reread the old ones and argue about which of us exaggerated. There's growing old together. That's the weight of it. That's a country's worth. A country is only the sum of the days its people intended to spend in it—and I intend—" her voice broke entirely, mended itself, "—I *intended* so many days."

The Door's screaming changed pitch.

Not quieter. Deeper. The note it had been straining toward since Kessler forced it open finally arrived, and it was not a note of pain. It was the sound a lock makes that has been stiff for fifty years and suddenly remembers it is a lock, and turns.

"Sebastian." Ilse gripped her hand hard enough to hurt, which Sebastian was grateful for; pain was proof of remaining. "Listen to me. If you give it the after—all of it—you'll cross with nothing. You'll be a stranger to yourself. You'll look at me and see a kind woman you don't know."

"Yes," Sebastian said.

"You'll forget why you fought. Why anyone fought. You'll forget *Vellmark*."

"Somebody will remember it for me." She nodded toward the Echoes, toward her grandmother, whose light had steadied as if fed by the mere idea. "That's the trick, isn't it? That's what the old rulers never understood, and what Kessler will never understand. Memory isn't a possession. It's a *practice*. You don't keep it. You do it. Together. Daily. Like sweeping a floor." She squeezed Ilse's fingers. "So here's my counteroffer, and you'll want to sit down, except you're already sitting down, which shows excellent foresight."

Despite everything—despite the blood and the army and the screaming door—Ilse made a sound that was almost a laugh. "Sebastian."

"I'm not going to give the Door the past," Sebastian said. "And I'm not only giving it my after. I'm giving it a *draft*. An unfinished thing. A country that doesn't exist yet, built here, on the Steppe, out of everyone the treaty threw away. Both bloodlines. Both banks of the old border. The Echoes first—they've waited longest, and they've earned citizenship over anybody living. No veils. No custodians. No trades. A country founded on the radical proposition that nobody gets folded out of it." She drew a breath. "A country that is mostly, gloriously, *not written yet*."

The plain went still.

Even the soldiers stopped. Even Kessler turned, slowly, his certainty meeting something it had no filing category for. Because he had come to seize a nation, and nations can be seized; but Sebastian was offering the Door an invention, and inventions cannot be seized, only joined.

"You can't found a country out of a feeling," Kessler said. "Where are its laws? Its treasuries? Its—"

"Its ledgers?" Sebastian said sweetly. "Funny you should ask. I happen to be the last apprentice of the Guild of Impossible Things, and I am *excellent* with paperwork."

* * *

She stood up.

Half of her was gone, and the half that remained stood up anyway, the way a sentence stands up when it finds its verb. She walked toward the Door—not running, not bravely limping, just walking, the walk she took every morning through streets that sometimes forgot themselves, the walk of a person doing diligence, doing devotion, doing the small unglamorous work that holds worlds together.

The seam of light bent toward her like wheat toward wind.

"I know what you are," she told it. Up close, the Door was not a mouth or a wound at all. It was a margin. The blank space at the edge of a page where the next line goes. Fifty years ago, frightened people had written their country into this margin to save it, and margins are patient places, but patience curdles. "You've been holding a bookmark in history for half a century, and everyone who comes to you brings another ending. Restoration. Ruin. Conquest." She glanced back at Kessler, who was shouting orders that the glass was no longer interested in. "Here. Try a beginning."

She opened her hands, and gave it the after.

All of it. The cottage and the garden and the dog whose name they'd never agree on. The winter ledgers. Ilse's hair going grey and Ilse pretending she'd arranged it that way. Forty, fifty, sixty years of ordinary Tuesdays, stacked and weighed and surrendered, a lifetime's worth of mornings paid out like coins from a jar marked *emergency countries*.

It didn't hurt. That was the strangest part. It felt like the last page of a good book—the grief and the gratitude arriving in the same breath, indistinguishable.

And the Door, having swallowed a future whole, did the unprecedented thing.

It exhaled.

Light rolled out across the Steppe like dawn deciding to get an early start. Where it passed, the glass softened—not melting, but remembering soil, remembering grass, the way an old woman remembers dancing. The soldiers' footprints filled with green instead of Sundays. The nostalgia traps dissolved into actual nostalgia, harmless, shared, the property of everyone. And through the widening seam—no longer a seam, a *doorway*, a proper one with a threshold you could sweep—came air that smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

The Door spoke. Not in words. In the language of terms accepted, contracts sealed, the deep satisfied hum of a ledger balancing.

*A future*, it seemed to say, *weighs more than a past. No one ever checks.*

"Don't tell anyone," Sebastian whispered. "It'll ruin the mystery."

Then the world behind her—Vellmark's veil, the great grey lie stretched over a nation for fifty years—felt the change in the weather, shivered once like a horse hearing a distant bell, and began, gently, to come undone.

Sebastian turned to find Ilse staring at her across ten feet of newly grassed earth, pale, bleeding, alive, and looking at her with the particular expression of a woman realizing she is about to be introduced to herself all over again.

"Well," Sebastian said. Her own name felt like a coat that almost fit. "I appear to have founded a country. Someone ought to write that down."

* * *

The hook: beyond the dissolving veil, beyond the hills, the outside world—maps printing, clocks stuttering, a million breakfasts interrupted—was about to wake up and find a new neighbor where the blank space used to be.
