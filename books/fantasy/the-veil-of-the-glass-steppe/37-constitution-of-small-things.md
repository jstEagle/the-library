## 37. Constitution of Small Things

The first constitutional convention of the country that did not yet have a name was held in a customs house.

Ilse appreciated the irony more than she could safely say aloud. She had spent eleven years in buildings like this one—stamped paper, brass grilles, the particular smell of ink and patience—and now the same furniture hosted something unprecedented: thirty-seven people trying to decide, on purpose and in writing, what they owed one another.

The table had been made by pushing four desks together. Someone had laid a green felt card table over the gap where two of them didn't quite meet, so that when you leaned your elbows down, the whole surface gave slightly, like a boat. Documents slid toward the middle no matter where anyone set them, as if even the paper wanted consensus.

Sebastian sat across from her, sleeves rolled, a carpenter's pencil tucked behind her ear where a pen should be. She had explained the pencil once: *pens presume. Pencils forgive.* Ilse had written that down on the back of her hand before she could stop herself, and had walked around for two days with *pencils forgive* smudged across her knuckles like a vow.

"Article One," read Tomas Brandt, who had been a schoolteacher in the old Vellmark and was now, by unanimous acclamation nobody remembered voting on, the chair. He cleared his throat the way he must once have cleared it before a room of children. "We propose: *This country exists because its people agree that it does.*"

Silence.

Then a woman near the end of the table—the miller's widow, Petra Hahn, who had been an Echo for fifty years and still spoke as though words were rationed—said, "That's how the veil started."

The silence changed shape.

Ilse had learned to read silences the way other people read weather. This one held fear, but also something under the fear, the way soil holds water. Everyone at this table had been erased once, or complicit in erasing, or both. The idea that existence might rest on agreement sounded, to them, less like hope than like a trap resetting.

"That's fair," Sebastian said. She didn't say it defensively; she never defended ideas, she just turned them over like stones to see what lived underneath. "But the veil was an agreement made *about* people, without them. Nobody signed it except the ones it was used against." She paused. "And Oren. But we've discussed Oren."

At the far end of the room, old Oren Voss sat wrapped in a blanket despite the mild air, lucid these days in long stretches, listening with his eyes closed as though the discussion were music he was trying to learn by ear. At the mention of his name he raised one hand—not a protest, more a conductor's acknowledgment—and let it fall.

"So we amend," said Brandt. "Anyone?"

Petra Hahn leaned forward. "Add a second sentence. *No agreement about the people may be made without the people.*"

"Or," someone called from the back—a young man named Ferro, formerly of the Custodian support staff, who had defected three days after Kessler fell and still flinched at doors opening suddenly—"add a third. *Any law may be repealed by the people it governs, including this one.*"

"A constitution that can repeal itself," muttered Herr Aldous, who had run a bookshop on the outside and considered himself the room's last defense against enthusiasm.

"Yes," said Ilse.

Everyone looked at her. She was still not entirely used to being listened to. For eleven years her job had been to be unheard—to observe, record, file, and vanish. The Custodians had trained her into a kind of professional ghosthood, and old habits died slowly: she still caught herself standing where the light wouldn't reach, still cataloged exits on entering rooms, still sometimes drafted reports in her head about conversations she was having.

But here was the thing about being a ghost who has decided to haunt *for* people instead of against them: you know exactly where all the locks are.

"The veil lasted fifty years," Ilse said, "because it was designed to be unbreakable. That was its pride and its rot. A country that cannot be undone by its own people isn't a home. It's a cell with good lighting." She tapped the felt table; the papers shivered. "If we build something worth keeping, we don't need to weld the door shut. We need to trust each other not to walk out. And if someone does walk out—" she shrugged "—the door should still open from either side."

Sebastian was looking at her with an expression Ilse had privately named *the ledger look*—the look she wore when someone handed her an entry too true for the old books. It went through Ilse like warm water.

"Motion to adopt Article One as amended?" said Brandt.

Hands went up—all of them, eventually, even Aldous, who raised his with the air of a man signing a surrender he intended to sabotage later, which everyone understood and forgave, because Aldous's sabotages always turned out to be footnotes.

* * *

The memory clauses took three days.

They took three days because Ilse insisted on them, and because insisting meant explaining, and explaining meant telling the whole table what the veil had actually eaten.

She had prepared a speech. She had rehearsed it in the customs house attic at night, alone, the way she'd once rehearsed cover stories. But on the day, standing at the head of the wobbly desk-table with thirty-seven faces waiting, she threw the speech away and simply told them what she knew. The ledgers. The trades. The old woman who had sold her wedding day for a winter's coal and spent the rest of her life wondering why she flinched at church bells. The guild's inventory of stolen recollections, row upon row, filed with loving care by people who believed they were preserving history rather than cannibalizing it.

"I helped maintain a system," Ilse said, "in which memory was currency. Not metaphorically. Literally. You could owe your childhood. Your grief could be repossessed." She let that sit. "I am asking you to write clauses that make it impossible for me—or anyone like me—to ever do that work again, even if I beg you. Especially if I beg you. Because the argument will always come dressed as mercy. *Just this once. Just the bad memories. Just to get us through the winter.*"

"And what do your clauses say?" asked Petra Hahn.

Ilse unfolded the page she'd written by lamplight over four nights, in handwriting that got steadier as the clauses got shorter:

"*First: Memory belongs to the person who lived it. It may be given, but never taken, traded, taxed, or inherited without consent.*
*Second: No institution, guild, government, or person shall keep inventories of other people's memories.*
*Third: What is forgotten naturally stays forgotten. Grief is not a debt to be collected.*
*Fourth: If any law is ever passed that breaks the first three, every official who signed it forfeits office immediately and forever, and their names are entered in a public ledger of shame, which shall be the only ledger in this country permitted to record disgrace.*"

Aldous, of all people, snorted. "A public ledger of shame. Very dramatic."

"It worked on me," Ilse said. "I've spent my whole life afraid of files. Fear of filing is the only punishment I trust."

The laugh moved down the table like wind through wheat—even Oren laughed, a dry rustle—and then Brandt called for objections and there were none, and then there was one, and it came from Ferro, who had his hand half-raised and looked like a man about to confess to a crime.

"What if someone *wants* to forget?" Ferro said. "What if remembering is the thing killing them?"

The room went quiet in a different key.

It was Sebastian who answered, and Ilse watched her take off the carpenter's pencil and hold it the way she held fragile things, which was everything, always.

"Then we help them carry it until they can put it down themselves," Sebastian said. "We don't take it. We sit with them. It's slower. It's much, much slower." She glanced at Ilse, and something passed between them that had no article number. "Slower is the whole point. Everything the old system did, it did fast. Speed was how you knew it was stealing."

Ferro nodded slowly and withdrew his objection, and the clauses passed, and afterward Ilse went up to the attic and sat on the floor among the dust sheets and shook for ten minutes—not from sorrow, exactly. From the specific vertigo of a locked door opening outward for the first time.

* * *

The street-naming crisis began on the fifth day and nearly ended the convention entirely.

The problem was simple: the new capital—three streets, a square, the customs house, the shell of the guildhall, and a great deal of ambition—had no streets with names, and everyone had opinions, and the opinions were load-bearing.

The Vellmark survivors wanted the old names back. Lindenweg. Salt Gate Road. The Miller's Turn. Names that existed only in Echoes' memories and in Sebastian's cellar map, which still lay glowing in the guild's basement like a sleeping animal, its roads warm underfoot.

The borderlanders—the families who had lived on the *other* side of the vanished line, who had crossed into the Steppe in the weeks since the Unveiling with their carts and their goats and their suspicion—wanted new names. Neutral names. Names that belonged to nobody's grandfather, because their grandfathers had been on the wrong side of everybody's war.

"They're just names," said Herr Aldous.

"They are *never* just names," said Frau Lindqvist, who had been an Echo for so long that she remembered the original Lindenweg twice, from two different decades, and contradicted herself cheerfully whenever challenged.

By mid-afternoon the session had devolved into the kind of argument that is secretly a festival. The miller's widow proposed naming a street after bread. Ferro suggested naming everything after weather, "since nobody's fought a war over fog yet." Someone's goat got into the hall and ate the margin notes off Article Seven, which Brandt ruled—deadpan, banging the chair's gavel (a soup ladle)—was "the first successful popular amendment in our history."

Ilse sat with her chin in her hand and watched, and found herself doing the thing she had forbidden herself from doing, which was comparing.

In the Custodian halls, decisions had arrived already made, laminated, and smelling faintly of ozone. Disagreement had been a security breach. Laughter during briefings had been logged. She had once watched Warden Kessler pause an entire regional audit because a junior officer smiled while reading a report, and the smile had gone into the man's file, and the man had gone to the salt mines within the year.

And here was Frau Lindqvist laughing so hard at the goat that she had to hold onto Aldous, whom she had called "a pedantic turnip" an hour earlier, and Aldous laughing too, protesting, patting her arm with the stiff courtesy of a man discovering late in life that he had arms for that purpose.

Nobody would go to the salt mines. There were no salt mines. There was a salt flat east of the Steppe where children were already racing paper boats across the shallow pools, and next spring, someone had proposed, there would be a market.

The compromise, when it finally came, was Sebastian's, delivered around dusk with the pencil behind her ear and chalk dust on her sleeve:

"Old names for the streets that the Echoes remember walking. New names for the streets nobody remembers. And one rule: any street can be renamed by petition of the people who live on it, once every ten years." She grinned—that quick, crooked grin that had undone Ilse on a glass plain in a memory-storm and undid her still. "Streets shouldn't outlive their usefulness any more than laws should. Let the names earn their keep."

"Ten years is arbitrary," said Aldous.

"Everything is arbitrary," said Sebastian. "Arbitrary and *ours*. That's the upgrade."

They adopted it. They adopted it laughing, which Ilse thought might be the most legally significant laughter in the history of nations, though she kept that observation to herself, because she was still learning how to spend her sentences out loud.

* * *

On the seventh evening, the drafting broke early—Brandt had lost his voice, the goat had eaten something else, and there was a general drift toward the cookfire someone had built in the square—and Ilse found herself alone at the desk-table with the day's pages, straightening them into order out of pure habit.

Sebastian appeared beside her with two tin cups of something hot and brown that claimed, implausibly, to be coffee. "You've been at this for seven days," she said. "I've counted. You correct grammar in your sleep. Petra heard you."

"What did I say?"

"'Antecedent unclear.' Loudly. Then you apologized to whoever you were dreaming about."

"That tracks." Ilse took the cup. Across the square, the firelight moved on faces that a month ago had been light without bodies, Echoes stepping carefully into evenings like people testing ice. "Do you ever think about what we'd be doing if none of this happened? If you'd never opened that folio?"

Sebastian considered it with the seriousness she brought to hypotheticals, which was the same seriousness she brought to breakfast. "Cataloging. Quietly. Growing stranger and more invisible until the guild dissolved and I dissolved politely along with it." She sipped. "You?"

"Filed reports about you," Ilse said. "For another decade. Until they retired me to some office where they keep the auditors who've seen too much, stamping forms about rainfall." She turned the cup in her hands. "I prefer the goat."

"The goat is a strong argument for revolution," Sebastian agreed. Then, quieter: "Tomorrow they want to debate the name of the country itself. Actual hours allocated. Brandt's reserved the morning."

"God help us."

"Ferro wants 'New Vellmark.' Lindqvist wants 'Vellmark,' full stop, nothing new about it. The borderlander delegation has proposed six different names and withdrawn five."

"What's the sixth?"

"'Home,'" Sebastian said. "Lowercase. They wrote it lowercase in the proposal. As in—we shouldn't capitalize it, because capitals are for things that stand above you, and this is supposed to stand *with* you."

Ilse looked at her. The firelight caught the edge of her face, the pencil still behind her ear where no pen should be, the woman who had walked into a prophecy about her own erasure and come out the other side having invented a country instead.

"Lowercase," Ilse said. "It'll never survive committee."

"No," Sebastian said happily. "But it'll survive the vote. Watch."

They stood together a while longer, two ex-ghosts at a wobbling table, while somewhere beyond the fire the Goat Committee—there was now, appallingly, a Goat Committee—argued about fencing.

Later that night, Ilse copied the final draft of the memory clauses into the fair copy herself, in her own hand, pressing hard enough that the words would survive careless handling. At the bottom, beneath Article Four and the ledger of shame and the whole careful architecture of refusal, she added a line no one had voted on and no one would object to, because it was simply true:

*Built by hand. Held by consent. Openable from either side.*

She blew on the ink, set down the pen—

and from the square below heard Sebastian's voice rise above the crowd, delighted and alarmed in equal measure, calling her name.
