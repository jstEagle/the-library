## 38. The New Inventory

The guild's new premises were, by any honest accounting, a disgrace.

Sebastian stood in the doorway of the ground-floor room on Larkspur Row and surveyed it with the professional eye of someone who had spent years cataloguing ruin. The windows were cracked along one pane each, symmetrically, as if the building had decided to be fair about its decay. A water stain on the ceiling had spread into the shape of a country that no longer existed, which felt less like coincidence and more like habit. The floorboards complained when walked upon, but they complained in a friendly register, the way an old dog grumbles when it means to follow you into the kitchen anyway.

"It has," said Oren Voss from behind her, "a certain grandeur."

"It has a hole in the roof."

"A skylight," Oren corrected, with the serene authority of a man who had once signed away a country and therefore could not be intimidated by real estate. "Every great institution needs a skylight. How else is the sky meant to inspect our work?"

He was lucid today — had been lucid for eleven days now, a record since the Unveiling, as if the new air itself were a kind of medicine, or as if some part of him had been holding his memory shut the way a man holds a door against wind, and had finally, at the sight of a horizon with no treaty line drawn across it, let go. He still lost things. Yesterday he'd called Ilse by the name of his brother, and then wept, and then eaten an enormous lunch and asked for seconds as though nothing had happened. Sebastian was learning that grief and appetite could share a table.

"The rent," she said, because somebody had to say it, "is paid in a currency that is four months old."

"Then we are among the first tenants in history to pay in the future." Oren patted her shoulder and went off down the hall, humming something that might have been a Vellmarkish folk song or might have been invented on the spot; under the new constitution, both were legal tender.

* * *

The truth was that the guild had followed her here the way a stray follows the person who fed it once.

When the veil came down and Vellmark — no, *not* Vellmark, the maps had argued about this for weeks before settling, with typical municipal compromise, on Meridian — when Meridian appeared on the world's breakfast tables, the survivors of the old Guild of Impossible Things had drifted toward Sebastian like iron filings toward a lodestone. There were nine of them left. Nine, out of a guild whose ledgers once ran to forty volumes. Nine people who knew how to carry a jar of borrowed thunder without spilling it, how to file a key to a house that burned so that the house might someday find its way home.

Marta Kessler-adjacent nobody, that was the joke Marta herself made — Marta of the wedding memory donated to power the map, Marta who had stood in the cellar and said *take it, I'll remember my husband better without it anyway, he snored like a landslide* — arrived first, carrying a crate of salvaged objects and refusing to discuss what else had been lost. Then Pell, the decoy who had never gotten to decoy anyone, tall and solemn and unexpectedly gifted at carpentry. Then the others, trickling in over the summer, each with their small salvaged impossible thing held against their chest like a bird.

They had all, at some point, asked the same question: *What are we now?*

The old answer was ash. The old answer had been written in ledgers full of stolen memories, cages bound in calfskin, and Sebastian could not look at those shelves without hearing the sound of a city politely forgetting itself. The ledgers had survived — most of them, warped and water-stained, hauled out of the cellar during the collapse — but she kept them locked away, not destroyed, because destroying evidence of your crimes is just another crime with better posture. They would be read someday, she supposed. Testimony. A warning. But they would not be the guild.

So she had thought for a long time — through the autumn of arguments over street names, through the winter of rationed coal and abundant hope — about what a guild of impossible things should do when the impossible had been accomplished once already and everyone was very tired.

And the answer, when it came, was so simple she laughed aloud alone in her rented room, and the landlady knocked to ask if she was quite well.

*We stop recording what is lost,* she wrote in her notebook. *We start recording what is kept.*

* * *

The ledger cost three days' wages from a bookbinder on Cordwainer Street who did not ask why Sebastian wanted the pages blank, sewn so they could lie perfectly flat, with margins wide enough for a generous hand. She had considered commissioning something magnificent — gilded, tooled, worthy of the founding — but standing in the shop she'd found herself thinking of the old folios, the prophecy of 1899 with tonight's date beside her name, and how the most dangerous book she'd ever touched had been beautiful. Beauty in a ledger, she decided, was like sugar in a lock. It looked like generosity. It opened doors it shouldn't.

So: plain boards, dyed a deep blue-green like the Steppe meadows coming in at the edges, and on the cover, stamped simply, in letters a child could read:

**LEDGER ZERO**

Beneath it, smaller: *A Record of What Is Kept.*

She carried it home through the evening streets of the new capital, and the capital — gods, the capital. Six months old and already it squabbled like a family at a funeral that had gone on too long and turned, somewhere around the second course, into a party. Lamplighters were arguing with the electrical committee again. Somebody had painted a mural of the Unveiling on the customs house wall and given the Door Out a decorative border of roses, which the veterans' association had protested as insufficiently austere, which the florists' guild had defended as economically vital. Two countries' worth of bloodlines arguing over everything and eating dinner together afterward, because that was the whole experiment, wasn't it. Belonging built, not granted. Built slowly, badly, loudly, out of arguments about roses.

She let herself in through the guild's front door, which stuck, and stood a moment in the empty ground-floor room while the light went amber through the crack in the windowpanes.

Tomorrow they would open. Tomorrow there would be shelves, and a counter, and a sign — Pell had carved it, GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS, EST. TONIGHT, because Oren had insisted the founding date be honest. Tomorrow the citizens of Meridian would come drifting in with their questions, their oddities, their griefs too strange for any other office: the woman who could no longer find her way to a house she'd lived in for thirty years; the boy who remembered a lullaby in a language that officially had no speakers left; the man who wanted, very quietly, to know whether it was normal, after a country comes back, to feel homesick for the time when you didn't know what you'd lost.

But tonight the room belonged to her and the ledger.

She set it on the counter. She uncapped her pen. And she sat for a long moment with the nib poised above the first page, understanding exactly what she was doing, and trembling slightly anyway.

Because the old ledgers had taught her this much: whatever is entered first defines the whole book. The first entry is the keystone. Write *debt* first, and every page after bends toward debt. The Custodians had understood this. Their master schedule — the schedule Ilse had carried out through burning air, the schedule that had told them the Door could be held open only during the anniversary of the Veiling — their whole architecture began with a single entry, decades back, in a hand that shook: *Received, from the city of ___ , the memory of ___, price agreed.*

Everything after that had merely been arithmetic.

So the first entry in Ledger Zero mattered. Not sentimentally. Structurally. Whatever she wrote here would be the grain the rest of the wood grew along.

She thought about writing the guild itself. *Kept: one institution, re-founded.* Or the seal of Oren Voss, warm in its box upstairs. Or the map under the map, its roads still faintly warm even now, planted in the cellar like a garden. All worthy. All true.

But her pen, which had apparently developed opinions during six months of being carried past certain people, dipped of its own accord.

*Entry the First.*

*Kept:* **Ilse Marchetti.** *Full name. All of her.*

Then, because a ledger entry wants particulars — the old guild had taught her that too, the discipline of the specific — she added:

*Kept entire: her stubbornness, which has saved my life no fewer than four times (see annex, pending). Her handwriting, which slants left like it's leaning away from authority. The way she reads standing up, as though sitting might trap her. The scar on her left palm from a customs-house blade, taken on the Steppe, in my place, which she describes as "a scratch" and which I have been instructed never to bring up at dinner. Her habit of falsifying reports in the direction of mercy. Her laugh, which she spent years hiding behind procedure and which I intend to spend the rest of the ledger documenting.*

*Provenance: acquired honestly, over many years, beginning at a customs post neither of us was guarding properly.*

*Value: not for trade. Not at any price. This entry is permanent.*

*Appraised by: S. Winterbourne, last apprentice of the Guild of Impossible Things, first keeper of the new one.*

Her hand was steady until the last line, and then it wasn't, and she let it blot, a small dark bloom on the page like a period at the end of a long sentence. The ink dried in the amber light. Outside, the lamplighter won his argument — she heard the cheer from the corner — and the street below came up glowing, one lamp at a time, a country lighting itself by hand.

She heard the front door stick, and then give, and footsteps she would have known anywhere, even blindfolded, even erased.

"You're here late," Ilse said from the doorway. She was in her coat, still smelling faintly of the committee rooms where she'd spent the day doing the thing she did now — sitting on the Commission for Memory Protections, arguing clause by clause that no citizen's recollection should ever again be currency. She'd fought for it the way she fought everything: literally, precisely, surviving interrogation once by telling only truths, and clearly intending to survive legislation the same way.

"I'm working," Sebastian said.

Ilse came around the counter and looked down at the open ledger. Sebastian watched her read the entry — watched her face do the thing it did, the controlled stillness first, the ex-Custodian reflex, and then the crack in it, the warmth getting through like light through the cracked panes. She read it twice. Sebastian saw her lips move on the second pass, silently, on the word *permanent.*

"That's not regulation format," Ilse said finally, and her voice wasn't entirely level, which was how Sebastian knew it was fine.

"No. It's better than regulation format."

"It says *all of her.* That's an inventory liability. What if I misplace something?"

"Then I file a report."

"With whom?"

"The guild. We handle impossible things. Finding the parts of you that go missing is squarely within our charter."

Ilse laughed — the real one, the one that had taken years to surface and still surprised her when it did — and leaned over the counter and kissed her, briefly, in the amber light, in the room with the hole in the roof where tomorrow a sign would hang reading *EST. TONIGHT.* When she straightened, she was holding her own pen, produced from her coat with the smoothness of a woman who had planned this, and Sebastian raised an eyebrow.

"Amendments?" Sebastian asked.

"One." Ilse bent over the page beneath the entry, and wrote, in her left-slanting, leaning-away-from-authority hand:

*Confirmed and countersigned. Also kept: the person who keeps.*

She capped the pen. Somewhere upstairs, Oren dropped something and said a word that predated both countries, and Marta's voice floated down telling him that language was not covered by the memory protections and he'd best watch himself.

"Come on," Ilse said, taking Sebastian's hand. "You've been living in this drafty mausoleum for a week. Dinner. And you're not allowed to bring the ledger, so don't try anything clever with your coat."

Sebastian closed the book gently, first entry drying safe inside, and blew out the lamp.

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the dark room — at the empty shelves waiting for tomorrow's strangeness, the stain on the ceiling shaped like everywhere they'd ever been, the counter where a plain blue-green book lay closed over two names and a promise.

Nine members. One charter. One entry.

It was, she thought, the wealthiest inventory the guild had ever held.

* * *

They were halfway down Larkspur Row, arguing happily about whether the restaurant on the corner counted as Vellmarkish or Meridianish cuisine (it served both, badly, and everyone loved it), when a boy came running up the street with a lantern and a look of official urgency that didn't suit his age.

"Beg pardon — are you the Impossible Things? The guild?" He thrust out a folded paper, sealed with the crest of the new Survey Office. "I'm meant to deliver this only to the keeper. Only — well." He hesitated, and lowered his voice, glancing at the lantern as if the news might be listening. "There's a thing happened out past the Steppe. At the old border stones. The survey team says—" he swallowed— "says the glass has started singing. And the words are ones nobody taught it."

Sebastian took the paper. Beside her, Ilse had gone very still, in the old way, the way of a woman reading forbidden files.

Under the Survey Office seal, in a clerk's careful hand, the report said only: *The Steppe is repeating a name. Come hear it.*
