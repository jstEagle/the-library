## 39. Anniversary, Revised

One year after the Unveiling, Vellmark's new capital woke up to the smell of frying dough and the sound of someone arguing about bunting.

Sebastian heard both from her kitchen window above what was now, officially, the Guild of Impossible Things, Reestablished — though the sign-painter had charged them extra for *Reestablished* because, he said, it was an unusual word for a Tuesday. She leaned out into the bright morning air and watched two of her guildmates conduct a small diplomatic crisis in the street below.

"It's crooked," said Marta, who had once donated her wedding memory to power the cellar map and now guarded every celebration as if it were a treaty signing.

"It is *festively* crooked," said Piotr, standing on his ladder with a hammer in one hand and a pastry in the other, which explained a great deal about the bunting.

"The left side is higher than the right."

"That's called *asymmetry*, Marta. It's artistic."

"We're a nation of former Echoes, defected Custodians, and guild clerks, Piotr. We don't know how to do art. We know how to do *inventory*."

Sebastian smiled and went back inside. Some things, she had learned, you did not fix. You simply made sure there were enough pastries for when the argument resolved itself, which it always did, usually over the pastries.

A year ago this city hadn't existed. A year ago it had been a flicker at dusk, a forgery with rent paid in dead currency, streets that failed to appear on morning walks. Now there was smoke from chimneys, and schoolchildren being drilled in a national anthem nobody had finished writing yet — the current draft ended mid-phrase because the committee couldn't agree whether the last rhyme should be "free" or "home," and so the children just sang the whole thing twice and shouted at the end, which everyone privately agreed was better anyway.

Today was the anniversary. The first anniversary of the Unveiling, when the veil had dissolved like sugar in warm water and the outside world had woken to find a country on its maps that wasn't there at breakfast.

There had been proposals, in those early weeks, to call the holiday something grander. *Liberation Day.* *The Day of Doors.* One enthusiastic councilman suggested *Vellmark Rises,* and had been gently informed by an eighty-year-old Echo named Halina that countries which needed to announce they'd risen generally hadn't. In the end they settled on the plainest possible name, because plainness had been the whole point: the Unveiling. As if a country were a piece of furniture someone had finally taken the sheet off of.

Which, Sebastian thought, pulling on her coat, was not entirely inaccurate.

* * *

She found Ilse at the market, exactly where Ilse always was on festival mornings: standing very still in front of a fruit stall while chaos eddied around her, evaluating apples with the same focused intensity she had once brought to auditing the integrity of veils.

"You've been looking at those pears for ten minutes," Sebastian said, coming up beside her.

"I'm assessing them."

"For what?"

"Structural soundness." Ilse picked one up, turned it over. Old habits. For six years she had examined everything in Vellmark for signs of imminent collapse, and even now, with the veil gone and the sky behaving normally, some part of her still checked the horizon for seams. "They're sound," she concluded, and bought four.

They walked together through the crowd, and Sebastian did what she did most mornings now, which was notice things. The lamplighter's daughter selling little paper doors on strings — the Door Out had become a motif; it was on the stamps, the coins, one deeply confusing municipal flag proposal. An old man teaching his grandson to skip stones at the canal, using the same underhand motion Sebastian's grandmother had once used on a river that had spent fifty years folded out of memory. Two former Custodians running a stall where, for a small fee, they would tell you what the weather had really been on any given day of the last half-century, since everyone's recollections had arrived back from the veil slightly shuffled, like books returned to the wrong shelves.

That was the thing about getting a country back all at once: everybody came home to a house where the furniture had been rearranged by strangers. Half the city spent the first months discovering that their childhoods had happened in different weather than they remembered. Grief clinics opened next door to bakeries. There was a certain national consensus that both were necessary.

"Oren sent a message," Sebastian said. "He wants us to stop by before we go up onto the Steppe. He says—" she unfolded the note, which was written in her old master's hand, still elegant, occasionally lucid, "—he says, quote, *tell Sebastian the seal is doing something odd again.*"

"Odd how?"

"He didn't say. He drew a picture instead." The picture, she showed Ilse, was of the founding seal, the brass disc Oren had pressed into her hands on the dawn he forgot her face. Underneath it, Oren had drawn what appeared to be the seal wearing a small hat.

Ilse studied this for a long moment. "Your mentor," she said, "was the greatest mind of the Guild of Impossible Things."

"He was."

"And he has drawn the seal a hat."

"He's earned the right," Sebastian said, pocketing the note. "Come on. Let's see what impossible thing it's done before lunch."

* * *

Oren lived now in a small room behind the new archive, surrounded by ledgers he could no longer fully read but insisted on dusting daily. When they arrived he was at his window, holding the seal up to the light.

"It hums," he announced, without turning around. "It didn't used to hum. I would remember a humming seal. Would I not remember a humming seal, Sebastian?"

"You might have filed it under something else."

"I have filed everything under something else. That is my entire condition." He handed it to her, and his eyes were clear today, one of his good days, and Sebastian felt the familiar ache-and-gratitude mixture she'd stopped fighting months ago. He remembered her now more often than not — the new country's air seemed to agree with him, or perhaps simply having work again did, real work, cataloguing what was kept instead of trading away what was lost. But the gaps remained, quiet rooms in him where whole years stood empty, and on good days he spoke of them the way other men spoke of old war wounds.

"What kind of humming?" Ilse asked.

"A contented one." Oren sat down heavily. "Like a kettle that has decided against boiling. And last night it grew warm. Not hot. Warm. Like a hand." He looked at Sebastian. "I signed an accord once because I thought I was ending a war. It turned out I was postponing one. I should like, in whatever time I have left, to never be surprised by that object again."

Sebastian turned the seal over in her hands. It looked the same as always: worn brass, the old guild mark, a century of thumbs. But when she held it to her ear — feeling faintly ridiculous, aware of Oren watching her with the gravity of a man passing on a diagnosis — there it was. A hum. Low, patient, almost pleased.

"Well," she said. "It's probably nothing."

"Nothing," Oren repeated, "is the word I used about a prophecy folio once. Look how that went."

* * *

By late afternoon they climbed out of the city and onto the Steppe.

This was the part of the day Sebastian protected most fiercely, the way some people protected church. The meadows had come in at the edges during the summer — grass first, tentative, then wildflowers, then, inexplicably, an orchard that three separate families claimed to have planted and none could produce receipts for. The glass showed through in patches further out, great pale sheets of it between the green, and walking there you saw the sky doubled beneath your feet, and sometimes, if you looked carefully, other things too. Memories, resting in the glass like coins at the bottom of a clear stream. Most people had learned not to stare. The memories weren't lost anymore; they were just *there*, part of the ground, the way water is part of a well.

Children loved it. Children always love the version of a place that shows you more than it should.

"They're saying next year they want to hold the festival actually out here," Ilse said as they walked. "Kessler's old parade route. Music, stalls, the whole thing."

"On the glass?"

"On the meadow. The safe edge. There's a committee."

"There's always a committee. Is it the same committee that can't finish the anthem?"

"The anthem committee has split into two committees. One of them is now writing a second anthem."

"Good," said Sebastian. "Every country needs at least two anthems so its citizens have something to argue about that isn't land."

Ilse laughed — still, after everything, a surprise every time, a sound Sebastian kept expecting the world to take back and it never did. They walked in the easy silence of people who had run across a hostile glass desert together and found ordinary conversation to be one of civilization's genuine triumphs.

Halfway out, they passed the memorial. There was only one, by deliberate choice, and it was small: a low stone wall with no names on it, just a single line in the old Vellmark script and the new trade-tongue both — *We were here the whole time.* People left things against it. Buttons. Letters. Once, famously, a wedding dress. Today someone had left a jar of honey and a note reading *for whoever comes back next.*

No one came back next. Everyone had already come back. But the note stayed, because hope, once it got into the habit of a place, was hard to evict.

* * *

The Door stood where it had always stood, at the heart of the Steppe — but oh, how it had changed.

During the first weeks after the collapse, people had come from all over the world to see it: a seam of light older than the country, the threshold that had swallowed an army's ambition and accepted a future over a past. Pilgrims, scholars, three separate documentary crews, and one extremely persistent philosopher who camped beside it for a month asking it questions. The Door answered none of them. The Door, in fact, gradually stopped being much of a Door at all.

Now it was just that: a doorway. A freestanding frame of pale stone — or what looked like stone; touching it felt like touching the moment before you speak — standing open onto ordinary air. Walk through it and you found yourself, anticlimactically, on the other side of it. The light had gone out of it slowly, over the seasons, the way a fire goes down to embers and then to ash that is only warm because the day is warm.

Sebastian had a theory about this, which she shared with anyone who stood still long enough: the Door had never been a door *to* anywhere. It had been a door *between* — between the veiled and the unveiled, the remembered and the forgotten, the country that was and the country that chose to be. With nothing left to separate, it simply stood there, open, doing the one thing doors do best when their work is done.

It let people through.

Today, as on most days, a few pilgrims lingered nearby. A young couple took turns stepping through it and laughing, as though testing whether it might, just once, refuse. An elderly Echo woman had set up a folding chair beside it and was knitting, occasionally patting the doorframe the way you'd pat a good horse. Sebastian recognized her distantly — one of Halina's friends, from the old quarter, one of the ones who had waited fifty years folded into the veil and come out of it determined to be unimpressed by everything, including miracles.

"Don't let the tourists tell you it's dying," the woman said, without looking up from her knitting. "It's resting. There's a difference. I rested for fifty years; I know the posture."

"Has anything come through?" Ilse asked. It was a routine question between them now — Ilse still checked, the way she still assessed pears for structural soundness. The world had ended once already, in a polite way, and neither of them entirely trusted it not to try again.

"Not through. But it hums sometimes." The woman glanced up, sharp-eyed. "You'd know that, though. Guild business."

Sebastian's hand had drifted, without her quite deciding it, to her coat pocket. To the seal.

Warm. Through the fabric, against her palm: warm, and humming that contented, kettle-deciding-against-it hum, louder here than it had been in Oren's room, louder than it had ever been.

She looked at the Door. The Door, being a doorway, looked back with magnificent indifference. Ordinary air moved through it. A bee investigated the doorframe and left, unsatisfied.

But under the hum of the wind and the click of knitting needles, Sebastian caught something else — faint, patient, unmistakable to anyone who had spent her life listening to objects that shouldn't make sound.

The Door was humming too. The same note.

"Sebastian?" Ilse had gone very still, in the old way, the customs-house way. "What is it?"

"I don't know yet." Sebastian drew out the seal and held it up. In the late light, the worn brass gleamed, and the hum rose to meet the Door's hum, two voices finding each other across a hundred years and one apocalypse, harmonizing like old friends at a festival. "But I think," she said slowly, "that something impossible is considering becoming possible again. And I think it wants to talk to the guild."

Behind them, the elderly knitter snorted. "Told you it was resting," she said. "Not dying. *Resting.*"

The sun went gold over the meadowed edges of the Steppe, and the paper doors on their strings fluttered all across the distant city, and the Door stood open onto ordinary air — humming.
