## 2. Ink That Remembers

The temple's archive breathed at night.

Paloma had learned its rhythms over nine years of indenture: the settling of the cedar shelves as the day's warmth drained out of them, the tick of the great ledger-room clock that no one wound but which never stopped, the whisper of vellum expanding in the damp that crept up from the river stone below. She knew the smell of it better than her own hands — tallow and iron-gall ink, the sweet rot of a thousand years of careful record-keeping. The temple kept everything. Births, deaths, debts, dreams entered into evidence during disputes. The valley had no magic, so the people had made memory their sorcery instead, and the temple was its tower.

She should not have been there after the evening bell. She was there anyway, with a sleeping child curled in the gap between the map cabinets where she'd hidden him an hour before the elders finished their audit, his breath slow and even against her rolled sleeve, which she'd given up as a pillow without ever quite deciding to.

Wren, he had written for her that afternoon. Not spoken — written, because his voice made the elders' oil lamps gutter and made Brother Aldous clutch his prayer-beads like a rosary of garlic. *Wren*, in a hand far too old for his small fingers, letters with flourishes that belonged to manuscripts three centuries dead.

*What are you?* she had asked him, tracing the word.

He had looked at her with those grey eyes — old eyes, patient eyes, eyes that had watched something end — and written back: *I am the question you were always going to ask.*

Now, by the light of one shuttered lamp, she worked.

The language would not leave her alone. That was the truth of it, though she kept circling the truth the way a tongue keeps finding the socket of a missing tooth. When Wren spoke — when he forgot himself and spoke, words spilling out of him like water finding a crack — she did not understand him. And yet she *almost* understood him, the way one almost remembers a dream upon waking: shapes of meaning dissolving even as she reached for them, a grammar felt in the ribs rather than heard in the ear. It frightened her more than the elders' fury had. Elders could be endured. This thing inside her own skull could not be explained away.

So she did what scribes do. She went to the records.

* * *

The pre-Conquest ledgers lived in the deepest gallery, behind a door locked with a key the current Keeper wore on a chain against her heart and pretended had been lost. Paloma had copied the key from wax two years ago, in the idle way scribes copy things, telling herself it was practice. Telling herself a great many things, it turned out.

The air down here was cold enough to see her breath. She set the lamp on a lectern and pulled the first ledger toward her — *Annals of the Valley, Volume the Fourth*, its cover scuffed to suede, its pages soft as cloth. The script inside was beautiful and half-illegible, a hand trained in some tradition the valley had since let fall out of its bones.

She began with the words she knew best. There were only fragments — sounds she'd caught from Wren that afternoon when he'd cried out in his sleep, thrashing against nothing: *vel-aneth. Korrin-dûn. Sha-vael.* She hunted them through page after page, index after index, and found mostly nothing, dust and tithes and the names of millers dead five hundred years.

Then, near midnight, in a marginal note beside an entry about a boundary dispute with the highland shepherds, she found *korrin-dûn.*

*Korrin-dûn: the speech of the Court Below. Spoken by none above since the Sealing. Let this valley remain deaf to it forever, for our grandmothers bought our silence dear, and silence is what keeps us.*

Paloma sat very still. In the cabinet-gap across the room, Wren slept on, unaware, or pretending to be.

*The Court Below.* Every child in the valley grew up on the phrase the way they grew up on bread — so familiar it carried no weight. Beneath the mountain, the grandmothers said, there is a court of nine, and it is hungry, and it cannot follow us here because our soil is dead to it. Dead soil, safe soil. The whole valley was founded on that arithmetic. No wonder nothing but wheat and sorrow grew in their fields. They had salted their own earth deliberately, generations ago, burned the charms out of it, drowned the standing stones in the reservoir — anything to make the ground worthless to the thing beneath the mountain. Safety through poverty. Exile as a wall.

She had always thought of the magic-dead valley as home. Now, reading that margin, she felt the floor tilt beneath the thought. Not home. *Refuge.* And refugees, she understood suddenly, with a cold spreading under her sternum, are people who fled something. Who were running from something still.

She turned pages faster now, hunting the Sealing, hunting the Court, hunting any mention of the language that lived half-formed in her blood. And an hour past midnight, in a ledger bound in black calf whose title page read simply *Obligations*, she found the contract.

* * *

It was folded into the binding itself, sewn in with the signatures — a single sheet of vellum that was not vellum, that had the faint warm give of skin and the pale gold shimmer of something grown rather than made. The ink on it was not brown or black. It moved. Slowly, the way a snail moves if you watch long enough, characters crawling a hair's width per hour toward the bottom of the page, where a line waited for them like a mouth.

The contract was in two tongues. One was korrin-dûn; she knew that now, knew it in her spine. The other was plain valley script, the formal hand used for deeds and dowries, and it read:

*Let it be entered in the rolls of the Ninefold Court, perpetual and irrevocable, that on this night of the new moon the undersigned does cede and convey one soul entire, together with all claims, names, and inheritances pertaining thereto, unto the Court beneath the mountain, in exchange for the term of thirteen moons, to be counted from this night, during which term the second party named herein shall be withheld from the Court's knowledge and keeping.*

Two names followed. The first, signed in that crawling golden ink, was a string of syllables she couldn't pronounce and didn't need to — the mark of the Court Below, its seal, its hunger given handwriting.

The second name was in ordinary iron-gall ink, in a hand she knew better than her own.

Her mother's.

Maren Thackeray. Maren Thackeray, who had died eleven years ago of the winter fever, who had taught Paloma her letters on a slate by the peat fire, who had smelled of soap and lamp-oil and had sung songs in no language anyone in the village recognized, songs Paloma had hummed all her life without knowing why. Maren Thackeray, whose daughter had spent nine years copying other people's debts in a temple because her family had nothing else to give.

One soul entire. Sold to the court beneath the mountain.

Paloma's first coherent thought was obscene in its smallness: *she could write.* Her mother had been a washerwoman, a widow, a woman the temple rolls listed as illiterate — and here was her signature, fluent and sure, with the same upward flick on the final *y* that Paloma herself put there every day of her working life. The realization arrived sideways and cut deep. Her mother had hidden this. Had hidden literacy, had hidden knowledge of the Court, had hidden — Paloma's eye dragged back up the clause — *the second party named herein.*

There was no second party named. The space where the name should have been was blank. Not erased — blank, as if the ink refused to hold it, as if whoever wrote the fair copy had left it empty on purpose, or as if the name itself had been taken somewhere else for safekeeping.

But Wren had appeared at the temple door during the debt-audit. Wren spoke the tongue of the Court Below. Wren had no soul, if the elders were right about the hollowness in him — and the elders, for once, were right about exactly the wrong amount of things.

Thirteen moons. Her finger found the phrase again and pressed on it. Thirteen moons, counted from the signing. She did the arithmetic the way she did everything, automatically, and then wished she hadn't. Eleven years ago was one hundred and thirty-two months. One hundred and forty-four months was twelve years. The term had ended two years ago.

The debt had come due, and the Court had come collecting, and somehow the collection had walked to her door wearing a child's body.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the archive's high slit windows, and Paloma discovered she had not moved in a long time. Her hand lay flat on the contract, on her mother's name, as though touch could make it make sense.

It wouldn't. Nothing about it would. Her mother — her quiet, tired, kind mother, who had mended other women's linens and never raised her voice — had stood before the Ninefold Court on the night of a new moon and sold a soul. Whose? A child's? Her own? The blank space where the name belonged gaped at her, and every answer she tried to fit into it hurt in a different place.

If it was Wren's soul — if her mother had sold a child to monsters — then the woman Paloma mourned for eleven years was a monster too, and the grief she carried like a second skeleton was built on a lie.

And if it wasn't —

If it wasn't, then her mother had bargained with the Court Below for thirteen moons of something, and the price had been a soul, and the blank name meant the Court was still owed, and debts in the valley did not die with the debtor. They passed down. Everyone knew that. It was the first law the temple taught its indentured children: *the ink remembers what the flesh forgives.*

Paloma looked across the dark room to the cabinet-gap where Wren lay watching her now, awake, his grey eyes steady and unbearably sad, as if he had known what she would find down here all along. As if he had walked a hundred miles to be found by exactly this woman, holding exactly this page.

"Who was it?" she whispered, and hated how her voice shook. "Whose soul did she sell?"

Wren slid out of the gap, small feet silent on the stone, and came to stand beside her at the lectern. He looked at the contract for a long moment. Then he took the stylus from her hand — gently, the way one takes a blade from someone thinking of using it on themselves — and wrote beneath her mother's signature, in his antique flourishing hand, four words:

*Read the blank again.*

She bent close. The blank space where the second party's name should have been was not blank after all. In the strengthening dawn light, faint as breath on glass, letters were surfacing in the vellum — rising slowly, the golden ink of the Court crawling backward now instead of forward, unwriting its concealment, letter by letter, as the thirteenth moon drew near.

The first letter was a *P*.

Paloma stepped back so fast her hip struck the lectern. The lamp rocked; shadows swung across the shelves like startled birds. Down in the valley the bells would soon ring for morning offices, and the elders would count their scribes, and find one missing, and ask questions she could no longer answer honestly.

Because the name surfacing in the contract was hers. It had always been hers. Her mother hadn't sold a soul to the Court Below.

She had sold her daughter's — and bought something with it that the golden ink was just beginning, letter by rising letter, to reveal.
