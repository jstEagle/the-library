## 3. The Debt-Bound Depart

The temple rolls hung in the Scriptorium like a skin flayed from the valley itself — forty feet of vellum, renewed each solstice, on which every indentured soul was written in iron-gall ink that could not be washed out. Paloma had copied names onto it for eleven years. She knew the weight of the pen required: firm enough to bite, light enough not to tear. A name entered was a name bound.

Her own sat in the third column, mid-scroll, in her mother's hand. *Paloma Thackeray, scribe, term of fourteen years.* Her mother had signed her into service two days before dying, and Paloma had never once wondered why the ink looked hurried, why the descenders of the letters dragged as if the pen had been moving faster than the hand that held it.

Now she wondered everything.

She stood before the rolls at dawn with the contract hidden against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth beneath her scribe's coat, and read the clause she had recited a hundred times to new arrivals without ever hearing it:

*Whosoever departs the bounds of the valley before satisfaction of term forfeits all holdings, all standing, and all record. The name shall be struck from the rolls and from all memory of the temple. The debtor shall go forth nameless, and no door of the faithful shall open to them.*

No record. No memory. In a valley whose soil drank magic and gave back nothing, whose only treasure was what its people remembered, erasure from the rolls was a kind of death — the slow kind, the kind where your body kept walking but every ledger, every letter, every carved lintel forgot you had ever leaned against it.

Paloma pressed her palm flat against the vellum, over her own name, and felt the dried ink's faint ridges like scar tissue.

Behind her, small and breath-held in the shadow of the map cabinets, Wren waited. He wore her spare cloak pinned twice at the throat. His eyes were fixed on the rolls with an expression she was learning to read: not fear exactly, but the wary attention of someone watching a trap being set who knows the trap is for him.

"It will be all right," she whispered, which was a lie, and he looked at her, and she saw that he knew it was a lie, and forgave it anyway. Children were merciless that way. They accepted lies the way they accepted weather.

* * *

She spent the day doing what she did best: making records agree.

Elder Cassory wanted the debt-audit ledgers continued through the week's end, and Paloma obliged, her pen steady, her columns true, while inside her chest something hammered like a bird against glass. She requested leave to visit the herb-terraces for her lungs — plausible; the mountain damp got into every scribe's chest by autumn. She requested a cart pass for the temple granary's quarterly exchange, and because she had written the request forms herself for three years, she wrote this one in the exact cadence Elder Voss approved without reading.

The forgery was perfect. That was the horror of it. Eleven years of obedience had made her fluent in the language of permission, and now she used that fluency to steal herself.

At dusk she went down to the grain stores. The exchange cart stood loaded under the eaves, sacks of barley and millet stacked to the rail, and old Harn's mule dozed in its traces. Harn himself was at his supper and would not miss the cart until morning, by which time it would be found empty at the south ford, mule grazing, a note of apology and full payment pinned to the seat. She would pay him back. Somehow, someday, when she had a name worth paying with.

"Up," she breathed to Wren, and lifted him into the hollow she'd made between two sacks near the center, deep enough that only a searching hand would find him. She packed barley around him like a nest. He lay down obediently, folding himself small the way children fold, knees to chin, and looked up at her through the gap between sacks.

His lips moved. That lost tongue slid out of him, soft as water over stones, and though she understood perhaps one word in ten, she understood this: *you will come back for me if they take me.*

"I'm not letting them take you," she said. "That's the whole point."

He said something else then, slower, watching her face, and she caught the shape of it — *the wardens smell* — and her stomach clenched, because yes. She had thought of nothing else since moonrise.

The valley's wardens were not soldiers, whatever their grey coats implied. They were sniffers. Magic, in a valley where magic could not live, announced itself the way rot announces itself in a clean room — a sweetness gone wrong, a thickness at the back of the throat. And Wren, soul sold to the Ninefold Court, was soaked through with the stuff, however mute and small and ordinary he looked. To a warden's nose he must be unbearable. To a warden's nose, the whole cart might as well be screaming.

She had prepared for it the only way a scribe could: with paper.

In her satchel were three sheets she had cut from spoiled audit pages and rubbed with ash from the incense burner in the Hall of Records — the one incense the temple imported from beyond the passes, resinous and bitter, burned precisely because its smoke was believed to *cleanse*. Superstition. But superstition was just knowledge nobody had tested lately, and if anything in the valley could drown the scent of the Court, it would be twenty years of that smoke ground into ash and scattered through the grain.

She worked it in handful by handful, layer over layer, until the cart smelled like a chapel after a funeral. Then she climbed onto the bench, took up the reins, and clicked her tongue at the mule.

The gates of the temple close at nightfall. The gate of the valley closes nowhere, because no one has ever needed it closed — the mountains themselves are the lock, and the single road south threads the gorge like a needle pulled through cloth.

The warden-post stood where the road narrowed above the river, a lantern, a brazier, two men in grey. Paloma had passed it a hundred times and been waved through a hundred times, a scribe in temple grey carrying temple papers. But tonight her hands were slick on the reins, and the lie in her pocket seemed to weigh as much as the child behind her, and every heartbeat sounded loud enough to wake the dead rolls themselves.

"Hold." The younger warden raised a hand. He was chewing something, unhurried. "Grain exchange isn't till tomorrow."

"Elder Voss moved it up," Paloma said. "River's rising. He wants the low ford crossed tonight." True, actually — the river had been rising for days. The best lies are load-bearing walls built into real houses.

The older warden came around to the cart's side, lantern raised, and Paloma's blood turned to river-water, cold and fast. He walked the length of it, unhurried, and she watched his face the way she watched a page for errors: the flicker at the nostrils, the slight narrowing of attention. He stopped. Leaned in. Breathed deep over the barley.

"What've you got under there?"

"Barley," Paloma said. "Millet. Incense ash from the Hall — we dust the stores against weevils." She let mild offense into her voice, the tone of a woman inconvenienced by competence questions. "Would you like to check the manifest?"

A pause long enough to count her own heartbeats: nine. Ten.

The older warden sneezed. Twice, violently, wiping his nose on his cuff. "Gods' teeth, that ash gets everywhere," he muttered, waving the cart on. "Go on, then. Mind the ford."

She drove past the lantern-light at a walk, at a walk, at a *walk*, because a fleeing cart gallops and a lawful cart walks, and only when the post was a smudge of orange behind her and the road bent west into blackness did she allow the mule a trot, and then her own breath came ragged, and she realized she was laughing — a thin, cracked, disbelieving sound, terror exhaling itself as joy.

* * *

The gorge swallowed the moon by degrees. Paloma drove through dark shot with river-sound, the wheels' rhythm a pulse in the stone, and somewhere in the third hour she stopped and climbed back and parted the sacks.

Wren looked up at her, barley in his hair, eyes enormous.

"You can come up," she said. "We're past them. Past all of them, probably."

He scrambled out and stood shivering on the cart bed, and when she wrapped her coat around his shoulders he leaned against her side like something starved for warmth finally allowing itself to be fed. Together they watched the valley fall away below — the temple's few lamps, the terraces, the smoke of hearths going grey into the fields. All of it shrinking. All of it becoming scenery.

It should have hurt more than it did. That frightened her most. Somewhere behind her ribs, where eleven years of belonging should have anchored her, there was only a strange, expanding lightness, as if she had been carrying the valley all this time and only now set it down and discovered how strong she was.

"I broke my name," she said aloud, to hear it in the open air. "You understand that? Whatever happens — I can't go back and still be anyone. The rolls won't have me. My own—" she almost said *my mother's grave*, and stopped, because the grief arrived late and sideways, as it had done since she paid Odile— since— 

She frowned. There was a blank in her evening, a smooth place where something had been worn away. She had wept recently; her eyes still stung with it. She could not remember why.

Wren tugged her sleeve. Pointed at her face, at the wet on her cheeks, and said one word of the old tongue, gentle, questioning.

"I don't know," she told him honestly. "I don't remember what I lost. Isn't that the trick of it? They take a thing and take your knowing it was taken, too."

Ahead, far down the gorge where the road dropped toward the lowlands and the caravan roads beyond, a scatter of fires glimmered — travelers, merchants, maybe worse. The broker-camps began past the border stones; she'd heard the stories her whole life, told to frighten children: people who bought and sold the invisible cargo, memories and years and names.

Names. She touched the thought carefully, like a tooth that might be loose.

If the Court beneath the mountain dealt in souls, and brokers dealt in everything souls were made of — then somewhere on those roads was a currency she could spend. And somewhere ahead, a price already taken that she couldn't even grieve properly.

Wren's small hand found hers in the dark and gripped it.

"Thirteen moons," she said. "One's already turning. Hold on to me, and don't let go even when I tell you to."

Below them the fires multiplied, and the first wind off the lowlands carried a scent she had never smelled in the magic-dead valley — green, wild, alive, and threaded underneath with something sweet gone wrong, like rot in a clean room.
