## 4. Road of Thirteen Moons

### Paloma

The valley let them go the way a fist lets go of sand — grudgingly, grain by grain, and only because it had no choice.

Paloma walked behind the grain cart with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, and every step away from the temple felt like tearing a stitch from living cloth. The indenture-ink she'd broken still sat beneath her skin. She could feel it cooling in her forearms, going grey and dead like a fire left untended, and with its dying went everything it had anchored: her bed in the scriptorium, her ration-marks, her place on the rolls of the memory-temple where her name had been written since she was six years old.

*Paloma Thackeray, scribe, third voice.*

Not anymore. Somewhere behind her, an elder was already striking the line through.

Ahead, the cart rattled. Beneath the sacking between the sacks of barley, Wren made no sound at all — he had learned silence the way other children learned speech, thoroughly and early — but she could feel him there, a small gravity at the center of the cart's noise. She had smuggled a soul out of the valley in a barley cart. Her mother's daughter. The thought arrived uninvited and bitter as green fruit.

The wardens at the valley gate were two brothers named Hask and Ferrow, and they smelled magic the way other men smelled rot: deep in the sinuses, with a kind of dread. Paloma had spent three days before leaving rubbing the cart down with tallow and ash and crushed wormwood, burying Wren's scent under honest filth, and it nearly hadn't mattered. As they passed, Hask lifted his head like a hound catching something on the wind, and his nostrils flared, and for one long breath Paloma stood in the road with her heart beating in her teeth.

"Barley," he said at last, frowning. "Smells like barley."

"It is barley," said Paloma.

Ferrow waved them through. And that was all — that was the whole of her great escape, four words and a wave, and she walked out of the only world she had ever known feeling cheated of her own drama.

By evening they were in the hill country, where the road climbed through stands of birch gone gold and bare, and the soil under her boots was no longer the magic-dead earth of home but something else, something *awake*. She felt it the way you feel a change in weather: a pressure, a listening. Twice she saw lights off the road that were not lanterns. Neither time did they approach.

She stopped at a stream to water the mule and pulled back the sacking.

Wren looked up at her with those eyes too old for his face, and touched his own chest, then pointed east — toward the mountain that had begun to show itself above the ridgelines, blue-grey and snow-crowned, wearing its perpetual ring of cloud like a crown it resented.

"I know," Paloma said. "I know where we're going."

He shook his head. Tapped his chest again. Then, slowly, he drew shapes in the dirt beside the cart wheel: a circle, and inside it a smaller circle, and inside that, nothing at all. He looked at the empty middle for a long moment. Then he wiped it out with his palm, hard, as if he were killing it.

Paloma crouched beside him. "That's what they took," she said quietly. "Isn't it. Your — " She almost said *soul*, and couldn't, not yet, not aloud, as if saying it would make the ledger true in some way that couldn't be undone. But it was already true. It had been true since the night she found a contract bearing her mother's signature, one soul sold to the court beneath the mountain, and understood that the debt was hers to pay.

Wren watched her face while she thought. He was very good at watching faces; she suspected he had once been very good at many things, before someone hollowed him out and left only the watching.

"We'll get it back," she said. "Thirteen turns of the moon. That's how long we have. That's what the contract says." She heard her own voice steady itself, scribe-calm, reciting terms. "We have eleven left."

The child reached out and took her hand. His fingers were cold. He held on the way small children hold on — without any negotiation about it, as if holding on were simply what hands were for — and Paloma, who had been alone in the world for exactly one day, discovered that she could not remember the last time anyone had taken her hand without asking something first.

They walked on toward the mountain.

### Wren

*(He remembers in pieces. He does not yet know which pieces are his.)*

There was a door once, tall as grief, and it opened inward onto music.

He remembered the music most. Nine instruments, or maybe nine voices, or maybe nine things that were both, playing a chord that never resolved because resolution would have meant choosing, and the Court did not choose. He had stood in that doorway — he had been small enough that the doorframe dwarfed him — and something vast and masked had leaned down to look at him, and he had felt himself being *read* like a page.

Then cold. Then the long dark of being carried, folded up small, passed hand to hand in the dark like contraband. Then a woman's face, tear-streaked, lit by a candle-end, whispering words over him in the lost tongue — words he now knew were a bargain, though whose bargain he could not have said. Then a valley where the ground drank magic and gave back nothing, and years of silence, and a door, and a scribe with ink-stained fingers who half-understood him when no one else could even hear him.

He remembered the moon counting. That was the strangest part — he had always known, somewhere under the silence, that the moon was counting something, ticking down like a patient clock, and now he knew what it counted. Thirteen turns. A statute of limitations written in the sky. When the thirteenth moon closed its circle, whatever claim remained on him would lapse, and the Court would erase the remainder rather than honor it. He didn't know how he knew this. He simply knew it, the way you know falling.

Now he rode in a barley cart toward the thing that had eaten him, and the strange part — the part he turned over and over like a stone in his pocket — was that he wasn't only afraid. Some small stubborn seed at the bottom of his hollow self wanted to see the Court again. Wanted to stand in that tall doorway and be *seen*, fully, by the ones who had discarded him. Wanted them to look at what they had thrown away and reckon with it.

He didn't have the words for any of this. He had very few words at all. But when Paloma talked to him — and she talked constantly, filling the road's silence with observations about the weather and the mule and the state of the barley — he began, carefully, to answer. Not in the old tongue; he saved that for important things. In gestures. In expressions. Once, in a laugh, rough and rusty as an unused hinge, that startled them both so badly the mule stopped walking.

At night he dreamed in fragments. A throne of woven antlers. A mask with no face behind it. A woman's hands signing a page — and here the dream always snagged, because sometimes the hands were his mother's and sometimes they were Paloma's, and he woke with his heart slamming, unable to say which crime he was dreaming of, or whether they were the same crime.

On the sixth night he woke Paloma by touching her shoulder, urgent, and drew in the dirt by firelight: the tall door, the nine shapes, and then — after a long hesitation — a stick figure with a crown.

Then he scrubbed it out again, fast, terrified, and would not meet her eyes.

### Paloma

The caravan found them on the ninth morning, or perhaps they found the caravan; on that stretch of road the distinction blurred.

It came up out of the mist like a rumor: forty wagons hung with bells that rang notes no bell should ring, drawn by oxen whose eyes were the flat reflective white of coins. The traders wore layered robes and smiles calibrated to the inch, and their wagons stank — there was no other word — of something sweet and wrong underneath the incense, like honey poured over a wound.

Paloma knew what they were before anyone said it. Every valley child knew the stories. *Soul-brokers.* Traders in the intangible. They bought your luck, your first love, the memory of your father's voice; they sold courage, talent, the ability to weep. Their prices were always fair and always ruinous.

Their mistress reined up beside Paloma's cart: a broad woman in a coat of midnight-blue silk, iron-grey hair braided with rings, a face built for laughing and currently doing so.

"Well," the woman said, looking at Paloma, then at the cart, then — longer, more carefully — at the space just to the left of Paloma's shoulder, where Wren's head peeked from the sacking. Something flickered across her face, quick as a fish. "Well, well. A runaway scribe and a cargo of barley. And where are two such honest pilgrims walking, this fine cold morning?"

"To the mountain," Paloma said. There was no point lying; she could smell the trade coming off these people like smoke.

"Mm. Everyone walks to the mountain eventually." The woman swung down from her saddle without waiting for invitation, which seemed to be her manner entirely. "Odile," she said, extending a hand heavy with rings. "Caravan-master. Purveyor of intangibles. And you are — "

"No one."

Odile's eyebrows rose. She glanced again at that space near Paloma's shoulder, and whatever she saw there made her smile widen into something with real edges. "No one. Traveling with no one's child, toward no one's mountain, carrying — if my nose serves me, and it does, it is the finest nose in three provinces — something that belongs to the Ninefold Court."

Paloma's hand closed around the little knife in her sleeve. "I don't know what you mean."

"Of course not." Odile examined her nails. "Then you won't be interested to hear that the mountain road passes through territory where my caravan has right-of-way, and where lone travelers have a way of arriving at the gate already emptied. Hollow-eyed. Walking past their own names." She looked up, and her eyes were suddenly, completely serious, all the laughter gone like a lamp snuffed. "Or you won't be interested to hear that I know the toll-takers at the Court's gate personally, having paid them more times than I can count, and that a broker traveling with a party is worth ten travelers alone."

"And the price?"

"Now *that*," said Odile, delighted, "is the voice of someone who has read a contract in her life. The price is negotiable. The price is always negotiable. Walk with us, feed yourselves at our fires, keep your cargo hidden from the things in the passes, and pay me when we reach the gate. One payment. Something small." Her smile returned, softer now, which somehow made it worse. "A memory. Nothing you'd miss. Everyone has memories to spare."

Beside Paloma, silent, Wren had gone rigid. He stared at Odile with an expression she had never seen on him before — not fear exactly. Recognition. And beneath it, faintly, hatred.

Paloma looked at the child. At the mountain, closer now, its cloud-ring visible even by day. At the road ahead, empty and full of things she could not see.

Eleven turns of the moon. She could not cross those passes alone. They both knew it, and she hated that they both knew it, and she hated most of all that Odile knew they both knew it.

"All right," Paloma said. "We walk with you."

"Splendid!" Odile clapped her hands, and the bells on forty wagons rang a chord that did not resolve. "One rule, scribe. One rule only, and it keeps everyone honest." She leaned close, and the smell of honey-over-wound was strong, and beneath it Paloma caught something older, something like the air of a sealed room. "Whatever you carry — don't tell me what it is. The moment I know, I'm obliged to report it. The moment I don't know, I'm free to forget I ever guessed." Her eyes flicked, briefly, to the cart. "Do you understand me?"

Paloma understood. It was the most honest thing anyone had said to her since she'd left the valley: *my kindness is a lie I am willing to maintain, if you will help me maintain it.*

"I understand," she said.

That night they camped within the caravan's circle of wagons, under a sky salted with stars, and Paloma lay awake listening to the brokers' fires and the bells and Wren's slow breathing against her side. Eleven moons. One memory, promised, like a coin set aside. She told herself it would be a small one. A taste. The color of a wall. The name of a street.

Across the fire, Odile sat sharpening a knife that reflected no light at all, and watching them, and smiling her calibrated smile.

And in the pass above the camp, where the clouds moved wrong, something with too many joints unfolded itself against the stars and looked down at the caravan — at the cart, at the sacking, at the hollow child sleeping beside the scribe who owed the Court everything — and began, patiently, to count.
