## 5. The Broker's Honest Lie

Odile Vasse had been trading in souls for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned one truth above all others: everyone lies about what they're willing to pay. The young ones lie loudest. They come to her fire with their chins up and their eyes wet and they say *anything, take anything* — and the moment her scales come out, the moment the silver pan begins to tremble with the weight of a memory lifted from behind the eyes, they flinch like burned children.

So she watched Paloma Thackeray across the caravan fire for two days before she made her offer.

The scribe sat apart from the others, as she had since the wagons rolled out of the low country. She sat with her spine straight the way people do when they've been taught posture by institutions rather than by mothers, and she kept the mute boy close enough that his shoulder touched her sleeve. Wren — Odile had pried the name out of him on the second night, not with questions but with a dried apricot held out on an open palm, the way you'd coax anything wild and starved — Wren slept against her side now, breathing in the small, even rhythm of a child who had learned somewhere, badly, how to be afraid.

Odile turned her tin cup slowly over the coals. The tea inside was bitter as regret and twice as useful. Around her, the caravan settled into its night noises: the groan of axles cooling, the mutter of the soul-brokers counting their wares in whispers, the cough of old Marek who carried, so it was said, forty years of somebody else's wedding days in a locket around his neck and could no longer remember his own.

"You're staring at my cargo," Paloma said, without looking up.

"My apologies." Odile did not stop staring. "I'm staring at my passenger."

"I paid for passage."

"You paid in coin." Odile smiled and let the smile carry all its professional warmth, which was none at all. "Coin gets you to the mountain, scribe. Coin does not get you under it."

That brought the girl's head up. Good. Odile liked them attentive. Inattentive clients ended up weeping in ditches or, worse, bargaining badly at the gate, and bad bargains at the Ninefold Court had a way of rolling downhill through entire caravans like spoiled milk through cloth.

"Explain," Paloma said.

Odile set down her cup and folded her hands, which was as much ceremony as the matter deserved. "You've heard what the Court takes at its gate. Everyone has heard some version. Some say blood. Some say firstborns. Some say a song you loved as a child." She shook her head. "The truth is duller and crueler. The Court deals in one currency only: *proof*. Proof that you are someone. Proof that you have something worth taking. A name is proof. A memory is proof. Walk up to that gate holding nothing they can weigh, and they'll turn you away — or worse, they'll let you in because you're already empty, and empty things amuse them until they break."

"And you know this how?"

"Thirty-one years, girl. I've made the descent eleven times. I've come back nine." Odile let that arithmetic sit in the firelight. Two of her colleagues were still under the mountain, in whatever sense the Court permitted things to persist. "The gate-wardens will accept a payment before you ever reach the Court proper. One memory. Not your choosing, mind you — theirs. They taste it first, the way a moneylender bites a coin. If it rings true, if it carries real weight, real love or real loss, the gate opens. If it doesn't—" She shrugged elaborately. "There's a reason I came back nine times instead of eleven."

Paloma's hand had moved, without her seeming to notice, to rest on the sleeping boy's hair. Odile noted it the way she noted everything: as inventory.

"What kind of memory?" the scribe asked quietly.

"The kind you don't want to give. That's the whole trick of it. The Court can smell a cheap offering the way you'd smell watered wine. It must be a day that built you. A day you'd bleed for." Odile leaned forward, and the fire painted her face the color of old brass. "And I'll tell you something else, for free, because unlike most brokers I believe an honest client survives longer than a cheated one: whatever you pay at the gate, you will not feel it leave. Not then. The cut is clean. It's after — walking around with a hole shaped like a day where a day used to be — that's when the price comes due."

* * *

They argued, of course. Or rather, Paloma argued and Odile listened with the patience of a woman who had heard every objection ever raised by every debtor since the invention of debt.

"It's theft," Paloma said, pacing the length of the wagon in the gray hour before dawn. "It's robbery dressed in ritual."

"It's commerce," Odile said, mending a harness strap with her teeth and her awl. "Everything is. Your temple taught you otherwise because temples deal in the one commodity they pretend isn't one — certainty. Ask yourself, scribe: what did your valley sell you when it sold you safety? What did your indenture cost? You've been paying in years since you were old enough to hold a stylus."

"That's different."

"Everything's different until it's yours being weighed." Odile bit the thread off and looked up. "Here is my honest lie, and you may quote it: I will get you to the gate alive, unrobbed, unbargained-with by anyone but the wardens themselves. I'll teach you how they test a memory, how to breathe while they drink it out of you, how to stand afterward. That much is true, every word." She threaded the strap through its buckle. "The lie is in what I won't tell you — that no one comes back from beneath the mountain whole, no matter what they pay. But you knew that already, didn't you? You read contracts for a living. You know every honest document contains a clause meant to be missed."

Something flickered across the scribe's face — the look of a woman recognizing her own trade in a stranger's mouth.

"When do we reach the gate?" Paloma asked.

"Three nights, if the weather holds. Four if the moon argues." Odile jerked her chin toward the narrow slit of sky visible between the wagon's canvas ribs, where the moon hung fat and pale and a little less than full. "Thirteen turns, they told you. You're burning them faster than you think."

* * *

On the third night, the road climbed into country where the trees grew white-barked and leafless, and the air began to smell faintly of iron and cold stone. The wardens' territory. Even the horses went quiet here, ears flattened, and the caravan's chatter died to murmurs. Odile called an early halt in the lee of a ridge and built no fire. There was no point. On the slope ahead, half-hidden by the bone-colored trees, the gate was already visible: a seam of darkness in the mountainside, too regular to be natural, framed in stone carved with script older than any alphabet Paloma Thackeray would have known.

Wren saw it and made a sound Odile had never heard from him before — not a word, not a cry. A low, continuous note, like a struck bowl, and every hair on Odile's arms rose, because the note harmonized with something in the rock itself. The mountain hummed back.

"Hush now," Odile said softly, kneeling to the boy's level. And then, because she was feeling either charitable or fatalistic, she added: "Whatever they took from you, little heir-of-nothing, tonight someone starts paying it back. Try to look grateful."

He looked at her with eyes far too old for his face, and Odile had the sudden, unwelcome certainty that he understood every word she said and always had.

* * *

They walked to the gate at midnight, as required. Odile, Paloma, and the boy, leaving the caravan behind with strict instructions to Marek that involved several threats and one bribe. The seam in the rock stood open now, though no one had opened it — a corridor of dark going down and down, lit by no lamp, and standing within it, three shapes that might once have been men.

The wardens wore robes the color of wet ash and masks of smooth grey stone with no features but mouths. The mouths did not move when they spoke; the words simply arrived, the way cold arrives.

"*What do you bring?*"

"A traveler bound for the Court," Odile said, bowing exactly the correct depth. "She seeks restitution of a debt. One soul, lawfully sold, thirteen turns of the moon remaining."

"*All travelers pay the gate.*" The middle warden's stone mouth curved, or seemed to. "*Weigh her.*"

Odile turned to Paloma. Up close, in the lightless light of that place, the scribe looked younger than her years and older than her lifetime, and her hands — those careful, ink-stained hands — were steady. Odile would remember that steadiness later with something dangerously close to respect.

"They'll choose," Odile murmured. "Don't fight the pull. Fighting makes it tear."

Paloma nodded once.

The nearest warden drifted closer, and where its face should have been there was only that carved mouth, opening now, exhaling a breath that smelled of deep wells and winter. Something invisible slid into Paloma — Odile had watched it happen ten times and still could not describe it better — a hook slipping past skin and bone into the softer architecture beneath. Paloma gasped. Her eyes widened, and Odile watched the memories surface in them like fish rising in a dark pond: glimpses, flashes, whole days offered up and turned over by the thing that tasted them.

The warden considered.

Then it chose.

What left Paloma left gently, the way a splinter leaves a wound when the flesh has softened around it. One breath she was full of herself; the next there was a room missing somewhere in the house of her, a door bricked over so smoothly you'd never know it had opened onto anything. She swayed. Odile caught her elbow — broker's reflex, nothing warmer — and held her upright while the wardens withdrew back into the dark, satisfied, the gate standing wide.

"*The debt-taster finds it rich,*" said the mouth. "*Enter. The Court receives at moonrise.*"

They stood a moment in the threshold's cold draft, the three of them. Wren had taken Paloma's hand and was pressing it, hard, against his own small chest, as though trying to give her something back through the palm.

"How much did it take?" Paloma whispered. Her voice sounded scraped. "What did I lose?"

Odile had seen this question a hundred times, and she gave the only honest answer there was: "A day. You'll have to find out which one by its absence."

They descended. The stair went down farther than any stair had a right to, and the dark was warm, almost kind, and somewhere below, music was playing — strings, slow and sweet, the sound of a party no one had invited you to.

Paloma stopped once on the stairs. Stopped dead, mid-step, and pressed both hands over her mouth, and tears ran through her fingers in silence.

"Wren?" Odile said. "What's wrong with her?"

But the boy wasn't looking at the scribe. He was looking down into the dark, toward the music, and his face had gone terrible and still.

"She's crying," Odile persisted. "Why is she crying?"

Paloma lowered her hands. Her cheeks were wet, her breath ragged — and her eyes, when Odile met them, were perfectly, horribly blank.

"I don't know," Paloma said. "I don't know why I'm crying. There's no reason. There's nothing—" She touched her own wet cheek and stared at the moisture on her fingertips as if it belonged to a stranger. "Why am I crying?"

Odile Vasse had traded thirty-one years in grief, and she had never once gotten used to this: the body remembering what the mind has sold. She said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a lie, and she had promised the girl only one of those.

The music swelled below them, patient as water wearing down stone, and the thirteenth moon's shadow lengthened behind.
