## 6. Beneath the Mountain, Above the Law

The road down was not a road. It was a throat.

Paloma felt it swallow them by degrees — first the caravan's lanterns dimming to embers, then the sound of Odile's wheels changing pitch as stone closed overhead, and last her own heartbeat, which seemed suddenly too loud for a place that had never heard one before. The walls of the descent were veined with something that glowed a slow, bruised gold, like light remembered rather than made. Wren pressed his palm against it once, and the vein brightened beneath his fingers, and Odile said, without turning around, "Don't do that again."

"Why?" Paloma asked.

"Because it knows you here," the broker said, "and knowing is how this place takes its toll."

They descended for what felt like two days and might have been two hours; time below the mountain moved like water through cloth, unevenly, pooling in odd places. Paloma marked the passage instead by the thirteen knots she had tied into her sash at the surface, one moon per knot, and touched each one as they went deeper, a rosary of arithmetic. Twelve knots would remain when they reached the Court. She intended to spend none of them foolishly.

Then the throat opened, and there it was.

The Ninefold Court did not rise so much as *unfold* — nine terraces carved into the mountain's hollow heart, descending in widening rings like the inside of a bell, every ledge hung with gardens that grew in colors Paloma had no names for. Above, where a sky should have been, there was only a slow-turning vault of stone set with lights that wheeled like constellations but arranged themselves in patterns no valley astrologer had ever charted. Water ran somewhere out of sight, endlessly, the way it ran in the memory-temple's fountains — and Paloma realized with a small cold shock that the temple's architects had copied this place without ever having seen it, the way a dreamer copies a room from a childhood they were told never happened.

"They've been waiting for you," Odile said, and for the first time since the grain cart, the broker sounded tired. "They always wait. That's the courtesy of it."

* * *

The sovereigns received them on the fourth terrace, beneath an archway grown entirely of pale wood that had been shaped — not carved, *grown* — into the likeness of hands clasped over a book.

There were nine of them, seated in a crescent upon thrones of black glass, and each wore a mask, and each mask was different, and each mask was wrong in a way Paloma could not stop noticing. The first was porcelain, smooth as an egg. The second was stitched leather, mouthless. The third was mirrored and showed her, when she looked at it, not herself but the temple kitchen at dusk, the kettle going cold on the hook. The fourth mask wept continuously, silver tears that vanished before they struck the throne. The fifth was simply a rectangle of absolute dark. The sixth, seventh, and eighth she could not look at directly at all; her eyes slid off them like rain off oiled cloth, leaving behind only headaches shaped like half-remembered arguments.

The ninth mask was blank. Not empty — *blank*, the way fresh paper is blank, the way an unwritten page waits.

"Paloma Thackeray," said the porcelain mask, in a voice like a bell rung underwater. "Scribe of the Third Indenture. Daughter of Maren Thackeray, who signed in our rolls with her left hand because her right was broken." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "We keep excellent records."

"I know," Paloma said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I'm told I'm good at records myself."

A sound moved through the crescent — not laughter exactly, but the place where laughter would live if the Court permitted such a thing. The mirrored mask tilted toward her.

"You understand us already," it said. "That is unusual. Visitors usually require interpreters, or madness, whichever arrives first."

"She's been reading your ledgers," Odile said from behind them. "Old ones. Stolen ones." And though Paloma had paid the broker honestly — a day of grief, still unaccounted for, a hollow in her chest she kept prodding like a missing tooth — she understood that Odile sold information the way other merchants sold salt: freely, to everyone, at all times.

"The child," said the weeping mask, and all nine faces turned to Wren.

He stood very straight. He had done that more and more since the caravan — straightened, widened, as though something were being poured back into him drop by drop with every mile beneath the earth. But now his hand found Paloma's sleeve, and gripped it, and she felt how cold his fingers were.

"He doesn't speak your language," she said.

"He speaks ours," said the dark rectangular mask, gently. "It is everything else he cannot speak."

And then they began — softly, courteously, one after another, like guests at a dinner taking turns — to tell her story back to her.

* * *

It was the most terrible thing the Court could have done, and Paloma understood, even as it happened, that it was done with perfect kindness. That was the horror of velvet courtesy. It gave you nothing to strike at.

The porcelain mask spoke first: *"She was born in the flood year. The river took the east fields and her father's name along with them, and after that her mother wrote everything down, because writing was the only thing the water couldn't eat."*

The stitched-leather mask continued: *"At six she fell from the temple wall chasing a paper blown loose, and broke her wrist, and did not cry until the elders praised her for being brave — because she understood, even then, that praise was a kind of debt."*

The mirrored mask: *"At nineteen she translated a funerary verse wrong on purpose, so that a widow would be told her husband waited for her, rather than that he had gone on alone. She has never confessed it. She thinks of it sometimes on staircases."*

Paloma's face burned. "Stop."

"We are only claiming what is ours to claim," said the weeping mask, tears falling and vanishing, falling and vanishing. "Every visitor's story comes to us in pieces. We hold the pieces. We are merely... introducing ourselves to yours."

*"She hides the child in the ledger-room,"* said the fifth mask, whose voice came from everywhere and nowhere, *"behind the shelf of tax rolls from the dead barony, because no one reads the tax rolls from the dead barony. She gives him her supper and tells the kitchen she is fasting for penance. She does not know yet why she lies for him. She will learn tonight, from us, and wish she hadn't."*

"Tell me," Paloma said. Her voice cracked down the middle. "Tell me about my mother."

* * *

The ninth mask rose from its throne.

Up close it was worse than blank — it was patient. It regarded her for a long moment, and Paloma had the sensation of being read, thoroughly, the way she read manuscripts: cover, spine, marginalia, the stains on the last page.

"Maren Thackeray came to us seventeen years ago," it said. Its voice was quiet, and quiet in this hall carried further than shouting. "Not as a seller. As a supplicant. You should know that word. Supplicant. It means one who begs."

"My mother didn't beg anyone."

"She knelt where you are standing," the ninth mask said, "for six hours. We counted. We count everything. She begged us to take something of hers — anything — and give her time. Time was the one commodity we trade that is never listed in any ledger your temple owns."

Wren made a small sound. Paloma looked down at him and saw that he had gone white, and that his lips were moving, shaping words in the lost tongue of the Ninefold Court — and this time she caught enough of it to feel her stomach turn over. *He remembers,* the shape of it said. *He remembers kneeling.*

"She brought him to the gate of the mountain wrapped in a shawl," the ninth mask went on, mercilessly gentle, "a foundling, mute, perhaps three years old — though age meant little to what he was, and less to us. She knew what he was. Do you?"

"No," Paloma whispered.

"Neither did she, precisely. She knew only that things were coming for him — hunters out of the old war, creditors of a crown that no longer exists, men who smell magic the way your wardens smell rot. She could not fight them. She could not hide him forever in a valley where the ground itself is dead to magic — though she chose that valley for exactly that reason, you understand. Your home is not barren, scribe. Your home is a locked door. She built the lock around her whole life."

The masked head tilted.

"But locks fail. So she came to us, and she begged, and we offered her the only bargain within our law. We would take the child's soul into our keeping — hidden in the vault of names, untouchable by hunter, creditor, or king. Soulless, he would be nothing. Nothing is the safest thing to be. And when the danger had passed —"

"When?" Paloma seized on it. "You said when. When the danger passed, you'd give it back?"

The ninth mask was silent long enough for the water-sound in the deep to become deafening.

"That was the shape of her prayer," it said at last. "We agreed to hold. Holding is not returning. Those are different words in our law, and your mother — who was a woman of records, like you — did not read the fine print, because we showed her none. We showed her mercy instead, and mercy is always the finer print."

"What did she pay?"

And here the Court did something Paloma had never heard a creditor do. The eight masks bowed their heads, slightly, in unison, as if in respect.

"She offered her own soul," the ninth mask said. "We refused it — it was already promised elsewhere, by older contracts than yours or mine. So she offered what remained. She traded us her daughter's future. Her daughter's name in the world's great ledger — her standing, her fortune, her prospects, the life that might have been hers had the valley's luck ever turned kind. All of it, commuted into time. Seventeen years of hiding-time for a mute child, purchased at the price of everything Paloma Thackeray might otherwise have become."

The words went into Paloma like cold water into a burn, and for a moment she could not tell whether they hurt or healed.

"That's why the indenture," she said slowly. "Why we were poor. Why the temple owned my apprenticeship. Why mother worked herself —" She stopped. The whole architecture of her childhood rearranged itself around her, beam by beam, and it had been standing on this foundation all along: her mother's tired hands, the thin winters, the elders' casual contempt for the Thackeray woman and her scribbling girl. Every hardship of her life had been a payment. She had lived inside her mother's sacrifice the way a snail lives in its shell, and had called the weight of it *the way things are.*

"She let you hate the poverty," said the weeping mask, softly. "It was kinder than letting you hate the truth."

"And she died," Paloma said, and reached for the memory and found — smoke, a bed, someone's hand, weeping with no reason attached, a funeral like a scene watched through frosted glass. A hollow. Odile's price, taken at the gate above. She almost laughed. "She died, and I can't even remember it properly. I paid that away to get here."

"Yes," said the ninth mask. "Grief travels poorly. We're sorry for it. We are, incidentally, sincere — sincerity is another thing people don't expect of us."

"Then give me the fine print," Paloma said. She stepped forward, out of the shadow of the clasped-hands archway, into the full strange light of the turning stone sky. "All of it. The contract. My mother's signature. Whatever clause says holding isn't returning. Because I'm a scribe, and I don't sign anything I haven't read, and neither —" she put her hand on Wren's shoulder, and felt him straighten under her palm — "does he."

For the first time, the ninth mask moved as if surprised.

"You want to see the vault," it said.

"I want to see the ledger," said Paloma. "Every court keeps one. Even yours."

The silence stretched — and then, terrace by terrace, the lights of the Ninefold Court dimmed and shifted, and the eighth mask, which she had never been able to look at, finally resolved into view: a face of open pages, inked edge to edge, and every line of it was turning, even now, to find hers.

"The moon turns twelve days hence," the ninth mask said. "On the thirteenth turn, the vault opens for audit. Until then, honored scribe — consider yourself our guest."

From the Court, she would learn later, there was no higher warning.
