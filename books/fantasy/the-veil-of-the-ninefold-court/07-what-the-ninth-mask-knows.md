## 7. What the Ninth Mask Knows

The Court did not sleep. Wren had learned that in the first night beneath the mountain, lying on a bed of woven silver-grass that hummed like a struck bell whenever he closed his eyes. Sleep was a thing the Court tolerated in its guests the way a spider tolerates the trembling of its web — with interest.

So when the ninth sovereign came to him, Wren was awake. He was always awake now. The closer they drew to the thirteenth turn of the moon, the more his skull filled with light, and light does not let you dream; it only lets you remember.

*Come,* said the voice, not in words but in the old tongue, the one that had lived in his mouth before he knew what mouths were for. *Walk with me.*

The corridors of the Ninefold Court were made of listening stone. Every footstep echoed twice — once behind him, once ahead, as though the mountain could not decide whether he was arriving or leaving. Torches burned without smoke, their flames bent sideways, all leaning toward the vaults below as if straining to hear the names stored there. Wren walked barefoot because shoes felt like lies here. His feet remembered this floor. That was the terrible part. Not his mind — his feet, his spine, the small bones of his inner ear. His body had been born in this place, even if his soul had been sold out of it before he could walk.

The ninth sovereign waited at the edge of the Mirror Garden, where nothing grew except reflections. Pale stems rose from black water, and each stem bore a blossom that showed a face: faces of the dead, faces of the unborn, faces of people who had never existed at all but might have, if some bargain elsewhere had gone differently.

The sovereign wore the mask all nine wore — smooth, featureless, moon-colored — but the ninth's mask was older. Cracked at the temple, faintly, like glaze on a cup that has survived too many hands. And through that crack, Wren saw something move. Not an eye. A memory. It looked at him the way memories look at you from inside your own head.

*You know why I have come,* the sovereign said.

Wren did not answer. He had spent most of his life unable to speak; silence was a country he owned outright. But the sovereign waited, patient as sediment, and at last Wren said, in Paloma's language, because it felt safer: "You want to look at me."

*I want to show you to yourself.* A pale hand extended, palm up. *There is little time. The moon turns tomorrow.*

He should have refused. Paloma would have told him to refuse — no, that wasn't true. Paloma would have taken the hand herself, asked three sharp questions first, and written down every word afterward in her beautiful cramped script. Thinking of her steadied him. She was somewhere above them in the guest galleries, probably not sleeping either, probably counting her remaining memories like coins she couldn't stop spending. She had given up the day her mother died so that he could walk through a gate. He could look into a bowl of water.

He touched the sovereign's palm.

The garden went dark. Not the darkness of absence but the darkness of a held breath — and then the Mirror Garden bloomed with images, all of them him.

A woman in the ninth mask, unmasked, laughing in a chamber lit by nine moons' worth of candles. Her hair was the color of wet slate. She held a newborn against her chest, and the newborn's cry rang in the listening stone and every corridor in the mountain leaned inward to hear it.

*My son,* she had said, in the old tongue. *Heir of the veiled throne.*

The visions came faster, tumbling like water over stones. A cradle of silver-grass. Nine masked figures bowing over it — eight bows sincere, one shallow, one grudging. A council chamber where the masks whispered in corners. *A child of both bloods cannot sit the veiled throne. The law is older than we are.* A night of argument. A night of fear. And then — and Wren felt his own small hands go cold inside the vision — a night of ink.

His mother, writing a contract by candlelight. Not selling him. Selling his *soul* — severing it, folding it into a vessel of blown glass, and carrying it down, down to the vault of names, where it was filed under a false designation among ten thousand other stolen things. Hiding her son's soul in the one place in the world no one would ever think to search: the Court's own archive. Because the law that barred a soulless child from the throne also protected him from everything else the throne required. Assassins needed a soul to find. Oaths needed a soul to bind. Prophecies needed a soul to read.

She had not sold him. She had buried him alive in the safest grave in creation.

And then she had carried the empty shell of him up through the mountain, out into the sunlit valley where magic could not follow, and left him at the door of a memory-temple, mute, because what use were words to a boy with nothing inside him to do the speaking?

The vision ended. Wren found himself kneeling in cold water among the mirror-blossoms, and every blossom around him had turned to show his face — dozens of them, hundreds, all watching him with his own eyes.

*Now you know,* the ninth sovereign said softly. *I am the last who remembers it truly. The others believe you died. I let them believe it. I have kept your mother's crack in my mask all these years so that someone, someday, could see through it.*

"Then she's dead," Wren said. His voice sounded strange to him, doubled, half in each language. "The eighth mask. It was her."

*The Court unmakes its sovereigns slowly,* the ninth said. *She sits among us still, but what sits there is mostly law now. Mostly mask. What remains of her loves you the way a ruin loves the shape of what it was.*

Wren stood. The water slid off him without wetting his clothes; the garden did not dare.

Above them, muffled by miles of listening stone, a bell began to toll. Slow. Enormous. Each stroke rolled through the mountain like the turning of a vast page.

*The twelfth turn,* the sovereign said. *Tomorrow, the thirteenth. When the cycle completes, the Court performs its Great Audit. All debts called in. All records balanced. All loose souls returned to the ledger or erased from it entirely.* The cracked mask tilted. *Your soul is in that ledger, child. So is hers — the scribe's. She has been paying tolls, spending herself piece by piece to reach you, and every coin she spends is recorded. By tomorrow's turn, the Court will know exactly what she is doing. And the law will do what law does.*

"Which is?"

*Erase what it cannot contain.* For the first time, something almost human moved through the old voice — weariness, deep as geology. *I have watched this Court erase children before. I swore after your mother that I never would again. But I am one mask among nine, and my cracks are showing.*

Wren looked at the blossoms. They had turned away from him now, all those borrowed faces, ashamed or afraid. In the nearest blossom he caught a reflection that wasn't a reflection: a thin woman with ink-stained fingers and a stubborn mouth, asleep sitting up in a gallery far above, her hand curled protectively around a ledger she'd stolen or bargained for or both.

Paloma. Who had crossed a wardens' line for him. Who had given up the day her mother died. Who thought she was rescuing a stranger's child from a debt she never owed — when all along the debt had run backward, from this mountain to her door, from his mother's pen to her mother's signature. Two mothers. One contract between them that neither of their daughters had ever been meant to read.

"What happens to her at the Audit?" he asked.

*What happens to anyone the ledger cannot balance. She will become a correction. There will be no record that a record was removed. Even I will forget her, if the vote goes as it must.* The ninth sovereign folded both hands, and the gesture was so precisely his mother's — the same way of holding grief like something breakable — that Wren's chest hurt. *Unless.*

"Unless what?"

*The vault of names opens once per cycle, at the final hour before the turn. During the masquerade of debts, when every sovereign attends and every guard is a dancer. Your friend has already guessed this; she has the look of a thief deciding.* The mask leaned close, close enough that Wren could smell the crack in it — old candle smoke, milk, something like lullabies. *But hear me, heir-of-nothing, son-of-the-ruin. If you take back your soul, the law will wake. You will be visible to every oath and prophecy and assassin in the Ninefold Court, and the throne will know its claimant lives. To save yourself, you must be willing to be found.*

"And if I don't? If I stay empty?"

*Then the Audit erases you both quietly, and the mountain keeps its peace another thousand years, and no one ever has to choose anything. That is always the other offer. Peace. It is astonishing how often it works.*

Wren stood in the Mirror Garden a long moment while the twelfth stroke of the bell faded through the stone. He thought about being empty. Empty had been safe. Empty had been eleven years of watching the valley through a window, wanting nothing because wanting required a self to want with. He had been a well-kept grave, and the valley had kept him kindly.

But Paloma hadn't come down here for a grave.

"I want to be found," he said.

The words came out whole, in the old tongue, in her tongue, in both at once, and the listening stone carried them down through every corridor of the mountain, and somewhere far below, in a vault full of sleeping names, Wren would have sworn — later, if there was a later — that he heard one of them stir.

The ninth sovereign straightened. Through the crack in the ancient mask, the memory that was not an eye seemed to smile.

*Then listen quickly,* it said, *because tomorrow the moon turns, the vault opens, and the Court begins to hunt.*

Far above, the twelfth bell fell silent. And in the pause after it, Wren heard — faint, frantic, unmistakable — the sound of a door opening where no guest was permitted to go.
