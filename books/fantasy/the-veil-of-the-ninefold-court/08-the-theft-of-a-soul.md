## 8. The Theft of a Soul

The masquerade of debts began at moonset, when the Court's candles burned blue and the sovereigns came down from their thrones to walk among the debtors like shepherds among sheep they intended to shear.

Paloma had been in the Ninefold Court for eleven turns of the moon. She had learned its grammar: that courtesy was a cage, that every gift was an invoice, that when a masked sovereign smiled at you across a banquet of pale fruits, it was counting what remained inside your skull. The Court did not take by force. Force was for kingdoms above ground. Here, everything was given freely, and everything given was owed.

Tonight the great hall had been transformed into a ledger made flesh. Banners of vellum hung from the vaulted dark, each inscribed with names, and debtors drifted between them in masks of their own devising — because at the masquerade, the only face you were permitted to wear was one that wasn't yours. Paloma wore a mask of white clay painted with a single black seam down the center, as if her face had been cracked and poorly mended. It felt honest.

Wren stood beside her in a mask shaped like a sleeping moth. He could not wear his own face here; the ninth sovereign's spies would know it on sight. His hand was cold in hers. Eleven turns ago he had spoken only in the lost tongue. Now, in the deep places where the old language pooled like groundwater, he spoke it fluently, and sometimes — when the Court's attention slid elsewhere — he told her things. What the vault of names looked like. Which corridors remembered footsteps. Where the soul-vessels slept.

"The vault opens during the third dance," he had said that morning, in the whisper-voice of a child learning to want things. "When all nine masks are watching the floor. That's when debts are *felt* most keenly. They'll be listening to the music instead of the doors."

"And you'll be at the banquet, where they can see you."

"Where they can see *a* boy in a moth mask." His fingers had tightened around hers. "Don't let them make you trade your name tonight, Paloma. Promise me. Nothing below the last."

She had promised. It was the first lie she had ever told him.

* * *

The first dance was a waltz of creditors. Sovereigns in masks of bone and brass and riverstone moved through the crowd, pausing before debtors, tilting their heads, listening to the sound memories make when they are about to be spent. Paloma kept to the edges, where the candlelight failed and the vellum banners hung close enough to read. She read them the way she had once read ledgers in the temple — quickly, hungrily, hunting for one signature among thousands.

She found her mother's name on the fourth banner. *Maren Thackeray. One soul, held in trust against the heir's majority.* Held in trust. Not sold. Not stolen. Her mother had signed herself into servitude of a different kind — the kind where the payment was deferred, and the deferral itself was the crime, and the interest accrued not in coin but in years of a child's life. Twelve years Wren had wandered mute through a valley that smelled no magic, carrying his soul in someone else's keeping.

The music swelled toward the second dance. Paloma pressed her palm flat against the banner, over her mother's signature, and felt the ink pulse once, like a heart remembering fear.

*Toll,* said a voice at her shoulder.

The second sovereign stood there in its mask of green riverstone, robes the color of pond water. It did not touch her. It never needed to.

*You have been reading the rolls, little scribe. Reading is a debt. The vault remembers who lingers.*

"I'm a debtor already," Paloma said. "I came to pay."

*Then pay.* It gestured, and a small table appeared beside them as though it had always been there: a dish of grey wax, a stylus of black glass. On the Court's terms, memory was withdrawn the way blood was drawn — written out of you, pressed into wax, sealed.

Paloma thought of Wren's promise-extracting eyes. *Nothing below the last.*

"Take the valley," she said.

The stylus moved before she finished choosing. It always knew. It wrote *Ashford Vale* in letters that gleamed wet and then dried to nothing, and the world went quiet in a specific place behind her eyes. She still knew what a valley was. She still knew valleys had streams and orchards and bells. But when she reached for the particular one — the crooked lane, the millwheel's groan, the smell of bread from the temple kitchens — she found a smooth blankness, like a page scraped clean for reuse.

She had grown up there. She could prove it by the ache, though the ache had no address anymore.

*The toll is accepted,* said the second sovereign, and glided away into the dance.

Paloma gripped the table's edge until her knuckles whitened, and walked toward the vault.

* * *

The corridor to the vault of names remembered footsteps. That was the horror of it: the stones repeated the pressure of everyone who had ever walked there, so that Paloma descended through a ghost-crowd of pressures, centuries of debtors, each stone faintly warm where a foot had fallen before hers. The walls were lined with niches, and in the niches sat vessels — some clay, some glass, some things that were neither, shapes that hurt to look at directly, humming at the frequency of a held breath.

Wren's vessel was in the deepest gallery. She knew it before she saw it, the way you know your own name being called from another room. A small jar of smoked amber, stoppered with wax that bore two signatures: Maren Thackeray's, and beneath it, older and stranger, a mark like a crown turned upside down.

She lifted it. Warmth moved through her palms — not heat but *presence*, a fluttering like a bird adjusting its wings in a too-small space. Twelve years in this jar. Twelve years of a child walking through the world hollow, speaking a language his body remembered but his self had been locked away from.

Behind her, a niche sighed open.

*That one is not yours to carry,* said the fifth sovereign. Its mask was porcelain, featureless except for lips, and the lips were smiling.

"It's not yours either," Paloma said. "It's his."

*Possession is a story we permit,* said the fifth. *You may keep what you carry, if you pay the gallery's toll. Each vessel leaves a shadow of itself behind, you understand. Balance must be kept. The vault abhors an absence.*

"How much?"

The porcelain lips parted. *Everything you have left that is not your name.*

So Paloma paid in installments, standing in the humming dark with the amber jar against her chest.

Her teacher first — Brother Aldous, and his dry voice reciting conjugations, the way chalk dust settled in the creases of his knuckles, the single afternoon he had told her she wrote beautifully and she had carried those three words like coins for twenty years. Gone. She felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave: shock, then a strange clean socket, then nothing. She knew she had loved him. She could no longer remember why, or how, or what his voice sounded like when it said her work was beautiful.

Then her village — whatever was left of it after the first toll. The temple's bell schedule, the warden with the laugh like a dog barking backward, the exact color of evening on the eastern hills. The stylus found these without her pointing, writing them off her like debts from a ledger, and each time the wax took more, and each time she stood straighter, because the straighter she stood the less the sovereigns leaned in to watch her sway.

Last of all, at the mouth of the gallery, the toll-taker paused.

*Your face,* it said, almost gently. *Your own face. Few offer it willingly.*

"Take it," Paloma said.

She did not feel it leave. That was the worst part — no tearing, no wound. She simply became a woman who owned a mirror but had never met its occupant. When she caught her reflection in the polished lid of a nearby niche, she saw a stranger with tired eyes and ink-stained fingers, and felt the mild curiosity one feels passing a window at night.

But the jar was warm against her ribs. And warmth, she was discovering, was the only currency the Court could not mint or counterfeit or tax. Whatever else they stripped from her, they could not make her unlove the weight of that small vessel. Love without its reasons was still love. It beat in her like a borrowed heart.

*The toll is paid in full,* said the porcelain lips, and the gallery exhaled.

* * *

Odile was waiting at the gate.

Of course she was. Paloma had known since the caravan crossed the mountain's threshold that the caravan-master's honesty had a price tag sewn into its lining, and now the woman stood beneath the gate's iron arch with her arms folded and six of the Court's wardens fanned out behind her like cards from a cheat's sleeve.

"You have something that belongs to the house," Odile said. Her voice was flat as a dealer showing the deck. "I sold your passage, scribe. I didn't sell you the right to walk out with the heir's soul under your cloak."

"Odile—"

"Don't." Something flickered across the broker's weathered face, quick as a fish under ice. "Thirteen turns I've run this road. Do you know what the Court does to brokers who shortchange it? They don't kill us. They *edit* us. There are caravan-masters walking the high passes right now who don't remember their own children, because they once let a debtor slip the toll."

The wardens shifted. Their masks had no eye-holes. They didn't need any; magic poured off the amber jar like rot off meat, and every warden's head turned toward Paloma's chest as one.

"Give it here," Odile said, holding out her hand, "and maybe I can bargain you down to a few good memories instead of all of them. It's mercy, girl. The best deal you're going to get tonight."

Paloma looked at the outstretched hand. She thought about the first night on the caravan, when Odile had caught Wren talking in his sleep in the old tongue and had not reported it — had sat up all night by the fire instead, sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, watching the boy like a woman watching a door she'd once failed to hold shut. She thought about how a person could spend thirty years selling souls and still flinch, somewhere deep, every time one changed hands.

"No," Paloma said.

The word came out steady. She was surprised by it. She was a woman with no village, no teacher, no face, standing in front of certain ruin with a jar of somebody's soul hugged to her breast like a nursing infant — and the word came out steady anyway, because it turned out that obedience had been the first thing she'd actually sold at the Court's gate, back on the first night, though no stylus had recorded it.

Odile's hand hung in the air between them. The wardens took one step forward.

And then Odile laughed — a short, ugly, wonderful sound, like a hinge that hadn't oiled in years.

"Gods damn it," she said. "Twelve years ago I ran a delivery down into this pit. A woman with ink-stained hands and a baby wrapped against the winter. She paid me everything she had to get him *out*, and I took it, and I've hated myself in installments ever since." Her eyes went to the jar. "Maren Thackeray's daughter. Of course. You have her stubborn chin. I'd tell you that to your face if you knew which one it was."

She turned to the wardens.

"These two are under my bond," she announced, loud enough for the gate-stones to carry it. "Caravan law. Any hand laid on my bonded passengers answers to the Broker's Guild, and the Guild answers to no court beneath any mountain." It was a bluff woven from half-truths and guild law stretched to screaming — and the wardens hesitated, because even the Ninefold Court moved carefully around contracts, and Odile delivered the bluff the way she delivered everything: as though the paperwork already existed.

They passed under the arch together, Odile's knife-hand steady, Paloma's arms locked around the jar.

At the top of the stair, where the false moonlight gave way to true starlight leaking down from the mountain's throat, Odile stopped her one last time.

"They'll know by morning," she said quietly. "The masquerade ends at the thirteenth turn, and the ninth sovereign counts its vessels like a miser counts coins. You have until moonset tomorrow. Run for the heart of the Court, girl — the one place a thief can't be followed, because it's where all the debts come due." She grimaced. "And pray you've got something left to pay with."

Paloma touched the jar. Inside, faint as a moth against glass, something fluttered.

She had her name. Only her name.

And tomorrow, the Court would come to collect it.
