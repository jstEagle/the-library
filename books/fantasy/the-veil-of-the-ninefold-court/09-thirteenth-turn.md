## 9. Thirteenth Turn

*Paloma*

The heart of the Court was a room with no walls she could name. Nine thrones of black glass stood in a ring around a pool of standing water so still it held their reflections like a second Court beneath the world — a drowned Court, an inverted one, where the masks floated face-down and the faces beneath were visible.

Above them, through a shaft cut in the living rock, the moon showed her last sliver of light. The twelfth turn had ended at dusk. The thirteenth had begun.

Wren's hand was small and cold in hers. They had run from the masquerade hall through corridors that breathed — actual breath, slow and tidal, the mountain inhaling them deeper — and now there was nowhere left to run. The vault of names lay behind them, its shelves emptied of one vessel, one small clay ampulla still warm from her grip, tucked inside Wren's coat against his chest like a second heartbeat.

The sovereigns had not chased them. That was the worst part. They had simply *arranged themselves*, the way frost arranges itself on a window, and now all nine stood between Paloma and the only door, their masks catching the moonlight: bone, iron, salt, ash, amber, glass, moss, rust, and the ninth — the ninth mask was blank, smooth as an egg, and it frightened her more than all the others together.

"You have stolen from the vault of names," said the mask of iron, and its voice rang like a bell struck underwater.

"Return what is not yours," said the mask of salt, "and we will consider mercy."

Paloma laughed. It came out cracked, half-mad, and she did not try to soften it. "Mercy. From the Court that sells children's souls to pay its own debts." She lifted her chin. "I've read your ledgers. All nine of you keep accounts. And I know how accounts are settled here — I've paid enough on the road down to understand the arithmetic." Her hand tightened on Wren's. "So let us settle mine."

The blank mask tilted, slow as a turning season.

"The vessel is stolen," said the mask of ash. "The thief must be unmade."

"Or," Paloma said, and her heart slammed once, hard, like a fist on a door, "the debt is transferred. That's how it works, isn't it? A soul sold can be bought back. A price paid can be repaid. You accepted memories at every gate — my mother's death-day, my village, my teacher, my own face. Memories are fragments. But a name—" She made herself say it. Made herself mean it. "A name is the whole account. Every record I appear in. Every ledger line. Every memory anyone holds of me, in this world or under it. Take it. Take all of it. And give him his soul entire."

Silence, then — the terrible velvet silence of the Court weighing a thing.

"Paloma," Wren whispered. His voice broke on the second syllable, because he knew — he had carried her name inside his stolen soul for years, the way a seed carries the shape of the tree it never became. "No. Please. There has to be another—"

"There isn't," she said gently. "And even if there were, love, we're out of moons."

The moonlight in the shaft above them thinned. Somewhere far above, the thirteenth turn was completing itself, and she could feel it like water rising around her ankles — time running out for him, the Court's patience ending, the erasure that would take *both* of them gathering at the edges of the room like a tide.

"Do you understand," said the ninth sovereign, and its voice was neither male nor female nor old nor young but simply *final*, "what you offer? Not death. Death leaves bones, and bones remember. This is quieter than death. Your mother will not have borne you. Your teachers will not have taught you. The ink will dry blank where your name was written. No one will mourn you, because no one will know there is anything to mourn." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "You will walk out of this mountain into a world that has no place for you. Is that the bargain?"

Paloma thought of the temple garden. Of Brother Anselm's cough in the scriptorium, of the smell of fresh bread from the warden's kitchen, of her mother's hands — the hands she had finally understood, in the throne room, when the truth had turned her betrayal inside out like a glove. Her mother had not sold a child. Her mother had bought one time, with the only coin she had left: her daughter's future.

*I'm paying it forward*, Paloma thought wildly. *Mama. Watch me.*

"Yes," she said.

"Say the terms," said the ninth mask.

She knelt, drawing Wren down beside her, and spoke clearly, the way she'd been trained to speak contracts since she was six years old, ink on her fingers, the law of the ledger in her blood:

"I, Paloma Thackeray, scribe of the valley temple, indentured and indenturing no more, surrender my name entire — my place in every record ever written, above the ground and beneath it, past and present and yet-to-come. In exchange, the soul of the child called Wren is returned to him whole and free, and neither he nor any part of him remains owed, bound, or claimable by the Ninefold Court, forever."

The pool of water shivered.

"It is heard," said the nine masks, together, in a voice like the closing of a very large book.

* * *

*Wren*

He tried to hold onto her. That was the truth he would carry longest, after everything else went soft: he grabbed her sleeve, both fists, the rough wool of it, and he pulled, and he said her name over and over — *Paloma, Paloma, Paloma* — as if saying it fast enough could stitch it into the world before the world unpicked it.

The Court began to collect.

It came out of her gently, which was somehow worse than if it had torn. Light rose off her skin like heat-shimmer, like breath in winter, and where it passed, things changed. He watched her face — the face she'd already traded at the toll-gates, the one she'd won back for the final walk just so he could see it one more time — begin to smooth, to blur, to become a kind face without a name attached, a stranger's kindness.

"No," he said. "Take mine instead. Take anything. I don't want it if it costs her—"

"The bargain is spoken," said the mask of glass softly. "Little heir. You should know better than anyone: spoken bargains bind even kings."

And then — because the ninth sovereign had said *forever*, and the Court kept its ledgers perfectly — the light changed direction.

It came *to* him.

It came up through the floor of the world, through the roots of the mountain, and it found the warm clay vessel against his chest, and it opened it the way dawn opens a window, and what poured out was himself. All of himself. The piece that had been sold before he could walk, the piece that had lived shut up in the dark vault among ten thousand names, the piece that had known, all these mute years, exactly what it was missing.

It poured into him like a river returning to a channel carved for it long ago.

He remembered everything at once, and it was too much, and it was wonderful. He remembered the Court from the inside — nursery-thrones, masked tutors, a mother-queen whose mask had been pearl. He remembered the night they took his soul out through his mouth while he slept, and sealed it, and wrote *exiled* beside his cradle-line. He remembered being set outside a temple door in a valley where magic couldn't follow, because someone had argued — argued with sovereigns — that a child deserved to grow up somewhere safe even if he never knew why.

Her mother. Her mother had done that.

And he remembered, newest of all, brightest, a woman with ink-stained fingers kneeling in the dark of the grain cart, whispering, *hold on, little one, I've got you.*

The moon completed its turn. The shaft of light above went silver, then white, then dark.

When it came back, the woman holding his hand was no one.

* * *

*Paloma*

There was a moment — she would not be able to remember it later, and later she would not be able to remember that she couldn't, and that absence would ache in her like a tooth gone suddenly quiet — there was a moment when she felt herself being folded closed, like a letter, like a book returned to a shelf in a library that had burned.

She looked down at the boy. She didn't know his name anymore. She wasn't sure she'd ever known it. But her hands were holding his hands, and they were cold, and someone ought to get him somewhere warm.

"Come along," she heard herself say, in a voice that belonged to nobody in particular. "It's late. Someone should see you home."

They walked out of the heart of the Court through nine bowing shadows, and the door behind them closed like an eye.

Above the mountain, the night was enormous and indifferent and beautiful. Snow lay blue under the new-risen moon. The woman who had been a scribe stood blinking in it, empty as a clean page, and did not know why her cheeks were wet, or why the wetness felt like the only true thing left in her.

The boy took her hand. He was crying too, though she couldn't imagine why; he seemed like a nice child, whoever he was.

"Don't cry," she told him kindly. "We'll figure something out."

He looked up at her with an expression she had no word for — grief and triumph wearing each other's clothes — and he opened his mouth, and the first word of the rest of everything fell out of him like a stone dropped into still water:

"*Paloma.*"

The name hung in the cold air between them.

Somewhere far below the snow, in a vault of names, in a drowned inverted court, a single ledger line refused to stay blank.

She frowned. "What did you call me?"
