## 10. The Name Given Back

The valley had not changed, and that was the cruelest thing about it.

Wren stood at the edge of the barley fields where the road narrowed to a footpath, and the mountains rose behind him like a held breath. The air here was thick with the smell of wet earth and woodsmoke and the particular sweetness of temple incense drifting down from the terraces — a smell he had known once, long ago, in a life that had happened to someone else. The soil of the valley was famously dead to magic. Nothing enchanted grew here; nothing enchanted could follow him home. He had counted on that. He had not counted on how it would feel to walk back into a place that had forgotten him before the Court ever touched his name.

He could speak now. That was the strangest mercy of all. For years his throat had been a locked door, and now words came out of him whole and plain, in the valley tongue, in the court tongue, in any tongue he pleased. He had spent the first day of the journey home saying *bread* and *river* and *cold* just to hear them exist. But there was one word he did not say aloud. One word he kept folded inside himself like a coal wrapped in wool.

*Paloma.*

It lived beneath his ribs, in the place where his soul had sat hollow for so many years. When the sovereigns had unmade her from every record — every ledger line, every carved initial on a temple bench, every memory in every mind from the mountain to the sea — the word had remained, because it had been carried out of their reach inside him, inside the vessel they had returned. They could erase a woman. They could not erase what she had hidden in the thing she saved.

He climbed the path to the temple as the sun went low and orange behind the western ridge. Wardens nodded to him without recognition — he was only a boy in a travel-stained coat, thin from the road, his eyes too old for his face. One of them frowned slightly, sniffing at the air near him the way they sniffed for magic, but the valley's dead soil had done its work; whatever residue the Court had left in him had bled away mile by mile, and by the time he passed through the gate he smelled of nothing but dust and sweat and barley.

The garden lay below the eastern cloister, terraced into the hillside: beds of sage and feverfew, rows of beans climbing twine, a fig tree older than anyone living. And there — kneeling in the dark loam between the rosemary and the wall — a woman with ink stains still ghosting her fingers, though no one would have known why anymore. She wore the plain gray smock of a temple laborer. Her hair had come loose from its braid and hung across her cheek, and she pushed it back with the back of her wrist, leaving a smear of earth along her jaw.

She did not look up when he stopped at the low stone wall.

"Can I help you?" she said, in the tone of someone who helped everyone, who belonged to everyone, who belonged to no one in particular because belonging required a name and hers had been paid away. "We're closing the beds for the night."

Wren's throat closed around the word again. He gripped the wall. The stone was warm from the day.

She glanced up then, and he saw it happen — saw the polite blankness in her face rearrange itself into something else, something troubled. Not recognition. There was nothing left in her to recognize him with. But the body remembers what the soul has been robbed of. Her hands stilled in the soil. Her breath caught, small and involuntary, the way a person flinches at a sound from a dream.

"Do I—" She laughed, uncertain, and pressed her dirtied palm against her sternum. "Forgive me. You remind me of... I don't know. Of nothing. That sounds mad." She looked at her own hand as though it had betrayed her. "There's a hole, sometimes. In the middle of the day. Like a tooth gone from my mouth and my tongue keeps finding the gap. Do you know that feeling?"

"Yes," Wren said. His voice cracked on it.

Her eyes widened. Not at the words — at the voice itself, perhaps, or at the way hearing it made her press harder against her chest. "You speak like someone I— no. No one. I'm sorry. Everyone says I'm sorry lately, and I don't know what for."

"You do," Wren said gently. "You just don't know to whom."

He climbed over the wall and crossed the garden path and knelt in the dirt beside her among the rosemary, and up close he could see the cost written plainly on her: faint shadows under her eyes that had no cause she could name, a restlessness in her hands, the way her gaze kept sliding toward the temple library as if pulled by a hook. Thirteen times she had traded pieces of herself on that road beneath the mountain. A village. A teacher. Her own face. And last of all, everything — every record, every memory, every syllable of who she was. She had walked out of the Court's heart a stranger wearing her own body.

But she had walked out. That was the part the sovereigns had never understood, huddled in their masks above their vaults of names. They thought erasure was an ending. They had never been a child alone in the dark with nothing but one word to keep him company. They had never learned that a soul given freely cannot be wholly taken, because giving is a form of keeping.

"Wren," he said, touching his own chest. "My name is Wren."

"Hello, Wren." She smiled, and it was a stranger's smile, kind and guarded. "I'd tell you mine, but—" She spread her empty hands. "They're still deciding, I think. The elders gave me work until I come up with one. Sister Maren calls me Sparrow, since I'm always in the garden. It doesn't fit. I keep telling her it doesn't fit, and I don't know why I care."

"It doesn't fit," Wren agreed. "Because you already have a name."

"I don't."

"You do. It wasn't lost." He reached into his shirt and drew out the cord that hung around his neck, and on the cord, the small clay token the ninth sovereign had pressed into his hand at the gate of the Court — fired earth, valley earth, dead soil, the one substance in all the Ninefold realms that magic could not touch and therefore could not unmake. Scratched into it, in a scribe's careful hand, were six letters.

Paloma had made it herself, months ago, before she understood what it was for. She had told him once, laughing in a caravan wagon: *A scribe's habit. If a thing matters, write it down somewhere magic can't reach.* She had thought she was making him a keepsake. All along she had been building an ark.

"She wrote it down," Wren said. "Before the moon turned. Before they took it. She hid it in the one place they couldn't follow — in the debt they owed *me*. Everything she surrendered, she surrendered twice: once to them, and once to this." He opened his hand and showed her the token lying on his palm, ordinary as a river pebble.

The woman went very still.

The evening breeze moved through the garden. Somewhere above them a bell rang the hour, and neither of them moved. She stared at the scratched letters the way a starving woman stares at bread — and then, slowly, terribly, she began to shake her head.

"That's not—" Her voice frayed. "I don't know that word. I don't know it, and yet I can *feel* it, it's like — it's like a room in a house I've never entered, and I know where the furniture is. There's a desk. There's ink. There's a window looking east toward the mountain, and someone taught me letters at that desk, and she had flour on her hands, and she—" A sob broke out of her, sudden and shocking, and she clapped her hand over her mouth as if she could push it back. "Why am I crying? Why do I *know* about the flour?"

"Because she loved you," Wren said. "Both of them did. Your mother sold a future to buy a child time, and the child grew up and spent everything he had to give you back yourself. That's the shape of it. That's the whole story." He took her hand — the one with ink ghosts on the fingers — and pressed the token into it, and folded her fingers over it one by one, deliberately, the way one seals a letter. "And stories, properly told, can be unmade. But debts can't. She left you a debt. Hold it."

Her fingers curled around the clay. And the change began — not lightning, not miracle, but something slower and more human, like dawn coming up behind a mountain: first gray, then gold. The shadows under her eyes seemed to lift. The restless hands went quiet. She breathed in, deep, and the breath caught halfway and stayed there, suspended, while behind her eyes something vast and long-blocked began, drop by drop, to flow.

"A door," she whispered. "There was a door, and a knock, and rain, and a child who wouldn't speak. Ledgers. Ink that remembered. My indenture — I *broke* my indenture, I left in a grain cart, the wardens— oh, oh no, Odile, the caravan, the honest lie—" Her eyes flew open, wet and blazing, and for one moment the woman looking out of them was entirely, unmistakably herself: the quiet defiance, the terrible stubbornness, the love that had walked into a masked court and refused to leave without what it came for. "*Wren.*"

"There she is," he said, and his own tears fell at last, the ones he had been carrying since the gate of the Court, since the thirteenth turn, since a woman had dissolved from every record in creation with her hand still raised in farewell. "Hello, Paloma."

She said the name after him — her own name, tasting it, weeping over it, laughing through it — and each time she spoke it, more came back. The temple. The elders. Sister Maren and the stolen bread. The day her mother died, surfacing now like a body rising in clear water, grief arriving whole with its cause finally attached. She gasped at that one, doubled over among the rosemary, and Wren put his arms around her and held on while thirteen turns of the moon poured home into her all at once.

When she finally lifted her head, the sun had gone and the first stars stood over the eastern ridge, over the mountain, over the sealed and sleeping Court.

"They'll know," she said hoarsely. "Sooner or later, they'll check their ledgers and find the vessel empty of its debt and the name still standing, and they'll come. Magic can't cross the valley soil, but they have brokers. They have Odile." She looked down at the token in her fist, then at Wren — at the returned heir, the abandoned son of nine masks, sitting in a temple garden smelling of rosemary and turned earth. "They'll come for *you*, especially. A throne doesn't forgive being refused."

Wren looked east. Far off, so faint it might have been weather, a light flickered beneath the mountain's shoulder — green-gold, patient, angry.

"Then we'd better decide," he said, "what we tell them when they arrive."

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, every ledger in the temple library slid open at once — and every page bearing the name *Paloma* began, letter by letter, to darken with fresh ink.
