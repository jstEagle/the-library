## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The luck-charm cost three coppers, and August Hartwell lied about every part of it.

"That's gull-wing silver," he told the fisherman, turning the little tin medallion in the lamplight of his stall so it caught a shine it did not properly own. "Blessed at the tide-turn. Old magic, friend. The kind they don't make anymore."

None of that was true. The charm was tin from a broken kettle, stamped with a sigil August had copied off a soup ladle in a tavern in Vessany, and the only blessing it had received was his own muttered apology to whatever god watched over kettle-smiths. But Old Tam was dying — everyone on the Saltrow knew it, the way you know weather coming in your knees — and Tam had come to buy courage for one last season on the water, and courage was the one thing August could not sell him honestly.

So he sold it dishonestly, and he made it good.

"Three coppers," Tam said, "for old magic."

"For *your* old luck," August corrected gently, pressing the medallion into the fisherman's cracked palm and folding the fingers over it. "There's a difference. Luck is what happens to you. This is what you carry into it." He leaned close, as if conferring something sacred, which in a way he was. "When the swell turns bad, you put your thumb here, where the mark is, and you say: *not today.* That's all. Not today. The sea's heard worse arguments, but she respects a man who makes them."

Tam laughed — a wet, rusted-hinge laugh — and paid his three coppers, and shuffled off toward the harbor with his shoulders straighter than they'd been in a year. August watched him go and felt the familiar small ache of the trade: warmth given under false pretenses is still warmth. He'd built twelve years on that principle.

Twelve years of being nobody. A stall on the Saltrow between a rope-maker and a woman who sold pickled eels with evangelical fervor. A room above a chandler's with a leak that sang on rainy nights. A face that no one looked at twice, because he had taught it not to be looked at. In Greyhallow, where half the city claimed some grandmother's hedge-craft and the other half informed on it, an invisible man could live long and quietly and even, on good days, happily.

He was a charm-seller. That was all anyone needed to know. His charms were tin and theater and a warm voice, and if they worked better than tin and theater had any right to work — well, that was a secret he kept even from himself most mornings, the way you keep a hand pressed over an old scar.

He was packing up for the night when the wards broke.

* * *

There was no sound at first. That was the wrongness of it. Greyhallow lived inside the hum of the border wards the way a child lives inside its mother's heartbeat; nobody heard it anymore, but everyone would have felt it stop. And now the whole city stopped with it, mid-motion — the eel-seller frozen mid-pitch, a dog lifting its head, the lamplighter halfway up his ladder with his taper guttering.

August stood very still.

Then the sky went out.

Not clouded. Not storm-dark. *Out.* He watched the stars vanish the way candles are snuffed — one by one by one by one, west to east, a long silent rolling darkness like fingers closing across the heavens — until there was nothing above Greyhallow but black, absolute and total, the oldest dark there is. No moon either. He hadn't noticed the moon until it was gone, and then its absence hit him like a blow to the chest.

Around him, the Saltrow woke into panic. Voices, shutters, someone weeping, someone praying loudly and badly. The eel-seller abandoned her cart entirely, which August suspected had never happened in the history of eels.

But August found he wasn't looking at the sky. He was looking north, up the coast road, where the horizon had begun — faintly, horribly — to glow white. Frost-light. He knew frost-light. He had seen it once before, twelve years ago, from the far side of a burning border, the night the Sundering ended everything and he had sworn himself invisible rather than be unmade with the rest.

"No," he said, to no one, in a voice he didn't use in front of customers. "No, no, that's finished. We finished that."

"You did finish something," said a voice behind him. "Though perhaps not what you think."

* * *

She was standing at the mouth of the alley beside his stall, and she had clearly been standing there for some time. A small woman, grey-cloaked, ink-stained fingers, the kind of tired that goes down to the bone and sets up housekeeping. Court-cut boots, though scuffed nearly anonymous. She held a ledger against her chest the way other people held children or weapons.

"Ivetta," she said, before he asked. "Second Scribe to the Office of Compacts and Bindings." Her eyes flicked to his stall, to the charms, to him, cataloguing. "You'll want to run. Everyone wants to run when I say my title. But you won't, because you've already guessed why I'm here, and men who guess don't waste time running."

"And why," August said carefully, "is a scribe of anything visiting a charm-seller on the worst night in twelve years?"

"Because you're not a charm-seller." She said it without drama, almost kindly, like a physician naming a disease. "And because the court beneath the mountain has concluded a new compact, and I was sent to find the one man alive whose name appears in the margins of the old ones. August Hartwell. Swordspeaker. Oath-sworn. Dead, according to our records, which —" she glanced up at the starless sky — "our records may wish to revisit."

The word *swordspeaker* went through him like cold water through a hull. Nobody had said it aloud in twelve years. He hadn't let them. He hadn't let *himself.*

"You're mistaken," he said. "I'm afraid you've confused me with a dead man. It happens. I have one of those faces."

"The dead man's oath-blade is buried in a lead-lined box under the floorboards of the room above Halvard's Chandlery, wrapped in oilcloth, third board from the window." She said it flatly, factually, and watched the blood leave his face. "I read the requisition for the lead myself. You were thorough. It was still findable. Everything is findable; that is rather my whole profession, and lately my whole sorrow."

August gripped the edge of his stall. Above them the sky stayed black, and to the north the frost-light brightened another shade, patient as tide.

"What do you want?" he said finally.

"The truth, mostly, though I doubt either of us will enjoy it." Ivetta hugged the ledger tighter. "Twelve years ago, when the order fell, the court lost its source of power. It has spent those years finding another. Tonight it found one. The new compact was signed at dusk — signed in soul, which is the only coin the deep court accepts. The winter coming down off that mountain will not end, August Hartwell. Not in a season. Not ever. Unless the bargain is unmade."

"Then unmake it. You're a scribe. Unmake it."

"A scribe can amend a contract. Only a party to it can break one." She opened the ledger at last, and turned it toward him, angling it to the lamplight, and August looked down at the page and felt twelve years of careful invisibility burn away all at once.

Because he knew the handwriting. He had loved the handwriting. He had fallen asleep watching it by candlelight, letters like tall grass bending in wind, the *w*s especially — he had once written a poem about her *w*s, gods help him, and never shown her, and thank every merciful power he never showed her —

Wren's name sat at the bottom of the compact. Not written. *Signed.* In the old way, in the true way, in the way that signs with a soul instead of ink. And below it, the terms, and below the terms, the price line, and on the price line, in the court's cold clerk-hand:

*One wardwright, whole. In perpetuity.*

"She volunteered," Ivetta said quietly. "That's the part I can't stop reading. They gave her a choice, and she took yours. The court wanted the swordspeaker whose oath still anchors the old wards. She signed herself in his place, and the court accepted, because a soul is a soul, and hers was —" Ivetta paused, choosing the word with a scholar's care — "*adequate.*"

The world had gone very simple and very loud. Somewhere behind him the Saltrow screamed at the empty sky, and ahead of him the frost-light climbed, and in between stood this ink-stained woman holding his entire past open in her hands like a wound.

"They hollowed her," Ivetta went on. "Her soul is woven into the winter's machinery now. She is the reason the stars went out. She is, in a sense that will keep you awake for the rest of your life, the reason for all of it. And the compact cannot be broken from outside. Someone has to go down beneath the mountain and stand in front of the Sundered King and unmake it to his face."

"Someone," August repeated.

"Yes."

"And you came to me because —"

"Because you're the only swordspeaker left," Ivetta said, "and because your oath-blade remembers you, whether or not you remember it, and because I have watched the records of your life for six years waiting for this night, and I am very tired, Mr. Hartwell, and it is very dark, and the winter is coming."

Above them, the sky stayed empty. To the north, the frost-light spread along the horizon like spilled milk, slow and certain, and somewhere beneath the mountain a woman with grass-bend letters for a signature hung hollowed in the machinery of an endless cold.

August looked at the stars that weren't there, and understood, with a strange calm, that the invisible life was over. The past had come collecting, and it had brought a ledger.

"Third board from the window," he said at last. "You said."

"I did."

He reached down and began, with hands that only shook a little, to pack up his stall.
