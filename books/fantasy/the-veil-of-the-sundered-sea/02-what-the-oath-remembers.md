## 2. What the Oath Remembers

The tide came in grey over Greyhallow, and August Hartwell woke before it, as he always did, with his hands curled around nothing.

Twelve years of mornings had taught his body to forget the weight of a blade. His fingers had not. They opened and closed on empty air like a man feeling for a door that had been bricked over, and then he rose, and dressed, and went down to the shop to grind dried nettlewort into powder that would do absolutely nothing but cost four coppers.

That was the honest trade, in its way. He sold luck to people who needed somewhere to put their hope.

He was weighing out a charm for the cooper's widow when the knock came — three sharp raps, then one soft one, which meant Brannoc Vail, because Brannoc had never once in his life done anything simply when he could do it strangely.

"Back door," August called. "You'll frighten the customers."

"You haven't got any customers." Brannoc let himself in anyway, all six feet of him folded under the low lintel, smelling of tar and salt and someone else's rum. He was a mountain of a man who moved like a rumor, and he set a wrapped bundle on the counter without being asked. "Fish. Caught it myself. Well. Watched Tamsin catch it and took credit."

"I'm busy."

"It's not even dawn."

"The cooper's widow doesn't care about your schedule." August tied off the little cloth packet and slid it across to her — old Marta, stooped and patient, who clutched her luck-charm the way other people clutched prayer. She pressed coins into his hand and patted his wrist. "Bless you, dear. The sea's been wrong lately. My husband said so, before."

Before. Before the wards went down. Before the stars went out like candles pinched by an enormous, indifferent hand. Three days now, and the whole city pretending it hadn't happened, the way Greyhallow pretended about everything: weather, grief, the smell of the fish market.

When Marta left, Brannoc leaned both elbows on the counter and looked at August with the expression of a man preparing to be extremely annoying.

"You're going to ask me for something," Brannoc said. "I can tell. You've got your asking face on."

"I don't have an asking face."

"You've got three faces, Hartwell. Shopkeeper, liar, and asking. That's the third one." He tapped the counter twice. "Ask."

August looked at him for a long moment. Then he went to the back room, knelt beneath the loose floorboard he'd never once told anyone about, and lifted out the oilcloth bundle that had waited there through twelve years of tides.

The oath-blade was rusted nearly orange. It had been beautiful once — swordspeakers' blades were grown, not forged, coaxed out of star-iron and living wood until they sang when their bearer spoke — and now it was a dead thing, a relic, contraband heavier than any smuggler's cargo. Under the Sundering, carrying it would earn him the gray cells under the magistrate's hall, if he was lucky, and the quiet cart to the ash fields if he wasn't.

He unwrapped it anyway.

* * *

Twelve years ago, there had been a courtyard full of rain, and Wren laughing at him across it.

"You take everything so seriously," she'd said, her wardwright's chalk dusting her sleeves white, her hands still moving — they were always moving, sketching sigils in the air like she was knitting the world together out of habit. "It's a name, August. It's just a name."

"It's my name."

"That's what makes it funny." She'd come closer, rain in her dark hair, and touched the hilt of his new oath-blade where it hung at his hip. The blade had hummed at her touch; it had always liked her more than it liked him. "One day you'll say it out loud to someone who isn't a sword, and the sky won't fall. I promise."

Then the Sundering had come, and the sky did fall, or rather the stars did, and every soul in the court beneath the mountain had been given a choice: bury your magic, bury your name, bury your blade — or be buried with them.

August had chosen silence. He'd been very good at it. Twelve years good at it.

And now Ivetta the scribe stood in his shop doorway with ink-stained fingers and terrible news, saying *they bought her*, saying *her soul is in the winter's machinery*, saying words that cracked the silence open like ice going out from under a man.

So here he was, kneeling on his own floorboards at first light, holding a rusted sword and talking to it like a madman.

"Hello," he said.

Nothing. Of course nothing. Rust and rot and twelve years of neglect.

"Hello," he said again, and this time he put something behind it — not power, not yet, just the shape of the old cadence, the rising note a swordspeaker used the way a tuning fork finds its twin. "You know me. You've known me since I was nineteen and stupid enough to swear an oath to a dead order."

The blade warmed.

It was very slight — the difference between cold iron and iron left near a hearth — but August felt it go through his palms and up his arms like water finding an old channel, and oh, it *knew* the channel. Twelve years of silt and the river remembered exactly where it ran.

*August.* Not sound. Pressure. A voice made of pressure, made of memory, made of every vow he'd ever spoken into steel. *You kept me in the dark.*

"I kept us both alive."

*Barely.* The warmth spread. *Say your name.*

"No."

*Say it and I will make you strong enough to tear the mountain open with your hands. Say it and we will walk into that court and no one — no king, no ward, no winter — will stop us. I remember what you are.*

"That," August said quietly, "is exactly the problem."

Because he could feel it now, the whole shape of the temptation, laid out before him like a banquet. The old power didn't offer gently. It offered *totally*. Come back, it whispered in the language of strength. Be the swordspeaker again. Be the thing that made kings flinch. Forget the shop and the charms and the small warm life — forget the man you built out of the wreckage, the one who sells luck to widows and waters a window-box of basil and has learned, slowly, painfully, how to be *small* and *safe* and *happy*, or something adjacent to happy.

The blade pulsed warmer. *She is underground,* it said. *Her soul is being spent, coin by coin, to keep a winter running. Every hour you spend being careful, she spends being fuel. Is your basil worth that?*

"That's cruel."

*I am a sword. Cruelty is the only honesty I was given.*

August laughed — a short, broken sound — and Brannoc's voice came from the front room, casual as anything: "Everything alright back there? Sounds like you're having a conversation with your floorboards."

"In a moment," August called back.

He looked at the blade. The blade looked — he'd have sworn it looked — back.

"Here's the bargain," he said. "We don't speak my name. Not yet. Maybe never. But you're going to wake up enough to cut what needs cutting, and I'm going to stay *me* — the shopkeeper version, the one who waters things. If you try to swallow me, I will throw you in the deepest part of the Sundered Sea and wish you a pleasant eternity. Are we understood?"

A long pause. Then, grudging as a cat coming in from the rain:

*Understood. For now.*

"For now," August agreed, and wrapped the blade back in its oilcloth, and felt it against his spine like a second heartbeat, warm and impatient and *his*.

* * *

Brannoc's ship was called the *Mercy of Thieves*, which Brannoc insisted was ironic rather than accurate, and which every sailor in Greyhallow insisted was neither.

They sat in the low cabin among crates of pickled herring while Brannoc listened to the whole of it — Ivetta, the court, the winter, Wren — with a stillness August had never seen in him. When it was done, Brannoc poured two cups of something that could legally only be called rum in the loosest sense, and pushed one across.

"You want me to sail us to the mountain coast and row you up a river nobody's rowed in twelve years, into a court that eats souls." He drank. "What's in it for me?"

"Name your price."

"Coin's boring." Brannoc turned the cup in his huge hands. "I deal in secrets, Hartwell. Everyone knows that. So here's mine, freely given, so we start square: I knew. About you. Four years now — saw you talk a storm flat off the Point during the wreck of the *Kestrel*, calm as a clerk filing papers, and I've said nothing, because a man who keeps one secret well keeps others well too, and you're the best customer I've ever had."

August stared at him. "You watched me—"

"Break the Sundering? Yes. Regularly. Small stuff. Luck-charms that actually work, that sort of thing." He shrugged. "Terrible crime, selling real luck. Anyway. My price: one secret from you. True, large, and freely spoken. That's the fare."

"That's all?"

"That's all. Though fair warning—" Brannoc grinned, and there was something unexpectedly gentle under it, "—the last person who paid me a true secret cried, and I make excellent tea."

August held the cup. Outside, the sunless sky sat over Greyhallow like a lid, and somewhere beneath a mountain a woman he loved was being spent, coin by coin, to keep the dark cold.

"My name," August said. "My true name. The one I swore into the blade. The one I haven't said aloud in twelve years."

Brannoc raised his eyebrows, waiting.

August took a breath — and found, to his horror, that the blade against his spine went utterly silent, listening, and that his own throat closed around the word like a fist. Because saying it here, now, in this floating crate of herring, would be the first crack in twelve years of silence, and cracks, he knew better than anyone, have a way of becoming avalanches.

"Not yet," he said finally. "But I'll pay it. On the water. When we're committed and can't turn back — I'll say it, and you'll hold it, and that will be the fare. That's the secret: I'll give you the name that could unmake me."

Brannoc studied him for a long moment, then stuck out one enormous hand.

"Fare accepted. We sail on the morning tide." He paused at the cabin door, and his grin faded into something almost kind. "Hartwell. Whatever's under that mountain — you should know the storm you flattened off the Point? I've sailed thirty years. That wasn't small stuff."

August touched the oilcloth bundle at his back, warm as a sleeping animal.

"I know," he said. "That's what scares me."

Above them, through the deck planks, the starless sky pressed down on Greyhallow like a held breath — and far inland, past the drowned marshes and the old border stones, the mountain waited with its bought-and-paid-for winter, and the woman whose soul kept it burning.
