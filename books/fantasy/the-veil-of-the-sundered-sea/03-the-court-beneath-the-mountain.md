## 3. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain had a throat, and it swallowed them politely.

August felt it first as a change in the air — the salt of Greyhallow scrubbed away, replaced by something mineral and old, like breathing inside a bell that hadn't been rung in centuries. The passage down was not carved so much as *persuaded*, stone flowing into arches and stairways with the smoothness of poured wax. Frost ferned along the walls in patterns too regular to be natural, spiraling inward, always inward, toward whatever heart beat at the bottom.

"Cheerful place," Brannoc muttered behind him. The smuggler carried a lantern he'd bought off a dead man's boat and had already asked twice whether they could turn around. "I've smuggled through worse, mind. There was a cave full of weeping statues once — long story, involves a duke's widow — but at least the statues didn't look at you like they were *keeping count*."

They were keeping count. August could feel it. Every frost-spiral they passed ticked over somewhere deep in his chest, a ledger being updated: *three intruders, descending, one of them sworn.*

He said nothing. He'd gotten good at saying nothing. Twelve years of practice.

* * *

Ivetta counted her sins on the way down, because counting was what scribes did when the ink ran dry and only memory remained.

She had written the bargain herself. That was the wound she carried, wrapped in neat clerk's shorthand: *One soul, freely surrendered by the Court's writ, in exchange for the Long Winter's maintenance.* She had copied it three times, filed two copies, burned the third as protocol demanded, and slept soundly that night for the first time in months. That was the part that shamed her most — how *soundly*.

Now she walked behind a charm-seller who was obviously no charm-seller, beside a smuggler who smelled of tar and bad decisions, carrying the third copy after all. Unburned. Folded against her heart where the paper had gone soft as cloth from years of body heat.

"You knew," she said to August, quietly, when Brannoc dropped back to argue with a stalactite. "Down in Greyhallow, when I told you. You already suspected."

"I hoped I was wrong." His voice was flat, careful, a man walking on river ice. "Hoping wrong is easier than knowing right."

"And now?"

"Now I know why the stars went out." He glanced at her sidelong. "You could have stayed, scribe. Filed your confession, kept your pension."

"Ivetta," she corrected. "If we're going to die beneath a mountain together, you may as well use my name." Then, because honesty was a habit she was only twelve days into and still clumsy at: "And because I wrote her name into that bargain, August Hartwell. Wren of the Wardwrights' Guild. My hand put her here. I intend my hands to take her out."

August stopped walking.

For a moment she thought he might strike her — and some traitorous part of her thought he'd be justified. Instead he looked at her with an expression she couldn't parse, something between grief and gratitude, and said, "Then keep up," and walked on.

* * *

The court announced itself before they saw it: a sound like a choir heard through three walls of ice, vast and patient. Then the passage opened, and there it was.

A cavern the size of a cathedral, its ceiling lost in darkness where stars should have been — and August understood, with a lurch, *where the stars had gone.* They hung here instead, dimmed and caged, drifting like lanterns in a slow black tide. Below them spread a court of white pillars and frozen fountains, and at its center, a machine.

It took him a moment to understand it was a machine at all, because it was beautiful. Rings upon rings of silver and glass, turning with the unhurried grace of planets, each ring threaded with light — and woven through the light, threads of a different color entirely. Warm gold. Human gold.

Soul-thread.

And at the machine's center, standing in a cradle of ice like a saint in a reliquary, stood Wren.

She was alive. That was the horror of it — alive, upright, eyes open, her dark hair still cut short the way she'd worn it the summer they'd met. But nothing moved behind her face. Her chest rose and fell in time with the great rings, breath borrowed from the machinery, and where her shadow should have fallen there was only a faint golden glow leaking out of her skin, feeding upward into the rings, thread by patient thread.

"Oh," said Brannoc softly. "Oh, that's foul. Even the weeping-statue cave wasn't this foul."

August crossed the frozen floor before he decided to move. The ice sang under his boots. None of the court's guards — tall figures in white lacquer, faces smooth as eggs — turned to stop him. Why would they? Nothing living came here. Nothing living mattered here.

"Wren." His voice cracked on the second syllable. "Wren, it's me. It's August."

Nothing. The rings turned. A thread of gold unspooled from her fingertips and wound away into the light, and somewhere above them, in a world none of them could see, snow fell on a harbor that would never freeze if she stopped.

"It's me," he said again, uselessly, and hated how small it sounded.

* * *

Ivetta watched the Sundered King arrive and understood, all at once, why the archives described him only in passive voice. *The bargain was struck. The winter was decreed.* Never *he*. Because to write *he* you had to look at him, and looking at him was like reading a book with every other word torn out.

He wore a crown of unlit stars. His face was handsome the way a treaty is handsome — correct, symmetrical, signed in someone else's blood. When he spoke, his voice came from everywhere in the cavern at once, courteous as a filing system.

"A swordspeaker. In my court. Twelve years late for muster, August Hartwell, and dressed as a peddler of trinkets." A pause that managed to convey disappointment without bothering with a face. "The Oathbound were better than this. The Oathbound were *silent*."

"He's very bad at silence," Brannoc offered helpfully. "We're working on it."

The King ignored him, which Ivetta suspected was the greatest insult in his repertoire. He drifted closer to August, robes moving like slow smoke.

"But lateness can be mended. Rank can be restored. You were First Blade of the Western Order before the Sundering — do you imagine I've forgotten? I keep records older than your guild, scribe." Here the starless eyes flicked to Ivetta, and she felt her third copy of the bargain grow cold against her ribs, a paper wanting to be found. "The order you served made this court possible. Its oaths built these rings. Its discipline keeps the winter turning. Come back to us, swordspeaker. Take up your rank. Take up your *blade*. Stand at my right hand as the world sleeps warm beneath our snow, ordered, obedient, safe."

Safe. Ivetta watched the word land on August like a hand on a shoulder, and saw the terrible thing underneath it: that part of him wanted it. Twelve years of hiding, of selling luck to dying fishermen, of being no one — and here was a voice offering to make him *someone* again, with the whole rotten architecture of his old life held up as if it were a gift.

"And her?" August asked. His voice was steady. His hands were not.

"The wardwright is the engine," the King said gently. "Engines are not rescued, swordspeaker. They are maintained. Grieve her properly, as your order taught you. Duty first. Feeling after. Isn't that the litany?"

* * *

August had recited that litany ten thousand times. *Duty first. Feeling after.* It had been stitched into him younger than memory, deeper than language, and standing in the white court with rank held out like a rope, he felt how much of him was still stitched — how much of him had never been his own.

Wren turned her head. Slowly, mechanically, the way a weathervane turns. Her eyes found his and there was — barely, faintly, at the very bottom of the gold — a flicker. Not recognition. Less than that. More like a door left open a crack somewhere in a burning house.

Her lips moved. No sound came out. But August had spent one perfect year learning to read her mouth across smoky tavern rooms, and he read it now.

*Run.*

"No," he said aloud, to the King, to the litany, to the stitched-up boy he'd been. "Duty first. All right. Let's talk about duty, then." He reached under his coat and drew out the rusted oath-blade, and the whole court flinched — pillars, guards, even the turning rings seemed to hesitate, one beat, like a heart skipping.

"You want a swordspeaker at your right hand," August said, and began, very quietly, to speak the first syllables of his true name.
