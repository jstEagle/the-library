## 4. A Name Spoken in the Dark

The cold in the heart-chamber had a sound to it. August had never noticed that before — a thin, ringing note, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass — and now that he listened for it he could hear it everywhere: in the frost climbing the pillars, in the breath of the courtiers frozen mid-curtsey around the walls, in Wren herself.

She stood at the center of the great wheel of ice, her hands outstretched into the machinery of it, threads of light running from her fingertips into a thousand spindles that turned and turned without turning anywhere. Her eyes were open. They had always been her best feature, Wren's eyes — quick, brown, full of mischief even during examinations when she was almost certainly cheating off his notes. Now they were open and empty as two coins dropped down a well.

"Wren," he said.

Nothing. The spindles sang their one cold note.

Behind him, Ivetta made a small sound, half prayer and half apology. Brannoc said nothing at all, which for Brannoc was practically a eulogy. The Sundered King sat his throne of black glacier-glass with his chin on his fist like a bored magistrate, and when he spoke his voice came out of the mountain itself.

"You may still have your rank, swordspeaker. Third Chair of the Oathbound, as you were. A warm hall. A name that opens doors. Walk away, and none of this need trouble you."

"This," August said, gesturing at the room — the hollowed woman, the dead stars, the twelve-year winter grinding its gears — "is what walking away got everyone."

"The alternative is grief." The King spread his hands, reasonable as a merchant. "Grief passes. Winter endures. That is not cruelty, Hartwell. That is arithmetic."

August looked at Wren. Twelve years ago he had stood in a marble courtyard and watched the Sundering fall like an axe, and he had kept his mouth shut because keeping his mouth shut was the oath, was the order, was survival. He had built a life out of silence — a little stall, a little luck-charm business, a face no one remembered twice. He had been so proud of how well he'd hidden.

And she had been sold. While he hid, she had been *bought*.

"Here's my counteroffer," August said.

He drew the rusted oath-blade. It came out of its wrappings singing that same thin note, and the old power rose in him like floodwater — familiar, enormous, *eager*. For one dizzying moment it offered him everything it had ever offered: strength, certainty, the sweet simplicity of being a weapon instead of a man. He felt his spine straighten with borrowed iron. He felt the temptation to simply cut the wheel apart, to take her by force, to be magnificent.

He let the feeling crest. And then, gently, the way you'd set down something breakable, he put it aside.

"No," he told the blade, quietly. "Not like that."

The power recoiled, offended. The King laughed, a sound like calving ice.

"Then what, little swordspeaker? You cannot fight me. You will not flee. What remains?"

"Words," August said. "It was always words. That's the joke nobody in this mountain ever got. You bought a soul with a bargain, and bargains are just words wearing armor. So."

He stepped into the wheel of ice. Frost bit through his boots; the spindles shrieked past him close enough to part his hair. He took Wren's cold hands in his own, and the threads of light ran straight through both of them now, and it hurt like holding a live wire, and he held on.

"I'm going to speak my true name," he said to the King. "Into your bargain. Freely given, which means freely binding. You wanted a soul to run your winter? Here's mine on the table next to hers. Let's see which one the arithmetic prefers."

For the first time in twelve years — longer, if he was honest, since the last time he'd spoken it aloud to another person who mattered — August Hartwell said his true name.

He won't write it here. Some things stay private even in the telling. But he spoke it whole and unhidden, every syllable of himself that the oath had folded away and buried: the boy who'd been afraid of the dark and became a man who lit lamps for strangers; the student who cheated off Wren right back; the coward in the courtyard; the charm-seller who watered a dying fisherman's flowers every Tuesday because the man had no family left to do it. All of it. All of him. Truth over loyalty. Truth over the dead order. Truth, finally, over hiding.

The bargain heard him.

Bargains are honest things, underneath all their armor. They weigh what they're given. And a soul worn smooth by twelve years of service — hollowed, rationed, spent spindle by spindle — weighed against a soul spoken aloud in full, flaws and fears and Tuesdays included? There was never any arithmetic about it at all.

The wheel shattered.

Not dramatically — or rather, dramatically, but *everywhere*, all at once, the way ice goes when spring finally wins an argument. The spindles burst into ordinary falling snow. The frost ran off the pillars in bright streams. The courtiers gasped awake mid-curtsey, several of them deeply embarrassed. And Wren's hands went warm in his, and her eyes filled back up like wells filling, brown and quick and furious.

"*August Hartwell,*" she said, "you absolute — you took *twelve years*—"

"I know."

"—I counted, do you understand, I had nothing to do in there but count—"

"I know," he said, and then she was holding him, frost-wet and furious and alive, and the Sundered King's throne cracked down the middle behind them, and somewhere above them, impossibly, warmly, the mountain remembered it had a top.

* * *

They climbed out through the long throat of the mountain while it groaned and shrugged off its ice, Brannoc complaining cheerfully the entire way ("Twelve years I've smuggled *goods*, friends, never once a *climate*"), Ivetta walking with her scribe's satchel hugged to her chest like a rescued child, already muttering about testimony and ledgers and exactly which names were going into the official record.

Outside, the sky was doing something it hadn't done in twelve years.

The first star came back while they stood on the mountainside watching — a single point of silver over the eastern ridge, tentative, like someone testing whether the room was safe. Then another. Then three more, then a scatter, then a slow tide of them pouring back across the black, each one arriving with a faint sound only Wren could hear, and she described it anyway: *like bells underwater, like someone remembering a song.*

By the time they reached the coast road, the sea itself was breaking up. Great plates of grey ice shifted and sighed apart, and beneath them the water moved — actually moved, dark and restless and smelling of salt and kelp and the whole thawing world.

August stopped at the top of the shingle beach and took stock of himself, the way you do after a long illness. No power. Not a spark. The blade in its wrappings weighed exactly what steel weighs, nothing more, forever. He was, by every measure that had ever terrified him, ordinary.

He found, to his enormous surprise, that he was laughing.

"What?" Wren said, suspicious.

"Nothing. It's just — I spent twelve years making sure nobody saw me. And now I can't even do a luck-charm. I'm the most visible I've ever been and the least interesting."

Wren looked him over — the frost-burned hands, the ridiculous wrappings, the man himself, plain as bread and grinning like a fool at the relit sky.

"Wrong," she said. "You're the most interesting thing on this beach. But don't worry." She took his hand, laced her fingers through his where anyone at all could see, and started down toward the water's edge. "Nobody here knows you either. We can be unhidden together. It's much better with two."

Behind them, the last of the mountain's snow slid into the sea. Ahead of them, under a sky busy remembering every star it owned, the tide came in warm for the first time in twelve years — and August Hartwell, ordinary man, walked into the morning without hiding from a single inch of it.
