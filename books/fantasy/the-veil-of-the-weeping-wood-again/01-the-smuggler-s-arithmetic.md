## 1. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

Fog in the Thornfells was not weather. It was a ledger.

Sable Sterling had learned to read it the way other guides read stars: the way it pooled in a hollow meant water below; the way it clung to a ridgeline meant stone that would hold a horse's weight; the way it *avoided* a stretch of trail — that was the kind of arithmetic that got people killed, because fog did not avoid things without a reason, and in the Thornfells the reasons were usually teeth.

They stood at the head of the caravan with one boot on a wet boulder and counted.

Six wagons. Fourteen mules. Nine drivers who smelled of cheap tobacco and cheaper confidence. One merchant — Haskel Broadgirdle, a name he had clearly chosen himself and defended like a birthright — whose coin was good but whose sense of time was not. And one guide, which was Sable, which was all the margin this expedition had.

"Two days through the pass," Sable said, not turning around. "Three if the fog holds past the Split. Four if any of you decides he knows a shortcut."

"And if the fog doesn't hold?" asked one of the drivers, a young man with the particular optimism of someone who had never yet been eaten by anything.

"Then it's two days and you'll have nothing to complain about, which I know will disappoint you."

That earned a laugh from the back. Good. Laughter kept men moving when their feet wanted to stop. Sable filed it away under *useful* and turned to face them properly for the first time that morning.

It was a mistake they made most mornings, and regretted every time. Faces wanted eye contact. Eye contact wanted conversation. Conversation wanted answers, and answers were the one cargo Sable Sterling never carried.

Haskel came puffing up the line, his coat straining at seams that had long ago surrendered their opinions. "Sterling. My man says there's a place up ahead where the fog just — stops. Like it's afraid of something."

"Your man has good eyes."

"Well? What's afraid of what?"

"The fog's afraid of the Hollow Step. So am I. We'll be going around."

"Around adds half a day!"

"Around keeps everyone breathing." Sable pulled their collar higher. The wool scratched against the scar at their throat, an old habit of a hand that still sometimes reached up as if to hide something fifteen years too late. "You can spend half a day or you can spend fourteen funerals. I've done the sums before. Funerals are more expensive."

Haskel opened his mouth — Sable could see the objection assembling itself behind his teeth like a wagon backing up — then closed it. He was greedy, not stupid, and Sable had learned early in this second life that greed and stupidity were distinguishable only by how quickly they folded.

"Half a day," Haskel muttered. "Fine. But you're eating last at supper."

"I always eat last at supper."

"You do?"

"It discourages conversation."

The fog pressed close, soft as spilled milk, and somewhere above them a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling. Sable set off, and the wagons groaned into motion behind, wheels complaining about the grade in voices that sounded suspiciously human.

* * *

The first hour was the easy arithmetic. Grade: gentle. Footing: gravel over old roadbed, probably Imperial, probably older than anyone wanted to think about. Water: audible to the left, which meant the cliff was to the right, which meant keep the mules' heads pointed away from the drop. All of it simple. All of it solvable with counting and attention.

The second hour introduced variables.

A dead crow on the trail, unmarked, undevoured. In the Thornfells, nothing stayed dead and uneaten for long unless everything else had decided to leave. Sable crouched beside it, touched the wing — cold, but not frozen — and looked at the fog ahead with new interest.

Something had told the scavengers to wait.

Not wolves. Wolves were honest; they left wreckage. This was the tidy kind of absence, the kind that came with intelligence attached. Sable stood, brushed off their knee, and adjusted the plan without announcing it: they would take the switchbacks wide, keep the wagons in single file, and put the loudest driver — a cheerful fool named Tam, who narrated his own mule's thoughts in a falsetto — somewhere near the middle, where his noise could serve as a decoy rather than a beacon.

Tam objected to being moved. Tam was overruled. Tam discovered, within ten minutes, that the middle of the column was warmer out of the wind, and declared it his idea.

Everyone has a talent, Sable thought. Mine is knowing which lies keep people alive.

The fog thickened as they climbed, until the world shrank to twenty paces in every direction and sound became the only map. Sable walked by ear now: the crunch of gravel, the wet slap of wheel-rims, the mules' breathing, and beneath it all a silence that moved when they moved, keeping its distance the way a debt collector does.

At the midday halt — a shelf of granite wide enough for the wagons, chosen because it had exactly one approach and Sable could watch it — Haskel found them again. Of course he did. Merchants could smell a moment of stillness the way sharks smelled blood.

"Odd question," Haskel said, settling onto a rock uninvited and producing a wedge of cheese from some inner pocket with the air of a magician. "My man Dole says you've been guiding these passes six years. Never takes a partner. Never stays in Ashmoor past dark. Never—"

"Never answers questions. He's learning fast."

"—never shows anyone that scar." Haskel tore the cheese with his teeth and chewed thoughtfully. "Throat wounds are funny things. Most folk who get them don't walk away talking. Most folk who walk away talking won't stop telling you about it."

"Most folk are tedious."

"Mm." Haskel studied Sable with small, shrewd eyes, the eyes of a man who had made a fortune noticing what others overlooked. "You know, I once employed a swordspeaker. Back before the Wardens burned the last of them out of the lowlands. Fine woman. Voice like a bell — you could hear the words land in your chest. They say the oath-tongue could cut steel, open doors, bind a man's tongue to truth." He chewed. "They also say the order wore scars at the throat. Oath-marks, Dole called them. From the binding ceremony."

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere above, loose stones ticked down a slope.

"If you're asking whether I knew her," Sable said evenly, "I know everyone in these mountains, and no one."

"That wasn't my question."

"Then it's a waste of good cheese."

Haskel laughed, big and unbothered, and offered the wedge. Sable took it, because refusing twice would be memorable, and memory was the enemy. The cheese was terrible. It was, Sable reflected, important to stay humble.

What Haskel couldn't know — what nobody in this caravan could know, what nobody left alive knew — was that he'd missed the mark anyway. The scar wasn't an oath-mark. It was older than the oaths, from a night of fire and screaming that Sable kept locked in a room at the back of their mind, and they visited it the way you visit a grave: briefly, standing, never sitting down.

The order was ash. The borders they'd guarded were sold. The Crown Wardens had hunted the survivors into myth, and myth was a fine place to live — drafty, but quiet. Six years of guiding smugglers through fog. No names spoken above a whisper. No voice raised above a work-song. The comfort of being no one was a real comfort, Sable thought, chewing terrible cheese in the belly of a cloud. It fit like an old coat. You stopped noticing the holes.

"Move out in ten," they said, standing. "And Haskel — the next mile, nobody talks. Not whispers, not prayers, not songs. If you must communicate, use hands."

"Why?"

"Because the thing that killed that crow is listening, and it listens better than you speak."

To their credit, the caravan went silent almost instantly. Even Tam managed it, though his face went through several stages of grief on behalf of his own commentary.

* * *

They rounded the Hollow Step by midafternoon, wide and slow, and whatever waited in the fog-free gap let them pass with what Sable chose to interpret as professional courtesy between solitary creatures. By dusk they had cleared the high ground and made camp in a bowl of pine below the last ridge — sheltered, defensible, and blessedly ordinary. Ordinary was the finest word in any language. Sable intended to petition to have it carved on their headstone.

The camp assembled itself with the comfortable clatter of people who believed the danger was over. Fires bloomed. Tam resumed narrating. Haskel counted boxes by lantern-light, lips moving, and Sable sat apart on a root with their back to a trunk and did the evening's final sum:

Fourteen souls delivered. Zero questions answered successfully. One near-thing at the Step, unresolved, noted for the return trip. Pay due: forty marks and the quiet gratitude of a man who would forget Sable's face by spring.

Perfect. Anonymous. Nobody.

They were reviewing the morrow's route — down out of the high pass, across the scree fields, two hard days to Ashmoor — when the wind changed.

Sable noticed it before they understood it: the fog, which should have been thinning with the falling temperature, was instead rolling back *up* the slope toward the summit, like water running wrong, like something drawing a curtain. The fires guttered and steadied. The mules lifted their heads all at once, ears forward, staring at the same point on the dark ridge.

And beneath the wind, faint and rising, came a sound Sable hadn't heard in fifteen years.

Not a voice. A melody. Wordless, high, threaded through with harmonics no human throat should manage — a song in the old tongue, the covenant-tongue, the language Sable's mother had sung over the wash-basin and the whetstone and the dying. Their whole body knew it before their mind caught up; their hands curled into the shapes of words they'd sworn never to shape again.

The song came down off the ridge, closer, closer, and stopped.

At the edge of the firelight stood a child. Small, barefoot on frozen ground, hair plastered dark with rain that hadn't started falling yet, watching the camp with eyes that caught the firelight the way deep water catches it. The child looked at the wagons, at the mules, at Haskel frozen mid-count with his ledger hanging open — and then those eyes found Sable's across the fire, and held, and *recognized*, and the recognition landed in Sable's chest like a blade sliding home.

The child's mouth moved. No sound came out. But Sable heard it anyway, the way you hear a bell after it stops — in the bones, in the scar, in the locked room at the back of the mind where the fire still burned:

*Found you.*

Behind them, miles up the mountain, thunder rolled in a sky that had promised clear stars.

Sable stood very slowly, every old instinct waking at once, doing the only arithmetic that had ever truly mattered and always coming out owing:

One child. No voice. No footprints in the frost.

And a storm coming down the pass like something sent.
