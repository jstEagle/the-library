## 2. A Knock at Moonrise

The storm came up the valley like a debt collector—no warning, no courtesy, and absolutely certain to take something from you.

Sable Sterling had spent the afternoon doing what they did best in the Thornfells, which was nothing, loudly and convincingly. They had split enough firewood for three days. They had re-caulked the window that leaked every autumn and would leak again regardless of caulk, because the mountain did not honor contracts. They had eaten a supper of smoked trout and hard bread and told themselves it was a feast, which was the kind of lie a person got good at after fifteen years alone.

Fifteen years was long enough to forget the sound of your own name spoken by anyone but yourself.

Sable had made sure of it.

So when the knock came—at moonrise, through rain falling sideways and wind that sounded personally offended by Sable's roof—they were not alarmed so much as *inconvenienced*, and inconvenience was an old acquaintance.

"No one knocks," Sable said to the empty room, because talking to yourself was another skill you got good at, though you were never supposed to admit it. "Nobody climbs four hours above Ashmoor in a storm to *knock*. Wolves don't knock. Bandits don't knock. Bandits kick."

*Knock.*

Three small sounds. Knuckles, not fists. Polite as a merchant's apology.

Sable looked at their sword where it hung by the door—the plain blade, the one that had never once been used as anything more than a tool and a threat—and felt the familiar ache behind the breastbone, the one shaped like a language they refused to speak.

"Coming," they said, and hated how much the word sounded like surrender.

They opened the door on a wall of rain, a slap of cold, and a child.

* * *

She stood on the threshold stones barefoot in the deluge, perhaps ten years old, perhaps twelve—storm-light made ages slippery—soaked through a dress that had been gray before the weather finished its argument with it. Her hair was dark and plastered flat. Her feet, Sable noticed with a professional guide's eye, were not blue or shaking. Barefoot in this cold, and she wasn't shivering.

That was the first wrong thing.

The second wrong thing was her expression. Children who appear at strangers' doors in mountain storms are supposed to be terrified. This one looked at Sable with the mild patience of someone waiting for a shopkeeper to finish counting coins.

"You're lost," Sable said. "Obviously. Come in before the mountain decides you're interesting."

The child stepped inside without hesitation, dripping a small lake onto Sable's floorboards, and looked around the cabin with open curiosity: the sword, the drying herbs, the maps pinned over the hearth (all false ones; the true routes lived only in Sable's head), the single chair, the cot, the shelf of chipped crockery arranged with the fussy precision of someone who owned exactly eleven things and knew each of them personally.

"Right," said Sable, closing the door against the wind. "Rules. Rule one: I ask questions, you answer them. Rule two: no touching the sword. Rule three—" they paused, struck by the practical problem, "—do you speak? Common tongue? Lowland? Trader's cant? I can manage a bit of High Court if you're insufferably well-bred, though I warn you I'll be sarcastic about it."

The child tilted her head.

Nothing. Not fear-silence, not sullenness. She simply watched Sable's mouth move the way one watches birds.

"Wonderful," Sable muttered. "A mute foundling. The mountain sent me a riddle wrapped in wet wool." They fetched the blanket off the cot—it was the good blanket, the one they'd traded two knives for in Ashmoor, and giving it up stung more than they expected—and draped it around her shoulders. "Sit. There's soup left. It's trout. It's always trout. Don't look at me like that; you climbed a mountain barefoot, you'll eat trout and be grateful."

The child sat. She ate. And while she ate, she hummed.

* * *

It started as nothing—a thread of tune under the clink of spoon against bowl, the kind of half-song children make without knowing. Sable kept chopping kindling they didn't need, keeping their hands busy, keeping their back turned.

Then the melody turned.

It stepped down where melodies step down, and rose where they rise, and then it did the thing—that strange, aching turn at the end of the phrase, the note held a breath too long and bent upward, like grief remembering it used to be joy.

Sable's knife stopped mid-stroke.

Their mother used to sing that. In the kitchen of the chapter-house at Veil's Edge, kneading bread or mending harness, low, under her breath, thinking no one listened. A lullaby in a language with no kingdom attached to it. A song from before songs needed permission.

The order had been slaughtered when Sable was seventeen. Their mother had died singing something else—something with power in it, words that burned—but it was this small kitchen tune Sable remembered at three in the morning, this one, this exact bend of the seventh note, and no one living sang it, because everyone who knew it had died in the same courtyard on the same night.

Sable set the knife down very carefully, the way you do when your hands have become unreliable witnesses.

"Where did you hear that?"

The child looked up, spoon halfway to her mouth. She hummed the phrase again. Slower. Deliberate. Offering it.

"That's not a song," Sable heard themselves say, and their voice came out rougher than intended. "That's not—people don't sing that. That's a *name*-song. You use it to call someone home." They stopped. Fifteen years of discipline said *stop talking.* "Who taught it to you?"

The child set down the spoon. She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, in the manner of all children everywhere across all kingdoms, and then she pointed—at Sable's mouth, and then at herself, and shook her head. Then she pointed at the fire, and at her own chest, and pressed her palm flat over her heart.

"Yes, you're cold, I know—"

She shook her head again, impatient now, and looked around the room until her eyes landed on the hearth tools. She crawled over—blanket and all—to the pile of ash at the fire's edge, and before Sable could say a word about carpets and common decency, she was drawing in the ash with her finger.

Sable knew the shapes.

That was the horror of it. Not that the symbols were strange, but that they weren't. Sable's hands remembered them the way hands remember a rope you climbed as a child—the curve of the first mark, which meant *hearth* or *home* depending on its tail; the crossed strokes beneath it, which meant *given* or sometimes *sold*, the tail deciding which; and below that, coiled like a sleeping snake, the glyph Sable had last seen branded into a doorpost the night everything ended.

The child drew a circle. Inside the circle she drew a smaller shape—a seed, or a stone, or a soul, there was no word in any common tongue for the difference—and then she drew a line running out of the circle, downward, and put the *sold*-glyph on the line itself, as if the line were a road and the little shape were traveling it.

Then she tapped the drawing twice. Looked up at Sable. Waited.

The rain hammered the roof. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the wind found the gap under the eaves and moaned through it like the whole mountain grieving.

"I don't know what you're telling me," Sable lied.

The child gave them a look of devastating, unhurried disbelief—the look of a person who has caught an adult in a falsehood and is prepared to wait out the consequences—and then, with terrible precision, she took Sable's hand.

Her fingers were cold. That at least was ordinary. She turned Sable's palm up, and with one finger she traced, slowly, in the meat of the palm where no ink could live and no one could burn it away, a single glyph.

Sable's body reacted before their mind could forbid it. Their fingers closed around hers—not pulling away, *answering*—and traced back the counter-stroke, the completion, the way two halves of a covenant-mark met. Muscle memory fifteen years buried, rising like a drowned thing surfacing.

They sat like that, hand in hand, two strangers completing a sentence older than any kingdom, and Sable felt the floor of their careful, constructed, nobody-at-all life tilt gently beneath them.

"Oh no," Sable said quietly. "No, no. You've got the wrong cabin. Wrong door. I'm a guide. I walk smugglers' goods over passes and cheat merchants on tolls and I mind my own business the way other people mind fires—constantly, and with great skill. Whatever you think I am, I retired. There's no such thing anymore. There's no one left."

The child squeezed their hand. Once. Firmly.

And then, because the universe had apparently decided the evening required finishing touches, she opened her mouth and sang the bend-of-the-seventh-note phrase again—and the fire answered.

Not dramatically. Nothing leapt, nothing flared. But the flames leaned toward her, all of them together, like wheat bending to wind, and held there, listening, until the last note faded.

Sable withdrew their hand very slowly and stood up, because some things you cannot process sitting down.

"All right," they said to the room, to the storm, to the mountain, to whatever patron saint of bad decisions had been assigned to them at birth. "All right. Here's what happens now. You sleep—you'll take the cot, I'll take the chair, don't argue, you weigh less than my pack. Tomorrow the storm breaks, and tomorrow I walk you down to Ashmoor, and we find out who sold you, or lost you, or whatever verb applies here, and I hand you off to someone whose life this actually is. Someone with a farm. Someone who sings normal songs about turnips. That is the plan. That is a *good* plan. Plans like that are why I'm still alive."

The child pulled the blanket up to her chin, settled into the cot with the boneless efficiency of the very young, and watched Sable bank the fire.

"And the song," Sable said, mostly to themselves, poking the embers harder than necessary. "We won't mention the song. Songs get people killed. Songs got an entire order killed, if we're being accurate, which I prefer not to be."

Behind them, soft and unhurried, the child began to hum again. The kitchen tune. Their mother's tune. The one that called someone home.

Sable stood with their back turned and let it wash over them, and hated how much it sounded like the front door of a house they'd burned down themselves.

* * *

Sleep did not come, which surprised no one, least of all Sable.

They sat in the chair with the sword across their knees—not for the child, who slept like a stone that had never been burdened by guilt, but for the dark beyond the windows. Fifteen years of instinct had woken up from its long nap and was pacing the room like a dog that smelled something on the wind. Storms brought wolves. Storms also brought cover for things that were not wolves.

Near midnight the clouds tore open for the space of ten heartbeats, and moonlight fell through the gap, silver and sudden, laying a bright bar across the floor.

Across the ash drawing.

Sable hadn't swept it. They should have swept it. Instead they knelt, feeling foolish and forty-one and entirely too awake, and studied it properly for the first time by honest light.

The circle. The seed-shape within. The road leading down. The *given-or-sold* glyph riding the road like a traveler.

And now, in the moonlight, Sable saw what the firelight had hidden: fine scratches around the main drawing, smaller marks, a border of tiny glyphs ringing the whole design the way a fence rings a field. Sable's lips moved silently, reading them the way you read a language you swore you'd forgotten—*thirteenth moon, thirteenth moon, thirteenth moon,* repeated over and over, patient as stitching, all the way around.

A deadline. Written by a child in fireplace ash, in a dead alphabet, on the floor of a dead woman's son.

Sable sat back on their heels. Outside, the clouds closed, and the moonlight vanished as though embarrassed to have been caught helping.

"One year," Sable said aloud, and their voice in the dark cabin sounded like a stranger's. "Whatever it is you're telling me, we have until the thirteenth moon."

On the cot, the child did not wake. But in her sleep, softly, without fear, she began to hum—and this time the tune was not the kitchen lullaby.

It was the other song. The burning one. The one their mother had died singing.

Sable reached for the sword and held it, and watched the door, and waited for morning to make things worse.
