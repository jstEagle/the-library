## 3. What the Oath Was For

Sable woke before dawn out of habit rather than necessity, which was how they had done most things for fifteen years. Habit was a reliable employee. It asked no questions and expected no wages.

The child—Wren, they had decided to call her, for the way she had appeared at the door like something small and fierce blown in on the storm—was still asleep by the hearth, curled inside Sable's spare blanket with her bare feet tucked up like a bird's. She had slept the way the exhausted sleep: utterly, suspiciously, as if sleep were a country she'd been deported from and had finally bribed her way back into.

Sable put water on to heat and tried very hard not to look at the markings.

They had spent the whole night not looking at them. This had required discipline. The marks ran up both of Wren's forearms from wrist to elbow, fine lines of silver-grey that might have been birthmarks to any other eye, arranged in rows and columns with the tidy cruelty of an accounting ledger. Any other eye. Sable's eyes were not any other eyes, and so Sable had looked at the fire instead, at the storm, at the bottom of their tea, anywhere that wasn't those arms.

But morning had come, and the tea was gone, and there was nothing left between Sable and the truth except cowardice, and cowardice, unlike habit, was a poor employee. It did shoddy work.

They knelt beside the sleeping child and took her small hand in theirs, turning the arm gently toward the grey light of the window.

The first glyph was a circle bisected by a descending line.

Sable's breath went out of them slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes somewhere far off in the house.

*The descent,* their mother had called it, tracing it once in spilled flour on the kitchen table of a safehouse that smelled of wet wool and rosemary. *First mark of the covenant, little blade. It means: what goes down must be spoken back up.* She'd laughed at her own solemnity and flicked flour on Sable's nose. Even then, even at six, Sable had understood that the joke was armor. That their mother made jokes the way other people prayed.

The second glyph was three vertical strokes crossed by a horizontal one. *The tally.* The third was a spiral broken at its crown. *The severance.*

Wren stirred but didn't wake. In her sleep she hummed two notes—the rising pair from the lullaby, *the song of the nine gates*, the one every swordspeaker's child learned before they learned their own name—and Sable sat back on their heels on the cold floorboards of their own cabin and felt fifteen years of careful, competent nothing begin, quietly and without permission, to burn.

* * *

Here is what Sable remembered, though they would have paid good coin to forget it:

The Hall of Nine Voices stood in the foothills where the Thornfells gave up their argument with the sky and surrendered to forest. It had been built before kingdoms, before crowns, back when the borders between the mortal courts and the deep places were not drawn on maps but *sung*—and the order of Swordspeakers had kept those songs. Not warriors, exactly, though they could fight. Not priests, exactly, though they kept holy things. They were speakers: people who carried the old tongue, the pre-kingdom language that stones understood and roots obeyed and the things beneath the mountains were bound by law—*their* law—to answer.

Their mother had been a Speaker of the Third Gate. Their father had died young and unremarkably, of a fever, and Sable had sometimes thought this made him the luckiest man in the family.

Because the deep places had learned, over the long centuries, a new trick. They could not take a soul by force—that law held; the old tongue still held—but they could *buy*. And mortals, being mortals, sold. A bad harvest, a gambling debt, a dying parent, a hungry winter: there was always a reason, and the Undercourt never asked for reasons, only for signatures written in glyphs upon the flesh of children too young to know what their parents' desperation tasted like.

The order's stance had been simple and unforgiving: the trades were abominations, and the Speakers would not recognize them, would not sing the bindings, would not stand aside.

And then came the night when Sable was eleven, and men came down the mountain road with Crown writs in their fists and pitch in their satchels, and the order learned that the Crown had decided mountain stability was worth more than the souls of peasant children.

Sable remembered the smell of the rosemary their mother had been bundling. Remembered her hands going still around it. Remembered the way she had said, not loudly, *Get under the floor*, and how the word *under* had been spoken in the old tongue, and the floorboards had opened for Sable like a mouth.

They remembered it all through the gap between two boards, in strips, in slices: boots. Firelight where firelight should not be. The Captain of the Wardens—a broad man with a voice like a landslide, whose name Sable had spent years refusing to remember and whose face they had never managed to forget—standing in the hall while the Speakers sang their refusal, the whole order together, the great binding-song that should have turned his soldiers' bones to lead.

It hadn't. Because he had brought a silencer, a hired thing wrapped in grey, and its counter-song ate the order's words out of the air like moths in wool. Sable had watched, through the gap, through tears they had been trained not to shed audibly, as words their mother had taught them were pulled apart mid-phrase. Watched the soldiers move in behind the silence.

Watched their mother, last of all, turn her head—impossibly, precisely—toward the floorboard gap, toward the eleven-year-old hiding in the dark beneath her feet, and speak four words in the old tongue, soft as flour:

*Live. Carry it. Speak.*

Then the world above the boards became noise, and after the noise became smoke, and after the smoke Sable climbed out of a cellar into ash and snow and a silence deeper than any silencer's, and began the long apprenticeship in becoming no one.

* * *

"Your tea's gone cold."

Sable started hard enough to nearly draw a knife they weren't carrying. Wren was sitting up, blanket around her shoulders, watching them with eyes the color of rainwater over slate. She had heard them get up, apparently, and had simply lain there letting them have their private agony in peace, which showed either remarkable courtesy or remarkable practice at waiting for adults to finish falling apart.

"I don't drink cold tea," Sable said, which was true, and also a way of saying *I've been kneeling here since before dawn staring at your arms*, which they were not going to say.

Wren tilted her head. Then, deliberately, she pushed the blanket off her arms and held them out—both wrists up, offered, the way you offer a thing you are tired of hiding.

"No," Sable said. "You don't have to—"

She shook her head once, impatient, and thrust the arms closer. There was nothing shy about it. Sable had guided smugglers through passes full of Crown Wardens and worse, and had met plenty of grown killers with less spine than this child.

So Sable took her arms again, and this time read them properly, aloud in their own head, glyph by glyph, the way their mother had taught them to read a covenant-marking: top to bottom, left to right, the descent, the tally, the severance, and then the long middle section that was not formula at all but *prose*—clauses, terms, conditions. A contract.

A soul, sold. Purchased by the Court Beneath. Payable in full upon the bearer's thirteenth moon.

"You understand this?" Sable asked stupidly, because the child couldn't answer, and asking anyway was easier than not asking.

Wren answered anyway. She tapped the descent-glyph, then pointed down—at the floor, through the floor, at the mountain itself. Tapped the tally. Held up fingers: ten, eleven, twelve. Then one finger folded down, slowly, like something wilting.

Thirteen. The thirteenth moon.

"How many passed?" Sable asked, hating the question. "How many moons since—"

Wren held up ten fingers. Folded two.

Eleven moons gone. One lunar year remaining. Two months.

Something in Sable's chest performed a maneuver best described as *falling while staying perfectly still*.

Two months. They had known the child was marked, had felt the dread since the storm-night knock, but knowing a thing in the ribs was different from knowing it in arithmetic. Two months to find knowledge that had been burned, scattered, hunted to the edge of extinction. Two months to do—what? Fight? The order had fought, and Sable had hidden under the floor while it lost. Bargain? The Undercourt bargained the way a landslide bargained.

And underneath all of it, quieter and worse: *why you?* You, who buried the language so deep you flinched when smugglers hummed. You, who chose the mountains and the fog and the comfortable anonymity of being nobody's anything. Fifteen years of arithmetic about cargo and weather and risk, and not one line of it had prepared Sable Sterling to be the last speaker of a dead covenant holding a sold child's wrist in the grey light of morning.

Wren was watching their face. She reached out—small, chapped, certain—and touched the scar across Sable's throat. The one the caravan merchants always wondered about and never asked about. The one that wasn't from any knife.

It was from the night of the fire, from a beam that had come down as Sable crawled out of the burning hall, and in the fever-weeks after, delirious in a shepherd's barn, eleven-year-old Sable had sung the healing-songs to their own ruined throat until the voice closed over wrong—not silenced, but *changed*, roughened, a sword dragged across stone where a bell used to be. The old tongue still worked through it. Barely. Like shouting through a wall.

Sable had told themselves for fifteen years that this was mercy. That a damaged instrument was a safe instrument. That the order was dead and its language deserved the grave it had been thrown in.

Wren's fingers rested on the scar, and she hummed the two rising notes again—*the nine gates, the nine gates*—and looked at Sable with an expression no child should have had occasion to learn: the expression of someone handing a burden to the only person within reach, and apologizing for it with their eyes.

"Oh, don't," Sable said, and their ruined voice cracked on it. "Don't look at me like I'm the answer. I'm a smuggler's guide. I calculate risks. Fog density, pass conditions, which guards take bribes. That's the whole of my magic."

Wren did not stop looking at them like that.

"The people who could actually help you are ash," Sable went on, and heard their own voice climbing, and hated it. "There were nine hundred Speakers when I was born. There is me, and my throat sounds like a gravel slide, and I haven't spoken a full phrase of the old tongue since—" Since ever, really. Since fragments, in nightmares, in the first terrible year, alone in caves, practicing the words just to keep from forgetting and then hating themselves for keeping them alive at all. "I hid under the floor. Do you understand? When it mattered, I was *under the floor*."

Wren considered this. Then she did something that undid Sable more thoroughly than grief or memory or fear had managed all morning.

She picked up the charred stick Sable used for marking cargo tallies, spat on the hearthstone, and wrote—in neat, careful strokes, in the old script, in a child's wobbly hand:

*YOU ARE ABOVE THE FLOOR NOW.*

Sable stared at it for a long time.

Then, entirely against their will, they laughed—a short, wrecked, astonished sound—and scrubbed a hand down their face. "Fifteen years of solitude," they said, "and the universe sends me a mute child with a better sense of timing than any general I've ever dodged."

Wren smiled. It transformed her whole face, and for a moment she looked exactly her age, and exactly like every child the order had sworn to stand between and the dark, and none of them saved, and this one here, breathing, waiting.

Purpose arrived the way pain does—not all at once, but with a specific location, and a demand to be attended to.

"All right," Sable said. "All right." They got to their feet, joints protesting, and began pulling open the chest at the foot of their bed—the false-bottomed one, the one they hadn't opened in years, that contained a knife, some coin, and a bundle of oilcloth that had once been a book. "Here's the arithmetic, then. The covenant records were kept in three places: the Hall, which is ash; the archive at Vael's Crossing, which sealed everything after the purges; and the memories of anyone who survived, which is to say—" they paused over the oilcloth bundle, not yet touching it, "—a very short list."

Wren leaned forward.

"There's a woman in Vael's Crossing," Sable said, and the words came out like teeth being pulled, each one separately and with effort. "An archivist. She knew what we were. She—" *loved me*, the sentence finished, in a voice Sable did not permit past their teeth. "She'll have access to the sealed ledgers. If any record of Wren's sale exists—if there's a clause, a flaw, a term miswritten—it'll be there."

And below the archive, older and darker roads. But that was a thought for a colder night.

Sable unwrapped the oilcloth at last. Inside lay a slim book, scorched at one corner, its cover stamped with the bisected circle of the descent. Their mother's field-book. Sable had taken it from the ashes with hands that blistered, and had not read past the first page in fifteen years.

They opened it now, to the first page, and their mother's handwriting marched across it, brisk and alive:

*If you are reading this, little blade, then the worst has happened, and I am sorry, and you are the covenant now. First lesson:*

Sable closed the book gently, as though it were sleeping.

"Wren," they said. "Pack light. We're going to need supplies, a map, and a great deal of luck, and I only know where to find the first two."

The child was already on her feet, already reaching for the blanket to fold it—of course she folded it, of course she did—and outside, far down the valley, the morning bell of Ashmoor rang thin and clear through the thawing air.

Two months. Eleven moons spent. One left.

Sable picked up their pack and their knife and the book that was heavier than any blade, and stepped out of the cabin into the white morning, taking the first step of a road they had spent half a lifetime swearing never to walk again.
