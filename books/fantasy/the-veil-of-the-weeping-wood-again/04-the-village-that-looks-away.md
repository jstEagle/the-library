## 4. The Village That Looks Away

The mule's name was Turnip, and she was the most honest creature Sable had ever known. She bit people she disliked, brayed at liars, and had once refused to carry a cargo of salted pork that turned out, three weeks later, to be something considerably less appetizing. Sable trusted her judgment over that of most magistrates.

Turnip liked Wren.

That was the first strange thing. The second was how the mountain path seemed to open for the child—no, *not* opened; Sable corrected themselves as they picked their way down through the pines. It was more that the path behaved itself. Loose stones stayed put under small bare feet. The fog, which had clung to the pass like a jealous lover all week, thinned into polite wisps wherever Wren walked, and closed up again behind them like a curtain after a performance.

"You're doing that on purpose," Sable said.

Wren looked up at them, gray eyes wide and guileless, which was itself suspicious. Guilelessness in a child who had walked out of a snowstorm barefoot and unafraid was either innocence or very good acting.

"The fog," Sable elaborated, gesturing broadly. "It's being polite to you. Fog is never polite. Fog has the manners of a tax collector."

Wren made a sound—not quite a laugh, not quite a hum—and touched two fingers to her throat, then pointed down the slope toward Ashmoor, visible now as a scatter of slate roofs and hearth-smoke in the valley below. Her meaning was plain: *Can we go there?*

"We can," Sable said. "We're going to have to, eventually. You eat like a woodpecker and I'm down to turnips, and no—" they held up a hand as Wren's face brightened, "—not the mule. Her name is Turnip. We are not eating Turnip."

Wren pressed both hands to her mouth, shoulders shaking, and this time the sound that escaped was unmistakably a giggle. Sable felt it land somewhere behind their ribs, in a room of their heart that had been swept and shuttered for fifteen years. They scowled to cover it.

"Come on, then. And listen well: in Ashmoor you say nothing. Not because what you'd say would be wrong, but because what you *are* is worth coin to certain people, and certain people drink at the Gilded Hart and talk too much when they do." Sable crouched to the child's level, adjusting the too-large coat they'd wrapped her in back at the cabin. "You're my sister's girl. Come down from Highmoor to stay while your mother's ill. Your name is Wren because it already is. If anyone asks where you learned to sing, you learned from goats. Goats are plausible. Nobody questions goat-song."

Wren considered this gravely, then nodded once, solemn as a magistrate.

"Good." Sable straightened, took Turnip's lead rope, and tried very hard not to think about how naturally lying for a child came back to them, like a language never quite forgotten.

* * *

Ashmoor smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and the river—cold stone water running off the Thorns, carrying the faint mineral tang of the deep places. Sable knew every smell the way a man knows his own hands. Fifteen years of guiding smugglers' caravans through the high passes meant fifteen years of buying supplies in Ashmoor under three different names, and Maren Cole, innkeeper of the Gilded Hart, had known all three of them were lies and charged accordingly, with a knowing look that cost extra.

"Stirling," Maren said when Sable shouldered through the door, ducking under the lintel out of habit. "Thought you'd frozen in some drift by now. Or married."

"Both seemed unlikely."

"Mm. One of them still does." Maren was a broad woman built like the bar she leaned on, gray-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be an actual nail. Her eyes went past Sable to the doorway, where Wren hovered half-hidden behind Turnip's lead rope as if a mule were adequate concealment for a child. "And who's this?"

"My sister's girl. From Highmoor. Mother's ill."

"Mm," Maren said again, in the tone of a woman filing information away rather than believing it. "Well, kitchen's got stew on and it's better than it deserves to be. Sit the little one down. I'll want a word about freight rates when you've fed her."

The common room was half-full—the early-lunch crowd of carters and one nervous wool merchant who kept glancing at the window as though the Crown might be hiding in the rain barrel. Sable steered Wren to a corner table, ordered two bowls without asking what was in them, and was performing the ritual arithmetic of supply lists—flour, lamp oil, boot grease, arrowheads, *do we need arrowheads, we always need arrowheads*—when the afternoon tilted sideways.

It happened quietly, the way disasters usually did in Ashmoor. Maren brought the stew herself instead of sending the serving girl. She set the bowls down, and then she didn't leave. She stood there wiping her hands on her apron, looking down at Wren, who was studying the stew with the cautious respect of someone who had recently eaten things from the forest floor.

"That color of eyes," Maren said.

Sable's spoon stopped moving. They hadn't even noticed they'd picked up a spoon.

"Gray," Maren went on, conversationally, terribly. "Like river ice before it breaks. There was a girl in this valley had eyes like that. Tam Vess's youngest. Little Elsbeth." She wiped her hands again, slower. "Sold, they said. Two winters past. Family fell behind on their debts to the mine factor, and one morning the factor's men came with a wagon, and the family signed something, and the girl went up the mountain road and never came down again. Tam drinks himself blind now. Her mother won't say her name aloud. Says saying it invites whoever bought her to come collect the *rest*."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the kitchen a pot lid rattled. Wren, who could not have understood more than half the words, understood enough—Sable watched it land, watched the small shoulders draw in like a closing hand—and reached across the table without looking and gripped Sable's sleeve.

Maren saw it. Of course she did. Maren missed nothing that happened in her common room except, by long practice, everything that was convenient to miss.

"She yours?" Maren asked quietly.

"No," Sable said. "She walked into a storm and knocked on my door."

"And you took her in." Not a question. An accusation shaped like a statement, or the other way around.

"She was barefoot in a snowstorm. What was I going to do, discuss it with her?"

"You could've sent her down to the reeve."

"I could've," Sable agreed. "I also could've sent her to the moon. Neither would've helped her."

Maren's jaw worked. For a moment she looked older than her years, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with hauling kegs. Then she sat down heavily on the bench opposite, uninvited, keeping her voice beneath the carters' noise.

"Listen to me, Stirling, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to pretend I never said it, same as everyone else in this valley pretends." She leaned forward. "Everyone here knows. You understand? Everyone. The mine factors buy debts that families can't pay, and the price is always a person, and the wagons always go up the mountain road, and the Crown wardens who ride through twice a year somehow never find a single thing to write down. Two winters past it was Elsbeth Vess. Last autumn it was old Hobb Rennick's grandson. Before that, the Aldery twins, and folk said *one of them*, just one, like the court up there runs a ration." Her knuckles whitened on the table edge. "And nobody says anything, because the mountain doesn't shake anymore. Hasn't shaken in a hundred years. My grandmother told me her grandmother remembered when the ground used to groan all winter and whole villages slid into the valley, and then somebody—*somebody*—made a bargain, and the shaking stopped, and the price of quiet was whatever went up that road."

Sable said nothing. Their mouth was full of ash and an old grief with new teeth. Because they knew what *somebody*. They had been raised on the name of it, trained under it, and had watched their entire order die refusing exactly this arithmetic: a mountain's stability weighed against a child's soul, and the ledger balanced in favor of the mountain.

"You're telling me this why?" Sable asked finally. "If everyone looks away?"

Maren met their eyes. "Because you're not looking away. And because someone has to tell you where the looking-away stops being safe." She glanced at Wren, who was watching them both with the terrible attentiveness of children who know the adults are talking about them. "Hedge-woman up past the mill. Odell. Old as sin and twice as crooked, but she reads moons the way I read tap debts. If that child's marked—if she's one of the *bought*—Odell will know what the marks mean and how long you've got. And Stirling—" Maren rose, gathering empty bowls that weren't hers to clear, resuming the armor of ordinary work, "—the factor's men come through Ashmoor at each quarter-turn. Checking on investments, they call it. Quarter-turn's in nine days."

She left. Sable sat very still.

* * *

They ate in silence, mostly. Wren ate the way she did everything—thoroughly, seriously, as though stew were a task entrusted to her personally—and halfway through the bowl she paused, looked at the kitchen door Maren had gone through, and then at Sable, and touched her own eyes with two fingers. Then pointed toward the mountains.

"Yes," Sable said quietly. "She knew someone. Someone like you."

Wren's brow furrowed. She set down her spoon—a child's deliberate gesture, *wait*—and began drawing on the table's dusty surface with one fingertip. A stick figure. A smaller stick figure beside it. Then lines radiating from the smaller figure's chest, and around them symbols Sable's hands knew better than their mind did: the covenant glyphs, pre-kingdom script, the writing their mother had sung into them before sleep. This particular sequence Sable recognized with a lurch. It appeared on boundary stones. On oath-blades.

It meant, roughly and inadequately translated: *what was given cannot be taken back, but what was given can be answered.*

Sable stared at the dusty glyphs until their vision blurred.

"Who taught you that?" they asked, uselessly, knowing the answer was nobody living. Knowing the child had been sold with a soul that carried the old tongue the way blood carries a parent's face.

Wren shrugged, small and helpless, and wiped the table clean with her sleeve, erasing the covenant from the dust as easily as Maren's generation had erased it from the valley's memory.

* * *

They bought supplies in the late afternoon, Sable conducting the errands with the mechanical competence of long habit while their thoughts ran elsewhere—up the mountain road, into the dark beneath it, along corridors of thought they'd boarded shut fifteen years ago. Flour. Lamp oil. Boot grease. Arrowheads, yes, obviously, they always needed arrowheads. A child's cloak, blue wool, because the one Wren had was Sable's castoff and hung off her like a sail. The cloth merchant asked if it was for a granddaughter, and Sable heard themselves say "daughter" before any decision was made, and spent the next three shops pretending they hadn't.

The lie of convenience, they told themselves. Nothing more.

By dusk the light was failing and the Gilded Hart's windows glowed amber onto the muddy street, and Sable was loading Turnip outside the stable when they felt it: the specific quality of attention that meant *watched*. Not the idle curiosity of villagers. Something with intent behind it.

Across the lane, in the shadow between the smithy and the tithe barn, two figures stood talking. Factor's men, by their cut—good boots, bad manners, the mine factor's colors stitched discreetly at the collar. One held a ledger book. The other was looking directly at Wren, who stood in the stable doorway with the new blue cloak clutched around her, and the man's expression was not curiosity.

It was inventory.

Sable finished cinching the load with steady hands. Walked around to the stable door. Put their body between the man's gaze and the child, casually, the way you'd step in front of a draft.

Inside, Wren was humming. Low, absent, the melody she'd arrived with—the melody Sable's mother had sung, the melody that had no business surviving anywhere but in the sealed dead mouths of the Swordspeaker order. And as the last light left the street, every lantern flame in the Gilded Hart's windows bent toward the sound, gently, like flowers toward sun.

The factor's man with the ledger stopped talking mid-word.

He stared at the windows. He stared at the stable door. He reached for the ledger.

Sable swung up into the saddle, pulled Wren up behind them, and rode out of Ashmoor at a pace carefully calibrated to look unhurried, while behind them the man with the ledger was already walking, fast, toward the house where the mine factor kept his correspondence and, Sable would have wagered Turnip's life on it, a pigeon coop.

Nine days until quarter-turn, Maren had said.

Make that considerably fewer.

There was one person in the valley who might know what the child's marks meant and how many moons remained—one crooked, ancient hedge-witch past the mill whom Maren had named with the reluctance of a woman handing over a lit fuse. Sable turned Turnip's head north along the mill road, into the rising dark, and rode.

Behind them, faint on the wind, Wren was still humming—and far above, past the cloud-wrack, the moon was already climbing, thin as a paring knife, counting down.
