## 5. Thirteen Moons

Odell had lived at the crook of the Mirefen road for forty-one years, long enough to know that trouble arrived in one of three ways: on horseback, on a bier, or on foot pretending to be nothing at all. The tall figure trudging up her lane through the drizzle was unmistakably the third kind.

She watched from her window, hands wrapped around a mug of nettle tea gone cold an hour ago, and took inventory the way she took inventory of everything—slowly, and with opinions.

A smuggler's guide, by the boots. Mountain-born, by the way they walked the mud like it owed them money. A sword under that oilskin, worn old-style across the back, which meant either a very stupid bandit or a very careful ghost. And slung against their chest, wrapped in a wool blanket with only its eyes showing, a child.

Children, Odell noted, did not ride against a stranger's chest like that unless both parties had agreed to the arrangement somewhere between desperation and trust. That was rarer than hen's teeth in this valley.

"Well then," she said to her cat, who was pretending to be a loaf of bread on the windowsill. "Looks like the mountain's finally spat something out."

The cat declined to comment. Cats always knew more than they said. In that respect, Odell considered them colleagues.

* * *

She opened the door before the knock came, because waiting for knocks was for people with better knees.

"Before you say anything," she said, "I already know you're not here for love potions, wart cures, or the good death. Nobody walks four hours off the high passes in weather like this for anything less than the kind of trouble I charge extra for."

The guide pulled back their hood. A woman—or close enough to it—with grey coming in early at the temples and a scar across her throat like a signature someone had tried to scribble out. Her eyes went around Odell's yard with a soldier's habit: the drying racks, the beehives, the goat, the goat's profound indifference.

"Odell," the woman said. "Of the Mirefen crossing."

"Everyone says it like that. Like I'm a crossroads and not a person." Odell stepped aside. "In, before the rain gets ideas. And mind the third floorboard, it bites."

Inside smelled of rosemary, tallow, and forty years of steeped things. Odell pointed the woman into a chair, took the bundle from her arms without asking—the guide stiffened, then let go anyway, which told Odell a great deal—and unwrapped the child by the fire.

The girl looked up at her. And Odell, who had read fortunes in tea leaves, entrails, cloud-shapes, and once, memorably, the arrangement of a spilled sack of turnips, felt every one of her reading-habits go quiet at once.

Because the child's eyes were wrong. Not ugly-wrong. Old-wrong. Eyes like well-water: clear all the way down until suddenly they weren't, until you were looking at something that had been sitting in the dark a very long time and had learned patience there.

"Name?" Odell asked the child directly, because grown folk always answered for children and children always resented it, rightly.

The girl's mouth worked. Nothing came out. She frowned at her own throat as if it were a door locked from the other side, then reached out and took Odell's hand—small fingers, chapped from cold—and traced a shape on her palm.

Three lines, a circle broken at the crown, a downward stroke.

Odell did not flinch. Forty-one years at a crossroads taught a woman to keep her face like a locked pantry. But inside, where she kept her real thoughts, something went very still.

"That's not a word," she said quietly. "That's a ledger-mark. That's a *debt-mark*."

The guide stood up so fast the chair skidded. "You know it."

"I know what I know." Odell set the child's hand gently down and turned. "And I know you know it too, swordspeaker, so don't stand there looking at me like I'm the strange one. Sit down. Both of you. There's barley soup and it's better than it looks, which is fortunate, because it does not look like much."

* * *

Over soup—it was better than it looked; Odell had standards even in humility—the story came out in pieces. Sable Sterling, though she gave the name reluctantly, like paying out coin. A cabin in the Thornfells. A storm. A barefoot child at the door humming a tune that had no business surviving anywhere but in the memory of dead women.

"The order's been ash fifteen years," Sable said. "Whatever wrote that mark on her is older than the ash."

"Older than the kingdoms, if we're being honest, and I'm always honest, it's cheaper than lying." Odell ladled herself another bowl. "Now. The important question isn't what she is. The important question is *when*. Debts like that have terms, and terms have moons attached. So we'll do this properly."

She cleared the table with a sweep of her arm that scattered crumbs into the territory of the cat—who had appeared, as cats do, precisely when food ceased to be likely—and laid down a square of black cloth. Upon it, from a drawstring pouch, she poured her tools: river stones, a knucklebone from something Odell declined to name, a bent iron nail, and a disc of horn polished smooth as ice.

"Sit," she told the child. Wren—Sable had given the name, said it soft, the way you handle a thing you're afraid of breaking—"sit there, little one. You don't need to do anything. Just be, which I suspect you're already expert at."

Wren climbed onto the bench. The fire caught her ruined eyes and made lanterns of them, and Odell thought, not for the first time in her long career, that the world kept doing things to children that would make grown soldiers weep if they ever truly looked.

"Right," she muttered. "Let's see what the sky's signed."

* * *

The reading took the shape readings take: partly craft, partly luck, partly the reader knowing which questions not to ask aloud.

She cast the stones. They fell in the pattern she'd seen perhaps four times in her life and hoped never to see again—a ring, closed upon itself, with the horn-disc caught inside it like a fly in amber.

"A binding ring," she said, mostly to herself. Then, because the swordspeaker was leaning forward like a drawn bow: "The moon's waxing now. Nine days to full, then it wanes, then dark, then it comes round again. Twelve turns of that, start to finish. On the thirteenth—" She tapped the horn-disc, sealed in its ring of stone. "On the thirteenth full moon, whatever holds her soul downstairs finishes signing for it. After that, she could walk out of that court with her body whole and her name gone. She'd breathe and eat and sleep and never once be *in there* anymore. I've heard tell of souls kept so long they forget they ever had hands."

The silence afterward had weight. Even the fire seemed to burn more carefully.

"One year," Sable said. Her voice came out flat and level, the voice of someone counting instead of feeling, and Odell recognized it because she used the same voice at funerals. "One lunar year."

"One lunar year, minus nine days, if we're precise, and I'm always precise, it's cheaper than hoping." Odell gathered the stones, then paused, thumb resting on the horn-disc. "There's more. There usually is. The bad news travels in pairs, like geese."

She turned the disc over. On its underside, faint as breath on glass, the surface had clouded—and in the clouding, shapes. Roots. Great branching roots, descending.

"This isn't my doing," she said, frowning. "Horn doesn't fog. Unless—" she glanced at Wren, whose small hand had drifted toward the table without her seeming to notice, "—unless something nearby is *very interested* in the subject."

The root-shapes sharpened. Branched. And along their lengths, tiny marks appeared—not etched, more like remembered—nodes and knots in the wood-grain.

"Doors," Sable breathed. "Those are doors. Root-doors."

"Are they." Odell studied the child, who was staring at the disc with an expression no thirteenth-year-old should own: recognition, and homesickness, and underneath both, a thread of fear thin as spider silk. "Then it seems your young lady knows the way to the front entrance better than any living mapmaker. Which raises the question of how, and I'll thank you not to answer that tonight, because some answers want daylight around them."

She wrapped the disc in linen and pushed it across the table. "Take it. It wants to go with her, and objects that want things are best obliged promptly."

* * *

Later—much later, after Wren had fallen asleep in the good chair by the fire with the blanket pulled to her chin and the cat draped over her feet like a sentry who'd chosen his post—Odell walked Sable out to the edge of the herb garden, where the rain had quit and the clouds were shredding to show a fat half-moon.

They stood in the wet grass and said nothing for a while. Odell found she liked the woman's silences; they weren't empty ones.

"You knew my mother's people," Sable said at last.

"I knew a woman came through here twenty-odd years back, singing songs in a language that made my bees settle and my goat sit down, which believe me is a miracle worth writing home about. She bought yarrow and salt and asked me questions about the mountain roads. I didn't ask hers. Crossroads etiquette." Odell shrugged. "She had your chin."

Something moved through Sable's face—brief, quickly shuttered, like a lamp behind a curtain. "She died with the rest."

"Mm. And yet the songs live. In a mute child who walked out of a storm to find the one person left who'd recognize them." Odell tipped her head back at the moon. "I'm an old woman and I don't believe in coincidence. Coincidence is just providence wearing plain clothes."

"What do you believe in, then?"

"Deadlines." She said it cheerfully, and meant it grimly. "Hope's a fine crop but it rots fast without a fence around it. So here's your fence, swordspeaker: twelve moons and nine days. Spend none of them standing still. The court below deals in truths spoken aloud—I remember that much from the old stories, and old stories about the Undercourt are the only kind that ever checked out. You'll need truths. Yours, most like. And those are heavier to carry than any sword."

Sable looked at her. "You talk like someone who's bargained with them before."

Odell smiled, and let the smile do what smiles do best, which is end conversations. "I talk like someone who sells herbs at a crossroads and minds her own beeswax. Go get some sleep. You can have the loft. Mind the ladder, it bites too—everything in my house has teeth except me, and I'm told I bit once, in '61, but there were no witnesses."

* * *

But she did not sleep. Old women rarely do when the moon's doing something interesting.

Instead Odell sat by the banked fire with a cup of tea that had actually managed to stay warm, and watched the child sleep, and thought about fences and deadlines and the price of hope.

The girl murmured in her sleep. Not words—something older, a fragment of melody, two notes and a fall, over and over, like a bell practicing.

And in the hearth's last coals, Odell saw it again: the shape of roots going down, and down, and further down than any tree had any business growing. Doors along the way. And at the bottom, at the deepest knot of all, something that was not a door at all but a *mouth*, patient and open, with thirteen moons left to chew.

"Well," she said softly, to the coals, to the cat, to the sleeping child, to nobody. "One year. Best not waste a week of it."

Come morning she would give Sable the rest—the dried stores, the silverweed for wounds, the blunt warning about Crown Wardens on the lowland roads. Come morning she would press a twist of paper into Wren's hand containing a sprig of rowan and instructions the child couldn't read but would somehow understand.

But for now, in the dark, with the moon climbing past her window like a debt collector checking his ledger, Odell allowed herself one honest moment. She looked at the sleeping girl—at the well-water eyes, at the small fist curled around nothing—and she said, very quietly, in a language she had sworn off speaking for thirty years, three words of blessing from the old tongue.

The fire flared green for half a heartbeat.

From upstairs came the sound of Sable Sterling sitting bolt upright in the loft.

Odell sipped her tea and waited for the footsteps on the ladder, smiling the smile of a woman who had just confirmed something she'd suspected since the child walked in—and who knew, now, exactly which road this whole sorry, hopeful business was going to have to take.

The road to Vael's Crossing, and everything waiting on it.
