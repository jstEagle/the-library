## 6. The Road I Swore Off

Wren slept curled against my pack like a cat who had decided, after careful deliberation, that I was furniture she approved of.

I watched her from the doorway of the cabin while the last of the moonlight drained out of the sky. Thirteen moons. Odell's words had lodged themselves under my ribs the way a splinter lodges itself—small, ignorable at first, and then throbbing every time I moved. One lunar year before the girl's soul sank into the Undercourt's ledger like a stone into a well, and no amount of pulling would bring it back up.

Which meant I could not stay in the Thornfells, being no one. Being no one was a luxury I had purchased with fifteen years of other people's silence, and it turned out silence had a price too.

I packed the way I always packed: fast, ruthless, everything essential and nothing beloved. Flint, dried meat, the good rope, the map fragment Odell had pressed into my hands with fingers like winter roots. The sword went on last. It always did. Some habits are just grief wearing work clothes.

"Where are we going?" Wren signed when she woke, using the old glyphs. *We* now. She'd started saying *we* on the second day, and I hadn't corrected her because correcting her would have meant admitting how much I liked it.

"Lowlands," I said aloud, then repeated it in glyph so she'd learn both. "There's someone I have to find."

She studied me with those eyes Maren had recognized—the eyes of a girl sold by her own blood two winters past—and then she signed something I didn't expect: *The man with the broken blade.*

My hands went still. "How do you know about him?"

She shrugged, the magnificent shrug of an eleven-year-old who knows exactly how unsettling she is being. Then she tapped her chest, touched her ear, and pointed east, toward the lowlands, toward the roads I had spent fifteen years not walking.

*The wood told you,* I signed.

She nodded once, matter-of-fact, and shouldered her own small pack, which she had apparently already packed herself. Of course she had. Children who survive being sold to a court beneath a mountain do not wait for adults to organize their departures.

I banked the fire, looked at the cabin that had been my grave for fifteen years, and felt nothing so simple as regret. Regret implies you'd choose differently. I only knew I was choosing differently now, and that the road ahead ran through country where my face might as well have been printed on wanted bills.

* * *

The Thornfells let go of us grudgingly, three days of switchbacks and scree before the pines gave way to oak and the air went soft and lowland-heavy. I took the drover's paths where I could, the ones sheep had worn into the hillsides, invisible from the proper roads. It was slower. Slow was fine. Slow was survival.

But Ashmoor sat in a valley, and valleys funnel you. By the fifth day we were forced onto the King's Road itself—a broad, rutted artery of commerce and surveillance—and I felt the change in my skin before I saw anything wrong. Fifteen years of smuggling teaches you to read a road the way a physician reads a pulse. This one had a fever.

No other travelers. That was the first sign. A King's Road in harvest season should have been thick with carts and peddlers and children chasing each other between wheels. Instead: emptiness, and the particular emptiness of a stage swept clean before a performance.

"Off the road," I murmured, and Wren was moving before I finished signing it, slipping into the bracken with the eerie soundlessness she'd brought down out of the mountains.

They came around the bend four minutes later—I counted, because counting keeps your hands from shaking. Six Crown Wardens in their slate-grey coats, riding in that loose stagger formation they teach you precisely so no single arrow can take them all. Their horses were shod for hard travel. Their eyes did the thing Warden eyes do, sweeping the verges, reading the land like tax collectors read ledgers.

At the head rode a woman with a scar bisecting her left eyebrow, and I knew her.

Seren Vail. She'd been a raw lieutenant the year the order burned. She'd stood outside the swordspeaker compound at Duskwatch with a writ in her hand and something like apology in her face, and when the killing started she had not joined it—but she had not stopped it either. I remembered her more clearly than I remembered most of the dead, which is its own kind of shame.

I lay flat in the bracken with thorns against my cheek and watched six riders pass close enough that I could smell horse sweat and leather oil, and I understood two things simultaneously.

First: they weren't patrolling. They were hunting. Wardens on patrol spread out; these moved like hawks that had seen movement in the grass.

Second: sooner or later, on this road or another, Seren Vail was going to find what she was looking for. Because what she was looking for was me.

Wren's hand found mine in the dark of the bracken. Her grip was steady. Steadier than mine.

*We go around,* I signed. *Cross-country.*

*You know this land?*

*No.*

Her mouth curved, just slightly. *Good,* she signed. *Better story.*

Gods preserve me from children raised by haunted woods.

* * *

Three days cross-country bought us distance but not safety, because the Crown doesn't need roads to hunt you—it needs informants, and informants grow everywhere, like nettles. We passed through hamlets where doors closed as we approached and windows filled with faces that watched us leave. In one village a boy of maybe nine stared at the wrapped hilt of my sword where it showed above my pack, and his mother yanked him indoors hard enough to rattle the shutter.

"They remember," Wren signed that night as we ate cold beans in a hollow under a fallen oak. She said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse.

"People remember what they're paid to remember."

"You don't believe that." She signed it slowly, deliberately, the way you'd correct a child's grammar. "You believe they're afraid. Fear is different. Fear you can forgive."

"Fear kept them silent when the order died."

"Yes," she agreed, maddeningly. "And?"

I had no answer for that, so I chewed my beans, and she hummed a fragment of the old tongue under her breath—a lullaby, half-remembered, the same melody my mother had sung over a cradle in Duskwatch before the world ended—and I sat there in the dark with my heart doing something complicated and unhelpful.

That night I dreamed of fire, which was standard. But in the dream the fire had a voice, and the voice used my name, and when I woke the stars were wrong overhead and Wren was sitting bolt upright, staring east.

*Riders,* she signed. *Many. Behind.*

"How many is many?" I asked, already rolling the bedroll.

She held up both hands, opened all ten fingers, closed them, opened them again. Twenty. Twenty riders couldn't be behind us unless they'd been sweeping the hills in a net, and a net means they knew roughly where we were, and knowing roughly where we were meant someone had talked.

I thought of the boy and the wrapped hilt. I thought of closed doors.

"Run," I told her aloud and in glyph, and we ran.

* * *

We ran through grey dawn and gold morning, through bramble and birch, along a streambed whose cold water hid our scent if scent mattered to anyone but dogs, and it did matter, because by midday I could hear the dogs. Not close. Not far. The terrible arithmetic of pursuit is that distance is a currency and it spends faster than you think.

Wren kept pace with a stamina that should have embarrassed me, a child of eleven running on half-rations and haunted-wood stubbornness, and I hated myself a little for the pride I felt watching her.

By late afternoon the streambed climbed into rocky ground, and rocky ground became cliffs, and the cliffs funneled us into a defile I recognized from a map I'd studied thirty years ago and a life I'd lived before that: Greywater Pass, a throat of stone between two ridges, the only crossing for twenty miles.

A trap, if they were competent. And Seren Vail had been competent at twenty-two.

I crouched at the mouth of the pass with Wren pressed against my side and listened. Wind. Water somewhere below. And faint, downstream and closing: hoofbeats, plural, unhurried. They didn't need to hurry. They knew the geography better than I remembered it, and worse—they knew I'd remember it too. They were herding me into the pass the way you herd anything into a throat.

Options, Sable. Enumerate them. Smuggler's arithmetic, chapter one of the trade.

One: fight. Six to twenty odds depending on which number was true, on open stone, with a child beside me. No.

Two: climb. The ridges were shear rock slick with lichen. Maybe for me. Not with Wren.

Three—

There was no three. There never is, until there is, and the three that exists is always the one you swore off.

Because there was a fourth option, and it had been sitting in my chest for fifteen years like a coal banked under ash, forbidden by oath and prudence and the simple animal desire to keep breathing. Sword-speech. The old tongue spoken through steel—not the language Wren hummed, but its martial twin, the covenant's true voice, the words that made a blade an argument the world had to answer.

Using it was a beacon. Using it was a signature written across the sky: *here, here, the last swordspeaker, come and collect.* Every Warden within fifty miles would feel it. Every sympathetic ear too. It would end my life as a smuggler, as a ghost, as no one.

Fifteen years of being no one. And here was the bill coming due, with interest.

The hoofbeats entered the pass behind us. Ahead, around the shoulder of rock, rode the outriders—four of them, cutting off the exit, and among them a slate-grey coat and a scarred eyebrow. Seren Vail herself. They had split their force. Competent. Cursed competent.

"Sable Sterling!" Vail's voice rang off the stone, amplified by some Warden trick-horn or just the acoustics of a place shaped like a shout. "In the name of the Crown, stand forth! You're wanted for questioning, nothing more!"

Nothing more. The order had been wanted for questioning too, right up until the questioning involved fire.

I looked down at Wren. She looked up at me, and there was no fear in her face, which frightened me more than the riders did. She signed one word, slow and clear:

*Choose.*

And gods help me, the past refused to stay buried any longer. It rose up in me like water finding its old channel—my mother's cadence, Master Ellery's corrections, the drill-yard liturgy I'd recited ten thousand times until the words wore grooves in my soul. My hand found the wrappings on the hilt and unwound them, and the bare steel came out singing a note I hadn't heard since Duskwatch burned.

*Sable.* Wren again, urgent now. *What are you doing?*

"What I swore off," I said.

I laid two fingers along the flat of the blade, drew breath the old way—from the belly, from the ground, from the deep places the covenant claimed kinship with—and spoke.

Not shouted. Spoken. The old tongue has no shouting; shouting is for languages that can lose. The words left my mouth and entered the steel and the steel did what steel does when a swordspeaker asks it a question in earnest: it answered. The sound wasn't loud so much as *total*, filling the pass wall to wall, a chord of voice and metal that I felt in my teeth and my sternum and the soles of my boots.

Every horse in earshot screamed and broke. The outriders' mounts bolted sideways, dumping two men into the scree; the pursuing column behind us dissolved into a chaos of rearing animals and dropped lances. And the words themselves—I chose them carefully, because the old tongue wastes nothing—were a command of *averting*: *turn aside, forget this throat, seek elsewhere*. Not compulsion, nothing so crude. A suggestion delivered with the authority of a language older than the Crown's oldest claim. Men's minds slid off it like rain off slate.

Through the ringing aftermath I watched Seren Vail fight her horse back under control, saw her stare down the pass at me—at the bare blade, at the posture she must have seen in old training diagrams, in evidence files, in nightmares. Her face went through several expressions and settled on something bleak and unsurprised, as if she'd known for years this meeting would happen and had dreaded mostly the paperwork.

"The last swordspeaker," she called, and there was no triumph in it. Only exhaustion. "Fifteen years, Sterling. We said you were dead. Why couldn't you let us keep saying it?"

I didn't answer. Answers cost. I sheathed the blade, wound the wrappings back around the hilt with hands that were not quite steady, and took Wren's hand, and we went up the goat-track on the north wall while the Wardens' horses were still calming, into country even I didn't know.

Behind us, faint but persistent, came the sound of a signal horn. Three notes, rising. The call that meant: *she lives, she speaks, alert the lowlands.*

So much for being no one.

* * *

We walked most of the night, putting miles between ourselves and the echo of that horn, and camped at last in a hollow of gorse where the wind couldn't find us. Wren fell asleep mid-sign, her hands dropping mid-word like a clock winding down, and I sat watch over her and felt the old tongue humming in my chest like a struck bell that wouldn't finish fading.

It was done. I had spoken. Somewhere in the lowlands, in a pit arena stinking of sweat and spilled ale, a drunk with ruined knuckles and the finest duelist's hands the order ever produced might already have heard the rumor traveling toward him like weather.

*Corvane,* I thought, and the name tasted like ash and like home.

I checked the map fragment Odell had given me, turning it toward the starlight. Two days south, if the roads allowed—which they wouldn't, but roads rarely do—and then the city of Vael's Crossing after that, and after *that*, a debt fifteen years overdue, wearing the face of Ilsabet and carrying keys to sealed archives.

One catastrophe at a time.

Beside me, asleep, Wren's hands twitched through fragments of signs, dreaming in the old tongue. And far to the south, three rising notes still hanging in the air like smoke, the whole weight of the Crown began, slowly, to turn its attention toward two figures walking through the dark.

I pulled my cloak over us both and did not sleep.
