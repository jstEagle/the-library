## 7. Ashes of the Second Blade

The pit at Gravenhollow smelled of wet straw, old blood, and the particular despair of men who had bet their boots on Corvane Duskwater.

He understood the last part intimately, because he was one of them. He'd bet his own boots twice this month. Currently he was wearing a dead man's sandals, which was the kind of detail that would have horrified the man he used to be—the finest duelist the Swordspeakers ever fielded, they used to say, the blade that sang. The man he used to be had owned seven pairs of boots and an opinion about all of them.

The man he was now spat into the straw and waited for the gate to open.

Across the pit, his opponent was a farmboy built like a grain silo, swinging a woodcutter's axe with more enthusiasm than geometry. Corvane read him in three breaths: weight too far forward, guard drifting left every time he breathed out, terrified of being hit in the face. Everyone was terrified of being hit in the face. It was the one universal truth Corvane had carried out of the massacre at Thornfell Pass, besides the guilt.

The gate rattled up. The crowd—maybe sixty souls, most of them already drunk enough to be generous with their hatred—began to chant something that was almost his name.

"COR-VANE. COR-VANE."

It wasn't affection. In Gravenhollow they chanted for whoever they'd bet against, on the theory that curses were cheaper than coin. He'd been the house favorite to lose for eleven straight fights now, and he kept obliging them, because losing paid the same as winning here and losing didn't require him to be anything in particular afterward.

Except tonight.

Tonight there was a purse. A real one—a merchant caravan had come through two days back, fat and nervous, and its master had put silver on the board for any fighter who could last five minutes against his hired blade. Five minutes. Corvane had done the arithmetic with the clarity available only to men three cups past sobriety: five minutes of not-dying equaled a room with a door that locked, a bottle of anything, and another week of not thinking about a mountain pass where forty-one people who loved him had died screaming his name.

*Corvane! Corvane, hold the line!* That had been Sable Sterling's voice, fifteen years ago, bright as a struck bell. *Hold the line, I'll get the children out.*

And he had held it. He had held it so beautifully that the histories, if anyone wrote histories anymore, could have carved it in stone. He held it while the Wardens' crossbows did their work, and then he ran, because holding lines is only noble until you're the last one on your side of them.

The silo-boy came on with a roar.

Corvane didn't draw his sword. He never drew his sword anymore; he fought with a length of iron pipe wrapped in leather, because a sword in his hands felt like blasphemy, or maybe like prayer, and he couldn't afford either. He sidestepped the first axe swing—it whistled past his ear with real conviction—and tapped the boy behind the knee, gently, almost apologetically. The silo went down into the straw with a sound like a dropped church bell.

"Sorry," Corvane said, meaning it.

The boy got up angrier. They always did. The second exchange lasted four seconds and ended with the axe in the straw and the boy facedown beside it, wheezing, while Corvane stood over him feeling like the world's largest fraud.

Then the merchant's hired blade stepped through the far gate, and Corvane's evening stopped being funny.

She was good. He could tell before she finished her first step—good in the way that made his chest ache, balanced like water finding its level, sword moving like it had opinions of its own. She looked at him across the pit and smiled the smile of someone who had been told to make it quick.

"Five minutes," she said. "Your money or your ribs, old man."

"Old man," Corvane repeated. "I'm forty-one."

"You're forty-one *and* you fight in a straw pit in sandals." She rolled her shoulders. "In the lowlands we'd call that ancient."

"In the lowlands," Corvane said, "they also call that stew 'cuisine,' so I'll take your geography under advisement."

The crowd laughed. Even the bookmakers laughed. And then she came at him, and nobody laughed, because she was fast the way weather is fast.

Corvane gave ground. He gave a lot of ground—he was giving ground professionally, artistically, the way a river gives ground to a valley—but somewhere around the ninety-second mark his body remembered things his heart had tried very hard to bury. His feet found angles. His off hand started lying to her about where his weight was. The pipe in his grip began to move less like a pipe and more like what it secretly wanted to be.

She noticed. Good fighters notice. Her smile faded into something sharper and more honest, and she said, half-breathless, "Where did you train?"

"A monastery," Corvane lied. "We studied silence and gardening."

"You're a *Swordspeaker*."

"I'm a drunk with a pipe."

"You're—" She cut off, because he'd finally done it: without deciding to, without permitting himself, he had drawn the line of a form so old that the words for it had died with everyone else who knew them. *Third Turning, River Stance.* His body spoke it fluently even though his mouth had sworn off the language. Her blade went wide, and the opening was there, clean as a hymnal, one strike from ending the fight and taking the purse—

Corvane turned the strike aside instead. Piped her flatly on the wrist until her sword rang into the straw, and put his free hand up.

"That's five minutes," he said. "I believe I win by surviving."

The crowd booed magnificently. The bookmaker tore his hat in half, which Corvane felt was excessive; hats are expensive. The merchant's champion stared at him with an expression he recognized, because he wore it himself in mirrors: the look of someone meeting a ghost wearing a stranger's clothes.

*You're a Swordspeaker.* Four words, and they followed him out of the pit like a debt collector.

* * *

He drank in the Broken Antler, which was the finest establishment in Gravenhollow in the sense that it was the only establishment in Gravenhollow. He drank in the corner, back to the wall, because some habits outlive their reasons. He was three cups into forgetting the word "Swordspeaker" when the door opened and the cold came in wearing a familiar shape.

He knew the walk before he saw the face. Fifteen years, and he still knew it—that low, economical stride, weight silent, eyes doing the room in quarters. Sable Sterling had once taught a class on situational awareness to forty-one students, and Corvane had spent the entire lesson watching how they moved instead of listening, which was either his greatest failure as a student or his greatest success, depending on the day.

They hadn't changed much. Older, harder around the mouth, a scar across the throat he didn't remember giving them cause to earn. Their hair was going grey at the temples in a way that was probably fashionable somewhere but looked, on Sable, purely accidental.

What had changed was the child.

A girl, perhaps eleven, small and watchful, clutching Sable's sleeve with both hands. Barefoot, in a borrowed coat three sizes too big, hair like storm-cloud wool. She looked at the tavern, at the drunks, at the pig gnawing a turnip by the hearth, with the serene disapproval of a duchess inspecting a hovel.

Corvane put his cup down very slowly.

"No," he said, to no one, to the universe.

Sable's gaze found him anyway, the way it always had, and crossed the room in four strides.

"Hello, Corvane."

"No," he said again, with more force. "Absolutely not. Whatever this is, whatever you think I can—no. I'm a pit fighter. I fight farmers. Last week I lost to a farmer."

"You lost to Brannick on purpose. He has six children."

"He cheats at cards. It's practically charity." Corvane gestured at the bench across from him, because his body was a traitor and had already decided to let them sit. "Sit down before you make a scene. You're making a scene. You always make scenes. You just do them quietly so no one notices."

Sable sat. Up close they smelled of pine smoke and road dust, and there was a tiredness around their eyes that went deeper than travel. "You look well."

"I look like a man who sleeps in stables."

"You always slept in stables."

"That was a *choice*, Sable. There's a difference between choosing simplicity and falling into it like a hole."

The girl tugged Sable's sleeve and signed something—quick, fluid shapes in the air, and Corvane's stomach dropped through the floorboards, because he knew those shapes. He'd known them at nine years old, sitting at his mother's knee, learning the covenant glyphs before he learned his letters. *Where are we? Why does it smell like this?*

"She doesn't speak?" he asked.

"Not in any tongue the lowlands use."

"And you brought her to *Gravenhollow*."

"I brought her everywhere. We've been walking for weeks." Sable leaned forward, and their voice dropped into the register they'd used for battle briefings, the one that made even the order's veterans sit straighter. "Her name is Wren. Two winters ago her family sold her soul to the Undercourt beneath the Thornfells. She has until her thirteenth moon before the binding goes permanent. I'm gathering what's left of us to go down and take it back."

Corvane laughed. It came out wrong—cracked in the middle, like cheap pottery. "Take it back. From the Undercourt. With what army? You, me, and—" He stopped. Looked at the girl. She was watching him with those enormous grave eyes, and there was something in them that made his skin prickle, some oldness, like looking into a well and realizing the bottom was farther than the light suggested.

"With whatever's left of the order," Sable said. "Which currently consists of me, and you, if you say yes."

"I'm not saying yes."

"Corvane—"

"No. Listen to me." He heard his own voice climb and hated it, lowered it, gripped the table hard enough that the wood complained. "Fifteen years ago you asked me to hold a line, and I held it, and do you know what I held it *with*? Forty-one graves. Every person who ever believed in us died in that pass because we thought our oaths meant something to the world. The world disagreed. The world sent crossbows. So I took my oath, and my sword-speech, and everything else, and I buried it in the deepest hole I could find, and I have been *very carefully not digging it up* for fifteen years. Do not ask me to dig."

The tavern noise went on around them—dice, argument, the pig's contented crunching—but at their table there was a silence with weight to it, the old battlefield silence, the kind that waits to see who breaks first.

Sable didn't break. They never broke first. But they didn't push either, which was worse, because it left room, and room was where Corvane kept all the worst parts of himself.

"It was my fault too," Sable said quietly. "I got the children out and I vanished. I let the order die believing I'd abandoned it. Maybe I did. But Wren didn't choose any of this, and she's going to spend eternity singing in a lantern under a mountain unless someone who remembers the old ways walks down there and says otherwise." They paused. "I'm not asking you to hold a line, Corvane. I'm asking you to come dig."

"I said don't—"

And then the girl sang.

It was one note first, low and round, a sound like a stone dropped into deep water. Then a second note above it, a third, weaving, and the language arrived inside the melody the way rivers arrive inside rain—sudden, inevitable, everywhere at once. Old tongue. True tongue. The covenant speech, the words-before-words, the language Sable's mother had sung and every swordspeaker had spoken when their blades needed telling what to do. Corvane hadn't heard it aloud in fifteen years. He had told himself he'd forgotten it.

His body informed him otherwise. Every scar he owned lit up like a struck match. His hands remembered their shapes. His throat ached with words he couldn't say, wouldn't say, and the song went on—small, clear, fearless in a den of drunks and dice—and the whole tavern went quiet without knowing why, even the pig.

She was singing the *Lament for the Watch*, he realized. The mourning-song. The one they sang for fallen brothers and sisters, the one that named each of the dead by their true-name so the world would remember them properly.

She reached the verse for the second blade. For him. *Corvane Duskwater, who held the line, whose song the stones remember.*

He didn't remember standing. He didn't remember crossing the room. But he was kneeling in front of her, and his face was doing something humiliating, something wet, and his hands were shaking so badly he had to press them flat against his thighs to make them stop, and the girl—Wren—looked down at him with those old, old eyes and kept singing until the verse was done, gentle as someone closing a door.

The silence afterward was enormous.

"Well," Corvane managed, after several failed attempts. His voice came out sanded rough. "That's cheating."

Sable said nothing. They were smiling, faintly, the way dawn smiles—barely, but unmistakably.

"That's *cheating*, bringing a child in here to sing the Lament. There should be rules." He wiped his face with his sleeve, discovered the sleeve was filthy, and decided he deserved that. "Fifteen years I built that hole. Fifteen years of careful, disciplined, *architectural* forgetting, and she kicks it over in thirty seconds with one song."

"Are you saying yes?"

"I'm saying—" He stopped. Looked at Wren. She was watching him, and slowly, deliberately, she raised one small hand and signed the old greeting, the dawn-sign, the one that meant *I see you; stand with me.*

Nobody had signed that at him since Thornfell. Nobody living.

Corvane Duskwater, finest duelist of a murdered order, drunk in a stable-smelling tavern in sandals, took a long shuddering breath and let fifteen years exhale out of him all at once.

"Yes," he said. "Gods help us all, yes. I'll need a real sword. And boots. If I'm going to die in the Undercourt, I refuse to do it in sandals, it's undignified, the histories will laugh."

"There are no histories," Sable said. "No one writes them anymore."

"Then I'll dictate one on the way down. Chapter one: Corvane, devastatingly handsome despite everything." He pushed himself up, swayed, found his balance—the old balance, the water-level balance—and something in his chest unclenched for the first time since the pass. "Where next? Who else is left?"

"One more," Sable said, and Corvane watched something complicated move behind their eyes, something that had nothing to do with war and everything to do with wounds. "In Vael's Crossing. An archivist. Court-trained. Her name is Ilsabet Veyne."

The name landed in the quiet like a coin down a well, and Corvane—who had spent fifteen years perfecting the art of noticing nothing—noticed everything at once.

"Ah," he said softly. "*That* Ilsabet."

"We leave at dawn."

"Of course we do. Dawn. Very dramatic." Corvane drained the rest of his cup, set it down, and picked up his pipe—the old iron pipe, which had just fought its last fight as a pipe. "One condition. When we get to Vael's Crossing, *you* knock on her door. I've seen what happens when you and doors and feelings occupy the same room, and I want to watch from a safe distance."

Sable's jaw tightened. "Fine."

"Wonderful." He slung his meager belongings over his shoulder—all of it fit in one hand, which suddenly seemed like a scandal rather than a convenience—and offered Wren his arm like a gentleman at a ball. "Come along, little songbird. Let's go buy me some boots with the pit-master's money. He owes me for the farmer."

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, the tavern's lone window showed the moon rising thin and sharp over Gravenhollow—one moon nearer to thirteen, and somewhere beneath the mountains, in the dark where lanterns burn, something patient began counting.
