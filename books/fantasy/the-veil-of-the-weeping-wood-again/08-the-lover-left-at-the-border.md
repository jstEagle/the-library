## 8. The Lover Left at the Border

Vael's Crossing smelled of river mud, frying onions, and other people's secrets—which was fitting, because the city had been built on all three.

Sable stood at the top of the pilgrims' stair with the wind coming off the Vael and did what they always did before walking back into a place that knew their name: they counted exits. Two down the stair, one through the tanners' quarter, and a fourth—if you didn't mind swimming—that led under the bridge pilings and out the far side of the river. Fifteen years ago there had been five. The Wardens had bricked up the smugglers' tunnel behind the chandler's shop. Progress, presumably.

"You're doing the face," Corvane said beside them.

"I don't have a face."

"You have *the* face. The one where you look like you're doing arithmetic on your own funeral." He shifted the pack on his shoulder and winced. His ribs were still the color of storm clouds from the pit fight he'd lost on purpose three nights before leaving Ashmoor—or claimed he'd lost on purpose, which was the same thing in his mouth. "It's a good face for cards. Terrible for reconciliation."

"I'm not reconciling anything. I need an archivist."

"Mm." Corvane looked out over the rooftops, at the copper spire of the Hall of Records catching the morning light like a coin someone had dropped in a gutter and decided to build a city around. "Fifteen years, and you're going to knock on her door and say *good morrow, mistress, I require some papers*."

"That's roughly the plan."

"She'll throw a book at you."

"Then she'll be unarmed for a moment, and I can get past her."

Corvane laughed, then stopped laughing because of his ribs, which served him right. Behind him, Wren stood with her chin tipped up, drinking in the city the way she drank in everything—with her whole body, as though the world were a song she was trying to learn by heart. She'd never seen so many chimneys. She pointed at them, one after another, and made a small sound like a question mark.

"Chimneys," Sable said. "Smoke goes up them."

She considered this. Then she pointed at the river.

"Water goes down it," Sable said. "Yes. The city is very fond of things going where they should."

Wren looked at Sable then, with that flat, patient gaze she deployed when she thought an adult was being deliberately stupid, and tapped the ground twice—the old-tongue gesture for *lie*. It wasn't. It just wasn't the whole truth, and the child had developed an unerring ear for the difference, which was going to be a problem when they reached the Undercourt, where lies were currency and truth was law.

"The two of you will wait at the Drowned Bell," Sable said to Corvane. "It's clean enough, the stew won't kill anyone who isn't already dying, and the keeper owes me. Nobody asks questions there."

"And if the Wardens ask?"

"They don't ask at the Drowned Bell. They pay."

Corvane grunted, which was his way of agreeing while preserving deniability. He crouched—slowly, wincing—to Wren's height. "Guard the inn," he told her gravely. "If anyone suspicious comes in, hum the third verse."

She nodded solemnly, as though this were a real plan and not an excuse to hear her sing, and took his hand, and the two of them went down the stair together—an ex-duelist limping like a man twice his age and a mute child who walked like the ground was glad of her. Sable watched them go until the crowd folded them away, and felt the old familiar loneliness arrive early, ahead of schedule, like a debt collector.

Then Sable turned toward the Hall of Records, and stopped making plans altogether.

* * *

The Hall of Records had not changed. That was the first cruelty of it.

Same worn steps, same smell of vellum and lamp oil, same bored clerk at the front desk picking at his thumbnail with a letter knife. Fifteen years, and the world had rearranged itself around this building without disturbing so much as its dust.

"I'm here about sealed materials," Sable said. "Pre-kingdom holdings. Restricted access."

The clerk looked up with the enthusiasm of a man being asked to move a mountain using only his eyebrows. "Sealed materials require a sponsor. Sponsor requires rank. Rank requires—" he gestured vaguely at Sable's road-worn leathers, the guide's scarves, the sword disguised as a walking tool across their back, "—well. You know."

"I know someone who knows someone."

"Everyone says that."

"Mine's true. Third gallery. The archivist's office. Tell her a guide from Thornfell is asking about root-doors."

The clerk sighed the sigh of the terminally employed and shuffled off. Sable waited in the entry hall beneath a frieze of the First Concord—the founding myth carved in marble, kings and swordspeakers shaking hands above the prostrate dark—and tried not to look at the swordspeaker figures too closely. Marble remembered what flesh wanted to forget. Every one of those carved faces was serene. None of them showed what it looked like to run.

Footsteps on stone. Not hurrying. That was Ilsabet all over: she would never give anyone the satisfaction of hurrying.

She came around the corner with a folio tucked under one arm and her spectacles pushed up into grey-streaked hair, and she was thinner than memory, and sharper, and exactly the same, and Sable's entire body forgot how the last fifteen years were supposed to work.

"Well," said Ilsabet. "A guide from Thornfell."

"Il—"

"*Mistress Archivist,*" she corrected, pleasantly, loudly enough for the clerk to hear. "In the Hall, we observe forms. You'll find the forms are quite thorough here. We file everything." Her eyes went over Sable's face like a hand checking for wounds. "Though some things apparently got misfiled. For fifteen years."

"I need to see the restricted holdings. Pre-kingdom contracts. Anything touching the Undercourt's purchase records."

"Of course you do." She set the folio down on the clerk's desk with a small, deliberate click. "And I need several things I have not received. Isn't it funny, how the world works. Clerk, we're closing the third gallery for inventory."

"It's not scheduled for—"

"*Inventory,*" said Ilsabet, in a voice that had made grown wardens apologize to furniture, and the clerk discovered a sudden passion for his thumbnail again.

* * *

The third gallery smelled of dust and tallow, and the light came through high amber windows the color of old honey. She locked the door behind them, and then stood with her back against it, arms crossed, and looked at Sable the way she used to look at a mis-catalogued codex: as a problem that would yield, eventually, to patience.

"Fifteen years," she said.

"Yes."

"No note. No grave to visit. No body, even, which I confess I checked for—I pulled every death roll from Thornfell province for six years running. Do you know what that does to a person, reading names? Hoping yours isn't among them and hating yourself for hoping either way?"

"Ilsabet."

"You let me believe you were dead." Her voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. Level meant she'd practiced. Level meant fifteen years of practice. "I grieved you, Sable Sterling. I did it properly, with rites, in a temple I don't believe in, because grief needs somewhere to put itself down. And now you walk in asking for favors like we're colleagues who shared a lunch once."

"You're right."

"Don't agree with me. It takes all the sport out of it."

Sable breathed out. There was no version of this that didn't hurt, so there was no point shopping for one. "You want the truth of why I left. All of it."

"I want it the way I want a tooth drawn. Necessary. Dreadful. Over quickly, ideally."

"It won't be quick."

"Nothing with you ever was." But she came off the door, pulled two stools from beneath a cataloguing table—the same table, Sable realized, where they had once sat shoulder to shoulder translating boundary stones while she stole sips of their tea—and sat, folding her hands, an audience of one preparing to be unmoved.

So Sable told her.

They told her about the night the order died—not the sanitized version from the ballads, the noble refusal and the heroic stand, but the true shape of it: the Magistrate's envoy arriving with contracts already signed by half the border lords, families selling children for grain and quiet, and the Swordspeakers voting, nine to four, to refuse enforcement. Thirteen voices against a court. The fires started before dawn.

They told her about the vote itself. About Corvane arguing for compliance because survival was also a duty, about Mother Caslin standing on the table to speak the counter-oath, about the sound the doors made when the Wardens' axes found them—*like a forest falling*, Sable said, and watched Ilsabet's hands whiten on each other.

They told her about the choice afterward. Four survivors. A decision to scatter, because a living order could be hunted to extinction but a rumor couldn't. About drawing lots for who carried which fragment of the covenant's knowledge, and Sable drawing the songs—the deep grammar, the words that unmake wards—and understanding that whoever held the songs would have to become unfindable.

"And you decided that meant leaving without telling me," Ilsabet said quietly.

"I decided that if the Wardens squeezed you, you'd have nothing to give them. Not a name. Not a direction. Not even a grievance worth following." Sable's voice came out rougher than intended. "If you'd known I lived, they'd have known you mattered to me. You'd have been the lever. I couldn't— I wouldn't make you into a lever."

"So you made me into a widow instead. Without the decency of a funeral." She stood, walked to the window, stood with her back straight as a shelf. "Do you know the worst part? I understood. Even angry, even these fifteen years, some cold little clerk in my chest kept filing it under *correct procedure*. You did the arithmetically sound thing. You always did." She turned. "I hated you for being right. It's a very lonely kind of hatred. I recommend against it."

"I'm not asking you to stop."

"No. You're asking for something better." She came back and sat, and something in her posture shifted—the archivist returning, the wound set aside in its proper case, labeled, shelved, not destroyed. Nothing with Ilsabet was ever destroyed. Everything was filed. "The Undercourt. The child. Odell sent word ahead—a hedge-witch's pigeon reaches strange places, and mine reached stranger. One lunar year, yes?"

"Eleven months now."

"And you mean to bargain with the Pale Magistrate. Who deals only in truths spoken aloud." She looked at Sable for a long moment, and Sable saw her do the thing she always did: take in the whole board at once, every piece, every trap, and arrive somewhere three moves ahead and unwilling to say so. "Then you'll need the original purchase contracts. Every soul sold under the mountain since the First Concord. Names, terms, consideration paid, and—this is the part that matters, Sable—the *release clauses*. Every contract has one. The Court writes them in. They think it's elegant. A court bound to honor spoken truth must leave a door in every agreement, or the binding turns on itself. It's the one law they cannot break, because they *are* the law."

"You've read them."

"I've *catalogued* them. Sealed. Restricted. Which brings us to the unpleasant part." She rose, went to the great iron-banded cabinet against the far wall, and rested her hand on it without opening it. "My seal opens this. My seal plus a countersignature from the Provost, who is a coward and a drunk and can be managed. What I will not do is hand you the keys to my life's work and watch you vanish through them. Again."

"Il—"

"So. Terms." She faced Sable squarely, and there was something underneath the archivist's steel now, something older and more frightening, which was hope. Carefully buried hope, the kind that had learned to survive underground, in the dark, for years. "You'll stay in Vael's Crossing while we work. Days, not hours. You'll sit at that table"—she pointed at the cataloguing table, and her finger did not quite tremble—"and you'll translate the pre-kingdom glyphs, because no one else alive reads them and we both know it. You'll eat meals. You'll answer questions when I ask them. And when this is finished—whatever *finished* means, whatever's left of everyone involved—you will come back here and tell me whether I was a fool to open this cabinet."

"That's it? That's the price?"

"That is an enormous price, and you know it." Her composure slipped, just once, just a hairline crack, and Sable saw fifteen years behind it. "Every day you're in this city is a day the Wardens might find you, and finding you means finding *me*, and I have spent a decade and a half building a life so quiet and so blameless that it makes me sick, and I am about to burn all of it for a person who taught me that burning is sometimes correct. So yes, Sable. That's the price. Time. Your time, where I can see it. Consider it interest on a debt."

Sable looked at her—at the grey in her hair earned one worry at a time, at the cabinet full of bought souls, at the stool still sitting where it had sat fifteen years ago—and felt the arithmetic finally fail. All those years of counting exits, of weighing risk, of keeping the ledger balanced so no one else could be spent. And here was the entry they'd been avoiding since the fires: love doesn't accrue interest while you're gone. It compounds. It waits in the dark like anything else buried, and it grows.

"All right," Sable said. "Days. Meals. Answers."

"And the return."

"And the return."

Ilsabet exhaled, long and slow, like someone setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying, and turned to the cabinet, and began working the seals with quick competent fingers.

"One more condition," she said, not turning around. "This child. Wren. I want to meet her. Tonight. If I'm committing treason over a soul, I'll know whose soul it is."

"She's waiting at an inn with a former duelist who thinks humming is a security protocol. You'll like them both."

"I doubt it," said Ilsabet, and unlocked the cabinet, and the doors swung open on rows upon rows of sealed folios, each spine marked with the sigil Sable had last seen branded into a burning doorframe—the pale lantern of the Undercourt.

She drew out the oldest one, blew fifteen years of dust from its cover, and set it on the table between the two stools.

"Well, guide from Thornfell," she said. "Sit down. We have work to do, and you owe me eleven months of conversation."

Outside the amber windows, far off in the direction of the garrison hill, a bell began to ring—not the hour bell, not the alarm bell either, but something between them, uncertain, arrhythmic. Ilsabet frowned at the sound, then at the folio in her hands.

"That's odd," she said. "That's the Wardens' muster bell. At midday. They haven't called a full muster in nine years."

She looked up, and met Sable's eyes, and for the first time since the door, the archivist looked afraid.

"What," she asked slowly, "exactly did you do in Ashmoor?"
