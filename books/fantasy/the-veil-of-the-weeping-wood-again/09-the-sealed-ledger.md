## 9. The Sealed Ledger

The Vael's Crossing Royal Archive smelled the way it had always smelled: dust, lamp oil, and the faint ghost of vellum that had been argued over for centuries. Ilsabet Corvaine had spent fifteen years learning to love that smell the way other people learned to love a spouse—imperfectly, stubbornly, and with a running commentary of complaints.

She had also spent those fifteen years perfecting the art of looking busy while doing something she absolutely should not be doing.

"Cataloguing," she announced to no one in particular, sliding a ledger of grain tariffs across her desk and drawing a much more interesting volume from beneath her cloak. "Deep cataloguing. The kind that takes hours."

The archive's night steward, a boy named Pem who was either very loyal or very incurious—Ilsabet had never determined which, and had decided it didn't matter—nodded without looking up. She had trained him well. Or possibly he simply didn't care. Either way, the result was identical.

The volume in her hands was bound in grey calfskin, unmarked, its spine worn smooth by hands that had carried it in secret for generations. It was not catalogued. It did not officially exist. It lived behind the false back of a shelf devoted to municipal drainage disputes—a hiding place so dull that no thief in history had ever looked there, which Ilsabet considered the single greatest act of archival cunning in the kingdom's history.

She unlocked it with the small brass key she wore on a cord around her neck, next to the one that opened her mother's locket. Two keys, two inheritances. One she'd chosen. One she hadn't.

* * *

Sable Sterling had been gone from her rooms for six days.

Six days of knowing they were in the city, somewhere in its crooked sprawl, and being unable to decide whether that knowledge was a gift or a wound. Six days of catching herself listening for a knock at her door that she had no right to expect and every reason to dread.

They had come, of course. They always came when the world required it of them, and never when she simply wanted them. That was Sable's whole tragedy in miniature: they would cross a mountain range at midnight for a stranger's child, but they could not sit through an afternoon tea without inventing an errand.

Ilsabet had given them the truth they'd demanded—the reason she'd agreed to open the sealed records—and she had watched their face while she said it. *You vanished because you thought leaving me was the price of keeping your oath. And you were wrong. It was never a choice between us and duty. You just made it one because choosing was easier than staying.*

Fifteen years of rehearsing that speech, delivered in under a minute, and then she'd turned away before they could answer, because some part of her had understood even then that if Sable answered—if they said what she suspected they might say—she would forgive them instantly, completely, and she wasn't finished being angry yet. Anger was the last piece of them she still owned outright.

So now: work. Work was reliable. Work never climbed out a window at dawn.

She spread the ledger open beneath her lamp and turned to the section marked only with a sigil she had once seen carved into a dead woman's sword: the interlocked crescents of the Swordspeakers' covenant.

* * *

The first thing everyone learned about the Undercourt, if they learned anything at all, was that it was rumor. A story smugglers told to keep each other honest. A shape in the mountain dark that mothers invoked at bedtime—*be good or the pale man below will weigh your name and find it wanting.*

The second thing everyone learned, if they read far enough and asked the wrong questions politely enough, was that the rumor had a budget line.

That was the part that had kept Ilsabet awake for three nights after she'd found it, seven years ago, in this same ledger. Not the souls. Souls sold beneath mountains were, horribly, easy to believe; desperation was the oldest currency and families in famine years spent everything they had. No—it was the *ledger itself*. The neat columns. The clerk's tidy hand. The royal seal at the bottom of each annual reconciliation, stamped in wax the color of dried blood.

Page 114. The year the order died. She turned to it now, though she knew it by heart, the way one knows a scar.

*Reconciliation of Subterranean Accord, Year 412 of the Concord.*

*Beneath the terms of the Accord renewed this season, the Crown affirms non-interference in matters transacted between the Court Below and citizens of lawful intent. In consideration, the Court Below affirms continued stability of the Thornfell passes and restraint from...*

And here the language went strange, as it always did, slipping sideways into phrasing that predated the kingdoms—the old tongue, transliterated by some clerk who clearly had not understood what he was copying:

*...restraint from the Unbinding of that which sleeps beneath the roots, whose waking would return the Weeping Wood to its former weeping, and the passes thereof to fire.*

Stability. That was the word the chancellery used. Stability of the passes, stability of trade, stability of the realm's northern spine. For one century, the Crown had guaranteed the Undercourt freedom to purchase souls from any family desperate enough to sign, and in exchange the mountain had stayed quiet, the roads had stayed open, and the tax revenue from northern timber and silver had flowed south like a polite, dependable river.

And when the Swordspeakers—the one order sworn to stand between such transactions and the people they consumed—had refused to stand aside? When they had declared the Accord an abomination and moved to break it?

The Crown had not defended them.

The Crown had *paved the way.* Warden patrols rerouted. Garrison gates left unlatched. A night, fifteen years ago, when every alarm in three fortresses somehow failed to sound, and the last oath-bound swordspeakers of the old covenant had been slaughtered in their sleep by men paid in Crown silver.

Ilsabet had verified all of it. Cross-referenced garrison rosters against pay ledgers. Matched dates. Found the requisition orders for the oil—oil, they had burned the sanctuary with *requisitioned lamp oil*, itemized, forty casks, signed for. The kingdom had murdered its own guardians with paperwork so thorough that any archivist worth her salt could reconstruct the crime from receipts alone.

No one ever had. Because no archivist who found it survived long enough to publish—or, in two cases she knew of, chose retirement in distant provinces instead, and taught themselves to love drainage disputes.

Ilsabet had chosen neither. She had copied the ledger, page by page, in a cipher of her own devising, and hidden the copies inside eleven volumes of agricultural almanacs scattered through the general collection, indexed under crop rotation. Let the Wardens search for sedition. Nobody in the history of the world had ever searched the crop rotation section.

*Wren's sale was legal.* That was the sentence she kept returning to, turning it over like a stone with something venomous underneath. The child's own family had signed. There would be a receipt. Somewhere in a hundred years of reconciliations there was a column, a line, a clerk's notation: *one soul, female, age eleven, received in good order.* Good order. As though the child were a shipment of wool.

* * *

A knock at the archive's side door—two short, one long, the rhythm Ilsabet had invented for them a lifetime ago and hated herself for remembering.

She closed the ledger. Old habit. Then, deliberately, she reopened it, because she was tired of closing things.

"Enter," she called. "Mind the cartography table. It bites."

Sable came in the way they always had, moving like someone perpetually apologizing to the furniture for occupying space. Fifteen years of mountains had weathered them—grey threading the dark hair, new lines around the eyes, the scar at their throat standing livid in the lamplight—but they still stood in her archive the way they had at twenty-two: too careful, as if afraid of breaking something merely by belonging to it.

"You got my message," she said.

"I got your message." They set down a wrapped bundle that shifted slightly and hummed. Wren, then, sleeping or pretending to. "Your message said *come alone after midnight, bring nothing you can't run with,* which I'll note is terrible advice to give a person carrying a child."

"She can run with herself. Children are famously portable." Ilsabet gestured to the chair across from her desk. "Sit. This will take a while, and I refuse to explain institutional treason to someone hovering."

Sable sat. Their eyes went to the ledger—to the sigil embossed faintly on its cover—and she watched every wall they'd built over fifteen years go up at once, brick by brick, behind their face.

"You've seen this before," she said. It wasn't a question.

"When I was nineteen. My mother showed me a copy of one page. She said if the order ever fell, the proof of why would be in the Crown's own accounting." Sable's jaw tightened. "She was right. I just spent fifteen years hoping she'd been wrong."

"The whole order knew?"

"The senior blades knew. The rest of us knew the shape of it—that the Crown had told us to stand down from interfering with 'northern commerce,' and our elders refused, and then—" They stopped. Their hand drifted toward the scar at their throat, an old gesture, half-guard, half-grief.

"And then forty casks of requisitioned lamp oil," Ilsabet said quietly.

Sable looked up sharply. "You have the requisitions?"

"I have everything. Pay ledgers, patrol schedules, the garrison gate logs for that entire week—every lock on the eastern doors logged as *inspected and functional* the night before, and *found rusted* the morning after. Someone forged inspection reports in advance, Sable. You don't forge maintenance records ahead of time unless you already know what's going to happen to the doors." She pulled a folder from her desk drawer and laid it flat between them. "I built the case fifteen years ago. I've been sitting on it ever since, waiting for either courage or company."

"That's not funny."

"I wasn't joking. Well. Partially joking. Courage has terrible timing, and I'm an archivist, not a martyr." She opened the folder. "But here is what you actually need tonight, and it isn't the massacre. You know about the massacre. What you don't know—what almost nobody knows—is how deep the rot goes. Look."

She turned the ledger to the current year's reconciliation and set her finger against a line item near the bottom.

*Received in good order: one (1) soul, female, approx. age 11, consigned by lawful holders, Ashmoor district. Fee rendered to consignors per schedule. Binding term: thirteen moons from receipt.*

Sable went very still. When they spoke, their voice had dropped into something low and rough that Ilsabet recognized from a single terrible night years ago—the voice they used when feeling things they refused to name. "Ashmoor."

"There will be a matching disbursement record. Her family was paid, Sable. Paid by the court below, permitted by the Crown above, recorded by clerks who probably took festival days afterward." Ilsabet heard her own voice sharpening and let it. She had earned the sharpness. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? This isn't a monster in the dark preying on children. Monsters we could hunt. This is a *contract*. Renewed annually. Stamped. Filed. Audited, presumably, by someone with a very tidy mind and no conscience whatsoever."

"The Wardens know?"

"The Wardens enforce it. That's the arrangement. The Accord keeps the mountain quiet, the mountain keeps the silver flowing, and the silver keeps the chancellery fat and agreeable. Every lord who's signed off on these pages for a hundred years has traded children for road safety and called it governance." She slammed the ledger's cover down—not hard enough to damage the binding, because she was furious, not a barbarian, but hard enough to make her point. "I have spent my entire career among these books believing the archive was where truth went to be preserved. It isn't. It's where truth goes to be *filed*. Alphabetized. Made harmless. Somebody wrote *in good order* about that child, and somebody else countersigned it, and neither of them lost a night's sleep."

From the bundle on the floor came a small sound—not quite a whimper, not quite a question. Sable knelt and unwrapped the blankets, and Wren blinked up at them both, moon-pale eyes taking in the lamplight, the shelves, the walls of recorded human memory.

Then the child did the thing she did sometimes that stopped Ilsabet's breath: she reached up and touched the ledger's cover, right over the interlocked crescents, and traced the sigil slowly, twice, like a greeting. Like recognizing a relative.

"She knows it," Ilsabet whispered. "Sable. She *knows* the covenant mark. How does a child sold two winters ago know the—"

"She doesn't just know it." Sable lifted Wren onto their lap, and the girl settled there with the unselfconscious gravity of a cat claiming a chair. "Watch." They held out their hand, palm up, and Wren pressed her small fingers against it and drew—quick, sure strokes in the air, shapes that hung in Ilsabet's memory like sparks: the opening figures of the swordspeaker binding-oath. The first litany. The one recited before a blade was ever drawn.

"She's speaking it," Sable said softly. "Not singing it. Speaking it, the way it's meant to be spoken, and nobody taught her. The language is in her, Ilsabet. Whatever the Undercourt bought, whatever it's binding her into—she carries the old tongue the way my mother's generation did. Maybe purer." Their voice cracked on the last word, barely.

Ilsabet stared at the child, and the fury in her chest—which had been burning hot and clean all evening—shifted suddenly into something colder and more useful. Because she had read every page of that ledger, every reconciliation for a hundred years, and she remembered something. A pattern. An anomaly she'd noted years ago and filed under *unexplained, pending*.

"Sable. The consignment records. Most entries are like hers—one soul, famine year, desperate family. But there are others. Rare ones. Maybe one a generation." She pulled the ledger back open, flipping fast. "Consignments marked with this—" she tapped a marginal glyph, a crescent with a line struck through it, "—and annotated *received singing*."

"Received singing," Sable repeated.

"Four times in a century. Four children, decades apart, different districts, unrelated families. And every one of them annotated the same way. Received singing. As though the court below weren't buying random souls at all, but *searching* for something. Waiting for a particular kind of child to be born into poverty somewhere, and buying her the moment she appeared." Ilsabet looked up and met Sable's eyes. "What does the Undercourt want with a child who speaks the old tongue natively?"

Sable's arms tightened around Wren. The lamplight caught the scar at their throat.

"I think," they said slowly, "that a court which deals only in spoken truths might want the one voice that could speak truths it couldn't survive. I think my mother's order wasn't slaughtered for refusing the trades." Their gaze dropped to the child in their arms. "I think it was slaughtered so that no one would be left alive who'd recognize *her* when she came."

The lamp flame guttered—as if the room itself had leaned in to listen—and in the silence that followed, from somewhere far below the archive's stone foundations, through pipes and bedrock and a hundred feet of the city's bones, came a sound so faint Ilsabet might have imagined it.

A choir. Singing. Underground.

Wren's head turned toward the wall, and she answered it—in the old tongue, two clear notes, soft as a lullaby.

And then came the knock Ilsabet had been dreading since the day she'd first opened this ledger: heavy, official, at the archive's front doors, accompanied by a voice announcing itself with the crisp boredom of routine authority.

"Crown Wardens. Open by order of the Chancellery. Records inspection."
