## 10. Wardens at the Door

The archive smelled the way Sable remembered it: dust, lamp oil, and the particular sweetness of old vellum that Ilsabet had once claimed was "the smell of people who never got to finish their sentences." She had said it with a smile, fifteen years ago, ink on her fingers and her hair escaping its braid. She said it now without the smile, sliding a ledger across the reading table as though she were handing over nothing more interesting than a grain tariff.

"Page two hundred eleven," she said quietly. "Don't touch it with bare hands. The ink is still... active."

Sable had heard that phrase from exactly one other person in their life—their mother, bending over covenant glyphs by candlelight—and hearing it from Ilsabet made their chest ache in a way they hadn't budgeted for.

They were in the restricted stacks of Vael's Crossing, three levels below the archive's public face, behind a door that opened only for archivists and the occasional very apologetic ghost. Wren sat cross-legged on the floor between them, drawing in the margins of a scrap of parchment with a piece of charcoal she'd appropriated and refused to surrender. Every so often she held up a drawing—a lantern, a tree with tears, a tall thin figure whose face was left deliberately blank—and pointed until one of them acknowledged it.

"The ledger confirms what Odell's map suggested," Ilsabet murmured, keeping her voice low out of professional habit even though the stacks were empty. "The Undercourt's contracts are filed under 'Mountain Accords, Ancillary.' There have been four hundred and six soul-transactions in the last century alone. All notarized. All *legal*, Sable. The Crown countersigned every one."

"I know," Sable said. "I read it."

"You read fast."

"I read angry. It speeds the eyes up."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but the memory of where smiles used to live. "You always did make jokes when you were frightened."

"I'm not frightened."

"You're holding that page hard enough to tear it."

Sable looked down. They loosened their grip. The entry stared up at them: *Sale of minor, female, approx. ten winters. Consideration: debt forgiveness, one hearth-household, Ashmoor valley. Binding effective upon thirteenth moon.* Wren's name wasn't even there. Just a description, like a horse or a shipment of wool.

Wren tugged Sable's sleeve and held up a new drawing: herself, small, standing next to a taller figure with a sword. She'd given the figure a smile. Then she crossed it out and drew a second, smaller smile underneath, closer to where a real one would go.

"That's better," Sable told her gravely. "That first one looked like you were being robbed by a cheerful bandit."

Wren nodded solemnly, as if this were an important scholarly correction.

Ilsabet watched the exchange with an expression Sable couldn't name—something soft and guarded at once, like a door left open exactly one inch. "She trusts you," she said. "That's either a compliment to you or an indictment of everyone else she's met."

"Both, probably."

"Sable." She paused, and Sable knew what was coming because he knew her pauses the way he knew weather. "Fifteen years. You owe me a truth. You agreed."

"I did."

"And I've been patient. I let you read. I let you bring a child into my archive, which is against nine separate regulations, several of which I wrote myself. So." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands, flat on the table, pressed a little too hard. "Why did you leave? Not the oath. I understood the oath. I hated it, but I understood it. Why did you vanish *from me*? You could have written. One letter. One word, even. I'd have taken one word."

The stacks were very quiet. Somewhere above them, the archive clock ticked through the afternoon.

Sable took a breath and found that the truth, which they'd carried so long it had grown smooth as river stone, was heavier to lift than expected.

"Because if I wrote to you, I would have come back," they said. "And coming back meant the Wardens following me to your door. The order was dying. Everyone who loved a Swordspeaker was a target—I'd watched it happen. Maren's brother-in-arms, Kess. They burned his family's farm *with him in it* just to draw the rest of us out. So I did the arithmetic. My heart against your life." Their jaw tightened. "I chose wrong. I've known I chose wrong for about fourteen years and eleven months. But knowing it doesn't unburn the farm."

Ilsabet didn't move for a long moment. Then, quietly: "You absolute idiot."

"I know."

"No—you don't. You *don't* know, because if you knew, you'd understand that I would rather have been burned at your side than safe and furious for fifteen years." She pushed back from the table. Her eyes were bright and wet and furious, which was the most beautiful combination Sable had ever seen on a person and the one they least deserved. "Fifteen years of fury, Sable Sterling. Do you know what I did with all that? I catalogued it. Alphabetically."

Despite everything, Sable felt their mouth twitch. "What letter won?"

"*R*. For 'ridiculous sword-waving martyr.' It's a very thick section."

Wren, sensing the emotional temperature shift without understanding the words, offered Ilsabet a drawing of two stick figures and a cat. There was no cat present. Wren simply believed the scene required one. Ilsabet looked at it, laughed wetly, and something in the room—something fifteen years frozen—cracked and began, cautiously, to thaw.

Which was, of course, precisely when the bells started.

* * *

Not the archive bells—those rang for closing time, a gentle two-note chime. These came from the street above, three quick strikes repeated, and Ilsabet went rigid in a way that told Sable everything before she said anything.

"Wardens," she breathed. "That's the district signal. A sweep."

"They can't know I'm here."

"They don't need to know. There's been a general order since last month—every archive, every records house, every library in the province gets swept for 'seditious historical materials.' Which means anything about the Swordspeakers. Someone in the capital finally noticed the ledgers have gaps where your order used to be." She was already moving, snuffing lamps, sliding the ledger back into its cradle with hands that shook only slightly. "How did they find you? Did you use your name at an inn?"

"Maren's inn. Ashmoor. But Maren wouldn't—"

"Maren wouldn't. Maren's nephew might. Or the tinker who saw you on the road. Or—" She stopped. Above them, boots. Many boots, on marble, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the building. "Front entrance is done. There's a service stair to the map room, then the coal cellar, then the alley on Tallow Street. Take Wren and go."

"Not without you."

"Sable—"

"I just spent twenty minutes explaining that leaving you was the worst thing I ever did. I'm not going to do it *again*, Ilsabet, I'll look ridiculous."

For one absurd heartbeat, in the middle of an archive raid, she almost laughed. Then the reading-room door above boomed open, and a voice—oily, official, carrying the particular relish of a man who enjoyed his work—called down the stairwell:

"Archivist Vael! Your presence is requested. Bring any restricted materials currently in your custody, and do be quick—we haven't got all evening, and neither, I suspect, have you."

Ilsabet closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was someone else—spine straight, chin level, the court archivist entire. Fifteen years ago Sable had watched her lie to a duke's messenger about the location of a disputed border charter, and it had been one of the things that made them fall in love with her: the way truth bent politely around her when it needed to.

"Go," she whispered. "Coal cellar. Now."

"What are you going to tell them?"

"The truth. Selectively." She squeezed Sable's hand once, hard, her grip saying everything her voice couldn't: *you fool, you dear fool, run well.* "Now get my child out of here."

*Our child,* Sable almost said, and didn't, and hated themselves for the cowardice of it even as they scooped up Wren and the scrap of drawings and slipped into the dark of the service stair.

* * *

The coal cellar was a masterpiece of archival neglect: decades of ash, a broken hand-truck, and a door that opened onto Tallow Street if one applied the correct curse to the hinges. Sable applied it. The door groaned open onto grey evening light and the smell of frying onions, and for three glorious seconds it seemed the gods had decided to be merciful.

Then Wren grabbed Sable's arm and pointed.

Down the alley, leaning against a wall with the studied casualness of a man who was absolutely not leaning against a wall on purpose, stood a Crown Warden. Young. Nervous. His hand kept drifting toward the short sword at his hip and away again, like a moth around a flame it distrusted.

Sable pulled Wren back into the shadow of the doorway and ran the numbers. One Warden, green, probably a lookout for the sweep team inside. Alley had two exits; the far end fed into Tallow Street proper, busy with evening foot traffic. If Sable walked out calm, hood up, child in tow, odds were decent the boy wouldn't stop two strangers. Odds were decent. Not good. Decent.

They were halfway down the alley, Wren's small hand swallowed in theirs, when the young Warden pushed off the wall.

"Hold there. Archive's closed for inspection. Where'd you come out of?"

"Cellar," Sable said, pitching their voice low and bored, the smuggler's guide again, no one at all. "Delivering coal. Terrible time for it, I agree."

"At this hour?"

"Coal doesn't keep a schedule. Neither does my employer." Sable kept walking, slow, unbothered, and it was working—the boy's eyes were more interested in the alley behind them than in two dusty travelers—

And then Wren sneezed.

It was, in fairness, a magnificent sneeze, coal dust being coal dust. The Warden startled, and his eyes dropped to the child, and then narrowed, because Wren was looking up at him with the frank, unblinking stare of someone who had never learned that certain people should be feared.

"That's her," he said suddenly, and his voice cracked upward with excitement. "The child. From the circulars—'may travel in company of an adult, possibly armed'—" His hand went to his whistle.

Sable moved before thought. A step, a turn, palm strike to the wrist—the whistle clattered to the cobbles—and a boot behind his knee dropped him gasping before his sword cleared leather. Textbook. Silent. Clean.

Except the alley wasn't silent anymore. Boots in the archive above. Shouting. And at the mouth of Tallow Street, two more gray cloaks turning at the noise, one already raising the alarm-horn to his lips.

There was no running past them with Wren. There was no talking past them. There was only the thing Sable had sworn off on blood-soaked ground fifteen years ago, the language that lived in the muscles and the marrow, the speech that made a sword more than steel.

*I'm sorry,* Sable thought—to Ilsabet, to Corvane, to the ghosts of Thornfell—and drew the plain smuggler's blade from their back, and spoke to it.

The words had no sound, exactly. They had *weight*. The blade woke with a hum that only Sable and Wren could feel, a vibration like a struck bell underwater, and light crawled along its edge—white-gold, old as the mountains. Sable spoke the warding-form, the simplest of the combat cant, three syllables of command that said: *stand apart, stand apart, stand apart.*

The air between them and the Wardens turned solid as glass. The horn-bearer staggered as though he'd walked into a wall of summer heat. Both men froze—not harmed, merely *refused*, their bodies suddenly convinced that forward motion was a bad idea proposed by an enemy.

"Run," Sable told Wren, and Wren ran, and Sable ran after her, backing down the alley with the singing blade between them and pursuit, the light guttering with every syllable spent. Sword-speech cost. It always had. Each word drawn from a well that refilled slowly and sometimes, if you drank too deep, not at all.

They burst onto Tallow Street into the crowd, into the onion-smell and the lamplighters and the ordinary blessed anonymity of a city at supper, and cut left, and left again, and down a stair Sable half-remembered from a smuggling route, until the shouts faded and the alleys closed around them like a cupped hand.

* * *

They holed up in the loft of a tannery that owed Sable a favor from three years back—a favor involving a lost mule and a discreet silence, the best kind of favor. Wren fell asleep within minutes, exhausted, curled against Sable's side with her drawings crushed in her fist. Outside, search-lanterns swung through the streets in slow, methodical arcs, and every few minutes a crier's distant voice announced a curfew and a reward.

Sable sat awake, blade across their knees, doing the arithmetic they were worst at.

*They took her.* The thought arrived without permission and refused to leave. Ilsabet had bought them those seconds in the cellar with whatever selective truth she'd spun upstairs, and the price of selective truths, in Vael's Crossing, was a cell in the Warden garrison—or worse. Sable had seen the garrison's cells once, from the outside, escorting a smuggled cargo of untaxed brandy. Stone. Iron. Windows the size of a hymnal. And Warden Captain Dree, whom rumor credited with a talent for making prisoners talk that made the Magistrate's lanterns look gentle by comparison.

Fifteen years ago, the arithmetic had been simple: one life weighed against another, and Sable had chosen, and been wrong. Now the scale held Wren on one side—Wren, with her moons running out, her soul mortgaged to a court beneath the mountain, her thirteenth moon less than a year away—and Ilsabet on the other, and Sable understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade between the ribs, that this time there was no choosing. This time both weights had to be lifted, together, or nothing meant anything.

Duty wasn't a choice between the people you served. That had been the order's oldest lesson, the one carved into the covenant stones: *a swordspeaker speaks for those who cannot speak for themselves.* Wren couldn't speak at all. And Ilsabet—brilliant, furious, alphabetized-fury Ilsabet—was locked in a stone room with her voice as her only weapon, and no one in that garrison who cared what she had to say.

So Sable would have to say it for her.

They looked down at Wren, asleep and breathing, at the charcoal smudge on her cheek, and felt the old terror rise—the same terror from the night the order died, the night Sable had learned what sword-speech cost when you spent it against an army—but beneath the terror, steady as bedrock, something else. Purpose. The ugly, stubborn, inconvenient kind.

Corvane would help. He'd pretend not to, he'd drink half a bottle and list seventeen reasons it was suicide, and then he'd start sharpening his blades, because that was who Corvane was under all the ash. And between the two of them—one drunk duelist, one smuggler with a half-empty well of the old tongue—they were going to walk into a Crown garrison full of Wardens hunting the last swordspeaker, and take back an archivist.

Sable touched the blade at their knee, felt the faint residual hum, and spoke aloud, softly, to the sleeping child:

"Don't tell Ilsabet I called her my child. She'll put it in a ledger."

Outside, a search-lantern swept the tannery wall and moved on, and somewhere across the city, in a cell built of stone and iron, a woman who catalogued her fury alphabetically was, at this very moment, presumably beginning a new section.

*S*, for *stubborn*. Sable intended to make it a very thick one.
