## 11. The Bargain of Broken Blades

Corvane had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not caring, and in one moon's turn Sable Sterling had dismantled it brick by brick, like a child kicking over a wall of stacked stones.

Now he stood in the reek of a tannery ditch behind the Warden garrison at Vael's Crossing, wearing a guard's uniform that smelled of another man's sweat and someone else's dinner, and he found—to his considerable annoyance—that he cared very much indeed.

"You're frowning," Sable said beside him. They were crouched in the shadow of the drainage culvert, and they said it the way they'd say *the rain's coming* or *that horse is lame*—flat, certain, unbothered.

"I'm thinking."

"Your face thinks loudly." Sable pulled a strip of dried venison from their coat and tore it in half, offering him the bigger piece without looking. That was the thing about Sable Sterling that fifteen years and a scarred throat had not changed: they noticed everything, and they gave away more than they kept.

Corvane took the meat because refusing would start an argument he'd lose. "Three gates," he said, chewing. "Main gate faces the square, always two men, four at nightfall changeover. River gate's barred from inside since the flood two springs back—nobody uses it, nobody watches it. And this." He tapped the culvert grate with his boot. "Which is exactly why somebody will be watching it eventually. Lazy garrisons get surprised once, then they check every hole for a year."

"So we go through the hole before they remember to look at it."

"That's your whole plan? *Before they remember?*"

Sable shrugged. "Plans are just prayers with better posture."

Corvane laughed—a short, ugly bark that he immediately regretted, because laughter was loud and loud was dangerous, and also because laughing felt suspiciously like hope, and hope had never once done him a kindness. "You haven't changed."

"I've changed plenty. I used to be insufferable. Now I'm merely difficult." Sable checked the little lantern-clock they'd bought from a pawnbroker with a limp and a conscience like wet paper. "Changeover's in forty minutes. Wren's in position?"

"Rooftop across the lane. With the pigeons." He paused. "She named three of them. If any die tonight she'll want burials."

"She'll get burials." Sable said it without a flicker of irony, and that was the other thing about them: they meant it. Every absurd small mercy, meant completely. "Signal when you're through. I take the records room, you take the cells. In and out before the watch bell rings ninth hour."

"And if something goes wrong?"

Sable looked at him then, and in the dark their eyes had that old quality he remembered from the massacre's aftermath—the quality of someone who had already counted the cost and paid it in advance. "Then we improvise. We're swordspeakers, Corvane. Improvising is the only part of the job anyone ever taught us properly."

"We're not swordspeakers. I'm a pit brawler with a vocabulary problem, and you're—" He gestured vaguely at all of Sable. "A smuggler with a savior complex."

"Aye," Sable said, and grinned, and it was such an unfamiliar sight on that scarred, weathered face that Corvane forgot, briefly, what they were walking into. "But tonight we're both liars, which is nearly the same rank."

He fed the culvert.

* * *

The grate came away easier than it should have, which told Corvane either that his information was good or that the gods had decided to make up for something. He crawled through muck that he chose not to identify, emerged into a courtyard stacked with firewood barrels, and pressed himself against stone while a sentry's boots scuffed past six feet away.

Eleven years in the pits taught a man patience of a particular flavor: the patience of waiting for someone to make a mistake so you could hurt them. This was different. This was waiting so no one got hurt at all, or as few as possible, and it sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

The cell block was a low stone annex off the east wall, one door, one window barred with iron older than the kingdom. He reached it without incident, which frightened him more than resistance would have. Garrisons this quiet were either empty or confident, and Wardens were never empty.

Through the window slit he saw her.

Ilsabet sat on the cell floor with her back straight as a lanceshaft, hands bound in front of her, and she was—gods help her—reading. She'd torn a strip from somewhere, or charcoaled a stick, and she was writing on the flagstone in tiny characters, memorizing something before it could be taken from her. Fifteen years ago he'd thought her the most stubborn person in the order after Sable. He'd underestimated her by half.

He put his lips near the bars and breathed the word rather than spoke it—the old tongue needed almost no voice, only intent given shape—and the lock's mechanism sighed open like an old man settling into a chair.

Ilsabet's head came up fast. Her eyes found him in the dark and went wide, then narrow, then—worst of all—*knowing*. "You," she whispered. "They gave me to the quiet one. Of course they did."

"Save the welcome-home speech. Can you run?"

"I've been planning my escape route since they locked the door." She rose stiffly, shaking blood into her bound hands. "Cut these."

He did, with a knife he'd stolen from a man who wouldn't miss it until morning. "Sable's getting the ledgers. We meet at the river gate."

"Sable's here." Something moved across her face—not softness exactly, but its cousin. "Of course they're here. They'd come back for a stray dog if it whistled the old tongue."

"They came back for you."

That silenced her for exactly one heartbeat, which for Ilsabet was practically a sonnet. Then the watch bell began to ring.

Not the ninth-hour bell. The alarm.

* * *

Later, Corvane would reconstruct how it went wrong, the way he reconstructed lost fights—blow by blow, looking for the moment the ground shifted. It didn't matter much. What mattered was that somewhere between the cell block and the river gate, a sentry had gotten curious about a culvert grate hanging by one hinge, and curiosity had turned to alarm, and alarm had turned to thirty Wardens pouring into a courtyard with lanterns and crossbows.

And what mattered most was that Wren was still on the rooftop, alone, with the escape route running through the middle of a kill-yard.

They made the woodpile at the courtyard's edge—him, Ilsabet, and Sable bursting from the records room with a satchel of ledgers and blood on their sleeve that Corvane desperately hoped wasn't theirs. Lantern light swung wild across the walls. A crossbow bolt buried itself in the barrel beside his head with a sound like a woodpecker having opinions.

"The gate," Sable gasped. "It's barred—we'll never—"

"I know." Corvane heard himself say it calmly, from a great distance, the way he heard everything in the last seconds of a fight going badly. "Get to the roof ladder. Get Wren. Go over the north wall—I cracked my shoulder on that wall twice in training, it's climbable even for archivists."

"No." Sable's voice came out cracked down the middle. "No, that's not—"

"It's exactly the plan. It was always the plan. I just hadn't mentioned the part where I stay behind, because you'd have argued, and you argue like a landslide." He stepped out from the woodpile into the lantern-light, into the open, into the sight of thirty drawn weapons, and rolled his shoulders like he was stepping into a pit ring, because in every way that mattered, he was. "Go. Now. Both of you, or I swear on the second blade I'll haunt you both and I am *insufferable* as a ghost."

"Sable!" Wren's voice, thin and terrified, from the rooftop edge. Below her, the ladder. Behind him, the Wardens advancing in a crescent, their captain shouting orders about taking the swordspeaker alive.

Alive. They wanted Sable alive. Which meant they wanted *him* however they could get him, and dead was acceptable, and that suited him fine, because dead men don't have to live with what they've wasted.

He turned to face them and did the last thing he'd sworn never to do again, and the first thing, maybe, he'd been born to do.

He spoke.

Not common words. Not the gutter-tongue of the pits. He spoke the deep grammar of the blade, the language his mother's mother's mothers had carried when kingdoms were young—words of edge and binding and the long patience of iron—and as he spoke, his sword woke. Not glowed, nothing so kind. It *woke*, the way a dog wakes, and the air along its edge went thin and singing, and the nearest three Wardens felt their own weapons grow heavy as fence posts in their hands.

"*Keth-varan,*" he said. *Hold.* The courtyard held. Crossbow strings sagged. Boots rooted as though the flagstones had grown fond of them.

The old tongue was a debt-coin. Every word spent drew on something in the speaker. He'd watched the order's masters spend themselves word by word until they were husks, and he'd sworn in the ashes of that night to never speak it again, and now he opened his mouth and poured out everything he had left, and it was more than he'd expected, and less than enough.

The captain fought the holding-word. Corvane saw it in his face—the struggle, the teeth gritted against iron will, and beyond him, at the wall, Sable hauling Wren over the parapet while Ilsabet climbed below them, cursing with scholarly precision.

Good. Good. That was the whole ledger, balanced.

He walked forward as he spoke, one slow step at a time, widening the circle of his holding, buying the wall, buying the ladder, buying the rooftops and the dark beyond them. Each word cost heat; cold crept up his arms like wading into winter water. His breath showed white in the warm night. Somewhere a Warden broke free of the binding and loosed a bolt that took him high in the chest, below the collarbone, and Corvane grunted, staggered, and kept speaking, because pain was just the body's opinion and the body had never once outvoted him on anything that mattered.

"*Varan-keth,*" he said. *Hold longer.* 

"*Duren-sai.*" *For them.*

He thought, absurdly, of Wren naming pigeons. Of Sable splitting venison and giving away the larger half. Of a girl sold by her family who chose it, who'd chosen it, to save her brother, and of the fact that children kept teaching adults the same lesson over and over because adults were too proud to learn it the first time.

*This,* he thought, driving the final word through the cold in his chest like a nail through oak. *This is what the oath was for.*

The last word left him and took the last of his warmth with it. The sword fell from fingers gone clumsy. He went down on one knee in the courtyard of the garrison he'd broken into, surrounded by enemies he'd frozen mid-stride, and above him, faint and fading, he heard Sable scream his name from the wall—

—and then, cutting through the alarm bells and the shouts, a sound that stopped even the captain mid-order:

Wren, singing.

High and clear and utterly fearless, the old tongue pouring from the rooftop in a melody Sable must have taught her in the wagon, in the woods, in the stolen hours between fear and sleep—and the song caught his falling spirit like a net. He knew that song. His grandmother had sung it. Everyone's grandmother had sung it, once.

*We do not fall forgotten. We fall as seeds.*

Corvane laughed, there on his knees, blood spreading warm and then cold beneath him. Redemption, it turned out, wasn't a door you walked through. It was a song someone sang over you while you lay bleeding in the dirt, insisting you were worth the trouble.

Boots ran toward him. Hands seized his arms. The captain's voice, hoarse from fighting the binding: "Keep him alive, damn you—he's the last of them, he's worth more breathing—"

Worth more breathing. Well. For eleven years, Corvane had doubted it himself.

Above the wall, silhouetted against the moon, three figures dropped from the parapet and vanished toward the river, and the smallest figure's voice carried back on the night air, singing still, growing smaller, never stopping.

Corvane let his head hang, and smiled, and let the darkness come up gentle as a tide.

*Take your time,* he thought, as the black closed over him. *I've nowhere to be but wherever they're going.*

And far to the south, past the lowlands, past the drowned farms, the Weeping Wood waited under the stars—weeping, as it always wept—but that night, some said afterward, the sap ran faster, as if the trees too had heard the song, and were crying for joy.
