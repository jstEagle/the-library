## 12. Into the Weeping Wood

Wren knew the forest before she saw it.

She felt it first as a hum in her teeth, then in her ribs, then in the soles of her bare feet where Sable had finally given up arguing about boots and wrapped them instead in strips of wool that smelled of smoke and old leather. The hum was not a sound exactly. It was more like remembering something she had never learned—a warmth behind the breastbone, a hand on the back of her neck, a voice saying *you are late, little one* without using any words at all.

The trees announced themselves long before the road did. First a smell: sweet and strange, like honey left too long in the sun, like rain on warm stone. Then a light: green-gold, sifting down through branches so dense that the sky above became a rumor. Then the tears.

That was what Sable called them, anyway, when they stopped at the treeline to check their blade and mutter at Corvane about walking quieter. Wren thought *tears* was both right and wrong. The sap that beaded along the great grey trunks and slid down the bark in slow silver ropes did look like weeping—but it did not feel sad. It felt patient. It felt like a thing that had been holding its breath for a hundred years and had decided, just now, to let a little of it out.

Sable crouched beside her, and for once they did not look tired or grim or like a person doing arithmetic with other people's lives. For once they looked almost afraid.

"Rules," Sable said, holding up fingers. "One: stay where I can see you. Two: if anything sings at you, you do not sing back until I say. Three—" They hesitated, and Wren watched the third rule die somewhere behind their eyes. "Three: hold my hand."

Wren took it. Sable's palm was calloused and dry and trembling very slightly, which Wren found interesting, because she had begun to suspect that Sable Sterling believed fear was a thing that happened exclusively to other people.

Corvane came up on Sable's other side, favoring his bandaged shoulder, his face wearing the expression of a man who has been talked into something by two people who cannot legally be argued with—one of them a mute child, the other himself.

"I hate this forest," he said conversationally.

"You haven't been in it yet."

"That's why I hate it. It hasn't even done anything yet and I already know." He spat, ceremonially, at the roots. "No offense meant," he added, apparently to the trees.

And here is what Wren would have told him, if words had lived in her mouth the way they lived in other people's mouths: the forest was not offended. The forest had heard far worse than a grumpy swordspeaker spitting at its roots. The forest had heard the Undercourt itself, and it had gone on bleeding its slow silver tears all the same, because that was what the Weeping Wood did—it remembered everything, and it forgave almost nothing, but it kept singing through both.

She stepped under the branches.

The world changed key.

It was like walking from a cold room into sunlight, except the sunlight was inside her. The hum in her ribs swelled into something with edges and colors, and every tree she passed lit up in her chest like a lamp being carried past a window. She could feel them—not see, not hear, *feel*—each one distinct, each one humming its own slow note, and all of them turning toward her the way flowers turn toward morning. Not all at once. That would have been frightening. But gradually, over the first hour, a kind of attention gathered around her, vast and gentle and older than any kingdom, and Wren walked in the middle of it and felt—for the first time in two winters, since the night her family took her to the mountain and sold her—like a coin that had come home to the purse it belonged in.

She sang. Just a thread of it, just breath shaped the way her mother-before-the-family-that-sold-her had shaped it, back before memory went soft. A wandering phrase of the old tongue, three notes rising, one falling.

The nearest tree shivered.

Sap rushed up its trunk like blood rushing to a face, and a rope of it slid down and hung at the level of her shoulder, gleaming, trembling—and stopped. Waiting.

"Wren." Sable's voice was low and careful. "What did I say about singing?"

Wren pointed at the sap. Then at herself. Then she made the sign she had invented in the first week, the one that meant *listen*, because Sable listened better than anyone she had ever met, even when there were no words to listen to.

Sable looked at the hanging sap for a long moment. Then they reached out—slowly, the way you reach toward a horse that might bolt—and touched it with one fingertip.

The sap clung, silver-bright, and when Sable pulled their finger back a thin strand stretched between them, and along that strand Wren saw Sable go very still, the stillness of someone hearing a name they thought was buried.

"What is it?" Corvane asked. "What's it doing?"

"It's showing me my mother's kitchen," Sable said quietly. Their voice had an odd crack in the middle of it, like ice on a pond in early spring. "The blue door. The chipped bowl. I haven't thought about that bowl in twenty years." They wiped their finger on their cloak with unnecessary firmness. "Right. New rule four. Nobody touches the crying trees."

But Wren touched them. Of course she did. All through that first day, while the light went amber and the shadows went long and Sable picked their way along a root-ridge that only appeared when needed, Wren drifted from trunk to trunk laying her palm against the weeping bark, and each tree gave her something. Not memories—she didn't have enough of her own for the trees to trade in. They gave her *songs*. Fragments. Half-phrases of the old language that filled in gaps she hadn't known were gaps, the way water fills a footprint. A lullaby's missing middle verse. A greeting she'd only ever heard sung wrong by the faded things beneath the mountain. A word for *homecoming* that had no common-tongue translation because it meant specifically *the moment the road stops being between you and the door.*

She kept them all. She hoarded them the way other children hoarded river stones, and when they made camp that night in the hollow of a fallen giant, she sat with her back against the dead wood and sang the fragments softly into the dark, and around her, faint as starlight, the living trees hummed harmony.

* * *

By the third day, the forest had opinions about Sable.

It was subtle, at first. Paths that had wound stubbornly for hours would suddenly straighten when Sable walked them. Thorns drew back from their sleeves. Once, when Corvane slipped crossing a moss-slicked root, a low branch swung out—slowly, deliberately, unmistakably—and caught him under the arms like a mother hauling a toddler out of a puddle.

Corvane hung there for a full three seconds, dangling, staring at the branch.

"Don't," Sable said, without looking up from the map fragment.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say something extremely loud."

"I was going to say," Corvane said, with dignity, as the branch set him gently down, "that I have fought nine men in a pit at once and never once been manhandled by shrubbery."

Wren laughed. It came out of her as a sound rather than a word—a bright, barking, entirely undignified noise—and both adults turned to look at her with such identical surprise that she laughed again, harder, until she had to sit down on a root and hold her stomach.

Because that was the other thing the forest was doing to her. It was loosening things. The old tongue lived in her deeper than her silence, and the deeper they went beneath the boughs, the more the language rose up through her like spring through frozen ground—not as speech, she still couldn't make her mouth shape common words, but as *song*, and song could carry meaning the way a river carries boats. When she sang now, Sable understood her. Not always; not perfectly. But enough.

On the fourth morning she woke before dawn and sang the word for *hungry* at a passing fern, and within minutes Sable emerged from the brush with three fat birds already plucked, wearing the expression of a man who has been drafted into service by his own breakfast.

"This forest," Sable said, dropping the birds by the fire ring, "is spoiling you."

Wren signed back, serenely: *good.*

"And it's learning sarcasm from you. Marvelous. Exactly what an ancient pre-kingdom woodland needed. Attitude."

Corvane, turning the spit, grinned around the stick in his teeth. "I like her better every day."

"I liked her first."

"You abandoned your post for fifteen years, Sterling. I'm recency-biased."

They bickered on, comfortably, the way people bicker who have run out of bigger things to be afraid of for at least a few miles—and Wren sat between them in the firelight and felt the trees lean in around their little camp, listening, keeping watch, and understood that this, whatever else it became, was the first place since her brother's small warm weight against her chest where she had ever stood guard instead of being guarded against.

She sang thanks to the forest for the birds. The forest, smugly, bloomed.

In the middle of autumn. Out of season. Three white flowers opened on the branch directly above Sable's head, and one of them dropped a petal into their tea.

"Point taken," Sable said.

* * *

On the fifth day, the forest stopped leading them along paths and started leading them *down*.

It began as a change in the light—the gold going deep, going green-black, the trunks widening until they walked between pillars rather than trees. Then the ground itself changed, soft loam giving way to root, and not scattered root but woven root, braided across the earth in patterns too regular to be accident, rising into low walls, then arches, then a corridor of living wood that sloped gently downward into the dark.

Sable stopped at the threshold with their hand flat against the first arch, and Wren watched them read it the way Ilsabet had taught them both to read ledger-pages: top to bottom, fast, twice.

"This isn't on Odell's map," Sable said. "This isn't anywhere. This is a root-door, and root-doors were sealed when my grandmother was a girl. Every account says they don't exist anymore." They looked at Wren, and there was something new in their face—not the cold competence of the pass, not the grief that flickered when they spoke of the order, but a kind of wondering respect, as though they were meeting her for the first time. "They don't exist anymore except where something wakes them."

Wren put her hand next to Sable's on the arch.

The wood was warm. Warmer than blood. And under her palm it beat—slow, enormous, patient—a pulse the size of a mountain's heart, and she realized with a jolt that it was beating *in time with her own*, that it had probably been beating in time with her since the day she crossed the treeline, that the whole forest was less a place than a body, and it had recognized her the way you recognize family across a crowded market: instantly, completely, before any sense can be made of it.

*You are late, little one,* said the forest again, and this time she felt it everywhere, in her teeth, her ribs, the wool-wrapped soles of her feet. *We held the door. We always hold the doors. Come down. There are others below who have forgotten how to knock.*

Others below.

The Hollow Choir.

Wren's joy dimmed, just slightly, the way a lamp dims when a hand cups around it. She had felt those faded ones in her dreams since Ashmoor—souls worn smooth as river stones, singing fragments they no longer understood, names worn away to nothing. If the door was opening downward, then they were not fleeing *past* the choir. They were walking toward it.

She looked up at Sable and made the sign for *afraid*, and then, after a moment, the sign for *anyway.*

Sable knelt to her height. "Whatever's down there," they said, "it doesn't get you. That's the whole reason we're doing this. That's the entire job." A pause. The ghost of their old smuggler's arithmetic crossed their face and was dismissed, unpaid. "Besides. You've got the only weapon down there that matters."

Wren tilted her head: *what?*

"A language nobody down there has heard sung *right* in a hundred years."

Corvane rolled his shoulders, winced, and drew his sword anyway. "After you, then," he said to the dark. "But if anything down there manhandles me, I'm filing a grievance with the management."

Wren stepped through the root-door first, because it was hers, because it had opened for her, and because somewhere below, in the dark, in the deep places where souls go to forget themselves, something very old and very lost was waiting for a song it half-remembered—and she was the only one alive who knew how it went.

Behind her the door sighed shut like a held breath released.

Ahead, the stairs went down, and down, and down, and far below—so far it might have been imagination, but she did not think it was—something began, very faintly, to sing back.
