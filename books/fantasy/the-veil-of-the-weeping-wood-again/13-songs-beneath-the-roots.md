## 13. Songs Beneath the Roots

The root-door closed behind us with a sound like a held breath finally let out.

I had expected darkness. What I got was worse: a soft amber glow that came from everywhere and nowhere, seeping through the walls of the tunnel as though we were walking inside a lantern. The passage itself was enormous—a hollowed artery of some tree so ancient it had stopped bothering with the distinction between wood and stone. The walls curved overhead in ribs of root, thick as temple pillars, and between them hung curtains of fine white filaments that trembled at our passing without any wind to move them.

"Cozy," Corvane said behind me. His voice had gone carefully flat, which in Corvane's particular dialect meant *terrified*. "Reminds me of my mother's cellar."

"You hated your mother's cellar."

"I did," he agreed. "That's what I said."

Wren pressed close against my side, her small hand finding the hem of my coat and gripping it the way she'd gripped it every hour since we'd entered the Weeping Wood proper. Above ground she had been transformed—walking straighter, breathing deeper, humming little phrases of the old tongue that made the trees lean toward her like courtiers. Down here, in the dark under all that living green, I could feel her fear through the fabric.

I knelt and took both her hands. *We go together,* I signed—the crude hand-speech I'd been teaching her, cobbled together from smuggler's signals and half-remembered covenant gestures. She'd learned faster than any student I'd ever had, because unlike most students, she already thought in silence.

She signed back: *The singing is louder here.*

I had heard it too. For the last hour, threading down through passages the map-fragment called—when Odell's cramped handwriting bothered to call them anything at all—*the Rootways*, I had caught fragments of sound drifting up from below. I'd told myself it was water. Water moving through hollow wood makes music; every mountain guide knows this.

But water does not sing in parts.

*Stay with my voice,* I signed to Wren. *Whatever you hear below, whatever calls you by name—stay with my voice.*

Her eyes went wide and dark. *Does something know my name?*

And there it was, the question I'd been circling for an hour, asked with the terrible directness of children. I thought about lying. Fifteen years as a smuggler's guide had made lying as easy as breathing, and usually kinder than the truth.

I signed instead: *Something might. That is why you hold on to me.*

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate weighing evidence. Then she nodded once, threaded her fingers through mine, and pointed forward. *Then walk fast.*

Corvane snorted. "Gods help the Undercourt," he muttered. "It's about to be negotiated with by a nine-year-old with opinions."

"She's eleven," I said.

"She's terrifying," he said. "Age is irrelevant."

* * *

The tunnel descended in long spirals, and the amber glow deepened to honey, then to the color of old resin, then to something almost like candlelight. The ribbed roots grew closer together, and the white filaments thickened into braids that brushed our shoulders as we passed. Each time they touched me, I felt a faint tug—not on my body but somewhere behind it, the way a hook tugs not on the fish but on the line.

Swordspeaker training had a word for that sensation. We'd called it *the listening*, and our masters had taught us to feel it the way sailors feel weather. Something below was listening back.

"Tell me again," Corvane said, his boots crunching on what I tried very hard not to identify as bone fragments scattered along the tunnel floor, "why we couldn't have taken the nice obvious cave entrance on the north face. The one with the ladder. Ladders, Sable. Civilization."

"The north entrance is watched. Odell marked three warden stations above it." I kept my voice low and even. In these tunnels, I suspected, voice carried further than it should. "This door has been sealed for fifteen years. Nobody remembers it exists."

"Except the woman who sold us the map fragment for two silver and a bottle of plum brandy."

"Hedge-witches are famously forgettable."

"Famously *expensive*," Corvane corrected, but there was no heat in it. He'd been different since the Wood. Since watching Wren put her palm against a bleeding tree and sing it quiet, since the sap had run clear instead of red for a full minute around her. He walked closer to her now, always within arm's reach, the way men who have lost one war walk near the thing they've decided to win the next one for.

The singing grew.

It came up through the floor of the world, thin and many-voiced, and I stopped being able to pretend it was water. It was the old language—or pieces of it, worn smooth as river stones, syllables missing their ends the way old coins miss their faces. A phrase would rise, lovely and complete, and then dissolve mid-word as if the singer had forgotten what they were saying. Another voice would catch the falling fragment and carry it a few steps further before it, too, slipped away.

I knew the song. Gods forgive me, I knew it. It was the Vigil Chant, the first thing every swordspeaker child learned, the melody my mother sang while grinding grain, while mending cloaks, while rocking me through fevers. *We hold the door. We hold the light. We speak the truth the dark cannot.* Except these voices only managed *we hold* before fraying, only *we speak the* before dissolving, over and over, a loom weaving the same three threads forever and never finishing cloth.

My mother had sung it whole.

"Wren," I said aloud, and my voice cracked on it. "Wren, look at me."

She was already looking past me. Her face had changed—softened, brightened, lit from inside with recognition. She took a step toward the sound. Then another, pulling my arm with her, gentle as tide.

*They know the words,* she signed one-handed, clumsy with eagerness. *They know the whole song. They can teach me the parts Mama never finished teaching me.*

"They can't," I said, too sharp, and her flinch cut me worse than any blade ever had. I softened my hands, my voice. "Little bird. Listen to me properly now. Those aren't singers. Those are—" I searched for words a child could carry without breaking. "Those are people who gave their names away. And when you give your name away down here, the song is the last thing you keep. Just the song. Not the meaning. Not the ending. They've been holding those three words for longer than Ashmoor has existed, and they cannot put them down, and they cannot finish them."

Wren looked at me. Then down at the floor. Then, terribly, back toward the singing, and I saw the calculation move through her young face like weather—the same calculation, I realized with a cold turning in my gut, that she must have made two winters ago in a farmhouse kitchen, when she'd looked at her infant brother and decided whose soul the debt would take.

*I could learn it,* she signed slowly. *And then I could teach THEM. Finish it FOR them. Someone has to finish it for them.*

"Oh, no," Corvane said quietly behind me. "No, no. Kid—"

But she was already moving, my hand sliding from hers, and the white braids were parting for her like a crowd making way, and the singing below swelled—not louder exactly, but *warmer*, the way a fire warms, gathering itself toward her, and every faded voice found its fragment at once:

*We hold. We hold. We hold.*

And beneath it, new, sweet, unmistakable, Wren's voice rose in answer, the old tongue pouring out of her unbroken, and the filaments began to curl toward her ankles like fingers.

* * *

I have crossed avalanches. I have talked my way past wardens with a knife at my kidney. I have stood in a burning chapter-house and heard my mother's last word. None of it prepared me for the specific horror of watching a child be *welcomed* into erasure—because that was the unbearable truth of the sound below. There was no menace in it. There was nothing but tenderness, vast and patient and utterly empty, the tenderness of a hole that wants to be filled.

"WREN!" My voice bounced off the root-ribs and died. Words were useless here; the tunnel ate them. So I did the thing I had sworn off on the road, the thing that had nearly gotten us killed outside Thornford, the thing that rang through the deep places like a struck bell.

I spoke sword-speech.

Not a word of power—I hadn't the strength or the heart—but the cadence, the command-tongue, the grammar they'd beaten into us as children alongside our letters. I spoke her name in it. Her true name, the one Odell had read off her markings, the one her family had traded away as casually as a cow—and names spoken in the old cadence do not drift. They *land*.

Wren stopped.

The filaments froze mid-curl. Below us, the Choir's warmth curdled into something else—not anger, nothing so alive as anger. Confusion. A thousand faded voices reaching for a word that wasn't in their fragment anymore.

*Come here,* I signed, hard and fast, every gesture a hammer-strike. *NOW, little bird.*

Her eyes focused. Whatever current had been carrying her broke against the shore of my signing, and I watched her come back into herself the way you watch someone surface from deep water—one breath, two, her shoulders rising, her fists clenching. She ran the distance back to me and hit me at waist height and held on hard enough to bruise.

I held on harder.

"It's okay," I said into her hair, in plain trade-common, ugly useful language, anchoring her to the surface world word by word. "You're here. You're with me. Your name is Wren. Say it—sign it—whatever you've got, use it. *Now.*"

Her hands shook against my back. Then, muffled: she pulled one arm free and signed against my chest, letter by letter. *W-R-E-N. Wren. Wren of nowhere, Wren who chose, Wren who is going to be SO angry at you later.*

"That's fair," I whispered.

When I looked up, Corvane was standing very still, one hand on his sword hilt, staring down the tunnel toward the source of the song. His face was grey.

"Sable," he said. "They're coming up."

He was right. Out of the honeyed dark below, figures were ascending—slowly, without hurry, the way frost climbs a window. They had been people once. I could see it in what remained: the shape of a smith's shoulders, the set of a scholar's stoop, one small figure no taller than Wren. Their outlines kept losing agreement with themselves, edges blurring like wet ink, and where their faces should have been there was only a smooth pale blankness—except that as they drew nearer, I saw faint impressions surfacing there, ghost-features rising and sinking, mouths opening on remembered shapes of words.

They formed a loose crescent around us. Not blocking the path. Simply *arriving*, the way weather arrives.

And then, all together, in voices like wind through winter reeds, they sang at me:

*"We hold."*

Waiting. The silence after was the worst part. They were waiting for me to finish the line for them. They had been waiting, I understood, for anyone at all to finish the line—for longer than kingdoms, for anyone who still carried the rest of the song in a whole mind.

My eyes burned. My mother had known this chant entire. Had sung it over wash-water and graves. And here was what became of knowing things, down where the Undercourt kept its accounts: you became a fragment, waiting centuries for a stranger to complete you.

So I did it. There was no strategy in it. I opened my mouth and gave them the line, in the old tongue, my voice rough and cracking and entirely mortal:

*"We hold the door. We hold the light. We speak the truth the dark cannot unmake."*

The effect moved through them like dawn through a valley. The blank faces rippled; for one heartbeat each wore its own features again—a laughing mouth, a stern brow, the small round cheeks of the child among them—and the child reached out one fading hand and touched Wren's foot, feather-light, before the clarity sank back beneath the pale. But something had changed in the crescent. The pull in the air eased. The filaments drooped, sated, or perhaps simply respectful.

And then every Hollow voice, in perfect unison, spoke a single word in trade-common, worn but recognizable, aimed up the tunnel ahead of us like a signpost:

*"Down."*

* * *

We walked. What else was there to do?

The Choir fell in around us as we descended—not touching, never quite resolving fully into shape, but accompanying, and I could not decide if it was escort or appetite or something older than either. Wren kept a fist knotted in my belt and her other hand locked in Corvane's, and between verses of silence she hummed—quietly, steadily, the Vigil Chant sung *whole*, and I noticed the Hollow ones nearest her tilting their blurred heads each time, drinking the completed lines like parched travelers at a well.

"They like her," Corvane murmured. "That frightens me more than if they'd tried to eat us."

"It should frighten you less than what it means."

"And what's that?"

I watched the amber light ahead deepen toward something redder, warmer, watch the tunnel begin to widen around us, watch the bone-dust on the floor thicken into true bones, arranged, over the centuries, into patterns too deliberate to be accidental. Watched the singing ahead take on structure at last—harmony, intention, an audience chamber's acoustics.

"It means," I said, "that everything down here wants her. The dead want her to finish their song. Which tells me exactly what the living want her for."

The tunnel opened.

Below us, filling a cavern vast enough to swallow Ashmoor entire, hung ten thousand lights—lanterns of amber glass, each one glowing with a slow inner pulse, each pulse a heartbeat that wasn't ours. And rising from the center of that sea of stolen souls, a dais of black root and white bone, and upon it a tall figure turning toward us with the unhurried attention of something that had been listening to our footsteps for hours.

Beside me, Wren's grip went rigid.

And every Hollow voice around us, gently, almost kindly, finished the Vigil Chant on her behalf—*the dark cannot unmake*—as the Pale Magistrate lifted his head and smiled.
