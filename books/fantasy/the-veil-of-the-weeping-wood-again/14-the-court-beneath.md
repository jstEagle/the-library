## 14. The Court Beneath

The root-door did not open so much as *admit* them.

Sable had braced for a struggle—for the wood to resist, for some final test of worthiness at the threshold—but the great root simply curled aside like a hand unclenching, and the dark beyond it breathed out against their face. It smelled of cold stone and honey and something older underneath both: the smell of a cellar that had never once been opened, where the air had been waiting a long time for someone to disturb it.

Wren went first. Of course she went first.

"Or we could all stand here in the dark contemplating our life choices," Sable muttered, and followed.

Ilsabet came behind them, one hand on Sable's shoulder strap—not gripping, just touching, the way she had touched things in the archive for thirty years: confirming they were real before she catalogued them. Corvane brought up the rear with his sword drawn and his jaw set in the expression he'd worn since the garrison gate, the one that said *I am fine* in a voice only corpses would believe. The wound across his ribs had been bound twice over with Odell's moss-wraps and Ilsabet's torn underskirt, and every step cost him something he was pretending not to count.

The tunnel sloped down through the roots themselves—or through what the roots had become. Sable's fingers found the walls as they walked, and what they found there made their stomach turn slow and quiet: bone. Not scattered bones, not a grave, but bone grown into structure, ribbed and vaulted, as if the Weeping Wood's deepest roots had wrapped around the skeleton of something vast and the two had decided, over centuries, to become architecture together.

"The old texts called it the Amberhold," Ilsabet whispered. Her voice had gone archivist-flat, which Sable knew was how she sounded when she was frightened and refusing to be. "Seat of the Undercourt. I always assumed it was a metaphor."

"I keep forgetting you're the sort of person who assumes things are metaphors."

"I keep forgetting you're the sort of person who's been underground before and didn't mention it."

They descended by Wren's light. The child had stopped needing torches an hour ago—a soft amber glow gathered around her now, rising off her skin like heat off summer stone, and where she walked, the weeping sap on the walls brightened in answer. She sang under her breath, that melody from the storm-night, and the tunnel carried it forward ahead of them like a servant announcing guests.

Sable wished she wouldn't sing. Every note echoed back changed, layered with other voices underneath—thin ones, frayed ones—and each echo was a small cold finger laid against the back of Sable's neck.

Then the tunnel opened, and Sable stopped wishing anything at all, because there was no room left in them for wishes.

* * *

The cathedral of bone and amber filled a cavern the size of Ashmoor's entire valley, and it was full of light.

Lanterns. Thousands upon thousands of lanterns, hung in tiers that rose into darkness beyond sight, each one glowing with a color that had no name in any language Sable spoke—warm golds shading into greens shading into blues that hurt gently to look at, like remembering something beautiful from too far away. They hung from chains of amber and from hooks of pale bone and from nothing at all in places, floating in slow constellations, and each one pulsed. Softly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing.

"They're souls," Sable said. Their voice came out very small in a space built for voices much larger than theirs.

"I know," said Wren—and Sable's head snapped around, because Wren did not speak common, had not spoken a single word of common in all the weeks since the storm—

But it wasn't Wren who'd spoken. The words had come from everywhere. From the lanterns.

*I know,* said another voice, higher, younger. *We are here. We are all here.*

*Here,* agreed a third, deeper, worn smooth as a river stone. *Here. Here. Here.*

The word rippled out through the tiers, lantern to lantern, fading as it traveled, until somewhere far above a single light flickered the last *here* and went dim again.

Beside Sable, Ilsabet made a sound they had never heard her make—not a gasp, not a sob, something between a scholar's recognition and a wound. "The Hollow Choir," she breathed. "Odell's map—the fragments—the singing we heard in the tunnels. These are the ones who faded." Her hand found Sable's and gripped hard enough to grind knuckles. "They can't remember their names anymore. That's why they only say—"

*Here,* the lanterns said, gently, all together, like rain beginning.

"Don't," Sable said aloud, to the lanterns, to the dark, to whatever was listening. "Don't answer me. I'm not staying."

Something in the vast dark laughed. It was not a cruel laugh. Somehow that was worse—it was patient, amused, the laugh of someone watching a mouse argue with the shape of a trap it had already walked into.

*You are already staying,* said the dark. *Everyone who arrives here is staying. Some merely have longer appointments than others.*

The floor of the cathedral—which was not a floor but a causeway of white bone arcing out over a depth Sable declined to measure—led to a dais, and on the dais stood a desk.

That detail, absurdly, was what broke through Sable's fear and lodged there like a splinter. After all the bone and the burning lanterns and the breathing dark, the Pale Magistrate kept a *desk*. A long, low writing table of black wood, immaculately ordered: inkwell, quills, ledgers stacked in ranks of perfect perpendicularity, and a small brass bell that looked exactly like the bell on Maren's counter at the Drowned Goose inn, if Maren's bell had been cast three thousand years ago by something that ate prayers.

And standing behind the desk was a figure tall and thin as a winter birch, robed in grey that might have been cloth and might have been fog, its face smooth and pale and almost—but not quite, never quite—in the shape of a human face, the way a footprint in snow is almost the shape of the foot.

Its eyes were closed.

"You've brought a guest," the Magistrate said, opening them. The eyes were amber. Every lantern in the cathedral flickered in time with their blink. "How thoughtful. We do so rarely get visitors. The families who sell to us prefer, naturally, not to see where their payments go. And the Crown prefers not to look at all. Your kingdom has excellent posture, swordspeaker. It has been looking away from us for a hundred years."

"We're not guests," Sable said. "We're here for her." They put their hand on Wren's shoulder. The child was staring up at the lantern-tiers with her mouth slightly open, and her glow had gone strange—pulsing in sympathy with the nearest lights, answering them, and Sable pulled her gently back against their side. "Whatever contract bought her soul, it's void. I'm here to void it."

"Mm," said the Magistrate. It drew a ledger toward itself across the desk without touching it—the book slid, obediently, three feet of polished wood, as though eager. "Sable Sterling. Last of the Oath-bound. Voice of the Third Covenant." It turned pages. Sable heard names whisper past—names of the dead, Sable realized, names of everyone the order had ever been, recorded in this thing's handwriting. "Your order was inconvenient. You are aware of that, I assume. Four hundred years of inconvenient. Guarding doors we had lawful right to walk through. Refusing to acknowledge properly executed purchases." A page turned. "We were very pleased when your kingdom solved that problem for us. Crown Wardens are such efficient contractors. We recommended them to several neighboring courts."

Sable's sword-hand ached. Fifteen years of silence pressed up behind their teeth. *Say the words. One true word, spoken into the bone of this place, and half these lanterns shatter—*

And then they looked up. At the tiers. At the thousands of small breathing lights, each one a person, each one humming *here, here, here* in the dark—and understood, with a lurch, exactly what one true word would buy. The Magistrate's accumulated truths had shattered Sable's blade-speech in the fight at the archive road; here, in the seat of its power, surrounded by everything it owned? They'd bring the whole cathedral down on the heads of everyone they'd come to save. The Choir. Wren. Themselves.

The trap wasn't the desk or the dark or the amber eyes.

The trap was that everything Sable knew how to destroy was also everything they'd come to free.

"You're doing arithmetic," the Magistrate observed pleasantly. It folded its long hands on the ledger. "I admire arithmetic. Shall I save you the sums?"

* * *

It came around the desk as it spoke, gliding more than walking, and the lantern-light bent toward it the way flowers bend toward sun. Up close, its face resolved into something worse than featureless: features that were *almost* there, a nose suggested, a mouth implied, as if a human face had been described to it secondhand by someone who'd never liked humans much.

"A century ago, your Crown came to us with a problem," the Magistrate said. "Mountains are restless things. The deep roads shift. Things crawl up. Kingdoms dislike this. We offered stability—a quiet border, sealed ways, no crawling—for a modest fee. Souls, surrendered freely by their families, delivered by their own consent. Free will, swordspeaker, is the oldest magic there is. We cannot take. We can only receive. Your civilization does the taking for us, and calls it debt, and calls it sale, and sleeps soundly after."

"Wren was seven," Sable said. "She didn't consent to anything."

"No. But her parents did. Beautifully documented." The Magistrate tapped the ledger. "Consent flows downhill, legally speaking. A parent's will covers a child, the way a roof covers a room. Your lover found the entry—I believe she showed it to you. Page nine thousand and forty-one. Very neat margins."

Ilsabet's grip on Sable's hand turned to iron. "You keep records," she said, and her voice shook with fury dressed in precision, "because records are your armor. As long as everything is written down, everything is *legal*, and as long as everything is legal, no one has to call it evil."

"And yet," the Magistrate said, with genuine warmth, "you are the first person in a hundred years to come down here and say so to my face. Do you know how refreshing that is?" It tilted its almost-face toward Wren. The child glowed brighter, defiantly, a small sun holding its ground. "Thirteen moons since her sale. One remains. When it wanes, the binding completes, and she comes to us—not to a lantern, she's far too interesting for a lantern, she'll serve at court—and her family's debt is settled, and the mountain stays quiet, and everyone lives happily within their arrangements."

"Not happening," said Corvane, from the rear. His voice was gravel and blood. He hadn't stopped pointing his sword at the Magistrate since they'd entered, and his arm had not wavered once, which Sable suspected was costing him roughly a lifetime of pain per minute. "Cut to the part where you make your rotten offer. There's always a rotten offer. You've been circling it since the door."

The Magistrate smiled. It was deeply unsettling, mostly because the smile arrived a full second after the mouth moved, like thunder after lightning.

"Perceptive," it said. "Yes. The offer." It returned to the desk and lifted from among its ledgers a single slim volume, bound in white leather, and set it down facing Sable. On its cover, embossed in silver, was a symbol Sable's hands remembered tracing in candlelight when they were six years old: the covenant-mark, the crossed blade beneath the open eye.

"The last swordspeaker voice in the world," the Magistrate said softly. Around the cathedral, the lanterns dimmed, leaning in. Even the Hollow Choir's murmur hushed. "Do you understand what you carry, Sable Sterling? Every word of the old tongue is a truth with an edge on it. Your order spent four hundred years learning to speak in a way that *unmakes lies*. Lies are our currency. Our contracts, our consents, our beautiful legal margins—all of it rests on the world believing that a sold soul stays sold. One voice, properly trained, could read our ledger aloud in the old tongue and every false entry in it would burn. Yours is the last such voice in existence. When you die—and you will, everyone does—we are finished with inconvenience forever."

The amber eyes fixed on Sable, and the whole cathedral seemed to hold its breath, ten thousand lanterns pulsing slower, slower.

"So here is my offer, and I will make it only once, and it is generous because I am, despite reputation, a creature of exact fairness. The girl goes free. Tonight. Binding dissolved, debt transferred, family record amended. She walks out of this mountain and lives whatever long, loud, ordinary life she likes." The almost-smile again. "In exchange, you give me your voice. Not your words—we have plenty of those. Your *voice*. The instrument. The thing the old tongue lives in when it leaves you. You will speak, afterward, in whatever flat common fashion you please, and no one will ever again hear a truth with an edge in it. The covenant dies with your voice in my keeping, and I will keep it very safely, in a lantern of its own, where it can threaten no one ever again."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and leans.

Sable felt it land in their chest like a hook finding meat.

Their voice. Their mother humming in a kitchen that burned. Their first oath-word, spoken at fourteen with bleeding palms. The command that had cracked a Warden's shield on the archive road. Fifteen years of silence chosen, and now the choice offered back to them inverted—give it up forever, willingly, and the one thing that defined them, the last living piece of everyone they'd loved, would hang in this dark forever among people who couldn't remember their own names.

*Here,* the lanterns murmured, pitying or inviting, impossible to tell.

"That's your price?" Sable managed. "One voice for one child."

"One *irreplaceable* voice," the Magistrate corrected gently, "for one child who was, let us be honest, sold cheaply. Two winters of harvest debt. Your order would have called it an insult. But then—" the amber eyes glittered, "—your order is dead, and I am still counting."

Behind Sable, Wren tugged their sleeve. They looked down.

The child was shaking her head. Slowly, solemnly, both hands raised, she made the signs Sable had taught her on the road—*no trade, no trade*—and then, hesitating, a sign Sable had never taught her, one her own hands invented on the spot: she pointed at Sable's throat, at the old scar across it, and then pressed both fists over her own heart, and held them there.

*What's in your throat is yours. What's in your heart is mine to protect too.*

Sable's eyes stung. Fifteen years of being no one, of sharpening themselves into arithmetic and fog and anonymity, and a mute eight-year-old had just seen through all of it in one gesture.

The Magistrate watched this exchange with the air of a merchant watching a customer fall in love with merchandise.

"You needn't answer tonight," it said, gliding back behind its desk, drawing the white ledger toward itself, dipping a quill in ink that gleamed like lantern-light. "Indeed, I insist you don't. Consider it while you rest—we have accommodations, quite comfortable, the Choir will sing you calm. The thirteenth moon rises in twenty-nine days." The quill paused above the page. "Though I should mention, in the interest of exact fairness, that your archivist friend has committed treason against her Crown by aiding you, and the Crown's Wardens have, as of this morning, been granted access to our upper ways. They are, even now, entering the root-tunnels behind you. Looking for the last swordspeaker." The amber eyes lifted, mild and terrible. "So whatever you decide, decide quickly. The mountain, as I said, is a restless thing. And it is about to be considerably less empty."

Far above, faint through miles of bone and root, came a sound Sable knew in their bones: the ring of boot-heels on stone. Many boots. Descending.

Corvane swore. Ilsabet's hand crushed Sable's. And Wren began, very softly, to hum.

*Here,* sang the lanterns. *Here. Here.*
