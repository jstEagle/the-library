## 15. Refusal and Ruin

The Pale Magistrate's offer hung in the amber air of the cathedral like a fly caught in resin.

Sable had been offered many things in their life. Gold by smugglers. Silence by the Crown Wardens. A quick death, once, by a man in Ashmoor whose wife Sable had guided safely over the pass — a kindness, he'd insisted, delivered with a knife. But no one had ever offered to buy the voice out of their throat before, and there was something almost flattering about it, in a horrible way.

"No," said Sable Sterling.

The word fell into the bone-and-amber cathedral and did not echo. Nothing echoed here. The Undercourt swallowed sound the way a miser swallows coins.

The Pale Magistrate tilted his head. He was tall the way a stalactite is tall — not grown but *deposited*, shaped by centuries of slow dripping patience. His face was smooth as an egg, his eyes were two lanterns turned down to their lowest wick, and when he smiled it was less an expression than a structural failure.

"I had hoped," he said, "for something more elaborate. You swordspeakers always were dramatic. 'No' is very economical of you."

"I've been economizing for fifteen years," Sable said. "Practice."

Behind them, Wren made a small sound — not a word, never a word, she hadn't spoken since the roots began — and Sable felt her small hand find the hem of their coat and grip it. Ilsabet stood on Sable's other side, arms crossed, archivist's composure stretched over fear the way vellum stretches over a binding: smoothly, and only if nobody pulled too hard.

"Then we proceed to the part I enjoy more," the Magistrate said pleasantly. "The demonstration."

Sable drew the sword.

It wasn't much to look at. Fifteen years of smuggler's guide work had taught them to carry unremarkable things, and the blade was plain steel with a plain hilt, the kind of weapon that said *I am here for wolves and bandits and nothing interesting*. But the steel remembered. Steel that has been sung to remembers the way riverbeds remember rain.

They planted their feet on the amber floor and spoke the first word of the old tongue into the flat of the blade.

Here is what sword-speech is, for those lucky enough never to have heard it: it is language given edges. A spoken word moves through air and fades. A word sung into a blade does not fade. It travels along the metal and comes out the other side as something between sound and fact, and what it says becomes briefly, terribly true.

Sable spoke *sunder*.

The word left the sword like a white bird leaving a burning house. It crossed the cathedral in a line of light and struck the nearest pillar — a column of woven root and amber thick as three men — and the pillar cracked from crown to base with a sound like a glacier clearing its throat.

Wren flinched. Ilsabet did not, though her knuckles went white where they gripped her own elbows.

"There," Sable said. "That was the polite version. Give me the girl and the archivist walks out beside us, and you'll never hear the impolite version."

The Magistrate regarded the cracked pillar with the mild disappointment of a man finding a hair in his soup.

"You broke my east colonnade," he said. "Do you know how long amber takes to grow here? Longer than your kingdom has existed. Longer than your kingdom will exist, if the Crown has its arithmetic right." He folded his long hands. "But thank you. Truly. You've made the next part easier."

"How do you figure?"

"Because now I know which truths you're carrying." His lantern-eyes brightened by a single wick. "Every swordspeaker's voice is built out of the oaths they swore. Yours was sworn at Thornfell altar, at moonrise, by a child of — what were you, eleven? Twelve? You swore to guard the borders between courts. To hold the line. To speak truth with edge and edge with truth." He leaned forward, and the amber around him seemed to lean too, the whole cathedral tilting in like a curious crowd. "And then, fifteen years ago, you ran."

The word landed in Sable's chest like a thrown stone.

"I didn't run—"

"*You ran.*" Not loud. The Magistrate never needed to be loud; the room did his shouting for him. "You ran while your order burned. You ran while Corvane held the gate. You ran past the border-stones your mother died on, and you kept running until you found a fog-wrapped pass where nobody asks questions and a man can be no one. And every word of sword-speech you have ever spoken since has been spoken *by a liar*, because the voice itself is an oath, and oaths cannot live in throats that abandoned them."

"That's not—" Sable started, and stopped, because here was the terrible thing: the sword felt lighter in their hand than it should have. Hollow, somehow. Like a bell with its clapper stolen.

"The court deals in spoken truths," the Magistrate said, spreading his hands with genuine warmth, a host welcoming guests to a table already set. "So let us speak some. Speak your oath aloud, last of the Swordspeakers. Right now. All of it. If your voice still believes you, break my other pillar. Go on."

Sable raised the sword.

They spoke *sunder* again — and this time they meant it, meant it down to the marrow, meant it with every year of grief and guilt and smuggler's cold mornings stacked behind it —

—and the word came out of the blade as smoke.

It drifted across the cathedral, thin and gray, and dissipated against the Magistrate's chest like breath on glass.

The silence afterward was total. Even Wren's breathing seemed to stop.

"Oh," Sable said quietly. Because they understood now. The accumulated truths of the Undercourt — centuries of confessions pried from desperate families, bargains witnessed, souls weighed — all of it hung in the amber walls like sediment in old honey, and against that weight, one broken oath-bearer's word simply *drowned*.

"Now you see why I wanted to buy it," the Magistrate said gently. "Even broken, it is the last instrument that could ever threaten me. Broken instruments are dangerous precisely because they don't know they're broken. But you know now, don't you? I could feel it land." He sighed, a sound like wind through a crypt. "Take the archivist."

Sable turned. Two figures had come out of the side gallery without any footstep Sable heard — wardens of the court, if such things could be called wardens, tall and jointed wrong, faces like unfinished sculptures. One of them had Ilsabet by both arms.

She didn't struggle. That was the worst part. Ilsabet Vael had spent thirty years among sealed ledgers and dangerous knowledge, and she knew a losing position when she stood in one. Her eyes met Sable's over the creature's shoulder, and they were furious, and they were afraid, and beneath both of those — this was the thing that would keep Sable awake for the rest of however long their life turned out to be — beneath both of them she looked *resigned*, the way you look when a debt you lent in love finally comes due and you'd known, all along, you'd known—

"Don't," she said. "Whatever you're calculating, don't."

"Let her go," Sable said.

"With what?" The Magistrate sounded honestly curious. "Your word is smoke. Your blade is a letter opener. Your ally lies bleeding somewhere above the roots, if he lives, which the odds say he doesn't. And the child—" he turned his lantern gaze on Wren, who stood her ground, chin up, bare feet planted on amber, thirteen moons of borrowed time ticking away inside her like water through a crack "—the child belongs to the ledger. She was sold legally, fairly, with signatures. The Crown countersigned. Your kingdom's entire mountain stability rests upon my collecting accounts in good order." A pause. "Although."

"Although," Sable repeated flatly.

"Although I am, at heart, a reasonable creditor." The Magistrate drifted closer, and up close he smelled of cold candlewax and old paper. "Here are amended terms. You have two moons until her thirteenth turn. Bring me your voice — freely given, fully surrendered, spoken aloud in release — before that moon rises full, and the girl's page burns, and the archivist goes free, and you may crawl back to your fog and your anonymity and die a no one, as you so clearly wish."

"And if I don't come?"

"Then on the thirteenth moon, the girl binds. Permanently. Irretrievably. And the archivist—" he glanced at the creatures holding her "—becomes a permanent resident of our archive. We are always short of willing readers. Most of our documents bite."

Ilsabet laughed. Actually laughed — a short, sharp, terrified bark of it. "You absolute monster," she told the Magistrate, "you've read my personnel file."

"I've read everything," the Magistrate agreed. "It's the job."

He snapped his fingers — or made the gesture; no sound accompanied it — and the creatures withdrew toward the deep gallery, Ilsabet between them. She twisted once to look back.

"Sable." Her voice carried strangely well in the dead-air cathedral, as if the room wanted her words preserved. "Two moons. Don't do anything noble. Do something *clever*. Nobility is just pride with a budget."

And then the dark of the gallery took her, and she was gone.

*

They fled upward through the root-tunnels with Wren's hand locked in Sable's, running past the places where the Hollow Choir's faded song still clung to the walls like moss. Sable half expected the song to reach for Wren again — in the deeper passages it had, tugging at her like a tide — but tonight the girl ran solid and warm and *present*, and once, scrambling over a root-fall, she squeezed Sable's fingers twice.

Twice. In the old tongue's hand-sign, the one mothers used: *I am here. You are here.*

Sable nearly wept and settled for nearly tripping, which was close enough emotionally.

They surfaced into the Weeping Wood near midnight, under a sky rinsed clean by earlier rain, and made a cold camp in the hollow of a bleeding tree whose sap fell around them in slow silver drops, like the forest crying politely at a distance rather than making a scene.

Wren sat with her knees drawn up, watching Sable's face with those too-old eyes. Then she picked up a stick and wrote in the soft black loam:

**YOU LOOK BROKEN.**

"Gee, thanks."

She crossed it out and wrote: **BAD-BROKEN OR THINKING-BROKEN?**

Sable huffed despite everything. The kid had developed a whole taxonomy. "Thinking-broken."

Wren considered this. Drew a small circle. Inside it, she drew a stick figure with a sword, and then, thoughtfully, a second smaller figure holding the first one's hand. She pointed at the second figure. Pointed at herself. Pointed at Sable.

*I am here. You are here.*

"It's not enough this time, sprout," Sable said softly. "You can't hold my hand through this one. They took Ilsabet because of me. Corvane's probably dead because of me. And in two moons either I cut out the only thing I ever was, or—" They stopped. Pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "Either way I lose. That's the arithmetic. There's no good column."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then she wrote, slowly, pressing hard enough that the stick snapped and she had to find another:

**MAREN SAID YOU ONCE CARRIED HER WHOLE CART UP THE PASS WHEN THE OX DIED. ALONE. WHY?**

"Because she'd have lost everything otherwise. It was just — heavy. Heavy isn't a reason not to."

Wren tapped the words. Tapped them again. **HEAVY ISN'T A REASON.**

"That's different. That was a cart."

Wren's look over the firelight was devastatingly unimpressed. Then she lay down against Sable's side, the way she'd done every night since the cabin, and within minutes her breathing went slow and even, trusting as a door left unlocked.

Sable sat awake, holding a sword that was now only a sword, listening to a child breathe, and did the honest arithmetic at last.

The Magistrate was right about one thing: the oath was broken. Had been broken for fifteen years, and no word spoken through that crack would ever fly true again. Every plan built on sword-speech, on being the last guardian, on the *voice* — all of it was a house with a rotted beam pretending to stand.

But.

But the Magistrate had also, in his smug cataloguing, missed something. He'd counted Sable's truths — the massacre, the running, the hiding — and weighed them, and found Sable wanting. Fine. True. Guilty as charged.

He had not counted *theirs*. He had not counted Wren choosing to be sold to save her brother. He had not counted Ilsabet spending her freedom on a fool who came back after fifteen years. He had not counted Corvane holding a gate with the last of his ruined voice. He had not counted Maren looking away, and then, later, *not* looking away. The court dealt in spoken truths, and the Undercourt's shelves were full of purchased ones, signed and sealed and owned.

Nobody had ever asked it to weigh truths that were given free.

Sable looked down at the sleeping child, and at the plain sword across their knees, and understood — with the clarity that only arrives at the bottom of a defeat, the way water only runs clear at the source — what mattered. Not the voice. Not the order, or the oath, or the name. What mattered was sitting right there, breathing against their ribs, and it was locked in a bone cathedral two moons from forever, alongside a woman who deserved better than a fool's nobility.

Clever, then. Ilsabet had said clever.

Sable looked up through the weeping branches at the moon — fat, rising, one turn past half. Two moons to full.

"All right," they whispered to it, to the wood, to whatever was listening in the dark. "Two moons. Teach me to be worth something without my voice."

Below them, far beneath the roots, faint as memory, the Hollow Choir had begun — very softly — to sing.

And for the first time, Sable noticed: the fragments they sang weren't random anymore.

They were practicing *her* song.
