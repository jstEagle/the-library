## 16. What Wren Remembers

The roots of the Weeping Wood do not sleep, and neither, Wren thinks, does anything else down here.

She lies in the hollow between two great root-fingers, curled the way a fern curls before it opens, and listens to the wood breathe. The breathing is slow and wet and warm. Above them, somewhere far away where the light turns green-gold, the trees are weeping again—she can feel it in her teeth, each drop of sap letting go of its branch like a word someone was too frightened to say. The sap falls. The roots drink. The wood remembers everything it is given.

Wren likes that about the wood. It remembers so you don't have to carry it alone.

Beside her, Sable is not sleeping either. Sable never truly sleeps; they hover at the edge of it like a bird on a fence post, ready to fly at any sound. Wren has learned the sound of Sable's almost-sleep: breath that pretends to be even, hands that stay curled near the knife even in dreams, especially in dreams. Ilsabet sleeps the other way entirely, deep and trusting as a stone sinking in a pond, one arm thrown over her eyes as if even unconsciousness requires dramatics. Wren has decided she likes Ilsabet very much. She talks with her whole face. Even angry—which she mostly is, at Sable, in that way that isn't really anger but love wearing armor—her face says everything twice.

The fire is small. Sable keeps it small, always, a coin-sized flame that eats dry root-lichen and gives off more light than heat. Wren watches the shadows climb the curved ceiling of the hollow and thinks about the thing she has not told anyone.

It is getting heavy, the not-telling. Heavier every day. It sits behind her ribs like a swallowed stone from the river at home—the kind she used to swallow accidentally when she drank too fast, except this stone doesn't pass. This stone grows.

Tonight, she decides, she will give it away.

* * *

Wren cannot speak. She has not spoken since the day they took her voice for the ledger—not her *words*, exactly, but something underneath her words, the deep current that made speaking feel like singing instead of shouting. In Ashmoor, before all this, before the mountain took her, people had shouted at each other across fences and no one thought it strange. Wren had tried once, after, to shout. What came out was nothing. Her mouth opened and the mountain closed its hand around whatever lived in her throat.

But she can draw.

Her charcoal stub is worn to the size of her thumbnail now, saved from the last village they passed through, stolen honestly from a dead man's satchel, which Corvane said didn't count as stealing because dead men have no use for art. She has paper too—three pages torn from the back of Ilsabet's least favorite ledger, given grudgingly and then guarded jealously by Wren ever since.

She waits until the fire gutters low and Sable's almost-breathing goes soft, until the wood's own pulse rises up through the ground to fill the quiet. Then she sits up, cross-legged, and smooths the first page flat against her knee.

She begins with home.

A house with a sagging roof, drawn the way houses look when you are small: enormous door, crooked chimney, smoke like a scribble. A fence. A goat—goats matter; the goat was named Turnip and bit everyone including Father, especially Father. Behind the house, the mountain, drawn huge because mountains are always bigger than your drawing wants them to be, and beneath the mountain she draws a door, and beneath the door she draws stairs going down and down and down, off the edge of the page, into the dark.

Then she draws the family. Mother by the fire, hunched. Father with his hat in his hands, which he only did when the debt-man came. And two children.

One child is big. One child is small.

Wren pauses over the small one. Her chest does the thing it does when she thinks of him—the tight squeeze, the ache with warmth inside it, like holding a coal in mittens. Tam. Tam who was born in the winter the debt came due. Tam who slept in the drawer of the dresser because there was no cradle. Tam whose fists were smaller than Turnip's nose.

She looks at the sleeping adults. Sable's eyes are open now. They are always open eventually; you cannot draw secrets in the dark without summoning a swordspeaker, apparently. Wren holds up the page anyway, tilting it toward the firelight, toward Sable's face.

Sable goes still in that particular way they have, the way that means *I am pretending to be calm and I would like you to believe me.*

* * *

Wren works while Sable watches.

The next page: the debt-man. She draws him tall and thin, with a coat that swallows his shoulders, and she draws the paper in his hand with little marks on it—marks like the ones in the ledgers, marks like the ones Sable showed her in the archive pages, the contract-script that tastes of iron when you look at it too long. Around the debt-man's feet she draws coins. Not many. Never enough. She draws Father's hat coming off, Father bending, Father's spine curving like a bow drawn back.

Then she draws the choice.

This part takes the longest, and her hand shakes, and she presses so hard the charcoal snaps once and she has to hold the stub with just her fingertips to keep going.

She draws herself. Small figure, straight-backed—she makes sure the figure is standing straight, this matters, this is the whole point—and beside her she draws Tam in his drawer, asleep, tiny, a curl of breath above his head. Then she draws an arrow from Tam to the mountain-door, and crosses it out. Crosses it out hard, three times, until the page nearly tears.

And then an arrow from herself to the mountain-door. And she does not cross it out.

She writes beneath it, in the old glyphs that live in her fingers the way songs live in other people's throats, the symbols Sable taught her without meaning to, humming over the cookfire, tracing in spilled tea: *I go. He stays.*

Sable makes a sound. It is not a word. It comes from somewhere below words, below the scar on their throat, a small cracked noise like ice shifting on a pond in early spring.

Wren nods. Yes. That one. That is the true one.

* * *

Here is what happened, and she will make them understand it if it takes every page in Ilsabet's ledger and both sides of the lichen:

Two winters past, the debt-man came in the black month, when the goat had frozen and the roof had leaked and Tam was new and screaming with hunger. Father owed money to men whose names you did not say indoors. Mother had already sold the good blanket and the wedding ring and the copper pot, and the debt-man had looked at all of it, at the empty corners where things used to be, and then he had looked at the dresser drawer, where Tam was sleeping with his fists tucked under his chin.

*They take children now,* the debt-man said, in the flat voice of a man reading from a paper. *The mountain pays better for young ones. Clean souls. Unmarked.* He tapped the ledger. *Or we settle another way, and the other way involves fire.*

Wren had been awake. She was always awake those months; hunger kept odd hours. She had heard everything from her pallet by the wall, and she had watched her mother's face break apart like wet bread, and she had watched her father reach for the drawer—

—and she had stood up.

She had stood up and walked across the room, seven steps, she counted them forever afterward, seven steps on cold floorboards, and she had put her hand on the debt-man's ledger, and she had pointed at her own chest.

*Not him,* she said, in the only language she had left that meant anything. *Me.*

She had known what she was doing. People will want to say later that she hadn't, that she was a child, that children don't understand bargains—but Wren understood. She understood the way the wood understands rain. She knew the mountain paid for clean souls, and she knew Tam's soul was the cleanest thing in that house or any house, new and unspent and smelling of milk, and she knew hers had already been touched by the old tongue, marked by it, sung to it by a grandmother who came from the high valleys where people still remembered. The mountain wanted marked souls. Marked souls were worth *more*. Which meant she wasn't just saving Tam.

She was worth more than Tam. For once—for the only time in her entire small life—being strange, being wrong, being the girl who hummed tunes nobody knew and drew symbols in the dirt and stared at the mountain like it was humming back, being all of that finally added up to something.

She had kissed Tam's fist goodbye, right on the tiny knuckles, while he slept. He would never remember it. That was the point. That was the whole beautiful point: she could spend herself completely, and he would never even owe her the memory.

Then she had taken the debt-man's cold hand, and she had walked out the door, and she had not cried until the trees started.

* * *

By now Ilsabet is awake too, sitting up with her hair loose and wild, one hand pressed over her mouth. Wren shows her the pages in order. House. Goat. Debt-man. Drawer. Arrow crossed out. Arrow not crossed out. *I go. He stays.*

Ilsabet's eyes are shining and furious at once, which is a very Ilsabet expression, and she says something soft and broken in the court-tongue, and then, because she is Ilsabet, she adds something drier that makes even Sable huff—a laugh-shaped sound with no laughter in it, but a huff nonetheless.

Wren saves the last page.

On it, she draws the thing she has never shown anyone, the thing that has sat behind her ribs growing heavier with every mile of this long dark road. She draws herself again—straight-backed, chin up—and beside the little figure she draws the scales. On one side of the scale: a small lump, a bundle, a baby-shape. On the other side: herself, and under herself she writes the glyph for *worthless*, and then she crosses out *worthless*, and writes instead the glyph for *enough*.

Because here is the truth she needs them to hold for her, since she cannot hold it alone:

Everyone keeps looking at her like she is something that was *done to*. Sable looks at her that way, with that storm-cloud face, all guilt and fury, as if Wren is a wound the world inflicted on their conscience. Even the hedge-witch had clucked over her like a hen over a lost egg. And Wren is tired of it. Not the kindness—kindness is fine, kindness is warm bread. But the *shape* of it, the pity-shape, the shape that says *poor thing, swept away, no say in any of it.*

She had a say. She had the only say that mattered. Out of everyone in that room—Father with his bent spine, Mother with her breaking face, the debt-man with his iron-flavored paper—Wren was the only one who chose. Everyone else was chosen *for*, by fear, by debt, by the mountain. She chose. And if choosing to walk into the dark so a baby could keep sleeping in his drawer isn't honor, then Wren would like to know what these oath-bound swordspeakers think honor actually *is*, because from where she lies in the roots of the Weeping Wood, it looks suspiciously like walking toward the thing you're afraid of so that someone smaller than you doesn't have to.

She taps the last page. Taps it again. Looks at Sable with the full force of her eyes, which Maren in Ashmoor once said were old eyes, mountain eyes, and which Wren hopes are saying now: *You understand. You of all people, you understand.*

Sable reaches out slowly—asking permission with the pause, always asking, having learned—and touches the crossed-out arrow. The arrow that pointed at Tam. Their fingers rest there a long moment.

Then they touch the uncrossed arrow. The one pointing at her.

Their mouth moves around words that won't come out right, words about waste, about children, about what should have been—and Wren shakes her head, firm, and pulls the page back, and points at the scale. Points at *enough*.

Ilsabet translates, quietly, her voice roughened: "She's telling you it wasn't taken. It was given." A pause. "And she'd like you to stop treating a gift like a theft."

Sable closes their eyes. When they open them again, something has shifted in there, some load-bearing grief quietly re-stacked into something that can be built with instead of something that collapses. Wren watches it happen and feels, for the first time in two winters, lighter. The stone behind her ribs has come loose. It is in the pages now. The pages can hold it. The wood can drink it.

She leans her head against Sable's shoulder—briefly, because Sable startles at touch like a cat at a cucumber, but they don't pull away, and after a moment their arm comes up around her, careful as handling a lantern flame.

Above them, far away, the trees weep. Down here, in the warm root-hollow, nobody does.

* * *

She is nearly asleep—truly asleep, the deep kind, the trusting kind, the kind she has only learned to do again since the wood began singing back to her—when Sable moves.

Wren feels it more than hears it: the change in the air, the coiling of muscle, the almost-sound of a blade clearing leather without quite being drawn. Her eyes snap open. Across the hollow, Ilsabet is awake too, frozen mid-motion, finger to her lips.

From deeper in the root-tunnels—from the direction of the Hollow Choir, the faded ones, the nameless singers—comes a sound that should not exist.

Footsteps. Many. And threaded through them, faint and rising, voices singing in the old tongue a song none of them taught the Choir—a marching song, an *oath-song*, one Sable has been humming fragments of for weeks without knowing Wren was memorizing it.

The song is getting louder. The footsteps are getting closer.

And beneath it all, so faint it might be the wood itself dreaming, Wren hears something else: a voice like dry paper, like a closing ledger, like a door at the top of a stair going down and down.

The Magistrate knows where they are.
