## 17. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The abandoned shepherd's hut above the Ashmoor treeline had three virtues: it was invisible from the road, it had a hearth that still drew properly, and its floorboards were wide enough to practice sword-speech without waking the dead beneath them.

That last virtue mattered more than Sable had expected.

"Again," they said, and Wren sighed the way only a child of twelve moons-and-counting could sigh—the theatrical, whole-body sigh of someone who would rather be doing literally anything else, including chores, including being kidnapped again.

The girl lifted her practice blade—a hazel branch Sable had trimmed and wrapped with leather—and traced the first glyph in the air. *Vessel.* The stroke should have hummed against the world like a plucked string. Instead it wobbled at the crossbar, and the faint warmth that gathered in the room guttered out like a candle pinched by a draft.

"It's crooked," Wren said, in the old tongue, because her common speech was still shy and halting but her mother-tongue—her real mother-tongue, the one sold with her soul—came easy as breathing.

"It's honest," Sable said. "Honest is better than pretty. Pretty gets people killed."

"That doesn't make sense."

"No," Sable admitted, settling onto the bench across from her. "But it's true, which is the same thing here. Sit. We'll do it slow."

They did it slow. That was the whole method, really. Fifteen years ago Sable had learned sword-speech the way the order taught everything: kneeling in cold courtyards before dawn, masters walking the rows with switches, repetition until the glyphs lived in the hand instead of the mind. Sable had hated every frozen morning of it and had never once doubted it worked. But Wren wasn't a recruit. She was a girl with one moon and a half-turn left before her soul settled into the Undercourt's keeping like sediment in wine, and she deserved to learn the language the way her ancestors must have first found it: not as a weapon, but as a voice.

So they started where Sable's own mother had started, on the night Sable remembered best from a childhood otherwise full of ash.

*Vessel,* Sable signed slowly, drawing the shape in the air between them with one finger. "This one means 'the thing that carries.' A cup. A boat. A body." They tapped Wren's sternum gently. "You are this one. So am I. Everything else we learn today hangs off it."

Wren copied it. This time the crossbar held straight, and something answered—a low resonance in the floorboards, a warmth like sun through wool.

Sable's throat tightened. They covered it by clearing their throat, loudly, twice, like a man with a cold.

"There," they managed. "See? You're already better than I was at your age. I once made *vessel* so badly that Master Corvin said I'd invented a new glyph meaning 'leaky bucket.'"

Wren grinned. It transformed her face. For two moons now Sable had watched her carry grief the size of a mountain behind those too-old eyes—the brother she'd saved, the family who'd sold her, the wood that sang to her—and when she grinned like that, all of it fell away for a heartbeat, and she was just a child learning letters.

"What does mine mean?" she asked, tracing the next mark in her sequence—the one born under her collarbone, inked there the day of her sale. "My mark. The one they put on me. What does it say?"

Sable had been dreading this question for two moons. They'd hoped she wouldn't ask until after. But children ask the hard questions early; it's adults who learn to wait.

"It says *kept*," Sable said. "It's a claim-mark. Like a brand on a barrel."

"Oh." Wren looked down at her hands. Then, in the smallest voice Sable had heard from her yet: "Can you teach me the word that un-keeps?"

And there it was. The reason for all of this, laid bare on the floorboards between them.

"Yes," Sable said. "But not today. Today you learn *vessel*, and tomorrow you learn *witness*, and the moon after that, if the gods are kind and the Wardens stay stupid, you learn the release-words. And here's the thing, Wren—" they leaned forward, caught her eyes, held them— "you learn all of it even if I'm the one who says them. Especially then. Because words die when only one mouth knows them. Your job isn't to save yourself. Your job is to be the cup that carries the language past me. Can you do that?"

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate weighing a land dispute.

"So I'm not learning to fight."

"You're learning to survive fighting."

"I'm learning to be a bucket."

"*Vessel*," Sable corrected, and Wren laughed, and outside the hut the wind moved through the pines carrying, faintly, a thread of melody that might have been wind and might have been the Weeping Wood calling its stolen daughter home.

* * *

Ilsabet's messages began arriving nine days later, and they arrived the way most important things arrive: absurdly.

Corvane brought the first one up from the valley, cradling a scrap of parchment like it was a relic, which in fairness it was. He'd traded three rounds of pit-fighting and a considerable amount of dignity to get it from a garrison laundry-woman whose sister owed him money.

"She gnawed it," Corvane announced, dropping onto Sable's bench and helping himself to the stew pot without invitation. "Your archivist. Gnawed it herself, with her own teeth, to make it look rat-eaten. Said the Wardens burn anything written but let the vermin-damaged pages pile up for kindling. Said—" he squinted at the parchment, turning it over— "well. You're the one who reads her handwriting."

Sable took it. The page was from an old trade ledger, the kind Ilsabet had catalogued ten thousand of in the Vael's Crossing archive, and along its margins ran columns of figures in her precise, upright hand. To a Warden's eye: accounting. Inventory. Nothing.

To Sable: a love letter written in numbers.

Ilsabet had spent fifteen years as keeper of the Undercourt's paper trail. She knew its ledgers the way Sable knew sword-forms. And the figures were wrong in exactly the ways only Sable could catch—because Sable had spent one feverish autumn in that archive, years ago, learning the cipher Ilsabet used for her private marginalia, back when their biggest worry was whether the order's elders would notice two junior members laughing too long over the same scroll.

*Entry 44: 3 barrels pitch, 2 lantern oil, 1 short.* Three Wardens' patrols on the east road, two on the west, one gap short at the mill ford, dusk.

*Entry 45: candles burned 40, remainder 12.* Forty days of her sentence served. Twelve days until transfer to the mountain garrison—which sat directly above the Undercourt, which meant once she was moved, no rescue on earth would reach her.

*Entry 46: the tall candle gutters unevenly. Note the flaw.* The Magistrate himself had taken personal interest in her interrogation. She was holding. Barely.

*Entry 47: remember the third shelf. It always leaned.* And there, buried in dry accounting, the actual message: *the third shelf always leaned.* Which was archive-speak for *the plan we made at the third shelf the night you told me you loved me*—the escape route Ilsabet had mapped through the garrison's records vaults, the one with the loose stone behind the evidence lockers.

She'd known. Even arrested, even interrogated by a creature made of accumulated truth, she'd known Sable would come, and she was telling them how.

Sable read it four times. On the fourth reading their eyes blurred, and they set the page down with exaggerated care, the way you handle something that might break—or might make you break.

"Well?" Corvane said, around a mouthful of stew.

"She says hello."

"Does she."

"In her way." Sable fed the page to the fire before the warmth of having it could become the danger of keeping it, watching the cipher curl and blacken. In her way. Always in her way—everything oblique, everything load-bearing, fifteen years of silence and still she sent Sable a map drawn in bookkeeping. *It always leaned.* As if the shelf, the archive, the whole crooked edifice of Sable's abandoned life had simply been waiting for someone to lean on it again.

"You're making the face," Corvane observed.

"What face."

"The face you made in the pit when you watched me lose. Pitying and stubborn at the same time. Very annoying face."

"We're going to need her," Sable said. "When we go down. Not just for the route—for the ledgers. The Magistrate's court runs on recorded truths. If anyone can find the entry that breaks Wren's claim..." They didn't finish. Didn't have to.

Corvane scraped the pot clean and stood, rolling his shoulders with the creak of a man whose body had opinions about heroism these days. "Then we go get her. Before the tall candle burns out." He paused at the door, and for once the drunkard's swagger fell away entirely, leaving the duelist underneath, the second blade of the order, scarred and steady. "You know she gnawed it herself. With her teeth. For us."

"I know."

"Fifteen years, and that woman is still doing the impossible work while we blunder around with swords." He shook his head almost fondly. "Marry her, you idiot. After. If we live."

"After," Sable agreed, and didn't trust themselves to say more.

* * *

The lessons continued through the darkening weeks. Wren learned *vessel* and then *witness*—the glyph that made a spoken truth bind—and she learned it so quickly that Sable stopped pretending they were teaching and started admitting they were remembering, dredging up forms from fifteen years of deliberate forgetting.

One evening, working the *witness*-form by firelight, Wren went still mid-stroke.

"Sable." Her voice had gone strange. Younger. "When I do this one, the trees answer. Outside. Do you hear them?"

Sable listened. Beneath the wind: a faint creaking, rhythmic, patient. The Weeping Wood's roots ran under these mountains like veins under skin. The wood had been listening to its lost daughter's lessons.

"They've been answering since we got here," Sable said. "I think they're counting the moons too."

Wren finished the glyph. It hung in the air, glowing softly amber, perfect—and somewhere far below, something ancient shifted its weight, like a creditor hearing footsteps on his stairs.

"Teach me faster," Wren said.

* * *

On the last night of the twelfth moon, Sable woke to find the girl standing at the window, practice blade in hand, tracing forms against the stars. She'd gotten good. Better than good—the strokes came off her fingers like water finding its level, and each one rang faintly in the world, and Sable lay still in the dark and felt something loosen in their chest that had been clenched for fifteen years.

If Sable fell in the deep places—and the arithmetic said they probably would—the language would not fall with them. It would walk out of the dark on twelve-year-old legs, humming, carried in a vessel that had already proven it could hold more than its share of sorrow.

Legacy, Sable thought. Their masters had called it that. They'd imagined banners and deathbed speeches. It was actually a skinny kid drawing letters on the dark with a stick, tongue poking out in concentration, getting it slightly wrong and getting it right anyway.

At dawn, Corvane returned from the valley at a run, snow flying, and thrust a fresh-gnawed scrap of ledger into Sable's hands.

*Final entry before transfer. The tall candle goes to the mountain in six days. The third shelf remembers. Come leaning.*

Six days.

Sable folded the message, looked at Wren—at the vessel standing in the doorway with the dawn behind her and the whole old language living in her hands—and heard themselves say, in a voice steadier than they felt:

"Wren. Fetch your boots. It's time we talked about the third shelf."
