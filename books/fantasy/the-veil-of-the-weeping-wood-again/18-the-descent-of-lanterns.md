## 18. The Descent of Lanterns

*Sable*

The parley flag had been Ilsabet's idea, scrawled on the back of a stolen ledger page in ink she'd mixed from ash and pine resin because, as she put it, "if we're going to commit treason against a court that eats souls, we might as well do it with proper heraldry."

It was not, strictly speaking, a flag. It was a strip of white linen torn from Corvane's spare shirt, dyed at one corner with the sigil of the Swordspeakers — a blade crossed over an open hand — which Sable painted with fingers gone clumsy and cold. The old glyphs around it they drew from memory, each stroke a small act of grief.

"Does it actually mean anything?" Wren asked, in the old tongue, watching them work. She could speak now, in that language only — her voice a small clear bell that made the roots of the Weeping Wood lean toward her when she sang. "Or is it just decoration?"

"It means *I come unarmed to speak truth.*" Sable tied the last knot. "Whether the Magistrate honors it depends entirely on how bored he is."

"And if he's not bored?"

"Then I'm about to have a very short afternoon."

Wren laughed — she laughed more easily these days, here under the trees that wept for her — but her eyes stayed serious. She took Sable's hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, over the place where the binding-glyphs pulsed faintly beneath her skin like embers under ash. Two moons left. Maybe less. The thirteenth turn had already begun; you could feel it in the wood, a slow tightening, like a fist closing around a candle flame.

"I'll be singing," she said. "The whole time. Loud enough to wake the Choir."

"Loud enough to wake the dead," Sable corrected gently.

"That too." She grinned, gap-toothed and utterly unafraid, which frightened Sable more than any warden ever had.

Ilsabet stood apart at the mouth of the root-door, arms crossed, wearing the expression she'd worn fifteen years ago when Sable had walked out of the archive and out of her life: furious, heartbroken, and refusing to let either show. Fifteen years, and then three months of running together through tunnels and garrison raids, and still they hadn't found the words. Perhaps there were none. Perhaps that was why Sable was walking into a court that dealt only in spoken truths — because everything unsaid between them was a debt, and debts could be weighed.

"You're stalling," Ilsabet said.

"I'm making sure the knot holds."

"The knot holds. You've checked it four times." Her voice softened by a single degree, which for Ilsabet was practically a sonnet. "Sable. Come back."

It wasn't a request. It wasn't quite an order. It was something older than both — the thing they'd never said at the border, all those years ago, standing in the rain while duty pulled one way and love pulled the other.

"I intend to," Sable said. "I've gotten rather good at surviving things I shouldn't."

"That's what worries me." She stepped forward and gripped their collar, hard, and pressed her forehead briefly to theirs. "Surviving isn't the same as coming back. Don't confuse them."

Then she let go, turned away to help Wren with the song-ropes — braided root-fiber strung with bells cut from the shattered lanterns of the Hollow Choir — and Sable understood that the conversation was over, in the way that some conversations are never over at all.

They shouldered the pack. They took up the flag. And Sable Sterling, last of the oath-bound, walked alone down into the dark beneath the Weeping Wood, humming a lullaby their mother had sung, so that fear would have something to keep time with.

* * *

*Corvane*

Corvane had fought in forty pit arenas across three provinces, and he had never once been this terrified.

That was the trouble with having something to lose. In the pits you lost blood, teeth, occasionally the use of a limb. Here, in the breathing dark of the root-tunnels, he stood to lose the only two people who had ever looked at him — drunk, disgraced, half-dead Corvane Ashfell — and seen anything worth saving.

He flexed his bandaged side and winced. Half-healed was generous. The warden's spear had gone in below his ribs during the garrison raid, and the hedge-witch's stitches pulled every time he moved, which was constantly, because the tunnels required constant moving. He'd named the pain *Bessie*, after a mule that had once kicked him into a river. Naming it helped. Bessie could complain all she liked; she wasn't stopping him.

Ahead of him, the Hollow Choir drifted through the roots like leaves on a slow current. There were eleven of them — souls the lanterns had cracked open, names half-remembered, faces like smoke given just enough shape to walk. Their leader had been called Merrin, once, before the Undercourt had eaten the name and left only the echo of it. Now she remembered it in pieces: *Merr-in, Merr-in*, sung under her breath like a rope she was climbing hand over hand.

"You're limping," Merrin observed, without turning around. Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way fog speaks.

"I'm pacing myself. It's a tactical distinction."

"Mm." A pause. Then, with the ghost of dry humor: "In my day, swordspeakers didn't lie so badly."

"In your day," Corvane said, "swordspeakers got massacred in their beds. I've adjusted the curriculum."

She laughed — a sound like wind through a hollow reed, strange and lovely and sad — and Corvane felt something in his chest loosen that had been tight since the night the order burned. This was why he'd come back from the pits. Not redemption. Not duty. This: walking beside the dead of his own order, carrying their memory in his feet, one step at a time, toward the thing that had eaten them.

The tunnel widened. Ahead, amber light bled up from the deep places, and the air grew thick with the smell of honey and bone. Somewhere far above, faint as a heartbeat through stone, he heard Wren begin to sing.

The whole wood leaned in to listen. So did the Choir. Eleven faded souls straightened, and for one moment Corvane saw them as they must have been — young, oath-bound, laughing around a fire — before the mountain had swallowed their names.

"There it is," Merrin whispered. "The gate. The little door."

And there it was: a seam in the root-wall, thin as a knife-cut, glowing amber along its edge. Behind it, the Undercourt waited — the cathedral of bone and amber, the Pale Magistrate, the lanterns full of stolen lives.

Corvane drew his blade. Not the dueling steel of the pits — the old sword, recovered from a warden's trophy case, the one he'd sworn never to draw again. Sword-speech glyphs crawled up his forearm where he'd inked them that morning, half-healed skin be damned. Bessie howled. He told her to get in line.

"On my count," he said quietly. "We go in loud, we find the lantern-hall, and we break every cage in the building before the Magistrate can finish his little negotiation."

"And if the negotiation goes poorly?" Merrin asked. "If he takes the swordspeaker's voice anyway?"

Corvane thought of Sable walking alone down that corridor with a bedsheet for a banner and a lullaby on their lips. Stubborn, self-effacing, infuriating Sable, who had spent fifteen years being no one so that everyone else could stay someone.

"Then," he said, "we remind that powdered corpse exactly what the last swordspeakers sounded like when they were angry."

He set his shoulder against the seam.

"One."

* * *

*Sable*

The approach to the Undercourt was a corridor of amber pillars, each one taller than a watchtower, and Sable counted their steps aloud in their head because counting was better than screaming.

Two hundred steps. Three hundred. The parley flag hung limp in air that did not move. The only sound was their own boots, and beneath that — growing louder — the sound of singing. Wren's song, threaded down through stone and root, bright and stubborn as a nail driven into the world's coffin lid.

At four hundred steps, the corridor opened, and there it was.

The Court Beneath. A cathedral built of ribs — whose, Sable declined to wonder — vaulted with amber the color of old grief, and hanging from every arch, lanterns. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one holding a small soft light that moved the way a sleeper moves, the way a dreaming person turns toward a voice calling their name.

Souls. Bought and paid for and shelved like preserves in a larder.

At the far end, enthroned upon a dais of fused vertebrae, sat the Pale Magistrate, and he was smiling.

"Swordspeaker." His voice filled the cathedral the way water fills a drowned bell. "You brought me a bedsheet."

"It's a flag."

"It's a *bedsheet*," he repeated, with the patience of a creature who had been alive since before bedsheets, "with finger-paint on it. And yet." He rose, and his shadow rose with him, and it kept rising long after it should have stopped. "You came. Alone. To bargain. Which means somewhere in those tunnels, my dear, you have hidden something you think I don't know about."

Sable's heart performed a maneuver that would have gotten a lesser organ dismissed from service. But their face, honed by fifteen years of smuggling contraband past men paid to notice, showed nothing at all.

"Your Excellency," they said, and planted the flag, and bowed — low, theatrical, insolent. "I've come to discuss the terms of a soul. One soul. Mine, if necessary. But first—"

Above them, faint through miles of stone, Wren's song changed key.

And every lantern in the cathedral flickered.

* * *

*Corvane*

The seam gave way like a rotten tooth, and Corvane fell through into amber light and chaos.

The infiltration plan — if *walk in fast and hit things* could be dignified with the word — had assumed guards. It had not accounted for the fact that Wren's song was doing something to the very architecture. The bone walls were *humming*. The amber vaults rang like struck glass. And the lanterns — gods, the lanterns — each one was flaring brighter with every note, the souls inside pressing against their prisons like seedlings against soil.

"The hall!" Merrin's voice came from six directions at once. "The lantern-hall! *Merr-in, Merr-in*—" and eleven faded shapes poured past him into the cathedral's side galleries, singing fragments of the old tongue, and wherever their voices touched, the amber cracked.

A guard-thing congealed out of the shadows — tall, jointed wrong, faceless except for a mouth — and Corvane met it the way he'd met everything for twenty years: badly, enthusiastically, and with excellent footwork.

"Sword-speech, lesson one," he grunted, carving a glyph through the air, the words burning up his arm like swallowing lightning. "*Open.*"

The guard-thing opened. Considerably further than it had intended.

Somewhere across the vast space he saw Sable — small, absurd, flag planted like a gardener defending a cabbage patch — standing before the Pale Magistrate and talking. Actually *talking*. While the world came apart around them.

Corvane laughed out loud, blood on his teeth, Bessie screaming in his side, and drove himself deeper into the fight.

*Trust the smuggler,* he thought. *Trust the child. Trust the song.*

*And whatever happens — don't let them hear you cry until it's finished.*

* * *

*Sable*

"—and therefore," Sable said, over the sound of distant shattering amber and what was unmistakably Corvane bellowing a battle-canticle he absolutely had not rehearsed, "the sale of the child Wren constitutes fraud against this court itself. You purchased a soul under false pretenses. She was sold by coercion, Your Excellency. And your court deals only in truths spoken aloud. So tell me—" they stepped forward, heart hammering, voice steady, "—was it true? Or does your entire foundation crack, right here, tonight?"

The Magistrate's smile did not falter.

But the lanterns did.

One by one, down the endless vaulted dark, they began to flare — and sing — and break. And the Pale Magistrate, for the first time in a thousand years, stopped smiling.

"You dare," he said softly, "bring *war* under a parley flag?"

"No," said Sable Sterling, drawing the old sword at last, its glyphs waking like dawn. "I brought the truth. The war came free."
