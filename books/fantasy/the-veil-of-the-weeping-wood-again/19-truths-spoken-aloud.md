## 19. Truths Spoken Aloud

The parley flag was a scrap of white linen Corvane had torn from his own shirt, and it looked absurd tied to the end of my broken sword. But the Undercourt honored absurdity. It was, after all, a court.

We walked in through the great amber doors with our hands empty and our hearts hammering, and the doors closed behind us like a mouth swallowing.

Ilsabet walked at my left. She had insisted on coming, and I had been too tired to argue, which she later told me was the only reason she'd won. Wren walked between us, small hand tucked into mine, her bare feet silent on the floor of woven bone. Above us, the cathedral of the Undercourt rose in arches of resin and rib, lit by ten thousand lanterns—and every lantern held a soul. They glowed like fireflies in jars, and some of them, as we passed beneath, dimmed slightly, the way a sleeper turns away from light.

"Do not look at them," Ilsabet murmured. "It's rude."

"It's *rude*?"

"In a court? Everything is rude. That's how they trap you." She said it lightly, but her fingers had gone cold where they touched mine, and I remembered—fifteen years late, as usual—that she had spent her whole life learning how courts worked while I learned only how to leave them.

The Pale Magistrate waited for us on his dais of fused antler and glass. He was taller than I remembered from the old accounts, or perhaps he simply grew the way debts grow. His face was smooth as river stone, his eyes two lanterns of their own, and around him hovered the accumulated truths of a thousand bargains, drifting like dust motes in amber light.

"Swordspeaker," he said, and his voice was the sound of a ledger closing. "You come under parley. Speak your purpose."

"I've come to buy back a soul," I said. "Wren of Ashmoor. Sold by her family two winters past. Her thirteenth moon comes in nine days."

"Then you have wasted a journey." He did not smile. I don't believe he could. "The sale is lawful. The Crown permits it. The ink is dry, the debt is owed, and this court deals only in truths spoken aloud. Is that not what your order once said of us?" Something moved behind the lantern-eyes—amusement, maybe, or hunger wearing its coat. "The Swordspeakers knew the law better than anyone. It is why they are dead."

Corvane made a sound low in his throat, half-healed ribs and all, and I put a hand on his arm without looking away from the dais.

"Parley," I reminded him. "Parley."

"Parley," the Magistrate agreed pleasantly. "Which means you may speak, and I must listen, and I must weigh whatever truth you bring. Those are the terms. Your order wrote them. Shall we begin with your offer? What will you give me for the girl?"

I had rehearsed this. In the root-tunnels, in the dark, with Wren asleep against my shoulder, I had built the argument brick by brick: the sale was coerced, the family was desperate, the Crown's permission was rot dressed as law. I had speeches. I had precedents. Ilsabet had dug three of them out of sealed ledgers herself.

And standing there under all those lanterns, I understood that none of it would work. Because the court dealt in truths spoken aloud, and I had come armed with everything except the one thing that was truly mine.

"You want a truth," I said. "I have one. It's not about Wren. It's not about her family, or the Crown, or your precious lawful ink. It's about me."

The lanterns above us flickered. Every soul in the room felt it—the way a room feels lightning before the thunder arrives.

"Sable," Ilsabet whispered. "*What are you doing.*"

"The only smart thing I've ever done," I said, which was possibly a lie, "which is telling the truth at last, and far too late."

* * *

Here is what I told them. Here is what I had never told anyone, because telling it would make it real, and I had spent fifteen years being very good at keeping things unreal.

"My name is Sable Sterling. I was the youngest swordspeaker ever oath-bound at the Thornfell gate. I was nineteen years old, and I thought an oath was a thing you wore, like a sword, like a scar. I did not know it was a thing you carried, and that carrying is heavier than wearing, always, forever.

"On the night the order died, I was not there. Everyone believes the swordspeakers fell together, blades drawn, singing the release-words as they burned. That is the story the mountains tell. It is a lie. The order fell because they refused to open the border for a soul-trade—a child, actually, a boy of six, sold by his uncle to pay a gambling debt, because there has never been a century when this was not happening, only centuries when no one said it aloud. My brothers and sisters said *no*. And for saying no, they were cut down in their own hall by men wearing the Crown's colors, and I—" My voice cracked, and I let it crack, because a truth that doesn't crack isn't finished yet. "I was three miles away. I had gone out to meet a smuggler I trusted more than I trusted my own elders' caution. When I saw the fires, I ran the other way.

"That is the first truth. The second is worse.

"Ilsabet Vane loved me. Loves me, if love can survive being abandoned, and I am about to find out in front of a court of souls, so let us all discover it together. When the order was slaughtered, the Wardens came next for anyone who might speak for us—for witnesses, for archivists, for the woman who kept our covenant records. I knew this. I had time to warn her. I had a horse, a road, and fifteen hours, and instead I used them to run north into the Thornfells, alone, because I told myself that a dead swordspeaker helps no one, and that was true, and I hid inside the truth the way a coward hides inside a trench. I let her face the Wardens without me. I let fifteen years pass. I told myself every night that staying hidden *was* the oath—that guarding a border meant nothing now, so I would guard my silence instead, and I called my fear duty until even I believed it."

I heard Ilsabet breathe beside me. It was not a gasp. It was long and steady, like someone setting down something heavy they had carried so long they'd forgotten it wasn't part of their body.

"And here is the third truth, Magistrate, and it is the one I came to say. For fifteen years I calculated. That was my whole life. Risks and tolls and passes and weights—I guided smugglers through fog because fog is honest, fog hides everyone equally, and in the fog I was no one, and being no one was the warmest thing I knew." I lifted Wren's hand, small and certain in mine. "And then a mute child knocked on my door humming my mother's songs, and I discovered that no one is a very cold place to live once you know warmth again. I discovered that I had not been keeping an oath all these years. I had been hiding from its corpse. And I discovered that the oath was never the words. The words were just the shape of it. The oath was *whom you speak for*."

The lanterns were burning brighter now. All of them. Ten thousand small lights leaning toward me like flowers toward sun, and the motes of amber truth swirling around the dais were churning, agitated, as though the air itself were trying to weigh what I'd said and finding the scales moving.

"So weigh it," I said to the Magistrate. "That's your law. You deal in truths spoken aloud, and I have spoken mine—all of them, unpolished, unwashed, still bleeding. Weigh my cowardice against your lawful ink. Weigh my fifteen years of silence against a family's desperation and a Crown's permission slip. Weigh the child who chose to be sold to save her baby brother"—and here Wren squeezed my hand, because that truth was hers and she gave it to me freely, and I will be grateful for that until I die—"against your ledger that calls her a debt paid. Weigh it all. I'll stand here while you do. I'm good at standing in uncomfortable places. Fifteen years of practice."

* * *

The court was silent in the way that avalanches are silent—one heartbeat before ruin.

The motes of amber light swirled faster around the dais. I could feel them working, could almost hear them, a sound like a thousand quills scratching, like a scale settling, like the deep slow arithmetic of a court that had spent a century buying truths and suddenly been handed one too heavy to shelve.

The lanterns nearest the dais flared white. Then the ones beyond them. A wave of light rolled outward through the cathedral, soul by soul, ten thousand flames rising—and in that light I saw them clearly for the first time, not as fireflies in jars but as faces, faint as breath on glass, turning toward the dais.

They were listening. They had always been listening. No one had ever said anything worth hearing before.

"What are you doing," the Magistrate said, and for the first time his voice was not a ledger closing. It was a ledger *falling*.

"Weighing," said a voice from everywhere—from the lanterns, from the bone arches, from the amber walls themselves. The Hollow Choir, the faded ones, the souls too worn to remember their names. Their voice was wind through hollow reeds, and it was getting stronger with every word I'd spoken, because my truth was feeding them the one thing they'd starved for: a reason to remember what truth sounded like. "You taught us the law, Magistrate. Truths spoken aloud must be weighed. This one is heavy."

"It is one man's confession. It changes nothing."

"It changes *everything*," said another voice, and this one I knew—it was thin and young and it came from a lantern near the front, and I understood with a lurch that somewhere under that flame was the boy of six, grown now, sold like Wren, sold like all of them, waiting fifteen years for someone to say the trade out loud and call it what it was. "He named it. He named the sale. Names are the first thing you take from us. He gave one back."

The motes around the dais were condensing, gathering, pressing inward against the Magistrate like water finding a crack. And I saw it then—saw the flaw in him that a hundred years of easy bargains had let him forget. His power was the ledger. His power was lawful ink and unspoken things, trades done in the dark where no truth need be weighed. He was a court built for secrets, and I had walked into his cathedral and committed the one crime he had no defense against.

I had told the truth, all of it, and made him *listen*.

"Wren of Ashmoor," I said, and my voice carried strange and clear in the ringing light. "Your claim on her was written in ink. Ink is not truth. Truth is what stands when the ink burns. And it is burning, Magistrate. Look at your own scales. Look at them *tremble*."

He looked. He couldn't help it. And in the amber of his eyes I watched the arithmetic go wrong—the numbers that had balanced for a century tipping, sliding, refusing to settle, because a court that deals only in truths spoken aloud cannot hold a claim that truth has contradicted, and my confession had contradicted everything: the legality, the necessity, the quiet lie that desperate sales were anything but theft with paperwork.

The wave of light reached the high arches. Somewhere far above, in the root-tunnels where Corvane's friends from the Hollow Choir were supposed to be waiting, I heard singing—actual singing, names being spoken, *my name is_, my name is_*, a hundred cracked voices remembering themselves at once, and each remembered name was a small explosion of light.

"No," the Magistrate said. He rose from his throne of antler and glass, and for the first time he looked less like a magistrate and more like what he had always been underneath: a collector, watching his collection catch fire. "No. The girl's soul is *paid for*. There was a transaction. There was *ink*—"

"There was a child," said the Choir, "who chose to be sold to save her brother. Weigh *that*, keeper of scales."

And the scales—heaved. The whole cathedral groaned like a ship in a storm. The amber walls ran with new light, and Wren stepped forward out of my shadow, tugging her hand free of mine, and stood in the center of the floor with her chin up and her bare feet planted, thirteen years old and utterly unafraid, and began to hum.

My mother's song. The oldest tongue. The language that was old when the Undercourt was young.

Every lantern in the cathedral blazed.

* * *

The Magistrate descended from his dais. He came down the steps slowly, and the crowd of lights parted before him, and I moved to stand between him and Wren, and Corvane limped up on my other side with his ruined sword raised, and Ilsabet—foolish, brilliant, beloved Ilsabet—stood her ground and said, "The court has weighed it. By your own law, the claim is void. Say otherwise and you break your own covenant, and then *nothing* here is binding, including your power over any soul in this room."

For a long moment, the only sound was Wren's song, and the crackle of ten thousand flames, and the creak of scales that would not stop trembling.

Then the Magistrate smiled. It was the worst thing I had seen in fifteen years of bad nights, because it was the first human expression I'd ever seen him wear, and it meant the arithmetic had found him a way out.

"Clever," he said softly. "Truths spoken aloud. Very well. The court weighs the swordspeaker's truth, and finds it... sufficient. The claim on the child wavers. Perhaps it breaks entirely, in time." He spread his smooth stone hands. "But you misunderstand commerce, Swordspeaker. A wavering claim is not a freed soul. The debt remains on my books until it is *discharged*—and discharge requires payment." The lantern-eyes fixed on me, and around us the light began, slowly, horribly, to dim. "So. Here is my final offer, spoken aloud, as the law requires. Give me the last swordspeaker voice in the world—willingly, fully, forever—and I will tear the page myself. Refuse, and we shall see how long a wavering claim takes to harden into iron. Nine days, I believe you said. I am very patient. I have lanterns older than your kingdom."

Behind me, Wren's song faltered—not with fear, but with fury, a thirteen-year-old's fury, magnificent and useless. Ilsabet's hand found mine in the dimming light and gripped it hard.

And I looked at the last instrument of my order, the voice my mother had sung into me, the words that had once guarded borders between mortal courts and the deep places—and I realized, with a calm that frightened me more than any terror ever had, that I already knew what I was going to say.

The trouble was, so did he.
