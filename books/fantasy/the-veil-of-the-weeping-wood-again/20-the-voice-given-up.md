## 20. The Voice Given Up

The silence after a confession is never truly silent. I learned that standing before the Pale Magistrate with the last of my truths still hanging in the amber air, listening to the way a cathedral of bone and lantern-light absorbs sound and gives nothing back.

Wren's soul-lantern had cracked. Not shattered — cracked, a single bright fissure running through the glass like a lightning-scar, and through that fissure her light poured out in threads that curled toward her small body where she stood pressed against Ilsabet's side. The Hollow Choir had begun, very softly, in the galleries above us, to sing.

The Magistrate watched it all with the patience of a glacier watching a river decide to be a lake.

"That was well done," he said at last. His voice was the sound of a ledger closing. "I confess I did not expect it. Truths spoken aloud are currency here, Swordspeaker, and you have just spent a fortune buying back a child worth perhaps three honest lies. The arithmetic does not favor you."

"Arithmetic," I said, and my throat ached around the word, because I had spent fifteen years doing arithmetic — counting miles and risks and the exact distance between myself and everyone who might have loved me. "That's what you'd call it."

"What would you call it?"

I looked at Wren. She was looking up at me, and she did something she almost never did: she smiled. Small. Crooked. Certain. As if we were already somewhere else, somewhere with bread and rain and no thirteenth moon bearing down on any of us.

"I'd call it the whole point," I said.

"Sentiment." He said it the way a man names a disease. "The crack in the lantern is real, I grant you. Her soul has heard itself named and remembered, and memory is a solvent I cannot counterfeit. But hear me clearly, Cyrus Dunmore of the Thornfells — Sable Sterling of the Weeping Wood, whatever name you keep folded in your pocket like a lucky coin." He leaned forward on his throne of fused vertebrae, and the amber light bent toward him as if it too were afraid. "A debt acknowledged is not a debt dissolved. The contract stands. Her family signed. The Crown countersigned. The mountain keeps its books balanced. You have loosened the knot; you have not cut it. For that, there is a price."

"There's always a price," Corvane said from behind me. He was half-collapsed against one of the bone pillars, his wounded side bound in strips torn from my cloak, his sword across his knees like an old dog that had fallen asleep. "There was a price for the ale in Ashmoor. There was a price for the woman who sold her own daughter. You're not special, your worship. You're just more expensive."

"Corvane," I said quietly.

"No, let him talk," Corvane said. "Let's hear the number. Every flesh-peddler in the lowlands quotes a number eventually. It's the only honesty left in the trade."

The Magistrate considered him for a long moment, the way a man considers a fly that has landed on a very old document.

"The price," he said, "is your voice."

And there it was. The trap closing, exactly as it had closed in my dreams every night since Odell read the moons over her crooked kitchen table. I felt it land in my chest like a stone dropped down a well, except the well was shallow and the stone made a terrible loud sound.

"Not your life," the Magistrate went on, gently now, the way one speaks to livestock. "Not your freedom — though where you would go, voiceless, among people who fear your order even dead, I cannot imagine. Only the instrument. The swordspeaker voice. The tongue older than kingdoms, the speech that unmade gates and shamed kings and once — do you remember? — once made my own court flinch behind its walls. Give it to me. Willingly. Freely. A gift cannot be taken, only given, and so it must be given. Speak your last words in it, speak *her* free, and then speak no more in it forever. What remains of you afterward will be yours. Whatever that turns out to be."

*Whatever that turns out to be.* That was the knife in it. Not death — erasure with a receipt. Fifteen years ago I had hidden my voice to survive. He was asking me to amputate it to save someone else, and the cruelty of the offer was that it wasn't cruel at all. It was fair. That was the worst thing about the Undercourt. It was always, horribly, fair.

"Sable." Ilsabet's hand found mine. Her fingers were cold — they had been cold since the garrison, since the cell, since the word *treason* was written next to her name in a book she used to keep herself. "You don't have to—"

"Yes," I said, "I do."

"You don't understand what you'd lose. I've read the archives. I catalogued what was left of your order. The voice isn't a tool, it's — it's how you *think*. It's the shape of every memory you have that matters. Your mother sang to you in it. You'll lose the songs, Sable. You'll stand in some kitchen someday trying to remember how they went, and there'll be nothing there."

"I know," I said. And I did know, and knowing didn't make it smaller, which is the thing about grief nobody warns you about. It doesn't shrink when you see it coming. It just waits, patient as the mountain, until you walk into it anyway.

"Mama sang wrong notes," Wren said.

We all turned. She almost never spoke common — her mouth hadn't been made for it, or the world hadn't been made for hers — but when she did, it came out plain and true as a hammer.

"In the old tongue," she said. "Mama sang wrong notes all the time. Papa laughed. The song wasn't the notes." She pointed at my chest, then at her own. "Song is here. Notes are just where you put it down."

Somewhere in the galleries, the Hollow Choir faltered — and then one thin voice picked up the melody Wren's mother used to hum, the same one that had knocked on my cabin door a year and a lifetime ago, and carried it, wrong notes and all.

"Well," I said, because if I didn't say something light I was going to come apart right there on the bone floor, "the child out-argued an immortal magistrate and a court archivist in under ten seconds. Ilsabet, remind me to feel stupid about this later."

"You'll have plenty of time," Ilsabet said. Her voice cracked on it. She was crying without any of the noise, the way archivists cry, efficiently, as though tears were documents that had to be filed quickly before business resumed. "Fifty years, if you're stubborn about it. You're always stubborn about things."

"Fifty years," I agreed. "Minimum."

I stepped forward. One step, two, until I stood at the foot of the dais and the amber light pooled around my boots like honey I couldn't taste anymore.

"Terms," I said, and my swordspeaker voice rose in me one last time, and oh, it was *home*, it was every home I had ever lost rolled into one — it was my mother's kitchen and the training yard at dawn and Corvane laughing and the borders singing themselves awake under my feet. When I spoke in it, the whole cathedral listened. Even the Magistrate sat straighter. Even the lanterns brightened, hungry for it, starving for it, fifteen years of famine in one room. "Hear them and be bound by them, Pale Magistrate, keeper of bought souls, because your court deals in truth and this is truth entire:

"I give my voice freely. Not sold — given. No contract holds it but my own word, and my word outranks yours, because yours is ink and mine is blood. In exchange: the soul of the child called Wren, daughter of Ashmoor, sold by the hands of those who should have died before selling her — release her. Cut the knot. Burn the page. Let no power beneath the mountain or above it claim one hair of her head again, now or ever, in this world or the deep places. This is the price. This is the payment. Witness it."

"Witnessed," said the Choir, fifty ruined voices at once, and the sound went through the amber like a bell through water.

The Magistrate rose. He was taller than I remembered, or the room had gotten smaller, or both.

"It is accepted," he said. "Speak the release-words, Swordspeaker. They will be your last in the old tongue. Choose them well — you will carry them longer than anything else you own."

Choose them well.

Here is what no one tells you about the last time you do a thing: it doesn't announce itself. The last time you carry your mother across a threshold, the last time a friend falls asleep mid-sentence beside you, the last ordinary evening — none of them wear black armbands. But this one knew. This one wore its weight openly, and I found I was grateful. I wanted to feel every ounce of it.

I knelt. Not to him — I want that written somewhere, carved into whatever survives of that night. I knelt to Wren, down at her level, eye to eye, the way you kneel to a child or a queen, which in her case was the same thing.

"Repeat after me," I said in the old tongue, and began the release-words, the ones the order used for freeing souls from false oaths, from binding spells, from debts that should never have been contracted by children. *Little flame, you were never theirs.* She repeated it, halting, her accent terrible, her wrong notes glorious. *The chain was drawn by frightened hands, and frightened hands hold nothing true.* She got most of it. She missed a syllable and glared at the syllable as though it had personally wronged her, and tried again, and got it. *What was bought was never owned. What was signed was never sealed. Go home to yourself.*

And then the last line, the line that ends every rite the Swordspeakers ever performed, the line my mother sang over my cradle without understanding she was teaching me my own funeral and my own birth, both at once:

*I was here. I saw you. You are seen.*

Wren said it back to me, clear as a bell over clear water, and the lantern cracked clean in half.

The sound it made was not glass breaking. It was breath — an enormous indrawn breath, fifteen years of held breath, a hundred years, a thousand, the whole mountain inhaling at once. Light poured out of the broken lantern and into Wren, and she lit up like dawn deciding to happen early, and she laughed — a real laugh, a child's laugh, the first one I had ever heard out of her, high and startled and delighted, as though she'd just discovered she'd been carrying it all along and had finally found room for it.

And then the other lanterns began to break.

All of them. Everywhere. In the galleries and the alcoves and the vaults beyond the vaults, hundreds of lights, thousands, souls bought and hoarded across a century of quiet legal horror, and each one cracked open on the echo of Wren's release. Because that is the secret the Magistrate had built his court upon and could not survive: a soul that remembers its name is contagious. Freedom, it turns out, is the one debt that pays itself forward.

The Hollow Choir stopped singing fragments and started singing *names*.

I heard them — gods, I heard them. Names in the old tongue and names in common, names of farmers and midwives and smugglers and soldiers, people sold by desperate families across a hundred winters, and each name landed in the amber dark like a lamp being lit in a window. An old man wept out *Tomas, my name was Tomas* and fell to his knees holding his own face like something returned from a long war. A woman who had forgotten she'd ever had hands looked down at them and screamed with joy.

The Magistrate stood amid the ruin of his collection, and for the first time in the long history of that court, the Pale Magistrate had nothing to say. His ledgers were burning. Not with fire — with truth, with the simple unbearable fact that everything in them had become worthless, that you cannot keep accounts in a currency that has decided to be free.

"You," he said to me, and his voice had cracks in it now too, beautiful cracks. "You gave it away. I took nothing. Do you understand what you've done? The instrument is *mine* now, by your own word, the last sword-speech in the world sits in my keeping—"

"No," I said.

And I said it in common. Plain, flat, mortal common, the language of market days and bad wine and telling a child it's time for bed.

"It was never an instrument," I said. "It was never a weapon, and it was never yours, and it was never even mine. It was a promise. And a promise kept isn't taken from you. It's finished. Like a bridge. You don't mourn the bridge because you crossed it."

His mouth opened. Closed. Somewhere deep in the roots of the mountain, something vast shifted its weight, and dust sifted down through the amber light, and I understood with a calm that surprised me: the Undercourt ran on hoarded truths, and I had just spent the biggest one in the building. *Identity was never the words you speak. It's whom you speak for.* Say that aloud in a court built on buying voices, and the foundations notice.

The first pillar cracked with a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

"Time to go," Corvane announced, hauling himself up the bone pillar with his sword as a crutch, wearing the expression of a man who has survived many collapsing buildings and intends to add this one to the list. "In my professional experience, when the architecture starts making opinions known, the architecture wins. Sable. *Sable.* Move."

Wren grabbed my hand. Her grip was strong, alive, humming with the old tongue that lived in her now, that would live in her long after my last word in it faded — and that was the thought that carried me, that was the whole of the arithmetic, the only sum that had ever come out right: the language wouldn't die with me. It would walk out of this mountain singing wrong notes, and it would teach them to children, and it would survive me the way love survives people, imperfectly and completely.

Ilsabet's hand found my other one. Corvane took point, swearing cheerfully in three languages, none of them magic, all of them heartfelt.

Behind us, the Pale Magistrate stood alone in a cathedral of empty lantern-glass, surrounded by a thousand freed voices singing their own names into the dark, as the amber light guttered and the roots of the Weeping Wood began, at long last, to let the morning in.

We ran for the surface — and the mountain, dying around us, began to show us the way out.
