## 1. Charms for the Godless

The fog came off the harbor at dawn the way debt comes off a man's shoulders—slowly, and never entirely. Zephyrine Joubert watched it roll up Graymarket Lane from his stall in the fishwife's row, where the tarred rope smell mixed with brine and old cabbage, and he thought, as he did most mornings, that Vhalcrest was a city that had learned to live with its own bad breath.

He arranged his wares with the practiced indifference of a man selling nothing worth noticing. A tray of luck-knots, braided from harbor-grass and cheap red thread. Tin bells that were only tin bells. Dried rosemary bundles tied with string. Little clay beads stamped with a spiral no one recognized anymore. If anyone asked—and the Watch rarely bothered with the gray market—he sold trinkets. Souvenirs of superstition for people too poor to be superstitious properly.

Which was true, mostly. That was the trick of it. The best lies were nine-tenths inventory.

"Morning, Joubert." Old Tam the eel-man shuffled past with his baskets, not looking at him directly. Nobody in the gray market looked at anybody directly before noon; it was considered rude, like commenting on a limp. "Fog's thick as widow's grief today."

"Fog's honest, at least," Zephyrine said. "It never pretends it'll lift by afternoon."

Tam grunted, which from Tam was practically a sonnet, and moved on.

Zephyrine settled onto his stool and let his hands do their morning work while his mind wandered to dangerous places, as minds will when the body is bored. His fingers braided a luck-knot—over, under, over, tuck—the pattern worn so deep into his muscle memory that he could have done it blind, drunk, or dead. Over, under, tuck. And beneath the braid, so faint that even he sometimes pretended he'd imagined it, something stirred.

A flicker. A warmth in the knot, like a coal remembered rather than felt.

Three years ago, that warmth would have been a hearth-fire. Three years ago, he could have set a whole knot glowing gold, could have poured blessing through it until the harbor itself seemed to lean toward the light. He'd been a hedge-mage then, a war-mage, a servant of a god whose name he still kept folded in his chest like a letter he couldn't bear to reread.

Now he was a trinket-seller who cheated, very slightly, at braiding.

*Where are you?* he asked, the way he asked every morning, every evening, and several times in between when the silence got teeth. *I'm still here. In case you're keeping count.*

Nothing answered. Nothing had answered in three years, two months, and eleven days—not that he was counting, except that he was counting, always counting, the way a drowning man counts waves.

He finished the knot and tucked the warmth down flat inside it, smoothing it away the way you'd smooth a wrinkle out of a bedsheet. A luck-knot with real blessing in it could carry a fisherman home through a squall that should have drowned him. It could also get its maker hanged from the customs-house beam with his hands displayed so everyone could see what they'd done. The Crown's edicts were painted on the harbor wall in letters tall enough to read from a rowboat: MAGIC IS THEFT FROM THE CROWN. THE GIFTED WILL REGISTER OR BE ROOTED OUT.

Rooted out. He'd always liked that phrase, in the way you like the taste of something that makes you sick. Gardeners rooted out weeds. Nobody wept for weeds.

"Zeph! Zephyrine Joubert!"

He knew the voice before he turned, and his stomach performed a small complicated maneuver involving dread and pity. Maren Coalharness came down the lane like weather—broad, dark, unstoppable—with her shawl clutched at her throat and her eyes red-rimmed in that particular way that meant she hadn't slept and wouldn't.

Maren sold mussels off a boat her husband had skippered until six weeks ago, when the sea took the *Kindly Merrow* and all hands but one, and the one it gave back wasn't her husband but a boy named Pip Ferris, half-frozen, who'd spent the voyage since trying to explain what happened without explaining what happened. Everyone in the fishwife's row knew the shape of it. Nobody said the shape of it aloud.

"Maren," he said gently. "Sit. I've tea. Well. I've hot water that dreams of being tea."

She sat on his second stool—the one he kept for exactly this kind of customer—and accepted the tin cup with both hands, the way grieving people hold things, as if warmth were a rope thrown across water.

"I want a luck-knot," she said. "For the boat. The new skipper's taking the *Merrow*'s route Thursday and I—" Her jaw worked. "I know it's nonsense. I know your knots are just knots. But my mother bought them, and her mother, and I'll pay double if you make it strong."

And there it was. The sentence that arrived at his stall once a week or more, dressed in different coats: *I know it's nonsense, but.*

*I know it's nonsense, but my daughter won't stop coughing.* *I know it's nonsense, but my husband gambles.* *I know it's nonsense, but I dreamed my brother called to me from the water and I can't shake it.*

People didn't come to Zephyrine because they believed. They came because believing had been taken from them along with everything else worth having, and a knot made by a quiet man with careful hands was the last coin they had left to spend on hope.

He could have told her the truth: that her mother's knots had carried real blessing, that he'd known her mother, that old Widow Coalharness used to bring him honey-cakes during the war and call him "that nice young man who smells of lightning." He could have told her that the gift was still in him, coiled and starving, and that making her a knot that truly held luck against the sea would take him less effort than this conversation.

He could have. And then he could have saved the Crown the trouble of rooting him out.

Instead he said, "Double's robbery and you know it. One price. And Maren—" he met her eyes, and put everything gentle he owned into his voice, "—no knot keeps a man from the sea. What it does is keep the sea honest. There's a difference."

Her chin trembled. She nodded.

He chose his materials slowly, because slowness was safety. Harbor-grass cut that morning, still green at the joint. Red thread from the spool, nothing special. And underneath, woven in so fine that no inspector with a magnifying lens and a warrant would ever find it, he let the flicker rise—one thread of warmth, thin as spider silk, laid along the braid like a whisper under a conversation.

Over, under, over, tuck. *Please,* he thought, not to any god in particular, because naming his god even in his head felt like shouting in an empty church. *Just this once. Just enough. You were always soft-hearted about fishermen.*

The warmth caught. Faint, guttering, like a candle flame cupped in a wet palm—but there, alive, answering. For three heartbeats Zephyrine felt what he'd spent three years pretending not to miss: the sense of being *heard*. Of pouring himself into the knot and having something pour back.

Then it guttered out, as it always did, and the silence closed over him again like cold water, and he finished the knot with nothing but skill, which was all he had left that was legal.

"There." He pressed it into Maren's rough hand. "Red for blood, green for return, and the middle loop is left open on purpose. That's where the luck sits. Don't close it, don't unpick it, and don't let any fool tell you to bless it in the temple—that's temple tax, nothing more."

"Temple tax." She almost smiled. Almost. "You always did hate the temples."

"The temples charge admission to places that used to be free." He said it lightly, and hated how lightly it came, because it was the closest he could come to saying: *I served a god who charged nothing, loved everyone, and has gone missing, and some mornings I cannot breathe for missing Him.* "Tie it to the mast-foot. Low, where the salt gets it. Salt feeds it."

That much was true. Salt fed it. So did rain, and moonlight, and the small stubborn faith of a mussel-wife who claimed not to believe.

She paid him—three coppers, exact, plus a fourth pressed into his palm before he could refuse, because Maren Coalharness had never in her life accepted charity and wasn't going to start by giving it—and stood, and squared her shoulders, and went back up the lane toward the boats with her spine straight as a mast. Zephyrine watched her go and thought: *There goes the bravest person I'll speak with all day.*

Because that was the other thing nobody understood about hiding. It wasn't the danger that wore you down. Danger was exciting; danger had a shape. It was the loneliness—the way he catalogued every kindness he witnessed like a miser hoarding coins he could never spend. Tam saving him the good eels. Wren, the little pickpocket who haunted the market, leaving a stolen apple on his stall every week like a cat bringing offerings. Maren's fourth copper. He collected these moments and kept them in a chest inside himself, and the chest was full now, overflowing, and there was nowhere to spend any of it. You couldn't love people out loud when loving out loud left fingerprints.

A hedge-mage survived by being forgettable. Forgettable was a craft, same as braiding. He'd cultivated it the way other men cultivated beards: the plain brown coat, the slightly stooped walk, the stall that was never quite the first one customers saw. He'd given himself a laugh that was just a bit too common, a face designed to slide off memory. Once, in another life, crowds had turned when he passed—not because of grandeur, but because his god's presence rode him like sunlight rides dust, and people felt warmer when he went by. Now he'd built himself into the opposite thing. A man-shaped absence. A stall that sold nonsense to the godless.

And it worked. Gods, how it worked. He was invisible as smoke, safe as a locked door in a house nobody wanted to rob.

He was also so lonely that some nights he spoke aloud to the empty room above the chandlery where he lodged, just to hear a human voice, and had begun—this was the shameful part—to enjoy the sound of his own opinions, which he shared with no one, which meant his opinions were getting strange.

*Still here,* he told the silence, midmorning, between customers. *Day four thousand-something of the quiet. The fog lifted, Maren's buying luck, and a gull stole my lunch. Report ends.*

Silence. The harbor bell rang the hour. Somewhere down the row, Wren's laughter rang out bright and criminal, followed by a fishmonger's roar of betrayed outrage, and Zephyrine found himself smiling despite everything, because some things in Vhalcrest stayed reliably wicked.

He was reaching for the next length of harbor-grass when the first bell began to toll.

Not the hour-bell. The proclamation bell—from the customs house, the deep bronze voice that the city only rang when the Crown had something to say that it wanted heard in every quarter, every alley, every cranny where people like him lived and hid and told themselves they were safe.

The market went quiet in patches, like candle flames pinched out one by one. Down the lane, a crier in royal blue was climbing the steps of the fish-weighing station, unrolling a scroll with the ceremony of a man who enjoyed his work far too much.

Zephyrine's hands stopped braiding.

The flicker of warmth he'd tucked into Maren's knot seemed suddenly, terribly loud—as loud as a shout in a silent church—and he looked down at his own fingers, at the careful, clever, traitorous hands that had spent three years learning to hide, and waited to hear what the Crown had decided to say to the hidden ones.

The crier cleared his throat.

* * *

The scroll, as it turned out, was long. Criers were paid by the proclamation, and this one clearly intended to earn his supper, so Zephyrine heard the whole of it twice—once from the customs house crier, and once, in overlapping echo, from the crier at the temple square, whose voice carried strangely across the water in the still air.

BY ORDER OF THE LORD PROTECTOR: LET IT BE KNOWN THROUGHOUT VHALCREST AND ALL HER TERRITORIES THAT THE PURGE OF UNREGISTERED GIFT IS HEREBY COMMANDED. ALL PERSONS POSSESSING, PRACTICING, OR HARBOURING MAGICAL GIFT SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR REGISTRATION WITHIN SEVEN DAYS. THOSE WHO REGISTER SHALL BE MARKED, TAXED, AND PERMITTED TO LIVE UNDER CROWN WATCH. THOSE WHO DO NOT SHALL BE SOUGHT, TAKEN, AND—

The crier's voice droned on through the legal furniture—penalties, rewards for informants, the schedule of the witch-hunters' arrival—and Zephyrine stood behind his stall of harmless nonsense and felt the ground tilt under him with the slow, sick patience of a ship beginning to list.

Seven days.

Around him, the gray market resumed its noise the way a wound closes—unevenly, painfully, with scar tissue already forming. He watched it happen. Tam the eel-man suddenly finding urgent business elsewhere. The fruit-seller glancing at the herbalist two stalls down, who'd always had an uncanny knack for knowing which child in a household would take fever. A woman Zephyrine had known for eleven years—the one who bought fertility charms every spring and had four children to show for it—catching his eye and then, carefully, deliberately, not catching it again.

There it was. The look. He'd seen it in two other cities before Vhalcrest, and it always arrived on the first day, before anyone had accused anyone of anything. It wasn't hatred yet. It was arithmetic. Neighbors doing sums: *Who among us is worth the informant's reward? Whose name goes on whose list?*

Eleven years of fourth coppers and saved eels and apples on his stall, and he could watch the sums happening behind their eyes in real time.

"Well," Zephyrine said aloud, to nobody, because there was nobody left to say it to. "That complicates the afternoon."

He packed the stall. He was quick about it but not hurried—hurried men drew eyes, and eyes were currency now. Luck-knots into the basket. Tin bells. Rosemary. The little clay spirals he swept into his palm and, after a moment's thought, dropped one by one into the harbor, where they sank without ceremony, taking their small forgotten god-mark with them.

Then he climbed to his room above the chandlery, barred the door, and did the hardest thing a careful man can do.

He burned his ledgers.

Not the money-ledgers—those were innocent, full of coppers and debts, and burning them would look like guilt. The other ledgers. Eleven years of them, written in his own cipher: who had the gift and how much, who needed what charm for what ailment, which families had hedge-blood in their line, which children had shown the flicker too young and needed watching—or protecting. Names. Hundreds of names. He'd told himself he kept them so he could help them. Tonight, by candlelight, feeding page after page into his little iron stove, he admitted what they really were: a map. A treasure map, drawn in his own handwriting, waiting for a witch-hunter's reward.

The paper curled and went orange and became ash, and names he'd sworn to protect became smoke that smelled, faintly, of rosemary.

When the last page was ash he sat back on his heels in front of the stove and addressed the silence directly, because his voice was steadier than he expected and he wanted to use it while it lasted.

"Right," he said. "Seven days. I've run before. Two cities, one border town, and that unpleasant year in Saltmarsh. I know the roads, I know which farmers sell horses without asking questions, and I know better than to say goodbye to anyone." He poked the ash with a poker, methodically. "So that's the plan. Again. Run, hide, braid knots somewhere new, learn new neighbors well enough to mourn them quietly when the next purge comes."

The silence, as ever, offered no counterargument.

"And I'll tell you what I told you in Saltmarsh," he went on, and now his voice did waver, just slightly, and he let it, because there was no one to see. "I am tired. Not of running—I'm good at running. I'm tired of being a locked door. I'm tired of collecting kindnesses I can't repay. I'm tired of braiding luck into a grieving woman's knot with one hand while I smother it with the other, and calling that survival. Three years you've been quiet, and I have been *so patient*, and I never asked why, and I want you to know that tonight, packing to run again, I finally understand that you're never coming back. Whatever happened to you, wherever you went—it isn't exile. Exile sends letters."

He stopped. The stove ticked as it cooled. Below him, the chandlery's dog barked twice at nothing, then thought better of it.

"So I'm going to pack," Zephyrine said. "And tomorrow I'm going north on the coast road, and in ten years I'll be some other forgettable man in some other gray market, and I will not think of you at all."

He was lying, and the silence knew it, and he knew the silence knew it, which was somehow worse.

He packed. Coat, knife, cipher-wheel melted in the stove, the small store of coins sewn into his hem, a spare shirt, and—at the bottom of the bag, wrapped in oilcloth, the way you wrap the thing you'd grab first in a fire—a knot. Not harbor-grass. White cord, silver-shot, braided in a pattern no one living had used in thirty years. His mother's pattern. The first knot he'd ever blessed, at fourteen, badly, with his whole ridiculous heart.

He'd carried it through two wars and three exiles. He didn't examine why. Some cargo you don't weigh.

He was cinching the bag's buckle when someone knocked on the door below—soft, twice, pause, twice again. Not the Watch's knock. The Watch knocked like they owned the building.

The knock belonged to somebody who'd learned it from him.

Zephyrine went very still. Nobody knocked that knock anymore. He'd taught it to exactly five people, in another life, around campfires that burned on battlefields that no longer existed, and he'd assumed—had made himself assume—that the war had taken all five, because the alternative was wondering, every day for three years, why none of them had ever come looking for him.

Soft. Twice. Pause. Twice.

He picked up his knife, put it down, picked it up again, and went down the stairs with his heart hammering out a rhythm that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with hope, which is the more dangerous of the two.

The lamp beside the chandlery door showed him a figure swaying against the wood—a woman, tall, gaunt, in a soldier's coat gone gray with road-dirt, one hand pressed flat against the doorframe as if the world had tilted and she was holding on. Dark hair cropped short. A scar he'd have known anywhere, running from her ear to her collarbone like a signature.

"Sera," he breathed, and fumbled the bolts. "Sera—gods' sakes, what—"

She fell forward into his arms before the door was fully open, and her weight was wrong, far too light, and her coat was wet in a way that coat shouldn't have been, warm and spreading against his hands.

"Zeph," she said, and her smile was the old grin, the battlefield grin, dragged up from somewhere deep. "Knew you'd answer. Knew it. Listen—no time for the reunion." Her hand found his collar and pulled him close, and her whisper was wet and ragged and urgent as a dying fire. "They have it. They have *your name*. Black deed, wax seal, your soul-price writ in it. The Pale Court, under the mountain—they hold the deeds, Zeph, they hold *all of us*—"

"Hush, hush, you're bleeding out, we need—"

"*Listen.*" Her grip tightened with the last of a strength that was leaving her by the heartbeat. "Your god didn't leave you. He *never left you*. He's theirs, Zeph. Chained under the mountain. Lantern of bone. Everything makes sense now, doesn't it? Why the silence started. Why—"

The words were slowing. He lowered her to the floor, pressing his palms hard against the wound that no pressure in the world would mend, and her eyes found his and held on with fierce, furious tenderness.

"Don't run north," Sera whispered. "Run *down*. Bring them back. Bring them all back. Promise me you'll—"

The grip loosened. The fierce eyes dimmed, fixed on something beyond the ceiling, beyond the sky, beyond whatever veils lie between here and the courts of the powerful and the prisons of the gods.

Outside, the fog rolled back in off the harbor, thick and white and merciless, swallowing the lane, the boats, the whole listening city. Zephyrine knelt on the floorboards of the chandlery with his oldest friend dying in his arms, and above them both, faint through the window, the proclamation bell tolled on and on, counting down seven days that had suddenly become the only days that mattered.
