## 2. The Proclamation

The morning the world changed, Zephyrine Joubert was arguing with a hen.

"You can't eat that," he told her, prying a beetle from her beak. "That's my best luck-beetle. Do you know how hard it is to find a beetle with the right sort of shine? Three days I've looked. Three days, and you'd swallow it like it's nothing."

The hen—named Duchess, because Wren had named her and Wren had never once in her life chosen a dignified name for anything—regarded him with the flat amber contempt of her kind, then went back to scratching at the dirt floor of his courtyard as though he were furniture.

Zephyrine sighed and set the beetle on his windowsill to dry. It was a small thing, the kind of work that kept a hedge-mage fed without ever quite being magic: you found a creature that carried a little natural luck in its shell, spoke three quiet words over it, and sold it to someone whose life needed tilting half a degree toward kind. No glow. No thunder. Nothing the Register-men would call *the gift*. Just a beetle, a whisper, and a fishwife's copper.

He was arranging his stall goods when the criers began.

It started at the far end of Graymarket Row, where the cobblestones gave way to mud and the fishmongers shouted loudest. A bell. Then another bell, nearer. Then a voice, pitched in that official flatness that made every word sound like it had been stamped with wax first.

*"By decree of the Crown, in the third year of the Regency—"*

Zephyrine's hands went still over his charms.

*"—all persons gifted in the arcane arts shall present themselves at the Hall of Rolls within fourteen days to be entered upon the Register of Magics. Those who register shall be examined, catalogued, and licensed according to the Crown's pleasure."*

A pause, precisely measured, the way a butcher measures between cuts.

*"Those who do not register shall be taken."*

* * *

Vhalcrest had heard proclamations before. War levies. Grain taxes. The year they repaved the Processional and everyone's front door faced the wrong way for a month. Proclamations were weather in this city—you grumbled, you paid, you went on.

But Zephyrine had lived through the last purge, thirty years ago, when he'd been small enough to hide under his mother's skirts and she had hidden him so well that even she seemed to forget where. He knew what *taken* meant. It did not mean arrested. Arrested implied you might come back.

He watched the first crier come up the Row—a boy of perhaps sixteen in crown-blue livery, flanked by two soldiers with the bored posture of men who had not yet been given anything interesting to do. The boy read the decree again from a parchment scroll, word for word, and Zephyrine noted with a professional's appreciation that the scroll was written large enough to read from a distance. They *wanted* people to see it. They wanted it read aloud in markets and taverns and kitchens, wanted neighbors reading it over shoulders, wanted the words passed mouth to mouth until they wore smooth as river stones and felt as inevitable.

Clever. Cruel, but clever. The Crown had learned that you didn't need many hunters if the hunted did half your work for you—watching each other, wondering about each other, remembering.

The crier moved on. The bell faded. And in the silence it left behind, Graymarket Row began very carefully not to look at anyone.

* * *

Maren Osk came by an hour later, as she always did on market mornings, for her luck-knots and her gossip in equal measure. She was a fishwife built like a mooring post, with forearms that could crack walnuts and a laugh that could crack them at forty paces, and for six years she had bought a charm from Zephyrine every season without once asking what was in it.

She stopped three stalls away.

That was the first sign. Maren Osk had never in her life stopped three stalls away from anything; she arrived at things like weather.

"Morning, Zeph," she said. Her voice was normal. Her eyes weren't. They flicked—to his hands, to the little table of knotted cords and dried beetles and river-stones, back to his face—with a quickness she clearly thought he wouldn't notice.

"Morning, Maren. Luck-knot today? I've got one braided with red thread. Good for boats."

"Boats," she repeated. "Yes. Boats."

He tied it while she counted out her coppers, and he did it slowly, the ordinary way, no whisper under his breath, because her eyes were still doing that flicking thing and there were soldiers at the end of the Row now, checking something at the chandler's stall. When he handed the knot across, their fingers did not touch. Six years, their fingers had always touched. Fishwives were not a hand-shy people.

"How's your sister?" he asked. "Still bad joints in the wet season?"

"Oh—fine. Fine." She tucked the knot away without looking at it. "You, ah. You'll be registering then, will you?"

The question dropped between them like a coin down a well. Zephyrine heard it falling all the way.

"I sell knots, Maren," he said gently. "Knots and beetles."

"Yes," she said. "Course. Only—" She stopped. Looked at the soldiers. Looked at him. Something moved behind her face that he recognized, because he had seen it before, thirty years ago, on the face of a neighbor who had known his mother and had said nothing and had never once met her eyes again afterward. Not betrayal exactly. Fear wearing betrayal's coat. "Only it's a funny time. For everyone. Isn't it."

"It's a funny time," he agreed.

She left quickly, and did not take her usual route past the tanner's, which meant she went the long way home, which meant she had not wanted to be seen walking away from his stall. Zephyrine stood in the gray light with his unsold luck-beetles and understood, with the particular clarity that comes only when something breaks quietly, that Maren Osk would not be back next season.

He could not even blame her. That was the genius of it, the Crown's true craftsmanship. They hadn't accused anyone. They hadn't pointed at anyone. They had simply asked a question in public, and let six years of kindness curdle into evidence. *Why would a charm-seller look frightened, if he had nothing to hide?*

He began packing his stall.

* * *

By afternoon, the city had developed a new texture. You could feel it under everything, the way you feel frost coming before you see it. At the fountain, two women drew water in silence, glancing at each other's children. At the baker's, old Tam Hesk—who had sung off-key at every festival since the dawn of time—was quiet. A man stood outside the Hall of Rolls already, though registration had been open less than a day: a thin fellow with ink-stained fingers, waiting in line alone, holding his hat. Zephyrine watched him for a long moment and felt something twist in his chest. That man wasn't gifted. Probably. But he was afraid of being thought so, and fear was contagious as fire in a thatched street, and somewhere down the line the Crown knew that too. Every innocent who registered made the guilty ones more visible.

Wren slipped into the courtyard just before dusk, through the gap in the wall she refused to use the door out of pure professional principle, and Zephyrine saw at once that she'd been crying, though she'd have bitten him if he'd said so.

"Well?" he said, keeping his voice light. "What's the news on the street?"

"Nothing." She sat on the step, knees up, arms wrapped around them. She was eleven and all elbows and sharp dark eyes, and she could lift a purse off a bishop, but right now she looked about four years younger than any of that. "Everyone's talking about it. The purge." She picked at a splinter in the step. "Mrs. Colvin at the pie stand told everyone Ilex isn't real. Said she'd seen me talking to nobody and it's the kind of thing that gets noticed now." Her jaw tightened. "Ilex says it doesn't matter what they think. Ilex is wrong. It matters enormously what they think. That's the entire problem with people."

Zephyrine sat down beside her. "Where is Ilex now?"

"Hiding. Spirits go thin when there's too much fear around. Like smoke." She scowled at the cobbles. "I hate them. All of them. The criers and the soldiers and Mrs. Colvin."

"I know."

"They're going to start taking children too. If the spirit counts. Nobody knows if spirits count. That's the worst part, nobody *knows*, so everybody's guessing loud." She looked up at him then, and her eyes were dry now but terribly clear. "You're going to run, aren't you."

It wasn't a question. Children who grew up on streets learned to read exits the way scholars read books.

"I'm thinking about it," he said.

"You're packing. I saw the oilcloth." She was quiet a moment. "I'm coming with you."

"Wren—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and hard as a thrown stone. "Don't do the thing where you decide what's safe for me. Safe got Mrs. Colvin saying my friend doesn't exist. Safe is a lie people tell you while they're deciding to leave you." Her voice cracked on the last word, barely, and she hated it, he could tell, hated it with her whole furious body.

Zephyrine looked at her—this pickpocket he'd caught lifting his own purse two winters ago and somehow, instead of turning her in, ended up teaching card tricks and feeding supper—and felt the ground shift again beneath him. His whole life in Vhalcrest had been built the way a wren builds a nest: small, hidden, wedged into cracks where notice couldn't reach. He had thought the walls held.

They had never held. They had only never been tested.

"All right," he said. "But we leave before the week's out, and you do exactly as I say on the road."

"That's two conditions."

"Both non-negotiable."

"Ugh," said Wren, which was, from her, a treaty signature.

* * *

That night, he burned his ledgers.

He did it in the courtyard, in the iron brazier he used for warming charm-wax, because burning them indoors felt too much like hiding and he was done pretending this was a small thing. Fourteen years of accounts. Names in his cramped careful script: who owed what, who had paid in eggs or mending or news, who took a courage-charm each winter against the dark months, whose arthritis he had quietly eased with a whispered word over a "medicinal" liniment, whose sick daughter slept because of a lullaby stitched with one thread of real working. Page after page after page. Not a confession, exactly. But a map, if anyone knew how to read it, and the Crown was hiring readers now—he'd heard that much in the market murmur. Readers, sniffers, finders. Men whose gift was for finding other men's gifts.

The fire took the pages slowly, the way fire takes anything worth having. He watched names curl and blacken. Tam Hesk. Maren Osk. The Widow Corvel. Little Piet with the stammer. Each name a small crime, each crime a small mercy, all of it ash by midnight.

He should feel lighter, he thought. People talked about burning the past like it freed you. But watching the last ledger-corner catch, Zephyrine felt only the vertigo of a man standing on ground that has remembered, all at once, that it is water. Everything he had built—the stall, the routines, the careful gray ordinariness of being nobody—had rested on one assumption: that if you were small enough, quiet enough, useful enough in harmless ways, the great machinery of the world would roll past you like a cart rolling past a mouse. Three years of silence from his god, and he had made his peace with it the same way. *Be small. Be still. Wait it out.* Even his prayers had grown cautious, apologetic little things, prayers of maintenance rather than need.

And now the cart had turned. The Crown had announced that smallness itself was suspect, that the harmless was a mask for the harmful, that the very invisibility he'd cultivated was, retroactively, evidence of guilt. There was nowhere small enough anymore. The Register would not distinguish between a hedge-mage selling luck-beetles and whatever terrible things the Regency's ministers imagined in their candlelit offices—because it was not meant to. It was a net with holes shaped like everyone.

From the doorway, Wren watched him feed the last pages to the flames. She had a bedroll over her shoulder already. Of course she did.

"What'll you do," she asked, "out there? Wherever we're going. No stall. No regulars. Just running."

Zephyrine looked into the fire a moment longer. He thought about answering honestly—that he didn't know, that he had spent so long being invisible he wasn't entirely sure what remained of him underneath, that a man could build a life so carefully around silence he forgot whether the god he prayed to had ever answered at all.

"We'll manage," he said. "I'm told I make an excellent luck-knot."

The fire settled. Somewhere beyond the walls, a bell rang the hour, and beneath it—so faint he might have imagined it, so faint he had almost stopped listening these past three years—something else. Not a voice. Not yet. Just a pressure, like a hand laid flat against the far side of a door.

Then boots on the lane outside. Torchlight, sliding yellow along the courtyard wall. A knock—not the neighborly kind, but the measured triple-rap of men who represented something—and a voice, official and flat as the crier's:

"Open in the name of the Register. We're making inquiries at every house on the Row."

Zephyrine rose, snuffed the brazier with one motion, and put himself between the door and Wren.

"Wren," he murmured. "The wall gap. Now."
