## 3. What Sera Carried Home

The knocking came at midnight, and it was wrong.

Zephyrine knew every knock his door had ever received. The fishwives knocked twice, quick, like gulls pecking. Old Maren from the chandlery knocked in threes because she counted everything—candles, coins, the years since her husband drowned. The tax collectors didn't knock at all; they simply announced themselves through the wood in voices like wet boots.

This knock was slow. Deliberate. Spaced like a soldier counting paces between drumbeats, which was a rhythm Zephyrine had hoped never to hear again on this side of the war.

He set down the luck-knot he'd been tying—a good one, sea-green thread with a sliver of hagstone at its heart—and blew out his candle out of pure habit before he remembered that hiding in the dark was pointless when whoever was outside already knew he was home.

"Who's there?" he asked, and made his voice flat and stupid, the voice of a charm-seller with nothing worth stealing.

"It's me, you great coward." A pause, wet and ragged. "Open the door before I bleed on your step. I know how you feel about your step."

Zephyrine's hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with magic and everything to do with a name he hadn't spoken aloud in three years.

*Sera.*

He opened the door, and the night came in wearing blood.

She was leaning against his doorframe the way a collapsing wall leans against its last standing beam—all her weight committed to something about to fail. Her leathers were dark with it, soaked through across her belly where someone had opened her up with a blade or worse. Her face was gray as Vhalcrest slate, but her eyes were still Sera's eyes: sharp, furious, and utterly unimpressed by death's attempt to take her seriously.

"Sera," he said stupidly, because three years of careful silence had apparently robbed him of all other words.

"You look terrible," she said. "Vhalcrest agrees with you like a bad oyster."

"You look—" He caught her as her knees went, hauled her arm over his shoulders. She weighed less than she should have. That frightened him more than the blood. "Kitchen. Now. Don't argue."

"Wasn't going to." She managed a ghost of her old grin. "You always did give orders like a quartermaster. *Don't argue.* As if anyone argued with you."

*Everyone argued with you,* he wanted to say. *That was half of what you were for.* But she was leaving a trail across his floorboards that would take a week to scrub and possibly a miracle, and he had exactly none of those left to spare, so he said nothing and carried her instead.

He got her onto the table—it was the sturdiest surface in the house, built for gutting fish, and the irony of that was not lost on either of them—and cut away the ruined leather with hands that only shook a little. The wound beneath was bad. Not dying-tomorrow bad. Dying-tonight bad. Something had torn through her lower belly and kept going, and whatever healing charms she might have bought along the road had been spent hours ago; he could see their residue fizzing uselessly under her skin like rain on a hot stove.

"I need thread," she said. "Needle. And don't you dare."

"Don't I dare what?"

"I can see it in your face, Zeph. You're doing the arithmetic. How much magic is one hedge-witch's stitchwork worth against the Register patrols. I've seen that face before. It's the face you wore when you told us we couldn't light the signal fires."

"This is different."

"It's never different," she said. "That's the whole tragedy of you."

He threaded the needle anyway, ordinary steel, ordinary thread boiled in salt water, and worked while she talked, because Sera had never once in her life endured pain quietly. She narrated it like a campaign report, cataloguing each fresh horror with the dry precision of a woman filing complaints with a superior who could do nothing about any of them.

"Three of them," she said. "Witch-hunters, proper ones, not the local thugs with torches. Hounds too. Smelled the deed on me, I think. Or smelled *me*. Either way, they ran us down at the ford at Colder's Mill." Her breath hitched as he drew a stitch tight. "I got two. Third one got me. Fair trade, if you ask me. Better than fair."

"I'm not asking."

"No. You never did." She watched him work with an odd softness breaking through the gray of her face. "You've gotten good at this. The stitching. All those years pretending to be useless, and here you are, neat as a surgeon."

"Pretending to be useless is a full-time profession," he said. "It keeps a man humble."

"It kept a man alive," she agreed. "Until tonight. Tonight it just kept a man *late*."

He tied off the last stitch and pressed a clean cloth over the wound, and she caught his wrist—not gently, not weakly either, with the last of that iron grip he remembered from the walls of Karsk Hold—and pulled his hand up so he could see what she'd been clutching since she fell through his door.

A ledger page. Black paper, blacker ink, heavy as a funeral shroud and cold as riverbed stone. And written across it in script that seemed to writhe slightly when looked at directly, the way water moves under ice:

**JOUBERT, ZEPHYRINE — son of none, hedge-sworn, god-quieted.**
**Held in trust. Price rendered: one soul, standard weight.**
**Signed in surety: R. HALVERIC, Commander, Third Free Company.**

Zephyrine read it twice. Three times. His own name looked back at him like a stranger's, and beneath it, in smaller characters, a figure that meant nothing and everything: the price of him, tallied like a sack of barley.

"That's not possible," he said.

"You're welcome," said Sera. "I crawled eighty miles with my guts held together with prayer and spite to bring you that, and your opening remark is *that's not possible.*"

"Commander Halveric signed—" He stopped. Started again. "Halveric was the one who told us the gods had gone quiet. Told us it was the war, the slaughter, the—said the gods turned their faces away because we were unworthy. We *believed* him, Sera. I prayed into that silence for three years thinking I'd failed."

"Oh, Zeph." And there it was, the thing in her face he'd dreaded more than the wound: pity. Sera Carrow, who had once broken a siege-engine with a maul and a bad attitude, looking at him with pity. "You didn't fail anything. None of us did. The silence wasn't the war. The silence was a *transaction*."

* * *

She talked because talking was easier than bleeding, though she was doing plenty of both, and he let her talk because stopping her felt like admitting what the talking meant.

"The Pale Court," she said. "Under the mountain, past the Weeping Wood. You remember the stories. Every army has stories about the mountain country—the lanterns underground, the pale folk who trade in things that shouldn't be tradeable. We thought they were stories."

"We were told they were stories."

"We were told a lot of things." She shifted on the table and winced with the particular economy of a veteran who has stopped expecting help. "Halveric sold us, Zeph. Before the last campaign. Sold the whole company—not our service, not our lives. Our *souls*. Collateral against his debts. Ten thousand names on ten thousand pages of black paper, and ours among them. That's why your god went quiet. That's why they all went quiet. The Court holds the deeds, and a god can't hear a soul that's been pawned. We're locked inventory. We've been locked inventory for years."

The candle he'd relit guttered, though there was no draft. Or perhaps there was; perhaps the whole world had become a draft, blowing through a house he'd built out of the assumption that his silence was his own fault.

"My mother," Zephyrine said. His voice sounded far away, coming from someone else's chest. "She prayed to the same god I did. She prayed every day of her life. Did Halveric sell her too?"

"Halveric sold everyone he could reach." Sera's laugh turned into a cough, and the cough brought blood up with it, thin and bright, and he hated how unsurprised she looked by it. "But listen—listen, because I haven't got the time to say it twice, and you're going to want to argue. The Court doesn't just hold the deeds. They *honor* them. There are rules down there, old ones, older than crowns. A deed can be contested. Redeemed. Stolen, if you're mad enough. That's why the Purge is happening now—that's why the Crown's hunting the gifted all of a sudden. Someone paid the Crown to keep new souls from wandering free before the ledger comes due. We're being *herded*, Zeph. All of us. Toward the mountain."

"And you came to me." He heard his own voice crack down the middle. "Eighty miles, gutted, hunted, and you came to *me*, the coward who runs a charm-stall in a fish market."

"I came to the man who broke Karsk Hold's gate with a word nobody taught him." She grabbed his collar with a bloody hand and dragged him close, close enough that he could count the threads of gold creeping through the red at the corner of her mouth. "You think I don't know? You think any of us didn't know? You used to pray at night like a man apologizing to someone. And now I know what you were apologizing *for*—for hearing nothing back. Well, I'm telling you what I found in Halveric's strongbox before I burned it: your god isn't silent, Zephyrine Joubert. Your god is *chained*. Under the mountain. In a lantern. And it has been screaming your name for three years."

The room tilted. He sat down hard on his stool and the black page lay on his knee like a stone, and somewhere outside a nightbird called, indifferent, ordinary, part of a world that had no idea it had just changed shape.

"They're coming after me," Sera said. Her voice was quieter now, running down like a clock. "The hunters. I lost them at the ford but not forever. So you have a choice, same as always. You can burn that page and run further, find some hole deeper than this one. Or—" she pressed the page into his hand and folded his fingers over it, her grip still astonishing, still hers, "—or you can go get yourself back."

"Sera—"

"Don't." She smiled, and it was the grin from the walls of Karsk Hold, young and obscene and certain. "Don't waste my last minutes making promises. Just—do me one courtesy. When you're down there, when you find the thing that's been listening to you all along—tell it Sera Carrow says hello. Tell it it owes me a drink."

"Sera."

"And Zeph." Her eyes were finding his with more effort now, but they found them. "The others. On the ledger. There'll be others like you—gifted, hunted, angry. You were never any good alone. You only ever thought you were."

Her grip loosened. Not all at once—Sera did nothing all at once—but by degrees, like a tide deciding to go out. He stitched what remained unstitchable and held her hand and told her, because she'd earned it, about the fishwives and the luck-knots and Maren who counted everything, small warm nonsense to fill the silence where a god should have been answering, and she smiled at the right places, and near dawn, between one breath and the next, she was gone with her boots on, which was the only way Sera Carrow would ever have agreed to go anywhere.

* * *

He buried her in the morning, behind the cottage, under the one crooked rowan, because she'd have hated a pauper's pit and loved the inconvenience of the location. He dug alone and said the old words over her, the ones his mother taught him, feeling like a fraud and a fool and knowing, now, that the words had probably never reached anyone—except that maybe they had, maybe every prayer of his life had been piling up unread against a chained door somewhere, and the thought undid him more thoroughly than grief did.

Then he went inside, washed the blood from his table, and sat down with the black page in front of him and the last of Sera's stolen papers beside it—she'd had a satchel; he hadn't dared look until now—and began, with the terrible calm that had gotten him through sieges and tax season alike, to read.

The first page was a list of names. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Soldiers, mostly, but not only—midwives, millers, a fiddler, three children born gifted in a village whose name he knew. Each with a price. Each with a signature.

And at the top of the newest page, in ink still faintly wet, a line that made the morning seem suddenly very cold:

*The ledger comes due at the next dark moon. All accounts will be collected.*

Zephyrine looked out the window at his little garden, his crooked rowan, his quiet street where Maren was counting candles and the fishwives would come knocking soon, wanting charms, wanting luck, wanting the small mercies of a man who had just learned that mercy itself had been mortgaged without his consent.

"Well," he said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead woman under the rowan, to the chained god in the dark under the mountain. "I suppose I'd better start assembling a crew."

He picked up the deed with his name on it, and for the first time in three years, he prayed—not for guidance, not for forgiveness, but the way a man knocks on a door he intends, one way or another, to open.

*I'm coming,* he said into the silence. *Whoever's listening. I'm coming, and I'm bringing receipts.*

The silence, for the first time in three years, felt less like absence.

It felt like a held breath.
