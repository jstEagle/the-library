## 4. The Ledger of Names

Zephyrine did not bury Sera immediately.

He told himself it was because the ground outside Vhalcrest was frozen hard as an anvil in the first week of Frostwane, and that digging a grave with a charm-seller's spade would take him until midwinter. This was true. It was also not the whole truth. The other truth was that he could not yet bear to make her absence permanent, and so he kept her wrapped in his good blanket—the one with the mended corner she'd always mocked—laid out on his bed like a guest who had simply drunk too much plum wine and needed to sleep it off.

"You always did overstay your welcome," he said to her, and his voice cracked on the last word, and he sat down heavily on the floor beside the bed and let himself grieve properly for a while, which is to say: badly, alone, into his sleeves.

When the worst of it had passed through him like a fever, he washed his face, ate half a heel of bread without tasting it, and turned to what she had left him.

She had left him two things. The first was the black deed bearing his own name, which sat on his table where he'd dropped it three days ago and which he still could not look at directly, the way one cannot look at the sun or at a debt collector. The second was a packet of stolen papers sewn into the lining of her coat—her old coat, the gray one with the burn hole on the left cuff from the night at Kessleford—and it was these he opened now, because the deed was about *him*, but the pages might be about everyone.

There were eleven sheets. He counted them twice, hands not quite steady, laid them out across his table in the lamplight, and began to read.

* * *

They were copies. That much was clear from the first glance—Sera's handwriting was a blunt soldier's scrawl, all forward slashes and no patience for flourishes—but they were careful copies, made by someone who understood that accuracy was the difference between evidence and kindling. Each page bore a column of names. Beside each name, in a different ink, older and browner, was a number.

Not a number of anything obvious. Not ranks, not dates. Just figures, some large, some small, written with the dispassionate neatness of a merchant tallying grain.

Zephyrine read down the first page. Names he knew. Names he had marched beside, bled beside, buried in shallow graves with stones for markers because there wasn't time for prayers.

*Tomas Ellery — 40.*
*Marta Voss-Idren — 115.*
*Cadoc Bell — 8.*

He knew Cadoc Bell. Cadoc Bell had been nineteen years old and could make flowers bloom out of season, which had seemed useless until the winter they'd starved in the passes and his gift had meant the difference between scurvy and soup for two hundred soldiers. Cadoc Bell had died at Redmere Ford holding a bridge nobody thanked him for.

Beside his name: 8.

Eight of *what?*

The answer came on the fourth page, and when it came, Zephyrine stopped being able to feel his hands.

It was a cover sheet, or the remnant of one—a fragment of a longer document, its top edge torn away, but enough remained. Enough of the language. Enough of the seal, pressed in wax gone dark as dried blood, showing a mountain split by a door.

*...hereinafter conveyed to the undersigned agents of the Court Beneath, as surety against the Crown's obligations, the souls of those gifted persons listed herein, their value assessed according to strength, rarity, and cultivation, such souls to be forfeit upon default...*

Souls. Assessed. Cultivated. Like orchards. Like livestock.

Zephyrine read the paragraph four times. On the fifth reading, he noticed the date at the bottom of the fragment, and the name of the signing officer, and he made a sound he did not recognize as coming from himself—something between a laugh and the noise a door makes when it comes off its hinges.

*Commander Alaric Vane.*

Vane. Who had eaten at their fires. Who had learned every mage's name in the company—not just their names, but their mothers' names, their fears, the little details that made a person feel *seen*, because Vane believed morale was won through attention. Zephyrine had worshipped the man. Had once, drunk on victory wine after Saltmarsh, told Vane things about himself he had never told anyone—that his magic felt like a conversation, that his god answered him sometimes in dreams shaped like weather, that he'd been afraid all his life that the gift would be taken away and he didn't know who he'd be without it.

And Vane had put a hand on his shoulder and said, "Nobody's taking anything from you, Joubert. Not while I draw breath."

Then Vane had drawn up a list. And sold them. All of them. Every gifted soul in the Ninth Company, entered line by line into a ledger, valued, signed over as collateral against debts the Crown had run up fighting a war the mages themselves had been sent to win.

Zephyrine stood up. He did not remember standing up. He found himself at the window, looking out at the gray rooftops of Vhalcrest, breathing the way you breathe when the air has gone thin.

*Their silence-praying god never abandoned him—it was imprisoned.* Sera's words, from three days ago, from between the blood on her lips. *They have your god, Zeph. They've had it the whole time. It isn't silent. It's chained.*

He had thought grief had bent her mind. He had thought—generously, pityingly—that a gut wound and three days of flight had scrambled her, that the deed was a forgery, a lure, a trap set by the witch-hunters to draw out mages who still believed in rescue.

But here were the pages. Here were the names. Here was Vane's signature, elegant even in copy, curling at the descenders like it was proud of itself.

* * *

The war came back to him the way floodwater comes back into a cellar—finding every seam, rising fast.

Saltmarsh. He was twenty-three again, kneeling in the mud of a trench with rain hammering his hood, doing the work that had kept the Ninth alive through the third year of the campaign: warding. Small wards, quick wards, the kind that frayed under artillery and had to be renewed nightly. His god had been loud then—or if not loud, then *present*, a warmth behind his sternum, a pressure in the ears before a storm, the sense of someone standing at his shoulder leaning to look at the same work he was looking at.

"Again," Vane had said, walking the trench line at dusk, cloak beaded with rain. "The eastern stretch is thin."

"The eastern stretch is fine," Zephyrine had said. "I did it this morning."

"And I'm asking you to do it again." Vane had crouched beside him, close enough that Zephyrine could smell wet wool and the rosemary oil the man used on his boots. "Humor me, Joubert."

So he had. He'd warded the eastern stretch a second time, pouring more of himself into it than the work required, and Vane had watched him do it with an expression Zephyrine had read, at the time, as fatherly concern.

Now, standing in his cold room eleven years later, he read that expression correctly at last. It had not been concern. It had been appraisal. A man watching how much a thing could produce, and at what cost, and noting both down for the buyers.

Because that was the other thing the pages showed, once he forced himself through them a second time. The numbers weren't random. They tracked. He found his own name on the sixth page—found it the way you find a wound, by the flinch—and beside it:

*Zephyrine Joubert — 340.*

High. Higher than anyone else on the page except Marta Voss-Idren, who had been a storm-caller and had burned out her heart raising a wall of wind at Kessleford. And the entries around his had annotations in Vane's hand, copied faithfully by Sera's blunter pen:

*Joubert, Z. Hedge-trained, self-taught. Devotional caster—power flows from covenant, not bloodline. Note: subject's devotion is unusually strong. Value contingent on continued faith. Recommend monitoring.*

*Recommend monitoring.*

Three years ago, his god had gone silent. Two years and eleven months ago, give or take—he'd never been able to pin the exact day; silence doesn't announce itself, it just becomes the weather. He had spent three years believing he had failed. That his faith had thinned somewhere without his noticing, the way a rope wears, and that his god—discreet, patient, undemanding—had simply stepped back out of disappointment, or exhaustion, or the natural cooling of things.

He had prayed into that silence every single day. Even after the outlawry, even when praying felt like shouting into a well, he had done it in the smallest ways: a thanks before meals, an apology when a charm went crooked, the old bedtime litany his grandmother taught him, murmured so quietly even he barely heard it.

*Recommend monitoring.* Value contingent on continued faith.

They hadn't wanted his faith to fail. His failing faith would have *devalued the asset*. Somewhere beneath a mountain, something had been keeping him faithful—had been answering his prayers with silence in a way engineered to preserve exactly the desperate, aching devotion that made him worth three hundred and forty of whatever the units were.

Or no. He stopped pacing. He made himself stop and think, because Sera had deserved better thinking than panic.

That wasn't quite it. Sera had said the god was *imprisoned*. Chained. So perhaps the silence hadn't been strategy at all. Perhaps his god had been shouting the whole time, and the chains had swallowed every word, and the only thing that got through was nothing at all. Perhaps the Court had assessed him correctly and cruelly: a man whose power flowed from love, and whose love would persist through any silence, and who therefore did not need to be managed at all. Only held. Only kept.

*It spent three years trying to reach you,* Sera had whispered. *It's still trying. That's why you have to hurry, Zeph.*

He sat down at the table again. The lamp had burned low. He trimmed the wick with fingers that shook only a little now, which was its own kind of horror—grief settling into something steadier, harder, more useful. He had seen that transformation in soldiers a hundred times. He had never expected to see it in a mirror.

* * *

Near dawn, he found the last page. It was different from the others—newer, the ink darker, the copying less hurried, as if Sera had saved it for last because she'd known it mattered most.

It was not a list. It was a map of the debt.

Sera had reconstructed it from fragments—fragments marked as such, with her brackets and her terse marginal notes in the soldier's shorthand they'd used to pass messages under officers' noses: *confirmed. unconfirmed. heard secondhand. trust level low.* But even filtered through caution, the shape of it was clear.

The war had been expensive. Everyone knew that; the taxes said so in every market square. What nobody knew was *how* the Crown had paid its bills in the lean years. It had borrowed. And its creditors were not banks.

*The Court holds paper on the Crown,* Sera had written, in her plainest hand. *Old paper. War paper. The collateral is us. Every registered mage, every unregistered one they could find and assess. Thousands. Maybe more—I couldn't get near the full ledger, only these leaves. The deed they carry is the master record. As long as the Crown pays, we are property in storage. If the Crown defaults—*

She hadn't finished the sentence. Instead, in the margin, in letters pressed deep enough to dent the page three sheets below:

*Zeph. Your god is not silent. It is COLLATERAL TOO. Do you understand me? They took it when they took the rest of you. It is the oldest entry in the book.*

He read that line until the words lost meaning and became shapes, and then regained meaning, and hit him all over again.

His god. The quiet warmth behind his sternum. The presence that had walked beside him since he was six years old and hiding under his grandmother's table during the tax riots, when he'd first felt it settle over him like a hand on his hair—not commanding, never commanding, just *accompanying*. He had built his entire life in the space of that companionship. His craft, his courage, his stubborn gentleness—all of it grown in soil that presence had kept warm.

And it was an entry in a ledger. Filed. Valued. Held in a lantern, Sera had said, though she'd refused to say how she knew—*trust level: confirmed, source mine, ask me never*—in a court beneath the Weeping Wood, among pale lights and courteous monsters who treated souls the way Vhalcrest merchants treated bolts of cloth.

Zephyrine looked at the black deed lying on his table. His name on it. His price on it. Three hundred and forty, in ink that predated the war's end by two years—ink that predated the silence by two years.

Two years. For two years after his soul was signed away, his god had still spoken to him. Warmth behind the sternum. Weather-shaped dreams. The sense of being accompanied.

Which meant the silence had come *after* the sale. Which meant the silence was not abandonment. Which meant—

Which meant every prayer he'd whispered into nothing for three years had been heard by something that could not answer, and had kept whispering anyway, and had loved him anyway, from inside a cage, in the dark.

"I'm sorry," Zephyrine said aloud, to the empty room, to the wrapped shape on his bed, to the god who could not hear him and might hear him anyway. "I thought you'd left. I'm sorry. I should have known you'd never—"

He couldn't finish. He put his forehead down on the table, on the stolen pages, on the names of the dead and the priced and the sold, and stayed there while the light came up gray over Vhalcrest.

* * *

When he finally lifted his head, the grief had finished whatever it was doing to him, and what remained was not comfort. It was direction.

He gathered the pages, squared their edges, and bound them with string the way Sera had—tight, soldier-neat. He took the black deed and, for the first time, looked at it fully. His name in the old brown ink. His number. The mountain-and-door seal. At the bottom, the same clause, in the same courteous legal language: *forfeit upon default.*

Default was coming. Anyone could smell it—the Purge, the registrations, the crown squeezing its own gifted population like a man wringing out a rag. And if the Crown defaulted on its debts, ten thousand souls would pass wholly into the Court's hands. Or worse—if the Purge was the Crown *renegotiating*. Selling off its mages outright, one cartload at a time, to settle accounts before the note came due.

Either way, the ledger was moving. Either way, everything Sera had died carrying pointed at one place: down. Beneath the roots of the Weeping Wood, past the trees that bled, into the court of pale lanterns.

He was one hedge-mage with a suitcase of luck-knots. He could not storm a mountain. But he had fought in a war alongside people who could do impossible things, and some of them, he suspected, had survived it—and survival, for people like them, had gotten very lonely lately.

Lonely people find each other. It was practically a law of nature, right after gravity.

Zephyrine banked the lamp, tucked the ledger-pages inside his shirt against his skin where Sera had carried them, and pulled his traveling coat from its hook. On the bed, Sera lay quiet under the mended blanket, and he touched her shoulder once, lightly, the way you'd touch a doorframe leaving a house you grew up in.

"Hold my place," he told her. "I'm going to go collect what's ours."

Outside, Vhalcrest was waking—bakers, fishwives, criers clearing their throats for the morning proclamations. Somewhere under all that gray ordinary noise, beneath the mountain, something chained was waiting for a word from him.

He meant to bring it more than a word. He meant to bring it a crew, a plan, and a fire hot enough to burn a ledger ten thousand names long.

He just had to find the crew first.
