## 5. A Crew of the Hunted

The deed to Zephyrine's soul weighed less than a sparrow's egg. He had carried it for two days now, folded into the lining of his coat where his luck-knots used to live, and he had decided — in the way a man decides not to look at a wound — that he would not think about it.

He thought about it constantly.

What he needed, Sera had said with her last breath, was people. Not soldiers. People who were already hunted, already half-lost, already angry enough to walk into a mountain on the word of a dying woman they'd never met.

Zephyrine had made a list. It had three names on it, which felt ambitious for a man whose entire network of allies had previously consisted of one dead friend and a fishwife who bought luck-knots.

The first name was Odile Vasseur, though nobody in Vhalcrest knew her by that name. In the city she was simply "the widow Marchand," who kept a milliner's shop on Candle Lane and wore gray the way other women wore jewels: expensively, and so no one would ever ask about anything else.

* * *

The bell above the shop door announced him, and Odile looked up from a hat she was trimming with a patience that seemed, as always, slightly performative — as if she had practiced being patient at a mirror until it looked natural.

"Madame Marchand," Zephyrine said. "I'd like to buy a hat."

"You're wearing a hat."

"I'd like one that makes me invisible."

Her needle did not pause. "Try standing very still and being poor. Works wonders." Then her eyes flicked up, took in his face properly, and the needle stopped. She set down the hat, walked to the door, turned the little painted sign to *closed*, and drew the curtain over the window with the unhurried grace of someone who had been expecting this visit for years and refused to seem pleased about it.

"Sera Carrow is dead," Odile said. It wasn't a question. Word traveled strangely through Vhalcrest's hidden veins — faster than criers, faster than fear.

"Three nights ago. In my kitchen. I buried her under the plum tree because the ground outside the walls has witch-hunters in it now."

"Plums," Odile said softly. "Good. She liked plums." She untied her apron, hung it on its hook with terrible finality, and then stood with her hand still resting on the hook as if it might anchor her to this small gray life she'd built. "Let me tell you what you've come to ask, so we can both save some dignity. You want me to come with you. Somewhere dangerous. To do something reckless. Because I'm the only illusionist you know who's survived this long without registering."

"That's roughly it."

"And you know how I've survived?" She turned to face him. Up close, in the good light, there was nothing remarkable about her face at all — that was the point of it. A face like a locked drawer. "Because I have never once done anything reckless. I passed as human when passing was merely rude, and I pass now that it's fatal, and I will pass until I die in a comfortable bed surrounded by hats. That is the whole architecture of my life, Zephyrine Joubert, and it is sound architecture."

"It is," he agreed. "I'm asking you to burn it down anyway."

"Why?"

And there it was — the question he'd rehearsed on the walk over, all his arguments lined up like tin soldiers. He opened his mouth to deploy them and found they'd all deserted. What came out instead was the truth, plain as bread.

"Because Sera died on my floor holding my name in her fist. Because there are thousands of deeds in a vault under the Weeping Wood and every single one belongs to somebody who never agreed to be collateral. And because you trim those hats every morning like a woman doing penance, and I've watched you do it for four years, and I don't think the penance ends until you stop pretending."

The silence stretched long enough that he heard the shop cat purring behind the counter, shamelessly indifferent to destiny.

"The widow Marchand can't leave," Odile said finally. "She has stock. She has obligations." She pulled off her gloves, finger by finger. "Odile Vasseur, however, owes a debt to Sera Carrow from the war that I never repaid, and she has been looking for an excuse to be rid of this shop since the day I opened it. Give me an hour to pack. Take that hat. It suits you better than yours."

It did not suit him at all. He wore it out of respect.

* * *

Brannoc was easier to find and harder to convince, which was the usual arrangement with Brannoc.

Zephyrine located him at the quarry works beyond the east gate, where the stone-singer had spent six years breaking rock by humming to it — work any mule and sledgehammer could do, taken deliberately, invisibly, by a man whose gift could raise cathedrals and who sang only lullabies to gravel so no one would notice.

He was easy to spot even so. Brannoc was the largest person at the quarry by a full head and both shoulders, with a beard like a storm front and forearms that made the pickaxes look ornamental. When he saw Zephyrine approaching along the cart road, he set down his pick, wiped his hands on his thighs, and sighed from somewhere near his boots.

"No," he said.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You've got your asking face on. It's worse than most men's begging faces. No."

Zephyrine climbed onto the flat rock beside him and sat, because arguing with Brannoc while standing was like arguing with a cliff while standing — technically possible, strategically foolish. Below them, forty laborers swung and cursed and sweated. Above them, gulls argued over something dead.

"Sera's dead," Zephyrine said.

Brannoc was quiet for a moment. "The little one with the knives and the opinions?"

"The same."

"She was good people." He said it the way he said everything: flat, certain, immovable. "Bad at cards. Good people."

"She died carrying proof that our souls were sold, Brannoc. Yours, mine, hers. Signed away as war-collateral by Commander Aldous Reyne, and the deeds sit in a vault beneath the Pale Court. The Crown's purge is just the topsoil of it. Underneath, there's a whole ledger of us, and they mean to collect."

Brannoc picked up a pebble, considered it, and crushed it idly to sand between finger and thumb — a habit that would have gotten him burned in any market square in Vhalcrest, performed here only because the quarry was loud and no one looked up. "You want me to come fight a court."

"I want you to come get back what's ours."

"I can't lie, Zeph." He said it without self-pity; it was simply a fact about himself, like his height. "Never could. If a hunter asks me if I'm gifted, I'll say yes, because it'll be true. If a courtier asks why we've come, I'll tell him, because it'll be true. You're recruiting the worst liar in the province for an infiltration."

"I know." Zephyrine watched the gulls. "Here's what I actually think. I think everyone else I could recruit lies so smoothly they'd lie to themselves first and I'd never see it coming. I want one person on this crew whose yes means yes. Whose word I can build a plan on like stone." He glanced sideways. "Also, you once told me — drunk, at the siege of Marrec Ford — that the thing you miss most about before the war was singing properly. Full voice. Stone that answers back. Six years of humming at gravel, Brannoc. That's not safety. That's slow suffocation with good benefits."

The big man stared out at the quarry for a long time. His jaw worked, grinding thoughts the way his hands ground pebbles.

"If I say yes," he said at last, "it's yes all the way down. No changing my mind at the wood's edge. No leaving you in a ditch because the ditch is sensible. That's how I'm built. You understand what you're getting."

"I'm counting on it."

"Then yes." He stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and collected his pick out of pure habit before setting it back down again with a strange tenderness. "One condition. When this is done — whatever 'done' turns out to be — I get to sing somewhere. Loud. Where the stone can hear."

"When this is done," Zephyrine said, "you can sing the mountains into dancing."

"Hm." Brannoc considered this. "That's true, actually. That's the trouble with me. Everything I say comes true or I don't say it." He shouldered his bindle, which appeared to contain one shirt and, judging by the clink, an alarming quantity of rocks. "Lead on, then. I've been waiting six years for somebody to give me a reason, and I'd begun to suspect no one would."

* * *

Wren found *him*, which was how it always went with Wren.

He was crossing Tanner's Square at dusk — new hat, old coat, deed against his ribs — when a small shape collided with his knee, apologized with tremendous insincerity, and was three steps gone before Zephyrine's hand closed gently around her wrist.

"Hello, Sparrow," he said.

Wren was eleven, maybe twelve — she'd stopped answering the question around ten — with a crop of black hair she cut herself and clearly resented, and fingers quicker than rumor. She'd been picking pockets in the gray market since before he'd known her name, and he'd known her name for five years, mostly because she kept stealing from his stall and returning things unprompted, which offended her professionally but suited her morally.

"You felt me," she accused, hanging limp in his grip.

"I felt *Puck* on my shoulder. Your friend's about as subtle as a church bell."

Wren went very still, the way she always did when anyone mentioned Puck. Her eyes flicked to the empty air beside her left ear — the place where, for as long as Zephyrine had known her, an imaginary friend lived. A minor spirit, he'd realized years ago; a hearth-wisp or hedge-thing, drawn to the child the way moths drew to candles, visible only to her. Children with spirits got burned in Vhalcrest now. He'd been waiting months for the right moment to have this conversation, and apparently the moment had chosen to have itself.

"He says you're sad," Wren said carefully.

"Puck talks to you about me?"

"He says you smell like the mountain. Like deep-down places." She twisted free of his grip — of course she did; letting herself be held had been a courtesy — and looked up at him with the flat, evaluating stare she usually reserved for unattended purses. "Something happened to you. You walk different. You keep touching your coat like there's a bird in it."

"There's a deed in it," Zephyrine said, and then, because lying to her felt like kicking a stray dog: "To my soul. Apparently I sold it during the war without knowing. There's a court under the mountain that owns it, and I mean to go take it back."

Wren absorbed this the way children absorb enormous facts: completely, instantly, and without the protective disbelief adults wrapped around such things.

"Can I come?"

"No."

"I'm the best thief in Vhalcrest."

"You're the best thief in Tanner's Square."

"Best thief in Vhalcrest," she repeated, with the serene confidence of someone stating the weather. "You're going to need doors opened and pockets emptied and someone small enough to fit places. And Puck can see in the dark, and he knows when people are lying, sort of, or maybe they just get itchy, I haven't worked out which." She crossed her arms. "Also, the Register-men came to the rookery yesterday. They took Old Nan Tewson. She wasn't even gifted, she just talked to her cats too nicely. So it doesn't matter how quiet I am anymore, does it? Quiet's over."

The words landed in Zephyrine's chest like stones dropped down a well. *Quiet's over.* The whole city learning, street by street, what he'd learned in his kitchen three nights ago.

"Wren," he said, crouching to her level, "what I'm doing may get everyone involved killed. I can't take a child into that."

"I'm not offering to be taken." She stuck out her chin. "I'm offering to come. There's a difference, and the difference is you can't stop me."

Behind her left ear, the empty air shimmered — just barely, just at the edge of seeing, a warmth like a coal remembered rather than seen. Puck, expressing an opinion.

Zephyrine thought of Sera saying *find people*. He thought of the list in his head with its three names, and how each of them had said yes in their own stubborn grammar — Odile through a hat, Brannoc through a condition, Wren through sheer refusal to be left behind. He had come hunting outcasts and had somehow instead assembled a family, which was a far more frightening proposition.

"Rules," he said. "You stay behind me when I say behind me. You run when I say run. And if I tell you to leave — truly leave, not bravely leave — you go, and you don't argue."

"That's four rules."

"The fourth rule is you follow rules one through three."

Wren considered the offer with the gravity of a magistrate. "Done," she said, and spat in her palm, and offered it before he could object, and shook on it before he could recover. Her grin was quick and crooked and entirely without fear. "Where are we going?"

"The Weeping Wood. And under it."

Wren's grin faltered — just a flicker, just for a heartbeat — and her eyes darted to the space beside her ear as though listening.

"Puck says," she reported slowly, "that the Wood is already talking about you. He says the trees know your name, and they're—" she frowned, translating, "—they're *counting* the days? Or the steps. Something like counting." She looked up at him, and for the first time since he'd known her, the little thief looked genuinely uncertain. "Zeph, what's waiting for us under the mountain?"

Zephyrine touched his coat, and the deed beneath the lining, and thought of a god grown silent, and a court grown patient, and a road that began tomorrow at first light.

"An audience," he said. "And I intend to make it a difficult one."

* * *

They gathered that night in the back room of the closed milliner's shop, four chairs around a hat-maker's table, a candle between them. Odile had packed in an hour exactly as promised and emerged transformed — not into beauty, but into ordinariness shed like a coat, revealing someone sharper-edged underneath. Brannoc filled a doorway and ate an entire loaf as though it were a personal insult. Wren sat cross-legged on the table itself because chairs were for other people, turning Zephyrine's black deed over and over in her small quick hands, frowning at it like a lock she intended to beat.

"So," Odile said, tapping the map she'd drawn from memory — roads south, the river, the ash-country beyond. "Four hunted fools walk into a forest that eats memories, to rob a court that owns their souls. Does anyone want to say the obvious thing?"

"That we're going to die?" Brannoc offered helpfully.

"That we should turn back."

Silence, then — the particular silence of people discovering, together, that none of them would.

Zephyrine looked around the candlelight at his impossible crew and felt the strangest thing stir in his chest, unfamiliar after three silent years. It took him a moment to name it. Hope — reluctant, badly dressed, wearing a stolen hat — but hope.

"No," he said. "Nobody wants to say that."

Outside, far to the south, thunder rolled — though the sky, Wren noted later, was perfectly clear.
