## 6. The Road Through Ashfield

Odile had worn many faces in her thirty years, but the one she wore now—*Maren Doss*, widowed herbwife, mild as porridge—was the only one that had ever made her feel like a liar.

It was a good disguise. That was the problem. Illusion was easy; she could have made herself a duchess or a dragon before breakfast. But *Maren* required no magic at all. Just a gray dress, a shawl, a stoop she'd practiced until her back ached honestly, and the daily discipline of being nobody. The others thought of it as safety. Odile thought of it as burial. She had been buried once before, in the marble corridors of her father's house, and knew exactly what the soil felt like.

"You're doing the face again," Wren said, trotting beside her on the rutted road.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're chewing something sour but politely."

"I am not—" Odile caught herself and sighed. "Yes. All right. The face."

Wren grinned up at her, gap-toothed, freckled brown from three days of open road. Ilex—the spirit no one else could see, or so they all politely pretended—was presumably riding on her shoulder, because Wren kept tilting her head to listen to him and giggling at things that hadn't been said aloud.

"He says you chew like a rabbit," Wren reported.

"Tell Ilex that rabbits are charming creatures."

"He says you would say that."

Ahead of them, Brannoc walked with his head slightly bowed, as he always did, as though apologizing to the road for the weight of him. He was a head taller than Zephyrine and built like a gatehouse, and yet he moved quietly, placing his great boots down with a stone-singer's care. Every few hundred paces he'd murmur something under his breath—a word to the gravel, a courtesy to the clay—and Odile, who had grown up around mages who treated the world as furniture, found it endlessly moving. Brannoc thanked the ground for holding him up. She had never once thanked the ground for anything.

And ahead of Brannoc walked Zephyrine, hood up even though the morning was mild, checking the horizon the way other men checked their purses. Constantly. Anxiously. As if at any moment the sky itself might demand to see his papers.

They were four days out of Vhalcrest, and Ashfield lay over the next ridge.

Odile knew this because Zephyrine had told them, standing over his map by last night's fire, his voice carefully flat. "Ashfield burned in the first purge week," he'd said. "Crown riders came through looking for a hedge-healer who'd set a broken arm without registering. They didn't find him. They burned the village anyway, as an example." A pause. "There will be survivors. There are always survivors. We go around."

"We go *around*?" Wren had said. "Through what, then, the burning part?"

"Around the people, Wren. We go around the people."

Now, cresting the ridge, Odile understood why he'd wanted to.

The smell reached them first—that particular stink of burnt thatch and burnt grain and burnt things she refused to name even inside her own skull. Then the village itself: a scatter of blackened houses like teeth knocked out of a jaw, chimneys still standing over nothing, a church roof collapsed inward like a sigh. Smoke still threaded up from two or three places, thin and lazy, days-old fires refusing to admit defeat.

"Oh," said Wren, and stopped giggling. Even Ilex, apparently, had nothing to say.

Brannoc took off his hat. He did not put it back on for the whole descent.

"We can skirt the edge," Zephyrine murmured, already angling them toward a hedgerow. "Ten minutes and we're past. Eyes forward. Don't—"

"Zeph," Brannoc said quietly.

A woman had seen them. She stood between two ruined houses with a bundle of scorched laundry in her arms that she seemed to have forgotten she was carrying, staring at the four strangers coming over the ridge as though they might be angels, or soldiers, or both.

Then she dropped the laundry and ran toward them, shouting over her shoulder in a cracked voice: "Tilda! Run and get the others! Travelers! *Travelers!*"

"*No,*" Zephyrine breathed—but there was nowhere to go. Within moments they were surrounded by perhaps thirty people emerging from cellars and half-standing barns, and every single face turned, with terrible hopefulness, to Odile.

Because she was dressed as an herbwife. Because Maren Doss had kind eyes and a healer's satchel. Because when your world has been burned by men hunting magic, and someone arrives who might be able to mend what cannot be mended, you do not ask their credentials. You simply fall to your knees in front of them.

Which is precisely what the woman did.

"Please," she said, clutching Odile's hem. "My son. His lungs filled with smoke and they haven't cleared, and old Tam's hands are burned to the bone, and the Ferrier girl—please. Please. You're a healer, I can see it, I can *see* it—"

Odile looked down at the woman and felt Maren Doss settle over her shoulders like a lead cloak.

"I'm sorry," she heard herself say. "I'm an herbwife. Salves and teas. I'm—I'm so sorry, but I can't help you."

It was even true, which was the cruelest part. Maren Doss couldn't help anyone. Maren Doss was a costume with nothing underneath but a woman who had spent fifteen years learning to make herself small enough to survive, and who had gotten very, very good at it.

The woman wouldn't let go. "Please. He's seven. He coughs and coughs and there's blood in it now, and there's no one left who knows anything, everyone who knew anything is dead or taken, please, I'll pay, I have nothing but I'll—"

"*I can't help you.*"

Her voice came out harder than she intended—harder than Maren, harder even than Odile—and the woman flinched as if struck. Around them, hope curdled into suspicion. She saw it happen in real time: thirty faces deciding whether strangers who refused to help were merely heartless, or something worse.

Zephyrine stepped smoothly into the gap, hood shadowing his face. "Our apologies," he said, in his market-charm voice, warm and forgettable. "We've little food and less medicine. We'll leave you to your grief."

He touched Odile's elbow. *We're going. Now.*

She let herself be steered. It was the sensible thing. The safe thing. The thing she'd done a hundred times in a hundred villages, walking past the sick and the begging while wearing a face that promised help, because the alternative was the pyre, and she had watched the pyres from her father's windows, and she had sworn—

She had sworn a great many things, once, before she understood what they cost.

They made it perhaps two hundred paces past the village's edge before Wren yanked her hand free of Brannoc's and planted herself in the road like a fence post.

"No," she said.

"Wren—" Zephyrine began.

"You *saw* them. There's a boy coughing blood. You *saw*."

"And I also saw thirty people who will remember our faces for the rest of their lives," Zephyrine said. His voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Wren. If we heal that boy, we heal him with magic. And these people are grieving and terrified, and terrified people talk, and the Crown pays silver for talk. By week's end, witch-hunters will be asking Ashfield about the travelers. About the herbwife who wasn't just an herbwife. About the child traveling with three adults and no family."

"So we don't stop being careful, we just let a kid die *carefully*?"

"That is precisely what I'm saying, yes."

"It's a stupid thing to say."

"It is a *survivable* thing to say," Zephyrine snapped, and then closed his eyes and pinched the bridge of his nose, because even he heard how it sounded. "Wren. Three years ago I would have agreed with you. Two years ago I *did* agree with you, and I watched a man I healed get dragged from his bed with his family screaming. Caution isn't cowardice. Out here it's the only kindness that doesn't kill somebody."

Wren crossed her arms. "Ilex says you're wrong."

"Ilex," said Zephyrine, with the weary patience of a man negotiating with weather, "is not here."

"He's *always* here. He says you're wrong and also that your hood makes you look like a suspicious mushroom."

For one unguarded second, Zephyrine's mouth twitched. Odile caught it. She suspected no one else did—or rather, she suspected Brannoc did, because Brannoc missed nothing about people, only everything about himself.

"Look," Odile said, and hated how Maren's mildness had soaked into her real voice too. "Zephyrine's right. It's monstrous and it's right, both at once. That's the trick of it. That's always the trick of it."

But even as she said it, she watched Zephyrine look back down the road toward the village, toward the thread of chimney-smoke, and she recognized the expression on his face, because she wore it most mornings in the mirror.

It was the face of a man doing arithmetic with his soul and finding the sums never came out clean.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow behind a fallen wall, cold-camped, no fire, because Zephyrine insisted and Brannoc backed him and Odile didn't argue. Cold rations, cold ground, and Wren complaining about both with the tireless artistry of a master.

"—cold oats are just *wet rocks*, Brannoc, you can't tell me otherwise, you're a liar—"

"I cannot lie," Brannoc said placidly. "Cold oats are nourishing."

"That's technically true, which means it doesn't count."

"Technically true is the only kind of truth I have."

Odile smiled despite herself, wrapped in her shawl, listening to the dark. This was the strange new thing, the thing she hadn't expected when she'd agreed to follow a hedge-mage into a mountain: that hunted people, huddled together, made a sound like a home. She had not belonged anywhere in fifteen years. She'd forgotten belonging had a sound. It sounded like bickering over oats.

She was drifting toward sleep when she noticed Zephyrine's bedroll was empty.

She sat up. Across the hollow, Brannoc's eyes were open too—he slept like a cat, in snatches, one ear always cocked—and he gave a tiny shrug that meant *I know, I'm letting him.*

Odile got up, quiet as her training allowed, and followed the faint path of disturbed dew back toward the village road.

She found him crouched at the edge of Ashfield's nearest house, the one where the coughing boy lived, if the wet ragged sound carrying through the broken shutters was any guide. He wasn't moving. He was sitting very still with his hands flat on his knees, and he was praying—to a god who hadn't answered in three years—which was either the saddest thing Odile had ever witnessed or the most stubborn, and she genuinely could not decide.

"You're going to do it," she whispered.

He didn't jump. He'd known she was there; he probably knew she'd followed before she'd decided to herself.

"No," he said.

"You're sitting outside a dying child's window at midnight telling me no."

"Yes."

"All right," Odile said, and sat down beside him in the dirt.

For a long time neither spoke. Inside the house, the boy coughed—a deep, drowning sound, the kind of cough that had a countdown attached to it. Somewhere a dog whined. The stars wheeled overhead, indifferent and lovely.

"My father sold souls," Odile said at last, because it seemed the hour for it, and because she'd carried the confession alone so long it had started to grow roots. "Did you know that? Not metaphorically. Actual souls. Mine included, eventually, though I didn't learn that until later. He called it *stewardship*. Everything in our house was called something prettier than it was." She hugged her knees. "I swore, when I finally ran, that I would never again be the person who watches and does nothing. And now here I am. Watching. Doing nothing. In a costume."

"Maren suits you," Zephyrine said mildly.

"She's burying me alive."

"I know." He was quiet a moment. "I know better than anyone."

Inside, the coughing worsened. Stopped. Started again, weaker.

"Three years," Zephyrine said softly. "That's how long my god's been silent. Three years I've prayed into a well and heard nothing come back. And I told myself it was abandonment. Punishment. Silence, silence, silence." He laughed, very quietly, without humor. "Do you know what the worst part is, Odile? Tonight, sitting here, I realized I've stopped praying for guidance. I just pray for permission. Like a child asking a father who's stopped speaking whether he may have the last biscuit. And the silence always says the same thing, which is nothing, which means I get to decide."

"And what does Odile-without-a-costume decide?"

He exhaled slowly. "That I have spent three years surviving instead of living, and I keep telling myself they're the same thing, and they are *not the same thing*. Sera died carrying proof that my soul belongs to monsters in a hole in the earth. My soul, Odile. Whatever else happens—whatever we find under that mountain—no one gets to tell me what my conscience costs anymore. Not the Crown. Not the Court." He rose, brushing off his knees. "Not me."

He slipped toward the shuttered window, and Odile, after a heartbeat of glorious, terrifying complicity, followed.

What she saw through the broken slats she would carry the rest of her life. Zephyrine knelt by the boy's pallet—a small figure, sweat-soaked, lips gray—and laid two fingers against the child's chest. No grand gesture. No light show. Just a hedge-mage's craft, cheap-looking and humble, the way he'd probably done it a thousand times in a thousand gray markets: warmth drawn up from somewhere deep, coaxed along, poured gently into flooded lungs like sunlight into a cellar.

The boy's breathing changed. The rattle smoothed. The gray went pink. He sighed—a child's boneless sigh of pure relief—and rolled over, and slept.

Zephyrine stayed kneeling a moment longer, forehead nearly touching the pallet, saying something Odile couldn't hear. A thank-you, maybe. An apology. Possibly both.

Then he straightened, turned, and found her watching.

"Well," he said. "Now you know I'm a hypocrite."

"I think," Odile said, "that I've just watched a man break his only rule to save a stranger's child, and I believe the technical term is *accomplice*."

"You should report me to the authorities."

"I'll draft the letter tomorrow." She glanced at the sleeping boy, at the mother asleep in the corner chair, worn through with worry. "They'll remember a kind stranger. One story, one family, one night. Maybe the risk is smaller than you fear."

"Maybe," Zephyrine said. "Or maybe tonight I've signed all our death warrants in invisible ink, and we'll find out when the hunters come." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all, and when they moved southward, toward the black line of the mountains where the Weeping Wood waited, Odile saw something in his face she'd only ever seen in mirrors of her own.

Dread, walking hand in hand with purpose.

"Come on," he said. "Before Wren wakes up and tells Ilex on us."

But as they turned back toward the hollow, Odile stopped.

Because far off—farther than Ashfield, farther than any farm or hamlet, down along the dark southern road where the trees grew thick—she heard dogs. Not farm dogs. Not hounds on a scent of fox or hare.

Hounds baying in that long, organized, hungry cadence that meant men were running them on purpose. Many men. Coming north.

Coming toward Ashfield. Toward the village with one story, one family, one night—and one healed child.

Zephyrine had gone very still beside her. In the starlight his face was pale as paper.

"They're early," he said.
