## 7. Hounds of the Register

Brannoc had never liked lying, which was unfortunate, because for the past eleven days he had been living inside one.

The lie was this: that he was a simple stonemason from the hill country, traveling south with three companions to find work on the aqueducts. It was Odile's lie. She'd built it the way she built everything—carefully, with a pleasing shape and a plausible seam—and she'd asked him to carry it because he had the face for it. Big, slow-looking, honest as a loaf of bread.

He did have the face for it. That was the trouble. When Brannoc lied with his mouth, his whole body knew it, and his body had never learned subtlety. His shoulders hunched. His hands went into his pockets. He sweated like a cheese in summer.

So he kept his mouth shut and let Odile do the talking at waystations, and he told the truth in small doses where small truths were safe, and he sang to the stones under their feet each night so they would warn him if anything heavy came up the road behind them.

Stones were honest. Stones were the only thing in the world, besides himself, that couldn't help being what they were.

It was Wren who heard them first, near midday, when they had come down off the high moor and into the gorge of the River Sere—a deep green-cut cleft where the water ran loud over white stone and the cliffs rose on either side like the walls of some giant's unfinished hallway.

"Horses," she said. She was walking backward on the ledge above the water, the way she did, arms out, utterly without fear of falling, which made Brannoc nervous in a way cliff-faces never had. "Behind us. And dogs."

"Dogs are nothing," Odile said. She didn't look up from adjusting her traveling cloak, making herself plain, making herself forgettable. "Every drover has dogs."

"Not these dogs." Wren's face had gone careful. Careful wasn't natural on her; it sat wrong, like a hat two sizes too big. "Ilex says they smell wrong. Ilex says—" she tilted her head, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear, "—Ilex says *run*."

Zephyrine stopped. He had been walking at the rear, as he always did, and Brannoc had noticed days ago that the hedge-mage walked like a man who expected the road itself to betray him. Now Zephyrine turned, looked back up the trail, and went very still.

Brannoc felt it then too. Not with ears—with soles. Through the packed earth and old river-stone, a rhythm: hooves, many, moving fast but not galloping. Walking-trot, walking-trot. The pace of hunters who knew their quarry had nowhere to go.

"The gorge," Zephyrine said quietly. "They've been herding us toward it since Ashfield. There'll be riders at both ends."

"There's a ford ahead," Odile said. Her voice was smooth as ever, but Brannoc saw her hand tremble once before she flattened it against her skirt. "Half a mile. If we cross—"

"They'll have crossed already," Zephyrine said. "You don't run sniffer-hounds along your own side of a river unless you want them crossing at a place you chose." He looked at Brannoc, and something passed between them—not words. Zephyrine never asked directly. Asking directly put a man in the position of refusing, and refusal between friends was a debt. He only looked, and waited.

Brannoc looked down at the river-stones gleaming under the water, at the grey shoulders of the cliffs rising on either side, and felt the familiar ache in his chest, the ache of a gift held shut like a fist.

"I can bring down the north wall," he said. "There. Where the scree has already given way once. It would close the trail behind us."

"And they'd know," Odile said softly. "A cliff doesn't fall by accident, Brannoc. Not like that. Not in a shape. Every hunter in Vhalcrest will know there's a stone-singer in the company. They'll put your name in the Register with a price beside it."

"My name is already mud," Brannoc said. "My family's farm burned in the first week. My mother sings to the stones in her sleep; she'll be taken whether I sing or not. But you four—" he looked at them, Wren with her spirit-friend, Odile with her buried history, Zephyrine with his terrible folded-up purpose, "—you four still have a chance of arriving somewhere alive."

"It's not just the fall," Zephyrine said. "Once you do it, there's no going back to being a mason looking for aqueduct work. No going back to any life. You understand that?"

Brannoc almost smiled. "Zeph. My friend. I have not been able to go back to any life since the day I was eight years old and sang a boulder off my sister. Everything since has been pretending. This would be the first true thing I've done in twenty years."

* * *

They ran for the narrows.

The gorge tightened ahead of them, walls drawing close enough that the river filled nearly the whole floor, leaving only a shelf of wet rock along the northern face where they could travel single file. Above them the cliff leaned out, forty feet of layered limestone and shale, old and tired, its bones already broken in places—Brannoc could feel every fault line the way another man might feel the cracks in his own teeth.

Below, the water roared. Behind, the hoofbeats came on.

And ahead—

Wren skidded to a stop so suddenly that Odile walked into her. At the far end of the narrows, maybe two hundred yards, riders had appeared. Six of them, in the grey-and-gold of the Crown Register, and with them loped four animals that moved wrong—too long in the body, noses down, casting side to side across the trail like fisher-birds.

Sniffer-hounds. Bred in the kennels beneath the palace, they said, on things Brannoc did not want to think about. They didn't hunt scent exactly. They hunted the residue magic left in the world, the faint sweet rot of it, the way a crow hunts shine.

One of them lifted its head and screamed. It was not a sound a dog should make.

"Well," said Odile, very calmly. "That's that, then."

"Both ends," Zephyrine breathed. "Both ends closed."

Brannoc pressed his palm flat against the cliff.

He had learned as a child that singing to stone was not commanding it. That was what lay-folk thought—that a stone-singer whistled a tune and mountains danced. It wasn't like that. Stone had lived a long time. Stone had opinions. What a singer could do was remind the stone of what it already wanted. And this cliff, this tired leaning wall of layered grey—Brannoc listened to it with his hand and his hum, low in his chest where no one could hear—and the cliff wanted, very badly, to let go. It had wanted to let go for a hundred years. It was only waiting for permission.

"You're sure?" Zephyrine said at his shoulder.

"No," Brannoc said honestly. "But the stone is. Get everyone below the bend. Past the fallen pine. The rock will come this way, mostly."

"*Mostly?*"

"Stone is honest," Brannoc said. "Honest things are never tidy."

Zephyrine gripped his arm, hard, once, and then was gone, herding Wren and Odile back around the bend where the pine lay. Brannoc heard Wren arguing—he caught the words *not without* and *stupid great ox of a man*—and then Zephyrine's voice, low and steady, and then only the river.

Brannoc turned to face the cliff, and took off his coat, and folded it, and set it down neatly on the ledge. If he was going to be a marked man, he would at least be a marked man in his shirtsleeves.

Then he began to sing.

Not with his mouth. Deeper than that—in the ribs, in the breastbone, in the hollow places the way his mother had taught him before either of them understood that teaching it was a crime. The song had no words. It was older than words. It was the sound a mountain makes when it settles in winter, the sound of patience ending.

*Let go*, he sang to the cliff. *You have carried it long enough. Let go.*

The cliff shivered.

Dust sifted down through the sunlight like flour through a sieve. Somewhere behind him, distantly, the sniffer-hounds began to scream again, all four at once, and hooves wheeled, and a man shouted an order that the river swallowed whole.

The crack came first—a sound like the world's largest door unlocking—and then the whole northern face of the narrows shrugged its shoulder and stepped forward into the air.

Brannoc stood in the rain of it. Small stones bounced off his shoulders; larger ones he sang aside, turning their fall by a hand's width, a foot, just enough, the way you might steer a drunk friend home. The mass of it went where he had asked—down and across the trail, filling the narrows, slamming into the far wall with a boom that he felt in his teeth rather than heard. Water leapt up white as milk. The river, furious at being interrupted, piled against the new dam and began, immediately, to look for a way around, because rivers are also honest and also untidy.

When the dust cleared, the trail behind was gone. Forty feet of cliff stood where forty feet of path had been, tumbled and raw and unmistakably deliberate—the rubble lay in a fan, in a shape, in a curve no avalanche ever made.

Brannoc picked up his coat and put it on with shaking hands.

His hands always shook after. Not from fear. From the singing—it cost something, it always cost something, a heat drawn up out of the marrow. He'd pay for this tonight in a fever, and tomorrow in a worse one. That was the bargain stone-singers made, and it was fair, he'd always thought. Nothing true should be free.

Around the bend came Wren at a dead sprint, and she hit him at full tilt, arms around his middle, her face mashed into his coat, and he staggered back a step and laughed despite everything.

"You're supposed to be running *away* from the landslide, sprout, not toward it."

"You're supposed to be *under* it, you great stupid hero," she said into his chest, muffled and furious, "and instead you're *here*, which is the only acceptable outcome, and if you ever do that again I'll—I'll pickpocket you into penury, I know where you keep your coin purse now—"

Odile arrived more composedly, though her composure had cracks in it, and behind her Zephyrine, who said nothing at all, only looked at the sealed gorge, and then at Brannoc, and nodded once, the way men nod at graves and at weddings.

Behind the fresh rockfall, faintly, the hounds still screamed. Then one voice among them—human, amplified by some registered trick of the air, carrying flat and cold across the water:

*"Stone-singer identified! Company of five, southbound! Marked for the Register—dead or bound!"*

The echo rolled away down the gorge and came back thinner, and thinner, and was gone.

"There it is," Brannoc said. He found he was grinning. It surprised him. "Twenty years of pretending, ended by a Tuesday."

"Don't joke," Odile said sharply. Then, gentler, because she could never stay sharp at anyone for longer than a breath: "Don't joke. Not about this. You've no idea what they do to stone-singers. They take them to the quarries at Kellin's Hollow and they work them until the gift burns out and then they take *that* too."

"Then it's fortunate," Brannoc said, "that we are going somewhere they cannot follow. Isn't that right, Zeph? Somewhere under a mountain, where the Pale Court holds all the papers?"

Zephyrine was quiet a moment. The river roared through the gap it had found around the rockfall, throwing spray across all of them, cold as honesty.

"We're past the point of turning around," Zephyrine said at last. "All of us. Whatever's left of my plan, it needs people who've already burned their boats." He looked around at them—at Wren dripping and defiant, at Odile with her cracked composure, at Brannoc in his dust-grey coat—and something in his face shifted, some weight rearranging itself. "I meant to do this alone, you know. Back in Vhalcrest. I meant to walk into that mountain by myself and die there politely."

"Terrible plan," said Wren.

"Truly dreadful," Odile agreed.

"Worst I ever heard," said Brannoc. "And I once met a man who tried to fight a mill."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped Zephyrine—short, startled, rusty from disuse. He turned south, deeper into the gorge, toward the country beyond, where the maps grew vague and the names of places grew old.

"The Weeping Wood is six days from here," he said. "If the wood doesn't turn us away. If the Court doesn't know we're coming." He paused, and his eyes went distant in a way Brannoc distrusted deeply, the look of a man hearing something no one else could hear. "We should hurry. I don't know why. But we should hurry."

They went, single file along the wet shelf of stone, five hunted souls in a gorge that rang with the sound of the river putting the world back together.

And far behind them, muffled by forty feet of honest, untidy rock, the hounds screamed on—and beneath their screaming, so faint that only a stone-singer could have caught it, Brannoc felt something else. A vibration in the ground under his boots. Rhythmic. Patient.

Hooves. On the far side of the fall, riders were already turning back—to spread the word, to carry the mark, to ride ahead.

The gorge had bought them time. Only time. And somewhere south, in the space between them and the Weeping Wood, that time was already being spent.
