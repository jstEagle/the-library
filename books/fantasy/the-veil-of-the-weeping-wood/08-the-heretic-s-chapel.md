## 8. The Heretic's Chapel

Wren had stolen a lot of things in her eleven years—bread, buttons, a silver hairpin once that she'd worn for a whole day before the guilt got too heavy—but she had never before stolen shelter. It felt different. Bigger. Like lifting something with roots.

The chapel crouched at the bottom of a ravine where the river bent, half-swallowed by ivy and one enormous hawthorn that had grown right through the roof, so that branches poked up through broken tiles as if the tree were politely raising its hand. The door was boarded, but Brannoc pulled the boards off like they were made of bread, and there it was: a little stone room with a fallen altar, a floor of cracked slate, and walls covered in carvings.

Wren stood just inside the doorway and stared.

She'd seen shrines before. Vhalcrest had them on every corner, all gold leaf and smug candlelight, all dedicated to the Crown's approved gods—the ones who smiled in portraits and never answered anybody. This wasn't like that. The carvings here were old and strange and a bit rude, honestly. There was a woman with antlers holding a snake. There was a fat man dancing with a skeleton, both of them laughing. There was a row of small figures holding hands, and above them someone had carved words so worn by weather and time that only three remained legible:

**ALL ARE WELCOME**

"Heretics," Odile said softly. She had her hood down for the first time since Ashfield, and in the gray light through the broken roof her face looked younger and more tired than her voice ever let on. "This is an Old Faith chapel. Before the Crown picked its gods. They worshipped everything—the small spirits, the wood-wives, the hearth-ghosts. Everything that talks."

"That's why it's outlawed," Zephyrine said. He came in last, as he always did, checking the treeline first. "You can outlaw a person easier than you can outlaw an idea, but you can outlaw *where* an idea lives."

"You," said Wren, "talk like a book somebody dropped in a puddle."

Zephyrine looked at her. For a moment she thought he might be angry, but then his mouth did the thing it sometimes did—not quite a smile, more like a smile's shy cousin. "Fair."

Brannoc set their packs down by the altar and immediately began inspecting the stonework, running his big flat hands over it the way other people petted dogs. "Good stone," he rumbled. "Old stone. It remembers songs." He paused. "It says this place hasn't been used in sixty years, except once. Somebody hid in here during the war. A soldier." His brow creased. "It doesn't say if he was hiding from anyone or just from being sad."

"Could be either," Zephyrine said quietly. "War does both."

Wren drifted along the wall while the grown-ups did grown-up things—Odile checking the wards she'd laid around the ravine, Zephyrine sorting what was left of their food into portions so small they were mostly philosophical. Ilex rode on her shoulder, weightless as always, though she could feel the faint warmth of him through her shirt collar, like a coin left in the sun.

Ilex was her secret. Had been since she was six, when he'd appeared in the corner of the lean-to behind the tannery where she slept, a flicker of green-gold light shaped vaguely like a fox, or maybe a very opinionated leaf. Nobody else had ever seen him. She'd stopped mentioning him years ago because the last time she had, the tannery mistress had looked at her with that particular expression—the one that meant *broken thing, dangerous thing, get rid of it*—and Wren had learned the most important lesson of survival: some truths you keep in your pocket, not your mouth.

But lately, traveling with these three, keeping Ilex in her pocket had started to feel less like wisdom and more like lying. And lying to these people felt wrong in a way she didn't have words for yet.

"Wren," Zephyrine called. "Food. Eat."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry. You ate a beetle yesterday and called it 'a bit gamey.'"

"It *was* gamey." But she came and sat, because the truth was the dried plums smelled good and her stomach was making demands like a debt collector.

They ate in the falling dusk, the hawthorn branches scratching gently against each other overhead like the chapel itself was trying to whisper something. Brannoc sang under his breath—one of his stone-songs, low and tuneless and somehow comforting, like a mountain clearing its throat. And Wren sat with her plum and her secret warming her shoulder, and decided.

Not forever. Just for tonight.

* * *

It happened later, when the argument was already going.

She should have known it would. They'd been circling it for two days, the way horses circle a fire they can smell but don't trust. Tonight, in the dark of the heretic chapel with one candle lit (Odile's doing; Zephyrine had wanted no light at all), the circling stopped.

"We should talk about turning back," Odile said.

Nobody gasped. Everybody had been waiting for somebody to say it.

"Back to what?" Brannoc asked. He sat with his back against a pillar, arms crossed over his chest like a drawbridge gone up. "Ashfield's ash. My face is on a hunter's board now—I saw it myself in Marlow's Ferry. They drew my nose too big, which was insulting twice over."

"They'll have drawn mine too by now," Odile said. "And yours, Zeph. And—" she hesitated, just slightly, "—they'll be looking for a child traveling with mages. Turning back means splitting up. Means Wren goes somewhere safe, and we—"

"No," Wren said.

Odile sighed. "Sweetling—"

"I'm not sweetling-ing my way onto a shelf somewhere. I know what 'somewhere safe' means. Somewhere safe is where people put you until they forget you." She heard her own voice go sharp and hated it a little, but not enough to stop. "You want to send me away because I'm small. But I'm the reason we found the road past the tollhouse. I'm the one who spotted the hunters' camp before Brannoc walked into it singing. And I'm not—" Her throat did something traitorous. "I'm not going to be the thing you leave behind. Not again. Not ever again."

The silence after that had a shape. It sat in the middle of the ruined chapel like another guest.

It was Zephyrine who broke it, and he didn't argue. That was the thing about him nobody expected, looking at his tired face and his charm-seller's hands—he never argued with fear. He just stood next to it.

"She's right that she's earned her place," he said. "But Odile's right that it gets worse from here. So let's say it plain, all of us, and then decide together." He looked around the candlelight. "The Pale Court holds our souls in a ledger. Sera died getting me proof. Every day we walk toward the mountain instead of away from it, we're betting our lives that dead women make better plans than living courts. If anyone wants out, this is the night to say it. No shame in it. I mean that."

"I want out," said a voice that belonged to no one in the room.

Wren went cold all over.

Because she knew the voice. She'd been hearing it inside her own head for five years—a dry rustling voice, sardonic and warm underneath, the voice of the green-gold flicker in the corner of her childhood. Except this time it hadn't come from inside her head.

It had come from *out loud*. From her shoulder.

Every face turned to her.

"Ilex?" she whispered. "Did you—you *talked*. Out loud. You've never—" Her heart was hammering somewhere up around her ears. "Why would you say that? We're not leaving!"

"I said what needed saying," Ilex said, and now everyone could hear it: a voice like leaves turning over, like wind through a keyhole, coming from the shadow on her shoulder where the candlelight didn't quite reach. "To see who flinches. The tall one didn't. The stone-man didn't. The pretty liar reached for a knife, which I respect enormously."

Odile's hand was indeed near her knife. She withdrew it slowly, eyes narrowed. "What *is* that?"

"He's my friend," Wren said, standing up, standing in front of her shoulder like she could shield him, absurdly, the way you shield a candle with your whole body. "He's been my friend since I was six. He's a spirit. A small one. A hearth-spirit or a leaf-spirit or—I don't actually know, he's never said, he's extremely annoying—"

"I'm a wight of hedgerow and threshold," Ilex said, with dignity. "Which your kind has forgotten how to name, which suits me fine. And I have kept quiet for five years, small thief, because quiet is how small things live. But we are nine days from a court that eats souls, and I find my policy of quiet has expired."

"Why now?" Zephyrine asked. He hadn't moved. He was watching the shadow on Wren's shoulder with an expression Wren couldn't read—something between hunger and grief. "Five years silent, and tonight you speak?"

"Because tonight I finally could," Ilex said. "Old places remember old permissions. This chapel was built when things like me could speak freely, and some of that permission soaked into the stone." A pause, a rustle. "Also, because I'm frightened, and I've discovered that being frightened silently is considerably worse."

That, Wren understood completely.

"The Court knows," Ilex continued, and the warmth went out of his voice, leaving only the dry rustle. "I felt it three nights ago, at the edge of the Weeping Wood. Something turned its attention toward us—toward *him*—" Wren felt the shadow shift, gesturing at Zephyrine, "—the way a spider turns toward a fly that thinks it's found a hole in the web. The Pale Court already knows Zephyrine Joubert is coming. I do not think they mind."

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

"Then it's a trap," Odile said flatly.

"Of course it's a trap," Zephyrine said.

"Then we turn back. Now. Tonight."

"And do what?" For the first time all night, Zephyrine raised his voice—not much, but enough that it rang off the old stones, and even Brannoc's stone-song stopped. "Wait for the ledger to activate? Wait for the Purge to reach every hedge-witch and stone-singer and child-with-a-spirit in the country? Sera didn't die so I could run *better*, Odile. She died so I could stop running at all." He passed a hand over his face. When he spoke again it was quieter. "I spent three years thinking my god abandoned me. Three years praying into silence and telling myself it was my punishment, my failing, my—my *fault*. And it turns out it was chained in the dark the whole time, spending itself down to nothing to keep people like us hidden." His jaw worked. "If it's a trap, then I spring it. That's what you do with traps. You don't leave them set for the next person."

Silence again. But a different silence. Wren knew silences the way other children knew lullabies, and this one was the kind that comes after something breaks open—painful, and clean, and full of air.

Brannoc spoke first. "My clan has a saying. *A stone does not choose which mountain it belongs to. But it chooses whether it stands.*" He unfolded his arms. "I stand."

Everyone looked at Odile. She was very still, and Wren—watching her the way she watched marks, watching for the tell—saw her fingers press flat against her knee, once, twice, like she was smoothing a wrinkle in herself.

"My family bought their safety," Odile said at last, so quietly Wren almost missed it. "Years ago. With currency I won't name in front of the child." Ilex made a small sound that might have been a snort. "I told myself it was different. It wasn't. So no—I won't turn back and wait for the world to keep working exactly the way that made me what I am. I'd rather break it." She looked at Zephyrine. "But when this goes wrong—and it will—you follow my lead in the vault, or I leave you in it."

"Agreed," Zephyrine said.

"And you, small thief," Odile added, turning to Wren, eyebrows up. "You've heard the spirit. It's a trap, and the trap knows your name now too, probably. Last chance."

Wren looked at them. The hedge-mage with his tired eyes and his stubborn spine. The illusionist pretending she wasn't terrified. The stone-man who couldn't lie and never tried. And on her shoulder, the friend she'd kept hidden her whole life, glowing faintly now in the dark like the last ember of a fire everyone else had given up on.

"Somebody has to steal your souls back," she said. "None of you can pick pockets worth anything. Brannoc, you once tried to steal an apple and apologized to the stall."

"It was a good apple," Brannoc said gravely. "It deserved acknowledgment."

Laughter went around the chapel—small and surprised and real, the kind that lives next door to crying. Even Zephyrine's smile's shy cousin came out to visit.

"So we stay," Wren said. "We go on. All of us."

"All of us," Zephyrine agreed. Then his gaze lifted past her, to the shadowed wall behind the fallen altar, and his whole body went still. "Wren. Look."

She turned.

The worn carving above the small hand-holding figures—the one where only **ALL ARE WELCOME** remained legible—was no longer worn. In the candlelight, letters were surfacing out of the ancient stone like fish rising to feed, line by line, in a script older than any kingdom:

***THE COURT SEES YOU COME. IT HAS LIT YOUR CHAIRS.***

Beneath it, smaller, almost gentle:

***COME ANYWAY.***

Nobody moved. The hawthorn scratched its branches overhead, soft as breathing.

"Well," said Ilex dryly, from Wren's shoulder. "For what it's worth, I *did* warn you."

And far off beyond the ravine, deep in the direction of the Weeping Wood, something that was not wind began—very faintly, very patiently—to sing.
