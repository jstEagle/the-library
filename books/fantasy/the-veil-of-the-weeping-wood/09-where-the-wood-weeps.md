## 9. Where the Wood Weeps

The Weeping Wood announced itself a full day before they reached it.

They smelled it first—a sweetness like honey left too long in the sun, thick enough to taste on the back of the tongue. Then the birds stopped. Not fled; stopped, all at once, as though someone had drawn a curtain across the sky and forgotten to open it again. Brannoc halted mid-stride with his head cocked toward the treeline, and Zephyrine watched the big man's face do something complicated.

"Stone doesn't like it here," Brannoc said at last.

"Stone doesn't *like* anything," Wren said. "It's stone."

"Stone likes me." Brannoc said it without a flicker of humor, because Brannoc could not lie and apparently also could not joke, which made him either the easiest or the hardest member of the crew to travel with depending on the hour. "The rock under our feet has been grumbling since noon. It wants us to go around."

"The rock can file a complaint," Odile said. She walked with her hood up even though there was no sun—there hadn't been proper sun since Ashfield—and her voice had the bright, brittle cheerfulness she wore like armor. "We didn't cross half the country and lose Brannoc's good name to a cliff collapse to go *around*."

"I didn't lose my good name," Brannoc rumbled. "I gave it away. There's a difference."

"There is," Zephyrine said quietly, "and I'll not forget it." He meant it to close the subject. It didn't quite; it never did. Every kindness he acknowledged seemed to weigh more on them than the cruelty of the world did.

Wren appeared at his elbow the way she always did, silent as spilled flour, chewing something she almost certainly had not paid for. "Zeph. The trees are bleeding."

"They're what?"

"Look."

He looked.

The first trees of the Weeping Wood rose out of the ground like mourners at a funeral no one wanted to name aloud. They were old—older than Vhalcrest, older than the crown, older perhaps than the idea of crowns—and from every knot and seam in their silver-gray bark ran slow beads of amber sap that gathered, swelled, and fell. Fell like tears. The ground beneath them was glazed with centuries of it, a road of hardened resin that caught the gray light and threw it back gold.

"It's just sap," Odile said, but her voice had gone careful.

"It's just blood, if you're feeling generous," said a small voice near Wren's shoulder, and everyone flinched except Wren, who beamed.

Ilex had been visible only to Wren for the entire journey—a shimmer, a suggestion, a child-shaped absence in the corner of the eye. But since the Heretic's Chapel, since the spirit had spoken its warning aloud, it had been growing less shy. Now it sat cross-legged on a root, faintly green, faintly there, regarding the weeping trees with an expression of ancient, put-upon familiarity.

"You know this place," Zephyrine said.

"I *am* this place," Ilex said, "in the way that a drop of rain is the ocean. Badly, and briefly." It picked at the bark beside it. "The Court's garden used to start here, long ago, before they dug themselves down and locked the door. These trees were planted to grieve for it. Nobody told them they could stop."

Zephyrine looked at the amber drops falling, falling, falling, patient as grief itself, and felt something cold walk down his spine wearing his own name.

"Well," he said. "Best keep moving, then. Grieving forests make poor hosts."

* * *

Inside the Wood, the light changed.

It came down through the canopy in long slanted columns, thick with pollen or dust or something finer, and it moved when there was no wind to move it. The crew walked in single file along the resin road, boots sticking faintly with each step—*shk, shk, shk*—a rhythm like reluctant applause.

An hour in, Wren started humming. An hour after that, Zephyrine realized the tune wasn't hers.

"Do you hear that?" he asked.

Odile nodded slowly. "Like singing through water."

"Not singing," Brannoc said. His knuckles were white around his walking staff. "Bargaining."

And then Zephyrine heard it properly, and wished he hadn't.

The whispers came from everywhere—from the trunks, from the sap, from the spaces between the light columns. Voices layered over voices, some cracked with age, some young and frightened, all of them murmuring the same grammar: *give me, give me, and I'll give you.* A woman's voice offering ten years of her life for one more look at her son's face. A man trading the memory of his father's hands for freedom from pain. A child—gods, a child—asking what a name was worth these days.

"The dead?" Odile whispered.

"Worse," said Ilex, who had climbed onto Wren's back like a small pale monkey and now peered over her shoulder with eyes gone dark as river stones. "The *kept*. Souls the Wood swallowed before the Court learned to write things down. They're still mid-bargain. They'll always be mid-bargain. That's how the Wood feeds—it doesn't eat you, it just never lets you finish your sentence."

"Don't listen," Zephyrine said. "Don't answer. Whatever you hear, whatever it offers—" He turned to face them, and saw that all three were already pale, already listening despite themselves, and understood that he'd have done better to say nothing at all, because now they knew there was something to hear. "It knows what you want. That's the whole trick. It only ever quotes your own heart back to you with interest."

"And what does it quote *you*, hedge-mage?" Odile asked. It wasn't cruel. It was worse—it was curious.

Zephyrine opened his mouth to say *nothing, I want nothing,* which was a lie so old it had grown comfortable as a slipper, and—

The Wood answered for him.

"*Hurry.*"

One word. One voice. It did not come from the trees or the dead or the whispering dark. It came from underneath everything, from beneath the mountain's roots themselves, and it was small and hoarse and worn thin as prayer-ribbon, and Zephyrine knew it the way you know your own heartbeat: not by listening but by being alive inside it.

Three years of silence. Three years of praying into a well and hearing nothing come back. Three years of telling himself the god had simply gone, the way gods went, the way fathers and wars and easy sleep went—and all that time it had been *here*, under his feet, saying his name in a voice scraped down to a whisper.

He stopped walking. He might have stopped breathing.

"Zeph?" Wren's hand found his sleeve. "Zeph, you've gone funny-colored."

"Hear something?" Odile asked sharply. "What did it offer you?"

He couldn't answer. His throat wouldn't hold the shape of anything true, and everything else would have been a lie, and lying in this wood felt suddenly, catastrophically unwise.

Because the word hadn't been *hurry* alone, not really. Words in a god's mouth carry their own luggage. Beneath the urgency he had felt—distinct as handwriting—the rest of the sentence, the part the voice was too weak to say:

*I am still yours. Come.*

His eyes were burning. When had his eyes started burning?

"Zephyrine." Odile stepped in front of him, and behind the illusion's borrowed face her real expression showed through for once—worried, sharp, human. "Talk to us. In a forest where voices quote your heart back at you, going quiet is how people get eaten."

"He's not quiet," Wren said, staring up at him with her quick thief's eyes. "He's *listening*. I know that face. That's the face I make when someone's counting coins in the next room and I'm deciding whether to climb through the window."

A laugh broke out of Zephyrine, wet and startled and nothing like dignity. "You're a terrible influence, Wren."

"I'm a *wonderful* influence. Ask anyone whose house I've robbed."

He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, rough and graceless, and made himself say it aloud, because if the god could spend three years whispering through stone for him, the least he owed it was three sentences to friends.

"That was my god. Just now. First word in three years." He watched their faces take it in—Brannoc going very still in the way of men who took oaths seriously, Odile's breath catching, Wren's grin arriving sideways and soft. "It said hurry."

"Hurry where?" Brannoc asked.

Zephyrine looked down the resin road, into the deepening gold-and-shadow of the Wood, toward the mountain whose roots drank from these bleeding trees. Somewhere ahead was a door sealed by a riddle of names. Somewhere below it, a lantern of bone. Somewhere inside that, a voice worn to a whisper, still keeping faith with a fool who'd spent three years assuming he'd been abandoned.

"Down," he said. "It said hurry *down*."

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow of a tree the size of a chapel, and the Wood let them—which, Ilex observed darkly, was somehow worse than if it hadn't.

"No fire," Odile said, settling against the bark. "Smoke reads for miles in a forest this quiet."

"No argument from me." Zephyrine divided out the hard bread and dried apples, and noticed—as he had been noticing for days—that Wren had taken to sitting pressed against his side while she ate, casual as a cat claiming a chair, and that he had stopped pretending not to notice. Some things you didn't name. Naming them made them fragile.

The whispers continued outside, patient as tide. *Give me, give me, and I'll give you.* Brannoc had wedged himself at the hollow's mouth with his palms flat against the floor, and when Zephyrine raised an eyebrow, the stone-singer shrugged.

"Listening to the rock again. It says the roots above us are arguing about whether to drop."

"Charming."

"It also says," Brannoc added, with the grave precision of a man reporting weather, "that you should stop fighting the word you heard. The whole mountain heard it. Stone carries sound further than air."

Zephyrine's bread stopped halfway to his mouth. "The whole—"

"Roots are connected to roots. What bleeds here runs there." Brannoc's eyes, in the gray dark, held something that in a man capable of smiling might have been gentleness. "Whatever's chained under that mountain has been talking to these trees about you for a long time, hedge-mage. I'd stake my marked hide on it."

Across the hollow, Wren had fallen asleep mid-chew, her head sliding off Zephyrine's shoulder onto his arm, one hand still curled around a stolen apple core. Ilex sat watch by her feet, dimmed to a coal-green glow, and for once the little spirit's ancient face held no irony at all.

"You heard it too," Zephyrine said to Ilex quietly. "Before any of us. Back at the chapel. You said the Court already knew I was coming."

Ilex considered him for a long moment.

"I said the Court knew," it said. "I never said the Court was the only one who knew. Things echo down there, hedge-mage. Chains are loud, if you know how to listen." It lay down, small and fading. "Sleep. Tomorrow the Wood will ask you what you love, and it will ask in your own voice, and you mustn't answer. None of you must answer."

"And if we do?"

"Then you'll join the choir," Ilex said, "and the trees will cry a little harder, and no one will remember why."

The spirit dimmed to nothing. Outside, the sap fell in the dark, drop by golden drop, and the dead went on whispering their unfinished bargains, and somewhere far below—so far Zephyrine had to strain to feel it, the way you strain to feel your own pulse—one worn-thin voice kept time with his heartbeat.

*Hurry.*

"I'm coming," he whispered, so softly that not even Brannoc's stone could carry it. "Hold on. I'm coming."

The Wood, gently, terribly, repeated it back to him in a hundred stolen voices—including, for one awful moment, Sera's—and Zephyrine closed his eyes and did not answer, and did not sleep, and counted the hours until dawn like a miser counting coins he already knew he'd have to spend.
