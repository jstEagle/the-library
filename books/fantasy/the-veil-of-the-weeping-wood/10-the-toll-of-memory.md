## 10. The Toll of Memory

Odile had learned long ago that the difference between a noblewoman and a fugitive was mostly footwear. A lady's slippers announced her before she entered a room; the cracked boots she wore now let her pass through the world like weather—unremarked, unremembered, which was precisely what she wanted.

She was remembering this wisdom, or trying to, when the trees began to weep in earnest.

They'd been walking since dawn beneath the canopy of the Weeping Wood, and Odile had privately catalogued at least eleven reasons to turn around. The sap that ran down the pale bark like tears. The whispering—she could no longer pretend it was wind, because wind did not say *names*. The way Wren kept a death-grip on Ilex's invisible sleeve and hummed tunelessly to drown out the voices. And above all, the silence where Zephyrine's god should have been, a silence so heavy it seemed to press on everyone's shoulders like a wet wool cloak.

Then the path simply ended.

Not narrowed. Not grew overgrown. *Ended*, as though someone had rolled up the forest floor behind them like a carpet, leaving them standing in a circle of white-barked trees whose sap ran so thickly it pooled at their roots in amber cups.

"Oh," said Brannoc, "I don't like this."

"You don't like anything," Wren said, but her voice wobbled on the second word.

"I liked the bread in Ashfield. Before the child spat in it."

"She didn't spit in it, she blessed it."

"It had grit in it."

Odile raised a hand for quiet, an old habit from ballrooms full of people far more dangerous than any child with blessed bread. It worked on the crew, which told her something both flattering and sad about how starved they were for someone to seem in charge.

The pooling sap began to move.

It gathered itself upward, drop by glistening drop, into a shape that was almost a person—almost, if people were made of honey-colored glass and stood nine feet tall and had eyes like two frozen ponds. When it spoke, its voice came from everywhere at once, including, Odile was horrified to note, inside her own skull, where it sounded disconcertingly like her grandmother.

**"Travelers."**

"Good morning," said Brannoc, because he could not lie, and apparently also could not be sensible.

The spirit turned its pond-eyes on him. **"You are in debt."**

"We haven't taken anything," Zephyrine said carefully. He had his merchant voice on—the one he used in the gray market, warm and reasonable and utterly concealing everything. "We've only walked through."

**"Walking is taking. You take the path. You take the shade. You take the silence between the whispers."** The spirit's form shivered, and the light through the canopy dimmed as though the wood itself were leaning in to listen. **"Nothing passes the Weeping Wood without payment. And here, coin is worthless. Here, we take only what cannot be counted."**

"Memories," Odile said quietly.

Everyone looked at her. She kept her gaze on the spirit, because looking at them would mean showing them her face, and her face, at present, would show exactly how much she understood.

**"The noble-born knows,"** the spirit said, with something like pleasure. **"Yes. Memories. Small ones, if your debts are small. Large ones, if you have lied about your debts."**

"How small?" Wren asked. Her hand found Ilex's sleeve again. "Like—what did I eat yesterday? Because honestly, that's not worth much to anyone."

**"Small as a scent. Large as a face."**

Silence. Somewhere overhead, a tree wept a single fat tear of sap that fell with a sound like a struck bell.

Zephyrine stepped forward, and Odile saw him do the thing he always did: put himself between danger and the others, casually, as though he were merely adjusting his position in line at a market stall. "All right. Let's talk terms. What do you need from each of us, specifically? Because I'll tell you now—" he gestured at Wren, "—that one is seven years old and if you take anything from her larger than her opinion of turnips, I will spend whatever remains of my life making your afterlife very tedious."

The spirit regarded him for a long moment. **"You bargain well for a man with nothing left to lose."**

"You'd be surprised what a man can lose and keep going anyway."

Odile's chest tightened. She knew the shape of that sentence. She had built whole years of her life out of sentences like that.

* * *

They paid one at a time, because the spirit insisted, and because there was no arguing with something that could speak inside your skull.

Wren went first—after a lengthy negotiation in which Zephyrine secured, in writing (the spirit traced glowing letters into the air, which Odile thought was either reassuring or deeply ominous), a guarantee that nothing would be taken beyond "a memory of being bored." Wren squared her little shoulders, marched up to the towering glass figure, and stuck out her chin.

"Take my memory of waiting outside the magistrate's office in Vhalcrest," she said. "That was the most boring afternoon of my whole life. Ilex says it too."

Ilex, who still had never spoken aloud where anyone but Wren could hear, must have agreed, because Wren nodded firmly and then closed her eyes.

The spirit bent over her like a heron over shallow water. One long finger touched her forehead. For a heartbeat, Wren's face went completely blank—not frightened, just empty, a door briefly open onto an unfurnished room—and then she blinked and frowned.

"Huh," she said. "Did something happen while I wasn't paying attention?"

"That's the idea," Odile murmured.

"What idea?" Wren asked brightly, and Odile felt the cost of it settle into her own stomach like a swallowed stone. The girl had traded away a piece of herself without ever knowing what piece. That was the mercy of it. That was also the horror.

Brannoc went next. He approached the spirit the way he approached everything: directly, hands visible, jaw set. But when the spirit asked what he offered, he hesitated.

"There's a song," he said slowly. "My mother sang it. Stone-singer families pass songs down like heirlooms, except you can't lock a song in a box. You carry it." His throat moved. "It's the last thing I have of hers that isn't stone."

"Brannoc," Odile started.

"It should be me." He wasn't looking at any of them. "The cliff I brought down back at the gorge—that was loud. Loud things draw hunters, and hunters follow us because of what we're carrying toward that mountain. If someone has to pay more than boredom, it should be the man who made us louder." He straightened. "Take the lullaby. Just—take the melody, not the words. Leave me the words even if I can't hear the tune anymore."

**"A fair division,"** the spirit said, sounding genuinely pleased, which somehow made it worse. **"You will remember that she sang. You will not remember how it sounded."**

"I know," Brannoc said. "That's the point."

He closed his eyes. The spirit touched his brow, and Odile watched a big, immovable man sway like a sapling in wind—and when he opened his eyes again, they were wet, and confused, and searching for something he could no longer name.

"My mother," he said slowly, "sang to me. I know she did. There's a hole where—" He pressed a fist against his sternum. "There's a hole shaped like a song."

"Yes," Odile said softly. "Come sit down. Come walk beside me."

And he did, this enormous stone-singer who could not lie, letting her guide him like a child, and Odile thought: *this is what fear does when it's shared properly. It becomes something you can carry together.*

Which meant it was her turn.

* * *

Odile had spent her whole life managing what people saw.

As a child in her father's house she'd learned which smiles opened doors and which ones closed them. As a student of illusion she'd learned to build faces the way masons build walls—beautiful, load-bearing, concealing everything behind them. And in the three years since the purges began, she had learned to wear plainness like armor: no grace in her step, no fire in her eyes, nothing worth a second glance.

But the spirit did not look at her face. It looked at whatever lived underneath it, and Odile felt seen in a way she hadn't allowed since she was nine years old.

**"And what will you give?"** it asked. **"You carry much. Rich cargo."**

"Don't," Odile said. Her voice came out flat. "Don't browse."

**"Everything that enters pays toll."** The pond-eyes glittered. **"Choose, or I choose for you. And I am very old, noble-born, and my taste runs toward treasures."*

There it was. No way around, no clever clause, no illusion that could fool something that weighed memories like a moneylender weighing coins.

So Odile did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the spirit first rose out of the sap.

Her sister. Anouk. Dead eight years—fever, the winter Odile was studying at court, the winter she'd been too busy perfecting her craft to travel home until it was too late. Odile carried Anouk everywhere: the gap in her teeth when she laughed, the way she sang off-key on purpose, the freckles, the crooked part in her hair, the particular tilt of her chin when she was about to argue. A thousand memories, and every one of them wore that face.

If she gave the spirit something else—a year of her schooling, her memory of her first commission, the face of some courtier who'd wronged her—it would hurt, and it would heal, and it would leave the important things intact.

But the spirit had said *my taste runs toward treasures.* And if she offered something small, it might refuse. Might dig deeper. Might take the whole of Anouk instead of a corner.

Better to choose the wound than have it chosen for you.

"The face," Odile said. Her voice did not shake, and she hated how much effort that took. "My sister's face. Keep the rest—keep that I loved her, keep that she died, keep everything else. Only the face goes."

"Odile—" Zephyrine had gone very still. "What are you—"

"A debt is a debt." She stepped past him. "Watch the others, hedge-mage. This is mine to pay."

The spirit bent. The finger, cold as well-water, touched her brow.

And Anouk's face went out of the world.

Not the memory of her—Odile still knew her sister had laughed, had argued, had existed, had died. But the *face* itself, the visual heart of eight hundred remembered moments, was simply... gone. Replaced by a smooth blankness, like a portrait with the paint scraped away, like a mirror fogged past all warming. She reached for her sister's smile and found a shape where a smile should be. She reached for her sister's eyes and found a question mark.

She was crying before she understood she was crying. She wiped her cheeks briskly, angrily, because a Vosse did not weep in front of witnesses, except that apparently this Vosse did, in front of three fugitives and a nine-foot honey-glass godling, and there was nothing dignified to be done about it.

"I'm fine," she said, which was a lie, and for once she wished she had Brannoc's curse, because then at least the universe would have corrected her.

Zephyrine took her elbow without a word and steered her to a fallen log. Wren, sensing catastrophe in the mysterious way children sense it, sat down on her other side and leaned against her arm, and Ilex—unseen, unfelt by everyone but Wren—apparently extended his protection to include Odile too, because she felt a faint warmth along her shoulder like sun through a window.

"He took my sister's face," Odile said. It came out steadier than expected. Almost clinical. "I can remember that I loved her. I can't remember what loving her looked like."

Brannoc made a low sound, the one he made instead of condolences because condolences would require certainty, and certainty would require lying.

"I chose it," Odile added, mostly to herself. "Remember that. I chose it, and I would choose it again, because the alternative was worse. That's the whole trick of survival, isn't it. Convincing yourself the trade was fair."

* * *

Which left Zephyrine.

He approached the spirit last, hands loose at his sides, and Odile watched him the way she watched all of them now—with new attention, having learned exactly what these payments cost. He looked tired. He had looked tired since Sera died in his doorway, and the tiredness had layers, like sediment: grief under purpose under guilt under, at the very bottom, something that might have been relief that someone had finally told him the truth about his life.

**"And you,"** the spirit said. **"The debtor with the hollow god. What will you pay?"**

"Whatever's fair," Zephyrine said. "Name it."

**"Oh, I could name many things. Your memory of Sera's face as she died. Your memory of the war. Your memory of the commander's signature."** The spirit's voice curled through the clearing, testing each item like a jeweler lifting stones to the light. **"Any one of those would cover your passage richly."*

"No."

The word dropped into the clearing like a coin into a well.

Odile sat up straighter. "Zeph—"

"I said no." He wasn't shouting. That was the frightening part. He sounded like a man reading terms of contract he intended to breach. "You can have a boring afternoon. You can have a lullaby's melody. You can have a face, if that's what someone freely gives." He glanced back at Odile, and his expression was terrible and gentle at once. "But I'm not paying in memories. Not today. Everything I remember is load-bearing right now. Take one beam out and the whole house comes down on everyone I'm trying to help."

**"Then you do not pass."** The spirit drew itself up, and the trees groaned, and the whispers in the branches rose to a hiss like rain on hot iron. **"The wood keeps its toll. The wood always keeps its toll."**

"Then take something else."

"There is nothing else—"

"Take *me*," Zephyrine said. "Mark me. Brand me, claim me, write my name in your sap—I'm already owned, did you know that? There's a deed with my soul's price on it sitting under a mountain, signed by a man I trusted. So whatever claim you want on me, go ahead and stand in line. But you will not take pieces of my mind and leave less of me to bring to that court. They've had enough of my pieces already."

The silence afterward lasted so long that Odile counted her own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

Then the spirit laughed.

It was not a comforting sound. It was the sound ice makes when a lake decides, all at once, to break.

**"Oh,"** it breathed. **"Oh, the Pale Court will *hate* you."** It drifted closer, bending its glass face down level with Zephyrine's, and one long finger hovered before his forehead—then pressed, hard, cold light flaring where it touched.

Zephyrine gasped. Odile was on her feet without deciding to be. Across the clearing, Brannoc had lurched upright too, and Wren was clutching Ilex's sleeve with both hands.

When the spirit withdrew its finger, a mark remained on Zephyrine's brow: a small sigil like a branching tree, or a crack in porcelain, glimmering faintly silver-white, sunk beneath the skin.

**"The wood marks those who will not pay,"** the spirit said. **"You are claimed now, hedge-mage. Claimed by the Weeping Wood, and everything in it, root and whisper and weeping branch. Every tree between here and the mountain knows your name. Every dead thing that bargains in the dark will know your face. My kin will watch you. Some will help you."** The pond-eyes glittered. **"Most will simply watch. We are very old, and watching is our favorite thing."**

"And the other side of it?" Odile asked quietly. "There's always another side."

**"Of course."** The spirit was already sinking back into the pooled sap, dissolving drop by golden drop, its voice thinning as it went. **"What is marked may be collected. When the debt comes due—and it will come due, traveler, it always comes due—the wood will call, and you will answer."**

The last drops settled into the earth. The path unrolled itself before them, winding northward, deeper into the weeping dark.

For a moment nobody moved.

"Well," said Wren finally, in the tone of a child determined to be brave and failing only slightly, "at least we got a discount."

"Wren," Odile said.

"What! He didn't pay *anything*. That's basically free."

Zephyrine touched the mark on his brow. His fingers came away clean, but the sigil glowed faintly brighter where he touched it, like an ember responding to breath.

"It doesn't hurt," he reported. Then, quieter, almost to himself: "That worries me more than pain would."

He started walking. The crew fell in behind him—Wren skipping to catch up, Brannoc tramping along with his fist still pressed absently over the hole where his mother's song used to be, and Odile last, carrying her faceless sister through the whispering trees.

Behind them, so faintly that Odile might have imagined it, the whole forest sighed—one vast, woody exhale that sounded almost like satisfaction.

Almost like anticipation.

And far ahead, deep in the dark where the trees grew thickest and the weeping never stopped, something ancient turned its attention toward the small band of travelers and began, patiently, to count their steps.
