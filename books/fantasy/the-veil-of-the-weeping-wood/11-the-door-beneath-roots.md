## 11. The Door Beneath Roots

Ilex led them sideways.

That was the first thing Zephyrine understood about following a spirit through the Weeping Wood: there was no such thing as a straight line here, and Ilex seemed to regard straightness as a personal insult. The little spirit—no bigger than a cat, all silver-green flicker and opinions—would dart ahead, stop, double back, and then insist on a path that appeared to go exactly where they'd already been.

"She's doing it on purpose," Wren said, hopping over a root that looked suspiciously like a knuckle.

"Spirits don't do things on purpose," Brannoc rumbled. "They do things on *meaning*. It's worse."

"Meaning of what?" Odile asked, stepping delicately around a tree whose bark had split to weep a slow amber tear. She kept her sleeve pressed to her nose; the sap smelled like incense and old grief.

"Of making us walk in circles," Brannoc said. "Probably penance for something."

"I haven't done anything," Odile said.

"That's what worries me."

Zephyrine let them talk. It was better than the alternative, which was listening to the wood. The trees had begun whispering again as the light failed—not words, not quite, but the shape of words, the way you could hear the shape of someone crying through a wall. He kept his hand near his knife and his thoughts away from the single word his god had spoken to him at the wood's edge. *Hurry.* Three years of silence, and when the voice finally came, it was one syllable long and terrified.

He hurried. They all did, or tried to, while Ilex zigzagged.

It was full dark when the spirit finally stopped—stopped entirely, folding herself down onto a moss-covered stone like a dropped lantern—and went still in a way that made every hair on Zephyrine's arms stand up.

"What is it?" he asked.

Wren cocked her head. "She says we're here." A pause. "She also says Brannoc stepped on her cousin earlier and she wants an apology."

"I did no such thing," Brannoc said.

"She says roots count."

"Roots are not cousins."

"Roots are how trees hold hands," Wren said, with the absolute authority of a nine-year-old repeating what a spirit told her. "That makes them cousins. Or hands. She wasn't clear."

Brannoc sighed from somewhere deep in his chest, the sound of a man negotiating with geology itself, and addressed the dark ground: "My apologies to your cousin." The moss underfoot shimmered faintly, satisfied. Brannoc looked personally betrayed by the universe.

Zephyrine crouched beside the spirit-stone. "Here where? There's nothing here."

"There's always something here," Odile murmured. She had gone very quiet, her illusionist's eyes moving across the clearing the way she read faces—for the truth underneath. "Look at the roots. Really look."

He looked. At first the great roots were just roots, gnarled and interwoven, rising out of the earth like the ribs of something buried. But the longer he stared, the more the pattern refused randomness. The roots braided in spirals. The spirals nested inside one another, each ring within a ring within a ring, and each ring was made of smaller rings, and—

"Names," he breathed.

Every root was carved—or grown—with names. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, worn nearly smooth, overlapping, climbing over each other like people pressing against a locked door. He traced one with his fingertip: *Maren Ostwick.* Another: *Tobbe Hale.* Another: *Sera Vane.*

His knees nearly gave.

Sera's name was here. Sera, who had crawled half a kingdom to die on his floor with a black deed clutched in her fist. Her name was written into the roots of this cursed wood like a brick in a wall, and so were ten thousand others, and somewhere beneath all of it—beneath the door the roots had grown over, guarded, and mourned—was his own name too.

"It's a seal," Odile said softly. "A vault door. The Court doesn't hide their entrance; they grow a cemetery over it." Her voice had flattened into the careful tone she used when she didn't want anyone to hear what she felt. "I've seen doors like this before. In the old family estates. You don't break them. Breaking wakes everything."

"Then how do we get in?" Brannoc asked.

"You answer it," Odile said. "Doors like this are riddles wearing architecture."

As if summoned—as if it had been waiting, patient as roots, for someone to say the word—the clearing spoke.

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way the god's voice had come, the way the dead whispers came, but older and colder and infinitely more tired. It did not echo. Sound simply arrived inside their heads without troubling to pass through the air.

***WHO SEEKS THE PALE COURT?***

Everyone flinched except Wren, who had spent her whole life around things adults couldn't see and had apparently decided being startled was for people with better prospects.

"We do," Zephyrine said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "We seek an audience."

***THE COURT HEARS MANY SEEKERS. THE DOOR OPENS FOR NONE.***

"Then we'll be the first," Zephyrine said.

Silence. Then, horribly, the whispering in the trees stopped—all at once, the whole wood holding its breath—and the voice returned with something almost like interest.

***THEN ANSWER, SEEKER. THE DOOR IS SEALED WITH A RIDDLE OF NAMES. NAME THE NAME THAT OPENS.***

Zephyrine's stomach dropped. "What name?"

***YOU WILL KNOW IT OR YOU WILL NOT. THE COURT DOES NOT REPEAT ITSELF.***

"That's helpful," Odile muttered.

"The names in the roots?" Brannoc asked. "One of those?"

"No." Zephyrine was already shaking his head, walking the spiral, reading names by the pale light Ilex shed. Maren Ostwick. Tobbe Hale. Sera Vane. "These are the sealed ones. The sold. A lock isn't made of what it keeps—it's made of what it recognizes. The key would be a name the Court trusts. A name that belongs to the Court." He thought of the Steward he hadn't met yet, only sensed, a cold attention behind the mountain like an eye behind a keyhole. He thought of Sera's last breath. "Or a name given freely."

The last three words left his mouth before he understood them, and the moment they landed, the roots began to glow—a faint, patient gold, ring after ring, waiting.

"Oh," Odile said quietly. "Oh, that's cruel. That's very much the Court's style. The door only opens for a name that was never bought, never stolen, never signed away. A free name." She looked at him, and her face did something complicated. "Do any of us have one of those?"

Zephyrine thought about it, and the honest answer was a wound.

His own name had been collateral since before he knew what collateral meant—signed over by a commander he'd have followed into hell, and technically had. Odile had been born into a house that traded souls like wine futures; whatever name she'd been given came pre-mortgaged. Brannoc—Brannoc shook his head slowly, answering the question nobody asked aloud. Stone-singers named themselves in the old way, taking the mountain's name along with their own, and the mountains had debts older than kingdoms.

Which left—

"Wren," Zephyrine said.

The girl looked up from scratching Ilex behind whatever part of a spirit corresponds to ears. "Me?"

"Your name. Your real one. Where did it come from?"

Wren shrugged, suddenly small in a way she rarely permitted herself. "Dunno. Had it as long as I can remember. Before the streets, even. Somebody gave it to me." Her jaw set. "Somebody who left."

"A name given and abandoned," Odile said gently. "Given, maybe. But not chosen. Not kept." She glanced at Zephyrine. "And the Court will know the difference. That's rather their whole business."

The gold light in the roots pulsed once, impatient.

***SEEKER. THE DOOR GROWS WEARY OF WAITING.***

"So does everyone else in this forest," Brannoc muttered.

Zephyrine stood in the center of the spiral with his heart going like a trapped bird, and he thought about names. He thought about the war, and the ledger pages, and the neat signature at the bottom of his own soul-deed—*given freely*, the commander had written, and both words had been lies. He thought about a gray market stall and a skinny pickpocket who'd lifted his purse twice in one week and then, caught the third time, had looked up at him with defiance and hunger in equal measure and asked, *What'll you give me not to tell?*

He hadn't given her coin. He'd given her supper, and a job, and—because she'd refused to tell him her real name, because she'd said names were things people took from you and she wasn't having any more taken—he'd given her a new one instead. He'd stood there in his little shop above the charm-stall, three years ago now, feeling more alive than he had since the god went silent, and he'd said:

*You're quick as a wren and twice as likely to vanish. So that's what you'll be. Wren. And nobody gave it to you but me, which means nobody can take it back.*

The first name he had ever chosen freely. Not inherited, not assigned, not signed away by some man with a commander's pen. Made, in a shop that smelled of lavender and cheap luck-knots, out of nothing but affection and a bird.

"Wren," Zephyrine said aloud. "Try your name. The one I gave you."

She blinked. "Mine? It's not real, though. You made it up."

"I made it up *for* you," he said. "There's a difference. That's the entire difference. Everything the Court owns was taken. Yours was given. Given things are the only things that are truly ours." He smiled, and found the smile was steady. "Go on."

Wren looked at Ilex. Ilex flickered—Zephyrine was learning to read her moods, roughly: green meant yes, silver meant yes-but-let-me-dramatize-it. She was very silver.

Wren turned to face the roots. She planted her feet the way she did before lifting a purse, chin up, weight forward, a professional approaching a mark. And in a clear, carrying voice—the voice of a child who had learned early that being unheard was dangerous, and had decided to stop accepting the terms—

"My name," she said, "is Wren Joubert."

Joubert. She'd added that herself, months ago, without asking, and Zephyrine had pretended not to notice because correcting her would have required breathing normally first.

The roots blazed.

Gold light ran the spirals outward, ring after ring after ring, faster and faster until the whole clearing was a wheel of fire that cast no heat, and the voice returned—and this time it sounded, unmistakably, *pleased*.

***A GIVEN NAME. FREELY MADE, FREELY TAKEN UP, FREELY HELD. THE FIRST SUCH TO KNOCK IN THREE HUNDRED YEARS.***

The ground shuddered. The great roots groaned and shifted, drawing back from the center of the spiral like curtains, like hands opening, like the earth unclenching a fist it had held shut for centuries. Beneath them was revealed a stair of black stone descending into warm golden light, wide enough for six abreast, lined on either side by lanterns that burned without flame.

Nobody moved for a moment.

"That was showy," Brannoc said finally. "I distrust doors that enjoy themselves."

"You distrust everything," Odile said.

"I have excellent reasons for everything I distrust."

Wren was staring at her own hands. "Did I—" She turned to Zephyrine, and her face was doing something young and frightened and fierce all at once. "Did I just open it? With my name?"

"With your name," Zephyrine said.

"But it's *your* name really. You made it."

"And it's mine now. That's how giving works." He crouched to her level, which put his eyes level with Ilex's smug silver glow. "Listen to me, Wren. Whatever happens down there—and something will happen down there—they will try to make you feel small. It's what they do. They hold deeds to souls, and they'll want you to believe your name is a debt. It isn't. It's the one thing in this whole cursed forest that was never for sale." He tapped her sternum lightly. "You carry the only free name in ten thousand miles. Don't you dare spend it cheaply."

Wren swallowed hard and nodded. Then, because she was Wren: "Does this mean I'm in charge?"

"It means you're the key. Keys don't give orders."

"Keys open things. Opening things sounds like being in charge."

"Wren," Odile said, "we can discuss the chain of command after we survive."

Ilex darted to the head of the stairs and hovered there, pulsing green-silver-green, urgent now. Wren listened, and her face changed.

"She says the Court knows we're coming," she translated. "They've known since the chapel." A pause, and her voice dropped. "She says... they've set out chairs for us. Like a dinner."

"Chairs," Brannoc repeated flatly.

"Lanterns too. Lots of lanterns." Wren hugged her elbows. "She says they're very polite down there. She says that's the worst part."

Zephyrine looked down the stair. Golden light climbed toward him like something rising from a well, warm as a hearth and utterly without comfort. Somewhere at the bottom of it was a chained god who had spent three years spending himself down to embers to keep strangers alive. Somewhere at the bottom was a ledger with Zephyrine's name in it and a price attached. And somewhere at the bottom, patient and courteous and cold, something was arranging chairs.

Three years ago he'd have prayed before a descent like this. Tonight there was no prayer left in him that worked—only the memory of one word, *hurry*, ringing in his skull like a struck bell.

"All right," he said, and drew his knife, and did not sheathe it. "Stay close. Answer nothing politely unless I say. And Wren—"

"Yeah?"

"If anyone down there asks your name again—" he managed a grin, thin but real— "tell them it's not for sale."

Wren grinned back, sharp as a filleting knife. "Never has been."

They descended. Behind them, high overhead, the roots closed slowly over the stair like fingers folding shut—and far below, faint through the black stone, Zephyrine heard the sound he would remember for the rest of his life, however long that proved to be.

Someone down in the golden dark was laughing.

Politely.
