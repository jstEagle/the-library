## 13. The Prisoner God

They took his hands.

That was the first thing Zephyrine understood about the Pale Court: it was polite the way a snare is polite. Two stewards in gray silk—faces smooth and ageless as river stones, smiles carved but never quite reaching their eyes—had bowed him through a corridor of pale lanterns, and then, without any change of tone, had said, *The Lord of the Small Mercies receives no one armed, nor gifted,* and held out two velvet-lined trays like men collecting coats at a door.

He had given up his knife. He had not expected to give up everything else.

The moment they touched him, the magic went out of him like breath from a doused candle. Not painfully. That was somehow worse. It was simply *lifted*, gently, thoroughly, the way you'd lift coins from a sleeping man's pocket—and Zephyrine stood in that corridor feeling lighter and emptier than he had since Sera died on his floor with his name clutched in her fist.

"Is that necessary?" he asked. His voice sounded smaller than he wanted it to.

"It is courtesy," said one steward. "You would not bring mud into a chapel."

*No,* Zephyrine thought. *I'd bring a god.* But he kept that behind his teeth, where he kept most things.

Behind him, Odile's hand brushed his shoulder—she couldn't speak, none of them could; the Steward's terms had been explicit about the audience being solitary—but her fingers pressed once, twice, three times against his shoulder blade. Their old signal from the war. *We're here. Come back.*

Brannoc stood beside her like a cliff wearing a coat, jaw set, unable even to lie with his face. Wren peered out from behind Odile's skirts with Ilex's faint green shimmer coiled around her wrist like a secret bracelet. The Court pretended not to see the spirit. The Court saw everything.

Zephyrine looked at each of them once, memorized them—the way you memorize a room you're not sure you'll see again—and walked forward alone.

* * *

The corridor narrowed. The lanterns changed.

In the outer halls they had been white and cold, burning nothing he could name. But here, deeper beneath the mountain, the light turned amber, then gold, then a color he knew in his bones before he knew it with his eyes: the warm honey-glow of a hearth charm, of luck-knot thread, of every small kindness he had ever smuggled into the world disguised as cheap trickery.

His god's light. Stolen, or stored, or—something worse—*harvested*. It hung in lantern after lantern down the long dark hall, and Zephyrine walked through his own faith made into furniture.

"You recognize it," said the steward ahead of him. Not a question.

"I'd know it anywhere."

"Yes," the steward said, and there was something almost gentle in the flat voice. "The faithful always do. It's why we keep it close to Him. He finds it comforting." A pause. "Or perhaps we find it efficient. Grief is such a reliable guide."

Zephyrine's hands curled at his sides. Without magic, they were just hands—scarred, ink-stained, good for knotting cord and little else. Three years he had prayed into a silence and told himself stories: *the god sleeps, the god has gone elsewhere, the god has abandoned me because I sold charms for coin instead of healing for grace.* He had built a whole theology of abandonment, brick by lonely brick, and lived inside it because a prison felt safer than a wound.

Now he walked toward the truth of it, and every step said: *you were wrong, you were wrong, you were wrong in the worst possible way.*

The corridor ended at a door of black wood set with no handle. The steward stopped.

"One audience," the steward said. "As agreed. He may speak. He may listen. He may do nothing else—we have made very sure of that. And you—" the smooth face turned, and for a moment Zephyrine saw something flicker behind it, old and tired and horribly human, "—you should decide before you enter what kind of visitor you intend to be. Weeping visitors upset Him. He spends strength He does not have comforting them."

"What kind are you?" Zephyrine asked. "Visitor, I mean."

The steward smiled the carved smile again. "I am staff," it said, and opened the door.

* * *

The chamber beyond was small.

That was the second thing Zephyrine understood, and it broke him more cleanly than anything the Court had done yet. He had imagined—a cathedral of bone, an abyss, some vast terrible grandeur worthy of a prisoner god. Instead: a room hardly larger than his own shop above the gray market, low-ceilinged, quiet, its walls hung with those amber lanterns until the whole space glowed like the inside of a struck match.

And in the center, hanging from a chain that rose into shadow and descended into shadow and had no visible anchor anywhere at all—

A lantern of bone.

It was small. That was the thing. It was the size of a bread loaf, the size of a sleeping cat, the size of a child's paper lamp at a festival, and it swung very slightly in a draft that came from nowhere, turning, turning, and inside it something glowed the color of embers banked for the night—not blazing, not radiant, just *holding on*, the way a fire holds on when everyone has gone to bed and no one will feed it till morning.

"Hello," said a voice from inside the lantern.

It was not the voice Zephyrine remembered.

Three years ago—or rather, four, five, back in the war, back when prayers were answered and the world had a shape—he had known his god's voice as soldiers know a drumbeat: vast, warm, felt in the sternum more than heard. *The Small Mercies*, the hedge-mages called Him. God of splints and soup, of luck-knots and last chances, patron of everyone too small for the great gods to bother with. His voice had been a hearth in winter.

This voice was thin. Dry as a wick burned to nothing. It was the voice of someone who has been talking for years to keep another person awake, and knows the talking must not stop, and knows it cannot matter.

"Hello," Zephyrine managed. His throat had closed to the width of a prayer bead. "I—"

"You're limping," the god said. "Left side. You've been favoring it since the gorge. Brannoc's cliff-drop rattled you more than you admitted to Odile, and you didn't tell Brannoc at all, because the two of you are still circling each other like cats over the fact that he lied to the hunters about your number."

Zephyrine stared at the lantern. "You can—"

"I can see through my own light," the god said, with the ghost of dry humor. "There is quite a lot of my light down here. They use it for lamps. It's very flattering, having one's soul made into interior decorating." The lantern swayed, settled. "Sit down, Zeph. You look like you're going to fall over, and I can't catch you. That's been the whole trouble, these three years. I can't catch anyone."

There was a stool. Of course there was; the Court was courteous. Zephyrine sat, and found his legs were shaking, and did not care.

"They told me you'd abandoned us," he said. "I mean—I told *myself*. Nobody told me anything. The silence just... started. And I decided it was punishment, or indifference, or—" He laughed, and it came out broken. "Gods forgive me, I decided you'd *left*, and I got angry at you, I got so angry, I stopped praying properly, I just went through the motions, I—"

"I know."

"—and the whole time you were *here*—"

"I know," the god said again, softly. "Zeph. Look at me. Properly."

Zephyrine looked.

Inside the bone lattice—ribs of some creature vast and patient, latticed like a birdcage built by grief—there was not a blazing form, not a radiant figure. There was a shape like a man made of banked fire, curled small, knees drawn up, wings—or what had been wings, once—folded tight around itself because they no longer fit the space and never had. One luminous hand rested against the inside of the bone, the way a prisoner rests a palm against a wall.

Small. Exhausted. *Awake*.

"Why are you so small?" Zephyrine whispered, and hated himself for asking, because he already knew.

"Because I've been spending myself," the god said. "Every coin I had. When they took me—three years ago, the night the Crown signed its compact—they thought they'd captured a weapon. A god of war would have been useful to them. A god of harvests. Something they could leverage, bargain with, hold over the heads of the faithful." The ember-shape shifted, and the light in the lantern guttered, steadied. "But they caught the wrong god. Or the right one. I'm the god of small mercies, Zeph. My whole power is made of tiny things. Luck-knots. Kindnesses. The difference between a bad night and a fatal one. So when they chained me and demanded power, I gave them—" the dry voice almost smiled, "—small mercies. Trickles. Enough to seem compliant. And every drop I didn't give them, I spent somewhere else."

"Somewhere else," Zephyrine repeated.

"On *them*. On all of you. Do you think it was accident, the Purge missing so many? Do you think the witch-hunters' hounds lost the scent on their own? Every mage who slipped a net, every register that misprinted a name, every door that happened to be unlatched the night the hunters came—me. All me. Three years of pouring myself out through cracks in this cage, thinner and thinner, keeping every one of my faithful hidden, and the whole time—" the ember-hand pressed harder against the bone, "—the whole time, hearing you pray. Hearing you *stop* praying. Hearing you decide I'd left you."

"I didn't stop," Zephyrine said, and his voice cracked clean through. "Not really. I prayed angry. I prayed like a man shouting at a locked door. But I never—I couldn't—"

"I know," the god said, for the third time, and now the voice wavered like flame in wind. "Angry prayer counts. It always counted. Zeph, I need you to hear this, because you've clearly spent three years building yourself a cell out of guilt, and guilt makes such sturdy walls—you did *nothing* wrong. You survived. You sold your luck-knots and hid your light and stayed alive in a city that wanted you dead, and every single charm you made carried a spark of real blessing in it whether you meant it to or not, because *that is how faith works when the god is caged*—it leaks. You were my hands the whole time. You just didn't know it."

Zephyrine put his face in his hands.

Shame and love collided in his chest like two armies meeting in a narrow street, and neither won, and both left wreckage. Shame because he had cursed this—this exhausted, stubborn, self-spending ember of a god—for abandoning him while it poured out its last strength keeping strangers alive. Love because—because there was no reason for it, no logic, it was simply there, the way it had always been there, since the first splint he'd ever tied and the first whispered blessing over a fisherman's nets: *this small god, this hearth-fire god, mine.*

"I'm sorry," he said into his palms.

"For what?"

"For taking so long. For needing proof. For coming down here with a crew and a plan and a ledger full of arithmetic instead of just—" He gestured helplessly. "*Faith.*"

The lantern swung, gently. "Zeph. If you'd come down here alone, unarmed, unaided, on faith alone—do you know what they'd have done? Added your soul to the collection and hung you next to me as a matching pair. Faith is beautiful. Faith without a crew, a thief, a stone-singer, and a noblewoman with a guilty conscience gets you *dead*." The ember-eyes seemed to fix on him, warm and tired and amused. "You brought people. That's the most devout thing you've ever done."

From somewhere far above—muffled by stone, by bone, by the weight of a mountain—came a faint sound. A chime. Then another.

The god went still.

"That's the vault," the god said quietly. "Something's happening in the vault."

"Wren," Zephyrine breathed, standing so fast the stool tipped. "Wren's in the vault. She's getting my deed, she—"

"No." The god's voice sharpened, and for one instant—one flickering heartbeat—the banked fire flared toward something ancient and vast and *furious*, the god it had been before three years of rationing itself down to embers. "Listen to me. The alarms aren't the danger. The danger is what the Steward does next. When the Court is threatened, the ledger activates—it doesn't wait for moonrise if it thinks the collection is at risk. Ten thousand souls. Every registered mage above ground. They'll burn the whole page rather than lose one entry."

"Then we take the ledger too—"

"The ledger is *me*," the god said. "Did you think the deeds were filed in a drawer? Every soul-deed in that vault is anchored to the chain that holds this lantern. I *am* the lock, Zeph. That's the last cruelty of it. They bound the ledger of ten thousand stolen souls to the god of small mercies, because a lock that loves what it locks will never pick itself." The ember-hand spread against the bone lattice, and Zephyrine saw, finally, what he had refused to see since entering: the faint seams in the lantern's ribs. Hairline fractures. Growing.

"And if it breaks—" he started.

"If it breaks, everything it holds breaks with it." The god's voice gentled. "Unless the fire inside burns hot enough, fast enough, to burn the binding clean before the shell shatters. Which would require fuel. Which I no longer have."

Zephyrine looked at the small, exhausted, magnificent prisoner in its cage of bone, and understood—with terrible clarity, the clarity he'd been dodging since Sera died—what the god was not saying.

What the god would never ask for.

Above them, the chimes grew louder. Somewhere, Wren was running. Somewhere, the mountain was waking. And Zephyrine Joubert, hedge-mage, seller of harmless charms, keeper of a god too small for the great wars and too stubborn to die, reached slowly into his shirt and touched the place over his heart where a soul keeps its address.

"How much fuel," he said quietly, "would you need?"
