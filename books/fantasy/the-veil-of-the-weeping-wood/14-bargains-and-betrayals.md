## 14. Bargains and Betrayals

Brannoc had always believed that stone was the most honest thing in the world.

Stone did not flatter. Stone did not promise what it could not keep. When a mountain said *hold*, it held; when it said *fall*, it fell, and it told you beforehand in the small creaks of its bones. He had spent fifty years learning to listen to that honesty, and he had built his whole self upon it — which was why lying sat in his chest like a swallowed river-rock, heavy and cold and impossible to pass.

It was also why, standing in the antechamber of the Pale Court with lantern-light dripping from the ceiling like pale honey, he knew before anyone spoke that something was wrong.

The Steward had left them alone for perhaps an hour — an hour of marble benches and bone-white lamps and the distant sound of water moving through places water should not be. Zephyrine paced. Odile sat very still, which for Odile was worse than pacing. Wren had fallen asleep against Brannoc's side, her head tucked under his arm as though he were a wall she had decided to lean on, and Ilex — the little spirit, visible now as a flicker of green-gold light — hovered above her shoulder keeping a watch that no child should need.

And Brannoc listened to the floor.

That was the thing about being a stone-singer: you never entirely stopped listening. The Court's floor sang to him even in his silence, a low endless chord of deep-rooted granite wearing marble like a mask, and beneath that chord there was another sound. Footsteps. Many. Approaching through corridors of rock, and among them one gait he recognized — the quick scuff-and-stop, scuff-and-stop of a man who walked like he was always deciding whether to run.

"They're coming," Brannoc said quietly. "All of them. And someone is walking like Aldous Vane."

Zephyrine stopped pacing so fast Brannoc heard his boots squeak on the marble. "Vane is dead."

"Dead men don't walk," Brannoc rumbled. "I've checked. Thoroughly. It's a hobby."

* * *

They came through doors of black wood carved with names — thousands of them, Brannoc realized, every inch of the surface covered in script worn smooth by centuries of touching — and the Steward entered first, exactly as he had before: tall, courteous, wrapped in gray robes that moved like smoke deciding to be cloth. His face was long and pleasant and utterly forgettable, the kind of face you'd struggle to describe to a witch-hunter even with a knife at your ribs.

Behind him came six courtiers, pale as candle-drippings, and behind *them* —

Brannoc felt Wren wake against his side. Felt her go rigid.

Aldous Vane stood at the back of the procession with his hands folded, wearing clean clothes and a clean expression, looking for all the world like a man who had merely stepped out for an afternoon and gotten slightly delayed by immortality.

"You," Zephyrine said. The word came out flat and strange, like a coin dropped down a well.

"Captain." Vane inclined his head. His voice hadn't changed either — still that dry, reasonable tone he'd used to order them over walls and into graves during the war. "You look well. Considering."

"Considering *what*?" Odile rose from her bench with the unhurried grace she wore like armor. "Do get to the point. Courtesies curdle quickly in this house."

The Steward smiled. It was a good smile, Brannoc thought. That was the horror of it. It reached all the way to his eyes, and his eyes were kind, and none of it meant a single honest thing.

"The point," the Steward said, "is that your expedition has been a delight to watch. Truly. We do not often receive guests of such quality. A noble illusionist. A stone-singer of the old schools. A thief of considerable promise." His gaze settled on Wren, gentle as frost forming on a window. "And the hedge-mage himself, come all this way to bargain for a soul he does not yet understand he has already spent."

"We know what we're worth," Zephyrine said. "You made sure of that when you showed me my deed."

"Oh, you know your *price*. That is different from knowing your worth." The Steward clasped his hands. "But forgive me. I am being rude. Before we continue our negotiations, I feel honor-bound to settle a debt of gratitude. Your journey here would have taken months. It took weeks. You crossed the Ashfield without losing a soul to the Crown's hunters. You found the door beneath the roots — a door that has remained hidden for nine hundred years — within days of entering the Wood." He tilted his head, birdlike, pleased. "You were helped. And help, in this place, is never free."

The silence that followed had texture. Brannoc could feel it pressing on his ears like deep water.

"Someone sold us," Odile said. Not a question.

"Someone *guided* you," the Steward corrected, with the mild air of a man correcting a pronunciation. "In exchange for safe passage home, unmarked and unmolested, once your business here concluded. A very reasonable arrangement. I have never understood why mortals treat such transactions as shameful. Information is only breath, arranged cleverly."

"Who," Zephyrine said.

And then — this was the part Brannoc would turn over in his mind for years afterward, the part that ached like a hairline crack in load-bearing stone — everyone looked at everyone else.

It happened fast. Faster than thought. Zephyrine's eyes went to Odile, because Odile had already confessed to serving the Court once, because guilt leaves a scent. Odile's chin lifted, cool and wounded, but her hands — Brannoc watched her hands — went still in her lap, too still, the stillness of a woman making herself not fidget. Wren looked at the floor. Ilex dimmed. And Brannoc felt every eye slide toward him last, because Brannoc could not lie, and everyone knew it, and a creature that cannot lie is a creature whose innocence proves nothing about anyone else.

"There it is," the Steward said softly, savoring it. "There is the fracture. You see how easily it comes? Trust is a keystone, friends. Remove it, and the whole arch comes down into the dark. We find it saves such time."

"Nobody here betrayed us," Zephyrine said, but his voice had lost its footing, and Brannoc heard it, and hated that he heard it.

"Don't they teach you anything in hedge-craft?" Vane spoke up from the back, dry as dust. "Every company breaks eventually, Captain. You taught us that yourself. The Ninth Company broke at Carrow Ford, and you held us together anyway. But you are not holding anyone now. Look at them. They're already counting exits."

"I don't need to count exits," Brannoc said. "I make them."

It was not a particularly clever remark, but it landed oddly — a small absurd stone dropped into black water — and Wren made a sound that was half sob and half laugh, and something in the room loosened by a single degree.

* * *

Afterward, Brannoc would think that it was Wren who broke first because children always break first — not because they are weakest, but because they are the only ones honest enough to carry a secret at full weight without learning to pretend it weighs nothing.

She stood up from the bench. She was very small in the vast pale room, and she had pulled her sleeves down over her fists the way she did when she was frightened, and Ilex pressed close against her ear like a hand on a shoulder.

"It was me," she said.

"No," Zephyrine said immediately.

"I didn't know!" Her voice cracked and climbed. "Back in Vhalcrest, before the proclamation, before any of it — there was a man at the fish market. Nice coat. Asked me if I'd ever seen magic, real magic, and I — everybody asks that, it's a game, grown-ups play it with kids, they say *ooh, show me a spell* and then they laugh when you wiggle your fingers —" She was crying now, furious at herself for crying. "So I told him about the luck-knots. And then later, when we were leaving, he found me again and gave me a copper for bread and asked where we were going, and I said the Weeping Wood, and I thought it was a joke, I thought *he* was joking, because nobody goes to the Weeping Wood, that's the joke —"

"Wren." Brannoc crouched down to her height, slow, the way you'd approach a spooked animal near a cliff edge. "What did he look like?"

She sniffed. Wiped her nose on her sleeve. "Tall. Gray coat. Face like —" she frowned, groping for it — "like when you can't remember someone's name? Like a face that's mostly the idea of a face?"

Beside Brannoc, Odile exhaled through her teeth.

"A collector," she said. "Or worse. Wren — did he touch you? Hand, shoulder, anything?"

"He —" Wren's eyes went wide. "He fixed my collar. When the wind blew it up. He fixed my collar and said, *mind how you go, little miss.*" Her voice dropped to a whisper. "My collar's been itchy ever since."

Ilex flared suddenly bright, and for the first time since the chapel the spirit spoke aloud, its voice like wind through ivy:

"*Not a mark. An ear. He has been listening through her since the market.*"

The word fell into the group like a lit match into straw.

"Listening." Zephyrine had gone very still. "Through her. So everything — the route, the door, the riddle —"

"They already knew," Odile finished grimly. "Of course they already knew. Why do you think the door opened for us? Why do you think the Court received us with tea and courtesy?" She laughed, short and bitter. "We weren't guests. We were expected. I stole us into a vault that may well have been unlocked."

"That's not true," Brannoc said.

Everyone looked at him. He wished, briefly and sincerely, that he were capable of letting things lie — but stones do not lie, and neither, apparently, did he.

"The vault was locked," he said. "I listened to it. Stone doesn't lie to me either. There were wards thick enough to choke a river on those walls, and they *broke*, and breaking costs a court something, even this one. If they wanted us to stroll in, the wards would have been theater." He shook his head slowly. "The man listened through the child. That much is true. But listening isn't the same as steering. Wren gave them our road. She didn't give them our hearts. Those are different cargoes."

Wren was staring at him with wet eyes. "You mean I'm not —"

"You are," Brannoc said, "a child who traded a copper for bread and got robbed. It happens to the best of us. It happened to me once for an entire sheep."

Despite everything — despite the pale lanterns and the traitor's smile and the Steward watching them like a man watching his favorite play — Wren snorted.

"That is not the same thing at all," Odile said faintly.

"It was a very good sheep."

* * *

The Steward let them talk. That was the worst part, Brannoc thought. A cruel host interrupts. This one simply waited, hands folded, letting the wound breathe, because a wound tended carefully scars deeper.

"You see?" he said at last, gently. "No betrayal at all. Merely a child's chatter, carried on a borrowed collar. And yet look what one afternoon of honesty has done. The hedge-mage doubts his illusionist. The illusionist doubts her plan. The stone-singer defends a thief, and wonders privately whether defending her was itself a kind of misdirection." His kind eyes moved across them one by one. "I did not need to plant suspicion among you. I needed only to mention it. Suspicion grows wild in mortal soil; it needs no gardener."

"You've made your point," Zephyrine said.

"Have I?" The Steward spread his hands. "Then let me make my offer, and you may weigh it with whatever trust remains to you. The terms stand as set: a soul of equal worth, by moonrise tomorrow night, or the ledger activates, and every registered mage in Vhalcrest dies in their sleep. But tonight — tonight, I extend a smaller mercy." He gestured, and a courtier stepped forward bearing a tray, and on the tray lay five small tokens of white bone, each carved with a name.

"Quarters for the night. Separate quarters. Each of you may speak with us privately, if you wish — to ask questions, to raise concerns, to revise any arrangement you find... burdensome. No one will know who came, or what was said." The Steward smiled his kind, bottomless smile. "Even stone, after all, keeps some secrets."

Brannoc felt the trap in it the way he felt a false floor — not through thought but through the grain of the thing. Separate rooms. Private words. By morning, none of them would know which of the others had knocked, and the not-knowing would do the Steward's work for him even if no one came at all.

He looked at Zephyrine. Zephyrine looked back, and for a moment the hedge-mage's tired face did something rare: it softened.

"We'll keep our own counsel," Zephyrine said. "Together. One room."

"As you like." The Steward bowed. "Though I note, for the record, that the offer remains open until dawn." He turned to leave, then paused, and glanced back over his shoulder with something almost like regret. "One more thing, hedge-mage. Your god sent you a word in the Wood. *Hurry,* wasn't it?" His smile thinned by a degree. "It was not urging speed. It was warning you about the moon. Ours rises earlier than the one you know."

The doors closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting.

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Wren scrubbed her sleeve across her face and said, thickly, "I'm sorry. About the collar. About all of it."

Zephyrine knelt and took the itchy thing in his fingers, examined it, and — to Brannoc's astonishment — began unpicking its stitching with a charm-pin, murmuring the way he did when he worked. "You gave away a road, Wren. Roads go both directions. Which means somewhere between here and Vhalcrest, there's a man who thinks he knows exactly where we are." He drew out a thread fine as spider silk, black and faintly writhing, and dropped it on the marble floor, where it curled and smoked and died. "Which means we know something too: they're confident enough to stop listening closely."

Odile raised an eyebrow. "That's either hope or hubris."

"With this crew," Brannoc rumbled, gathering Wren up onto his shoulder where she belonged, "it has historically been both."

They followed the courtier toward their single shared room, and Brannoc listened as he walked — to the granite under the marble, to the water moving where it shouldn't, to the small exhausted heartbeat of a god chained somewhere far below — and underneath it all, so faint that only a singer of stone could have caught it, he heard something new.

Digging. Far below. Patient, rhythmic, deliberate.

Something in this mountain was already working on an exit of its own.

He decided, in the spirit of honesty, that the others should probably know before dawn.
