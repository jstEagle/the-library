## 15. The Price Named

The Steward let the silence do its work.

Zephyrine had learned, in the war, that cruelty came in two flavors: the loud kind, all screaming and fire, and the quiet kind that simply waited for you to fill the space with your own dread. The Steward was a craftsman of the second kind. He sat behind a desk of black glass—no, not a desk, Zephyrine realized with a lurch; it was a slab cut from the mountain's own bone, veined pale like an old man's knuckles—and he folded his long hands atop it and watched them the way a fisherman watches water where he knows the fish are rising.

They stood in a chamber lit by lanterns of green glass, each one humming faintly, each one holding—Zephyrine could feel it now, the way you can feel a stare on the back of your neck—a soul. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The whole cavern was a library written in captive light, and somewhere among those shelves hung his own name, his own price, filed and dusted and kept warm.

"Well," said Brannoc, into the silence. "That's a lot of lamps."

Zephyrine nearly laughed. Nearly. It was Brannoc's particular gift, being unable to lie: he could not pretend to composure he didn't have, so instead he simply said true things at moments when everyone else was too frightened to say anything at all. It shouldn't have been comforting. It was enormously comforting.

"Indeed," said the Steward. His voice was dry as paper turning. "Fourteen thousand, two hundred and six. Though I confess I don't light them myself. They light." He paused, savoring it. "Themselves."

Odile had gone very still beside Zephyrine—the particular stillness of a woman holding her illusions together by their corners. Wren had done what Wren always did under threat: she'd shrunk toward Zephyrine's shadow until only her eyes showed, bright and furious, over the collar of her stolen coat. And Ilex—Wren's spirit-friend, invisible to everyone but her, though Zephyrine had begun lately to catch a flicker of him at the edge of sight like heat-shimmer—was nowhere he could see at all. Hiding, or watching. With Ilex it was hard to tell the difference.

"You've read the deed," the Steward continued, drawing a slim folio from beneath the desk. Zephyrine's stomach turned over. He knew that black leather. Sera had carried its twin against her gut for three hundred miles while she bled out. "You know the terms of the original instrument. Collateral, held against the Crown's war debt. A debt which—" he consulted nothing, needing no consultation, having spent decades as the Court's clerk and keeper of accounts, "—remains unpaid."

"The war ended," Zephyrine said. "Nine years ago. The king signed the treaty himself. I was there. I watched him do it with a pen that cost more than my street."

"The war between kingdoms ended." The Steward smiled, and it was a courteous, terrible thing, like a door opening onto a drop. "No one ever settled the *other* account. Your commander understood this. Your commander, as it happens, still understands many things."

*The commander.* The name rose in Zephyrine like bile. Aldric Vane, who had signed the ledger with a flourish at the officers' mess while the mages drank his toast, who had sold ten thousand souls for supply lines and reinforcements and then shaken every one of them by the hand at the victory parade. Zephyrine had spent nine years believing Vane died at Redmarch. The Court's courtesy had already shown him otherwise—or hinted it—and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"What do you want?" Odile asked. Her voice was level. Only Zephyrine, who had traveled three weeks beside her, heard the hairline crack beneath it.

"Want is such a grasping word." The Steward rose. Behind him, the green lanterns dimmed and brightened in slow waves, breathing. "We prefer *terms*. Terms keep everything civil. So. Here are ours."

He opened the folio. On the page, ink moved—actual living ink, crawling like ants across the vellum—and Zephyrine read his own name written there in a hand he recognized even after nine years. Vane's hand. Beneath it, a figure. Not coin. Not land. A number that meant nothing and everything: the assessed worth of a hedge-mage's soul, third-rate, self-taught, damaged goods. He almost laughed at how small they'd priced him. He almost wept at how precisely they'd been right.

"By the next moonrise," said the Steward, "you will deliver to this Court a soul of equal worth, freely given, freely signed. In exchange, your own deed burns, and the account against your god's upkeep"—he gestured vaguely upward, toward some deeper vault where, Zephyrine knew, a small exhausted prisoner burned inside a lantern of bone—"is transferred and discharged. Everyone goes home happy. Well. Everyone who matters."

"And if we don't," Zephyrine said quietly.

The Steward spread his hands, apologetic, a host regretting the weather.

"Then the ledger activates. All of it. Every registered mage whose soul we hold—and we hold souls registered under the new Purge as well, did you know? Your wise king's criers have been so *helpful*. Every gift we hold will be called due. Called home. Withdrawn from the body entirely." He tilted his head. "You may know the sensation. Some of your comrades experienced it during the war. We call it *collection*. The mages who survived it were, statistically, none."

The floor did not move. The walls did not move. But Zephyrine felt the whole world tilt anyway, the way a ship tilts when the wave hasn't even arrived yet but you've seen it coming over the shoulder of the sea.

Every registered mage. The fishwife's daughter in Ashfield who'd hidden in a root cellar through the purge week. The old stone-singer upcountry who'd taught Brannoc his first true song. Children. There would be children on the register, because terrified parents had registered their children hoping obedience would be mercy, and mercy had never once in history come from a ledger.

"How many," Zephyrine said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "On the register now."

"Since the Proclamation? Two thousand and change." The Steward examined his nails. "Registration has been brisk. Fear is excellent for business. We should send the criers a fruit basket."

Brannoc made a sound low in his chest, half growl, half grief. Beside him, Odile's hand found his arm—not for comfort, Zephyrine saw, but to hold him still. Stone-singers cracked cliffs when they grieved. She'd learned that fast.

"You're asking me to choose," Zephyrine said slowly, "between one soul and two thousand."

"I'm asking nothing of the sort." The Steward's smile widened by a degree, and Zephyrine understood, with the sick clarity of a man stepping off a ledge, that he had walked exactly where he'd been meant to walk since the moment Sera bled out on his floor. "*You're* choosing that. I'm merely offering arithmetic. That's the beauty of terms, Master Joubert. They make the horror *yours*. We hold the paper. You hold the guilt. It's an elegant division of labor—we've used it for centuries. Kings adore us."

And there it was. The trap, laid out plain, wearing no disguise at all because it needed none. They didn't want a soul delivered to them. They wanted *him* to carry one down here, wanted him to stand in this green-lit cavern and put someone else's name on the line to save his own skin and everyone else's—wanted the man who'd come to shatter the ledger to pick up the pen instead. Because a soul freely given, freely signed, by *him*, would bind him tighter than any chain. You couldn't fight a court you owed. You couldn't burn a ledger your own signature warmed. They wouldn't just get a soul. They'd get his consent. His authorship.

They wanted him to become the thing he hated.

Not the Steward's coldness. Not Vane's treachery. The quieter thing underneath both: the arithmetic itself. The part of him that was already, horribly, *doing the sums*. Because that was the trap's true teeth—he wasn't immune. He was standing there right now, unbidden, weighing faces. Wren? No—never, and too young besides, worthless collateral by their cruel metrics, which meant the Court would refuse her and the refusal would eat him alive anyway. Odile, who'd already sold souls once to buy her family's safety and had confessed it with shaking hands and had sworn never again—asking her would kill something in her that had only just started healing. Brannoc, who could not lie and therefore could never consent to this falsely, who would say yes because saying yes would be true, because he'd give his soul for any of them without blinking—

*Stop.* Zephyrine shut his eyes. *You're doing it. You're shopping.*

When he opened them, the Steward was watching him with the patience of a man who had watched ten thousand people do exactly these sums and had never once seen the answer come out clean.

"You see?" the Steward said gently. "It's not so monstrous. People do it all the time. Mothers for children. Kings for crowns. Your commander did it for a supply route and slept wonderfully afterward—I kept his ledgers, I would know. The soul given isn't harmed, Master Joubert. It's merely... relocated. Kept. Cared for." He gestured at the breathing lanterns. "Does that lamp look like it suffers?"

Zephyrine looked at the nearest lantern. Green light pulsed within it, slow and rhythmic, and for one vertiginous moment he thought of the fishwife from his first week in Vhalcrest, the luck-knot, the way she'd pressed his hand and said *you've got kind eyes, charm-man*, and he thought: *that light looks like her laugh. That light could be anyone. That light is everyone, eventually, if I sign.*

"It looks like it's waiting," Zephyrine said.

Something flickered across the Steward's face—there and gone, so fast Zephyrine might have imagined it. Annoyance? Recognition? The expression of a man hearing an echo of a sentence someone else had once said to him, long ago, before the desk and the dry voice?

"Moonrise tomorrow," the Steward said, closing the folio. "The entrance you came by will remain open. Refreshments are provided in the gallery. The Court is nothing if not hospitable to guests considering a transaction." He paused, and added, almost kindly, which was somehow the worst thing yet: "You needn't decide alone. Deliberate with your friends. They seem fond of you. That, too, is useful."

They were escorted out by lantern-bearers who did not speak and did not blink, down corridors of veined bone, into a high-vaulted gallery where a table had been set with food Zephyrine had no intention of touching. The moment the last bearer withdrew, Wren exploded.

"They can't *make* you—"

"No," Zephyrine agreed.

"But if you don't, everyone—"

"Yes."

"So you'll find someone, right? Someone *bad*? There must be someone bad enough, some murderer, some—some slaver, someone whose soul is *worth* taking—" Her voice climbed, desperate, doing her own arithmetic now, and Zephyrine's heart broke a little to watch it, because she was twelve and the Court had already taught her the lesson he'd sworn she'd never learn: that lives could be weighed.

"Wren." He crouched to her height. "There's no such thing as a soul bad enough that giving it to *them* makes it good. That's the trick. That's the whole trick. The moment I pick anyone—saint or monster—I'm theirs. I'm holding the pen."

"Then don't hold the pen!" She grabbed his sleeve with both hands. "Burn it! Steal it! You stole the deed from the vault, Odile said—you can steal the *ledger*—"

"We had alarms waking and half the Court hunting us for one page, little thief." Odile sank onto the gallery bench, elegant even in exhaustion, rubbing her eyes. "The ledger is fourteen thousand pages and sits in whatever room the Steward keeps his heart in. Which, knowing clerks, is probably also locked and warded and full of things worse than us."

"Then we run," Brannoc said flatly. "Take the deed we have. Run. Let the ledger sleep."

"It won't sleep." Zephyrine stood, walking to the gallery's edge. Below, the cavern city of the Pale Court sprawled in tiers of green and white light—elegant, orderly, obscene. "Sera ran. Look what running bought her. And even if we vanished into the deep roads, the register stands. Two thousand names. They'd collect them whether we watched or not. Running just means dying *after* everyone else."

Silence. Somewhere below, faintly, the lanterns breathed.

"I gave my sister's face to a tree," Odile said suddenly, to no one, to everyone. "In the Wood. To buy passage. And I told myself it was a small price, a fair price, a *necessary* price, and I have told myself versions of that sentence my entire life, and every version of it ends with someone else paying while I walk away intact." She looked up at Zephyrine, and her eyes were wet and blazing. "Don't you dare pay their price. Don't you *dare* become tidy about it. I know what tidy costs. I'm still finding pieces of myself in the installments."

"I don't intend to," Zephyrine said.

"That's what worries me. You've got that look." She jabbed a finger at him. "The look men get right before they do something noble and stupid and fatal and leave the rest of us to bury them."

"He's had that look since I met him," Brannoc offered. "It's why I follow him. Also he owes me money."

"*I owe you*—you lost that dice game on purpose and we both know—"

"Gentleman doesn't accuse a lady's friend of charity," Brannoc said serenely.

Despite everything—a knife at the world's throat, a moonrise ticking down—Zephyrine felt something loosen in his chest. Warmth, absurd and stubborn, in the belly of the thing that held ten thousand stolen lights. *This*, he thought. *This is what they can't price. This is the asset off the books.* The Court held deeds to souls, but deeds were paper, and paper could not record the way Brannoc lied badly about dice to make a frightened child laugh, or the way Odile's fury was just love wearing armor, or the way Wren's hand hadn't left his sleeve.

"Wren," he said. "Your friend. Ilex. He went quiet in there. Does he do that often?"

She frowned. "Never. He talks *constant*. Like a magpie with opinions." Her frown deepened. "He's talking now. But not to me. Or—to me, but it's strange. He says—" she cocked her head, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear, and her face went through surprise, then wonder, then a fear so pure it silenced the gallery. "He says the lanterns aren't all full."

"What?"

"He says some of the lights in the vault are *hollow*. Shells. He says the Court has been spending souls faster than it collects them—that the ledger isn't a treasury, it's a *cover story*—" She blinked, translating, her lips moving. "He says the Steward is afraid. He says the Steward needs your soul not because of the war debt. He needs it because the Court is *hungry and running out*, and a freely-given soul tastes different, lasts longer, and yours—" she faltered, looking at Zephyrine with something like awe, "—yours has been *fed* for three years. Something's been pouring strength into it from the other side of the chain. Yours is the richest deed in the vault. They don't want collateral. They want the *harvest*."

The gallery went very still.

Three years. Three years of silence, and Zephyrine had believed himself abandoned, unworthy, forgotten by his god—and all that time, every scrap of power his god could wring from its prison had been flowing not to him but *into* him, banked, accumulating, ripening. He wasn't a debtor. He was a larder.

"They sent the hounds," Zephyrine whispered. "The proclamations. The purge. They've been herding the gifted into the register for years, fattening the ledger—but the ledger's empty shells, mostly. And then a mage walks in carrying a deed that glows like a feast-day candle, and suddenly the Steward remembers his manners." He laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged. "It was never about the war debt. The debt was real once, maybe. Now it's a menu."

"Then the choice is false," Odile said sharply. "Give them a soul and they take two thousand anyway, once they're strong enough. Refuse, and they collect early. Either way we lose unless—" She stopped. Zephyrine watched the idea arrive in her the way weather arrives over mountains: visible from a long way off, unstoppable. "Unless the shells can't collect. Unless collection takes strength they don't have. Unless the ledger is a bluff with teeth that fell out years ago."

"Or it isn't," Brannoc said. "Can't lie, so I'll say it plain: we'd be betting two thousand lives on a spirit-child's gossip and a feeling."

"Ilex isn't gossip," Wren said, chin up, fists balled. "Ilex is *never wrong about food*."

For a heartbeat—despite everything—Zephyrine almost smiled. Then the warmth drained out of the moment, because he understood what they were actually discussing, and what it would require, and where it would lead. If the Court was starving, then the ledger was weak. And if the ledger was weak, it didn't need to be bargained with, or fled from, or paid.

It needed to be *burned*. Here. Now. Before moonrise. From the inside.

Which meant going back down into the vault. Which meant facing a Steward who had centuries of practice and nothing left to lose. Which meant—

A bell rang somewhere deep in the mountain. Once. Soft. Almost polite.

All four of them froze. Then, at the far end of the gallery, the doors opened, and the Steward entered alone—no bearers, no escort, his dry paper voice gone strangely careful—and Zephyrine saw, for the first time, that his hands were trembling.

"Master Joubert," the Steward said. "Forgive the interruption. There has been a development in the vault." He swallowed, and the courteous mask slipped just far enough for Zephyrine to see the starving thing beneath it, ancient and afraid. "One of your companions' deeds is missing from its shelf. And the lantern it belongs to—"

Behind him, faintly, from the depths of the vault, came a sound none of them expected: a single note, high and clear, like a struck bell. Like a word.

Like a god, clearing its throat.
