## 16. What Odile Buried

The cave they found was less a shelter than a wound in the mountainside, a hollow where some ancient river had given up and left the rock to remember the shape of water. Odile sat apart from the others, near the entrance, where the gray light of late afternoon could reach her. She had chosen that seat deliberately. It was the seat of someone who might need to leave quickly.

Below her, in the blue dusk of the cave's interior, Wren slept curled against Brannoc's enormous side like a kitten against a boulder. The stone-singer had not moved in two hours. He claimed he didn't need to—that stillness was a kind of prayer for men like him—but Odile suspected he simply knew that moving would wake the girl, and the girl needed the sleep more than any of them needed his comfort.

Zephyrine stood at the far wall, turning Sera's stolen pages over in his hands for perhaps the hundredth time. Even now, even here, he read them as though one more pass might change what they said. As though somewhere between *sold* and *collateral*, between the commander's signature and the Pale Court's seal, there might be a kinder word hiding.

There wasn't. Odile knew. She had helped write those words once.

"Tell me about the vault."

She had known this was coming since the chapel. Since Ilex had spoken its warning and Zephyrine had asked the only question that mattered: *how do we get into a place that has never been broken into?*

Zephyrine hadn't looked at her when he asked it. That was the courtesy of the man—asking with his eyes fixed on the fire, giving her room to pretend she hadn't heard. But Brannoc had turned his head, slow as a millstone, and Wren had gone very still against his ribs, and the pretense had died quietly in the space of a breath.

So Odile folded her hands in her lap, and did the thing she had been practicing in silence for three days.

"I can tell you," she said, "but you should know first that everything I'm about to say makes me either your worst enemy or your most useful friend, and I genuinely don't know which."

Zephyrine looked up. In the dimness his face was all shadows and patience. "Odile."

"No." She raised a hand before he could soften it further. "Let me finish, or I'll lose my nerve, and I've spent three days losing my nerve, and I'd rather spend the next ten minutes doing something braver than that."

The fire popped. Somewhere deeper in the mountain, water dripped with the regularity of a clock.

"My name is not Odile Vasseur," she said. "It is Odile Arnaux-Mireval. My family held lands east of the Sereine for six generations. We were minor nobility—the kind that matters enormously within forty miles and not at all beyond it—and we were drowning. Taxes, harvests, my father's talent for backing losing horses and losing men. By the time I was seventeen we owed more than the land was worth, and the creditors had begun to talk about the Mireval name the way one talks about a dead language."

She heard Brannoc shift slightly. Not judgment. Just attention. The man listened the way stone listens: completely, without hurry.

"A man came to our house in winter. Fine coat, wrong accent. He said he represented certain interests beneath the mountain, and that our debts could be resolved. All of them. Forever." Her mouth curved, and it was not a smile. "I was clever, you see. That was always my tragedy. Clever girls from ruined houses are exactly the sort of people such men come looking for, because we have imagination and desperation in equal measure and no one has ever taught us the difference between them."

"What did they want?" Wren's voice, small and sleep-roughened. Odile hadn't realized the girl was awake. She decided not to acknowledge it; acknowledging it would stop the confession, and the confession had to happen now, while her nerve held.

"They wanted an apprentice. Someone presentable, educated, capable of illusion—" she gestured vaguely at her own face, at the careful human features she wore like a well-tailored glove, "—because the Court does its business in person, mostly, and people will sign anything a charming young woman puts in front of them if she smiles correctly."

The silence stretched. Zephyrine had stopped moving entirely.

"I was their clerk's assistant," Odile said. "I carried the ledgers. I witnessed the signatures. I sat across from frightened people in fine rooms and explained, gently, kindly, with tea and sympathy, that selling their soul-deed was really a formality—a legal fiction, a guarantee, nothing to fear. And when they hesitated, I told them the Court never collected. That the deeds just... slept. Paper promises, nothing more." She swallowed. "I told that lie so many times I dreamed it. Four years. I was very good at my work."

"You were nineteen," Wren whispered.

"I was nineteen," Odile agreed. "And twenty, and twenty-one, and twenty-two. Age doesn't launder anything, little thief. It just gives you more time to get used to the weight."

She made herself look at Zephyrine. His face had gone the color of old ash, but he didn't look away from her, and she loved him a little for that—not romantically, or not only, but in the way one loves a person who refuses to make your confession easier by flinching.

"You understand what I'm telling you," she said. "Sera's pages. The names. Some of those signatures—I watched them signed. I poured the tea while they were signed. There may be people in that ledger whose faces I remember better than my sister's, because I spent four years memorizing reassurances instead of anything true."

"And your family," Zephyrine said quietly. "Their debts."

"Paid in full. My father lived another eleven years without ever asking where the money came from. My mother asked once. Once was enough." Odile looked down at her hands. They were steady. She had trained them to be steady; it was part of the smile. "I left when I understood what the deeds were actually for. When I found the collection schedules. A clerk's assistant finds things out—it's remarkable what people write down in front of you when they think you're furniture." She laughed, short and ugly. "They nearly caught me. Would have, if I hadn't been, as I mentioned, very good at illusions. I've been running and hiding and passing as human ever since. Odile Vasseur, hedge-witch, harmless. Another invented woman. I'm rather a collection of them at this point."

Brannoc spoke then, his voice low and grinding, like a landslide deciding to be gentle. "Why tell us now?"

"Because tomorrow we'll be inside the mountain, and lies are heavy luggage on a climb like that." She met his eyes. "Because Wren already confessed her small betrayal and you all gave her grace, and I watched that grace and wanted it and hated wanting it. And because—" her voice caught, and she let it, for once not smoothing the catch away, "—because I know the back way into the vault, and if I don't tell you that, we're all going to die politely in a receiving hall while the Steward explains, with tea and sympathy, why resistance is a formality."

Zephyrine crossed to her. He didn't sit beside her—he crouched, lowering himself below her eye level, which was a trick he had, making himself smaller so that other people could feel taller. Three years of watching him sell luck-knots to the grieving, and she had never seen him deploy that particular kindness until now, for her.

"The back way," he said. "Tell me."

"There's a service stair. Clerks' passage, cut into the eastern root-system of the Wood itself. The Court moves its ledgers through it—new deeds in, activated ones out. It's warded, but the wards answer to clerk's tokens." She reached into the lining of her sleeve and drew out a thin disc of pale bone, worn smooth, stamped with a lantern sigil. She had carried it for six years the way other people carry guilt: everywhere, always, touching it to be sure it was still there. "Mine still works. Or it doesn't, and the moment I touch it to the door every warden in the mountain knows exactly where the traitor is standing. Fifty-fifty. I've had worse odds."

"That's not fifty-fifty," Wren said, sitting up fully now, eyes enormous. "That's a coin with 'disaster' written on both sides."

"It's a coin," Odile corrected, "with *chance* written on both sides, and chance is more than the front door offers."

Brannoc rumbled forward out of the dark, and for one terrible heartbeat Odile thought he meant to strike her. Instead he knelt—an avalanche folding itself down to her level—and pressed one broad palm flat against the cave floor.

"I cannot lie," he said. "You know this. So hear what I cannot help saying." The stone under his hand hummed, faintly, a note like a struck bell buried deep. "The mountain remembers you, Odile Arnaux-Mireval. Stone keeps what passes over it. Six years ago something wearing your face walked these passages, and the rock grieved after it. Grief leaves a shape. I can follow the shape of your grief home." He looked up at her. "You are not furniture. You never were. Furniture does not grieve its way out of hell carrying a map."

Odile discovered, somewhat to her horror, that she was crying. She let it happen the way one lets a fever break—with relief and resentment in equal measure.

"Well," she managed. "That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me, and it was delivered by a man who talks to gravel."

"He talks to gravel *beautifully*," Wren offered.

"Gravel has excellent taste," Zephyrine said, and there it was—the ghost of his market-day warmth, surfacing through the exhaustion like a fish rising in gray water.

But then the warmth faded, because it had to, because he was who he was. He took Sera's pages and laid them flat between them, and his voice went quiet and precise, the voice he must once have used for war.

"If the token works—if we reach the vault—we take one deed. Mine. Nothing else. No grand theft, no freeing of the ledger, no heroics. The moment anything else is touched, the alarms wake, and the Steward collects us all along with the paper." He looked around the circle of them: the child, the stone-man, the woman made of borrowed faces. "We go in quiet, we go out quiet, and everyone comes home. Swear it. All of you. If it comes to choosing between my deed and any one of you, the deed burns."

"You'd give up your god," Wren said. "After everything. After hearing him again in the Wood."

"I waited three years to learn he hadn't abandoned me," Zephyrine said. "I can wait three more to find a way to him that doesn't cost me any of you." A pause, and then, gentler, with the terrible honesty that was his real magic: "Besides. I've been godless a long time, Odile. I know how it's done. It's done by getting up in the morning."

Odile wiped her face with her sleeve, and felt something strange settle over her—not peace, nothing so kind, but purpose with the rot scraped off it. Guilt, transmuted. Weight, redistributed.

"Then here is how we go," she said, and unrolled in her mind the map she had carried for six years behind her eyes, every corridor, every warding-seam, every place where the bone lanterns burned too bright for shadow. "Clerks' stair descends past the third gallery. The vault sits below the receiving hall—deliberately, so that petitioners stand directly above their own souls while they negotiate. There are two sentries at the token-door, but they rotate at the change of watch, and the change of watch is marked by a bell that sounds like..." she closed her eyes, hearing it across six years, "...like a wet finger on crystal. You'll know it. Everyone hates it."

"Wonderful," said Wren. "A haunted doorbell."

"A bureaucratic one," Odile said. "Far worse." She stood, brushing the cave dust from her skirts, and for the first time since the Ashfield road she stood straight—all the way straight, no borrowed slouch, no hedge-witch's apology in her spine. "My token opens the door. Brannoc reads the stone for the empty stretches. Wren lifts the deed—small hands, no gift-signature, nothing for their wards to taste. Zephyrine stands in the middle and looks harmless, which I am given to understand is his native country. And I—" she breathed out, long and slow, "—I pour the tea."

Zephyrine rose with her. For a moment he simply looked at her, and whatever moved behind his eyes, it wasn't suspicion anymore.

"Why now?" he asked. "Six years running. Why finally turn around?"

She considered lying. It would have been effortless, elegant, the work of half a breath.

"Because I sold souls to keep people safe who never asked what it cost," she said. "And now I know people who would refuse to save themselves at anyone else's expense, and I find I'd rather be ruined in their company than comfortable in mine." She smiled, and for once it was not a trained thing. "Also, your god owes me a favor for this, and I intend to collect."

Above them, far up through the roots and rock, the mountain gave a sound like a great door remembering its hinges. The bell. The wet finger on crystal, ringing somewhere in the dark, marking the change of watch in a court that had already been warned they were coming.

Wren looked at the ceiling. "It's early," she whispered. "That's not the evening bell. That bell isn't supposed to ring again till midnight."

Odile's blood went cold, and then colder, because she counted backward from the sound and understood what it meant: the watch had changed ahead of schedule. Someone had ordered it changed.

The Court wasn't waiting for them to arrive.

It was adjusting the schedule to receive them.

* * *

They broke camp in silence and moved.
