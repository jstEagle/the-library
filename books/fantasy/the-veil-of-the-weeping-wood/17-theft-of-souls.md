## 17. Theft of Souls

Wren had stolen a lot of things in her nine years of life, and she had a private ranking of them, the way other children might rank sweets or saints.

A loaf of bread was easy but boring — two points out of ten. A silver hairpin from Lady Marenne's window box had been satisfying because the lady was mean to her cat. The best theft she'd ever pulled off, before all of this, had been an entire wheel of cheese rolled out of Vhalcrest's market square under her coat while the cheesemonger argued with a man about the proper way to worship a god nobody could hear anymore.

But tonight, crouched behind a pillar of black glass in the vault of the Pale Court, Wren decided that stealing souls was going to be the new number one on her list. Assuming she survived long enough to update it.

*Don't think like that,* Ilex said inside her head, which was where the spirit-friend usually lived, warm as a pocketful of summer.

*I'm thinking like a professional,* Wren thought back. *Professionals count the odds.*

*Professionals who count the odds stay home.*

*Professionals who stay home don't eat.* She smoothed her hands down the front of her borrowed court-clothes — pale gray silk that Odile had charmed to look like livery — and peered around the pillar at the vault floor below.

It was not what she'd expected. She had imagined something grand: iron doors taller than houses, rivers of gold, maybe some skulls for atmosphere. Instead the vault was quiet and almost humble, a long hall of smooth stone lit by lanterns that gave off no heat, their flames the color of old milk. And everywhere, everywhere, were shelves.

Shelves upon shelves upon shelves, rising into darkness so far above that Wren couldn't see where they ended. And on every shelf, folded neat as linen, lay deeds. Black paper, silver ink, each one humming very softly, like a bee trapped under a cup.

Ten thousand bees under ten thousand cups, all humming the names of people who didn't know they'd been sold.

*There,* Ilex said.

Wren followed the spirit's attention across the hall, past two guards made of lantern-light wearing the shapes of men, to a shelf near the far wall, about eye-height for an adult, which meant a climb for her. One deed sat apart from its neighbors in a little case of glass, the way important things always did. Even from here she could feel it pulling, a faint tug behind her ribs, like a fishhook set gently in her chest.

*That's him?* she asked.

*That's him.*

Zephyrine's soul, folded up on a shelf. She thought about how his hands shook when he sold luck-knots, how he always gave the grieving fishwife a discount and pretended it was a mistake, how he hadn't slept properly in three years and told everyone it was his back. All of that, folded into black paper on a shelf in the dark, filed away by a man with too many teeth and a smile like a locked door.

Wren cracked her knuckles.

*Right,* she said. *Time to do some shopping.*

* *

The plan had taken four days to build and eleven minutes to argue about, and it worked like this:

Odile went in first, because Odile could walk through the Court's corridors looking like anyone at all, and tonight she looked like a minor steward named Pellam whose real self was currently asleep in a supply closet with a headache he wouldn't remember having. Her illusions weren't just pictures; they were complete, down to the smell of ink on his fingers and the slight wheeze in his breath. Watching her work was like watching someone fold light.

Brannoc came second, which was the hard part, because Brannoc was built like a door and couldn't lie even when lying would save everyone's lives. So he didn't lie. He simply stood in the vault's antechamber, put his palm flat against the stone, and sang — quietly, under his breath, in a language that wasn't a language so much as a permission slip the mountain had been waiting three thousand years to receive.

The stone listened. The stone always listened to Brannoc. That was the tragedy of Brannoc, Wren thought: everything trusted him except people.

And Wren came third, small and quick and forgettable, the way she had been her whole life before anyone bothered to give her a name worth remembering.

*Remember,* Odile had said, gripping Wren's shoulders in the smuggler's tunnel beneath the shrine, her beautiful face tight with fear she wasn't hiding well enough. *You take the deed, you bring it back here, you do not stop to look at anything else. Do you hear me? Nothing else.*

*I heard you the first four times,* Wren had said.

*I'll say it a fifth time anyway, because you're nine and you once stole a cheese.*

*And I got away with the cheese.*

Odile had laughed despite herself, and Zephyrine had ruffled Wren's hair, and Brannoc had said gravely, *If you are caught, sing nothing and say my name and I will bring the ceiling down between you and them, though it may cost me more than I wish to pay.* Which was Brannoc for *I love you, be careful*, said in the only grammar he knew.

Now, moving along the shadowed edge of the vault, Wren repeated the plan to herself like a prayer to a god she actually believed in: *Deed, tunnel, done. Deed, tunnel, done.*

She got within thirty feet of the glass case before the first thing went wrong, because plans are just stories we tell ourselves before reality starts talking.

One of the lantern-guards turned its head.

Not toward her. Toward the wall. Toward Brannoc's stone-song, which was doing its slow patient work on the vault's back face, thinning the rock into a doorway only they could use. To Wren's eyes the guard was just a man-shaped flicker of pale fire, but she saw it tilt its head like a dog hearing a whistle no one else could hear.

*Uh oh,* said Ilex.

*Is it bad?*

*It's listening.*

*Listening is fine! Listening isn't telling!*

The guard drifted toward the wall. Wren's stomach performed a maneuver she'd last felt during the cheese incident.

Then Odile moved.

She crossed the open floor without hurrying, exactly as fast as a bored steward walks when he has somewhere duller to be, and stepped directly into the guard's path with an armful of scrolls she absolutely hadn't been holding a moment ago. "Forgive me," she said in Pellam's wheezy voice, "but these requisitions won't file themselves, and the Steward wants the eastern stacks reconciled before moonrise—"

The guard paused. Considered. Lantern-light has no face, but Wren would have sworn it sighed.

"—and if they're not reconciled," Odile continued, with the weary authority of a man who had suffered greatly, "it will be *your* section that comes under review."

The guard turned around and floated back to its post with the particular slowness of a creature that hated its job. Wren nearly applauded.

*Go,* Odile breathed, barely moving her lips. *Now. While it sulks.*

Wren went.

* *

Here was the thing nobody understood about thieving, the thing the ballads always got wrong: it wasn't about being fast or clever or brave. It was about being *interested*. A thief had to love things — really love them, the weight of them, the way they wanted to belong to somebody — because you couldn't take what you didn't understand.

Wren understood this deed. She stood in front of the glass case and looked at Zephyrine's name written in silver — *Zephyrine Joubert, hedge-born, price assessed* — and underneath, in smaller letters, a figure in some currency of the deep places that made her eyes sting to read, and beneath *that*, one line that stopped her cold:

*Bearing the mark of the Silent God. Collateral. Held against the debt of the Northern Campaign.*

Collateral. Like a coat left at a door. Like her mother leaving her at the foundling steps with a note that said *back soon*, which was also collateral, in its way, and was never redeemed either.

*I'm getting it back,* she told the deed silently. *Both of us. You'll see.*

Ilex poured himself — itself — herself? Ilex had never settled the matter and Wren had stopped asking — down through the seam of the glass case like water finding a crack. The lock inside was a knot of pale law-magic, all rules and bindings, the kind of thing that would freeze solid anyone who touched it without leave.

But Ilex wasn't touching anything. Ilex was *suggesting*. Spirits were born of the space between things, and between the lock and its duty there was, technically, a gap — a tiny, shimmering question mark where the law forgot to look.

The case clicked open.

The hum of the deed rose to meet her, and up close it wasn't a bee under a cup anymore. It was a voice. Faint, frayed, familiar. Wren had spent three weeks listening to Zephyrine mutter in his sleep on cold nights, and she knew the shape of his voice the way she knew her own heartbeat.

She lifted the black paper free of its case.

For one shining moment — one golden, perfect moment — Wren Joubert, pickpocket, orphan, alias-giver, held a man's entire soul in both hands and it weighed almost nothing at all.

*Done,* she whispered, tucking it inside her shirt against her heart, where it beat faintly against hers like a second, sadder pulse. *Deed, tunnel, d—*

The lanterns changed color.

All of them, at once, from milk-white to a red like the inside of a wound, and the hum of ten thousand deeds rose together into a chord that was not a sound so much as a *shout*, and every guard in the vault turned its burning head toward the small girl standing at an open case with guilt written all over her in a script any child could read.

*RUN,* said Ilex, in a voice that filled her whole skull.

* *

Wren ran.

She was very good at running. She'd run from bakers and beadles and one memorable occasion, a goose with opinions. But the vault floor had begun to move — the stone rippling like the skin of a struck drum — and the guards weren't chasing her so much as *converging*, drifting inward from every direction with the calm of things that had all the time in the world and knew she didn't.

A hand of lantern-light closed on her shoulder.

It didn't burn. That was the horrible part. It was warm and gentle, almost kind, and it turned her around slowly, and the red light bent itself into a face that wasn't a face, and from somewhere impossibly far away came the Steward's voice, amplified through a thousand mouths of flame at once:

"*Little thief.*"

Wren did the bravest thing she had ever done in her life, which was also the simplest.

She said, "Brannoc."

* *

The mountain answered.

It came up through the vault floor like a whale surfacing — not an explosion, nothing so crude, but a great slow *bloom* of stone, rising between Wren and the guards, folding itself around her like a mother's arms. The lantern-hand tore away. The red shout stuttered. And through the new-made wall of rock, worn smooth and hollow as a seashell, came Brannoc's voice, singing, straining, each note costing him something visible even from here.

*The tunnel,* Ilex gasped. *Wren, the tunnel, MOVE—*

She moved. Through the hollow stone, down and down, hands and knees now, the deed pressed against her hammering heart, behind her the vault shrieking in a chorus of ten thousand voices waking all at once — confused, frightened, furious voices, souls realizing after years in the dark that the door had been opened even a crack.

Ahead: gray light, cold air, Odile's face appearing at the tunnel mouth, beautiful and terrified, hauling Wren out by the collar like a kitten.

"Got it?" Odile demanded.

Wren pulled the black paper out and held it up, and even crumpled and singed at one corner, it caught the torchlight and gleamed, and for half a second everyone grinned —

— and then Brannoc staggered out of the tunnel behind her, gray-faced, blood running freely from his nose, and beyond him the mountain began to ring like a struck bell, and the bell was not stone.

It was alarm.

Every lantern in the Court, all at once. Every corridor flooding with light. And rising over it all, courteous and cold and terribly awake, the Steward's voice rolling through the deep places like weather:

"*Someone has taken what is Mine. Bring them to me. Bring me their names.*"

Odile looked at Wren. Wren looked at Odile. Somewhere overhead, boots were starting to move — many boots, in step, coming down.

"Well," said Wren, clutching a man's soul to her chest, "the good news is, I definitely got away with the cheese."
