## 18. The Steward's True Face

The alarms of the Pale Court did not sound like bells. They sounded like weeping—ten thousand thin voices rising through the caverns at once, a chorus with no words in it, only grief given pitch.

Zephyrine ran.

Behind him came the others: Brannoc's boots hammering the pale stone, Wren tucked under one arm like a stolen loaf, Odile's skirts hissing as she took corners at speeds no lady should survive. In Zephyrine's fist, crumpled against his heart, was the deed that bore his own name—the paper he had spent three years not knowing existed and two days learning to hate.

"Left," Odile gasped. "The Steward said left, but the Steward *lies*, so—"

"Right," Brannoc rumbled.

"He lied about lying?"

"He lies about everything. It's very restful, once you get used to it."

They went right, and the corridor obligingly bent toward them instead of away, which Zephyrine had learned to distrust on principle. The walls here were bone-white stone shot through with veins of lantern-glow, and every vein pulsed now, faster, like the Court itself had a heartbeat and they'd just stuck a knife in it.

Wren squirmed free of Brannoc's arm and ran beside him, small feet slapping stone. "I got it! I *got* it! Did you see? I pickpocketed a *vault*!"

"I saw," Zephyrine said, and despite everything—despite the weeping alarms and the veins of light quickening around them—he grinned at her. "You're going to be insufferable about this for years."

"Years," she agreed solemnly. "Decades."

Then the corridor ended.

Not in a wall. In a room.

It was the kind of room that made you understand, viscerally, that architecture could be a form of condescension. A long table of black glass. Chairs that had never been sat in because sitting was for people who tired. And at the far end, beneath a lantern that burned without flame or heat, stood the Steward of the Pale Court, hands folded, expression composed into something almost apologetic.

"You're in my way," Zephyrine said. "We'll be leaving now."

"I imagine you'd like to." The Steward gestured, gently, and doors of stone sealed themselves across every exit with a sound like teeth closing. "But you've made rather a mess of my ledger hall, and I find I'm not ready for you to go."

Zephyrine's eyes adjusted to the light, and adjusted again, and this time they refused to stop adjusting—as though the truth kept arriving in installments.

He knew that face.

Not the face itself. The face was wrong—younger than it should be, smoother, preserved like fruit in honey. But beneath the preservation were the bones of a man he had followed into three campaigns. The set of the jaw when he was about to give an order he didn't believe in. The way his left eyebrow sat a half-finger higher than the right. The scar—no, not a scar anymore, smoothed away, but Zephyrine remembered where it had been, just under the ear, from the skirmish at Corven's Ford, because he'd been the one to stitch it shut by lantern-light while the man joked about how he'd tell the story differently later.

"*Aldous*," Zephyrine said.

Commander Aldous Verreth. Dead eleven years. Buried with honors, or so they'd been told; the war had moved on too fast for most of them to attend. Sera had wept. Zephyrine had carried the man's spare cloak for a season afterward because throwing it away felt like a second funeral.

"Ah." Something flickered across the smooth face—pleasure, or its imitation. "You remember. I wondered if you would. People rarely look closely at clerks."

"You're dead."

"I'm *employed*," the Steward corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference is tenure."

Odile had gone very still. Zephyrine watched her put it together a heartbeat behind him—the commander whose signature filled Sera's stolen pages, the name signed ten thousand times, selling souls by the column. Her hand crept toward her knife.

"You sold us," Zephyrine said. His voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were using it. "At Vell. At the Fens. You sat at our fires. You learned our mothers' names. And then you—" He stopped, because the ledger pages rose in his memory unbidden: names, prices, signatures. His own name among them, priced like a bolt of cloth.

"Yes," Aldous said simply. "All of it. Every word you're searching for—I did it, I knew what I was doing, and I would do it again, and none of those sentences will land the way you want them to, because you're still imagining I had a choice. I didn't. That's the first thing the Court teaches you." He spread his hands. "They needed a clerk who knew soldiers. Who knew how mages love, and fear, and sign things when their friends are dying. I knew all of it. I was *good* at all of it. And in exchange—" he touched his own unlined cheek with something that was almost wistfulness— "they let me keep working. Forever. Do you know how rare it is, Zephyrine, to be certain of your usefulness?"

"I know how rare it is to be certain of *anything*," Zephyrine said, "and it isn't worth this."

"No," Aldous agreed pleasantly. "But then, you've always been sentimental. It's why you never rose above sergeant."

Brannoc made a sound low in his chest, like a landslide deciding whether to happen. Wren, bless her sharp little heart, had gone quiet in the way she went quiet before she cut a purse—and Zephyrine shifted slightly, casually, to put himself between her attention and the Steward's.

"The deed," Aldous said. "You have it. Of course you have it; the vault doesn't lose things, it loses *track* of them, briefly. Give it back, and we can discuss terms."

"We discussed terms already."

"And you found them distasteful. Someone must take your place—yes, I heard your little speech. Very moving. The Court was unmoved, being a court." He stepped forward, and the lantern-light moved with him, and up close the preservation was worse, not better: skin like good parchment, eyes like ink that had dried in the well. "So let me offer you different terms. Terms between *men*."

"There are no men here," Zephyrine said quietly. "You made sure of that."

"Terms between old comrades, then." Aldous smiled, and it was the smile from the fires at Vell, the one that had made young mages feel seen. Zephyrine understood now that it had been a tool, honed over centuries, and hated it more for having once warmed him. "Walk out of here. Take the crew. Go back to Vhalcrest. The purge will end—the Court will call off the Crown; it's a simple matter, they hold the Regent's grandmother's deed and always have. Magic will be legal again by spring. Your god will stay chained, yes, but gods are chained everywhere, Zeph; yours is merely honest about it. And you"—his voice dropped into warmth, real warmth, the terrible counterfeit of it— "you open your little charm stall in the daylight. No more gray market. No more hiding the flicker. You could be *known*. Respected. You could teach."

The words went into Zephyrine like needles finding old wounds, and that was how he knew they'd been made for him specifically, measured against him, by a man—or the remnant of a man—who had once known him well enough to do exactly this.

His old life. Handed back to him, polished, with interest.

All it cost was four people standing behind him.

"That's the offer," Aldous finished softly. "Your life. The one they took. All you have to do is hand me the girl, the liar, the rock, and the illusionist, and sign one line confirming their deeds as forfeit." He produced, from nowhere, a pen.

Silence. The weeping-alarms had faded to a distant keening, as if even the Court wanted to hear the answer.

And Zephyrine found—to his own surprise—that he wasn't angry. Not yet. What he felt first was something colder and more clarifying: recognition. He looked at Aldous Verreth, at the honey-preserved face of the man who had shaped him, taught him to march and to ward and to bury friends efficiently, and he thought:

*This is what I would have become if I'd loved safety more than people.*

The rage arrived after that, and it arrived clean.

"You stitched my shoulder at Corven's Ford," Zephyrine said. "Do you remember what you said while I worked? You said, *'Careful with the needle, Sergeant, I need this arm for signing things.'* We laughed. Gods, we laughed for an hour." He shook his head slowly. "You were joking about the ledger even then. You'd already signed some of us by then, hadn't you? The ones who died at the Fens. You were pricing us before we were cold."

Aldous said nothing, and the nothing was confirmation enough.

"Sera Carried came to my door dying," Zephyrine went on, and now his voice did shake, and he let it, because some things deserved to shake. "She carried your handwriting in her fist for eleven years, looking for me, because you signed *my* name and she couldn't live with it. She died on my floor telling me my god hadn't abandoned me. My god spent three years starving itself to keep people like Wren hidden from men like you, and you want me to trade four souls for a *stall license*?"

"I want you to be practical."

"I want," Zephyrine said, "you to have stayed dead."

He heard Brannoc move behind him—a slow grinding shift, stone gathering intent. He heard Odile murmur something in the old tongue, weaving the first thread of an illusion around the room's edges, buying seconds. He heard Wren breathe the way she breathed before running.

But Aldous raised one finger, and the whole room raised itself with it—lantern-flames leaning, shadows stretching, the sealed doors growing teeth of stone.

"You misunderstand the situation," the Steward said, and the comrade fell away from his voice entirely, leaving only the clerk. "This isn't a negotiation. It's a *deadline*. Moonrise, Sergeant. Bring the Court a soul of equal worth, or the ledger activates, and every registered mage in Vhalcrest dies in their sleep—including, should you refuse, the four standing behind you, whose deeds I will collect personally." He set the pen down on the black glass table with a small, precise click. "Oh—and the child's deed. She has one now. Did you know? Signing her alias into my door bound her into the registry. She's been collateral since the threshold." His dried-ink eyes flicked past Zephyrine, and softened with something horribly genuine. "You gave her your name, and names are currency here. Sweet, really."

Wren made a small sound. Zephyrine's stomach dropped through the floor and kept falling.

"So," Aldous said, folding his hands again, patient as paper. "Moonrise. One soul, equal worth. Or ten thousand burn and the girl burns first. Choose, Zephyrine. You were always better at choosing for other people than for yourself."

The stone teeth on the doors parted—not an escape, an eviction. Behind the Steward, deeper in the mountain, something began to glow: a slow red bloom spreading through the veins of the wall, climbing toward the surface, climbing toward moonrise.

Zephyrine picked up the pen.

Aldous's face lifted with triumph.

Zephyrine snapped it in half and dropped both pieces on the black glass.

"I don't choose for other people anymore," he said. "That was your lesson. Thank you for teaching it."

For one instant—one naked instant—the preservation cracked, and Zephyrine saw underneath it: a tired man, eleven years dead, who had once laughed at his own joke by lantern-light and meant it. Then the clerk closed over the man's face like water.

"Moonrise," Aldous repeated. "I'll prepare the furnace."

They fled down the corridor with the red glow climbing behind them, and Wren grabbed Zephyrine's hand and held on hard, and nobody said anything until Brannoc, wheezing, managed:

"Well. At least now we know who to haunt."

But Zephyrine was watching the red veins climb the walls, counting, doing the arithmetic he'd sworn he wouldn't do—because Aldous had made one mistake, the same mistake he'd always made. He'd assumed Zephyrine would choose someone else's soul.

He hadn't considered that Zephyrine might choose his own.

"Odile," Zephyrine said quietly, as the red light bloomed toward moonrise above. "That back way into the vault. How fast can you show us?"
