## 19. Moonrise Over the Mountain

The Steward's smile was the worst thing in the cavern, and Zephyrine had spent the last hour looking at a god chained inside a lantern of bone.

"Think of it as bookkeeping," the Steward said, spreading his pale hands over the ledger that sprawled across the obsidian table like a sleeping animal. Ten thousand names, ten thousand prices, ten thousand souls bound in ink that gleamed wet even now, three centuries after it dried. "No one suffers. One soul steps into a very comfortable arrangement—eternal tenure, excellent benefits, a certain dignity—and ten thousand mages walk free. You'd be a hero, Zephyrine Joubert. They write songs about men who make hard choices."

"You mean songs about men who make *other people* their choices."

"I mean," the Steward said patiently, the way one speaks to a slow child, "that the moon is rising, and when it clears the mountain, the ledger activates. Every registered mage dies at dawn. Unless—" he tapped the empty line at the bottom of the page, beneath ZEPHYRINE JOUBERT, SOUL-PRICE: PAID IN FULL, PENDING SUBSTITUTION, "—someone signs here."

Behind Zephyrine, Brannoc's stone-song hummed low and dangerous in his chest, a vibration Zephyrine felt through the floor. Wren had gone very still, which for Wren was the equivalent of screaming. Odile's illusions were peeling away from her by degrees—he could see her true face emerging, tired and furious and magnificent.

"No," Zephyrine said.

The Steward sighed. "You said that last time. And the time before. I admire stubbornness; I married into this job for it." He straightened. "Then we proceed with the alternative. Dawn comes. The registered die. You will have watched your own signature condemn them, because you were too precious to—"

"I said no to *that* too." Zephyrine reached into his coat.

Here was the thing the Steward had never understood about him, in all the years between the war and now: the commander who had signed away his soldiers' souls had looked at Zephyrine and seen a useful instrument. A hedge-mage with a knack for small magics and no ambition worth mentioning. A man who kept his head down, who sold luck-knots in gray markets, who prayed to a silent god and told himself the silence was mercy.

The Steward saw the same man now. That was his mistake.

Zephyrine pulled out the deed.

It was heavier than paper had any right to be—the weight of a name, of everything he'd ever been, folded down to something that fit in a coat pocket. Wren had stolen it from the vault not two hours past, through illusion and stone-song and the finest pickpocketing ever done on a cosmic scale, and they had run through corridors that screamed alarms in voices like breaking glass, and they had been cornered here, in the heart of the Court, with nowhere left to go.

Everyone thought he'd stolen it back so that he could refuse the substitution. So that he could hold his own soul hostage against them.

He'd let them think it. He was, after all, a man who sold cheap charms hiding real magic underneath. Misdirection was the family trade.

"Zeph," Odile said quietly. She'd read his face. Odile always read his face; it was the curse of traveling with an illusionist. "Zeph, whatever you're thinking—"

"The lantern," he said to her, not taking his eyes off the Steward. "How far?"

"Forty feet. Maybe fifty." Her voice cracked on the arithmetic. "And the whole Court between us and it."

"Not the whole Court." Brannoc's voice rumbled like a quarry deciding to become a landslide. "Just everyone who wants to keep their ledgers."

* * *

Later—much later, when he had time to think in anything but fragments—Zephyrine would try to reconstruct those forty feet and find they wouldn't hold still in memory. Memory was a coward's country; it blurred the things that hurt.

What he remembered was this:

Brannoc singing. Not the gentle stone-songs he hummed while cooking, but the deep ones, the ones he'd been born knowing and sworn never to use on living rock, and the floor of the Pale Court answering him like an old friend—*yes, brother, yes, I remember you too*—and rising. Not much. Just enough. Just a wave of stone rolling outward from Brannoc's planted feet, unhorsing guards made of candle-smoke and courtesy, cracking the obsidian table down its spine so that the ledger shuddered and its ink ran like veins waking up.

What he remembered was Wren laughing—not her street-laugh, sharp and armored, but a real one, wild and young—as she went up Ilex's spiraling arm of green light like a squirrel up an oak, over the heads of the Court's attendants, small fingers already busy lifting keys, seals, anything shiny and important, because a distraction was just a theft you hadn't finished yet.

What he remembered was Odile stepping forward and dropping the last of her masks entirely—every glamour she'd worn since Vhalcrest falling away like ash—and the Court flinching, because Odile unmasked was not a woman, she was a verdict. She filled the cavern with faces. Hundreds of them, thousands: every mage whose name sat in that ledger, alive and defiant, projected vast upon the walls until the Pale Court stood accused in a courtroom of its own victims.

And what he remembered, most clearly, was running. Forty feet of polished bone-floor, the deed clutched in his fist, the Steward shouting behind him in a voice that had stopped being courteous forever—

"*Joubert! You signed nothing! You have no right!*"

"I know," Zephyrine panted, and grinned, actually grinned, because three years of silence and a lifetime of careful invisibility had finally boiled down to this one glorious insolent truth: "That's rather the point!"

The lantern of bone stood on its plinth where he had found his god an hour before, and his god hung inside it—small, exhausted, dim as a coal wrapped in grey wool—and Zephyrine skidded to a halt before it and pressed both hands to the bone.

"Hello again," he whispered. "Sorry I'm late. Traffic was murder. Several murders, actually, mostly metaphorical—"

*Hurry,* said his god, in a voice like the first warm day after winter, and it was still only one word, but oh, how much the word carried now.

"I'm hurrying, I'm hurrying." His hands shook. He made them stop. Some things you do trembling; some things you owe yourself steady. He unfolded the deed, and there was his name, and beneath it the price paid by Commander Aldric Vane—by the Steward, by the man he had once trusted enough to follow into hell—and beneath *that*, in fresh ink that seemed to writhe, the waiting line for substitution.

"You know what this is?" he asked his god conversationally, the way he'd talk to a friend across a market stall. "This is the receipt for my soul. Apparently I've been collateral for fifteen years. Which explains my credit rating." His throat closed. He pushed through it. "I could give this to someone else. The Court would take anyone. Wren offered—I told her absolutely not, don't make that face, all of you, I can see you planning—"

"Zeph." Odile had reached him. Her hand found his shoulder. Her eyes were wet and she was smiling anyway, the way you do at funerals of things that deserved to die. "Whatever you're about to do—do it fast. Brannoc can't hold the floor much longer."

"I know." He turned back to the lantern. To the small dim prisoner inside. Three years of silence, and the silence had never been abandonment. It had been a god spending itself drop by drop to keep its faithful hidden from hunters, hidden from registers, hidden from the very Court that held its cage. Every charm that had worked better than it should. Every near-miss that hadn't happened. Every night the witch-hounds had passed his door. *You were paying for me,* he thought. *All this time, you were paying for me.*

"So here's my counteroffer," Zephyrine said loudly, to the Steward, to the Court, to the ledger and everyone in it. "You wanted a soul of equal worth. Fine. You get mine. All of it. But not *to you*—"

He fed the deed into the seam of the lantern.

"—*to him.*"

* * *

Bone is porous, it turns out, to honesty.

The deed slid between the ribs of the lantern like a letter into a slot, and for one heartbeat nothing happened, and Zephyrine had the mad clarity of a man on a ledge—*oh no, it's a slot for complaints*—and then his god drew a breath.

Not a small breath. A breath the size of a season changing.

Light came off the little chained figure like heat off a forge, and the chains—chains the Court had forged from silence itself, from three years of unanswered prayers—glowed white, then gold, then snapped, each link ringing a note Zephyrine felt in his teeth, in his scars, in the place behind his sternum where his magic lived.

"*Oh,*" said his god, aloud, in the hearing of everyone, in a voice that made the stalactites weep, "*oh, I have been wanting to stretch.*"

"What do I do?" Zephyrine shouted over the rising light. "What do you need?"

"Everything," his god said happily. "Give me everything. It's all yours, isn't it? Every scrap you ever fed me without knowing. Prayers, charms, luck-knots, the fishwife's grief, the child in Ashfield, all of it—you banked it with me, Zephyrine. I kept your deposits safe." The small figure grew. Filled the lantern. Outgrew it. "Now I'm making a withdrawal."

"Wait," Zephyrine said. "Withdrawal—from *me*—"

But his god was already moving, and gods do not wait, and Zephyrine understood, in the last instant before the light took him, exactly what he had done. The deed was the anchor of his soul-price. The god was fire, and the ledger was fuel, and the only thing connecting fire to fuel was the name written in the deed.

His name.

His magic poured out of him like water from a broken jug. He felt it go—not painfully, worse than painfully, *lovingly*, his god cradling each spark as it left him the way you carry embers through wind—and the light roared down into the ledger, into ten thousand lines of binding ink, and the ink began to burn.

The Steward screamed. Not in rage. In *accounting*. He threw himself across the great book as if he could smother a sunrise with his body, and where the flames touched him the centuries fell away, and underneath the immortal clerk was only Aldric Vane, old and mortal and horrified, watching his masterpiece burn.

Names lifted off the pages like sparks. Zephyrine saw them go—saw souls rise through the cavern roof as if it were made of morning fog, ten thousand bright threads streaming upward toward the moonlit world. Somewhere above, he knew, mages were waking in cells and chains and gray-market stalls, feeling something return that they'd forgotten they'd lost.

The mountain began to come apart around them.

"That's our cue," Brannoc bellowed, catching Wren under one arm like a sack of turnips. "ZEPH! MOVE!"

Zephyrine didn't move. He couldn't. He was on his knees and empty, hollowed to the walls, and his god knelt before him—free, blazing, vast and still somehow small, still somehow *his*—and pressed something like a hand to something like his cheek.

"You gave everything," his god said. "Do you know how rare that is? Everyone offers *something*. You offered the self."

"It wasn't—" Zephyrine's voice came out in pieces. "It was never really mine while they held the deed. Now it's nobody's. That's the same as everyone's, if you squint."

His god laughed—a sound like the first bell of morning—and leaned close, and said, in the voice that had once been his only company through three silent years:

"I was never in the gift, Zephyrine. The magic was a loan. What you are—that was yours the whole time, deed or no deed. Guard it well." And then, softer, with terrible tenderness: "I must go. There are ten thousand people out there learning to pray again, and I intend to answer *all* of them. Even the annoying ones."

"Especially the annoying ones," Zephyrine managed.

"*Especially* the annoying ones." The light gathered itself. Paused. "Odile. The girl. The stone-singer. Tell them—" the god's radiance flickered, almost sheepish— "tell them thank you. Nobody has ever broken into a prison *for* me before. Usually it's the other way around."

And then the god was gone, up through the crumbling dark, a comet with somewhere wonderful to be, and Zephyrine collapsed into Odile's arms as the ceiling of the Pale Court came down in glittering ruin behind them.

* * *

They ran.

Brannoc sang the stairs wide and then sang them closed behind them. Odile threw illusions ahead of falling stone, painting doors where there were none and, twice, doors where there *needed* to be. Wren rode Ilex through gaps no grown body could have crossed, shrieking directions with the flawless memory of a child who had mapped the vault for fun. And Zephyrine—drained, mortal, lighter than he had ever been—ran on his own legs, because his legs, at least, had never belonged to anyone else.

Behind them the mountain groaned like an old ship. The Court of Pale Lanterns, which had fed on bound souls for three hundred years, was finding out what happened when the food walked free: it starved, all at once, magnificently. Chandeliers of bone guttered out. The Steward's voice chased them down one last corridor, thin and human and ruined: "*Without the ledger there is no Court! Without the Court there is no ME!*"—which, Zephyrine thought, gasping, was the first honest thing the man had said in centuries.

They burst out through the Door Beneath Roots into moonlight and snow, and turned, all five of them plus one spirit, to watch the mountain die.

It did not explode. It *exhaled*. Ten thousand years of hoarded dark leaving through every crack at once, and where the dark had been, the Weeping Wood began—to Zephyrine's astonishment—to bloom. Sap ran clear instead of bloody. The whispering dead fell quiet, or perhaps went home. And overhead, streaming up past the moon like a river deciding to flow backward, the freed souls rose and scattered and rose again, ten thousand lights finding their own ways to their own skies.

Wren grabbed Zephyrine's sleeve. "Did we win? Is this winning? It feels chaotic. Is chaos winning?"

"This," Zephyrine said, sliding slowly, gently, inevitably down onto the cold ground with Odile and Brannoc catching him between them, "is what winning looks like when it's been asleep for three hundred years and just woke up hungry. Give it a minute."

"Zeph." Odile crouched over him, her face silver in the moonrise, and her expression did something complicated. "Your magic. I felt it go. When the god took the deed—it took *you* with it. Can you feel—"

He checked. Carefully, honestly, the way he'd once counted coins in the gray market.

Nothing. No flicker. No warmth behind the sternum. The well was not dry; the well was gone, filled in with clean ordinary earth. For the first time since childhood, Zephyrine Joubert was only a man.

He started to laugh. It alarmed everyone, which made it funnier.

"Zeph?" Wren's voice wobbled. "Why are you doing a laugh? This doesn't seem like a laugh situation."

"They'll register it tomorrow," he wheezed, tears freezing in his lashes. "The purge. The Crown. They'll test every soul in Vhalcrest, and I'll come up *empty*. Do you understand? I'm the safest man in the kingdom. Fifteen years hiding what I was, and now—" he spread his empty hands to the moon— "now I finally match my paperwork."

Brannoc snorted. Odile laughed until she cried. Wren looked between them, decided on faith, and joined in, and Ilex spun slow green circles overhead like a maypole with opinions.

Above them, faint as a memory of warmth, something vast and newly free paused in its flight—one thread of light among ten thousand, lingering just long enough to be sure—and Zephyrine would have sworn, all the rest of his life, that he heard it say one final word.

Not *hurry*.

*Mine.*

Meaning him.

Then the light streamed on toward the dawn, the mountain settled into its long sleep, and Odile tucked Zephyrine's head against her shoulder and said, "Right. Home, then. All of us. And Zeph—when we get there, you're teaching me to make those luck-knots. Properly. If you're going to be merely a man, you're going to be a *useful* one."

Zephyrine smiled into the dark, emptied of everything except, it turned out, the things that mattered, and let his crew carry him down out of the mountains—toward Vhalcrest, toward Sera's grave, toward whatever small life was waiting for a man with no magic and, at last, no debts.

He slept before they reached the tree line, and dreamed of nothing at all, and it was the best rest he'd had in fifteen years.
