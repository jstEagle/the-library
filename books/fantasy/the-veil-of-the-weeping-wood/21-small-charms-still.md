## 21. Small Charms Still

The luck-knots sold better than Zephyrine expected, which was the whole problem.

He had made them honestly—hemp cord, three knots apiece, a shell bead from the harbor market, and nothing else. No whisper of working in them, no borrowed breath, no prayer tucked into the braid like a coin sewn into a hem. Just knots. Anyone could tie knots. He tied them well because he had been tying them for fifteen years and his hands had opinions about it now.

"Three coppers," he told the fishwife, "and I'll tell you what my grandmother told me: wind it twice around the left wrist. The left. Luck comes in on that side."

"My grandmother said right," said Maren Odo, who bought a knot every month and had never once caught more fish for it and had never once stopped buying.

"Your grandmother," Zephyrine said gravely, "was a right-handed woman with right-handed luck, and I respect her, but you are not your grandmother." He wound the cord around her wrist himself, twice, and knotted it off with a flourish he absolutely did not need but performed anyway, because a charm without theater is just string. "There. Now the herring will practically leap into your nets to escape your cooking."

Maren laughed, the way she always laughed, and paid him four coppers instead of three, and went down toward the water whistling.

Zephyrine sat back on his stool under the striped awning of his stall—the awning was new; the stall had a roof now, actual shingles, and a little iron stove for cold mornings—and looked at his hands.

Empty hands. Quiet hands. Hands that could not so much as warm a cup of tea by wanting it warm.

Six months since the mountain. Five months since he'd stopped flinching whenever he reached for the gift and found the place where it used to be, the way a tongue returns again and again to the socket of a lost tooth. Three months since he'd stopped reaching at all.

And here was the thing he could hardly say aloud even to himself: he did not mind.

Not entirely. Not in the way he'd feared. There was still a grief in him shaped exactly like his god's name, and there were mornings—he would admit this only to the stove—when he woke with his palms already open, asking, before remembering there was no one to answer. But underneath the grief was something sturdier, something that surprised him daily: relief. Because the magic had never really been his. It had been a loan, a tenant, a lodger who paid rent in wonder and kept him awake with its noise. What remained when it moved out was, apparently, *him*. A man who tied excellent knots. A man who knew which wrist luck preferred.

A man with a roof over his stall in a city where nobody was looking over their shoulder anymore.

* * *

Vhalcrest had changed the way a room changes when someone finally opens the shutters.

The Purge edicts had been rescinded in the spring, after the Court fell—or rather, after the Court's collapse became public knowledge, which took longer, because governments are slow to admit they've been paying taxes to a cave full of soul-brokers. The Register had been burned publicly in Founder's Square, and Zephyrine had gone to watch, standing at the back of a crowd of thousands, and had felt precisely nothing when the pages went up. Names on fire. Ten thousand names, freed by a bonfire that was months too late and entirely beside the point, and he had stood there thinking: *the real ledger is ash already, and it didn't need your torches.*

Now there were mages selling charms openly two stalls down—a fat, cheerful weatherworker named Pellow whose rain-charms actually worked, and who undercut everyone shamelessly. There was a hedge-witch at the temple gates charging honest fees for honest fevers. There was, unbelievably, a school. A small one, above a bakery, run by an exiled Thaumine sisters out of Corvess, where children learned cantrips between lessons on their letters, and the worst danger anyone faced was setting the bread alight.

Magic was merely legal again. That was the phrase people used—*merely legal*, with a shrug, as if it were a tax on wool. As if six months ago a man could be dragged from this very square with a sack over his head for the crime of warming soup with his mind.

Zephyrine sold knots next to all of it and kept his prices low and his claims lower. *Charms only,* his hand-painted sign read, in careful letters Wren had corrected twice for spelling. *No magic. Never any magic. If you want weather, see Pellow.*

People came anyway. They came, he suspected, for the same reason they'd come before—not for the charms, which did nothing, but for the five minutes of being seen. The grieving fishwife got a knot and a joke. The boy with the dead father got a knot and permission to talk about him as long as he liked. The young couple quarreling over a wedding got a knot each and instructions to tie them together and hide them somewhere neither could find alone, "so that finding each other stays a habit."

That one had worked, actually. Word had gotten around. Zephyrine had taken pains to explain that it was not magic, it was marriage, but nobody wanted to hear it.

It was late afternoon when the pickpocket arrived.

He knew she was there before he saw her, which was not magic either—it was simply that after eight years in gray markets and two years of war, a man learns the particular silence of someone moving through a crowd who does not wish to be part of it. He kept his eyes on the knot he was tying and waited.

A shadow fell across the stall. Small. Familiar.

"You're spelling *separate* wrong again," said Wren.

"It's spelled however I spell it. It's my sign."

"That's not how signs work." She dropped onto the stool beside his, all elbows and knees, twelve going on thirteen and suddenly too tall for everything, hair cropped short for summer and freckles darkened across her nose like spilled pepper. She picked up a finished knot and turned it over with a critic's frown. "These are good."

"They're knots."

"I know." She said it quietly, and he understood that she meant it as praise, and that she knew exactly what she meant. Wren was the only person in Vhalcrest who could say *these are good* about an empty charm and have it land like a benediction. She had been there, at the door beneath roots, when he'd given her a name freely. She had been in the vault when he'd fed his deed to the flame. She knew the exact weight of what he wasn't holding anymore.

"How's the school?" he asked.

"Boring. Ilse keeps making us do breathing exercises. Breathing exercises, Zeph. I've been breathing my whole life, I'm extremely good at it."

"And Ilex?"

At the name, the air over the stall's counter shimmered very slightly, the way heat shimmers off cobblestones, and a voice like dry leaves said, "*I am present and unimpressed.*"

Wren grinned. Six months ago, Ilex had spoken aloud for the first time in the heretic's chapel, and had apparently decided there was no going back. The minor spirit now accompanied Wren everywhere, invisible to most, offering commentary to all. The Thaumine sisters pretended not to know about it. Wren's classmates thought she had a spectacular imaginary friend, and Wren had stopped correcting them, because "spectacular" was accurate.

"He says the school is a den of pedants," Wren reported.

"*I said it was a nest of pedants. Nests are warmer.*"

"He wants you to know he misses your stall," Wren translated. "You give better gossip than the sisters."

"I don't gossip."

"*He is lying,*" said Ilex comfortably.

Zephyrine laughed—it came easier these days, laughter, another thing he'd thought he'd traded away and hadn't—and pushed the basket of knots across the counter. "Take some for your classmates. Tell them they're from a man with no powers whatsoever, so if they work, it's their own fault."

Wren stuffed her pockets. Then she paused, and her grin faded into something more careful, the look she got when she was about to be brave, which on Wren was a distinct facial expression involving a slight jut of the chin.

"Odile sent a letter," she said. "It came to the school. She said—" Wren dug in her coat and produced a folded page, sealed with green wax. "She said to tell you she's coming home. For the harvest festival. And she said—" Wren squinted, reading, "'tell him Brannoc says the mountain sings different now.'"

Zephyrine took the letter. Odile's handwriting was beautiful, of course; everything about Odile was beautiful in the way of things that had survived being broken and put themselves back together deliberately, seam by seam. He read it twice. Most of it was news—her sister's recovery, the estate in the south finally settled, her ongoing feud with a duchess over drainage rights—but the last line was simple:

*I keep trying to remember my sister's face and I keep getting yours instead. I think the forest gave me the better bargain after all. —O.*

He folded it away carefully, next to his heart, which was a dramatic gesture he would have mocked himself for a year ago.

"She'll stay at mine," Wren said. "I've got the loft now. Officially. With a lease and everything. Master Pell swears I'm reformed."

"Are you?"

"Obviously not." She stood, stretched, and pointed at the sign. "*Separate*. Two e's. Fix it before Odile sees it or she'll be insufferable about calligraphy for a week."

She went, weaving through the crowd with a lightness that had once been theft and was now just joy, and Ilex went with her, and the shimmer of him lingered over the counter like the ghost of a smile.

* * *

Brannoc arrived at dusk, the way stone arrives: gradually, then all at once.

One moment the market was emptying; the next there was an enormous shape standing at the edge of the awning, ducking under it, folding himself onto the ground beside the stall with the care of a man who knows exactly how much weight the earth beneath him is prepared to forgive. He was broader than Zephyrine remembered, or perhaps Zephyrine was smaller. His beard was braided now, river-stone beads worked through it, and his hands were scarred from quarry work.

He didn't say hello. He never did. He sat, and listened to the evening sounds, and after a long moment he began to hum.

Stone-song. That was what they'd called it in the war, though Brannoc had hated the term—*it isn't singing at stones,* he'd said once, *it's listening to what they're already saying.* The gift was gone from it now, mostly; the Purge's fall hadn't returned what the toll-wood had taken, and Brannoc had given up his mother's lullaby besides, and some doors once closed stay closed. But he still sang. He sang because the stones liked it, he claimed, and because a man should make something beautiful even when it changes nothing.

What he sang tonight was slow and low, a working song, the kind quarrymen use to keep rhythm with their hammers. Passersby slowed. A few stopped. An old woman closed her eyes. Nobody knew what it was; everybody felt it, the way you feel distant thunder in your back teeth.

When he finished, the market was quiet, and then it wasn't, and Brannoc said, in his voice like gravel settling:

"The mountain sings different now."

"So I heard." Zephyrine handed him a knot—reflex, habit, the gesture that meant *friend* in whatever language the two of them had built between mortar rounds and river gorges. "Odile wrote."

"Mm." Brannoc turned the knot over in his huge fingers. "She writes to me too. Long letters. She apologizes in all of them." He shook his head slowly. "Told her to stop. Told her: you sold souls to buy your family. I broke a cliff to save mine. We both paid. Nobody at that table owes anybody." He looked up. "She doesn't believe me."

"She believes you. She just needs another ten years to let it settle." Zephyrine smiled. "Give her until the festival. She'll apologize in person and we can both tell her to shut up and eat."

Brannoc rumbled something that was probably a laugh. Then he was quiet again, the particular quiet he carried, and Zephyrine waited, because Brannoc's silences had architecture, and you could usually find the door if you were patient.

"There's a hill north of Ashfield," Brannoc said at last. "Where the hounds cornered us. Where I brought the rock down." He flexed his scarred hands. "They've raised a stone there. A marker. For the witch-hunters' dead." A pause. "Nobody marked ours."

Zephyrine felt the old ache shift, rearrange itself. "You want to mark them."

"I want to sing to the hill." Brannoc's jaw worked. "Can't move the stone they raised. Don't want to. Let the hunters have their rock. But there's a hollow behind it, where our people ran, and the ground there is—" he searched for the word, and settled on, "—lonely. Ground shouldn't be lonely."

"No," Zephyrine agreed softly. "It shouldn't."

"After the festival. All of us, if they'll come." Brannoc rose, unfolding like something geological. He looked down at the stall, at the knots, at the sign with its defiant misspelling, and something moved through his weathered face that in a less granite man would have been called tenderness. "You look settled, Zeph."

"Do I?"

"Like a stone that's finally found its bed."

He went, humming, and the ground under the market seemed—for just a moment—less lonely.

* * *

Zephyrine closed the stall as the lamps came on. He counted his coppers—fewer than Pellow's, more than he needed—and banked the stove, and walked home through streets that had once taught him to walk them like a thief in his own life: hood up, eyes down, gift buried deep enough to pass any sniffer-hound.

He didn't walk like that anymore. He'd caught himself, a month ago, striding down Cordwainer Street with his face to the sun like a man with nothing to hide, because he had nothing to hide, and the realization had stopped him mid-stride so completely that a cart driver had cursed him roundly. Nothing to hide. Fifteen years of careful invisibility, undone not by a decree or a war but by the simple, outrageous luxury of being known.

His rooms above the chandler's shop were small and clean and full of other people's traces, which was the best thing about them. Wren's spare boots by the door, two sizes too big, saved against growth. A scarf Odile had left, silk, absurdly fine, that he wore anyway. A river-stone on the windowsill that Brannoc had given him without explanation and that Zephyrine had never asked about, because some gifts are just rocks and some rocks are just prayers.

On the table lay the letter from Odile, and beneath it, weighted under the inkwell, a second letter he had not yet answered—from the Thaumine sisters, asking, politely and for the third time, whether he might consider teaching. *Not magic,* Sister Elowen had written, in the same careful script. *We have plenty of magic. What our students lack is what you seem to have in surplus: the knowledge of how to be ordinary and unafraid of it.*

He still didn't know what he'd answer. But he noticed that he hadn't thrown the letter away, and that he'd read it more than once, and that when he imagined himself standing in front of a room of frightened children explaining that the world would not end if they were only themselves—well. Something in his chest turned over and looked around, like a bird waking in a nest it had forgotten it built.

He banked the fire. He opened the window to the night, and Vhalcrest breathed in—fish-smell and bread-smell and lamplight and the far-off clatter of the harbor, a living city, a forgiven city, or at least a city learning the grammar of forgiveness.

And in the quiet, the way it always came now—not as thunder, not as command, but as gently as dust settling—came the memory of a voice. *Hurry,* it had said in the Weeping Wood, three words compressed into one. He had hurried. He had burned the ledger and emptied himself doing it, and the god had gone free into the wide world, whispering thanks, taking nothing with it, leaving him whole and hollow and, impossibly, more himself than he had ever been.

But lately, in the quiet hours, Zephyrine had begun to notice something. The voice was silent, yes. The lantern was ash. And yet—

Yet when he tied a knot for a grieving fishwife, something eased in her shoulders that had nothing to do with hemp cord. Yet when Brannoc sang, the ground seemed to lean in. Yet when Wren walked through a crowd, strangers smiled at her without knowing why. Small things. Charms, almost. As if the freed god, going out into the world, had scattered itself thin as pollen—and as if some fraction of it had chosen, of all the places in all the world, to settle here. In a gray-market stall. In a man's empty, open hands.

Or perhaps, Zephyrine thought, banking the last lamp, that was nonsense, and kindness had always worked like this, and he'd simply spent too many years believing power was the only currency worth counting.

Either way, he slept well.

In the morning there would be knots to tie, and a sign to correct—two e's, *separate*, he'd remember, probably—and letters to answer, and a festival to plan, and friends converging on the city from three directions like rivers finding the sea. And after the festival, a hill north of Ashfield, and lonely ground waiting for a song.

And after that—one errand longer deferred than any of them. A grave outside the city walls, unmarked except in his memory, where a woman named Sera had died with his name in her fist and a terrible purpose in her blood. He owed her more than memory. In the Weeping Wood, he had cut a single branch from a tree that wept, and it had sat in a jar of water on his windowsill all these months, stubbornly, inexplicably alive.

Spring was coming. Time to plant it.

Time, at last, to find out what grew from grief, when grief was given soil and light and a free hand to choose its own shape.
