## 22. The Veil Lifted

The hill above Vhalcrest had no name that anyone agreed on. The fisherfolk called it the Windy Seat because the wind never stopped there; the tanners called it Tanner's Regret because of what they'd once dumped on it; and the children called it Nothing Hill, which Zephyrine had always thought was the most honest name a place had ever been given.

Sera's grave stood at its crest, under a sky the color of rinsed wool, in ground he had bought with the last coin from his charm-box.

It was a modest marker. He'd carved it himself, which meant the letters wandered like drunkards and the S in SERA leaned against the E as if it needed support. Brannoc had offered to stone-sing the whole thing smooth and perfect, and Zephyrine had said no — not out of sentiment, though there was plenty of that, but because Sera would have laughed herself sick at his terrible lettering, and he wanted something on this hill she could laugh at.

*Here lies Sera Carrow,* the stone said. *She carried things home.*

That was all. It was enough. It was more than enough.

He knelt in the grass beside her with a clay pot between his knees, and inside the pot, wrapped in damp cloth, rode a cutting from the Weeping Wood — a slender whip of gray-green wood about the length of his forearm, taken (with permission; Ilex had insisted on permission, and Wren had spent twenty minutes negotiating with the spirit over the terms) from one of the trees near the door beneath the roots.

Everyone had told him it wouldn't grow. Odile, in her precise court-bred way: "Trees that bleed don't take to ordinary soil." Brannoc, blunt as bedrock: "It's dead wood. I can hear it. Dead." Even Wren, gently, the way you tell a child the dog has gone to live on a farm: "Maybe plant something else?"

But the cutting had been green when he took it. And it had been green through the winter on his windowsill, sulking in its pot like a cat that disapproved of the household. And it was green now, this morning, as he unwound the cloth from its roots and set it in the hole he'd dug.

"Right," he said to Sera's grave. "You get the difficult one. You always did."

The wind answered, as wind does, by shoving his hair into his mouth. He spat it out and kept talking anyway, because talking to her had become a habit somewhere over the past months and habits were the only prayers he had left.

"You missed the spring festival. Well — you didn't miss much. Odile danced. That's the whole report. She danced, and three different merchants proposed to her before the second song, and she turned them all down with such politeness that two of them apologized to *her*. Brannoc sang the old stone-songs openly, in the square, in front of the new magistrate, who cried. Actually cried. Big man, face like a dropped pie, weeping into his sash. Wren stole his handkerchief during it, but she gave it back, so we're counting it as growth."

He tamped the soil down around the cutting with his palms. The earth was cold and smelled of rain coming, that green-metal smell Vhalcrest wore every spring like an old coat.

"Ilex says hello," he added. "Well. Ilex doesn't say hello. Ilex says, quote, 'Tell the loud one the tree remembers her,' which I have decided is a greeting, because if I decide it isn't, I'll lie awake wondering what it means instead."

* * *

It had been eight months since the mountain came down.

Eight months since he had fed his own deed — his own *name*, written in the Pale Court's cold silver script — into the lantern of bone, and his god had burned the ledger, and ten thousand souls had gone up through the roots of the Weeping Wood like sparks up a chimney. Eight months since he had woken on the far side of the collapse with nothing left in him but muscle and stubbornness and a headache that lasted eleven days.

His magic hadn't come back. He had checked. In the early weeks he'd checked constantly, embarrassingly often, the way you prod a tooth after the dentist swears it's fine. He'd hold a luck-knot in his hands and feel... nothing. Thread. Knot. String. A cheap trick for a copper piece, honestly priced for the first time in his life.

For a while that had frightened him more than the Court ever had. Then, one gray morning in the market, a fishwife — a different fishwife than the first one, years ago now, but the same grief in the same tired shoulders — had bought a knot against drowning and paid with a smile she didn't quite mean, and Zephyrine had realized he was going to be all right, because he still wanted to hand people something warm even when he couldn't cheat warmth into the giving.

The Purge had ended the week the ledger burned. Nobody could quite explain how the Crown knew. But the registers had gone quiet, the witch-hunters' hounds had gone strange and then docile, and within a month the proclamation boards carried a new notice, stiffly worded, obviously drafted by lawyers at spear-point: *the practice of the gift is lawful within the realm.* Magic hadn't merely become tolerated. Somewhere in the burning of ten thousand deeds, the Court's claim on the world had come apart entirely, and the world had noticed.

Vhalcrest being Vhalcrest, the city had adjusted the way a river adjusts to a fallen log — grumbling, rearranging, carrying on. There were licensed hedge-mages now, which made Zephyrine laugh every time he thought about it, because half of them were frauds selling luck-knots that were only knots, and the other half were frauds selling luck-knots that were only knots *and charging triple.*

"The honest ones are ruined," Odile had observed dryly, over tea. "How do you compete with 'genuinely does nothing'?"

"You price it fairly," Zephyrine said, "and you sleep well."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is. I recommend it."

* * *

The cutting went into the earth with very little ceremony, which felt right. Zephyrine pressed the soil around it, poured out the water he'd hauled up the hill in a dented pail, and sat back on his heels to look at the thing: a bare stick in bare dirt on a bare hilltop, looking for all the world like a mistake.

"Don't judge yet," he told it.

Then he sat down properly in the grass, because his knees had opinions about hills these days — thirty-two years old and creaking like a ship, thanks, he supposed, to a decade of war and a year of running — and let the wind have its way with him.

He thought about Sera.

He thought about the night she'd arrived at his door with her blood soaking through her coat and a black deed clutched in her fist like the last card in a losing hand. He thought about how she'd died saying his name — not his rank, not his unit designation, not the number the Court had assigned his soul, but his *name*, the one his mother had given him before any commander or clerk or pale lantern-lit thing had ever put a price on it. She had died insisting he was worth saving. And for three years before that, and one terrible month during, he had believed his worth lived in the gift — that the magic was the man, and take away the fire and you're left holding ash.

He knew better now.

Not because some god had told him. Not because the mountain had taught him. Because of smaller things. Because Wren had solved the riddle of names using the alias he'd given her — *Pip Ashkettle*, the first name he'd ever chosen freely, given away like a coat to a shivering kid — and the door had opened, and he'd understood in that moment that identity was never a possession the powerful could deed away. It was a thing you handed each other, back and forth, until it held.

Because Odile had confessed her worst sin in a collapsing shrine and the crew had not turned from her, and she had led them through the vault anyway, and afterward she'd said, quietly, "I expected to pay for that," and Brannoc had said, "You already did," and that was the whole of it.

Because Brannoc had brought a cliff down on his own old life without hesitation, and never once asked for it back.

Because survival, it turned out, had never been the price at all. The price — the true toll, the one the forest had demanded and the Court had collected and the war had taken from all of them — was *forgetting*. Forgetting who you were. Letting the ledgers write your name until you couldn't read your own handwriting anymore. Sera hadn't died because she carried proof. She died because she refused to forget him, in a world built on forgetting.

Zephyrine wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand and blamed the wind, out loud, to nobody.

* * *

He stayed on the hill through the afternoon, doing the small necessary work of grief, which mostly consists of sitting still. Around him Vhalcrest went on being Vhalcrest: gulls arguing over the harbor, the bell in the low tower ringing the hour slightly flat because nobody had fixed it since the Purge and everyone had grown fond of it, smoke rising from a hundred chimneys in crooked, contented columns.

A shape climbed the hill path late in the day — small, quick, hooded against the wind, moving with the particular economy of someone who had learned to climb fences before she learned long division.

Wren dropped onto the grass beside him without asking, which was correct. Asking had never been part of their arrangement.

"She bloomed?" Wren said.

"Not yet."

"You said she might today."

"I said she might. Trees keep their own calendar. I've learned to respect that."

Wren squinted at the cutting, which remained, defiantly, a stick. "Ilex will be smug."

"Ilex is always smug."

"Ilex said — and I'm quoting — 'the tree will do what the tree owes.' Which I think means it's waiting for something."

"For what?"

"How should I know? I'm twelve, not a priest." She dug in her pocket and produced, wrapped in a scrap of waxed paper, two honey buns of deeply questionable provenance. She handed him one. He ate it. Neither of them remarked on where it had come from, which was also part of the arrangement.

They sat in companionable silence while the light went long and gold across the water. Then Wren said, quieter, in the voice she saved for things that mattered:

"You ever think about her? Like — really. What she'd say. About all of it."

Zephyrine considered. The honest answer was *every day*, but honesty deserved better than reflex.

"I used to imagine her telling me I'd done enough," he said. "That I could rest. And it took me months to realize that wasn't her voice at all. That was mine, borrowing her. The real Sera would look at all this—" he waved a hand at the town, the hill, the blooming-not-yet tree, himself, "—and say something completely useless like *took you long enough*, and then steal my honey bun."

Wren looked down at the remaining honey bun in her hand.

"...I'll leave it on the stone," she said solemnly.

"That's her influence on you. I want you to know that. Before Sera, you'd have eaten both."

"I still considered eating both."

"Growth," Zephyrine said. "We're counting it as growth."

* * *

He was gathering his things as the light failed — the empty pail, his coat, Wren's waxed paper, folded small — when he saw it.

He almost missed it. The wind had been worrying the little tree all afternoon, bending it flat and letting it spring back, and so when it moved again he looked away. But then it moved *differently* — a slow unfurling, deliberate as a hand opening — and he turned, and stopped.

Along the cutting's gray-green stem, where all day there had been only bark and patience, buds were swelling. Three of them. Four. They opened as he watched — not the wood's pale weeping flowers, not the ghost-white bells of the trees that bled, but something else. Something the Weeping Wood had never made, because nothing in that dark country had ever asked it to.

They were the color of dawn over the harbor. Warm gold at the heart, soft rose at the edge, five petals each, opening wide and unashamed in the last of the light. No sap ran from them. No tears. The tree that had wept for ten thousand stolen souls stood on a windy hill above a gray city and *bloomed*.

"Oh," said Wren, very softly.

Zephyrine knelt. Up close, the flowers smelled faintly of rain and honey buns and something older underneath, something like the moment before a storm breaks, or the moment after — the clean air when everything terrible is finally over.

He understood then, kneeling in the darkening grass with his dead friend's name leaning drunkenly on its letters behind him, what the tree had been waiting for. Not permission. Not spring. It had been waiting to be planted somewhere that remembered why it was planted. Roots need a reason. Every living thing does.

Survival was never the price. He had survived the war, the silence, the Purge, the mountain — survival had been the easy part, brutal but simple, a matter of keeping the heart beating and the feet moving. The price had been forgetting. Forgetting the god who had spent its last strength hiding its faithful. Forgetting the comrades. Forgetting, worst of all, that a man is not the sum of what he can do but the sum of what he refuses to become. The Court had held the deeds to ten thousand souls, and every one of those souls had been bought with the same currency: *forget who you are, and the debt is cleared.*

He had refused to pay.

And here was the interest, compounded on a windy hill in the form of five impossible flowers: he had walked out of the mountain hollow and mortal and magicless, and he had lost nothing that mattered, because everything that mattered had been given to him along the way by people who chose to stay. A name given freely to a pickpocket. A confession trusted to strangers. A cliff brought down in sacrifice. A friend who died carrying him home. Belonging had been waiting for him the entire time — patient as a planted tree — not in the gift, not in the god, not in the ledger or the bargain or the flame, but in the small daily act of handing yourself, piece by piece, to the people who hand themselves back.

"Hello, Sera," he said quietly.

The flowers nodded in the wind. Or the wind moved them. On this hill, he had learned, the difference mattered less than you'd think.

Wren put the honey bun on the grave stone, tucked it under the leaning S so it wouldn't blow away, and stood beside him with her hands in her pockets, watching the tree glow faintly in the dusk like a lamp someone had finally lit.

"So," she said, after a while, in the tone she used when she was about to make trouble. "Odile's coming through town next week. Brannoc's singing at the dedication of the new chapel — the real one, the legal one, with the stained glass and everything. And there's a ship docking Thursday that Ilex says has something aboard that 'remembers the Wood.'"

Zephyrine looked at her. Twelve years old, sharp-eyed, spirit-touched, grinning like tomorrow owed her money and she intended to collect.

"Something that remembers the Wood," he repeated.

"Something," Wren confirmed. "Ilex wouldn't say more. Said we'd want to see it ourselves." She paused, savoring it. "*We.*"

Zephyrine looked back at the blooming tree, at the grave, at the city lights kindling below them one window at a time — ten thousand small warm answers to the dark — and found, to his complete lack of surprise, that his feet were already interested.

"Well," he said, shouldering his pail and taking Wren's hand as they started down the hill together, "we'd best go see what's come home."
