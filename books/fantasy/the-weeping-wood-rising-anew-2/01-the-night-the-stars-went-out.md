## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

There are three rules to guiding a caravan through the Thornfell high passes at night, and I've broken all of them at least once. Rule one: never trust a client who smiles too easily. Rule two: never take a load you can't lie about convincingly at a checkpoint. Rule three — and this is the one that keeps men alive — never, under any circumstances, stop moving on the high shelf between Wolf's Tooth and the Gray Stair.

We broke rule three because of a mule.

Her name was Duchess, which should have been my first warning, because nobody names an animal after royalty unless they intend to be disappointed by it. She'd gone lame two miles back, favoring her near hind leg with the theatrical suffering of a creature who has learned that limping gets you extra oats. Old Marta, who owned half the caravan's goods and all of its opinions, declared we would rest until dawn rather than "murder a poor dumb beast for the sake of schedule," and since Marta paid the tolls that kept us off the garrison roads, schedule lost.

So there we were, twenty-two souls and thirty-one animals camped on the high shelf, four thousand feet of nothing on either side of us, fires banked low, the whole ragged family of smugglers and teamsters and one very smug mule huddled against a wind that had been coming down off the ice fields all evening like it held a grudge.

I sat apart from the others, as usual, sharpening a knife that didn't need it. Fifteen years of walking these passes teaches you that idle hands make noise, and noise up here carries the way gossip carries in a small town — fast, far, and always to the wrong ears.

That was when I noticed the stars going out.

Not all at once. That's what I want understood, whoever ends up reading this account, if anyone does: not all at once, like a lamp snuffed, but one by one, in a slow ripple, starting in the east and rolling westward across the sky like someone drawing a curtain over a window the width of the world. First the bright ones went, then the middling ones, then the faint dust of them, until the whole vault above Thornfell was black as the inside of a closed fist.

I have seen storms eat stars. I have seen high cloud, ash-fall, the aurora so thick it washes the sky clean of everything but green. This was none of those things. The sky was *clear*. Clearer than clear — the kind of hard, cold, wind-scoured clarity you only get at altitude, the kind where you can count the Pleiades without trying. And there were simply no stars in it.

"Harrow," I said. Harrow was our rear-guard, an ex-soldier turned mule-hand with the eyes of a man who'd seen too much and the temperament of a man determined to see considerably less. "Look up."

He looked up. He was quiet long enough that I heard Duchess chewing contentedly somewhere behind us, utterly unbothered by the end of the visible universe.

"That's wrong," Harrow said finally.

"Yes."

"Stars don't do that."

"No."

"Should we wake Marta?"

I considered this. Waking Marta meant waking the whole camp, and the whole camp included the Kettle brothers, whose solution to every problem from rockfall to bad weather was to get loudly drunk about it. But there are moments when the wrong reaction beats no reaction, and a sky full of nothing felt like one of them.

"Yeah," I said. "Wake everyone. Quietly."

* * *

Here's a thing most people don't know about me, and which I have spent fifteen years and considerable effort ensuring stays that way: I used to be somebody. Not somebody important — the order I belonged to was important precisely because nobody knew it existed — but somebody *bound*. A swordspeaker of the Vigil of the Broken Marches, sworn to the border wards of Thornfell, marked at my ordination with the oath-sign burned into the inside of my left forearm: a line crossed by seven smaller lines, one for each ward-stone chain from the Gray Stair to the sea cliffs.

The Vigil died when I was twenty-three. Plague, mostly — a wasting fever that took nine of us in a single winter — and attrition, and the slow bureaucratic murder of any institution that outlives its purpose in the minds of the people funding it. By the time the last of my brothers and sisters buried the last of the others, there were two of us left. Then one. Then just the mark, and the oath it stood for, and me carrying both around like a debt owed to creditors who no longer kept offices.

The mark had done nothing in fifteen years. It sat under my sleeve like an old scar, which is what I told people it was. A scar from a forge accident. Everyone believes a forge story; forges are honest places, or at least everybody agrees to pretend they are.

When the last star guttered out, the mark woke up.

It didn't sting. Sting would have been bearable. It *burned*, white-hot, as though someone had laid the brand fresh against my skin, and I made a sound I'm not proud of — something between a gasp and a word in a dead language, which, given what the swordspeakers used for their oaths, it may technically have been. I went down on one knee in the frozen mud with my arm clutched to my chest like a boy who'd grabbed a kettle handle.

And through the pain, worse than the pain, I felt something else. Something coming up through the ground itself, through stone and root and the deep bones of the mountains: a sound like a great bell breaking. Not ringing — *breaking*, a cracked note that went on and on, fading not into silence but into absence, the way a room goes quiet after a clock stops.

Every ward within fifty miles. I couldn't have told you how I knew that, any more than I could tell you how I know when a rope bridge is rotten before I step on it. You spend your life bound to a thing, you feel it in your teeth. Fifty miles of warding, from the Gray Stair to at least the Ashen Ford, and every strand of it had just been cut.

Not broken. Cut. There's a difference. Wards break when they're overwhelmed — weather, years, brute force. What I felt was surgical. Deliberate. Someone had walked the veins of the border and severed them one by one, and they'd done it tonight, tonight of all nights, while I sat on a mountain holding a knife I didn't need to sharpen.

"August?" Marta's voice, close, cautious. She'd come up beside me without me hearing her, which should have embarrassed me more than it did. "You're bleeding."

I looked down. In the starless dark I couldn't see much, but I could smell copper, and when I peeled my fingers away from my forearm the mark beneath the sleeve was wet. The old brand had split along every line of it, seven cuts crossing one, seeping like the skin had remembered being new.

"It's nothing," I said, which was rule two applied to myself. Lie convincingly. "Old wound. Weather turns, sometimes it opens."

Marta looked at me the way she looked at invoices she suspected were forged. "Weather," she said.

"The sky did something strange." I got to my feet, testing the arm. It worked; it hurt; those seemed acceptable terms. "Get everyone up. Full camp. We're moving."

"We're not leaving Duchess."

"Duchess rides a wagon. Nobody leaves anything. But we are not spending another hour on this shelf, and I'll thank you not to argue, because I'm the one who knows these passes and right now every instinct I own is telling me to be lower down and further south before—"

Before what, I didn't say. I didn't know. That was the worst part, the part that sat in my chest like swallowed river-stone: fifteen years of knowing every fold and shadow of Thornfell, and for the first time since I'd taken the oath, I had no idea what was coming. Only that it was big, and deliberate, and that the borders between the two realms had just stopped meaning anything at all.

* * *

We moved. It took the better part of an hour to strike camp in the dark — no stars, remember, and the moon nowhere to be found either, which I noticed around the time I noticed the moon was also missing, and which I decided not to mention to anyone because panic is contagious and I was already carrying enough of it for the whole caravan.

Harrow walked beside me at the head of the column, leading Duchess's wagon, and after a mile of silence he said, conversationally, "Sky's wrong."

"Sky's wrong," I agreed.

"Been on this border eleven years. Garrison before this. Never seen the like."

"Mm."

"My brother's stationed at Harrowgate," he went on, and I heard what he wasn't saying underneath it: *my brother is behind whatever that was, and I want to turn around, and I'm asking you to give me a reason to or not to.*

"Harrowgate's downhill," I said. "Downhill's where we're going anyway. If your brother's in trouble, we're the nearest help he's got, whether he wants it or not."

That settled him, more than it should have. People trust guides the way they trust priests — on faith, and usually undeserved. I thought about the mark on my arm, still throbbing in time with something I refused to name, and wondered what exactly I was a priest *of* now, besides lies and back paths.

The descent toward the valley went easy for two hours. Then the dogs started.

Caravans keep dogs the way ships keep cats: half for work, half for luck. We had three, and all three began howling at once — not the wind-howl, not the wolf-howl, but the flat, continuous, hair-raising cry dogs make when something is *wrong* in a way their bodies understand and their mouths can't describe. Then, faint and far below us, other dogs answered. Farm dogs. Village dogs. Dozens of them, all down the length of the valley, all singing the same note of dread.

"They hear it too," Harrow said.

"There's nothing to hear."

"No," he said. "But they hear it anyway."

He was right, and I hated him for it. Because now that I listened — really listened, the way I hadn't let myself since the mark woke — I could feel it again, that broken-bell note, except it hadn't faded. It had changed. It was still coming up through the mountain, through my boots, through the marrow of me, and it had resolved into something almost like rhythm. Almost like speech. As if whatever had cut the wards wasn't finished cutting, and was working its way down the border line with the patience of a man rowing a long boat, stroke by stroke by stroke.

South. It was moving south. Toward the lowlands. Toward the towns.

Toward the Weeping Wood.

I stopped walking. The column bumped into me, swore at me, and stopped, and in the absolute dark I stood there with my burning arm and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the first star died.

The Weeping Wood sat on the old border, the true border, the one that predated both realms and every treaty ever inked over them. Every ward-stone chain in Thornfell anchored, ultimately, to the wood. That was the oldest lesson of my training, the one Vess drilled into us novices until we dreamed it: *the stones are fingers, Brightwater. The wood is the hand. Guard the fingers if you must, but if the hand ever closes, nothing you do to the fingers will matter.*

The Vigil was dead. Its wards were dead. And something was walking down the veins of the world toward the heart of everything my order had sworn — uselessly, secretly, for two hundred years — to protect.

"August," Marta said behind me. "Why have we stopped?"

I realized I'd drawn my sword. I don't remember deciding to. The blade caught no light, because there was no light to catch, and somehow that was the detail that finally frightened me properly — not the missing stars, not the split mark, not the mountain murmuring its broken bell-song, but my own bare steel hanging invisible in a darkness total enough to hide the end of the world.

"Nothing," I said, and sheathed it. "Keep moving. Harrowgate by midmorning."

"And then?"

And then, I thought, we find out what happens to a border when its wards die. We find out what walks through in the dark. We find out who did this and why, even if finding out kills me — because the Vigil was gone, and the oath remained, and an oath without anyone left to keep it doesn't fade. It just waits. Fifteen years, it had waited, quiet under my sleeve. Tonight it had woken up screaming.

"Then," I said, "we find out what kind of trouble we're all in."

Behind us, at the top of the pass, where the sky met the ridge line, there came a glow. Faint, red-orange, flickering. Not dawn — dawn was hours away, and dawn comes gold in Thornfell, not like that, not like fire reflected off snow.

Harrowgate was burning. Or something near it was. And every dog in the valley howled louder.

I put my head down, tightened the strap on Duchess's wagon, and led the caravan down into the dark toward whatever was waiting at the bottom — the last swordspeaker in Thornfell, armed with a sword, a lie about a forge, and an oath whose owners had all gone to their graves keeping it.

It occurred to me, somewhere around the third switchback, that I might be the only person in either realm who'd felt the wards die from the inside. Whoever had cut them would want to know about that. So would whoever had failed to stop them.

One of those people was me. I intended to find out who the other was before they found me.

The red glow on the horizon pulsed, steady as a heartbeat. We walked toward it.
