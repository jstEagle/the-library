## 2. Ash on the Toll Road

There is a particular silence that follows a lie told by the sky itself. I had heard it for three days now — since the stars went out and took every ward in Thornfell with them — and it was not the silence of emptiness. It was the silence of a held breath.

The toll road down from the high passes into Harrowgate wound like a grey ribbon someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up again. My mule, Duchess, hated it. She expressed this by walking as close to the inner edge as her dignity allowed and giving me looks over her shoulder that could curdle cream.

"I know," I told her. "I don't like it either."

The caravan had scattered at the top of the pass, which is what sensible smugglers do when the sky breaks. Old Marta had taken the eastern fork with half the goods and all the good sense. The Corvane brothers had simply vanished, which was their way of contributing. That left me with Duchess, two crates of Vellish brandy nobody would be taxing anytime soon, and the growing certainty that I should have stayed on the mountain.

Fifteen years I'd carried the oath-mark on my left forearm. Fifteen years of it sitting quiet as a scar, a faded knot of silver lines that marked me as the last swordspeaker of an order everyone else had the good fortune to die out of. On the night the stars went out, it had burned like a coal pressed to the bone. It still burned, faintly, a low ache I'd stopped mentioning because there was no one left to mention it to who wouldn't look at me like I'd grown a second head.

Swordspeakers were supposed to feel the wards. That had been rather the point of us. Every ward-stone from the Thornfell border to the Weeping Wood itself sang one long sustained note, and we were the tuning forks, sworn to hear it and keep it whole. When the order died — fever, mostly, and bad luck, and one memorable avalanche — I'd assumed my part in the song was over. A tuning fork for a broken instrument.

But the mark burned, and burning marks mean something, and so here I was, walking toward the trouble instead of away from it, because apparently fifteen years of careful neutrality counted for nothing against an old habit of duty. Oaths are like that. They don't die when the order dies. They just get lonely.

* * *

Harrowgate announced itself before I saw it: smoke, first, too much and too black for cookfires. Then the smell — wet ash and something underneath it, charred wool, burnt hair. Then the town itself, spilling down its hillside toward the river crossing, and I stopped at the ridge and looked at it for a long moment while Duchess shifted her weight beside me.

The ward-line should have been visible from here. Harrowgate sat on one of the great ward-stones, a granite pillar taller than a man, older than the town that had grown up around it like moss. You could always see the shimmer off it at dusk, a heat-haze ripple in the air, the visible proof that the realm's borders held and the things on the other side of them stayed other.

The stone stood bare. No shimmer. And around its base, where pilgrims used to leave offerings and garrison quartermasters used to leave their laundry to dry —

Shards. The ward-stone hadn't been toppled or cracked. It had *burst*, blown outward from within, granite fragments strewn across forty feet of cobbled square like the innards of some enormous egg. Dust still hung in the air around it. Ash, fine and grey, lay over everything — the rooftops, the well, the little shrine to the road-gods with its tin offerings.

From inside. Shattered from the inside.

I'm not a scholar. Wren Calloway will tell you this at length, and with footnotes, if you ever give her the chance. But even I knew what that meant, or thought I did. Wards fail from outside. That's what wards are *for* — taking the blow so the town doesn't. A ward that bursts inward is a ward that was hollowed first, eaten through, undermined until nothing was left holding it up but its own momentum. Somebody hadn't broken the wall. Somebody had spent months digging under it.

And then the mark on my arm flared, sudden and white-hot, and I nearly went to my knees in the road.

Not pain, exactly. Or not only pain. It was more like hearing your own name called from very far away, except the name was wrong, and the voice was wrong, and the direction it came from was *down* — through the shattered stone, through the hillside, through whatever lay beneath the border that I had spent my whole career pretending didn't matter.

Duchess bit my shoulder. Hard. Which, in fairness, is what she's for.

"Thank you," I said, rubbing the bite. "Your counsel is noted."

We went down into Harrowgate together, because the alternative was standing on a ridge being lectured by a mule, and because — I'll admit it, though I denied it loudly later — the burning in my arm felt less like a warning and more like a summons. The last oath-bound man, walking into a town full of frightened people with weapons. What could possibly go wrong.

* * *

What went wrong was everything, in the usual order.

The garrison had done what garrisons do: shut the gates, doubled the watch, and started looking for someone to blame. In Harrowgate, the arithmetic of blame was simple. The ward fell; smugglers use the passes; therefore smugglers broke the ward. Never mind that no smuggler alive had the first idea how a ward-stone worked — we know roads, not runes. Never mind that half the garrison's officers bought their winter brandy from us at rates they'd deny under oath. Fear doesn't do arithmetic carefully. Fear does it fast.

They'd strung up two of the Corvane brothers' hired hands already. Not hanged — Harrowgate wasn't quite there yet — but tied to the whipping post outside the garrison house, bleeding, while a sergeant read charges off a paper he clearly didn't understand. A crowd had gathered to watch, and crowds are weather. This one had a storm in it.

I should have kept my hood up and walked the river path. There's a version of me that did exactly that, and in that version I am presumably rich and unbothered and sleeping in Vellish comfort right now. Instead I stopped, because one of the bound men was Tam, sixteen years old, who once carried me three miles through snow with a broken ankle and complained about it for a decade afterward, and because hanging him wouldn't fix a single shard of that ward-stone and everybody in that square deep down knew it.

Also — and this is the part I keep circling back to — my arm was burning, and it burned harder the closer I got to the post, and I have learned in a long life that when the old magic starts insisting on things, it's cheaper to listen than to argue.

So I did something stupid. I pulled back my hood, walked up to the sergeant, and said:

"Those boys can't have touched the stone. I can prove it."

The square went quiet in the specific way squares do when a stranger says something interesting and possibly fatal. The sergeant looked me over — road-worn coat, mule, brandy crates, the general air of a man who paid his taxes nowhere — and decided, reasonably enough, that I was exactly what I appeared to be.

"Can you, now." He said it flat, but he lowered the paper. Small mercies. "And who's asking?"

"August Brightwater. Guide. I run the high passes." I let that sit. Half the garrison knew the name; guides who work the Thornfell crossings are known the way weather is known. "And I'll tell you what I told your captain last autumn when he asked about the ward-roads: nobody goes near the stones. Smugglers fear the wards worse than you do. They're the only thing keeping both armies honest about where the border sits. Break a ward and you don't get rich — you get a war with no map on it."

"That's a pretty speech."

"It's free. The truth usually is." I nodded at Tam. "That boy can't lift the crate he sleeps on. Look at him."

He couldn't. Tam weighed perhaps nine stone soaking wet, and most of that was opinions. The sergeant looked, and the crowd looked, and for a moment I thought the weather might turn mild.

Then somebody in the crowd shouted, "Then who did?" and the moment curdled, because it was a fair question and I didn't have the answer, and crowds can smell a missing answer the way sharks smell blood.

"They'll say it was the wood-folk next," another voice called. "Wards break, stars die, and it's never the Crown's fault—"

"It was the smugglers!" someone else insisted, and a stone came out of the crowd, and after that the evening stopped being negotiable.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about riots: they're slower than you think and faster than you can plan for. There's a long moment where everything hangs — where the crowd is still a crowd of individuals, farmers and fishwives and bored teenagers — and then a stone hits something it shouldn't, and the individuals dissolve into a single animal with one intention and no ears.

The stone hit the garrison house window. The sergeant shouted orders nobody followed. And in the churn, somebody — I never found out who, and I've made peace with not knowing — pointed past me and screamed, "There! One of them! Ward-toucher!"

They meant the girl.

She'd come out of the garrison house during the commotion — a young woman, ink-stained fingers, satchel clutched to her chest like a shield, wearing the grey-and-silver livery of the Royal Survey. Ward-scholar. I recognized the cut of the coat because I'd robbed a Survey supply train once, professionally speaking, and kept the coat. She'd come out to look at the shattered stone, because that is what scholars do — walk straight at the dangerous thing with their notebooks out — and the crowd saw the Survey colors and the ink stains and put the whole night together wrong: *the King's own rune-readers broke the ward.*

The animal turned toward her.

I want to say I thought it through. I didn't. Later — much later, in better light, with wine — I constructed a very reasonable explanation involving strategy and the value of Survey knowledge and the tactical wisdom of preventing a lynching. All true, probably. But in the moment it was simpler: my arm was burning, a girl was about to die for a crime she almost certainly didn't commit, and I have never in my life managed to walk past that particular spectacle without getting involved. It's my worst quality. Ask anyone who's ever traveled with me.

I got to her before the front rank of the mob did. Grabbed her by the satchel strap, spun her around.

"You're with me," I said. "Walk. Don't run. Running makes them chase."

Her eyes were huge and brown and entirely too intelligent for the situation. "I don't—"

"Now would be the walking part."

She walked. So did I, arm through hers like we were courting, hood down, face open, smiling the smile I use on customs men. Behind us the crowd's attention wavered — a riot needs a fixed point, and we were moving, becoming background. A drunk lunged out of an alley and I put him down with two fingers to a nerve in his neck, gently, the way you close a door, and the girl made a small sound and kept walking.

"The stable yard behind the Saltcellar," she said suddenly. Her voice shook but her mind was working; I remember noticing that, and admiring it, and being annoyed about it. "There's a gate. I've seen the carts go through."

"Lead on, scholar."

She led. Through the yard, through the gate, into a lane that smelled of tallow and horse, and from there down to the river path where Duchess waited with the patience of a creature who has watched humans be foolish for twenty years and expects nothing else.

Only when the garrison fires were behind us and the dark had closed in did the girl stop, lean against a fence post, and start shaking. Not crying. Shaking. There's a difference, and the difference is control, and I recognized it because I'd shaken exactly like that once, a long time ago, in a shrine that no longer exists.

"They were going to kill me," she said.

"Yes."

"For looking at the stone."

"For wearing the wrong coat at the wrong time. Looking at the stone didn't help."

She laughed — a short, startled bark of a laugh, gone as fast as it came. Then she looked at me properly, and I watched her do the sums: stranger, smuggler, saved my life, wants something. Everyone does sums like that eventually. Hers finished fast.

"You knew what the burst pattern meant," she said. "'Broken from the inside.' You said it at the square. Smugglers don't know ward mechanics."

"This one knows a little."

"A little." She opened her satchel — still clutching it, I noticed, even now — and pulled out a battered journal, pages soft as cloth from handling. "Then maybe you can explain why every fragment reading I took today says the same impossible thing. The resonance signature isn't foreign, master guide. It's not enemy craft, not wild magic, not anything from across the border." She held the journal out toward me, and her hand wasn't shaking anymore, and her eyes had gone hard and bright as struck flint.

"The wards weren't attacked. They were *unmade*. By someone using our own survey methods. Our own notation. Someone who learned it—" and here her voice finally did break, just slightly, on the word, "—here. Inside."

I didn't take the journal. I looked at it, and at her, and at the starless sky above the hills where the Weeping Wood sat somewhere in its long dark, and my oath-mark pulsed once, slow and patient, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"My name's Wren," she said. "Wren Calloway. And I think I helped whoever did this."

Well. There it was.

I should have walked away. Every instinct I'd polished over fifteen quiet years said walk away, Brightwater, this is a hole with no bottom. But the mark was burning, and the stars were gone, and this ink-fingered liar was the first person since the sky broke who'd said a true thing out loud.

"Duchess and I are heading south at first light," I heard myself say. "Toward the wood. You can carry the journal."

She blinked. "Just like that?"

"Just like that. Consider it payment for the stable-yard tip." I swung up into the saddle, because it's easier to make decisions you'll regret from a position of elevation. "Sleep. Tomorrow we find out what you know, and tomorrow, scholar — you tell all of it. I've had my fill of people telling half."

She nodded slowly, hugging the journal to her chest again, and above us the empty sky pressed down like a lid.

Somewhere south, in the dark where the Weeping Wood stood weeping sap instead of light, whatever had crawled through my roads was waiting.

My arm burned all night. I was starting to suspect it wasn't going to stop until I answered.
