## 4. The Smuggler's Ledger

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the mountains after the stars go out. It isn't the silence of snowfall or of held breath. It's the silence of a room where someone has just stopped speaking mid-sentence, and you're waiting for them to finish, and they never will.

Wren Calloway walked behind me down the old toll road with the particular stride of a scholar who has decided that if she looks competent enough, no one will notice she's terrified. It wasn't working, but I respected the effort. I'd been using the same trick for fifteen years.

"The ward-stones," she said, for the fourth time since Harrowgate. "They shattered from the inside. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means whoever unmade them didn't have to touch them."

"It means they had the *keys*, August. All of them. Every ward on the border has a different resonance signature. To collapse them simultaneously—" She stopped walking. "Someone didn't break the wards. Someone *retired* them."

"Retired." I tested the word like a bad tooth. "That's a very tidy way to describe fifty miles of dead country."

"That's what the Survey called decommissioned wards. It's in the manuals." Her voice went thin. "I wrote some of the manuals."

I didn't answer that. There wasn't anything useful to say, and I've found that most cruelty is just honesty delivered at the wrong altitude.

The toll road curved down through the black pines toward the river crossing at Miller's Bend, and every hundred paces or so we passed the things I'd spent half my life learning to read: a cairn with a stone turned sunwise, a blaze cut into a pine at the height of a rider's knee, a cairn-stone painted with lichen that only grew on the north face. Smuggler's script. My mother tongue, if I'd ever had a mother, which technically I'd had several, all of them crews.

I'd walked these roads since I was eleven years old, first as a runner for the Harrowgate crews, then as a swordspeaker guarding caravans that officially didn't exist carrying goods that officially weren't moving. The border wards had made the trade necessary — everything crossed the Thornfell line, salt and steel and medicine and people, and the Crown pretended not to notice as long as the tariffs got paid somewhere. The roads were veins. I'd always thought of them that way. Blood moving through a body that both realms needed alive even as they swore they wanted it dead.

I was about to learn what it feels like to find out the veins have been carrying poison.

* * *

Miller's Bend was empty.

That was wrong on about six levels. The crossing had a ferryman, a toll-house, a stable, and a woman named Osk who sold fried dough to travelers and gossip to anyone with coin, and she never closed — not for storms, not for feuds, not for the time the river froze solid and she'd fried the dough on a shovel over a brazier. Osk's philosophy, delivered to me once over a plate of dough that could have stopped an arrow, was that the world ending was no reason to lose a day's earnings.

The toll-house stood open. The fire in the hearth was dead, and it had been dead for days — the ash was cold and undisturbed, no one had banked it. Osk's ledger sat on the counter, open, a dipper of ink gone dry beside it.

Wren touched nothing. I'll give her that; scholars who survive fieldwork learn fast or not at all. She hovered at my shoulder while I turned the ledger toward the light.

"Is that a toll record?"

"It's a toll record." I turned a page. "It's also a message."

The entries were wrong. Osk kept her books the way all good smugglers did — three sets, one for the Crown, one for the crews, and one true one that lived in her head. The ledger on the counter was the Crown set, which meant it should have been full of bland fiction: honest tolls, honest travelers, honest nothing. Instead the last three weeks of entries were a thicket of small irregularities. A toll paid in "nine coppers" when the rate was eight. A cart of "wool" entered on a night the roads were closed for weather. A party of "pilgrims, four" — and pilgrims never traveled in fours, because four was a corpse-count, a funeral party, and no ferryman on the Thornfell line would write that number unless he wanted every crew within fifty miles to sit up and pay attention.

"Nine coppers," I said. "Overcharge by one. Wool in a storm. Pilgrims in fours."

"That's a cipher?"

"That's a *ledger*." I tapped the page. "Every smuggling route on the border runs on double meanings. The numbers tell you what moved and when. Someone's been using Osk's crossing as a signal station for weeks. Maybe months." I flipped back through the pages, and the irregularities went back, and back, and back. "Longer than months."

Wren had gone very still in the way she did when her mind was running ahead of her mouth, which was most of the time. "August. If the wards needed keys — resonances, signatures — someone had to gather those. Catalog them. Move them."

"Move them where?"

"Where do the roads all lead?" She said it gently, which was worse than saying it cruelly. "Every route you've taken us down since Harrowgate. Every marker. They don't fan out, August. I've been watching. They *converge*."

I already knew what sat at the center of that convergence. Every child in Thornfell knew, the way you know where your own heart is. The Weeping Wood. The forest that stood exactly on the line between the realms, older than both, the one place the maps went blank not because no one had surveyed it but because surveyors came back wrong. The place the border wards had been raised to *protect* — that was the official story — or to *fence off*, depending on which realm's grandmother was telling you the story.

The oath-mark on my forearm, the old burn-scar that had been silent for fifteen years, gave one slow throb. Not pain. Attention.

"Osk," I said, mostly to myself. "Where's Osk?"

* * *

We found her in the stable loft, and I'll spare you the details except to say that whoever did it had been in no hurry and had wanted her to talk first. Wren was sick behind the stable. I stood in the loft for a long time, because Osk had fed me fried dough and sold me lies at fair prices for twenty years, and it seemed like the least I could do was not look away.

Under her body, folded into the straw where she could reach it — Osk to the end, still keeping her books — was a scrap of oilcloth wrapped around a strip of birchbark. On the bark, in her cramped hand, was the true ledger. The one that lived in her head.

She'd written it in the last days of her life. The entries were fresh, the birchbark still pale where the knife of her stylus had cut it. And it wasn't a toll record at all.

It was a count of what had come through her crossing, in the old crew-cant that only about forty living people could read. I sat on the stable floor and translated it line by line while Wren read over my shoulder, and with each line the floor got further away.

*Seven loads, "lamp-oil," northbound, paid in old coin.* Old coin — realm-mint, not Crown. Someone from the other side of the line was funding this.

*Three loads, "quarry-stone," northbound, heavy carts, axles replaced twice.* Quarry-stone that needed new axles wasn't stone. Ward-stone is dense as sin. Or something else entirely, something I didn't have a name for yet.

*One passenger, "the Quiet Man," northbound, thrice.* Thrice. The same passenger, three times, going back and forth. Coordinating.

*One load, "seed-corn," northbound, sealed, no toll taken, crew of the* Vessel *compensated.*

I stopped.

The *Vessel* was a crew name. Not a ship — there were no ships on the Thornfell line — but a caravan outfit, one of the oldest, and I knew it the way you know a scar. Its master had taught me the high passes when I was fourteen and too clever by half. Its runners had pulled me out of a snowslide when I was nineteen. Its master's daughter had been the closest thing to a friend I'd had before the order took me, and the closest thing to a friend after, on the nights the oath got heavy.

"Whose crew is the Vessel?" Wren asked, because she'd learned to read my silences faster than most people read my words.

"Brannic Hale's." My mouth was dry. "He held the north routes when I was running. He — " I stopped. Started again. "When the order sent me to guard caravans, his was the caravan I guarded. For six years. He taught me which passes lie. He named me *kith-of-the-road*, which in the old cant means — it means you'd take a knife for each other and think nothing of it."

"And his crew moved sealed cargo north. Toward the Weeping Wood. For months."

"Under someone else's coin. With a passenger called the Quiet Man." I looked at the birchbark, at Osk's last careful hand. "And Osk wrote it down knowing what it would cost her, because she wanted someone to know the roads had been turned."

Wren sat down on the straw beside me, which was either solidarity or an admission that her legs had stopped working, and knowing Wren it was both. "August. The wards didn't break. They were retired. With keys. And the keys — or whatever fed the unmaking — went north along the smuggler roads. The roads nobody watches. The roads that are *invisible* to both realms by design."

"The veins," I said.

"What?"

"Nothing. Something I used to believe." I folded the birchbark into my coat, over my heart, which felt dramatic and also accurate. "The roads were the knife, Wren. Somebody sharpened my mother tongue into a blade and I never even heard the whetting."

* * *

We made the old waystation at Colder's Rest by dark, or by what passed for dark now, which was a sky like a closed lid. The station was a burned shell — burned recently, and not by accident; the scorch pattern was wrong, concentrated, deliberate — but the root-cellar beneath it was intact, and cellars are where smugglers keep everything that matters, including themselves, historically.

I got a fire going small and screened while Wren cataloged the cellar's contents with the grim efficiency of someone who needed to organize her hands or lose her mind. Salt. Dried apples. A crate of ward-salts, the raw mineral kind, the sort the Survey used for calibration — which made Wren go quiet and touch the crate like it was a grave.

And on the cellar wall, in charcoal, in the old cant, a route-mark. Fresh. Weeks old at most.

I knew the mark. Every runner knew it. It was a *holding-mark* — *cargo cached here, safe, do not touch, the Vessel will call.* Under it, a tally. Eleven strokes. Eleven loads cached at Colder's Rest and moved north.

And under the tally, in a different hand, a single word that was not cant, was not Crown-script, was not anything I could read — a looping, rootlike script that seemed to move when I wasn't looking directly at it.

The oath-mark on my arm woke up all the way.

Not the slow throb from the road. This was the old pain, the full burn, the one I hadn't felt since the night the order died — the mark answering something. I stripped my sleeve and the scar was glowing a faint, sullen amber, and Wren looked at it, and then at the script on the wall, and then at my face.

"August," she said. "That's covenant-script. I've only seen it in fragments, in — in the oldest Survey documents, the sealed ones. That's the language the border wards were *written* in."

"Then someone wrote it here. Recently. In a smuggler's cache."

"Someone who knew the roads." She was very careful now, the way you're careful walking on river ice. "Someone with a crew. Someone who could move cargo for months without either realm noticing, because the roads are *yours*."

I sat with that. The fire crackled its small dishonest comfort. Somewhere above us the starless sky pressed down like a thumb on a bruise, and my arm burned, and I thought about Brannic Hale teaching a fourteen-year-old to read the high passes, laughing at my mistakes, calling me kith-of-the-road. I thought about the sealed cargo. The Quiet Man. Eleven loads moving north through the dark toward the one place in the world where the maps go blank.

The roads I loved had been the knife. And the hand on the hilt was a hand I knew.

"We go north," I said.

"August—"

"We go north, and we find the Vessel, and I ask Brannic Hale to his face what he carried and for whom. And if he's guilty, then—" I stopped. The oath-mark pulsed, patient, expectant, like a dog waiting at a door. "Then I'll do what the oath requires."

"Which is what?"

And that was the trouble, wasn't it. The order that had bound me was ash and bones in a burned chapter-house, and the oath lived on in my arm with no one left alive to interpret it, and for fifteen years I'd carried it like a coin I couldn't spend.

"I don't know," I said. "But I'm about to find out. Because that mark hasn't burned in fifteen years, Wren, and it started burning the night the stars went out, and it's burning *harder* the closer we get to the Wood. The oath knows something. And I'm starting to suspect the oath is the only honest thing I have left."

Outside, in the black and starless dark, from the direction of the north passes, came a sound I hadn't heard since I was a child and had hoped never to hear again: a long, low, grieving note, like a bow drawn across the ribs of the world.

The Weeping Wood, weeping. Audible from fifty miles away.

Wren's hand found my sleeve in the dark. "Tell me that's the wind."

"It's not the wind," I said, and shouldered my pack, and turned my face north toward the roads that had betrayed me, because they were still the only roads I knew — and because somewhere along them, a man I'd have died for was waiting with an answer I was afraid to hear.
