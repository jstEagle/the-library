## 5. The Child Who Hears the Wood

I want it on the record that I did not want to go into the waystation.

The record being, of course, my own head, which is the only court that has ever bothered to hear my testimony in full and has never once ruled in my favor.

"Smoke's cold," August said, crouching at the edge of the clearing where the Old Toll Way met the shepherd cut. "Two days, maybe three."

"Then whoever burned it is gone."

"Or dead in it." He said this the way other people comment on weather. Fifteen years of smuggling will do that to a man; grief stops being an event and becomes a line item.

"And if they're dead in it, we don't need to check. Dead people famously don't need rescuing."

He looked at me over his shoulder with that expression he had — the one I'd come to think of as *the oath-mark face*, because it always preceded him doing something inconvenient on principle. "Wren. The wards are down. Every ward from Harrowgate to the White Pass. If there's anyone alive in there who knows what happened—"

"They're not going to know what happened. They're going to be a corpse or a bandit, and I've had enough of both this week."

"We're checking."

"We are *not*—" But he was already moving, because August Brightwater had never once in his life lost an argument by walking away from it, and I followed him into the ruins like a fool follows a fool, which is, I'm told, how most found families start.

* * *

The waystation had been called Miller's Rest, according to the half-melted sign, though I suspect no Miller had rested there in a century. It had been one of those border buildings that belong to no realm and therefore to everyone: a toll-house, a chapel, a barn, a place where smugglers paid off guardsmen and guardsmen pretended not to be paid. My father had stopped at places like it on survey trips. He used to say the border wasn't a line, it was a conversation, and these stations were where the two realms cleared their throats.

Someone had ended the conversation with fire.

The roof had gone in. The walls were charred lace. Under the collapsed beam of what had been the common room, the ashes held shapes I made myself stop looking at after the third one. August didn't make himself stop looking. He walked through the whole ruin slow, boots crunching, reading it the way I read ward-notation — and when he reached the far wall he stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into his back.

"Look at this."

"I've seen fire before, August. It's very dramatic. It goes up."

"Not the burn." His lantern rose. On the blackened plaster, scored deep through the char, someone had cut a spiral of marks — and around them, in a ring, smaller glyphs I knew intimately, because I had spent four years cataloguing every variant of them for the Royal Survey. Ward-script. Old dialect. First Covenant era, maybe older.

"That's not vandalism," I said, and my voice came out wrong, thin. "That's a binding formula. Or — no. It's a *release*. Look at the terminal strokes. Someone was unbinding something here. Formally. With witnesses' marks."

"Witnesses." August touched one of the small glyphs flanking the spiral. "How many?"

I counted. My stomach did something complicated. "Eleven."

"The number of the original signatories," he said quietly. "Vess made me memorize them when I was twelve. Said any swordspeaker who couldn't recite eleven names had no business holding a blade."

"So somebody reenacted the signing here. In reverse." I stepped back and felt the cold of the ruined room settle into me properly for the first time. "August, the Survey mapped weaknesses in the wards for eighteen months. Weaknesses in *old* binding points, exactly like this. If someone took my work — our work — and used these stations as release sites, walking the poison along the roads station to station—"

"Then you already told me this story in Harrowgate," he said, not unkindly, "and I already said we'd deal with it when we found the proof. This is proof-shaped. That's all. Come on."

"Proof-shaped," I repeated bitterly. "That's what I am too, then. Proof-shaped."

He paused at the doorway. For a moment I thought he'd say something gentle, which would have been unbearable. Instead he said: "You could have lied just now. Told me the glyphs meant nothing."

"I considered it."

"I know." And he smiled — small, tired, real — and went out into the dark, and I stood alone a moment longer among the ashes of a building whose only crime had been sitting on a road, feeling the strange new thing that had been growing in my chest since Harrowgate. It wasn't forgiveness. I hadn't earned that. It was lighter and more terrible: the sensation of being believed anyway.

Then I heard August say, from the barn, in a voice I'd never heard him use:

"Oh. Oh, hello."

* * *

The child was in the hay loft of the burned barn, in the one corner where the roof had held, wrapped in a horse blanket gone gray with ash, sitting cross-legged with the composure of a magistrate.

They were maybe seven years old. Maybe nine. Small for either. Hair the color of wet river-stone, cropped short and uneven — self-cut, by the look of it, with a knife. Their eyes were the problem. Not their color, which was ordinary brown, but their focus: they were looking at us and also slightly past us, the way you look at someone standing behind the person you're talking to.

Except there was nobody behind us. There was never anybody behind us anymore. The sky had seen to that.

"Are you alone?" August asked. He'd lowered himself to a crouch, hands visible, voice pitched the way men pitch their voices at frightened animals. I noted, distantly, that mine had gone soft without my permission.

The child considered the question with great seriousness.

"She says the roots remember," they said.

"...Who does?"

"The lady under the hill." The child tilted their head, listening to something. "She says: *eleven voices sealed the door, and the door was a throat, and what was swallowed still sings.*" A pause. "She's sorry about the stars."

The silence in that loft lasted approximately one geological age.

"Pip," the child added, as if resuming an interrupted introduction. "That's my name. I named myself. The old name belonged to a dead person and it seemed rude to keep using it."

"Hello, Pip," August said, steady as bedrock, though I could see the pulse jumping in his throat. "I'm August. This is Wren. We came up the toll road from Harrowgate."

"Harrowgate burned its stones." Pip said it flatly, factually, the way children report the deaths of insects. "The stones cried first. Did you hear them cry? Nobody ever hears them cry."

"I heard them," I said.

Pip looked at me — really looked, focus snapping home like a latch closing — and whatever they saw in my face made them nod once, satisfied, as if a question had been answered correctly on an exam.

"You're the one who counted them wrong," Pip said.

My mouth went dry. "What?"

"In the Survey house. The counting room. You counted the wards wrong on purpose. You wrote the weakness into the eastern ledger because they told you to, and you thought if you wrote it small enough it wouldn't matter." The child's voice never changed register. No accusation in it. Just reportage. "It mattered."

Every survival instinct I owned screamed at once. Run. Deny. Laugh it off. I opened my mouth to do the second thing while planning the first, and August — damn him, *damn* him — put a hand on my arm and said, gently:

"Pip, who told you that?"

"The wood tells me things." Pip shrugged inside the blanket. "It talks all the time now. It used to whisper. Now that the door's open it shouts. I don't sleep much." A pause, and then, with the devastating pragmatism of the very young: "Also you were talking about it in the common room yesterday. Through the wall. Walls are thin when they're burned."

The relief hit me so hard I nearly sat down in the hay. Then, immediately behind it, the horror arrived on schedule: *how long had they been listening? How much of my confession — my stupid, guilt-drunken confession by a dying fire, the one I'd made to exactly one person in the world — had soaked through the char?*

"How long have you been here alone?" August asked.

"Since the fire." Pip's gaze drifted past us again, to that invisible companion. "The bad men did it. They wore the king's colors but they weren't soldiers, soldiers don't laugh. They were looking for the road-marks. The chalk signs. They burned the station and they burned—" the child's hands curled in the blanket, the first crack in the composure, tiny and total, "—they burned Marta, who was going to take me to her sister in the low country. And then the wood started talking to me instead of just humming, and it said *stay*, and I stayed, because everything else I loved was on fire and the wood was the only thing left talking."

August closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, he'd done something to his face — packed the grief away in some smuggler's strongbox — and he said, simply:

"Well. You can't stay. The bad men might come back, and the snow's coming, and the wood talks but it doesn't cook."

"I can stay," Pip said. "The wood says—"

"The wood," said August, with the absolute authority of a man who had spent fifteen years out-arguing border guards, rival crews, and avalanches, "is not feeding you breakfast. I am. Wren is carrying the oats. Aren't you, Wren."

I was, apparently. I discovered my pack already open and my hands already measuring out oats, because apparently somewhere between Harrowgate and this hayloft I had become a person who fed orphans, and nobody had consulted me.

"There's a stream a quarter mile east," I heard myself say, gruffly, because if I couldn't be consulted I could at least be competent. "Still running. We'll need water boiled. Pip, can you walk?"

"Can *you*?" Pip asked, with genuine curiosity, and got up, and shook ash off the blanket like a small indignant bird, and that was that. That was the whole negotiation. Three days of my life arguing with August over route choices, and a seven-year-old joined our conspiracy in under four minutes.

Found families, I would later learn, are less like marriages and more like ambushes. You never see the moment it happens. You just notice, afterward, that you've been taken.

* * *

We made six miles before full dark, to a hollow beneath an overhang where the wind couldn't find us, and I cooked oat porridge with the last of our honey while August checked the backtrail twice and pretended he hadn't. Pip ate with the focused ferocity of a child who had learned exactly how long food lasts, then wrapped themselves in the horse blanket against August's side without asking, the way cats do, as if proximity were a natural law rather than a request.

And then, into the firelit dark, in a voice pitched low and rhythmic, Pip began to recite.

Not a song. Not quite. The cadence was liturgical — I knew liturgical cadence, I'd grown up on it, every scholar does — but the words were wrong for any rite I knew. Old words. Older than the Survey's oldest fragment. Words with the bones of First Covenant grammar showing through them.

"*Eleven voices sealed the door,*" Pip murmured, eyes half-closed against August's shoulder. "*And the door was a throat. What was swallowed still sings. The tree gave its heart so the sky would give its light, and the light was a loan, and the loan comes due when the singers forget the song. We do not forget. We do not forgive. We remember the price and pay it gladly, world without end—*" A breath. "*World without end,*" again, softer, sadder, and then sleep took the child mid-word, mouth still shaped around the phrase.

Neither of us moved for a long time.

"That's covenant language," I whispered finally. "That's — August, that's the *signing liturgy*. There are three fragments of it in the Royal Archive and scholars have killed each other over less than what this child just recited in their sleep. 'The door was a throat.' 'What was swallowed still sings.' Those phrases don't appear anywhere in the surviving corpus. Where did Pip *learn*—"

"The wood." August's voice was strange. Flat, careful, a man walking on ice over deep water. "The wood taught them. Which means the wood remembers. Which means—"

"Which means the covenant isn't just failing." My mind was doing that awful thing it did, assembling implications at speed, each one worse than the last. "It means the covenant has a *memory*. It means the terms were — August, 'the tree gave its heart.' Trees don't *give* hearts. That's sacrifice language. That's a *price* language. The founding of the covenant — the peace both realms have been living under for a thousand years — might be priced in something we were never told about."

"Say it plainer."

"If the peace was bought with a murder," I said, "and the victim has been singing about it ever since — then everything falling apart right now isn't a malfunction. It's an invoice."

August stared into the fire. The oath-mark on his wrist — he'd pushed his sleeve up to warm it, an unconscious habit — caught the light, that old scarred brand that had burned awake the night the stars went out. He looked at it the way a man looks at a letter he's afraid to open.

"Fifteen years I've spoken my oaths every dawn," he said at last. "Fifteen years of believing the order kept faith with something true. And now a child falls asleep on my shoulder singing the receipt." He pulled the sleeve down. "Get some sleep, Wren. Tomorrow we follow the chalk-marks north, and tomorrow we're probably going to learn worse than this."

I lay back against the stone and did not expect to sleep. But exhaustion is a tyrant, and sometime in the black hours I woke once, briefly, to two sounds.

The first was the wind in the high passes, carrying that long grieving note from the north, fainter now but constant, the Weeping Wood mourning at fifty miles' distance.

The second was closer. August, sitting watch, had begun — quietly, almost inaudibly, in time with the wind — to hum the child's melody. The liturgy of a covenant he'd sworn his life to, sung back to him by a tree through the mouth of an orphan, and he was learning it. Learning it the way you learn anything that matters: without deciding to.

I fell asleep again before I could decide what to feel about that, which is the family way. We were three now. None of us had voted.

* * *

Dawn came gray and starless, as dawns did now, and with it came the thing I would spend the rest of this story being grateful for and terrified of in equal measure.

Pip was standing at the mouth of the hollow, facing north, absolutely still, listening with their whole body.

"It stopped," they said, when I joined them.

"What stopped?"

"The crying. The wood stopped crying." Pip turned, and their brown eyes were wide and bright with something I had no name for yet — awe, maybe, or warning; in a child who hears the world's grief, I suspect the two run together. "Just now. While it was still dark. It stopped crying, and it said — it said, *they're coming. The ones who laugh in the king's colors. Eleven of them. And behind them, wolves on the road, wearing two kinds of uniform.*"

Behind me, I heard August set down the porridge pot very carefully, the way men do when their hands want to be doing something else.

"Pip," he said. "Which road?"

"Ours," said the child, and smiled, sweet as spring, and utterly ruined the morning. "They'll be here by midday."
