## 6. Wolves of Both Realms

There's an old smuggler's saying: *when the road goes quiet, the road is listening.* I'd said it a hundred times to green runners who flinched at every owl. I'd never once believed it applied to me.

Three days out of Harrowgate, the high forest went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with owls.

No birdsong. No wind in the pines, though the branches above us swayed. Even Pip had stopped chattering — and Pip, as far as I could tell, had been built without an off switch, a small ragged engine of questions that ran on acorns and other people's patience. Now she walked between Wren and me with her hand pressed flat against the nearest tree trunk, dragging it along the bark like she was reading braille.

"She says they're coming," Pip announced.

"Who's *they*?" Wren asked. She'd taken to deferring to the child faster than I liked, mostly because it saved her from deferring to me.

Pip frowned, listening to something none of us could hear. "The ones with iron hearts. Two kinds. They're both angry. One kind is angrier."

"Wonderful," I said. "Both realms' border-guards, and one side is having a worse day than the other." I'd been expecting this since Harrowgate. A town burns its suspicion into you; a garrison writes your description on paper and sends it riding in four directions. I was a known face on both sides of the line — known as a smuggler to the Crown, known as a Crown collaborator to the Thornwarden clans beyond the pass. The wards died, the stars went out, and every soldier within fifty miles needed someone to hang it on. Someone with my face fit nicely.

Wren adjusted the strap of her satchel for perhaps the two-hundredth time that morning. The woman fidgeted like a cat in a sack. "How close?"

"If the trees are right? Close enough that we should stop talking about them." I turned off the deer trail we'd been following and cut upslope through the bracken, toward a granite spine I remembered from years back — a ridge called the Old Man's Teeth, all broken stone and scree, where a dozen people could vanish for a week if they knew what they were doing. I knew. It was one of the few things left I was sure of.

We climbed for an hour. Pip kept her hand on the trees as long as the trees lasted, then pressed it against the stone instead, which apparently worked just as well. Wren wheezed. Scholars, in my experience, are built for chairs.

It was near dusk when I saw the smoke.

Not campfire smoke — too thin, too disciplined. Signal smoke, three columns of it rising from the valley below in the old Thornwarden pattern: *prey sighted, closing.* And answering it, from the north, a single red flare of Crown powder. The clans didn't use powder. The clans used horn calls, and a moment later I heard those too, low and mournful, carrying up the valley like something grieving.

"They've found each other," Wren said quietly. "The two hunting parties."

"They've found each other," I agreed, "and decided that catching us matters more than shooting each other. That's new. That's very new." Borders meant something once. Men who'd spent ten years glaring across a ward-line did not suddenly join forces unless someone above them had told them the borders no longer mattered — or unless whatever killed the wards had frightened both realms down to their boots. Fear makes strange bedfellows. Fear with a budget makes stranger ones.

Pip tugged my sleeve. Her eyes were enormous in her small grimy face. "August. The angry ones are coming up our path."

* * *

I'll give the Thornwardens this: they were quiet. Six of them came over the Teeth from the south while I was watching the north slope, and if Pip hadn't grabbed my wrist and hissed — actually hissed, like a kettle — I might have lost us the whole game before it started.

As it was, I got Wren and Pip behind a fallen slab of granite and stepped out into the open with my hands visible and my sword still sheathed, because there's a language to these things, and the first word is always *I'm not here to bleed you.*

The six spread out along the ridgeline with the ease of people who'd done this on this exact rock before. Clan-marked leathers, ash-grey cloaks, faces I half-knew under their hoods. Their leader pushed back his hood and I felt my stomach drop through the mountain.

"Hale," I said. "Corwin Hale. You're a long way from the Salt Ford patrols."

"August Brightwater." He said my name the way you'd say the name of a disease. He was maybe thirty, broad as a door, and he'd been a boy of nineteen the last time I'd run him a cargo of fever-bark past a Crown checkpoint, laughing the whole way. "They said it was you. I told them no. I told them Brightwater wouldn't do this."

"And yet here I am, allegedly having done *this*. Which is what, exactly? In case anyone's keeping score."

"The wards, Brightwater." His knuckles were white on his spear-shaft. "Fifty miles of wards, dead in a night. Our dead keepers' stones split like kindling. And the first man seen moving on the old roads after — a swordspeaker, no less, an oath-bound man walking free with the marks still on him — is *you*."

"I know how it looks."

"Do you?" He laughed, and it wasn't a nice sound. "Do you know what it's like out there? Children asking why the stars went away. Keepers cutting themselves open trying to feed power into stones that won't hold it. My mother's village has wolves in the streets now, real wolves, because whatever held them back is gone. And the Crown sends soldiers, and the council sends hunters, and everyone agrees on one thing — that somewhere there's a traitor, and his name is August Brightwater."

Behind me, Wren made a small sound. I didn't turn around. Turning around would have confirmed everything.

"Corwin." I kept my voice level. "I guided a caravan through the passes that night. I watched the stars go out same as you. Whatever unmade those wards did it from inside, deliberate, and I'm chasing it the same as everyone else — except I'm the only one chasing it in the right direction."

"The right direction being *away*."

"The right direction being toward the Weeping Wood, which is where I'd bet my last coin this all leads. Come with me if you want. Or let me pass. Or—" I sighed, because there was no good third option and we both knew it. "Or don't."

He looked at me for a long moment. Behind the anger there was something worse: grief, and under the grief, doubt. He *had* known me. That was the tragedy of being known — it buys you seconds you can't afford.

"Heard the Crown's got a party on your trail too," he said finally. "Under Ferro. Daine Ferro. You remember her."

My chest did something complicated. "I remember her."

"They linked up at the ford yesterday. Shared water, shared maps. First time in living memory." His mouth twisted. "That's what you've done, Brightwater. Made the two bitterest enemies on this border hold hands. Whatever you're chasing, you've already caught it, and it's wearing your face."

Then he raised his spear, and the conversation was over.

I don't love fighting. People think a swordspeaker loves fighting the way a fisherman loves drowning, but no — the oaths aren't about violence, they're about precision, about speaking the blade into the shape of the moment. Still. Six against one on broken ground, with a scholar and a child behind me, is not a moment I wanted shaped.

"Pip," I said quietly. "Take Wren and go east along the ridge. Don't stop for anything with feathers."

"You're going to do the thing," Pip said. Not a question. There was a note in her voice I couldn't read — excitement, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes.

"I'm going to do the thing."

Hale came first, spear low, and two flankers with him while the others circled wide. Good tactics. Textbook. I drew, and spoke the first word, and the world went narrow and bright.

Swordspeaking is hard to explain to people who've only seen the street version. Every oath-word reshapes what the blade is *for*. *Hold* makes it immovable. *Part* makes it find gaps. *Return* — well. I spoke *return*, and when Hale's thrust came I wasn't where it landed, and my flat took him across the wrist hard enough to numb his sword-hand for a week. He grunted, adjusted, came again. The flankers tried the pincer. I gave them the ridge's own scree to slip on and one of them went down swearing in three dialects.

I could have hurt them. I want that understood, by whoever reads this, if anyone does. I could have hurt all six of them badly and been over the far side of the Teeth before they stopped moaning. The oaths would have allowed it; the oaths allow most things, that was always the order's dirty secret. But these were Corwin Hale's people, my people, the border-folk whose roads I'd kept and whose children I'd carried on my shoulders through flooded fords. So I fought like a man defending a reputation rather than taking a life, and it cost me, and it nearly cost me everything, because while I was busy being merciful to the south, the north arrived.

A whistle cut the dusk. Crown-issue, two-tone. And then boots on the northern scree, and lantern-light, and a voice I knew better than my own reflection.

"Drop it, Brightwater!"

Daine Ferro stood twenty feet away with a crossbow leveled at my heart, and behind her eight royal regulars in winter greys, and beside *them*, sharing a map by lantern-light like old friends, three Thornwarden hunters with clan-marks on their cheeks. Wolves of both realms, running in one pack.

Some nights the world hands you a joke so perfect you almost admire it.

* * *

"Daine Ferro," I said. "Last I heard you were garrison-sergeant at Colder's Mill, bullying recruits into polishing their boot-buckles."

"Last I heard *you* were a legend of the roads." She didn't lower the crossbow. Of course she didn't. Daine never lowered anything in her life except her standards for me, and she'd lowered those years ago, and look where it got her: standing on a freezing ridge at dusk, pointing a weapon at the only person she'd ever trusted farther than she could throw a horse. "And now you're the most wanted man on either side of the line. Congratulations. Truly. The promotion suits you."

"You know him, Sergeant?" One of the Crown officers — a lieutenant with soft hands and a hard voice — glanced at her sidelong.

"I know him," Daine said. Flat as slate.

"Then you know what he's capable of."

"Better than you ever will, sir."

I took a slow breath. The Thornwardens were regrouping below — Hale bleeding from a split lip, his eyes furious and wet. The Crown regulars were spreading into firing line with the mechanical calm of men who'd done this to smugglers a hundred times. And Daine's crossbow bolt had not moved one inch from my sternum, steady as a drawn line on a surveyor's chart.

"Daine," I said. "The wards didn't fall. They were unmade. From inside, deliberately, over months — moved along the smuggler roads like freight. I've seen the markings. I'm not running *from* this. I'm the only one running *toward* it."

"Everyone says something, August." But her voice cracked on the second word, just slightly, just enough. "The garrison at Harrowgate says the smugglers did it. The clans say the Crown did it. The Crown says the clans. Everyone's got a story and every story ends with somebody swinging."

"What does *your* story say? You've known me eleven years. You carried me half a mile on your back when the Mirefoot fever took my legs out from under me, and I never once asked you why, because I knew the answer. So ask yourself the question now. Look at me. On this ridge. With *them*—" I nodded at Hale's people, "—and tell me what story fits."

"Traitors make the best liars," the lieutenant said. "Sergeant. Your orders are to take him alive. Fire if he runs."

Something moved behind Daine's eyes. I've seen soldiers weigh duty against conscience before; it looks like a woman standing very still while two large animals fight inside her. She'd spent her whole life being the uniform. I'd spent mine learning what the uniform covered. And here was the whole rotten arithmetic of the border laid bare: her orders against her knowing, her oath against her eye.

She looked at me. Eleven years passed between us in about three seconds.

Then she shifted her aim — six inches left — and put her bolt through the lantern.

Darkness dropped on the ridge like a lid. I was already moving; so was she. I heard her shout — "*Scatter, they're on the rocks!*" — loud and commanding and entirely false, sending her own regulars blundering into scree and shadow. I heard Hale bellow something in clan-speech, and his hunters peel away into the dark rather than fire blind at Crown soldiers. Chaos is a smuggler's oldest friend. We shook hands and parted ways.

I found Wren and Pip huddled in the lee of the granite, Pip's hand flat against the stone, whispering to it like it was a nervous horse. I hauled them both up and we went east along the ridge in the dark, fast and low, Wren gasping, Pip silent as a moth.

Behind us: shouting, lanterns relit, the lieutenant's voice cracking with fury — and one voice, Daine's, ringing clear over all of it: *"They went south! South slope, MOVE!"*

Sending them wrong. Sending them wrong at the top of her lungs.

We ran until the ridge became forest again and the forest became a steep fern-choked ravine I remembered from leaner years, and there we stopped, all three of us bent double, breathing steam.

"That," Wren managed eventually, "was the sergeant. The one you mentioned. The one whose letter you keep in your coat."

"I don't keep a letter."

"You keep a letter with a pressed mountain-flower in it that you think nobody knows about."

"*Tansy,*" I said, with dignity, "is a practical keepsake. Medicinal."

"She shot her own lantern, August." Wren's voice had gone strange — not mocking anymore. Wondering. "She threw away her commission. Do you understand what happens to a Crown sergeant who loses a prisoner like that? Who fires wild in the dark? At best a demotion and a court inquiry. At best."

I stared back the way we'd come. Far off, faint, the search parties were still crashing around the southern slopes, chasing Daine's beautiful lie.

"No," I said slowly. "At worst, nothing. At worst, she finds us again tomorrow, alone, without her pack, and pretends she's still hunting us." Because I'd watched her shoot that lantern, and I'd seen where her feet went after — not back toward her line. Sideways. Toward ours. Some choices you make with your mind; hers had been made with her boots, and her boots had always been honest.

Pip tugged my sleeve. "Someone's coming," she whispered. Then, tilting her head, listening to the trees: "One someone. Iron heart. But it's — " she frowned, hunting for the words, " — it's *cracked*. Cracked open. Like ours."

We waited in the fern-brake with my sword drawn and Wren clutching a rock like it was going to argue on her behalf, until footsteps came down the ravine — uneven, unhurried, deliberately loud. Deliberately loud, from a soldier, means *don't stab me.*

Daine Ferro stepped into the moonlight with her crossbow slung and her sergeant's badge torn off and hanging loose in her fist like something she'd pulled out of her own chest.

"Before you say anything," she said, "I want it on record that this is the stupidest thing I have ever done, and I once wrestled a mule across the Sorrel River to win a bet."

"Noted," I said.

"And I'm not defecting. I'm *investigating*. There's a difference. If it turns out you're the traitor after all, I'm arresting you myself, and I'll enjoy it."

"Also noted."

Her eyes found mine in the dark, and all the iron in them had rust all through it now, and underneath the rust, the same stubborn girl who'd carried a fevered smuggler on her back because leaving him was unthinkable. "Eleven years, August. You'd better be worth eleven years."

"I'm worth eleven years and a pressed flower."

Wren made a noise that was suspiciously like a laugh being strangled at birth. Pip simply took Daine's hand as naturally as if she'd been holding it for years, and pointed up the ravine, east, where the dark deepened toward the old high country.

"That way," Pip said. "The trees want to show us something. There's a place where the wood remembers out loud. It's sad there. But it talks."

Daine looked at me. "Does she do that?"

"Constantly," I said. "Come on, Sergeant. Walk with the wolves."

And somewhere behind us, in the ruined shrine-country under the dead stars, the Weeping Wood began, very softly, to speak.
