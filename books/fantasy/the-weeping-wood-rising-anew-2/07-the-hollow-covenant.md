## 7. The Hollow Covenant

The shrine found us before we found it, which is the way of shrines and other things that want to be discovered.

We'd been walking two days since the waystation — two days of Pip saying strange things in a voice that wasn't entirely a child's, and August pretending not to notice the way I flinched each time, and Daine marching behind us like a woman rehearsing all the reasons she hadn't deserted yet. The passes had gone quiet in a way that felt less like peace and more like held breath. Even the smuggler cairns had stopped. August said that meant we were off his maps now, and he said it with the particular pride of a man announcing he has finally run out of bad ideas.

Then the trees thinned, and there it was: a shrine folded into a hollow of grey stone, its roof fallen inward like a cupped hand that had given up holding anything. Moss ate the walls. A lintel carved with interlocking rings — human knots on one side, fae spirals on the other, meeting in the middle in a pattern no single craft could have made alone — hung crooked over a doorway full of shadow.

Pip stopped at the treeline and wouldn't come closer.

"Pip?" I knelt. "It's just an old shrine."

"It's *listening*," Pip said, in the wood's voice, the one that made my teeth ache, and then blinked, small again. "Sorry. It says — it says the floor remembers. It wants someone to read it out loud before it forgets how to say itself."

I looked at August. August looked at me. This had become our entire method of governance: two liars exchanging glances until someone volunteered.

"Well," I said. "I am professionally qualified to read old things out loud."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a ward-scholar for the Royal Survey: you spend years learning to read inscriptions, and almost none of them are interesting. Ninety percent of ward-work is tax law written in old High Thorneic. Boundary clauses. Tithe schedules. Which lord owes which grove three baskets of hazelnuts every solstice. I used to joke that if the border ever fell, it would be because somebody misfiled a basket count.

I was joking. That's the thing. I'd spent so long translating tedium that when the actual dangerous text appeared in front of me, some traitorous part of my brain still expected it to be boring.

The shrine floor was the problem. The main chamber had sunk — or been built over something older — and beneath the collapsed flagstones lay a second floor, older stone, densely carved. August pried up slabs with his sword while Daine held a lantern and pretended she wasn't curious, and Pip stood at the doorway the whole time, relaying commentary from a tree somewhere in the dark.

"The floor is nervous," Pip reported.

"Tell it we're nervous too," August said. "Misery loves company."

"It doesn't know that word. It knows *hollow*. It keeps saying hollow." Pip frowned. "Like when you bite bread and there's no bread in the middle."

I got down on my knees in the dust and began to translate.

The script was Old Concordant — pre-Sundering, pre-anything they let scholars touch without three permits and a chaperone. My hands shook. Not fear, exactly. Something closer to stage fright. Because I recognized the hand. Not the specific scribe — but the *school* of it. The formal, careful lettering of the original covenant records, the ones locked in the Survey vaults that I had catalogued weaknesses for. I had studied copies of copies of this language my entire career, and here it was in the flesh, under a ruined shrine fifty miles inside Thornfell, where no royal scholar was supposed to have ever walked.

"Read it," August said quietly. He had that stillness he got sometimes, the swordspeaker stillness, like a drawn blade deciding where to point.

So I did. Aloud, stumbling, correcting myself, while Daine held the light steady and Pip whispered along in a tongue that should have been dead but apparently had just been waiting for someone to ask.

*In the year the realms were sundered by war past counting, the emissaries of crown and court came to the Weeping Wood and asked the wood to stand surety for their peace.*

That part matched the official histories. Fine. Standard surety-formula. I kept going.

*And the wood refused, saying: a covenant sealed upon a living thing binds the living thing, and your war is not done with killing.*

My mouth went dry.

"And then?" August asked.

"I'm getting there." I wasn't stalling. I was *terrified*, which looks similar. I traced the next lines twice to be sure of the grammar, because grammar was the last shield I had left.

*And the emissaries despaired, for neither realm would yield. And the eldest of them — the name is broken here, the stone's chipped — proposed a deception. They told the wood that the rite would bind only the boundary stones. And the wood consented to this lesser thing, opening its heart-root for the working.*

*Daine,* I thought absurdly, *your lantern's shaking.* But it was my voice shaking. Hers was steady as a wall.

*And when the heart-root lay open, they drove the covenant into it whole — every clause of the long peace, every debt and tithe and truce, all of it — into the living wood, which had agreed to carry only stones. The wood cried out. But a covenant spoken cannot be unsaid, and a lie told to obtain consent poisons the thing consented to.*

I stopped.

"Keep reading," August said. Not unkindly. Like a man asking a surgeon to keep cutting because the knife's already in.

*Thus the peace of the realms was founded upon theft. Thus the wood bears a weight it never chose, and weeps sap where it should bear fruit, and its grief rises each night into the sky as — *

I couldn't finish the sentence. Not because the grammar failed me. Because the word was impossible.

"As what?" Daine demanded. "As what?"

"*Stars,*" I said. "Its grief rises into the sky as stars. That's — " I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and cracked. "That's not poetry. That's a claim. That's the inscription claiming that the stars over Thornfell are the Weeping Wood's grief, given light."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

Then August said, very evenly, "The night the wards died, the stars went out first. All of them, within an hour. Before the wards fell."

"I know," I said. "I was in Harrowgate. I saw."

"So the wood stopped grieving." His jaw worked. "Or the wood started dying. Either way—"

"Either way the sky noticed," Daine finished flatly. She set the lantern down and sat on a fallen slab, hard, like her legs had resigned without consulting her. "I grew up on stories about the Sundering. Everyone did. The brave emissaries, the wise wood, the blessed peace. There's a festival. There's a *festival*, with cakes shaped like leaves."

"There's always a festival," I said. "Festivals are how you get people to stop asking questions. Trust me. I've filed the paperwork."

* * *

Here is the confession I haven't made yet, and since we're apparently doing honesty in ruins now:

When Wren Calloway catalogued the Thornfell wards for the Royal Survey — when she measured their resonance, mapped their anchor-points, noted which ones were "fatigued" and which were "anomalous" — she assumed she was doing maintenance science. When her supervisor asked her to cross-reference the weak points against trade routes, she assumed logistics. When he asked her to write the summary report herself, in her own neat hand, because his eyes were bad — she was *flattered*. Do you understand? She was twenty-two years old and flattered that an important man trusted her with important work.

She didn't learn what the report fed into until the night the sky went black. And even then, even standing in Harrowgate with a mob shouting about traitor scholars, some cowardly corner of her mind kept insisting: *you measured things. Measuring isn't a crime. Someone else decided what the numbers meant.*

But I wrote the summary. In my own hand. My handwriting is on the document, wherever it is, and the wards are dead, and a child at a doorway is telling me that a floor remembers being hollow, and there is no version of the arithmetic where I come out innocent.

August caught me staring at nothing. "You've gone somewhere," he said.

"Nowhere pleasant."

"Those are usually the true places." He sat down beside me on the cold flagstones, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched mine. Below us, half the old covenant floor still lay buried; above us, through the broken roof, a sky with no stars in it pressed down like a lid. "Say it anyway."

"Why? So you can decide I'm the enemy after all? You saved me from a mob, August. That buys a person a lot of benefit of the doubt, and I've been spending it shamelessly, and I keep waiting for the bill."

"Not from me." He turned a small stone over in his fingers. "From yourself, maybe. I know how that works. Fifteen years I told myself the order's silences were discipline. Discipline sounded noble. It took me until this week to hear it as what it was."

"What was it?"

"Fear wearing armor." He glanced at me. "You measured the wards. Did you break them?"

"No. But I drew the map someone else used to do it. I just didn't know that's what the map was for."

"And now you do." He nodded slowly. "Welcome to the order. Membership's free. The dues are terrible."

Despite everything — despite the dead sky and the poisoned covenant and the child listening to floors — I laughed. A real one, short and startled, like a bird flushed from cover.

"You're saying I'm a swordspeaker now."

"I'm saying everyone who finds out what the peace actually cost becomes one. That's the whole initiation. Finding out." He stood, offered me his hand, and pulled me up. "Come on. Let's see what else the floor remembers. Better we hear it than the Crown does first."

* * *

We worked by lamplight until well past dark, and what the floor remembered reshaped everything I thought I knew about the last week — possibly the last century.

Because the inscription didn't end with the crime. It continued. It was, gods help me, a *record*, maintained across generations, added to in different hands: human letters and fae spirals alternating down centuries like two people taking turns keeping a diary neither was allowed to show the other.

The later entries were the damning ones.

*Year 412 of the Long Truce: the wood's weeping worsens. The sap-runs reach the boundary stones. The wards drink of the sap and grow strange.*

*Year 690: the court sends envoys to confess the founding lie to the crown. The crown refuses audience. Confession, the crown's ministers write, would invalidate the covenant retroactively and plunge both realms back into war. Better a poisoned peace than an honest war.*

*Year 733: the wood begins withdrawing. Its dreams darken. Travelers speak of a winter that follows the tree-line south.*

*Year 801 — the last entry, in a shaky, hurried hand: the wood will die within a generation unless the lie is spoken aloud by both realms together at the heart-tree. Both realms refuse. Both realms have too much invested in the story they tell their children. We hide this record where only the desperate will find it. If you are reading this, be less cowardly than we were.*

"Eight hundred years," Daine said. She'd been silent so long I'd forgotten she was there. "Eight hundred years, and both sides knew, and both sides decided a lie was cheaper than the truth, and nobody — " Her fist closed. "I marched that border for six years. I froze on those walls for a *lie*, and worse than a lie — for a lie everyone in charge *knew* was a lie."

"That's why the wards fell so completely," I heard myself say, the scholar in me grabbing hold of the one solid fact like a drowning sailor grabbing a spar, because facts were easier than feelings. "Don't you see? The wards weren't built *on* the covenant — they were built *out of* it. They drank the wood's sap, the entry says so. They were the covenant's capillaries. When the wood began to fail, everything downstream failed. Whoever unmade the wards last month — they didn't destroy anything. They just — cut the last thread of a garment that was already rotting."

"Then who did it?" August asked. "Someone still aimed the knife."

"Maybe. Or maybe someone just opened the door on a house that was already falling down and let gravity take the credit." I rubbed my eyes. "Either way — the sabotage, the smuggling routes, whatever moved through the passes these last months — that's a symptom. August, don't you understand what this means? Everyone's hunting whoever broke the wards. The Crown, the court, Vess's people, whoever. But the wards were already dying. This was never just sabotage. The sabotage is a *symptom* of something eight hundred years deep. Somebody might have hastened the end, but the sickness — "

"The sickness is the peace itself," Pip said.

We all turned. The child had finally crossed the threshold, drawn by something, and stood in the doorway with the starless night behind them and the lamplight catching tears on their face that Pip themselves didn't seem aware of.

"Hearts are heavy," Pip said, in the doubled voice. "Hearts are heavy and no one asks to help carry. The floor says: tell them the floor is sorry. The floor says: we hid it because we hoped no one would need it." A pause, and then Pip's own small voice, wobbling: "The tree is really sad. It's been sad my whole life. It's been sad longer than anyone's whole life."

Daine crossed the room and picked the child up — awkwardly, like a soldier handling something explosive and precious — and Pip put their head against her shoulder pauldron and stayed there.

Nobody spoke for a while. Outside, wind moved through the trees, and for the first time I heard what Pip must have been hearing all along: not wind at all, but something rhythmic and vast and sorrowful, like breathing, like weeping, like a heartbeat slowed to the tempo of centuries.

* * *

We camped in the shrine that night, because none of us could stomach walking away from it. It seemed rude, somehow, to learn a place's secrets and then leave before dawn without so much as a thank-you.

I couldn't sleep. I lay on my bedroll under the broken roof, looking up at a sky with nothing in it, doing what I do best: organizing. Cataloguing. Turning horror into bullet points because bullet points can't cry.

One: the covenant binding our realms was founded on a deliberate lie told to a living being. Two: the lie has been poisoning the wood — and through the wood, the wards, the seasons, the sky — for eight hundred years. Three: both governments have known for most of that time and chose concealment. Four: the cure, per the record, requires both realms to confess the founding sacrifice aloud, together, at the heart-tree. Five: neither realm will ever agree to do that voluntarily, because confession means admitting the peace is illegitimate, and no crown surrenders its founding story while it still has an army.

Conclusion, arrived at around the hour when night is darkest and honesty gets aggressive: nobody was going to fix this from a throne. Which meant the four of us — a disgraced scholar, an oath-bound smuggler, a deserter with a child on her shoulder, and a kid who spoke for trees — were, functionally speaking, the most important diplomatic mission in eight hundred years.

Gods help us all.

I rolled over and found August awake too, sitting against a pillar with his sword across his knees, watching the dark the way soldiers watch it.

"Can't sleep either?" I whispered.

"I'm listening to Pip breathe. Making sure it stays ordinary." A pause. "You're composing something over there. I can hear the ink drying."

"A list."

"Of what?"

"Of everything that's true, now, that wasn't true yesterday." I sat up, hugging my knees. "It's a shorter list than I expected. Mostly it's just one item, repeated."

"Which is?"

"That the world's been lying to us politely for eight hundred years, and the polite lies are what killed the stars." I looked at him. "Your oath, August. Your order. All of it — it was built on top of this. On keeping this quiet."

His silence stretched long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice was low and stripped bare.

"I know. I've known since the second entry. Every rule Vess ever taught me — speak truth, guard the border, serve the covenant — every one of them was a wall around this floor." He looked down at the buried inscription as if he could see it through the stone. "Tomorrow we go find Vess. He'll know what to make of this. He taught me that the order's first duty was the covenant's health." A bitter ghost of a smile. "I used to think that meant keeping it strong. Now I'm wondering if it meant keeping it quiet."

"And if it did?"

"Then tomorrow," August said, "the last swordspeaker in Thornfell walks into his teacher's house and asks him a question that breaks everything. And either Vess gives me the truth—" he rose, sheathed his sword, and looked toward the north, toward whatever waited for us beyond the passes, "—or I finally learn why the order really died."

Above us, through the broken roof, the starless dark pressed close. Somewhere far to the north, I imagined — I *felt*, the way Pip must feel it — a great tree weeping into a sky that had forgotten how to answer.

The floor had said: *be less cowardly than we were.*

Tomorrow we'd find out if any of us could manage it.
