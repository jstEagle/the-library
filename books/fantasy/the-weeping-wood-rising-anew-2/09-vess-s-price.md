## 9. Vess's Price

I made six miles before I let myself think about it, which for me is a kind of discipline. You learn, on smuggler roads, that thinking is something you do at camp, by the fire, with your boots off and your knife where your hand can find it. On the trail you just put one foot in front of the other and let the mountain do the worrying.

The mountain had plenty to worry about. The snow was coming down wrong — too dry, too fine, falling without wind, like ash from a fire burning somewhere above the sky itself. And every mile or so there was another dead ward-stone leaning out of the drifts, their script gone gray and dumb, and when I passed them the oath-mark under my collarbone gave that low ache it had been giving since the night the stars went out. Not burning anymore. More like a tooth that knows rain is coming.

Two scrolls against my body. One true, one forged, and both of them warm from riding against my skin like they knew what they were.

I stopped at the ruined waystation at Miller's Bend to wait out the worst of the light — no, that's not honest. I stopped because my hands were shaking, and a man carrying two lies across a mountain should not shake. I got a fire going small and smokeless, the way Vess taught me when I was nineteen and all knees and opinions, and I sat down to read the forged scroll properly for the first time.

It was beautiful work. That's the thing about Vess that people who only met him late in life never understood. They saw the shabby hall and the wine-stained tunic and the lectures that circled like gulls after a fishing boat, and they thought: *old man, past his prime*. They never saw the craft. The forgery was perfect — the same vellum age, the same iron-gall fade, the same little tremor in the final flourish that the last Grandmaster of my order had carried in his wrist for forty years because of an old break nobody ever wrote down. Except someone had written it down. Someone had studied that tremor long enough to weep it into ink.

Only one flaw, and it was a flaw of intent, not execution. A true covenant-scroll ends open. It ends with the binding words trailing into blankness, because a covenant isn't a finished thing — it's a promise still being kept. Vess's forgery ended closed. Full stop. Signed, sealed, *done*, the way a contract ends when both parties are satisfied and nothing more will ever be asked of anyone.

A lie, even a beautiful one, always tells on itself somewhere.

I was still staring at that period at the end of a thousand years of promise when I heard the crunch of boots in snow behind me, unhurried, and I didn't reach for my sword because there was exactly one person alive who walked that loudly on purpose.

"You took the north fork," Vess said, coming into the firelight with his hood back and snow in his white eyebrows. "The south fork has shelter at Graystone and you hate Graystone. Also I bribed the ferryman at Cold Ford, who would have sold you out to the Crown by tomorrow anyway, so really you should thank me twice."

"You're seventy years old."

"I'm seventy-one, and I know every shortcut on this mountain, most of which I cut myself before your father was whelped." He lowered himself onto the stone beside my fire with the groan of a man who had earned every one of those years honestly and intended to complain about all of them. He held his hands to the flames. "You read it, then."

"I read it."

"And?"

"And it's good," I said. "It's the best forgery I've ever seen, and I've seen forgeries that hanged men."

"*Forged* is such an ugly word," Vess said. "I prefer *restored*. The original text existed once — I based it on the Ashfall fragments, which are genuine, which I spent eleven years authenticating while the Royal Survey laughed at me. All I did was complete it. Fill in the ending the centuries ate." He smiled, and it was the old lecture-hall smile, the one that said *follow me here, student, follow me and be amazed.* "August. Listen to me now, because I'm only going to say this once and I'm old and the cold makes me ramble."

"I know how you get."

"The wood is dying. The wards are dead. The Crown is mustering three companies at Thornfell City and the realm-court below the mountain is starving in the dark, and between those two armies stands a swordspeaker with a true scroll he cannot use, because the true rite requires a confession that will unmake him." He turned his head and looked at me across the fire, and his eyes were sharp as they'd ever been, sharp as the day he found a guttersnipe kid with quick hands and told him he could be a weapon instead. "Or a swordspeaker with a completed covenant. One spoken line. One gesture. The wards rekindle, the winter breaks, the realms settle back into their long uneasy peace, and *no one has to say anything true at all.*"

"It wouldn't hold," I said. "A covenant sealed on a lie—"

"Held for a thousand years the first time!" His voice cracked out over the snow, and somewhere upslope a load of powder slumped off a pine bough. He caught himself, lowered his voice, leaned in. "What do you think the original was, boy? You read the fragments. You know what I know. The first covenant was *already* a beautiful lie — sealed over a sacrifice neither side would name. A thousand years of peace built on a sentence nobody spoke. I am offering you the same deal the founders took. The same deal every peace ever struck has taken. Lie beautifully, live long, keep the weather kind."

"And the wood?"

"The wood," Vess said, with the smallest flicker of contempt, "is a tree."

* * *

We sat with that for a while. The fire ticked. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled in a sky too cold for thunder, which is the sound the world makes lately when it disagrees with itself.

"You taught me the oaths," I said finally. "All of them. You stood me on that freezing practice yard when I was twelve and you made me speak the First Oath until my voice broke, and when it broke you made me start again. Do you remember what you told me?"

"I tell students many things."

"You told me: *the words don't bind the world, August. The words bind you. Everything else is commentary.*" I pulled the forged scroll out of my coat and laid it on the stone between us, face-up, its neat closed ending catching the firelight. "This is commentary."

"August—"

"No. My turn." Fifteen years I'd been waiting to say some version of this, and I'd imagined it angrier, and here it came quiet instead, which surprised us both. "When I was twenty-two, you sent me to Harrowgate to sit on the ward-scholar's bench for a season. Officially, to study. Really, to report on which stones were soft. I did it. I wrote the letters. I told myself it was maintenance, it was prudence, the order needed to know. Then the order died keeping its oath and I never asked what my letters were *for*, because asking meant knowing, and knowing meant I'd helped dig the grave." I looked at him. "Wren Calloway did the same work for the Survey and she's been sick with guilt about it since the night the stars went out. She's half your age and she's carrying it better than you are."

"Ah," Vess said softly. "The girl. Yes. Ferro's dispatches mentioned her." Something moved behind his eyes when he said *dispatches*, some small animal shifting its weight, and I was tired and grieving and I missed it. Remember that. Write it on your own heart if you're keeping score: the sharpest blade in the world is an old teacher's disappointment, and it cuts best when you're looking elsewhere.

"You want me to speak a lie into the heart of the Weeping Wood," I said, "because speaking the truth might cost me something."

"I want you to *survive*," Vess said, and here at last the anger fell away and underneath it was the thing I'd crossed a mountain dreading, which was love. Old, stubborn, badly maintained love, like everything else he owned. "I buried your masters, boy. I dug half the graves myself. I have watched every soul I trained go into the dark one at a time for an order that lied to them, and I will not dig yours. Take the scroll. Speak the line. Be alive in spring. Hate me if it helps — I've been hated by better students."

And here is the truth, since this whole miserable year has apparently decided to be about truth: I wanted it. There in the firelight, with the ash-snow falling and my friends three days south trusting me, some starved animal part of me looked at that beautiful closed ending and *wanted* it. One line and the winter breaks. One line and Daine stops flinching at her own uniform, and Pip stops waking up screaming in a language older than kingdoms, and Wren gets to stop being guilty, and I get to keep the mark under my collarbone and the name that comes with it and the comfortable story that I have been a good man doing a hard job.

That's what a comfortable falsehood is. Not a comfort. A theft. It steals the pain today and bills your people for it later, with interest, and the interest on a thousand-year lie is paid in stars.

"Vess," I said. "If I seal the wards on a forgery, they'll hold five years. Ten, if the wood is merciful, and it is dying precisely because nobody's been merciful to it. And in ten years there'll be another night like the one the stars went out, except next time Pip will be fourteen and standing closer to the blast. You aren't offering me survival. You're offering me the chance to hand my debt to a child." I picked up the forged scroll. "I'm sorry. I loved you before I loved almost anyone. But no."

For a moment — one heartbeat, maybe two — I swear his shoulders dropped with relief, the way a man's do when a long pain finally names itself. Then the pride came up over it like water over a deck, and the moment was gone, and he was Master Vess of the Swordspeakers again, and I had just become the student who failed.

"So that's it," he said. "Truth. At any price."

"At any price," I agreed, "which is why it's expensive. Cheap things everyone buys."

He stood, slowly, joints announcing each stage of the journey. He looked down at me for a long moment, and I waited for a curse, a blow, a disowning. What he did was worse. He adjusted my collar — the way he used to before examinations, before ceremonies, before every day that mattered — and tucked the true scroll flat against my ribs with two fingers, gentle as a nurse.

"Keep that one safe," he said. "You'll want proof, where you're going. Everyone wants proof, in the end." And he walked out into the ash-falling snow, north, back toward his empty hall, and he did not look back either, because apparently that was a lesson he'd taught us both.

* * *

I burned the forged scroll in the fire. It seemed polite. It had been a great deal of work, and whatever else Vess was, he was a craftsman, and craftsmen deserve funerals.

Then I sat a while longer, because my legs weren't ready to forgive me yet, and I tried to work out what bothered me about the goodbye. He'd taken it too easily, for one. He'd mentioned Ferro's dispatches, for two — Daine hadn't reported to anyone in weeks, which meant someone else was writing dispatches in her name, which meant Vess had sources inside the Crown's muster, which meant—

Which meant he hadn't come up a seventy-year-old man's mountain through an ashstorm to plead his case out of love. Love had gotten him as far as the door. But old men don't cross mountains for lost causes. They cross mountains for arrangements.

The oath-mark went hot under my collarbone. Not the ache. Hot, sudden, specific, the way it had burned the night the wards died — and I remembered then what I'd known for fifteen years and never said aloud: the mark doesn't warn me about danger to *me*. It warns me about broken faith. About oaths bending. About trust changing hands in the dark.

Vess had taught me that too, in another life. *The mark reads the heart, August, not the road.*

I kicked the fire dead and ran.

* * *

I came down off Miller's Bend with the light failing and cut the switchbacks two at a time, and it wasn't fast enough, because of course it wasn't; he'd had hours and I'd had a fireside chat. From the shoulder of the pass I could see the toll road below winding down toward the river flats, and I could see the bonfires.

Not refugee fires. Refugee fires huddle. These stood in ranks, spaced like soldiers' mess fires, a whole company of them strung along the ford where the north road met the river — and above them, on the signal tower at Cold Ford, a ward-lantern swung in the old garrison pattern I hadn't seen lit since before the stars went out. Three swings, pause, three swings. *Target sighted. Hold position.*

They weren't hunting me yet. They were waiting. Somebody had told them exactly where four travelers on three horses and a pony would come down out of the White Pass in two days' time, and somebody had drawn them a map with a smuggler's loving precision, because only a man who'd cut half those trails himself could have drawn it that well.

Three days south, Wren was teaching Pip ward-script in the dirt with a stick. Daine was sleeping in her armor with her sword unsheathed, the way deserters sleep. And I stood on the shoulder of the mountain in the falling ash with a true scroll against my ribs and no one left alive to sell me out, understanding at last that Vess hadn't offered me a choice at all.

He'd offered me the price. And when I refused to pay it, he'd found someone who would.

I turned south and started running again, because two days is a long time, and I had a family to reach before the bill came due.
