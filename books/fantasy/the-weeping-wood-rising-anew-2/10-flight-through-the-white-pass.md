## 10. Flight Through the White Pass

I've been a soldier for nineteen years, and in that time I learned exactly one universal truth about mountains: they don't care. Not about your warrant, not about your wounds, not about the little speech you rehearsed about how you were going to explain all this to your commanding officer once you caught them. The White Pass didn't care that I was Sergeant Daine Ferro of the Thornfell Border Guard, formerly, allegedly, and it certainly didn't care that I was leading three fugitives up its throat in the teeth of a storm that had opinions.

The storm came down off the peaks around midday, which by then meant nothing, because the stars had gone out and day was just a rumor the sky told sometimes. Grey light, grey snow, grey rock. We climbed anyway, because behind us there were royal hunters with dogs, and dogs don't care either, but at least they're honest about it.

"Tell me again," I said, hauling myself over a shelf of stone, "why we couldn't take the low road."

"Because the low road has forty soldiers on it," August said, from somewhere above me where he'd apparently materialized without breathing hard, because of course he had. Fifteen years of smuggling and swordspeaking had made him into something less a man than a weather pattern. He turned and offered me his hand. I ignored it, on principle, and he withdrew it without offense, which was one of maybe four things I liked about him so far.

"Forty soldiers," Wren said behind me, breathless, clutching her satchel of ward-notes like it contained her actual skeleton. "With a dispatch rider. Daine read the orders herself."

"I read the orders," I confirmed. "That's why I'm here freezing instead of there being warm." I didn't say the rest: *that's why I'm a traitor now.* I'd left that part back on the trail two days ago, along with my uniform buttons, which August had suggested I scatter so the dogs would lose the scent. It felt like burying myself alive in installments.

Pip said nothing. Pip had said very little since the waystation, except when the words weren't really theirs — when the wood spoke through them in that old dry voice that made the hair on my arms stand up under three layers of wool. Right now the child walked between Wren and August like a small determined ghost, snow gathering on their shoulders, eyes fixed upward toward the pass as though they could see something in the whiteout that the rest of us couldn't.

Maybe they could. That was the whole problem with Pip.

* * *

We sheltered that first night in a hollow beneath an overhang, backs to the stone, sharing body heat in a configuration that would have gotten any of us drummed out of any military in either realm. August took first watch because August always took first watch; I suspected he slept the way smugglers sleep, one ear open, half his mind counting exits.

I couldn't sleep anyway. I sat watching the storm erase our back-trail and did the arithmetic every deserter does. *Turn around now, catch them before the pass, claim you were tracking them alone.* The numbers almost worked. Almost. Then Pip stirred against Wren's shoulder and murmured something in that voice like wind through dead branches — *"the gate remembers"* — and Wren smoothed the child's hair without even waking fully, the way mothers do, the way I never learned to, and the arithmetic fell apart in my hands.

You can't un-hear a child speak with the voice of a murdered tree. Believe me. I checked.

"You're doing the sums," August said quietly. Not looking at me. Watching the white dark.

"What sums?"

"The ones where you go back." He said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "I did them for fifteen years. Every night. The ones where you give them what they want and everything goes back to normal."

"Did you ever run them?"

"No." A pause. "I just never stopped doing them. There's a difference." He fed a stick to our miserable little fire. "The sums stop eventually. Or you do."

Charming man. I pulled my cloak tighter and, somewhere around the third hour of the watch, without deciding to, I stopped doing the sums.

* * *

The second day was where the mountain started killing us in earnest.

The White Pass earned its name honestly — a knife-edge of limestone and ice where the trail narrowed to the width of a coffin and the wind came through sideways with intent to harm. I'd crossed it twice in my career, both times in summer, both times wondering who'd been mad enough to blaze it. Now I knew. Nobody sane blazed it. Somebody desperate did, and desperation is a fine mapmaker but a poor engineer.

Halfway along the spine, the drifts had welded themselves into a wall taller than a wagon. August stood before it for a long moment, and I watched him do something I'd seen him do only once before — in Harrowgate, when the mob had Wren cornered and he'd drawn steel. He went still in a particular way. Listening.

"That's wrong," he said.

"What's wrong?"

"The mountain." He pressed his bare palm flat against the snow-wall. His oath-mark, that faded brand on his wrist, had started glowing faintly — the first time I'd seen it do more than ache. "Mountains are quiet. This one's *holding its breath.*"

Wren came up beside him, squinting through the stinging white. "Could the wood be—"

"Pip," August called. Not loudly. The storm ate loud voices whole.

The child picked their way along the ledge to us, small face raw with cold, and looked at the wall the way you look at a locked door. Then they reached out and put their hand next to August's, tiny fingers splayed in the snow.

What happened next, I have tried to describe to myself perhaps forty times since, and I still haven't found words that fit right, because I'm a guard and guards get plain language or none.

The snow *opened.*

Not melted. Not collapsed. Opened — the way a hand opens, deliberately, fingers of drift curling back and apart until there was a corridor through the wall, smooth-walled, blue-lit, wide enough for us to walk abreast. Warm air breathed out of it. It smelled, impossibly, of sap and green growing things, of summer in a place where summer had no business existing.

Behind us, the storm howled like something cheated.

"Well," Wren whispered. "That's not on any survey."

"No map holds this," August said softly. He glanced at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen there before — not the smuggler's calculation, not the oath-bound grimness. Wonder. Bare, undefended wonder. "Daine. Your call, Sergeant. You know this pass better than any of us. Is there a route through here?"

I stared at the corridor. I knew this stretch of the White Pass the way I knew my own barracks — every switchback, every scree field, every place the mountain had swallowed a mule train over the years. And there was no corridor here. There was solid limestone and forty feet of engineered winter, and there was absolutely, categorically, no warm tunnel smelling of June.

"No," I said. My voice came out strange. "There's nothing here. There's never been anything here."

"Then I suppose," August said, stepping into the impossible passage, "we're the first people ever to walk it. Try to look dignified."

* * *

Inside, the world changed rules.

The walls of the corridor weren't quite snow and weren't quite stone — they had grain to them, like vast pale wood, and veins of amber light running through them slow as honey. Our footsteps made no echo. The storm's roar faded behind us until it was just a memory of anger. Pip walked ahead now, sure-footed, and the veins of light brightened gently wherever the child passed, dimming after, like candles being tended by someone patient.

Wren kept touching the walls and pulling her fingers back. "It's responding to them. To all of us, a little — look —" She held her palm near the surface, and a faint thread of amber gathered under it, curious. "But mostly to Pip. The wood's reaching. Through the child. Through—" she laughed, shaky, "—through whatever Pip actually is."

"And what do you think Pip actually is?" I asked.

She was quiet a moment. "Something the wood is trying to keep alive," she said finally. "Something it's spending itself to protect." Her voice cracked on the last word and she pretended it hadn't.

August brought up the rear. I noticed he kept checking back the way we'd come, and I noticed the corridor behind us closing gently, sealing itself, erasing our passage — the mountain tucking us in and locking the door. Any other time that would have terrified me. Trapped in a tunnel with no exit, following a child possessed by a tree. Instead I found myself thinking: *nothing else can follow us in here either.* For the first time since Harrowgate, since I'd knelt over those dead ward-stones and realized my own garrison's reports had helped break them, I wasn't listening for hounds.

"So," I said, because silence and I have a bad history, "this is normal, for you lot? Walking through the inside of a mountain's daydream?"

"Nothing about these people is normal," Wren said, "which is why I like them."

"You've known them nine days."

"Nine days is longer than I knew the Royal Survey, and I gave them five years."

Fair hit. I let it land. We walked on, and the amber light thickened until it was almost golden, and somewhere ahead water was dripping in a rhythm too regular to be accidental — drip, pause, drip — like a heartbeat learning patience.

Then Pip stopped.

The corridor ended in a chamber, round, high-vaulted, and at its center stood a thing that made my knees consider resigning: a sapling, thin as a shepherd's crook, growing straight up out of bare blue ice. Green leaves. Actual green leaves, in the death-month, in the White Pass, under a starless sky. It was small and it was stubborn and it glowed like a coal wrapped in silk, and every vein of light in the walls ran toward it, feeding it, the way streams run to the sea.

Around its base, pressed into the ice, were marks. Old ones. Wren knelt by them and went the color of whey.

"These are covenant-script," she breathed. "First-era. This is a waypoint. This was a *station* on the old covenant road, before the realms sealed it. The wood planted a heart here once. A seed of itself, way out here, past the wards, past everything — keeping a door open." She looked up at Pip, and her eyes were wet. "It's been waiting for someone who could hear it."

Pip laid their hand on the sapling's trunk, and the little tree shivered, and every leaf turned — slowly, all together, like a crowd turning to look at something hoped-for — toward the four of us.

And Pip spoke, in their own voice this time, small and hoarse and utterly certain:

"It says you're late. But it says — it says you came anyway. It says that counts."

I am not ashamed to report that I cried. I'm a little ashamed to admit it here, but I promised myself honesty, since honesty is apparently the whole cursed point of this quest. Nineteen years of service, of standing post in all weathers for oaths I now knew were built over a grave, and nobody had ever told me that coming anyway counts. The Crown never said it. The border never said it. A sapling in the ice said it, through a starving child, and it went through me like a spear through wet parchment.

August put his hand on my shoulder. Didn't say anything. Didn't need to. That was the moment, if I'm marking it precisely — not the defection at the river, not burning my uniform — that's when I stopped being the outsider guarding the fugitives and became one of the guarded, one of the family, whatever this ragged, lying, tree-whispering collection of criminals was.

A family. Gods help me. I'd had a regiment once. This was better and infinitely more frightening.

* * *

The sapling opened the far side of the chamber for us — another corridor, descending, and Wren worked out from the script that it would bring us out below the pass's kill-zone, past where the hunters and their dogs could follow, onto the southern approach to the Weeping Wood itself. Three days' travel saved. A week of dying avoided.

Before we left, August paused at the sapling. He crouched, the way you'd kneel at a shrine, and said quietly, "Thank you. When this is done — when the covenant stands again, if it stands — I'll come back and plant a forest around you. That's a promise. Not an oath." His mouth twisted. "Oaths are for things other people make you do. Promises are for things you choose."

The leaves turned toward him. All of them. And the amber light pulsed once, slow, like a nod.

We walked out of the mountain into falling snow and grey false dawn, and behind us I heard — I swear on my mother's grave I heard — the faint sound of the corridor sealing shut, gentle as a door closed by someone who loves you.

Ahead, down the long white slope, the trees began. Black firs at first, and then other shapes among them, bent and strange and glistening with sap that caught the lightless sky like tears catching candlelight. The Weeping Wood. The place all roads — smuggler roads, ward roads, covenant roads, and now ours — had been running toward since the night the stars went out.

And on the wind, threading through the storm's last growls, came a sound that stopped all four of us at once. Faint. Rhythmic. Wrong.

Drums.

Not ours. Not the wood's. Military cadence, disciplined, distant, and moving toward the same heartwood we were.

Wren counted beats under her breath, face draining. "That's not a patrol," she said. "That's an army."

August looked at Pip. Pip looked at the trees. And the trees, all together, all at once, began to weep faster.
