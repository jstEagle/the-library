## 11. The Court of Thorns Below

I have spent my adult life among documents, and I will tell you the first thing they teach you — or rather, the first thing you learn, because nobody teaches it, they just let you find out the way children learn not to touch stoves — is that every archive lies a little. Not maliciously. Archives lie the way rivers lie about their depth: smoothly, by omission, by what they choose to carry and what they let sink.

The Royal Survey's records of Thornfell contained eleven thousand pages on the border wards. Soil composition. Resonance frequencies. The precise angle at which ward-stones wept sap during high moon. What those eleven thousand pages did not contain was a single mention of what lay *beneath* the wards.

I had assumed that was an oversight.

Standing at the mouth of a staircase that had no business existing — a seam in the mountain's root that Pip had walked up to and simply pushed open, the way you'd push open your own front door — I revised my assumption. It was not an oversight. It was the largest omission in the history of scholarship, and I had helped maintain it, one carefully footnoted catalog entry at a time.

"Everyone's very quiet," Pip observed, in the tone of a child noting that the sky was up.

"We're being tactically silent," Daine said.

"You're being scared," Pip said. "It feels different."

August, who had been silent for the better part of an hour in the way that only August could be silent — not absent, but present so intensely that his silence had weight, like a held breath — crouched to the child's level. "Which is it for you?"

Pip considered this with the seriousness Pip brought to everything, which was considerable. "The wood says go down. The wood says it's been waiting so long it forgot how waiting works." A pause. "Also I'm a little scared."

"Good," August said. "Scared means you're paying attention." He looked up at me then, and Daine, and whatever he saw in our faces must have satisfied him, because he turned and started down.

I want to record, for the sake of any future scholar foolish enough to follow me, that the stairs went down for forty minutes. I counted, because counting is what I do when terror arrives; I count things until they become numbers instead of monsters. Forty minutes of stairs cut from living rock, except that "living" was doing a great deal of work in that sentence, because the rock was veined with wood — pale, fibrous channels running through stone as though a tree had decided to become a mountain and gotten most of the way there. Sap moved through those veins. I could see it moving, slow as honey in winter, faintly luminous.

"It's all one tree," I whispered. "Underneath everything. The whole range is—"

"Don't," Daine said quietly. "Don't narrate. Not here."

She was right, though I resented her for it. Some places you document. Some places you survive first and footnote later.

* * *

The court announced itself before we saw it — first by warmth, which after the White Pass felt like a physical blow, actual unfrozen air rising up the stairwell smelling of loam and something sweeter, resin maybe, or fruit going soft; then by light, a green-gold glow seeping around a bend ahead like dawn under a door; and finally by sound. Voices. Dozens of them, thin and reedy, and beneath the voices a low continuous moan that I did not at first recognize because I had only ever heard it from outside.

Then I recognized it, and my stomach dropped.

It was the sound the Weeping Wood made. The sound that gave the wood its name — trees bleeding sap from every wound, every seam, a forest that grieved audibly, ceaselessly, and had done so for three hundred years while human cartographers wrote *"acoustically notable"* in their margins. I had catalogued that sound. Frequency, duration, seasonal variance. I had never once asked myself what it sounded like *from inside*.

Answer: it sounded like a city crying in its sleep.

We came around the bend and I stopped counting things.

The cavern opened like a cathedral that geology had been drafting for ten thousand years and finally got right. Stalactites hung in ranks from a ceiling lost in green-gold haze, and threaded among them — growing *through* them, roots descending from some impossible canopy above — were trees. White-barked, silver-leafed, their branches holding up lamps of solidified sap that burned without flame. And filling the floor of that vast space, terraced down toward a dark pool at its center, was a city. Houses grown rather than built, shaped from living wood, their windows glowing. Bridges of woven branch. Terraces of garden beds that should have been overflowing and were instead gray stubble under frost-rime that had no business existing underground.

And people. Or. People-shaped. Tall, too tall, and slender past the point of anatomy, with skin like birch bark and hair that moved when there was no air moving it, colorless eyes catching the sap-light. Hundreds of them, huddled in the terraces, and every single one of them thin. Gaunt the way the refugees in Harrowgate had been gaunt, cheekbones standing out like architecture, hands like bundled twigs.

They were starving. Down here, under all that ancient power, under a ceiling full of light — they were starving, and they were cold, because the frost on their gardens didn't care whose side of a treaty they'd signed three centuries ago.

"They're *farming*," Daine breathed beside me. "They've been down here the whole time. Under the wards. Under—" She stopped, and I watched a career of border-guard certainty come apart behind her eyes like wet paper.

"The wards weren't keeping them out," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, small in all that space. "Or not only. The wards were..." I reached for the scholarly word and found it wouldn't come, so I used the true one instead. "The wards were a lid. On a wound."

Pip walked forward.

I want to be clear that none of us authorized this. Pip simply detached from our little frightened cluster the way a drop of water detaches from an eave, and walked out onto the lowest terrace, and every eye in that cavern — hundreds of colorless eyes — followed. A murmur ran through the court like wind through wheat. And Pip stood in the middle of it, small and ragged and unafraid in a way that made my chest hurt, and said, clearly, in a voice that was half a child's and half something older that made the sap-lamps flicker:

"The wood sent you. Finally. Finally, finally."

* * *

They took us to her, of course. There was never any question of anything else. When your entire civilization has been starving in the dark for months, and a child arrives speaking with the voice of the thing you worship, protocol bends fast.

Lady Serevan received us in a hall grown from a single bole — a chamber where the walls curved like the inside of a heart, ribbed with pale wood, lit by a chandelier of frozen sap that threw moving shadows across her face. She sat — or rather she *was seated*, but sitting seemed almost decorative for someone like her, as if gravity had negotiated special terms — on a throne that was less furniture than an outgrowth, wood spiraling up around her like cupped hands.

She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, which is to say: precisely, coldly, and with an undertow. Her face was long and grave, birch-smooth, and when she looked at us I felt catalogued. Turned over, examined, entered into a ledger I couldn't read. I knew that feeling intimately. I had spent years making other people feel it.

"So," she said. Her voice was low and resonant, a voice built for pronouncements, worn now at the edges like everyone else's voice down here. "The wood sends me four humans. A swordspeaker." Her gaze touched August, and something flickered there — recognition, and under the recognition, hatred so old it had gone smooth. "A guard of the Crown." Daine flinched; Serevan noted the flinch the way I'd have noted a variant manuscript reading. "A liar of the Survey."

My whole body went cold. "How—"

"I am old," Lady Serevan said, "and the wood remembers what passes over its roots. You walked the toll road at Harrowgate with survey-chalk in your pocket, girl. You measured our wounds and wrote down the numbers." She leaned forward, and the chandelier-light caught the hollows of her cheeks. "Do not look surprised. Do you imagine we don't know what your kind does? We have watched humans for three hundred years. We watched them build their wards on the bones of our covenant. We watched them forget us, then mythologize us, then forget us again. And now the wards fall, and do you know what your realms did?"

Nobody answered. She hadn't wanted an answer.

"They blamed smugglers," she said. "Small men, small crimes. Your Crown massed soldiers at the border to hunt *traffickers*, while the sky went starless and the world began to freeze. Because the truth requires looking downward, and humans have never once, in three hundred years, looked down." She rose. Up close she was taller than August, and thinner, and there was frost on the hem of her gown that I understood suddenly was not ornament. "So tell me why I should not seal this stair and let the ice take the surface, since the surface has already decided to abandon us."

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind, both at once.

And then August did the thing August does, the thing that I think is the actual reason the wood chose him, though he'd hate hearing me say it. He didn't argue. He didn't draw his sword or invoke his order or deliver the speech I could see assembling itself and dying in Daine's face. He just looked at Lady Serevan — at her frost-edged hem, her hollowed face, her throne of cupped dead wood — and said, quietly:

"How many of your people died this winter?"

The question landed in that heart-shaped room like a dropped glass.

Serevan's stillness changed quality. "That is not your concern."

"No," August agreed. "But I'm asking anyway. Because I've seen the refugee columns in the passes. Human dead, human starving. And now I've seen this. And I notice that neither of us wants to say the number out loud, because once it's said, it belongs to both sides, and blame gets complicated." He spread his empty hands. "I'm not here to defend what my realm did. I'm here because I think we did the same crime. I think we both broke something together, a long time ago, and we've each been pretending the other did it alone ever since. And I think the wood is dying of the pretense."

For a long moment Lady Serevan looked at him with those colorless eyes, and I watched — actually watched, the way I'd watch a difficult passage resolve — something move behind them. Not softening. Recognition. The particular, unwelcome recognition of meeting your own grief wearing a stranger's face.

"Four hundred and six," she said at last, very evenly. "Since the stars went out. Children first. They always go first; they're newest, least rooted." Her hand closed on the arm of her throne, and the wood creaked. "Now ask me how many died in the year your ancestors sealed the covenant. Ask me what was buried under your pretty border stones. Ask me—" and her composure cracked, just a hairline, just enough to show the molten thing underneath, "ask me why the wood *weeps*, swordspeaker. Three hundred years of weeping, and your scholars wrote down the *frequency*."

I felt that one land in my own chest, because of course she meant me. She didn't even know she meant me specifically, and she meant me exactly.

"I know," I heard myself say.

Her head turned to me.

"I catalogued the weeping," I said. My voice was shaking and I let it shake, because I was done performing steadiness; I had been performing steadiness since I was nineteen years old and a supervisor told me that objectivity meant never letting the subject matter. "Eleven thousand pages. I wrote two hundred of them myself. Frequency, duration, seasonal variance. I never asked what it was *for*. I never asked what it was *about*. And then the wards fell, and I found out that the weaknesses I documented — the ones I thought were just scholarship — had been copied out of my files and used to unmake the border, and I have been running ever since, from the mob in Harrowgate and from the Survey and mostly from—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "Mostly from the fact that I helped build the lid. I didn't know it was a lid. That doesn't matter. It doesn't not-matter either. Both are true, and I'm tired of choosing the comfortable one."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere above us, faintly, through miles of stone and root, I would have sworn I could hear the wood listening.

Lady Serevan studied me for a long time. Then, slowly, she sat back down — and it was strange, but the gesture looked less like regaining a throne and more like setting down something heavy.

"A liar of the Survey," she said, "who confesses unprompted. In my long life I have met perhaps five humans who did that, and I married none of them." Something passed across her face that in a human I'd have called wry. "Perhaps the wood knows its business after all."

"Pip says it usually does," August offered.

"Pip," said Lady Serevan, turning to the child with an expression I can only describe as a queen attempting to figure out what one does with a small ragged oracle, "says a great many things. Half of them in voices that predate my grandmother's grandmother." She exhaled, and the frost on her hem retreated a visible inch, which I suspected was involuntary and which frightened me more than her anger had. "Very well. You want truth, swordspeaker? Truth has a price, and the price is that it must be spoken *by both sides*, aloud, before witnesses, in the place where the lie was born. That is the old law. That is the only rite that ever worked. But understand what you are asking." She leaned forward again, and now there was nothing cold left in her face at all, only exhaustion and a ferocious, terrible hope. "Because when my people speak their part, your realm will learn what your founders did to make their precious peace. And when yours speaks, mine will hear the shape of our own guilt confirmed. Neither side has wanted this conversation for three hundred years. Entire institutions exist to prevent it. Your Crown is marching an army toward the wood as we speak — yes, we hear them through the roots, boots are loud — to burn the question shut forever."

Daine made a small sound. Serevan's eyes cut to her. "Something to add, guard of the Crown?"

Daine's jaw worked. I watched her decide — really decide, the hinges of it visible — and then she said, flatly: "There's a dispatch order. Scorched-earth contingency for the Weeping Wood. I thought it was theoretical. Standing orders always are, until they aren't."

"Then you understand," Serevan said softly, "that we are running out of time in a way your kind measures in days and mine measures in breaths." She straightened, and the frost crept back, and she was a queen again, but a queen holding out her hand across three hundred years of silence. "If you truly wish to open this stair between us, swordspeaker, there is a rite older than your order. Before the court, before both realms' witnesses, you will speak — all of it, every oath your masters kept and every one they broke, the whole rotten beautiful truth of the Swordspeakers' covenant. The wood will judge whether it is enough."

August went very still. I had learned his stillnesses by then, and this one was the deep kind — the kind that comes when the floor gives way and a man discovers what's underneath him.

"And if it isn't enough?" he asked.

"Then the wood keeps weeping," Lady Serevan said, "the ice keeps falling, and in a season there will be no one left on either side of the mountain to remember there was ever a difference." She folded her hands. "We begin at moonrise. I suggest you spend the hours deciding which truths you can afford — because the wood does not accept edited manuscripts."

I caught August's eye across the heart-shaped room. He looked the way I felt: terrified, lighter than he'd been in weeks, and about to do something irreversible.

"Between you and me," I murmured, as we were led away to the guest terraces, "I've never met an archive that accepted edited manuscripts either. It always comes out eventually. Everything does."

"Yes," August said. "That's rather the problem."

Above us, through miles of stone, the first tremor of the Crown's army reached the roots — faint, rhythmic, patient as a heartbeat.

Boots. Thousands of them. Coming down the mountain toward everything we'd just found.
