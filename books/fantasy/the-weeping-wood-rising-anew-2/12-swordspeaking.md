## 12. Swordspeaking

The court of thorns below Thornfell did not have a dungeon, which I counted as a point in its favor. It had a *guesting hollow*, which was the same thing with better acoustics.

They kept us there for two days. Or what I assumed were days — down here beneath the roots of the world, time moved like sap in January. Wren measured it by candle stubs she'd charmed into burning at honest intervals, because she was the sort of person who didn't trust a clock that might be lying to her, and I loved her a little for it. Pip slept curled against Daine's side like the guard was furniture the child had decided to keep. And Lady Serevan's people brought us food — pale mushrooms, dark honey, bread that tasted of bark and patience — and watched us eat with eyes like coins at the bottom of a well.

On the third candle-day, they came for me.

Not for all of us. For me. Two of the root-guard, tall as doorframes, their armor grown rather than forged — plates of living wood over muscle that creaked faintly when they moved, like old ships in calm water. They said nothing. They didn't need to. The whole hidden realm had been saying nothing at us for three days, a silence so complete it had texture, and every hour of it had been asking me the same question:

*What are you worth?*

Wren stood when they did. "August—"

"It's fine." It wasn't fine. My hands were steady anyway, which is a trick you learn on smuggling roads: your body can lie even when you can't. Fifteen years of practice. "If they wanted me dead I'd already be compost."

"That's not comforting."

"I wasn't going for comforting. I was going for accurate." I touched her shoulder on the way past — brief, clumsy, the way you touch people when you've spent fifteen years touching no one — and followed the trees out of the hollow.

Pip's voice chased me down the corridor, small and clear and wrong in a way that made the hair on my arms stand up: *"The sword must speak. The sword must speak or the wood forgets how to listen."*

Yeah, kid. Thanks. No pressure.

* * *

They brought me to the heart of the court, if a place without walls could be said to have a heart. The cavern opened vast as a buried sky, ribbed with root-vaults that arched overhead like the ribs of something sleeping. Fungi glowed along every surface in shades of blue and gold, and the light came up from below as much as above, so that everything seemed lit by its own small courage.

And the people. Court is too small a word. There were hundreds of them — the folk my grandmother's generation called the thornborn and stopped calling anything at all once the wards went up and convenient forgetting became policy. Tall ones and strange ones and ones shaped almost like men but not quite, the way a river is almost like a road. They filled the galleries of root and stone in tiers, silent as snowfall.

Lady Serevan sat above them on a throne that was less a seat than a stump — the sawn-off base of some enormous tree, polished by centuries of grief. She looked at me the way you look at a debt collector who has come to the wrong house: with courtesy stretched thin over contempt.

"August Brightwater," she said. Her voice carried without effort, the way water carries downhill. "Last swordspeaker. Last of an order that swore itself warden of the covenant between our peoples. Do you know what we called your order, below?"

"Tell me," I said, because it seemed rude to guess.

"The Liars' Shield." A ripple went through the galleries — not laughter. Recognition. "For five hundred years, whenever your realms needed a crime dressed as duty, a swordspeaker performed it. Whenever a border needed moving, a swordspeaker witnessed it. Your oaths were beautiful, man-of-iron. We could hear them ringing across the wards. Beautiful things, wrapped around rotten cores."

I'd been insulted by professionals. Kings' marshals, guild masters, one memorable duchess. None of it had landed like this, because none of them had been right.

"You're not wrong," I said.

The silence changed quality. Curiosity has a sound, even among the thornborn. It sounds like held breath.

"But before you bury me under it," I went on, "you should know what you're burying. All of it. Not the version my order taught me. Not the version I told myself for fifteen years. The whole ledger, debts and thefts both." I unslung my sword — Ashmark, my grandfather's blade, the last swordspeaker's blade — and laid it flat across my palms. "There's a rite for this. You may have heard of it. It requires an audience willing to listen, and I've finally found one."

Serevan's eyes narrowed. "The full speaking has not been performed in living memory. Even above, they do only fragments. Oaths of service. Oaths of office." Her gaze sharpened. "You would speak the *whole* rite? Knowing what it binds you to say?"

"That's rather the point, my lady."

"And knowing what it costs?"

"I've been paying installments for fifteen years," I said. "Might as well settle the account."

She studied me for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for, she found enough of it, because she lifted one hand — fingers long as winter branches — and the galleries settled.

"Then speak, swordspeaker. Let the wood judge what your truth is worth. It has been listening to lies for five hundred years. It knows the difference by now."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about swordspeaking: it isn't performance. It's plumbing. Every oath a speaker ever swears runs through the blade like water through a pipe, and the blade remembers. Ashmark remembered four generations of Brightwaters and every oath any of us ever took, and the rite required me to open the pipe end to end and let it all come through.

I set the blade point-down on the stone. I put my hands on the pommel the way Vess taught me — *flat palms, August, you're holding a friend's shoulder, not throttling a chicken* — and I spoke the opening words, the ones every child in the borderlands used to know:

*"Iron remembers. Wood remembers. Let what was sworn be spoken, and let what was spoken be true."*

The blade hummed. Not metaphorically — a low note rose out of the steel, and the blue glow of the fungi dimmed toward it, leaning in. Around the galleries, the thornborn went very still, in the way of people listening to something they'd been told was extinct.

"The First Oath," I said. "Sworn by Aldric Brightwater, in the year the wards rose. *I swear my voice to the border, my steel to the weak, and my word to the covenant between realms, until the stars go out or I do.*"

True. That one was true, and it rang true, a clean bell-tone that made several of the thornborn close their eyes. Good. Start with the true ones. Get the room on your side before you confess to grand arson.

"The Oath of Passage. *No traveler under my watch shall be turned away for blood or birth.*" Also true. Also clean. My grandfather had died enforcing that one, escorting a thornborn family back below during the Purging Years, and the story of it had been the spine of my childhood.

Then came the others.

"The Oath of Silence. *That which the order conceals, the speaker conceals; that which the order denies, the speaker denies.*"

It came out of me like a fishhook. I felt it tear on the way up. Because that oath was also true — I'd sworn it at nineteen with Vess's hand on my shoulder and pride in my chest like swallowed sunlight — and it was *rotten*, and the blade knew the difference. The note it gave back was ugly, a wire pulled taut, and I watched the thornborn flinch at the sound of it. They could hear it too. Of course they could. They'd been hearing that particular dissonance humming through the wards their entire lives.

"The Oath of the Quiet Border," I said, and now my mouth had gone dry, because this was the one. This was the one Vess had made me swear last, alone, in the cellar of a waystation that officially did not exist. "*I swear that the covenant is whole. I swear it though I see it failing. I swear that no word of its failing shall pass my lips while the order stands, and should the order fall, I swear the silence still.*"

Silence in the cavern so deep I could hear my own heartbeat, and underneath it, faint as a pulse through a wall, the slow groan of the Weeping Wood far above.

"Lady," I said, "do you want to know what your wards heard for the last century? Not lies. Lies would have been kinder. They heard *oaths*. Hundreds of them. Every speaker swearing the covenant whole, in good faith, with clean hearts — swearing a falsehood so faithfully that the wards took it for bedrock." My voice cracked on the next part, and I let it. "We didn't break your borders. We *bricked them up around a wound* and swore the wound wasn't there, and every one of us believed we were guarding the peace. That's what the Liars' Shield means. We weren't shields against lies. We were the lies' shield *against you*."

An old thornborn near the front — ancient, bent, skin like birchbark — made a sound. It might have been a sob. It might have been the oldest laugh in the world.

"The Oath of the Quiet Border," I repeated, "was false when spoken. And here is the cost of it, which the rite requires me to name: my masters knew the covenant was failing. Knew the wood was starving. Knew the heart-tree had been wounded at the sealing — *knew*, and hid it, and taught every speaker after them to hide it, and built the wards not to protect the peace but to protect the *story* of the peace. And I—" my hands were shaking on the pommel now, and I didn't fight it, "—I enforced those wards for fifteen years. I turned travelers away. I burned smuggler caches whose only cargo was medicine bound below. I did it believing I was keeping faith. That is not innocence. That is innocence *manufactured*, and I helped manufacture it."

I looked up at Serevan. At the hundreds behind her.

"That's the ledger. Debts and thefts both. Judge it as you like."

* * *

Nothing happened. For a long moment — ten heartbeats, twenty — absolutely nothing happened, and I stood there in the ringing quiet thinking, *well, Galloway, you've finally done it, you've bored a buried nation to death,* and then the wood spoke.

Not Pip's voice. Not any voice. The whole cavern shivered, root-vaults flexing, and every fungus-light in the place flared gold at once, and the note Ashmark was holding swelled from a single tone into a chord — layered voices, four generations of Brightwaters, my grandfather's gravel and my grandmother's warmth and voices older than either, all of them saying the same thing in the language oaths are written in:

*Spoken. True. Witnessed.*

The chord broke over the galleries like a wave. Thornborn began to kneel — not all at once, but spreading outward from the old birch-bark elder, tier by tier, the way frost crosses a field. Not to me. I understood that with a clarity I'll carry to whatever grave gets me: they weren't kneeling to the last swordspeaker. They were kneeling to the first true sentence their ears had caught from the surface in five hundred years.

Serevan remained standing. But her hand had gone to the arm of her stump-throne, gripping it, and her face — that cold, courteous, contemptuous face — had cracked somewhere around the middle, and what showed through was tired. Old-tired. Five-hundred-years-tired.

"You knew," she said quietly. "Your order knew, and they sealed the wards anyway, and they let us starve behind them in the dark and call it peace."

"Yes."

"And you tell me this now. Why? Why should the truth be worth more coming from your mouth than from anyone else's?"

"Because it's not worth more," I said. "It's just the only coin I have left. Spend it however you want, my lady. But I'm told there's a rite that can reforge the covenant, and I'm told it needs a human voice that the wood will believe." I lifted my hands off the pommel. They left prints in the frost of my own sweat. "You have it. What's left of it. Use me or throw me out, but don't decide based on the order that made me. Decide based on what I just said. That part was mine."

She was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, the contempt was gone, and honestly I missed it; contempt I knew how to handle.

"There is more you must know before you offer yourself so freely, swordspeaker. About the sealing. About what your ancestors *did* to make their peace." Something moved behind her eyes that I would learn, later, to recognize as pity. "And it is worse than anything you have confessed tonight. Much worse."

"Of course it is," I said. "It always is. Tell me anyway."

She rose from the stump, and the whole court rose with her, and the gold light guttered down to blue as if the cavern itself were drawing breath.

"Then come," said Lady Serevan, "and let me show you the heart of the lie. Bring your sword. You'll want something to hold on to."

* * *

Later — much later, in the guesting hollow, with Wren asleep sitting up against the wall and Pip murmuring wood-words into Daine's shoulder — I sat sharpening a blade that no longer needed sharpening, because it was the only prayer I knew.

The full speaking hadn't freed me. I'd thought it might. Catharsis, Wren would have called it, with her scholar's faith that naming a thing shrinks it. But naming the rot had just shown me how deep the rot ran, and tomorrow Serevan was going to show me deeper still, and somewhere above us the winter fell and fell on a world that was running out of time to hear the truth.

Ashmark hummed under the whetstone, soft and steady. Four generations of oaths in the steel, and every one of them now spoken aloud, and the blade seemed — lighter. Cleaner. As if it too had been waiting fifteen years for someone to say the quiet part out loud.

*One voice that the wood will believe.*

Gods help us all, it had picked mine.
