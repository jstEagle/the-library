## 13. The Lie Beneath

I have lived three hundred years, and in that time I have learned that the hardest words in any language are not the curses or the battle-cries. The hardest are the small ones. *I did.* Two syllables. A child could speak them.

It took my people four hundred years to forget how.

They came to the Court of Thorns Below at dusk — the human called Brightwater and his strange little constellation of strays: the scholar with ink-stained fingers and a liar's careful eyes, the border-guard who kept touching her sword-hilt as if to reassure herself it was still hers, and the child. The child who spoke with my grandmother's voice, which was also the wood's voice, which was also the voice I had spent three centuries refusing to hear.

I had them seated in the long gallery where the sap-lanterns guttered. My court watched from the shadows, hollow-cheeked and hungry, because the wards had fallen and the slow seep of magic that had kept us fed for centuries had thinned to a trickle. Starvation makes a court quiet. Quiet courts make poor decisions. I was determined not to make one more.

"You want the truth," I said to Brightwater. "Your order's truth. Very well. But understand what you're asking for, swordspeaker. Truth is not a gift in my experience. It's a debt collector."

"Madam," said Wren Calloway, "we've come a very long way to be insulted by you specifically—"

"Wren," said Brightwater, in the tone of a man who had learned in one week of travel exactly when to stop her.

I liked him immediately, which annoyed me. One does not survive three centuries by liking the instruments of one's ruin, and I had already guessed — correctly, as it turned out — that this man would be the instrument of mine.

* * *

Let me tell you what my people remember, and then let me tell you what actually happened, and you may judge for yourself which version a starving court would prefer to keep.

The memory we keep is this: Long ago, before the realms had names, there was only the Weeping Wood, and it covered everything from the salt coast to the high passes. It was not a forest as you understand forests. It was a *mind*. Slow, vast, dreaming in sap and season. And we — the folk you now call the hidden realm, the thorn-court, the fair folk when you're being polite and worse things when you're not — we were its thoughts given legs. Its dreams walking about and arguing with each other.

Then humans came. Humans always come. They cut and burned and planted their quick little crops in the wood's corpse-soil, and the wood, being vast and slow, could not fight them except by weather and winter and the long patience of roots cracking stone.

So the wise ones of both peoples met beneath the heart-tree — the oldest tree, the first thought the wood ever had of itself — and they made a covenant. The wood would share itself: its magic flowing outward through ward-stones to warm both realms, its seasons gentle, its borders open. In exchange, both peoples would tend it. Speak to it. Remember it.

That is the memory. It is even true, as far as it goes.

Here is what we do not say aloud, what has not been said aloud since the ink dried on the covenant-scrolls: the wood did not agree to share itself. Sharing requires a self that consents, and by the time the wise ones met beneath the heart-tree, the wood was already dying. Not from axes. From *us*.

You see, our immortality — the long centuries of my court, the beauty your poets sing about badly — it was never ours. We drank it from the wood, the way a tick drinks from a deer, and we told ourselves it was communion. For a thousand years the wood gave and gave, because it was vast and generous and did not know how to refuse its own thoughts. But it was thinning. Fading. By the time of the covenant, the heart-tree stood leafless half the year, and the deep places had gone silent, and the youngest of us were born *small*, born dim, born mortal-adjacent, though no one dared say so.

The covenant was supposed to be a sharing. What it became was a transfusion. The ward-stones didn't carry the wood's generosity outward to both realms. They carried the wood's *lifeblood* outward, and most of it came here, to us, to the Court of Thorns Below, where we told ourselves we deserved it because we were old and beautiful and had been its thoughts once.

And the heart-tree? The heart-tree was dying faster than the rest, because the transfusion ran through it first. So the founders of the covenant — my ancestors, your ancestors, the wise ones whose portraits hang in both capitals — made a decision. They could not save the whole wood. Perhaps they could save the *covenant*. So they cut out the heart-tree's living core and bound it into the rite itself. Every renewal, every sealing, every generation's reaffirmation of peace drew directly from the tree's remaining life.

We did not sacrifice the heart-tree in a single terrible night, the way your ballads would have it if your ballads knew. We sacrificed it *slowly*. A cutting here. A draining there. Four hundred years of polite, scheduled, committee-approved murder. That is how the worst crimes are always done — not with knives in the dark but with quills in well-lit rooms, by people who use words like *necessary* and *proportionate* and *the alternative is war*.

And the weeping? You call it the Weeping Wood because of the sap that runs like tears from every wound. We named it that too, in the early days, when we still pretended the tree was grieving some ancient loss rather than grieving *us*.

The stars went out because the wood's magic is what holds your realm's sky-thread taut — yes, that part of your scholars' theories is correct, Calloway, I read your Survey's work, it was better than your masters deserved. When the wood weakened past a certain threshold, the sky began to fray. The winter falling between the realms now is not a weapon anyone aimed. It is a body cooling.

* * *

I finished speaking and the gallery was silent except for the sap-lanterns, which hissed softly, as if they too found the story distasteful.

Brightwater sat very still. I had expected anger, or horror, or that particular human expression — pity — which I find intolerable. Instead he looked at the child.

Pip was sitting cross-legged on the cold floor, because children cannot be trusted with chairs, and the child was looking up at the vaulted root-ceiling of my gallery with an expression I recognized. Wonder, threaded with grief. The look of someone hearing a song they'd forgotten they knew.

"The tree remembers," Pip said. Not in the child's own voice. In the other voice, the old one, the one that had been reciting fragments of dead covenant-language since the burned waystation. "It doesn't hate. That's the worst part. It remembers and it doesn't hate and it just keeps *weeping*—"

"Hush, sprout," said the guard, Ferro, gently, but the child wasn't finished.

"It says the confession has to come from both sides. Both sides together, at the heart-tree, spoken true. That's what renews it. Not blood. Not cutting. *Telling.*" Pip's small face screwed up. "It says nobody ever tried telling."

"No," I said. "Nobody ever tried telling."

Wren Calloway had produced a notebook from somewhere — scholars are like squirrels that way — and was writing fast. "The rite of renewal," she said. "In the archives there are gaps in the liturgy. Whole passages redacted, marked *sealed by joint accord*. I assumed political embarrassment. Trade disputes. Something normal." She looked up at me, and her eyes were fierce and wet. "It was this. The original crime was written into the ceremony and then scratched out, and every renewal since has been a lie performed over a wound."

"Yes."

"And that's why they've been failing. The renewals. Each one weaker, because—" She stopped. She understood before I said it. Scholars usually do; it's the telling that defeats them.

"Because each renewal demanded more of the heart-tree, and the heart-tree had less to give, and the lie underneath grew heavier to hold up. Yes. Your Royal Survey catalogued the wards' weaknesses — I don't blame you, girl, you were a clerk, clerks don't choose what the powerful do with their lists. But the weakness was never in the stonework. The weakness was that the whole edifice rested on a corpse we refused to bury properly."

"And the person who unmade the wards," Brightwater said slowly, "deliberately. Whoever that was."

"Was trying to force this moment," I said. "Or something like it. I do not know who. Do not look at me like that, swordspeaker — if I had unmade the wards myself I would have done it *well*, and my court would not currently be eating boiled bark." A ripple went through the shadowed galleries at that; hunger makes even my dignity porous. "Someone walked the smuggler roads with the knowledge to unwind four centuries of weaving. Someone who wanted the realms desperate enough to finally listen. Whether they intended salvation or simply collapse, I cannot tell you. But I can tell you what the wood wants now, because the child has told us, and the child does not lie. The child is incapable of it. Believe me, I've tried to imagine why anyone would bother lying through such an inconvenient mouth."

"So the rite," Brightwater said. "Confession. Both realms. At the tree."

"Together. Spoken true. The original crime named plainly — not *a sacrifice was made*, not *hard choices were necessary*, but *we killed it, we drained it, we fed on it and called it peace*. From a human voice and from mine. Both. Because the covenant bound both, and the lie was shared, and a shared lie can only be replaced by a shared truth."

* * *

I will tell you what happened next in my court, because it matters, and because I am apparently in a confessional mood tonight and may as well be thorough.

My council heard the terms and reacted exactly as councils do. Elder Maelis, who is older than I am and twice as frightened of irrelevance, rose with what remained of her dignity and said the words I had known were coming since the child first spoke.

"You would have us kneel before humans and confess ourselves *vampires*. Parasites. You would hand them the moral victory and the leverage both. They will demand concessions — territory, tribute, the dissolution of the court itself. Lady Serevan, I have followed you through two wars and one plague, but I will not follow you into humiliation."

"It isn't humiliation if no one demands it," said Wren Calloway, surprising everyone, including possibly herself. "No one's asking for concessions. Look at them. Have you *looked* at them?" She gestured at Brightwater and his strays. "A hunted man, a deserter, a clerk who cries when she's angry, and a kid. That's not an invasion. That's a rescue party."

"They are four," Maelis said. "Behind them are two armies and a winter."

"Behind *you*" — Calloway's voice cracked upward, and I saw the guilt in her come all the way to the surface, the guilt she carried like a stone in her belly, the same stone I carried — "behind you is nothing, because your magic is starving and their soldiers burn things, and if everyone keeps protecting their pride instead of telling the truth, *everything dies*. The wood, the court, the human farms, all of it. Pride is not food. I checked. I've been living on pride for a year and I'm *famished*."

I could have silenced her. I nearly did. Old reflexes die slower than trees.

But Brightwater spoke instead, quietly, in the voice of a man who had clearly rehearsed hard truths recently enough to know their weight. "Lady Serevan. My order died keeping oaths built on the lie you just described. I've spent fifteen years believing I served something clean. Yesterday I learned I served a cover-up with a sword. So believe me when I say: I know exactly what it costs to stand in front of people and say *I was wrong about everything I was proud of*. And I'm telling you it costs less than the alternative. I've priced both."

"The arithmetic," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt. "Yes. Let us speak plainly of arithmetic, since the lady calls it humiliation. Here is my sum. Option one: we stay silent, the winter deepens, your Crown burns the wood — oh, yes, we have heard the rumors too, and my scouts confirm them — and when the wood dies, the last thread of our immortality dies with it, and my court becomes a museum of beautiful corpses within a decade. Option two: we walk up to the heart-tree in front of armed humans and say the unforgivable thing out loud, and perhaps we survive as something smaller and poorer and *honest*." I spread my hands. "I have run this sum every way I know. There is no third column. There never was. We simply never added the columns before, because adding them required writing down what was in them."

Maelis sat down. It was not agreement. It was exhaustion wearing agreement's coat. I would take it. Three hundred years teaches you that exhausted consent is still consent, and that revolutions are mostly made of tired people deciding the old lie weighs more than the new shame.

* * *

Later — the humans bedded down in the guest alcoves, the child asleep against the guard's shoulder mid-sentence, which I am told happens — Brightwater found me at the gallery's edge, where the living root-wall meets the stone, and where on clear nights you can feel the wood's pulse through the rock. Slower now. Much slower. A heartbeat counting toward something.

"You hesitated," he said. "Before you told us. Why? You'd already decided."

"How do you know I'd decided?"

"Because you told us the *whole* truth. People who haven't decided give you the truth in installments, sized to what they think you can handle. You dumped the entire crime on the table at once." He almost smiled. "That's a woman who's made up her mind and is daring herself to follow through."

"You are an irritating man, Brightwater."

"I've been told. Professionally, lately."

I looked at the root-wall a while. "I hesitated because once it is said, it cannot be unsaid. Three hundred years I have kept my people alive on that secret the way you keep a fire alive — carefully, feeding it, never looking directly at it. And I realized tonight that the fire was burning *us*. That the secret was the starvation. That every year we hid the crime, we committed a smaller piece of it again." I turned to him. "Do you know what I felt when I finished speaking? Not relief. Not dread. *Hunger*. Real hunger, for the first time in years — because saying it out loud loosened something, and whatever the wood has left to give flowed a little freer for an hour. The truth isn't just the price of survival, swordspeaker. It might be the mechanism of it."

"Then we go to the tree."

"We go to the tree." I said it, and the root-wall pulsed under my palm — once, faintly, unmistakably. Pip, asleep across the chamber, murmured something in the old voice and smiled.

But I am three hundred years old, and I did not survive three centuries by trusting good news. So I will write the rest plainly, the way one writes a warning: the Crown's army was already moving. My scouts brought word at moonrise — torch-lines in the White Pass, supply wagons, siege-carts carrying barrels of oil. They mean to burn the Weeping Wood to ash and call the ashes a cure.

And between us and the heart-tree stood four days of winter, one mountain, and every soldier in Thornfell.

The confession would have to be spoken soon.

Whether anyone would live long enough to hear it was another matter entirely.
