## 14. Daine's Report

I have read a great many royal dispatches in my eleven years of service. Most of them were boring enough to cure insomnia — grain requisitions, patrol rotations, a memorandum about the proper wax for sealing correspondence that ran to four pages and changed nothing in the world. I used to find that comforting. Bureaucracy, I thought, was just the shape peace took when nobody was looking at it.

The dispatch I held in my hands that night in the burned waystation was not boring. It was also not going to change anything in the world.

It was going to end it.

* * *

We'd taken it off a courier two days prior, and I want it on the record — whatever record survives this winter, which may be none — that taking it was my idea, and that I did not enjoy being right about what was inside.

The courier had been riding alone through the foothills below White Pass, which no competent officer orders in wartime, so either the Crown was desperate or the Crown didn't consider us worth more than one rider. Both possibilities insulted me. He'd surrendered without a fight when August stepped out of the treeline looking like exactly what he is, which is a man who could take apart an armed escort with a stick if the mood struck him. The courier was nineteen, frostbitten, and crying, and Pip gave him half their bread before we even searched his satchel, which tells you everything about how our little family works and why we are all going to die of kindness eventually.

Wren did the searching. She has careful hands, the way scholars do, and she went through the dispatch case with the same reverence she brings to everything written down, even things written down by people trying to kill her.

"Three letters," she said, laying them out on the waystation's broken table. "One's routine. One's personal — someone named Marta writing to someone named Ollie about a cow." She paused over the third. Her fingers stopped moving.

"What?" I said.

"It's sealed with the Lord Commander's mark." She looked up at me, and I saw something in her face I hadn't seen there before. Wren lies beautifully — I've watched her do it, watched her construct a whole false career out of thin air and nerve — but she cannot lie to herself about other people's handwriting. Whatever she was reading in that seal, she already knew it was bad.

"Open it," August said quietly.

She did. And then she read it once, silently, and went white in a way I recognized from field hospitals, from men looking at wounds they couldn't fix.

"Out loud," August said again. His voice had gone very still, the way it does when his oath-mark burns. He'd told us about the burning by then. He told us most things by then; that was the strange, slow miracle of those weeks, watching a man built like a locked door decide, one hinge at a time, to open it.

Wren read it aloud. I am going to set down what it said as best I remember, because some things deserve to be remembered accurately, and because I need to say the words myself at least once or they'll keep growing in the dark of my head.

*By order of the Crown and in the name of the realm's survival: upon confirmation of the blight's origin within the Weeping Wood, the Wood is to be destroyed in its entirety. Fire is deemed the only certain method. The operation is designated CLEANSING FIRE. Command falls to Lord Aldous Vane. No evacuation of border settlements within the burn perimeter is authorized, as displacement would compromise operational secrecy.*

No evacuation. I want you to sit with that phrase the way I sat with it, in a cold room, with a child asleep against my side. *No evacuation.* There were eleven hundred people in Harrowgate alone. More scattered through the passes, in the waystations, in the little hardscrabble farms that cling to the border country like moss to stone. And the Crown had decided, in its infinite wisdom, that telling them to run would be inconvenient.

"They think the wood is the source," Wren said. Her voice had gone flat and scholarly, which is what her voice does instead of shaking. "The blight, the winter, the dead wards — they've concluded it all flows from the Weeping Wood, so they'll burn it, and the problem will—" She stopped. "Except the wood isn't the source. The wood is the *patient*. It's been bleeding out for centuries. Burning it won't stop the winter. Burning it will kill the only thing keeping the covenant's bones together, and the cold will come down like—"

"Like what?" Pip asked. They'd woken up. Children always wake up at exactly the wrong moment; I'm convinced they can hear the future changing shape.

"Like the sky falling," Pip answered their own question, in the wood's voice, which is a sound I still haven't gotten used to — old and slow and sad, coming out of a face maybe nine years old. "It would be like that."

* * *

Here is the thing about horror that nobody warns you: it doesn't feel like screaming. Screaming is easy. Horror feels like arithmetic.

I sat with the dispatch in my lap after Wren finished reading it, and I did arithmetic. How long to muster? The Crown had been moving troops toward the passes since the stars went out — I'd seen the columns myself, back when I was wearing the uniform instead of running from it. Call it two weeks to concentrate a force big enough to burn several thousand acres of ancient forest, accounting for the weather, which had been doing its level best to kill everyone regardless of allegiance. Call it another week to position. Which meant we had perhaps three weeks before soldiers with torches and alchemist's oil reached the Weeping Gate.

And the Weeping Gate was five days' hard march away, and between us and it stood the worst storm season in living memory, a pass full of refugees, and every soldier in two realms who had ever seen my face on a warrant.

"We can make it," I said, because somebody had to say something, and optimism is a kind of discipline. My mother taught me that. She also taught me to count spoons before the tax assessor came, so her lessons came with caveats.

"Can we?" Wren asked. Not hostilely. Genuinely. That's the thing about Wren — she never argues to win, only to know. "Daine, look at the map. Look at the snow line. If we go straight for the Gate we abandon every settlement in the burn perimeter, and 'no evacuation' means those people don't even know—"

"I know what it means," I said. Sharper than I meant to.

Because here is the other thing, the thing I had been carrying since the stars went out, and the dispatch set it down heavy on top of everything else: I had spent eleven years believing in that seal. The Lord Commander's mark. I had stood at attention under that mark. I had marched refugees out of flood zones under it, held a bridge at Corrin's Ford under it, buried friends under it. It was supposed to mean that the ugly things — and there are always ugly things, in any army, in any kingdom — were at least *necessary* ugly things. That somebody smart and decent had done the math and this was the least terrible answer.

There was no math in that letter. There was fear wearing math's clothes. *Fire is deemed the only certain method* — certain of what? Certain to feel decisive. Certain to let the Crown tell itself it had acted. You don't put out a wound by setting fire to the patient. Every guard who ever worked a plague post knows that. Somebody in that command structure knew it too, and signed anyway, because the alternative was admitting that the realm's founding covenant was real, and broken, and their grandparents' grandparents' fault.

My allegiance hadn't been betrayed. That was the part that made my hands shake. My allegiance had simply been pointed at a cliff by men who never intended to look over the edge themselves.

"Daine." August's hand landed on my shoulder. He's not a tactile man — swordspeakers train the softness out of themselves, or try to — but when he touches you it lands like a decision. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing like you're about to charge something."

"Maybe I am."

"Good," he said. "But charge with us, not ahead of us. I've made that mistake. It costs more than it pays." And he smiled, that crooked tired smile of his, and I remembered Ferro — Sergeant Ferro, the woman I used to be — standing in the rain at the ford with her sword at his throat and choosing, in one breath, to be a person instead of a uniform. He'd spared her. She'd defected. Some debts you pay forward whether you want to or not.

* * *

Pip had climbed into my lap at some point, which children do the way cats do, with total disregard for the gravity of the situation, and possibly because of it. They were turning the dispatch over in their small hands, frowning at the seal.

"The tree knows this already," they said.

Everyone looked at them. Wren asked the sensible question: "Knows what, love?"

"The burning." Pip traced the Lord Commander's seal with one finger, and where their finger passed, faintly, the wax seemed to weep — a bead of golden sap welling up out of nothing, smelling of resin and rain. "The wood felt them measuring it. Men with chains and instruments, counting the trees, walking the edges. It felt them the way you feel a spider walking on your arm while you're pretending to sleep." Their voice slipped older as they spoke, the wood rising through them like water through a reed. "*It has decided not to be afraid. It says: I have died before. I grew back once.*"

"But it can't grow back from fire," Wren whispered. "Not from *that*. The heart-tree—"

"No," Pip agreed simply, back in their own small voice, and looked up at me with eyes that had seen too much for their years and carried it with a grace I hope someday to deserve. "So we should hurry."

Out of the mouths of babes, as my grandmother used to say, usually right before telling me off. Out of the mouths of oracles, in our case. The child distilled three hours of adult agonizing into four words: *we should hurry.*

"So we go now," August said. "Tonight. Snow's dropped enough to travel."

"And the settlements?" Wren pressed. "The burn perimeter? Eleven hundred people in Harrowgate, and the waystation families, and—"

"I know." I stood up, dislodging Pip, who protested. "I know, Wren. But we're five people. Four, if you don't count the oracle-in-training. We cannot evacuate a valley and reach the Gate in time to stop the army. We can't split — splitting means nobody reaches the wood with the strength to do whatever it is August has to do in there." I looked at August. "Unless."

He raised an eyebrow. He's very good at eyebrows. It's his second-best weapon after the sword-work.

"Unless we send someone ahead alone. Fast, light, who knows the military roads and the checkpoints and how to walk through a picket line like smoke." I heard the words coming out of my mouth and realized I'd been composing the argument for the last hour without admitting it. "Someone the patrols might hesitate to shoot. Someone whose name is still on a muster roll somewhere, whose face the sentries at the Gate approach might recognize and challenge instead of kill."

"You're volunteering," Wren said flatly.

"I'm *nominating*," I corrected. "Me. Alone, ahead, straight down the military roads. I move faster without this lot slowing me down — no offense, you're all terrible walkers. I get to the wood first, I scout the approaches, I find out exactly where Vane's force is staging, and I buy time however I can. Sabotage their supply of oil if I can reach it. Misdirect them. I spent eleven years learning how the Crown thinks; it's about time the Crown regretted teaching me."

"No," August said.

"Yes," I said. "August. Be reasonable."

"That's my line, and I hate hearing it used against me." He rubbed his face. "You'd be walking into the army that has your desertion warrant pinned to every mess tent door."

"Ferro's a common name," I said. "I'll wear a hood."

"They'll hang you."

"They'll try." And then, because I could see him building the refusal, I played the card I'd been saving, the one I wasn't proud of. "You taught me something at the ford, August, even if you don't know it. You taught me that the person matters more than the duty — but sometimes the duty and the person point the same direction, and then you don't get to choose comfort. Those people in the perimeter are mine. My posting. My watch. I walked past their doors for six years. If the Crown burns them for a secret, then at least one guard who wore the seal gets to stand between them and the torch. That's not negotiable, and we both know it, because you'd say exactly the same thing and we'd both pretend it was strategy."

Silence. Wren was looking at me with an expression I couldn't parse — something between grief and pride. Pip tugged my sleeve and handed me the last heel of bread, solemn as a knighting.

It was August who broke, because August always breaks toward sense eventually; it's his best quality and his worst. "Alone, but not unarmed, and not unprovisioned, and you wait at the Weeping Gate until we arrive — you don't go in. Nobody goes in before the rite. Swear it."

"On what?" I said. "I hear oaths are unreliable these days."

"On the fact that if you die stupidly I'll be insufferable about it for eternity," he said, and stuck out his hand, and I shook it, and that was that. Decided. The family, splitting like light through a prism — each of us carrying a different color of the same urgent thing toward the same dark place.

* * *

I left at moonrise, such moon as there was — the stars still absent, the sky a clean black bowl overhead, and I confess I kept looking up at it as I walked. Fifteen years of nights under those stars and I'd never once thanked them. Now they were gone and I missed them like teeth.

The snow squeaked under my boots. Behind me, faintly, I heard Pip call something — not words, more a note, the wood's voice folded around it — and warmth slid down my collar like a hand resting briefly on the back of my neck. A blessing, maybe. Or a warning. With that child it was hard to tell the difference, and I suspect they liked it that way.

I walked south, toward the fires that were coming, carrying a stolen letter and eleven years of a loyalty I no longer owned, thinking about seals and what they actually bind — not paper, it turns out, never paper. Just people. People who choose, every morning, to keep believing, or to stop.

Three weeks. Maybe less.

I made good time.
