## 16. Ambush at the Weeping Gate

The Weeping Gate is not a gate.

I should have known better than to trust a name given by smugglers, who name things the way drunk men name horses — for the last thing that went wrong near them. It was a cleft in a ridge of black granite, maybe forty feet across, hung with icicles the size of ship's masts, and beyond it the trees began: white-barked, silver-leafed, standing so thick and so still that the forest looked less like a place than like an audience waiting for someone to say the wrong thing.

Pip stopped at the edge of the cleft and tilted their head, listening, the way they always did — chin up, eyes half-closed, like a cat deciding whether the sound in the walls was a mouse or something that ate mice.

"It's sad," Pip said.

"The wood?"

"No." Pip's face did something complicated. "It's trying not to be."

Wren pulled her coat tighter and consulted her battered notes by feel rather than light, because we had learned over three days of running that light was a debt you paid in blood. "The rite has to happen at dawn," she said, for perhaps the ninth time. "The tree drinks first light. If we miss it—"

"We won't miss it." I said it with more confidence than I owned. Confidence, in my experience, works like the forged covenant-scroll Vess had offered me: it keeps people moving right up until the moment it kills them.

Daine ranged ahead along the treeline, moving like a woman who had spent fifteen years learning exactly how soldiers think and had recently decided to use that education against them. She came back at moonset, low and quick, and her expression told me everything before her mouth did.

"Campfires," she said. "Two companies' worth, maybe more, strung along the southern approaches. They're not chasing us anymore, Brightwater. They're herding us. Everything else is closed. This is the only door left open, which means—"

"Somebody wants us to walk through it," Wren finished.

"Or somebody knows we will regardless," Daine said, "and would rather we walked through it tired."

Pip looked up at me. Children are merciless that way; they ask the questions adults have learned to swallow. "Are you tired, August?"

"Terribly," I said. "But I've been tired for fifteen years. It's practically my natural state. Come on."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about walking toward your own death: the body treats it as any other march. Feet ache. Breath fogs. You count your companions the way a miser counts coins — Wren stumbling but upright, Daine silent and watchful, Pip's small hand trusting in yours — and you think, absurdly, about breakfast. About whether there will ever be breakfast again. The mind refuses the large horror and busies itself with the small ones, and you let it, because the alternative is to stand in the snow and scream.

The cleft narrowed as we went deeper. The trees changed too — older, closer together, their bark streaked with resin that caught what little light remained and held it, so that walking between them was like walking between candles. Somewhere far off, water dripped. Or wept. With this wood it was hard to say where one ended and the other began.

Daine's hand came up.

We stopped. Snow fell without wind, straight down, patient as arithmetic.

"There," she breathed.

And there it was: the ambush, unfolding out of the landscape like ink dropped in water. Soldiers rising from behind fallen trunks. Crossbows on the ridgeline above us, already cocked, already aimed. Men and women in Crown grey and Crown blue both — the realm had apparently decided its old quarrel could wait until the inconvenient swordspeaker was dead — and in front of them all, unhurried, hands folded behind his back like a schoolmaster on a morning stroll, stood Vess.

He looked smaller than I remembered. That was the first cruelty of it. All my life he had filled doorways, filled courtyards, filled the entire horizon of what a man of the oath could be. Now the snow came up past his knees and he seemed to sink into it, an old man in a black coat who had walked a long way to be disappointed.

"August," he said, and his voice carried in the cold the way voices do when the whole world is holding still to listen. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other side of the pass," I said. My mouth does this. It has done it since I was nineteen years old and terrified in his training yard. Wit as armor; he taught me that too, though he'd deny it now.

"I imagine I will, shortly, from the wrong direction." He sighed, and the sigh was genuine, and that was somehow worse than if it had been performed. "Put down the sword, boy. Not the one on your hip. The other one. The story you're carrying. Put it down and take the scroll instead, and every one of us walks out of these trees alive."

"The scroll is a lie."

"The scroll is a *mercy*," Vess said, and took a step forward, boots crunching. Behind him, forty crossbows tracked us. "Do you know what happens if you speak your confession into that tree? Do you truly understand it? The covenant doesn't just bind realms, August. It binds *everything sworn beneath it*. Every ward, every treaty, every oath ever spoken in either court. Including—" his voice gentled, which from Vess was the most dangerous register he owned, "—yours. Your mark. Your order. Everything you have been since you were fourteen years old and knelt in the frost promising to be worth something. Speak the truth into that wood and all of it burns away. Is that a price? Or is it just suicide with better lighting?"

I felt Wren go very still beside me. And Pip's hand tightened in mine — not afraid, I realized. *Listening.*

"You sold us," I said. "To the Crown. To keep me from telling the truth. You'd rather the world froze honest men than thaw on a confession."

"I'd rather the world *continued*, you sentimental fool." For the first time something cracked in his voice, and I heard under it not anger but exhaustion — the exhaustion of a man who had carried a secret for sixty years and had built his entire spine around keeping it. "I have watched that covenant rot since before your father drew breath. I watched the order decide that a managed lie was kinder than a catastrophic truth. I buried friends who died maintaining that lie, August, and I will tell you what I told myself every morning at the graveside: better a lie that holds than a truth that drowns everyone. Take the scroll. Renew the covenant with paper and theater. Nobody will ever know. The stars come back. The winter ends. Everyone lives, and lies a little, which is the human condition anyway."

"And the wood?" Pip said.

Small voice. Enormous question. It went through the clearing like an arrow goes through wet paper.

Vess looked down at the child — and here was the second cruelty: he didn't hate Pip. I saw that plainly. He looked at Pip the way a man looks at a bill he cannot pay.

"The wood is dying regardless," he said quietly. "It has been dying for two hundred years. We are only choosing the manner of the funeral."

"No," said Pip.

"Pip—" Wren started.

"No," Pip said again, louder, and the trees answered.

I don't have another word for what happened next. The resin-light in the bark flared. The dripping somewhere off in the dark stopped, all at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone says the true thing. Snow slid from a hundred branches. And every crossbowman on that ridgeline flinched as if the wood had spoken their names.

Vess's jaw tightened. "Kill the child first," he said to his soldiers. "Then the traitor Ferro. Then the scholar. August last — he deserves to watch."

And there it was. Complete. The fall I had been refusing to see since the shrine, since the forged scroll, since the ledger with his handwriting in the margins. My mentor. The man who taught me that a swordspeaker's word was the only currency that never debased. Offering murder in a calm voice, in a sacred wood, to save a lie he had loved longer than he had loved anything living.

I mourned him then. Right there, crossbows leveled, I stood in the snow and grieved a man who was still breathing, because grief, I have learned, does not wait for death. Sometimes it simply arrives early and waits politely for the body to catch up.

"I'm sorry, old man," I said. And meant it, which frightened me more than the arrows did.

Then I unsheathed my sword and spoke.

Not a battle-cry. Swordspeaking was never that — whatever the ballads tell you. It is speech made structural, speech that takes hold of the world's joints. I gave the first word to the air and the air took it, and the temperature dropped so sharply that breath turned to falling glass. I gave the second word to the ground and the ground shrugged, and the drifts the soldiers had hidden behind rose up around them to the waist, gentle as a mother tucking in a child, and absolutely immovable. The third word I gave to the crossbows themselves, and forty strings snapped in unison with a sound like a giant plucking a broken harp.

I did not kill anyone. That mattered to me, in that moment, more than I can cleanly explain. Vess had spent sixty years believing the lie was mercy. I would not answer mercy's corrupted ghost with slaughter. Let him be defeated by restraint. Let it gall him.

His hired blades were better than his soldiers. Six of them came through the trees in a wedge, fast and quiet, and Daine met them like a door slamming. I have seen a hundred fights and forgotten most, but I will keep this one: Daine Ferro, deserter, turning two swords and pure offended professionalism against six killers among the candle-trees, while Wren dragged Pip behind a trunk and shouted ward-fragments that made the air thicken like honey around anyone who tried to reach them. The found family, working. Nobody had planned it. Nobody needed to.

I saved Vess for last, and by then it wasn't really a fight. He was old and he knew it, and some part of him — the part that had once knelt beside me in the frost — had stopped fighting the moment I spoke instead of lied. Our blades met four times. On the fourth, I turned his wrist, and his sword went spinning into the snow, and I put my point at his throat and then, deliberately, lowered it.

"Surrender," I said. "Live with it. That's harder than dying, and you've always liked hard things."

For a moment — one moment — his eyes were the eyes from the training yard, and I thought it might end differently.

Then he smiled, thin and terrible. "You always were my best student," he said, and drove his hand into his coat, and I moved, and the knife he'd drawn opened my forearm instead of my throat, and then Daine's pommel came down behind his ear and the oldest liar I ever loved folded gently into the snow.

We bound him. We gagged him, after he tried words instead of knives — and his words were good, they were always going to be good, he'd had sixty years of practice. We left him sitting against a white-barked tree with three soldiers for company, alive, defeated, mourned.

My arm bled. Pip pressed their small palm over the cut and the wood's warmth came up through their skin like spring rehearsing, and the bleeding slowed, and I understood — finally, completely — what Pip was, or at least whose voice wore their smallness like a borrowed coat.

"The gate," Wren whispered.

Ahead, the trees had drawn apart. Not a path carved — a path *offered*. And through it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, we could feel the heart-tree. Dying. Waiting. Two hundred years of wept sap and stolen stars balanced on whatever happened next.

Behind us, down the southern slopes, horns began to sound — the Crown army, coming up the only open door, exactly on schedule.

"Dawn's in an hour," Daine said, looking back the way we'd come, then forward into the silver dark. "So is an army. Pick your deadline."

I picked up my sword, and we walked into the Weeping Wood.
