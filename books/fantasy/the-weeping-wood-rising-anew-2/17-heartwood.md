## 17. Heartwood

The tree knew my name before I said it.

That should have scared me more than it did, I think. But I'd been hearing its voice in my head for two years by then — the weeping, the whispering, the long slow sentences that took all night to finish — so when August pushed open the great split in the wood's heart and the tree went *there you are* through every bone in my body, it felt less like a stranger grabbing me and more like someone finally saying the thing out loud that everyone had been too polite to mention.

"It knows me," I said. My voice came out small. Wren squeezed my hand so hard my fingers went white.

"Of course it knows you," she whispered. "You've been talking back to it for years."

The heartwood chamber was not what I expected, though honestly I don't know what I expected. A room made of tree, maybe. Something with walls. But there were no walls. There was the trunk of the Weeping Wood itself, wide as a castle keep, silver-grey and split down the middle like a loaf that hadn't finished baking, and inside the split everything was amber. The whole hollow glowed gold-brown, thick light like honey held up to a candle, and the air was warm in a way nothing had been warm since the stars went out. Sap ran down the inner walls in slow ropes, each drop catching the light and holding it, and every drop made a sound when it fell — a note, a small wet bell-note — and together they made the weeping I'd heard my whole life.

Except now I could see it wasn't sad weeping. Or it wasn't *only* sad weeping. It was the sound of something trying very hard to keep talking while it died.

"Pip." August crouched down to my level, which he did sometimes even though his knees cracked like green branches when he did it. "Whatever happens in here — whatever it shows you — it isn't a punishment. Do you understand? Trees don't punish. They just grow things true."

"That's worse," I said.

He laughed, short and surprised, the way he did when I accidentally said something honest. "Yes," he admitted. "It is."

Daine stood at the entrance with her hand on her sword hilt, which was a stupid place to put your hand if you thought you might need to fight a tree, but Daine did things like that when she was frightened. She'd been frightened since the White Pass. She'd been frightened longer than that, if you asked me, though nobody had asked me yet. That was going to change soon. That was the whole problem with truth-trees, as I was about to learn.

Wren had her ledger out. Of course she did. "I want to record whatever it shows us," she said. "For the ward-school. For whoever comes after."

"If anyone comes after," Daine muttered.

"Somebody always comes after," August said. "That's how we got here."

And then the tree stopped waiting politely, because trees are not polite, they are only patient, and patience runs out differently than politeness does.

* * *

I went first. I didn't choose to go first; the roots just moved under my feet like a tide coming in and carried me forward until I was standing against the amber wall with sap soaking through my boots, and the tree said *look*, and I looked.

I saw the waystation burning.

I saw myself at six years old, standing in the snow outside the flames, except in the seeing I could finally look at the part I never let myself look at: I hadn't wandered off. I had walked away. The soldiers came over the ridge — Crown soldiers, our own realm's colors — and the woman who kept the waystation told me to run into the trees and not come back no matter what I heard, and I heard plenty, and I ran, and I kept running, and I told myself ever after that I'd gotten lost. Lost children get found. Lost children get taken in by caravans and smugglers and kind hard men who teach them to read road-marks. Lost is a story with an ending where somebody comes looking.

Nobody came looking. Because I hadn't been lost. I had obeyed the last instruction anyone who loved me ever gave me, and I had survived it, and survival felt so much like abandonment that I'd folded them together and carried them as one thing for four years.

*That is yours,* the tree said, gentle as falling sap. *But it is not the deepest one. Look again.*

"I don't want to," I said out loud, and heard Wren make a sound behind me like she wanted to tell me I didn't have to, and couldn't, because none of us had to and all of us would.

I looked again.

This time I saw further back than my own memory goes. I saw the heart-tree young and green, before it was silver, before the wards, before anything. I saw hands — human hands, court hands, wearing rings I somehow knew were Serevan's rings when her house was younger — pressing something into the soil at the tree's roots. Not planting. Burying. A seed wrapped in a cloth that had words stitched around its edge in a language that made my teeth ache, because I knew the language. I'd been speaking pieces of it in my sleep for two years without knowing what it was.

And the seed was alive. And the seed was listening. And the seed was afraid.

*I gave you my last seed,* the tree said, and its voice was everywhere now, in the amber light and the falling notes and the warm dark behind my eyes. *When they cut my heart to seal their covenant, I could not save what I was. So I spoke it away. I spoke it into flesh. Small enough to hide. Human enough to pass. You.*

The world got very quiet and very loud at the same time.

"No," I said. It came out as a squeak, which under the circumstances I stand by. "No, I'm — I'm from Harrowgate side, I'm a waystation kid, I'm —"

*You are the part of me that learned to be hungry,* the tree said, and there was something almost like humor in it, dry as winter bark. *You learned it very well. I am proud. Hunger is how my kind survives winters.*

Behind me, Wren made a noise that was half sob and half laugh, because she understood before I did that this was the tree being funny. Being *kind*. Telling the worst truth in the world in a voice designed to hold a child together while the child heard it.

"But I'm *me*," I whispered. "I'm Pip."

*Yes,* the tree said. *That is the miracle of it. That is what they never understood, either side, either court. A thing spoken can become true. You were a seed. You became a child. Both are real. Neither cancels.* A pause, long enough for three drops of sap to fall. *But child — hear the rest, because the rest matters. When I die, if you have not been planted, what I am dies with me entirely. And then the covenant has nothing left to bind to. And then the winter does not end. It deepens. Forever.*

"So I'm not just a kid," I said slowly, working it through the way August worked through a ledger. "I'm the spare key. I'm the backup."

*You are the heir,* the tree corrected. *There is a difference. A key opens a door. An heir inherits a house — and a house has people in it who must be fed.*

I turned around. Three faces looked back at me. August's face had gone still in that way he had, the swordspeaker stillness, except his hand wasn't anywhere near his sword; both hands were open at his sides, the way you stand when you're ready to catch something. Daine had actually taken her hand off her sword. Wren was crying openly into her ledger and writing at the same time, which seemed like it should be impossible and wasn't, because Wren.

"You're still Pip," August said firmly, before I could ask. "Hear me? Whatever else you are, you were Pip first. Four years of being cold and clever and stubborn — the tree didn't make that. You did."

"The tree made the stubbornness," Wren sniffled. "It's a tree trait."

"*Wren.*"

"What! It's comforting! Genetic stubbornness is very comforting!"

And I laughed, which I think was the point, because the tree loosened its grip on my feet and let me stumble back to them, and Daine caught my elbow, and for one whole minute we were just four people standing in a warm gold room while a dying tree pretended it hadn't just turned my whole life inside out like a pocket.

Then the amber light shifted toward Wren, and the tree said *you next, little liar*, and there was no pretending after that.

* * *

The tree showed Wren her catalog.

Not the survey work itself — she'd confessed that weeks ago, on the toll road, in the rain, in the conversation where she and August had recognized each other like two halves of a broken seal. No. The tree showed her the night she'd decided not to notice.

She was twenty-one, in the Survey archive at Thornfell, and a superior — a man whose name she'd carefully never written in her ledger — had asked her to reorganize the ward-stress tables. Not recalculate. Reorganize. Sort by region rather than severity. And she'd known, even then, some small sharp animal part of her had known, that sorting by region buried the pattern. Buried the fact that every weak point sat along the smuggler roads. Buried the shape of the knife. And she'd done it, because the alternative was asking questions that ended careers, and she'd needed the career, needed the stipend, needed to send money home to a mother whose lungs were failing in a town whose name Wren had also carefully never written down.

*You hid the pattern to protect your mother,* the tree said, showing her the ledgers, the neat regional columns, her own handwriting marching obediently across the pages. *And the pattern hid inside your tidiness, and traveled, and killed the wards, and the winter reached your mother's town anyway. She died in the dark you helped tidy.*

"No," Wren said. Just once, quietly, the word falling flat in the amber room. Then, because she was Wren: "The sort order wouldn't have changed anything. Someone senior already knew. Hiding it was a symptom, not the cause —"

*True,* the tree agreed, maddeningly calm. *And also beside the point. Truth-telling is not arithmetic, scholar. You do not get credit for accurate fractions of confession. The question is not whether you caused it. The question is whether you will spend the rest of your life correcting it, or merely explaining it.*

Wren stood very still. Then she closed her ledger, which I had never seen her do while information existed, and said, "Correcting it. Obviously. Why do you think I'm here?" And the tree's light warmed around her like a hand on the shoulder, and I understood that for Wren, that exchange had been the rite — not the seeing, but the answering.

* * *

Daine fought it. Of course Daine fought it.

When the roots came for her she actually drew the sword, and August said "Daine," in the voice he used for horses and frightened recruits, and she said "I know what it's going to show me and I already live with it every day, I don't need a *tree* doing a reading of my—"

*Desertion,* the tree said, simply, and showed her anyway.

The border post at Cael Ford. Two years ago. A raid — cross-realm raiders, or dressed as them, it hardly mattered — and Daine in command because her captain was dead, and twelve soldiers, and a decision: hold the ford and lose six of them buying time for reinforcements, or fall back and let the ferry village burn. She'd done the arithmetic. Every guard did the arithmetic. The manual said hold. She'd ordered the fallback, saved nine lives, lost the village — eleven dead, a granary, a chapel — and reported it honestly, and been passed over for promotion for two years while the report circulated with the phrase *lacked resolve* attached to her name like a burr.

But that wasn't the desertion. The desertion came later, in the showing, in the part she really lived with: the moment three months afterward when she'd stood at the rebuilt chapel in the burned village and watched the villagers rebuild around the graves of the eleven, and a child had asked her if the soldiers were coming back to stay this time, and Daine — Sergeant Daine Ferro, sixteen years of service, spotless record except for one phrase — had said yes. Yes, they're coming back. And then requested transfer to the far passes specifically so that she would never have to be there when they didn't.

*You lied to a child to buy yourself one hour of peace,* the tree said. *And you have been paying interest on it ever since. That is why the uniform stopped fitting. Not the fallback. The lie.*

"They needed—" Daine started, and stopped, and her jaw worked, and finally she said, in a voice like gravel sliding, "Yeah. Okay. Yeah." And she drove her sword into the root-soft ground, deliberately, the way you plant something, and left it there, and the tree's light went gold around her, and I swear the weeping-notes changed pitch, like approval.

* * *

August went last, and his was different. His wasn't shown to us — the tree pulled him close against the amber and the light swallowed him whole, and we saw nothing, and I think that was mercy, or maybe privacy between old acquaintances, because I got the sense the tree had been carrying August's secret for fifteen years too, humming it in the background of every weeping night I'd ever heard.

He was in there a long time. Long enough that Wren gripped my hand and Daine picked up her sword from the ground. When the light finally thinned and set him back down on his own feet, he looked like a man who'd been underwater. His oath-mark — the burn-scar on his forearm, the brand every swordspeaker carried — was visible because his sleeve was shoved up, and it was *faded*. Paler than I'd ever seen it. Not gone. Faded, like a scar deciding whether it still belonged to him.

"August?" Wren said.

"It showed me the order," he said. His voice was steady, which cost him; I've spent enough time around adults to know what steady costs. "All of it. What they knew. What Vess knew." He looked at his arm. He looked at us. And then he did the most August thing I have ever seen: he smiled, tired and crooked, and said, "Well. Now the tree's caught up with the rest of you."

"You're bleeding," Daine said, nodding at his nose, because apparently when August went into truth-light he bled a little, and none of us had noticed, and he wiped it with his cuff like it was weather.

"Worth it," he said.

And the tree sighed — a whole-forest sigh, every rope of sap shivering at once, the notes ringing high and clear instead of low and wet — and said:

*Now. You have each seen your own truth. Hear the last one together, because it belongs to all of you, and to neither of your realms alone, and because there is not much time. The army is three days out. The Lady Serevan is one day out, and wounded. And I have perhaps two days left in which a seed may still be planted in living ground.*

The amber dimmed. Somewhere above us, far up through the crown of the wood, wind moved through bare branches with a sound like turning pages.

*Listen,* the tree said. *Because the covenant was not broken at the borders. It was broken here, at my roots, on the day it was made — and what was done to me was done willingly, by consent, by sacrifice — mine and another's — and the confession that renews the pact must name that other. Not me.* The notes fell slower now, deliberate, each one placed like a stone in a wall. *Ask the Lady Serevan what her house buried beneath my roots on the night the stars were first hung. Ask her what she has spent three hundred years pretending her family's grief was about.*

Wren's pen had stopped moving. "The original sacrifice," she breathed. "It wasn't just the tree. There was a *second*—"

*One day,* the tree said. *Bring her. Bring her truth and yours, and bring the child who is my heir, and pray the Crown marches slower than grief does.*

The light guttered like a lamp running dry. In the sudden half-dark, August looked at each of us — Wren, Daine, me — and squared his shoulders the way he did before bad roads.

"Well," he said. "We've got a day, a wounded lady, and an army. Everyone get some sleep. Tomorrow we go collect a confession three centuries overdue."

I lay down against a root that was warm as a sleeping dog and thought about being a seed. It wasn't the worst thing I'd ever learned about myself. But it was going to be, if we didn't hurry.
