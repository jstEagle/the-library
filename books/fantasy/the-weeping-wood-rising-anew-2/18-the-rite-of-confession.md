## 18. The Rite of Confession

The heart-tree was dying the way old soldiers die — slowly, stubbornly, and with an expression that suggested it had opinions about your performance.

I stood beneath it in a chamber that shouldn't have existed, a hollow vast enough to swallow a cathedral, its walls ribbed with pale wood and slick with sap that fell like slow rain. Every drop that struck the ground made a sound I can only describe as a word I wasn't meant to hear. The tree's trunk was split down its whole height, a wound older than any nation, weeping amber into pools at our feet.

"Charming place," Daine said, because Daine said things like that when she was terrified. "Very tasteful. Good acoustics."

"It's a tomb," Wren whispered.

"It's a confession booth," I corrected. "And I'm the only priest left."

Pip stood closest to the trunk, one small hand pressed flat against the bark. The child had been quiet since the heart-tree showed us what it shows everyone — Pip's own face looking back from inside the wood, a seed given human shape to walk among us and remember. When Pip finally spoke, it was with the doubled voice I'd come to dread and love: a child's voice, and beneath it something vast and grieving.

*It asks if you are ready.*

"I've been ready since Harrowgate," I lied. "Lying is traditional at confessions, isn't it? Consider this practice."

Wren made a noise that might have been a laugh or might have been a sob. Hard to tell, with scholars. She'd laid out her notes on a flat root like a surgeon laying out knives — fragments of the covenant, translated from Pip's recitations and Serevan's court-records and the shrine texts we'd nearly died for twice. She'd worked through the night by sap-light, and it showed: ink on her fingers, shadows under her eyes, and the particular rigid calm of someone who has decided that if the world ends today, it will end with her bibliography in order.

"The rite has four movements," she said. "The swordspeaker confesses his order's oaths — kept and broken. The wood confesses what it witnessed. And then—" she hesitated, because the next part was the part that had kept all of us up, "—then a representative of each realm must confess the original sacrifice. Aloud. By choice. There's no way around it. The covenant was sealed with a lie, and it can only be resealed with the truth."

"And if nobody speaks it?"

"Then the winter keeps falling," Pip said simply, "until there is nothing left to be cold."

I drew my sword.

Not out of habit — though after fifteen years of oath-binding, drawing steel before speaking felt like washing hands before supper. The blade came up singing, or rather, I sang it up, the first true words of the rite rising in my chest like water finding an old channel. Swordspeaking isn't magic, whatever the ballads claim. It's memory given a voice, and the voice given weight. My order had spent three hundred years perfecting the art of saying true things beautifully so no one would notice they were also saying false things quietly.

I was done with both halves.

"I am August Brightwater," I began, and my voice filled the hollow, and the sap fell slower, listening. "Last of the Order of the Kept Edge. Sworn to guard the border wards. Sworn to speak truth to power." A breath. Here came the hard part, the part Vess had spent my entire apprenticeship teaching me to skip. "Sworn oaths my masters broke within my lifetime. The wards were not failing. They were being fed lies about their own condition. My order knew the covenant was thinning. My order chose comfort over truth, and taught me the shape of the lie so well I carried it fifteen years without knowing I carried anything at all."

The words hurt going out. That surprised me. I'd expected the cost to come later, at some dramatic moment, priced and itemized. Instead every truth was a small tearing, like scar tissue letting go, and I found I wanted to stop — found my mouth shaping around the comfortable silences Vess had installed in me like furniture.

I kept talking.

"My mentor, Vess Corvane, offered me a forged renewal. I refused it. He sold our road to the Crown for the price of his own innocence. I do not forgive him tonight. I note it as fact, which is harder." My hand shook on the hilt. "I loved the roads more than the realm. I loved the freedom more than the duty. I told myself smuggling through dying wards was mercy — moving medicine, moving people — and sometimes it was, and sometimes it was just pride wearing mercy's coat. I don't get to choose which half was true. Both were. That's the confession. That's mine."

Silence. The kind of silence that has texture.

Then the tree answered.

It didn't speak as such. The whole chamber shivered, and every drop of falling sap landed in unison — one vast syllable, then another — and Wren, translating in a whisper gone hoarse, gave us the gist: *I saw. I wept. I let them take my heart rather than break the peace I held too dear. My silence was also a lie.*

Even the tree was confessing. Somewhere above us, under fifty miles of stone and snow, I imagined the sky taking notes.

"That's three," Daine murmured. "Who's fourth?"

Which was when the screaming started.

* * *

It came down the entry passage we'd sealed behind us with rockfall and prayer — the prayer having clearly lapsed. Boots, many boots, and the particular disciplined clatter of Crown regulars who expected resistance and had brought the equipment for it.

"Pip, behind the roots," I snapped. "Wren—"

"I'm not leaving the notes."

"Wren."

"They're *load-bearing*, August."

Daine was already at the rubble, sword drawn, ear against stone. "Forty men, maybe fifty. Commander's voice is familiar." A pause. "It's Halloran."

Captain Maren Halloran. I'd smuggled her a crate of fever-medicine two winters back and she'd pretended not to notice. One of the decent ones, which made her infinitely more dangerous than the cruel ones, because cruel ones you can predict.

"We can't fight fifty," Wren said.

"No," I agreed. "But we were never going to win this with swords anyway. Put them away."

They looked at me like I'd lost my mind, which was fair; I'd been losing it gradually for weeks and this was simply the terminal stage.

"The rite needs witnesses," I said. "It needs *them*. That was always the trap and the answer, wasn't it? You can't confess into an empty room. You need the people who'd rather you stayed silent. You need the blades at the door." I looked at Wren. "Where's the fourth movement?"

Her face went grey. "Serevan. We need Lady Serevan or someone of her court. And she's—" 

She's forty miles away behind enemy lines with a broken court and no reason to trust a human holding a drawn rite in the belly of the tree her people believe humans murdered.

"She'll come," Pip said.

Everyone turned. The child hadn't moved from the trunk. Their eyes had gone the deep amber of heartwood, and the doubled voice spoke with terrible calm.

*I called her. I called her when we entered. The wood carries sound through root and stone faster than any horse. She has been walking since midnight.* A pause, and the child-voice returned, smaller. "She was crying. I heard it through the dirt."

Boots in the passage. Sap-light guttering. And I stood in the oldest wound in the world with a sword I no longer knew how to put down and a truth in my throat like a swallowed coal, waiting for a wounded queen and an army to arrive in whichever order fate preferred.

Fate, being fate, sent them together.

* * *

They broke through the rockfall in a wash of torchlight and cold air — Halloran's vanguard first, shields up, expecting ambush, and behind them the rank and file filing in until the chamber's edge bristled with steel and nervous faces. Fifty soldiers in a cathedral of weeping wood, their torches reflected ten thousand times in the wet walls, so that the dark itself seemed armed.

Halloran came forward alone. Grey at the temples now, jaw set like she'd been clenching it since White Pass. Her sword was drawn, but low, which from Halloran was practically a diplomatic communiqué.

"Brightwater." Her eyes tracked the chamber — the tree, the child, the scholar, the defected sergeant standing conspicuously on the wrong side of the room. "By order of the Crown, you're under arrest for the collapse of the border wards, the deaths at Harrowgate, and treason against—"

"The wards weren't collapsed," I said. "They were unmade. From inside. Using routes mapped by the Royal Survey and catalogued by its scholars, one of whom is standing right there and will happily show you the correspondence, Captain, if your orders permit you to read evidence instead of just enforcing it."

Something flickered in her face. Doubt, maybe. Or the exhaustion of a woman who'd marched through the worst winter in living memory on orders she couldn't reconcile with the frozen refugee columns she'd passed on the way.

"You'll have your trial," she said. "Stand down. All of you."

"Captain," Wren said, stepping forward with her notes hugged to her chest like a shield, "if you arrest him now, the winter doesn't end. Ever. The covenant can be reforged — tonight, here — but only if it's witnessed. Only if *you* witness it. Please. Read the translations. I'll answer every question. But not with him in chains."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in six months," Daine said flatly, "your uniform won't matter, because there won't be anyone alive north of the passes to salute in it. I've seen the passes, Captain. I've seen what's coming down them."

Halloran opened her mouth —

— and the far wall opened first.

Not broke. *Opened*, the way a throat opens: the wood parting along a seam no eye could find, and through it came Lady Serevan of the Thorn Court, upright by pure aristocratic spite, one side of her body blackened with frostburn, leaning on a spear of living briar, flanked by a dozen of her starving courtiers whose beauty had gone gaunt as winter apples. Her crown was twigs. Her eyes were the green of the last leaf.

Behind her soldiers, behind his soldiers, behind everyone, the chamber held its breath.

"Halloran," Serevan said, in the accent of a language older than the Crown, "draw that blade another inch and my people will show you what thorns remember."

"Lady Serevan." Halloran's knuckles whitened on her grip. "You're harboring traitors."

"I am attending a funeral," Serevan said. "Mine, possibly. Ours, certainly, if either of us is foolish." Her gaze found me, and there was hatred in it, and grief, and beneath both something worse: hope, worn thin as a coin rubbed smooth by too many desperate hands. "Swordspeaker. You asked my court to trust you with the truth of your order. I have walked all night on burned feet to hear whether you were worth it." 

She looked at the tree. At the great split in its heart. Something in her composure cracked, just slightly, the way ice cracks before it goes all at once.

"So speak," she said. "Finish your rite. And then, gods help us both, I suppose it is my turn."

I turned back to the heart-tree. Fifty soldiers, twelve starving courtiers, a captain with her sword half-raised, a wounded queen bleeding frost onto ancient roots — every soul in that chamber wanting silence, wanting safety, wanting the comfortable lie that someone else would fix the world while they guarded the door against it.

The rite demanded otherwise.

"Fourth movement," I said, and raised my voice to fill the wound in the world. "Let both realms hear what they did."

And Lady Serevan closed her eyes, and began to speak.
