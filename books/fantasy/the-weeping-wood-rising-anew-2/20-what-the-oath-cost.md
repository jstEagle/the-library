## 20. What the Oath Cost

I have been asked, in the year since, what it felt like. People want a word for it. *Pain*, they suggest, or *loss*, or sometimes — from the poets, who are worse than scholars and twice as expensive — *rebirth*.

None of those words fit. What it felt like was this: someone reaching into my chest and taking out an organ I'd forgotten I had, holding it up to the starlight, and asking me, very politely, if I'd like to see how much of my life I'd been living around it.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The stump shivered first.

* * *

The heart-tree's stump was older than both realms, or so Serevan claimed, and it looked the part: black as old iron, split down the middle, weeping sap that ran in slow silver threads into the roots like tears running off a chin. For three centuries it had bled. That was the whole of the confession, when you boiled it down — both realms had cut out its heart to seal their peace, and then built their laws and borders and oaths on top of the wound, and called the arrangement holy.

The confessions were done. Mine, Serevan's, spoken aloud before soldiers who'd marched here to burn the place down and instead stood in the snow with their swords lowered, watching stars come back one at a time like lamps being lit in a house long dark. The covenant had taken the words the way dry ground takes rain. You could feel it happening — not a sound so much as a pressure, a great held breath finally let go, rolling outward through Thornfell in every direction at once.

Then the stump shivered, and Wren grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise, and Pip said, in that voice that wasn't quite Pip's:

"It's giving back what it borrowed."

"What did it borrow?" Daine asked, hand on her sword out of pure habit.

"Everything anyone swore on it," Pip said. "Every oath. Every binding. It held them all so they'd hold true." The child's eyes were fixed on the stump, wide and unblinking, silver sap-light reflected in them. "It doesn't have to hold anymore. So it's letting go."

And I felt it go.

* * *

I need to describe the oath-mark properly, because you can't understand what I lost without knowing what I carried. It sat over my heart — a small knot of scar-tissue shaped like a sword laid vertical, given to me at seventeen when I spoke my first binding vow. Fifteen years it had been part of me. It warmed when I spoke truth under oath. It burned when I broke one, or witnessed one broken, or — once, memorably, involving a toll-keeper's daughter and a promise I should never have made — merely *considered* breaking one. When the order died and left me the last swordspeaker in existence, the mark became something else entirely: a headstone I wore on my skin. Proof that the thing I'd given my life to had existed, and ended, and that somebody still remembered.

When the covenant released it, the mark didn't hurt. That's the detail nobody believes. It simply came *loose*, the way a tooth comes loose when everything beneath it has healed — and then it was warm, warmer than my own blood, and then it was light, faint gold bleeding through my shirt, rising off my chest in a curl like smoke from a snuffed candle, drifting up through the cold air toward the new stars.

I caught myself reaching after it. Both hands, actually, like a fool trying to cup water. Wren was still gripping my arm; she pulled me back before I embarrassed us both further.

"August," she said carefully. "Your shirt."

I looked down. Over my heart, where fifteen years of scar-knot had lived, there was now only smooth skin, pale as any other part of me. New skin. Skin that had never been sworn to anything.

"Well," I said, because I am a professional and professionals always find the words. "That's going to make bathing complicated."

Nobody laughed. Not even me.

* * *

Here is what happened inside me while everyone else watched the tree begin, impossibly, to regrow — green shoots splitting the black stump, sap turning from silver to clear, the whole clearing filling with a smell like spring arriving early and unannounced: I stood absolutely still and discovered that I could not feel my own name.

Not literally. I knew my name. August Brightwater, smuggler, guide, last of the Swordspeakers — except no, not that last one anymore, not *last swordspeaker*, because there were no swordspeakers at all now, the covenant's release having unwound every binding ever sworn through the wood, which meant the order hadn't died after all. It had been dissolved. Retroactively, in a sense, by a tree.

For fifteen years I had been *the man who remembered*. It was a terrible identity. It was also the only one I had. Every morning I woke and the mark was there and I thought: *you are what remains.* Every night I slept and dreamed of my masters' faces and woke thinking: *someone must carry what they hid.* Grief and duty braided together so tightly I couldn't tell you where one ended. They had become load-bearing walls in the house of me.

And now a tree had knocked them out in a single breath, and the whole structure was standing in open air, creaking, held up by nothing.

"August." Wren again, closer now, her voice pitched low, the scholar's voice she used when she was worried a subject might bolt. "Talk to me."

"I'm fine," I said.

"You're white as the snow."

"I'm always white as the snow. It's a northern complexion."

"August." She turned me gently, the way you turn a friend away from a corpse, though the only corpse in sight was mine, technically, the old one, the marked one. "You just lost the thing you've been carrying since before I met you. You're allowed to—"

"To what? Fall apart in front of two armies?" I managed something that was probably a smile. "Bad form, Wren. Very bad form."

She hit me. Actually hit me, a short sharp blow to the shoulder that would have staggered a smaller man, delivered with the full righteous fury of a woman who had spent months cataloguing wards she knew would be weaponized and had decided, somewhere along the way, that she was finished letting the men around her perform stoicism at her expense.

"Don't," she said, "do that. Don't you dare do that. I lied to survive. Daine deserted to save us. Pip is a *tree*, apparently, and handling it better than you are. We have all spent this entire winter being honest at enormous personal cost, and you do not get to stand there with your brand-new unmarked chest and pretend the price didn't come due."

The clearing had gone quiet around us. Serevan was watching from near the regrowing stump, her court gathered behind her, and beyond them the Crown soldiers, and beyond them the darkening sky with its returning stars. Two armies, one scholar, one child-tree, one deserting sergeant, and me, rubbing my shoulder, feeling the first crack run through whatever dam I'd built.

"Wren," I said, "I don't know who I am."

There. Said it. Out loud, in front of everyone, which after a winter of enforced honesty was perhaps only fitting.

She took my face in both hands, which was unexpected, and kissed my cheek, which was more unexpected, and said: "Good. Now you get to find out. Most people never do."

* * *

Daine found me later, sitting on a fallen log at the clearing's edge, watching Pip.

The child hadn't left the stump since the covenant released. Pip knelt in the snow with bare hands pressed to the new green shoots, and where the shoots grew fastest, the snow was melting, pulling back in a widening ring. Spring, starting from a single kneeling child, in the dead of winter, at the top of the White Pass. I had seen many impossible things that year. That one was the hardest to look away from.

"She chose it, you know," Daine said, dropping onto the log beside me. Her armor was dented; her sergeant's insignia was gone; she'd torn it off somewhere between the ambush and the rite, and I don't think she'd noticed yet. "Staying partly the tree's voice. I heard her tell Lady Serevan. Nobody made her."

"I know."

"And you're sitting out here feeling sorry for yourself about a scar."

"Daine—"

"Because here's what I saw tonight." She counted on her fingers, a habit from briefing rooms that she'd never shaken. "I saw a man walk into a camp of royal soldiers who wanted him dead, and speak truths that stopped them mid-march. I saw a smuggler read a ledger cipher that four scholars couldn't crack. I saw him carry Pip down the White Pass through a killing storm on his back, and I saw him spare my life when he had every reason not to, twice, and neither time did his hand shake. Whatever the mark did for him—" she shrugged, "—it wasn't doing the work. He was."

I looked at her. She looked back with the flat patience of a woman who had guarded border towns for twelve years and was not going anywhere until the conversation concluded satisfactorily.

"The mark was the proof," I said finally. "That it all mattered. The order, the oaths, the years. Without it I'm just..." I gestured vaguely at myself. "A man with a sword and a talent for finding passes."

"A man with a sword, a talent for finding passes, and a family that will gut the next person who suggests he's nothing without a piece of government-issued scar tissue." She stood, brushing snow off her breeches. "Get used to being enough, Brightwater. It's harder than it sounds. Trust me — I'm finding out too."

She walked back toward the fires. And because the night clearly intended to finish dismantling me completely, that was when Pip turned from the stump and looked straight at me across the clearing and smiled — and in the child's mouth, briefly, was the wood's old voice, the voice of the dead-and-risen covenant:

"*Swordspeaker. Your debt is paid. Walk free.*"

* * *

I did not cry. I want that on the record, for posterity, for the ward-scholars who will inevitably write this all up: I. M. Galloway notwithstanding, August Brightwater did *not* cry in front of two armies.

What I did was sit on a log until the sky finished filling with stars — dozens of them now, then hundreds, constellations reassembling themselves overhead like an audience taking their seats — and I put my hand flat against my chest, over the smooth new skin, and I grieved.

Because here is the truth underneath all the wry commentary, and I promised myself I'd stop lying, even the comfortable kind, even to myself: I loved the oath. Not the order — the order had betrayed everything — but the oath itself, the weight of it, the way it made the world simple. *Protect. Speak truth. Keep faith.* A compass needle doesn't wonder who it is. It points. For fifteen years I had been a needle pointing at a north that no longer existed, and I had told myself the pointing was the purpose, and now the tree had set me down and said *no, the pointing was never yours, it was ours, take your hand back and decide what it holds.*

Grief and liberation, in one breath. That's what it was. They aren't opposites, whoever told you that. They arrive together, twins, holding hands, and you cannot welcome one without the other, and the trick — the whole trick of being alive, I suspect — is letting both of them in the door.

"August?" Small feet in the snow. Pip had crossed the clearing, leaving melted footprints behind, and stood before me with sap-stained hands and an expression far too old for the face wearing it. "Are you sad?"

"A little."

"The tree says sadness after a big letting-go is just love with nowhere to go yet." The child considered this. "So you should find somewhere to put it."

I looked past Pip at the fires — Wren arguing terms with Serevan's heralds, already, tireless, ink-stained fingers sketching in the air; Daine organizing the Crown soldiers into watch rotations because apparently you could take the girl out of the guard but not the reverse; the whole ragged, impossible family of them, stitched together out of liars and deserters and orphans and one oathless smuggler, holding.

"Yeah," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I meant it to. "I think I already know where."

Pip nodded solemnly, the way children nod when adults finally catch up to something obvious, and went back to the stump.

I stayed on the log a while longer. Then I stood, sheathed my sword — my sword, plain steel, no enchantment, no blessing, just a sword — and walked toward the firelight, toward the voices, toward the people who had, without ever saying so, been rebuilding me all winter out of materials I hadn't known I had.

Behind me, the heart-tree grew. Ahead of me, morning was coming — actual morning, the first thaw-breath of spring moving through Thornfell — and with it, Wren had already announced, with the terrifying brightness of a scholar who has found her life's work, the question of what to *plant*.

Because the old heart was dead wood now. But the tree, in its giving-back, had left something behind in the stump's hollow. Something small. Something seed-shaped.

And Pip, when I asked, had only smiled that old-young smile and said, "It's waiting to be chosen."
