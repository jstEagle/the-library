## 21. Planting the Seed

I have drafted treaties before. Well — one treaty. A term paper on ward-law for the Royal Survey that argued, with the confidence of a nineteen-year-old who had never been outside a library, that border disputes could be solved through "structured mutual acknowledgment." My instructor gave it high marks and a note: *Lovely in theory. No one in power will ever agree to this.*

It took a dying heart-tree, an army frozen mid-march by a returning star, and a child who speaks in sap and starlight to prove him wrong. I'd like to say I've matured past smugness. I have not.

The morning we planted the seed, I sat on a fallen log with my drafting board across my knees, ink freezing in the pot every four minutes, while Lady Serevan of the realm-court and Commander Elara Voss of the Crown army stood six feet apart and pretended they weren't both looking at me like I was a bomb that might go off.

"Read it again," Serevan said. "The third clause."

"The third clause," I read, "states that neither signatory shall claim jurisdiction over the Weeping Wood or its approaches, which are henceforth held *in common trust* — meaning jointly, meaning nobody gets to be greedy about it."

"I know what *in common trust* means," Serevan said, with the particular frost of an immortal being patronized by a human.

"Just checking. You've been alive for centuries; sometimes the definitions drift." I turned the page. "Clause four: joint ward-school at Harrowgate, staffed by scholars of both realms, teaching the covenant as it actually happened. Including the sacrifice. All of it. The ugly parts especially."

Voss shifted. She was a tall woman with a commander's posture and a scholar's eyes, and she had knelt in the snow three weeks ago when the first star came back, along with two thousand soldiers, and she still hadn't fully recovered from what that moment did to her understanding of her career. "The Crown will resist publishing the full history."

"The Crown resisted a lot of things," August said mildly, from where he leaned against the heart-tree's ruin with his arms crossed. He didn't even open his eyes. "It resisted the wards failing. It resisted the winter. It resisted admitting either one was its own fault. Look how those went."

"You're enjoying this," Voss said.

"A little," he admitted. "Fifteen years of oath-bound silence. Let me have my gloating phase. Wren says it's healthy."

"Wren says no such thing," I said, and signed my own name to the document as drafter, because if you want a clause to survive negotiation, put your signature under it before anyone can argue. Old Survey trick. The only thing I learned there worth keeping.

Serevan signed next. Her sigil was a living branch, grown from her palm over the course of a minute, and when she pressed it to the parchment it took root in the fibers and flowered. Show-off. Then she looked at Voss.

Voss looked at the paper for a long time. Then she drew her sword — everyone flinched, including me, and I'd written the clause about disarmament myself — and used the flat of the blade to press her seal into the wax.

"Ceremonial," she said. "Force in service of law, not against it."

"That's the most honest thing a soldier's ever said at a signing," August said. "Wren, write it down. Put it in the appendix."

I wrote it down.

* * *

The seed had come out of the old heart-tree three days after the rite, the way a splinter leaves a wound — slowly, and then all at once, and then everything is better except the hole where it was.

Pip found it. Of course Pip found it. The child had been spending more and more time inside the ruined hollow of the tree, sitting where the great trunk split open, listening with their whole body in a way that made the rest of us feel like we were listening with our elbows. On the third morning they came running back to camp holding something cupped in both hands like it was made of dawn, which, optically speaking, it nearly was.

"It let go," Pip said. "It said it was done being afraid of letting go. It said—" Pip frowned, searching for the words, because translating wood into speech was still hard for them, like carrying water in cupped hands. "It said *this part isn't dying. This part never got to live yet.*"

August took off his coat and spread it on the ground so Pip could set the seed down gently. We all gathered around it. Daine picked it up, weighed it, and said, "Feels like an acorn."

"It is not an acorn, Sergeant Ferro," Serevan said from the edge of camp, "any more than you are a particularly ambitious mushroom."

Daine, to her credit, just nodded and put it back down. Three months ago she'd have bristled. Now she treated the court-folk's insults as a kind of weather — occasionally unpleasant, rarely personal, always passing.

The question of what to do with the seed occupied us for two days, which surprised me until it didn't. Everything important does. Serevan wanted it planted in the court's deep gardens, tended by hands that understood slow growth. Voss wanted it somewhere defensible. August, characteristically, said nothing at all for a long time, and then said the thing he'd been circling:

"It should go where the tree fell. Where it wept. If we've learned anything this winter—" and here he looked at each of us in turn, and I felt the look land like a hand on the shoulder, "—it's that things grow better in honest ground."

So: the hollow. The great broken bowl of the tree's remains, where the sap-weeping had carved channels over centuries and where, since the rite, the weeping had stopped. Dry ground for the first time in living memory. Ready, in the way that ground is ready after it finally finishes grieving.

Pip chose the spot within it. Not by measuring — by standing very still in eleven different places and then announcing, with total certainty, "*here*," the way children announce things, as though the universe had been waiting on their verdict.

We dug together. That's the part I keep coming back to, when I can't sleep. Not the rite, not the stars, not even the confessions — though God knows those live behind my eyelids too. The digging. An immortal lady of the hidden court kneeling in thawing mud with her sleeves pushed up. A Crown commander breaking frozen ground with a borrowed spade, grumbling that this was beneath her rank and doing it anyway. August turning the earth with the same careful attention he gave to sharpening a blade. Daine hauling stones out of the way without being asked. And me, the liar-scholar, doing the one thing I'm genuinely good at, which is taking notes — marking the depth, the soil, the drainage, because someone should write this down properly, someone should make a record that doesn't lie, and apparently that someone is me now.

Pip placed the seed. We all put our hands in the dirt over it, which sounds ridiculous and felt like nothing at all and then, briefly, like everything — a pulse, faint, patient, the heartbeat of a thing that had waited centuries and could wait a season more without complaint.

Then we covered it up. And that was it. That was the whole ceremony. No chanting, no blood, no sacrifice. Just five people and a child burying a beginning.

"Well," said Daine, wiping her hands on her trousers, "now what?"

"Now," said Serevan, "we wait for spring."

And spring — the real one, the earned one — was already coming. You could hear it. Under the snow, all across Thornfell, water was moving.

* * *

I want to record, for the archive I am apparently now keeping, the exact terms of the Harrowgate Truce, because history has a way of forgetting the small clauses that actually matter:

One. The wards would be rebuilt — not restored to their old shape, which had been built on concealment, but redesigned openly, by scholars of both realms. I would lead the human contingent. I still don't entirely know how that happened. One minute I was a fugitive; the next, people kept calling me "the architect of the truce" and waiting for me to have opinions. Having opinions turns out to be mostly saying things out loud before you're sure of them and then being right often enough to build a reputation on.

Two. The smuggler roads would become actual roads. Registered, patrolled, taxed lightly and honestly. August fought hardest for this one, and I watched him do it — watched the man who'd spent fifteen years walking shadow-paths argue, calmly and devastatingly, that secret routes were how poison traveled, and that light on the road was worth more than any wall. Vess's network dissolved within a month. Some of his people came in from the cold. Some didn't, and that's a problem for another chapter, and another year, and probably another person.

Three. The truth. Published. All of it — the sacrifice of the heart-tree, the order's concealment, the Survey's weaponized catalogs, the Crown's plan to burn the wood. My name is in there. Wren Calloway, complicit. It cost me two sleepless nights to write that sentence and then, strangely, nothing at all. Shame, it turns out, weighs less when you set it down where other people can see it than when you carry it around pretending it's luggage.

Four. Pip. The clause everyone argued over. In the end we wrote it the only honest way we could: *the child Pip is recognized as the voice of the renewing wood, with the right to choose, anew and always, how much of that voice to carry.*

Because that was the other thing. Pip's choice.

* * *

They told us at the fire, two nights after the planting. Pip sat between August and Daine — their preferred configuration, the swordspeaker and the soldier, the two most dangerous people in any room, who were both completely helpless around this child — and announced it the way Pip announced everything: plainly, with no idea how much it would land.

"The tree asked me something. Through the seed. It asked if I would stay its voice."

The fire popped. Nobody breathed.

"What did you say?" August asked quietly.

"I said yes. Some." Pip held up a hand and pinched fingers almost together. "Some voice. Not all. The tree needs someone to say its words out here, where people can hear them. But I'm also still Pip." A frown of tremendous concentration. "It understands. It says growing means choosing your shape, not just accepting the pot you're put in."

"And if someday you change your mind?" Daine asked. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't.

"Then I tell it so." Pip shrugged, seven years old and infinitely wise and seven years old again in the space of a breath. "It's not a cage, Daine. It's a door."

Daine nodded several times, looked into the fire, and later that night I heard her telling August, in a low rough voice, that she'd been a door once too, opened by the wrong people, and that maybe doors got to choose who walked through. August didn't answer. He just put his hand on her shoulder and left it there.

As for me, I cried a little, privately, behind the woodpile, and then came back and made jokes about needing to draft a formal annex defining the term *some voice*, because that is also who I am, and Pip laughed, and the laugh was worth the woodpile.

* * *

The thaw broke for good in the second week of Thawmonth. I remember because I was mid-clause — clause nine, water rights, the least romantic text ever produced by mortal hands — when Daine stuck her head into my tent and said, "Wren. Come see. Bring nothing. Just come."

The passes were running with meltwater, silver threads stitching down the black rock, and the snow on the high shoulders of Thornfell had gone soft and blue at the edges, and everywhere — everywhere — green. Not the desperate green of things surviving. The reckless green of things deciding.

At the hollow, the planting site had changed overnight. A single shoot stood where we'd buried the seed, no taller than Pip's hand, with two leaves unfurled like a sentence just getting started. Around it, in a perfect ring, the old deadwood of the ancient tree had begun to bud. Impossible. Serevan said so herself, staring at it, and then amended: impossible *by the old rules*. Which, she added, seemed increasingly to be the point of everything.

Pip knelt and touched the shoot and reported, solemnly: "It says thank you. It says it has a lot of catching up to do. It says—" and here Pip grinned, a gap-toothed, wholly childish grin, "—it says *tell the scholar her clauses are boring but necessary.*"

"I'll have that engraved," I said, "on my headstone. *Her clauses were boring but necessary.*"

August laughed — really laughed, the unguarded sound I'd only started hearing from him since his mark faded — and clapped me on the back hard enough to stagger. "Boring and necessary. The family motto."

*Family.* He said it easily now, the way you'd say *road* or *bread*. I don't think he noticed he'd said it. I noticed. I wrote it down that night, in the margin of clause nine, and I'm writing it here, and if the archive judges me for sentimental marginalia then the archive can draft its own treaties.

* * *

The signing was held at Harrowgate, in the town square where a mob had once tried to hang me for a crime I hadn't committed, which I insisted upon as a venue purely for the poetry of it. They rebuilt the shattered ward-stone at the square's center first — the first of hundreds — and we signed the truce on a table set against it, in front of anyone who cared to watch. Most of the town did.

Serevan wore her court-form, tall and silver-barked, terrifying and magnificent. Voss wore her uniform, and had chosen, I noticed, to leave the ceremonial sword at home. August stood behind us both, unarmed, unmarked, wearing a guide's oiled coat instead of an order's colors, and looked more like himself than I had ever seen him look.

When it was done — when both seals had dried and the crowd's murmur rose into something like weather — a woman near the front called out, one of the garrison wives, "Is it true? What they're printing? About the tree? About what was done to start it all?"

Every official present developed an urgent interest in their boots. I stepped forward.

"It's true," I said. "All of it. It's being printed exactly as it happened, and if you find a part that's been softened, bring it to me and I'll fix it. Hard truths only, from here on. That's the whole treaty, really. Everything else is plumbing."

Someone laughed. Someone else started applauding, uncertainly, the way crowds do when they're testing whether joy is permitted. Then it caught, and rolled, and Serevan looked at me across the table with an expression I'll be translating for years — something between amusement and astonishment, the look of a being watching a species surprise her.

"You enjoy this," she said.

"Enormously," I said. "Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation for reluctant heroism to maintain."

"Your secret is safe. You were never reluctant."

* * *

That evening, the four of us climbed the ridge above Harrowgate — our ridge, though none of us said so, we just kept ending up there — and watched the stars come out. All of them now. Whole constellations back from wherever the long dark had kept them, thick as spilled salt across the sky, and among them, faint but unmistakable, the new star: the one born the night the covenant was remade, hanging above the Weeping Wood like a lamp left burning in a window.

Pip pointed at it. "The tree says that one's ours."

"Possessive of a star," Daine said. "That's got to be some kind of legal complication."

"I'll draft a clause," I said.

August said nothing. He was looking up with his hands in his pockets and his face open, and I thought about the man I'd met on the toll road in Harrowgate — the one carrying fifteen years of silence like a stone in his chest, saving me from a mob because he couldn't figure out how to stop being responsible for things. He wasn't carrying anything now. Or rather, he was carrying the same weight everyone carries — other people, chosen ones — and had somehow discovered it weighed less.

"Clause ten," I said, mostly to hear how it sounded out loud. "Annual gathering, this ridge, first clear night after thaw. Attendance mandatory. No exceptions for immortals, soldiers, scholars, or guides."

"Make it eleven," August said. "Ten's taken."

"By what?"

He smiled at the sky, at the new star, at whatever private joke the universe was letting him in on. "By whatever happens next. Leave room for it."

So I left room. And far below us, in the dark hollow of the Weeping Wood, something small and patient pushed another inch toward the light, and began — slowly, in its own tongue, in its own time — to grow.
