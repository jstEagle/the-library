## 22. Roads Worth Walking

The pass called the Widow's Teeth earned its name honestly, and I have never once been tempted to soften it for a client. If you want soft, walk the river road. If you want to live, listen to the man who's been falling down this mountain since before your father could shave.

"Mind the scree on the left," I told the caravan, my breath fogging in the early light. "It looks stable. It is not. It has opinions about travelers, and none of them are kind."

There were eleven of them this trip: two wool merchants, a surveyor with more instruments than sense, a nervous young priest of the Returned Stars — a new order, only months old, growing like weeds after rain — and six hired porters who had already learned that when I said *walk wide*, they walked wide. A year ago I'd have guided them with my hood up and my face turned from theirs. A year ago, guiding meant smuggling meant survival, and survival meant never letting anyone know your name.

Now my name was on a ledger at Harrowgate's new waystation, written out plain as bread: *August Brightwater, guide. Rates posted. References available.*

References. From Sergeant Daine Ferro, no less, whose reference read: *"He spared me when he should've killed me. That's either the best or worst recommendation you'll ever get. Take it."*

Wren says it's good marketing. I say it's blackmail with better grammar.

* * *

We made the high shelf by midday, and that was where the boy saw them.

Not one of mine — the priest's nephew, come along for the walking, all elbows and questions. He stopped dead on the trail and pointed up past the crags where the sky does its bluest thinking.

"Sir. Sir, look."

I looked.

Stars. In daylight, faint as fingerprints on glass, but there: three of them hanging above Thornfell's spine, pale and patient. The first ones had come back the night of the Rite — a single star, so bright the whole Crown army stopped mid-march to kneel in the snow, and I will carry the sound of eight hundred soldiers going quiet at once until I die. Since then they'd returned one by one, like guests arriving late to a feast they'd been invited to a century ago.

The scholars argue about what it means. Wren argues loudest. The wards are relearning their work, she says; the covenant is knitting itself back into the bones of the world, and every star is a stitch pulled tight. Pip says simpler things. Pip says the wood is happy, and Pip would know, being — well. We'll get to Pip.

"They came back," the boy breathed. "My mam said they'd never come back. She cried when the first one did."

"People cry easier than they admit," I said. "Come on. The Widow doesn't wait."

But I stood a moment longer than I needed to, looking up. Old habit, checking the sky. New habit, finding something there worth checking for.

* * *

Harrowgate had changed the way a scar changes: same shape, different story.

A year ago the town had been all ash and accusation — ward-stones shattered from the inside, garrison pointing fingers at every smuggler who'd ever walked a toll road, and a mob working itself up to stringing up a terrified ward-scholar because someone had to pay and she was convenient. I'd broken two noses getting Wren Calloway out of that square. One of them belonged to a man who now sold me eggs every market day and pretended we were friends. Small towns keep grudges the way rivers keep silt, but they also forget, given enough honest work to do.

The ward-school occupied the old garrison hall, which pleased me in a way I couldn't quite explain even to myself. The building where soldiers had planned to burn the Weeping Wood now held chalkboards, star-charts, and forty children learning to read the language of wards. Daine drilled them in the yard each morning like a sergeant of very small recruits, and they loved her with the pure ferocity children reserve for people who pretend to be stern. Wren taught inside, and had somehow become an authority, which amused her not at all and terrified her completely.

"The Survey offered me my old post back," she told me over supper that evening, in the cramped kitchen behind the school where the four of us ate most nights when I wasn't on the road. "Full rank. Research charter. They want the person who found the flaw to help build the new wards." She pushed beans around her bowl. "The person who *was* the flaw, more accurately."

"You were a tool," Daine said flatly. She said it the way she said most true things, without decoration. "Tools don't get blamed. Makers do."

"The makers got pardons and pensions," Wren said. "Funny how that works."

"So take the post," I said. "Fix it from inside. You're the only one alive who knows exactly how it broke."

She looked at me across the table, and I watched her do the thing she did — weigh every word twice, assume the trap, check for the trap, find no trap, distrust the absence of trap. A year of knowing her hadn't worn that off. Probably nothing would. Some scars are structural.

"And leave the school? Leave—" She glanced toward the window, toward the garden where a small figure was talking to the fence posts. "Leave Pip?"

"I didn't say leave anything. I said take the post. Wren Calloway has never once done a thing the simple way in the time I've known you. Why start?"

Daine snorted into her tea.

* * *

Pip came to meet my caravans at the town gate whenever word arrived that I was coming down the mountain. This was not negotiable and never had been. The first time I'd tried to arrive unannounced, the child had been standing at the gate anyway, arms crossed, having announced to everyone within earshot that "the wood says August is grumpy and almost home," which was accurate on both counts and mortifying in both.

This time they ran the last hundred yards and hit me at a speed no child that size should generate.

"You brought the priest's nephew," Pip said, pulling back to inspect me. "He's going to be a stargazer. I can tell. His dreams are full of counting."

"He's seven, Pip. Everyone's dreams are full of counting at seven."

"Not *his* counting." Pip took my hand with the proprietary ease of family and tugged me toward the school. "Come see the garden. The heart-seed sent up a runner. Wren said I shouldn't tell you because you'd worry about frost, but you always worry about frost and I'm tired of keeping secrets from you, you make too many faces."

"I do not make faces."

"You're making one right now."

I was. I checked. I was.

The garden sat in the lee of the school wall, sheltered and southern-facing, and in its center grew the runner — a pale green shoot rising from soil that had been Harrowgate rubble a year ago, threaded through with veins of silver that caught the dusk light like the stars they answered to. The heart-seed. Pip's seed, if you asked Pip; Pip was, as far as any of us could determine, the last seed of the Weeping Wood's heart-tree, spoken into human form generations ago and raised as an orphan without knowing why the forest felt familiar. The tree had shown them that truth in its dying branches, along with showing Wren her complicity and Daine her desertion, and the child had carried it the way children carry everything: openly, and with more strength than the adults around them.

"It wants to go home eventually," Pip said, crouching beside the shoot with the seriousness of a general reviewing troops. "To the wood. But not yet. It's still deciding what kind of tree to be. I told it not to rush. Trees rush and then they're oaks when they wanted to be willows and then they're sad forever."

"Sound counsel."

"Wren's taking the job," Pip said abruptly, not looking at me. "The Survey one. But just part-time. She'll still teach. She asked the wood. Well — she asked me to ask the wood. The wood says it's a good path." A pause. "The wood also says you should stop blaming yourself for Vess."

I kept my face very still, which Pip immediately identified as a face.

"I didn't blame myself for Vess," I said.

"You blamed yourself for him for a long time. For not seeing what he was becoming. For fighting him at the gate instead of saving him." Pip stood and patted my arm, which was possibly the most condescending gesture I have ever received from anyone under four feet tall. "He chose his road. You chose yours. The wood says roads matter more than blood, and the wood would know, it's been watching people walk roads for a thousand years and it says most of you pick badly but some of you don't."

"Some of us don't," I agreed.

"You especially don't." Then, because Pip was Pip and mercy and cruelty lived in the same small body: "Also you snore. The wood hears you through the walls. It thinks it's funny."

* * *

Spring came to the passes late that year, but it came honest — real thaw, real meltwater, the first true season in living memory that owed nothing to any covenant's failing. The long winter had broken the night the confessions were spoken, Wren's careful human truth and Serevan's harder fae truth laid side by side at the roots of the dying tree: *we sacrificed your heart to seal our peace, and we let you weep rather than admit it.* Two realms, one crime, spoken aloud at last. The tree had taken those words and done what trees do with such things — grown.

The new truce terms hung in Harrowgate's square now, drafted in Wren's small precise hand, signed beneath the returning stars by a Crown commander and a Lady of the thorn-court who had arrived expecting treachery and found, to her visible disappointment, paperwork. Trade had followed. Then pilgrims. Then, inevitably, tourists, whom I tolerated the way mountains tolerate weather: without enthusiasm and without alternative.

Serevan herself had visited once, in autumn, drifting through the schoolyard while the children gawked and Daine hovered with her hand nowhere near her sword, which was its own kind of discipline. The Lady had studied the heart-seed's runner for a long time.

"It forgives faster than my people do," she'd said finally.

"It's younger," Pip had replied.

And Serevan — Lady Serevan, centuries old, keeper of a starving court's grief — had laughed. Actually laughed. I'd nearly dropped my pack.

* * *

It was on the Widow's Teeth, a month later, leading the spring's first big caravan through the thaw-melt and the mud, that the traveler asked me the question.

He was a scholar type, one of the new generation of ward students heading north for field study, chatty in the way of people who hadn't yet learned that guides conserve words like water. He'd been working up to something for two days, circling it the way moths circle lanterns, and on the third morning, crossing the high shelf with the dawn coming up gold behind us, he finally landed.

"They say you were a swordspeaker," he said. "The last one. That your oath died with the old covenant when you spoke the truth at the heart-tree." He hesitated. "Do you miss it?"

I stopped walking.

Not because the question wounded. Because it deserved better than a quick answer, and because the view happened to be perfect where we stood — the whole of Thornfell spread below, the smoke of Harrowgate thin and gray, the green line of the Weeping Wood rising renewed in the middle distance, and overhead, in a sky still too bright for stars, the faint ghost-light of three of them holding on through the daylight anyway. Stubborn things. I'd always liked stubborn things.

Did I miss it?

I thought of fifteen years with the mark burning cold on my forearm, the oath that defined me before I understood it, the order dead and its secrets buried in me like shrapnel. I thought of the rite, and speaking every broken vow aloud before the court of thorns, and the strange lightness afterward — grief and liberation in one breath, like setting down a weight I'd mistaken for a spine. I thought of the empty place where the oath-mark used to be, and how for a season I'd kept touching it, checking, the way you probe a missing tooth.

And I thought of the rest. Of Wren weighing her words across a kitchen table and choosing, finally, to trust the absence of traps. Of Daine drilling children in a garrison yard, teaching them to guard things instead of fear them. Of Pip patting my arm and telling me the wood thought my snoring was funny. Of a caravan of strangers trusting me with their lives because my name was on a ledger, plain as bread, with references.

Survival, it turns out, is cheap. Anyone can survive alone; I'd been doing it competently for decades. What I'd paid for on the Widow's Teeth and the White Pass and the Weeping Gate wasn't survival. It was this — the paying of it *not alone*, which is the only version of the price worth anything.

The scholar waited. The caravan waited. Somewhere below us, a hawk rode the thermals over the wood, and the wood stood green and forgiving under it, and the stars hung on in the daylight like they had nowhere else to be and all the time in the world to be there.

"No," I said.

Then I smiled — at the question, at the sky, at the ridiculous improbable fact of being alive and unbound and known — and turned back up the trail.

"Keep moving," I called. "The Widow doesn't wait."

And ahead of us, the road went on — open now, lit now, worth every step — all the way home.
