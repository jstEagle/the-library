## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The mist came down the mountain every morning like a tide going out in reverse, and every morning Dominic Winterbourne stood at her gate to watch it swallow the world.

It was a ritual she had never named and could not have explained. Before the goats were fed, before the fire was banked and the bread set to rise, she climbed the low stone wall that bordered her croft — the wall her grandfather had built, and his father before him, from river stones worn smooth as knuckles — and she looked east, toward the treeline where the Weeping Wood began.

The valley lay below her like something cupped in two hands. Forty hearths' worth of smoke rising thin and gray from Thistlebrook's chimneys. The millpond catching whatever light the dawn allowed. Terraced fields stitched up the slopes in green and gold, and the river running through it all with the patient murmur of water that had never once been asked to hurry.

And beyond it, ringing everything, the trees.

They called it the Weeping Wood because of the way the branches dripped, even on days without rain — great beeches and black pines whose leaves shed moisture in slow silver beads, so that walking beneath them felt like passing through a curtain of falling stars. The old folk said the trees wept for the world outside. The young folk said nothing at all about the wood, because there was nothing to say. It had always been there. It had always been the edge of everything.

Dominic watched the mist crawl up out of the valley's throat and dissolve against those dripping boughs, and she felt the feeling she always felt: the one that lived somewhere between gratitude and grief, though she'd never found a word that held both.

*Safe,* said one voice in her, small and reasonable. *You are safe here.*

*Buried,* said the other, quieter, deeper, in a register she did not let herself listen to often.

She climbed back over the wall and went to feed the goats.

* * *

There were eleven of them this spring, counting the two kids born under the lean-to in the wet week of thawing — Maren and the little black one with the crooked ear whom Dominic had named Turnip, mostly to annoy herself. The goat-shed smelled of hay and warm animal and the sweet-sour musk of milk, and it was, Dominic thought as she worked the milking stool into place beside Bramble's flank, the most honest room in the world.

"None of your opinions," she warned, because Bramble was giving her the look — the flat-lidded, faintly contemptuous stare that meant the feed was late or the straw was damp or the moon was in the wrong quarter, depending entirely on Bramble's private calculations.

Bramble flicked an ear and submitted to the pail.

That was the thing about goats. They were honest the way weather is honest. They wanted what they wanted and they told you plainly, with their bodies, their bellows, the precise geometry of their stubbornness. A goat would not lie to you. A goat did not know how.

People were another matter. People lied so constantly, so fluently, that they had stopped noticing they did it — and Dominic knew this, and hated that she knew it, because knowing it made her strange, and being strange in Thistlebrook was a debt that came due.

She finished the milking, carried the pails up to the house — a squat stone cottage with a roof of turf gone mossy green, three generations of Winterbournes packed into its bones — and set them in the cool of the pantry. Her grandmother's spinning wheel still sat in the corner, though Gran had been dead nine years. Dominic kept oiling it. She couldn't have said why. Some things you keep oiled not because they'll turn again but because letting them rust would mean admitting they never would.

Outside, the neighbor boy Tam Osgood was already on the path, driving his family's geese toward the common pasture, and he raised a hand when he saw her.

"Winterbourne! You coming to market-day? Da says there's a wool factor come all the way from Lowmark."

"Maybe," Dominic said.

"You always say maybe."

"And I'm always right." She smiled to soften it, and Tam grinned and went on, geese complaining behind him like a committee of small disgruntled senators.

She watched him go and felt the familiar tightening in her chest. Tam was seventeen, sun-brown, kind, and utterly uncomplicated, and he had been looking at her lately in the way boys look at girls when they've decided something. In another life she might have decided something back. But deciding things — about Tam, about anything — required a whole self to decide with, and Dominic's self was a house with most of its rooms locked.

Her mother used to say: *A valley keeps what it keeps, Dommie. Be glad of it.*

Be glad of it. As if safety were a gift you could only receive by refusing to open your hands.

* * *

The trouble — the strangeness, the locked rooms — had a shape, and the shape was this:

At the valley's heart, Dominic Winterbourne was nobody at all unusual. A goatherd with strong shoulders and her father's dark hair and a habit of talking to animals, which everyone did; half of Thistlebrook chattered at their beasts like gossips at a well. Nobody thought twice when she scolded the dog or praised a good ewe. Talking *to* animals was human. It was the oldest human thing.

But at the valley's *edge* — at the boundary stones, at the treeline, in that narrow ribbon of land where the mist thinned and the wood's shadow fell long across the grass — something else happened.

There, her words changed weight.

She had discovered it before she had words to describe it, which was perhaps why no adult had ever caught her at it. She must have been six. She'd chased a runaway kid all the way to the last standing stone, past where any sensible child went, and there, breathless, furious, she'd snapped at the creature — *Stop, you wretched little fool, stop* — and the kid had *stopped*. Not out of obedience. Out of understanding. It had turned and looked at her with eyes gone wide and ancient, and something had passed between them like a struck bell, and Dominic had fallen backward into the grass with her heart hammering, certain she had just done something unforgivable.

She hadn't known the word *gift* yet. She wouldn't learn it for years, and then only in whispers, in the story of the woman they'd burned.

But she had understood, even at six, even wordless: *this does not belong here.* Whatever lived in her had crossed some line it wasn't meant to cross, and the valley itself had noticed.

Because the valley forgot. That was the truth of the Weeping Wood that no one spoke aloud, though every soul in Thistlebrook arranged their lives around it. Magic could not follow you in. Witches who fled persecution came here and found their powers dimming like coals in rain, fading within a season to nothing, until they were merely tired women with odd memories. Cursed objects lost their curses crossing the standing stones. The valley was a bowl of quiet, and into that quiet, the wildness of the world simply — drained away.

Which meant that whatever Dominic was, the valley starved it daily. Kept it small. Kept it *safe*.

Kept *her* safe, said the first voice.

Kept her *half*, said the second.

* * *

In the afternoon she took the herd up the western slopes, away from the treeline, the way she always did — the way her mother had taught her without ever saying *teach*, by simply never once grazing the flock near the eastern woods. Old habits, inherited like the croft itself.

But the grass on the western slope was grazed short this late in the season, and the clover patch below the ridge had been stripped bare by Osgood's cattle, and so — telling herself it was practicality and nothing else, hunger in eleven throats and nothing more — Dominic turned the herd along the high path that curved toward the east, toward the boundary, toward the trees.

The change came gradually, the way dusk comes.

First the light shifted, the valley's haze giving way to a cleaner, cooler air that smelled of pine and wet stone. Then the sound changed — the wood's perpetual dripping, soft as breathing. And then, threading through it, the other thing. The thing that had no name in Thistlebrook because Thistlebrook had burned the last person who might have named it.

The goats grew restless. Not frightened — restless, the way children grow restless on the eve of a festival, full of some energy they can't spend. Turnip butted her knee. Bramble lifted her head and stared east with an expression Dominic had learned, over years, to read as *attention*.

And in Dominic's chest, behind her breastbone, something turned over.

It was like a muscle waking after sleep. Like a door in a dark house opening onto lamplit air. The nearer they walked to the standing stones, the louder the world became — not with sound, exactly, but with *meaning*. The wind had intentions. The trees had patience, vast and slow. Somewhere upslope a fox was thinking about eggs, and the thought arrived in Dominic's mind as clearly as if she'd thought it herself: *eggs, under the third stone, the brown hen's nest, yes.*

She stopped walking.

This was the farthest she allowed herself. This was the line she had drawn at seven, at ten, at fourteen, redrawing it every year like a scar. Here the gift stirred and stretched and pressed against the inside of her skin like a bird against a window — and here she made herself turn back, every time, before it woke fully, before it opened its eyes.

Because she knew what happened to people like her in the world beyond the mist. Everyone in Thistlebrook knew. There was a field north of the village, a pleasant meadow by the river, where nothing much grew even now, thirty years on, and where the older folk still crossed themselves without quite knowing why. Marta of the Fen. The beast-talker. The witch. Dominic had been a child when she'd first heard the story told at a winter fireside — told comfortably, the way you tell a story with a moral — how Marta had spoken with wolves and crows, how the sickness in the flocks had been laid at her door, how the men of three villages had gathered on a dry autumn day, and how the smoke had been visible from the valley walls.

Told comfortably. With cider passed around after.

Dominic had sat very still through the whole telling, hands folded, face arranged into the mild horror expected of her, while inside her the locked rooms had all slammed shut at once.

*Marta of the Fen,* she thought now, watching the treeline drip its slow silver rain. *I am what you were.*

The fox-thought faded. The stirring behind her breastbone settled, sullen, back into its sleep. She turned the herd west, downhill, home.

* * *

Evening came down blue and cold. Dominic milked the evening milking, ate barley stew with leeks from the garden, washed the bowl, set it on the shelf beside its four identical brothers. The house was silent except for the tick of the turf-roof cooling and, far off, the river's endless murmur.

Her mother had been dead five years; her father longer. No siblings. The Winterbournes had thinned to a single thread, and Dominic was it, and sometimes at night she felt that thread vibrating with a tension she couldn't source — as if it were tied at both ends to things she couldn't see, and something on one end had begun, very slowly, to pull.

She sat in her grandmother's chair with a rushlight guttering beside her and tried to read, and failed, and instead did the thing she did on nights like this, the dangerous thing, the secret thing she permitted herself perhaps once a month like a miser counting coins.

She remembered.

Not the valley. The *edge*. The one time — she had been nineteen, and it had rained for a week and the herd had scattered, and she'd pursued a strayed nanny all the way past the standing stones, past sense, past safety, right up to the wood's first rank of trees with their leaves shedding silver — and there, soaked and desperate and past caring, she had spoken.

Not talked. *Spoken.* The way the gift spoke, when it was awake.

*Nanny. Come back to me.*

And the whole wood had answered. Every living thing within earshot had heard her — she'd felt it land on the forest like a hand laid flat on water — and the nanny had come crashing back through the bracken to press her blunt, warm head against Dominic's chest, and the crows had risen from the canopy in a single black exhalation, crying out words, actual words, ragged crow-words of alarm and wonder: *she speaks, she speaks, the speaker wakes—*

For one heartbeat Dominic had stood inside her own true self, entire, unhidden, enormous—

—and then she had run home through the rain, and scrubbed the memory down, and buried it deep with all the others, and in the morning she had been plain Dominic Winterbourne again, goatherd of Thistlebrook, keeper of a quiet croft in a valley that forgot.

*Safe,* whispered the first voice, drowsing by the fire.

*Half,* whispered the second.

She pinched out the rushlight and lay in the dark listening to the rain begin, soft on the turf above her head.

* * *

Three mornings later, the mist came down the mountain as it always did, and Dominic stood at her gate to watch it — and saw, at the far edge of vision, where the treeline met the eastern sky, a single figure break from the trees.

He came stumbling down the slope toward the valley at a broken, lurching run, a man-shaped smear of brown and red, and even at that distance Dominic could see the way he fell and dragged himself up and fell again. Behind him the wood stood silent, dripping. Behind him, farther off than sight, something had clearly driven him out of it.

The goats were at the fence, heads up, utterly still. All eleven of them staring east.

Bramble turned her head and fixed Dominic with one yellow eye, and though the valley's quiet pressed down as it always pressed, muting the world to mere animal — Dominic could have sworn, in that moment, that the look meant something. That it was a warning shaped like a glance.

The man crossed the boundary stones and collapsed in the wet grass twenty paces from her gate, and did not get up.

Dominic stood frozen, the mist curling cold around her ankles, her heart slamming against the locked rooms of her ribs — and then she was running.
