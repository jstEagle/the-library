## 2. A Stranger at the Treeline

The morning the stranger came, the fog lay over the valley like a shroud laid by careful hands.

Dominic Winterbourne had risen before dawn, as she always did, to the sound of rain on the thatch — not true rain, but the fine, ceaseless drizzle that gave the Weeping Wood its name and kept its mosses green and its streams fat and its people soft with damp. She had banked the fire, milked the goats, argued with Bramble about eating the washing line, and set out along the eastern path with a crook in one hand and a heel of yesterday's bread in her pocket. Ordinary things. The valley was made of ordinary things, stacked like stones in a wall: chores and neighbors and market days and the slow turning of seasons, each one pressing down on the last so firmly that nothing strange could grow between them.

She told herself she liked it that way.

The goats knew better. The goats always knew better.

*Edge today,* said Sorrel, falling into step beside her, her amber eyes reflecting the grey light. *You smell of edge.*

"I smell of nothing," Dominic said. "I smell of goat."

*Goat and edge,* Sorrel insisted, and butted her hip companionably, which was as close as any creature came to contradiction in Dominic's presence.

She let the argument go, because there was no winning it, and because Sorrel was right, though neither of them had words for what *edge* meant exactly. The Weeping Wood occupied a fold in the world where magic could not follow — everyone said so, though no one said it plainly, the way no one said plainly what had happened to Marta Ellery two generations back, or why the standing stone at the treeline was scorched black on one face, or why children who talked too much to animals were taught, gently and then less gently, to stop. Magic came to the boundary of the valley and stopped, like a tide meeting glass. And whatever lived in Dominic — whatever had lived in her grandmother's stories and her mother's silences and her own sleepless nights — stopped too, pressed flat, folded small, buried under all those ordinary stones.

Most days she could almost forget it was there.

Almost.

* * *

The treeline stood half a mile beyond the last croft, where the pasture gave way to elder and alder and the ground turned spongy with centuries of fallen leaves. The boundary itself was invisible — no wall, no fence, only the standing stone with its burnt face and the sense, felt rather than known, of crossing from one kind of silence into another. On the valley side, the silence was human and safe. Beyond it, the silence was older, watchful, full of things that whispered when they thought no one was listening.

Dominic went to the edge perhaps once a month, no more. It was a discipline, like fasting. She would stand just inside the treeline with the wind moving through the branches overhead, and she would feel the buried thing in her chest stir and uncurl like something waking from a long winter, and she would let herself feel it for exactly as long as it took to drink from the spring and fill her flask — no longer — and then she would walk away, fast, before it could speak through her throat, before the birds could answer, before she remembered what it was to be whole and had to spend another month learning how to be broken instead.

Today she had meant only to check the far paddock fence where the posts had gone soft.

She found the courier instead.

He was lying in the bracken twenty paces past the standing stone, on the far side of the boundary, and even at a distance she could see the wrongness of him — the angle of his legs, the dark stain soaking the ground beneath his ribs, the way the mist seemed to gather around him as if drawn there. Her first thought, absurdly, was that he was dead already, and her second was that he was not, because his hand moved.

"Don't—" His voice cracked across the morning like a whip. "Don't cross. Don't— come round. The stone. Come round by the stone."

She froze, one foot lifted. He had seen her. He had seen her and his first word had been about the boundary, and something cold walked down her spine, because strangers did not know about boundaries. Strangers saw a valley and walked into it. Only people who understood what the Weeping Wood was for would warn her off the direct path.

"Who are you?" she called.

"Come round," he said again, and then coughed, and the cough was wet and terrible. "Please. I can't come to you. Can't cross it. They can't either." A laugh that was mostly blood. "Small mercies."

Every instinct she owned told her to run for the village, for her father's old friends with their staves and dogs, for anywhere that was not here. But he had said *please*, and he had said *they*, and both of those words were hooks, and she hated herself a little for what she did next, which was to skirt wide around the standing stone, keeping carefully inside the valley's edge until she reached him — reached the place where the ground changed under her feet like a threshold, like stepping from a warm room into deep water — and knelt in the wet ferns an arm's length away.

Up close he was young. Younger than she'd thought — twenty, maybe twenty-five, with a courier's lean frame and mud-spattered riding leathers and a face that had been handsome before someone had opened it up along the jaw with a blade. The wound had been bound, badly, with a strip torn from a cloak, and the binding was black through. His eyes found hers and held them, and they were fever-bright and desperately clear all at once, the way eyes get when a man knows he is spending his last hour and intends to spend every coin of it.

"You're from the valley," he rasped.

"I'm from the valley."

"Then listen. Listen, because I don't have the time to say it twice and you don't have the time to pretend you didn't hear." He fumbled at his chest, at a lacquered tube strapped there under his cloak, and his fingers wouldn't work right, shaking, slick with blood, and Dominic heard herself make a small sound and leaned in despite everything, despite the voice in her head screaming that this was how trouble came, this was exactly how it had come for Marta Ellery, trouble arriving bleeding at your feet and asking to be picked up.

He got the tube free. Inside it, a scroll wrapped in oilskin; inside the oilskin, a map, heavy parchment gone soft with handling, folded around something stiff. He tried to unfold it and couldn't, so he pushed the whole bundle against her chest with the last strength of his arm.

"Take it."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it." His breath whistled. "That's the point. Nobody wants it and everybody needs it and the ones who want it most are the ones who must never—" Another cough, folding him nearly double, and she caught his shoulder without thinking, and he gripped her wrist with shocking strength and pulled her close, close enough that she could smell the iron reek of him and see the burst vessels in his eyes.

"They're coming behind me," he whispered. "Witch-hunters. Grey cloaks, ash-grey, six days back on my trail, maybe five now. They burned Hallowmere for a rumor. A rumor. Whole town, and the well poisoned after, so nothing would grow. If they find the map—" He swallowed. "If they find it, the Heartroot wakes to their hands instead of ours, and everything the wood ever loved dies screaming. Do you understand? Everything."

"The Heartroot," Dominic repeated. The word came out of her strangely, tasting of something old. She had never heard it before, and yet some buried part of her flinched at it the way a body flinches at a name called in the dark.

"The root of all of it. All the wild magic, every gift, every beast-tongue, every—" He stopped. His gaze sharpened suddenly, going from her eyes to her mouth, to her hands still gripping his shoulder. "Wait. Wait. You're a Winterbourne."

It was not a question. It landed on her like a dropped stone.

"How do you know my name?"

"Your grandmother." Something like relief broke across his ruined face, terrible to see, hope in a dying man being worse than despair. "Edda Winterbourne. She drew half this map. She hid the other half where I couldn't reach it in time. She said—" He laughed again, that bloody bubble of a laugh, "she said if ever it came down to a stranger at the treeline, trust the valley to send a warden's blood."

"My grandmother kept bees," Dominic said. Her voice sounded very far away. "She died eleven years ago."

"She kept more than bees." His grip on her wrist tightened. "And now she keeps you, whether you like it or not. Take the map east. Follow the river Vane to the drowned chapel, then up, always up, to Thornfell and past it. There's a grove above the snowline. You'll know it when—" Coughing took him again, longer this time, and when it released him he sagged back into the ferns, grey-lipped, spent.

"I'm not going anywhere," Dominic said. "I'm going to get help. Old Tam has a cart, we can carry you—"

"No." It was almost a shout. "No cart, no village, no help. You carry me past that stone and I die on the other side, do you hear me? Whatever wards this valley — it doesn't suffer things like me, things touched by the outside. And worse—" his eyes darted past her, toward the treeline, toward the mist, "worse, they've learned to track the crossing. Anyone who carries me in leads them straight to every soul in that valley. You'd be bringing the fire home. You understand? The pyres. Like Hallowmere. Like Marta Ellery."

The name hit her like ice water. She hadn't said it aloud. He had read it in her face, or in her blood, or in whatever it was that grandmothers left in their granddaughters along with maps and burdens.

"Marta was a story we tell children," Dominic said.

"Marta was a warden," the courier said. "Like your grandmother. Like you. That's the story they tell instead."

* * *

Afterward she could never piece together the order of things. Whether he spoke first or pressed the map first, whether the light was failing or only seemed to, whether the drizzle stopped or simply became impossible to notice. What remained was fragments, worn smooth by retelling in the privacy of her own skull:

His hand folding hers closed over the map, forcing her fingers shut around it one by one, as if sealing a letter.

The wax seal, deep green, stamped with a tree whose roots spread wider than its branches.

His whisper, closer than a whisper, directly into her ear, so that the words bypassed her mind entirely and lodged somewhere below her heart:

*"The Heartroot wakes. Tell it — tell it we didn't forget. Tell it somebody's coming."*

And then the weight of his hand going slack, and the mist closing in, and the extraordinary quiet of the Weeping Wood, which had never in her life seemed quiet to her before.

She sat in the wet ferns for a long time. The drizzle resumed, fine as sifted flour, beading on the map's oilskin wrapping, beading on her lashes. Somewhere upslope a wren sang three notes and stopped, as if listening.

Bramble found her there — the old billy goat, who never ranged this far, who should not have been able to cross the boundary at all and yet stood placidly at the treeline chewing, regarding her with his flat rectangular pupils. Behind him, further back, safely on the valley side, stood Sorrel and the rest, a ragged white-and-brown row along the fence line, all of them watching her.

*Dead,* said Bramble, conversationally, nodding at the courier.

"Yes," Dominic said, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word.

*They are coming,* said Sorrel from the fence, and there was something in the ewe's tone that Dominic had never heard an animal use before. It took her a moment to recognize it, because it was not a sound beasts made. It was fear. Plain, human-shaped fear, spoken in a sheep's voice.

*Grey men. Smoke-smell. Coming.*

Dominic looked down at the map in her hands. At the courier's cooling body, already beginning its slow argument with the moss and the beetles. At the standing stone with its burnt face, which had guarded this valley for longer than anyone remembered and which had, she understood now with a clarity that felt like falling, been built to keep something in as much as to keep something out.

Her grandmother had drawn half this map. Her grandmother had hidden her, here, in the one place magic could not follow — not to save her from destiny, maybe, but to hide the last ember of something in the one place the hunters' fires could not see.

Or maybe that was vanity. Maybe Edda Winterbourne had really only kept bees, and the map was a madman's riddle, and the grey cloaks were five days away from nothing at all.

But the goats were afraid, and in twenty-three years of understanding them, Dominic had never once known them to be afraid of nothing.

She got up. Her knees were wet through. She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, tucked the map inside her shirt against her skin where it lay cold and stiff as a second breastbone, and stood a moment looking down at the dead man who had crossed a kingdom to die at her feet with a stranger's name in his mouth.

"Warden's blood," she said aloud, testing the words, hating them.

The wren sang its three notes again. Down the slope, the goats began to move toward the village, unhurried, deliberate, and she understood that they were waiting for her to follow, and that following them meant walking back into a life that had ended sometime in the last hour without bothering to inform her.

Behind her, far off in the mist beyond the treeline — so far she might have imagined it — a horn sounded. Once. Low and patient, like something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Dominic Winterbourne turned her back on the treeline, on the dead, on the burning edge of her old life, and walked down into the valley that forgot.

She was already counting the days. Five, he had said. Perhaps four.

She had never once, in her whole careful buried life, imagined she would need to pack quickly.
