## 3. What the Goats Knew

Dominic buried the courier at dusk, in the soft ground just inside the valley's edge where the birches grew pale and close together like a congregation of the silent.

She had not wanted to touch him at first. The blood had gone black on his doublet, and his skin had taken on the waxy stillness of things that no longer belong to the living world. But no one else would come for him — the valley had made that plain by afternoon, when she'd run back down the sheep track to the millhouse and found doors closing, one after another, like a slow blink of refusal. Old Marta had looked at her through a crack in her shutters with eyes like river stones and said nothing at all. The smith had spat into his fire. Even the children had been called indoors, as though death itself were a contagion she carried now in her pockets alongside the wax-sealed map.

So it was Dominic alone who dug, alone who rolled him into the shallow grave with a gentleness that surprised her, alone who whispered over him the only words the valley kept for such occasions: *Go where the water goes.* It was what they said at funerals here, in a place where nothing was permitted to linger — not magic, not grief, not the dead.

His hands had been the worst part. They were a courier's hands, rope-scarred and weathered, and they had been curled around something even as he died, fingers clenched on empty air as if the map were still there, as if letting go of it was the one thing he could not do even after everything else had failed him.

*The Heartroot wakes,* he had whispered. She turned the words over now as she filled the grave, patting down the dark earth with the flat of her spade. She did not know what they meant. She suspected, with a cold weight settling behind her ribs, that knowing would be worse than not knowing.

The goats knew before she did.

She heard them from thirty paces off — a sound she had never heard from them before, a keening, high and threaded through with something that was unmistakably fear. Bramble's voice above all the others, the old matriarch who ruled the small herd with contemptuous indifference, crying out like a kid separated from its mother.

They were standing at the boundary line.

Dominic stopped. Her breath went strange in her chest.

Everyone in the valley knew about the line, though no one spoke of it directly; it was one of those truths that lived in the way people glanced at the treeline rather than in anything anyone dared say aloud. It ran along the northern rim of the valley where the Weeping Wood began in earnest — where the trees stopped being the tame, half-hearted birches and alders of home and became something older, darker, hung with moss that wept perpetual moisture into the air. The valley forgot. That was its nature and its gift. Whatever moved through the world beyond — sorcery, curse, blessing, blight — came to the edge of the hollow and slid away like rain off oiled cloth.

And Dominic's gift — the thing she had never named aloud, the thing that lived coiled and dormant beneath her breastbone like a second heart — forgot too.

At least, it mostly forgot. At the boundary, on days when the wind blew wrong, she sometimes felt it stir. A pressure behind her eyes. The sense that the birdsong overhead contained grammar. The unsettling conviction that the goats' bleating was not noise but complaint, specific and articulate, and that if she only leaned closer to some invisible door in her own mind, she would understand every word.

Her mother had caught her once, when she was nine years old, crouched in the pasture with her ear nearly touching Bramble's broad grey muzzle, listening with her whole body. Her mother had gone white as flour and dragged her indoors and held her so hard her ribs ached, and said, in a voice scraped raw: *Never at the line. Never past the line. Promise me, little bird.* And because her mother had been dying even then, slowly, of something the herb-wives couldn't name, Dominic had promised. She had kept the promise for fifteen years. It had been easy, in the way that starving is easy — a daily practice, a discipline of not-listening, until the not-listening became so habitual she could almost believe there had never been anything to hear.

Now Bramble stood three strides past the boundary stones, and she was speaking, and Dominic could understand her.

*"It comes, it comes, it comes up the trail with iron smell and dog-smell and the stink of burned hair, it comes, it comes—"*

Dominic's knees nearly folded. It was like being struck. Fifteen years of silence torn open all at once, and the world roared in — not just Bramble, but everything: the jays screaming territorial insults from the alders (*mine mine mine mine*), the red deer ghosting through the ferns beyond the line (*run run the two-legs are running*), a fox somewhere upslope muttering to itself in a dry, sardonic voice about the shortage of voles this season. The whole world had been talking all along. All along. And she had spent her life in a room with the door bricked shut, calling the silence peace.

"Bramble," she said, and her voice came out cracked and absurd, human speech suddenly feeling like a child's crude imitation of language.

The old goat swung her horned head around. Her horizontal pupils fixed on Dominic with an intensity that raised the hair on her arms.

*"You hear."* Not a question. Wonder and accusation tangled together in the flat, chewing tones of goat-speech. *"You finally hear, girl-of-the-milk-hands. Where have you been? We have been shouting for years. YEARS. Did you think we liked bleating?"*

"I didn't—" Dominic pressed a hand to her mouth. Her eyes were burning. "I couldn't. There's a line. I'm never—"

*"You're standing at the line now."* Bramble picked her way closer, hooves precise on the leaf litter, and the rest of the herd crowded behind her — Marigold and the twins and young Sorrel, all of them staring, all of them silent for once, which from goats was perhaps the greatest tribute they could pay. *"Why are you at the line? You never come to the line. You keep your herd safe and dull, safe and dull, like a good grass-mother."*

"A man died," Dominic said. "Past the line. He gave me something."

The change that came over Bramble then was terrible to witness. The old goat's ears flattened. Her neck lowered until her horns aimed forward like spears, and a low vibration started in her chest, a sound felt more than heard, and every other goat took it up until the air itself seemed to tremble.

*"Iron,"* Bramble said. *"Dog. Fire. Burned hair. They follow the dead man's trail, girl. Four of them and their beasts and something worse than beasts walking at the center, something that smells of nothing at all. They crossed the river ford at moonrise. They will reach the place where he fell by tomorrow's full light."* Her voice climbed, cracked, broke into that keening again. *"Nothing good has ever followed that smell. Nothing good has ever followed it and LIVED."*

* * *

She should have gone back down into the valley. She knew that. Every instinct bred into her by fifteen years of careful, suffocating safety screamed it — go home, bar the door, bury the map under the hearthstone or drop it down the well, let the hunters find a dead stranger and a closed valley and nothing else, because that is how you survive, little bird, that is the whole of survival: you make yourself small and you stay forgotten.

But the map was in her hand. She had carried it up from the graveside without deciding to, and now she stood at the boundary with the last light failing and the wax seal cold against her palm, and the gift roared in her ears like a spring flood, and she understood — with the clarity that comes only at the exact moment when understanding becomes useless — that hiding was already over. The courier had bled out on the valley's threshold. Hunters tracked his blood across three counties. The valley's forgetting might blind them to magic, but it would not blind them to footprints, to a disturbed grave, to a girl who had been seen carrying a stranger's burden.

And underneath the fear, quieter but insistent as a heartbeat, was Bramble saying *we have been shouting for years*, and the memory of every conversation she'd never had, every mind she'd never touched, pressing against the broken door in her chest and refusing to be shut again.

"Show me," she said to the fox.

The fox had drifted downslope while the goats panicked, drawn by curiosity, its rust-red shape flickering between the ferns. It sat now at the very edge of the line, tail curled over its feet, regarding her with amber insolence.

*"Show you what?"* it said. Its voice was exactly as sly as its face. *"I show many things. Chickens, mostly. Sometimes where the ground hares sleep. My rates are reasonable."*

"The trail the men are following. The dead man's trail. Where does it lead?"

The fox licked its lips. *"Ah. THAT trail."* It glanced northward, toward the deep wood, and for the first time its insolence faltered. *"That trail leads to the old grove. The green dark place. The place my mother's mother told me never to sniff, never to dig, never to dream about, on pain of becoming a story to frighten kits."* It looked back at her. *"You carry his scent on your hands, milk-girl. Dead-man scent and wax and old paper. The iron-men will smell it on you too, if they catch you. And they will catch you. They always catch the ones who smell of the grove."*

"What's in the grove?" Dominic asked, though some part of her already knew, had known since the courier breathed his last whisper into the cold air between them.

The fox's ears went flat, mimicking Bramble without meaning to. *"The Heart,"* it said simply. *"Or what's left of it. The thing the wood dreams around. The thing that is going out."* It stood, stretched, and shook itself. *"Going out like a fire left untended. Going out the way your gift went out, girl-of-the-line. Have you never wondered why the valley forgets? Things don't forget on their own. Things are MADE to forget."*

The words landed in her like stones dropped into a well — she heard them fall and fall and never heard them land. Made to forget. Her whole life arranged around a silence someone had built.

Bramble butted her knee, hard enough to bruise. *"Stop standing. Stop standing and thinking, thinking is how prey die. The iron-men come with the full light. You are at the line. Your scent is on the wind. DECIDE, milk-girl."*

Dominic looked down into the valley. Lamplight was kindling in the scattered houses, warm and gold and utterly inadequate — a handful of flames against the dark that was coming. Somewhere down there, Marta was banking her fire. Somewhere down there, the smith's forge had gone cold. They would shutter their windows tonight and tell each other the trouble had passed them by, because trouble always passed them by, that was the covenant of the valley: give us nothing, ask nothing, and be forgotten safely.

They had closed their doors on a dying man. They would close their doors on her.

She looked north instead. The Weeping Wood rose black and breathing against the last violet band of sky, dripping, ancient, patient. Somewhere in its depths, a heart was going out. Somewhere behind her, four riders and something that smelled of nothing were following a dead man's blood up a mountain trail, and tomorrow they would find a fresh-turned grave and read it like a signpost.

The gift surged in her chest — terror and wonder braided into a single unbearable cord — and for the first time in her life, Dominic Winterbourne did not push it down.

"Watch the herd," she told Bramble. Her voice didn't shake. That surprised her most of all.

*"Where will you go?"* the old goat asked, and beneath the gruffness, unmistakably, was grief.

"Where the water goes," Dominic said. "Where the trail leads." She stepped over the boundary stones.

The moment her boot came down on the far side, the world changed. Not dramatically — the trees were the same trees, the dusk the same dusk — but the quality of the air shifted, thickened, as though she had walked through a curtain of cold water. Magic could not follow into the valley. Now, standing outside it, she felt what had been kept out: a vast, slow pressure all around her, the heartbeat of the wood itself, faint and irregular as a failing pulse.

The fox trotted beside her, uninvited. *"I'll walk with you as far as the stream,"* it said. *"Not into the green dark. I have standards."* Then, after a few strides, quieter: *"The iron-men killed a wolf-pack's den-mother on their way up the vale, did you know? Shot her from horseback and laughed. The wolves remember. Wolves always remember."* It glanced up at her, amber eye gleaming in the gathering dark. *"If I were a girl with your particular ears, milk-girl, I would start making friends very quickly indeed."*

Behind her, dimming with every step, the lamplights of the valley shrank to embers. Ahead, the wood opened its wet black throat.

Somewhere in it, the Heartroot was dying. Somewhere behind her, death was following.

Dominic pulled her cloak tight, put the map against her heart, and walked into the dark.
