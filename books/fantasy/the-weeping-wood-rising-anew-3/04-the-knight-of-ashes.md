## 4. The Knight of Ashes

The rain had followed Ser Aldric Vane for three days, as though the sky itself held a grudge against him, and he had begun to accept that perhaps it did.

He rode a grey mare named Patience, because naming a thing after what you lacked was the only honest prayer he knew anymore. She picked her way down the switchbacks of the Dolorous Pass with the sure-footedness of an animal that trusted its rider even when the rider could not be said to trust himself. Below them, the land opened out in long grey-green folds — farmland gone to seed, hedgerows grown wild, a road that had once carried pilgrims now carrying nothing but wind and Aldric's warrant.

In his saddlebag lay that warrant, sealed in white wax with the Crown's sunburst pressed deep into it. *By order of His Majesty's Grace: let no gift of the old blood go unpunished. Let no root of magic remain unburnt.* He had read it so many times the words had worn smooth inside him, like river stones, like the pommel of his sword where twenty years of his hand had polished the leather to bare wood.

Twenty years. He had been nineteen when he took the white cloak. He remembered standing in the Hall of Bells while the Lord Commander fastened it at his throat, and he remembered thinking — with the bright, uncomplicated certainty of youth — *this is the work of cleaning the world.* Filth, they called it. The taint. The rot that had crept up from the Heartroot when the world was young, seeping into the bloodlines of certain families until their daughters spoke to wolves and their sons called lightning down from clear skies. The Crown had decided, some ninety years past, that the world would be cleaner without it. And someone had to do the cleaning.

Aldric had been very good at it.

He reined Patience off the road where the courier's trail left it. Three days old now, the tracks, churned soft by rain but still legible to a man who had spent half his life reading ground the way priests read scripture. A single rider, pushing hard, changing mounts at the way-stations — no, not changing them. *Stealing* them, or buying them at ruinous price. A man in a hurry who did not want to be hurried. Twice the trail bent toward high ground where a rider could look back down the road behind him.

Someone had been following him too.

Aldric dismounted at the treeline and walked, leading the mare, letting her ears do half his work. The forest here was not the Weeping Wood itself — nothing grew quite so dense as the Weeping Wood — but it had that same wet hush about it, the sense of being watched by something green and patient. Birch and alder, black pools standing between the roots, moss thick enough to muffle his boots entirely. Somewhere overhead a crow said one dry word and then thought better of it.

He found the courier just past midday.

The man had crawled. That was the first thing Aldric understood, crouching over the body — he had been struck or cut somewhere back along the trail and had kept moving, hand over hand through the bracken, dragging his legs, for hours. There was a great deal of blood, most of it dried to a brown crust across the leaf litter, and the smell of it was old and iron-sweet beneath the smell of rain. He had died on his side, knees drawn up, one arm flung forward as if reaching for —

Aldric turned and looked where the dead man reached.

The valley. The Weeping Wood. It lay below him like a cupped hand, green and sheltered, smoke rising thin from a handful of chimneys. From this height he could see the fields, the goat-paths stitched along the hillsides, the little stone bridge over the water they called the Mourning Beck because everything in that country had to weep about something. It looked, from up here, exactly like what it pretended to be: the most harmless place in the kingdom.

But Aldric had spent two decades learning what places pretended.

*The Weeping Wood. Where magic cannot follow.* Every child learned the rhyme. *Where the witch's word falls dead, where the spell runs dry, where the hunted go to hide.* Ninety years ago the Crown's artificers had raised the severance — a working so vast that no one living understood it anymore — and the valley had become a wound of silence in the fabric of the world. Magic could not enter. Neither, then, could anything magic sustained. The gifted who fled there were safe, and hollowed, and slowly forgotten.

Which made it a prison. Which made it, in the Crown's arithmetic, a solution.

And which meant, now, that a dying courier had dragged himself to its very edge and reached across that invisible line toward it — and whatever he had carried had gone into the valley instead of staying with him.

Aldric knelt and went through the body with the methodical care of long habit. Pockets first. Then the doublet, seam by seam. Boots. Belt. The courier had been young, mid-twenties perhaps, dark-haired, well-fed for a courier — someone's favored man. In his inner lining, slit open and empty, there remained the ghost-shape of a document pressed flat against his ribs: a rectangle of slightly less faded cloth, and three drops of white wax still clinging to the wool like tiny teeth.

White wax. The Crown's seal.

Aldric sat back on his heels and let out a breath he had been holding, it felt, since morning.

So. The courier had carried a sealed dispatch of royal make, hidden in his lining rather than in any satchel a thief would search, riding hard, watching the road behind him, and dying with his arm stretched toward the one place in the world where the Crown's writ went strange. And the dispatch was gone — taken, Aldric would have wagered his cloak, in the courier's last minutes of life, because the crawling track ended here, at the boundary, where a man from the valley might plausibly have come out to gather firewood or check snares and found a stranger bleeding at the edge of his safe little world.

Someone from the valley had taken it.

He stood, and Patience shifted behind him, and the crow above spoke its one word again — *hah, hah* — and Aldric looked down into the green cup of the valley for a long time.

His orders were simple, as the worst orders always were. Recover the map. Burn it unread if possible; memorize and destroy if not. Identify any local who had handled it and assess whether they required the attention of the Commission. *Assess*, in the language of the white cloaks, was a word with ash under its fingernails. Assess meant pyres, sometimes. Assess meant wagons leaving villages before dawn with families pressed into them, bound for the trials in the capital, where the machinery of judgment ran all winter like a mill.

He had delivered assessments before. He could count them, if he let himself, and he did not let himself, except at night, except in the space between waking and sleep where the counting came anyway, uninvited, patient as debt.

*Eleven,* said the space between sleeping and waking. *You lit eleven yourself, and you signed for nine more.*

One of them had been small. One of them had asked him, before the end, whether it hurt, and he had told her the truth — *yes, but not for long* — because he had never been able to make himself lie to them, and that, more than the burning, was the thing that had followed him ever after. The mercy of honesty. The coward's mercy.

He shook it off the way a dog shakes off water. It never worked entirely. It never had.

* * *

He made camp that night at the treeline, within sight of the body, because he wanted to know who came for it.

Nobody came.

That was its own kind of answer. A village that found a corpse at its boundary and left it for the crows was either a village that hadn't found it yet, or a village that wanted very much not to be seen finding it. Aldric built a small fire, cooked hard bread and salt pork, and watched the valley lights — six of them, maybe seven, scattered hearth-glows in the dark — the way other men watched dice.

Near midnight, the animals began.

It started with the dogs. Far off, down in the valley, a farm dog began to bay — not the ragged alarm of a fox-chase but something longer, lower, rhythmic, almost formal. Then a second voice joined it, and a third, and then, fainter, from the hills beyond the valley's rim, came sounds Aldric had not heard in years and had hoped never to hear again: wolves. Not hunting-cries. Something older and stranger, long rising howls that bent upward at the end, that sounded less like hunger than like — grief. Or warning. As if every animal within a night's travel of that valley had received the same terrible news and was announcing it to the dark.

Patience pressed close to him and trembled.

Aldric put a hand on her neck. "Easy," he said quietly, uselessly. "Easy, girl."

But his own heart was going hard, and his mouth had gone dry, and the old cold feeling — the one he got in the presence of the taint, the one he had once mistaken for righteousness — crawled up his spine and settled at the base of his skull like a hand.

Because he knew what animal-cry like that meant. He had heard it once before, eleven years ago, in a village called Marrow's End, on the night before he lit his eleventh pyre. All the beasts in the district had cried out together, dogs and cattle and crows and one wolf pack far up in the fells, and in the morning the girl they had condemned had told him — she had *told* him, looking straight at him with those calm grey eyes — *they're saying goodbye to me. Can't you hear it? They're sad.*

And he had lit the fire anyway, because the warrant said, and the Lord Commander said, and the world was supposed to be cleaner.

The howling rose and fell, rose and fell, and then, near dawn, stopped all at once, everywhere, like a door closing.

Aldric sat alone by his dead fire and understood, with the weary clarity of a man watching a wall crack, that whatever the courier had carried into the valley, it had already begun working on the place. News travels strangely in the old blood's world. The beasts had heard something. Which meant the gift was awake in there somewhere — dormant, buried, starved by the severance, yes, but awake enough to listen.

Or waking.

*The Heartroot wakes.*

The words surfaced in him unbidden, and he frowned, because he didn't know where they'd come from — and then he did. Dispatch gossip. The Lord Commander's closed meetings, the ones even senior knights weren't admitted to, the ones where the commander spoke of the commission's true purpose in a lowered voice: *not containment, Vane. Completion. Ninety years of cutting the weed and we've only pruned it. There is a source. There has always been a source. Find the map and we finish the war our grandfathers started.*

Aldric had nodded, then. He had believed, in his gray way, that finishing wars was a kindness.

Now he sat at the edge of the Weeping Wood with a dead courier cooling beside him and listened to the silence where the howling had been, and found that the belief had a hole in it, and through the hole he could see the girl at Marrow's End, and her grey eyes, and her question he had never answered: *can't you hear it?*

No. He couldn't. He had never been able to hear anything but orders.

* * *

Morning came up gray and slow. Aldric buried the courier — not from tenderness, precisely, though there was some of that; mostly because a body left for the crows drew questions, and questions drew the wrong hunters. Rival bounty men worked these hills, freelancers with commissions and no cloaks, men who would burn a whole village for the reward on a single map. If the Crown's own knight could not keep this quiet, the Crown would lose it to vultures.

He dug the grave shallow among the birches, stacked stones over it, and marked it with the courier's own belt-buckle nailed to a sapling. He said the few words the rite allowed — *ash to ash, clean to clean* — and heard, as he always heard, how much of the rite was wishful thinking dressed as doctrine. Nothing about the world was clean. Not the work. Not the worker.

Then he saddled Patience and rode down toward the valley, taking his time, letting the grey mare amble like a man with nowhere particular to be. A peddler's cap and a trader's coat went on over his mail; the white cloak stayed rolled and hidden beneath the saddle flap. At the stone bridge over the Mourning Beck he stopped, as a traveler naturally would, to water the horse, and studied the village with a peddler's idle eyes.

Small. Forty souls, perhaps fifty. A smithy, a shrine with a weatherworn saint, terraced gardens climbing the slopes. Goats everywhere — goats on the hillsides, goats in lanes, a boy asleep on a wall with a goat standing guard over him like a soldier. And people who moved, when they moved at all, the way people move who are used to being watched: unhurried, incurious, eyes sliding past a stranger and away.

Except one.

A woman, young, dark-haired, coming down the hillside path with a crook over her shoulder and two goats trailing her — not driven, he noticed. *Trailing*. Walking at her heels like dogs, close enough to touch, and when she paused at the bend of the path, they paused. When she looked back over her shoulder at the treeline — at the exact stretch of trees where the courier had died, where the grave now lay — the goats looked too.

She stood there a moment too long, face lifted toward the forest, and even across the distance Aldric saw the shape of what crossed her expression. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a person hearing a name called from a house they'd been told was empty.

Then she caught sight of him at the bridge, and everything in her shut at once — face, shoulders, hands — the practiced blankness of the valley folk, only faster, only harder, as if she'd had a lifetime more practice than the rest.

Their eyes met across the water. Hers were the color of the valley in autumn: green going gold. And behind the blankness, behind the careful nothing she wore like armor, something looked back at him and weighed him, and he had the sudden vertiginous certainty that she knew exactly what he was.

He tipped his peddler's cap. She did not return it. She turned up the lane with her goats flowing after her like spilled shadow, and was gone between the houses.

Aldric stood a moment longer, water dripping from his cupped hand, and felt the crack in his conviction widen by another finger's width.

*Assess,* said his orders. *Identify and assess.*

He looked up the hill where she had vanished, at the smoke of the valley rising straight and innocent into the gray air, at the treeline where a young man lay under new-laid stones with a belt-buckle for a headstone, and he thought about fires, and about grey eyes, and about how many times a man could tell himself the world was being cleaned before he admitted he was only sweeping ash.

"Find the map," he said aloud, to the running water, because saying it aloud was easier than thinking it. "Just find the map."

The beck said nothing. But up on the hillside, faintly, a goat began to call — one long, low note, repeated — and though Aldric could not have said why, he had the distinct, unwelcome feeling that it was calling *him*.
