## 5. Taken on the Road

The goats woke her before dawn.

Not the ordinary waking — not Bramble butting the door latch because he'd learned to work it, or the ewes bleating at the grey light as though it owed them something. This was different. A low, rolling murmur through the barn wall, a chorus of unease, and beneath it, threading through Dominic's sleep like cold water through a floorboard crack, a single word that was not a word at all but a shape of meaning pressed against the inside of her skull:

*Run.*

She sat up in the dark of her loft, heart hammering, and for a long moment could not remember where she was. That was the way of it here in the Weeping Wood, where magic could not follow — her gift came to her only in fragments now, half-dreams, the ghost of a sense she'd spent thirty years burying alive. At the valley's edge it stirred. In her own bed it should have been nothing.

But the goats were speaking. She could feel them the way you feel a storm coming in an old scar.

"Quiet," she whispered uselessly, and swung down from the loft into the cold.

The barn was dim and smelled of hay and animal warmth, and every head turned toward her when she came through the door — nine pairs of eyes catching the lantern glow, all of them silent now, all of them watching her with a terrible, deliberate attention. Animals didn't look at people that way. Animals looked *through* people. But these nine regarded her as a frightened congregation regards its priest, waiting for her to tell them what they already knew.

Bramble stepped forward. He was the eldest, a grey-bearded billy with one clouded eye, and when he spoke — not in words, never in words here, but in that pressure of meaning against her mind — what came through was simple and enormous:

*Riders. Many. Coming down from the north pass. They smell of iron and burning.*

Dominic's hands went cold around the lantern handle.

She had known this day would come. She had known it since old Tamlin died at the treeline with his blood soaking into her skirts, since his fingers had closed hers around a wax-sealed map and he'd breathed *the Heartroot wakes* into the space between them like a curse and a prayer in the same breath. Three days ago. Three days of telling herself no one had seen, no one had followed, that the hunters who killed him would chase their trail along the high road east and never think to look into a valley that forgot the world.

Three days of lying to herself so thoroughly she'd almost believed it.

"They're coming *here*?" she said aloud, her voice cracking on the second word. "Into the valley?"

The herd's answer came as a shudder through all of them at once, a ripple of hide and horn. The valley would not stop them. The valley stopped magic, not men. It had never been a sanctuary for anything except the things that hunted her kind — because what better place to cage a beast-speaker than a fold where her gift went deaf and dumb?

Her father had known. Her grandmother before her. The wardens' line, penned in the Weeping Wood like sheep whose wool the Crown intended someday to shear.

There was no time to think about that. There was barely time to breathe.

Dominic moved the way fear teaches a body to move — fast, certain, without deliberation. The map was hidden in the third loose stone behind her hearth, wrapped in oilcloth; she'd told herself the hiding place was temporary, that she'd decide what to do with it within the week. Now her fingers pried the stone free and took the bundle and pushed it deep into the satchel she kept for market days, and then — after a heartbeat's hesitation — added bread, cheese, a knife, flint, her grandfather's heavy cloak. Her hands shook so badly she dropped the flint twice.

Outside, the sky was going from black to bruise-grey over the eastern ridge. Frost silvered the grass. Somewhere far off, downslope past the mill and the smithy and the cluster of cottages that made up the whole of Hollowmere, a dog began to bark — and then cut off sharply, mid-note, as though a hand had closed around its throat.

They were already here.

* * *

She meant to go out the back, across the stream, up into the birch slope where the trees grew thick enough to swallow a woman whole. She had the route planned in her head even as she laced her boots: over the ridge by first light, down the other side, gone before anyone in the village knew she'd left. Let the hunters find an empty house and a cold hearth. Let them rage and question and eventually ride out again, and Hollowmere would go on forgetting, safe in its blessed amnesia, and Dominic Winterbourne would simply cease to exist.

That was the plan. It died in Marta Quill's dooryard.

Marta was her nearest neighbor — a widow of sixty with forearms like a stevedore's and a tongue that had never once been stilled by propriety or kindness or common sense. She was also, though neither of them ever said it aloud, the closest thing to family Dominic had left. When Dominic's father died, it was Marta who sat with her through the long night of washing the body. It was Marta who brought soup for weeks after, unasked, leaving it on the step because grief made people useless and she refused to watch anyone starve out of pride.

And it was Marta who was standing in her own yard in the freezing dawn, barefoot, arms spread wide before her own front door, facing four men in the grey-and-white livery of the Crown's Office of Purity.

Dominic froze behind her woodshed, forty feet away, the map a dead weight against her spine.

"—told you twice," Marta was saying, and her voice carried clear across the frost-hard ground, pitched loud on purpose, Dominic realized, pitched to carry to any listening ears. "I live alone. I've lived alone eleven years. Nobody else in that house but me and my mother's ghost, and she's welcome to you if you want her."

The leader of the four was a lean man with a face like a folded letter, unreadable, creased down the middle. He wore no knight's spurs but he carried himself like one, and at his belt, beside the sword, hung the thing that made Dominic's stomach turn over: a short iron rod caged at the tip, and inside the cage, a dull red coal that glowed without heat and gave off no smoke.

A truth-iron. She'd heard of them the way children hear of wolves — stories, warnings, nothing real. The witch-hunters' tool that burned in the presence of the gifted, or so it was said. Here in the valley it hung dark and quiet, smothered by the same silence that smothered her gift.

But it was pointed at Marta's door, and the man holding it said, in a voice like dry paper, "We have reason to believe a courier passed through this settlement three nights past. Wounded. He did not continue on the road."

"He continued somewhere," Marta said. "Roads go places."

"The body we found had been tended." The man unfolded a step closer. "Bandaged. Someone in this valley has hands that know field-surgery, and someone moved his effects. You will open your house, mistress, or we will open it for you."

"And I'll say it a third time, then, since you're slow—"

"Mistress Quill." Another voice, from the lane behind the riders, and Dominic's blood turned to ice water because she knew it — everyone in Hollowmere knew it. Aldric Vane. Ser Aldric, the Knight of Ashes, who had ridden into the valley two days behind the others with a warrant sealed in white wax, courteous as a funeral, asking questions no one had answers to. He came around the woodpile now at a walk, unhurried, his pale hair tied back, his grey eyes moving over the yard with the patience of a man who had done this a hundred times and expected to do it a hundred more. "Please. No one needs to be hurt this morning. We're looking for a young woman. Dark-haired, twenty-five to thirty years of age. Keeps goats." His gaze drifted, casual as weather, across the woodshed where Dominic stood. "Keeps to herself."

Behind the shed, Dominic stopped breathing.

"She keeps to herself because she's sensible," Marta said flatly. "Half this valley keeps to itself. You want a list? I'll write you a list. Start with you, likely, once folk get a look at that—" she jerked her chin at the truth-iron— "fancy candlestick you're carrying about."

Someone among the riders laughed shortly. The lean man did not.

"Search the house," he said.

"You'll not—"

Two riders moved past Marta, and when she planted herself in their path one of them simply caught her shoulders and set her aside — not roughly, which somehow made it worse, the way you move furniture, the way you move a thing that is in your way and does not matter — and went through her door into her home, her mother's home, her husband's home, the house she'd kept alone for eleven years.

Marta stood in her yard in her bare feet and watched strangers carry out her linens and shake them in the cold air, tip over her pantry jars, pull open the drawers where she kept her husband's letters wrapped in twine. And she held her chin up the entire time, furious and unbowed, because that was who Marta Quill was.

And Dominic, forty feet away with a stolen map against her back, understood with perfect, sickening clarity what was happening.

They hadn't found her. They didn't know her name yet, perhaps, or perhaps they suspected everything and were casting nets. Either way, they were turning over every house in Hollowmere, and they would keep turning them until either the map surfaced or someone gave them a reason to look harder — and Marta was giving them that reason right now, with her bare feet and her insolence, because Marta had seen Dominic's face in the crowd the day the courier died, had helped her boil the bandages, had asked no questions, and would rather be dragged to the capital in irons than let these men learn one true thing from her mouth.

*Go,* Marta's whole rigid back was saying. *Go, girl, while they're busy with me.*

Dominic's feet would not obey. She stood rooted behind the woodshed with frost soaking through her boot-soles and her heart tearing straight down the middle, and the cowardly arithmetic ran itself anyway, merciless: *if you step out, you die, and the map dies, and whatever the courier bled for dies, and Marta's sacrifice buys nothing.*

Then the lean man found the letters.

He came out of the cottage holding the bundle of twine-wrapped papers, and he weighed it in his hand, and he said to Aldric, almost idly, "Correspondence. Take it for the archive." And Marta — proud, impossible Marta — lunged for it.

She got two steps before the rider caught her arm, and she twisted, and she spat — actually spat, full in the face of a soldier of the Crown — and the yard went very still.

"Well," said the lean man, dabbing at his cheek with one finger, examining the result as if it were an interesting specimen. "Assault upon an officer of the Crown in the execution of his duty. Mistress Quill, you are detained pending questioning. Bind her."

"No—" Dominic's voice escaped before she could kill it, a strangled syllable, and she clamped her hand over her own mouth too late.

Aldric Vane's head turned toward the woodshed.

For one suspended moment their eyes nearly met across the frost and the woodsmoke-smelling air, and there was something in his face — not triumph, not suspicion exactly, something wearier and stranger, a question being asked that she couldn't hear — and then the rider struck Marta across the mouth for the spitting, a short backhand that cracked like green wood, and every thought in Dominic's head dissolved into red.

She was moving before she knew it. Two steps, three — and then Bramble hit her.

The old billy came out of nowhere, shouldering her sideways behind the shed with astonishing force for a creature of his years, and the pressure of his meaning crashed over her, urgent and furious:

*Fool. Fool child. Dead, you save no one. RUN.*

Down the lane, they were putting ropes on Marta Quill's wrists. Marta wasn't struggling anymore; she was looking at the woodshed, deliberately, openly, one last time, and her split lip curled into something that was not quite a smile — *go, you stubborn fool* — before they turned her toward the horses.

Dominic ran.

* * *

She went up through the birches with the satchel bouncing between her shoulder blades and tears freezing in the wind of her own speed, and she did not stop until the valley floor had fallen away below her and her lungs burned like swallowed coals. Then she knelt in the bracken under a grey sky, gasping, and let the guilt arrive, because it was going to arrive regardless and it was better to meet it standing than have it ambush her later.

*Marta.* Bound, bleeding, taken. Taken because a dying stranger had chosen Dominic's arms to fall into. Taken because Dominic had been too slow, too careful, too much the valley's daughter — trained since birth to keep her head down, to hide the strangeness, to survive. Survival, her father had taught her, was a discipline. He had never mentioned what it cost, or who paid it when you were good at it.

She could go back. She could give herself up, trade herself for Marta — the thought glowed briefly, beautifully, like a lie. They wouldn't trade. They'd take both, and the map besides, and the commander whoever he was would have his weapon or his pyre or both, and the courier's blood and Marta's blood and her father's long vigil would all have bought the same nothing.

No. There was only one road left, and it led out of the valley, east along the ridges, following the map toward the Heartroot — toward the thing the Crown feared enough to burn villages for. If the map reached its destination, if whatever the Heartroot wanted of a warden could be answered, then maybe — *maybe* — none of this had been for nothing. Maybe there was a justice larger than the Crown's, and she would drag it back here with her own hands if she had to.

Below her, faint with distance, she saw them ride out through the north gate: six riders, seven, the lean man leading, one spare horse trailing with a bound figure slung across it like a sack of grain. Like a thing that did not matter.

"I'm coming for you," Dominic said to the wind, to the dust of the road, to Marta wherever she was, and her voice broke on it, and she let it break, and then she stood up and wiped her face with a sleeve that smelled of goat and woodsmoke and everything she was leaving behind.

Bramble had followed her partway up the slope. He stood a few yards off, breathing steam, his one good eye fixed on her, and the last thread of meaning he sent her was gentle and unbearable:

*The wood remembers its own. Go. Be its voice again.*

She touched his rough forehead once, the way she had since she was a child, and turned east, and did not look back — because if she looked back she would see the valley, her whole life, small and silver and safe in its bowl of mountains, and safe was the one thing she could no longer afford to be.

By noon she had crossed the boundary stone.

By noon, the world came flooding back in — the birds suddenly loud and articulate overhead, warning each other about *the woman, the woman walking east, the woman who listens* — and the map lay warm against her back like a living thing, and somewhere behind her, on the roads of the world, witch-hunters rode with a widow's blood still wet on their knuckles.

Dominic Winterbourne walked out of the Weeping Wood and did not intend to return until the Crown had learned to fear the things it buried.
