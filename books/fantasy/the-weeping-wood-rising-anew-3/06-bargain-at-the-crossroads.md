## 6. Bargain at the Crossroads

The rain had followed her for two days.

Dominic had learned to read its moods the way she'd once read the goats — the fat, indifferent drops that meant weather, and the thin, needling kind that meant the sky itself was watching. This was the watching kind. It came down in gray threads through the skeletal alders along the old north road, pooling in the ruts, erasing hoofprints, erasing everything except her own stubborn tracks leading away from everything she had ever been.

She walked with her hood down because a hood made her look like someone hiding, and someone hiding was exactly what the hunters were looking for. Instead she walked like a peddler's daughter gone dull with grief, shoulders rounded, eyes on the mud. Behind her, tied by a length of rope rather than a halter — she would not put a halter on Marigold, not after what the goat had done for her — Marigold plodded and complained in the low, grumbling way that was not language but was not nothing either.

*They are behind us,* the doe had said, three nights past, from the edge of a barley field, her great dark ears trembling. *Men in white and ash-grey. They have dogs that do not bark. They took your neighbor's smell from your doorstep and gave it to the dogs as if it were bread.*

Marta. Taken in Dominic's place, dragged from her own kitchen while her stew still breathed steam into the rafters. Dominic carried that image now the way a splinter works its way toward the heart — every mile driving it deeper.

The crossroads appeared out of the rain near dusk: four worn ways meeting around a leaning post that had once borne signs and now bore only lichen and the rusted stub of an iron crown nailed there by some loyal fool decades ago. A shrine to Saint Verity stood at its corner, roofless, its saint long since pried from her niche by men who believed saints were just magic wearing a kinder face. Rain fell through the empty niche as if even the stone were weeping.

There was a fire ring there. Old char, half-sheltered under the shrine wall. Dry enough.

Dominic told herself she'd stop because Marigold needed rest. She told herself the truth about it only once, briefly, and then refused to look at it again: stopping was a mistake, and she was too tired and too heartsore to care.

She got a small fire going in the lee of the wall, fed Marigold the last of the oats, and sat with her back to cold stone, listening to the rain eat the world.

That was when the hair on her arms lifted — not from cold.

Marigold went rigid first. The goat's head snapped up, nostrils flaring, and she made no sound at all, which from Marigold was more alarming than any bleat. Then the doe's voice was simply there in Dominic's skull, arriving without wind or warning, thin with terror:

*He is already here.*

Dominic moved on instinct older than thought. She kicked dirt over the fire, snatched the map — wax-sealed, courier's blood still crusted in its folds — and pressed herself into the shrine's shadow behind the broken altar. Marigold, bless her contrary soul, planted herself in the open like a statue of a goat, chewing, staring at nothing.

Footsteps in the mud. Slow. Unhurried. The footsteps of a man who did not need to rush because he already knew how this ended.

"I know you're there." The voice was deep, roughened, tired in a way that went down to the marrow. "Your fire smoked. Green wood always smokes. And your goat has been staring at that altar for a quarter hour like it owes her money."

Dominic closed her eyes. *Run*, she thought. *Through the trees, north, lose him in the dark—*

But the dogs that do not bark. The men in white and ash-grey might be one man or ten, spread through the treeline, and Marta's face floated up behind her eyes, and her legs would not obey.

So instead she did the thing she had sworn never to do in the open world: she reached sideways, into that other sense, and asked the night for help.

The answer came immediately, and it was wrong.

*No one else is near,* said something small and skittering from beneath the shrine stones — a rat, she realized, a rat with a voice like dry leaves. *Only him. Only the ash-man. He walks alone and he smells of old burning.*

One man. Alone. She could hear it now — the singular rhythm of the footsteps, no others answering in the dark.

Which meant she had a chance. Which meant, some cold practical voice in her noted, she also had a choice.

She stepped out of the shadow with the knife held low against her thigh and her chin up, letting the hood fall back so he could see her face and know she was not afraid, whether or not it was true.

The knight stood perhaps fifteen feet away at the crossroads' center, rain streaming off his pauldrons, and he was not what she expected. She had imagined witch-hunters as zealots, all fire-eyed certitude. This man looked like something the war had chewed up and spat out — grey at the temples, a scar splitting his brow to cheekbone, and eyes that held the particular exhaustion of a man carrying a debt he could never repay. His sword was drawn, but low, pointed at the ground. Ash-grey cloak. White surcoat beneath, blazoned with the Crown's sunburst, rain-darkened almost to grey itself.

"The courier," he said. "You were there when he died."

Not a question. Dominic kept the knife where he could see it and said nothing.

"His name was Tam Voss. He carried letters for my father, years ago, before either of us knew what the Crown really wanted carried." Something moved across the knight's face — grief, quickly shuttered. "I buried him an hour ago. I'd like to know if his last hours were afraid."

"Why?" Her voice came out harder than she intended. "So you can write it in your report? *Subject died compliant.*"

Something flickered in those grey eyes. Not anger. Recognition, maybe — the look of a man hearing his own cynicism spoken back to him in a stranger's voice.

"So I can know," he said quietly, "whether there's anything left in this work worth doing honestly."

*Careful,* the rat whispered from the stones. *Ash-man's hands are steady. Steadier than his voice.*

"Give me the map," the knight said. "And walk away. That is the bargain I'm offering. You're a valley girl — you're outside the writs, outside the trials. No hunter wants your name. Give me what you took from a dead man's hand and go home, and no pyre gets lit over you."

"Home," Dominic repeated. The word cracked in half coming out. "Your men burned through Harrowmere three days ago. They took Marta Ostley out of her own kitchen because my scent was on my own doorstep. Is that the bargain? She burns so I can walk?"

The knight went very still.

"Marta Ostley," he said. "Grey woman, limps on the left side, keeps bees?"

Dominic's stomach dropped. "You know her."

"Everyone in the valley knows everyone," he said, and then, seeing her face: "Girl, I've been riding alone since Ford Anselm. My commander's men took your neighbor. Not me. There is a difference between me and them, and you will want to find it before you decide what I am."

"And what are you?"

"Ashes," he said simply. "Ser Aldric Vane, once Knight-Commander of the Order of the White Sun. Now the last honest instrument of a dishonest order." He raised the sword a few inches — a formality, an apology in steel. "The map. Please. I am asking, which should tell you something. I stopped *taking* things from girls a long time ago."

*He is telling the truth,* said a new voice, low and vast, and Dominic nearly staggered — a crow had settled on the shrine's broken lintel above them both, feathers slicked black with rain, and its voice poured into her mind like cold well water. *His guilt is a live coal he carries in his chest. But careful, speaker. Truthful men serve lies all their lives.*

Dominic's breath caught. And Aldric saw it catch. Saw her eyes track upward to the crow. Saw whatever happened in her face when another mind touched hers.

He didn't move. But everything in him changed — the weariness sharpening into something older, colder, the knight surfacing through the ashes.

"You," he breathed. "You're the one they're calling the Speaker."

"I'm a goat girl from a valley," Dominic said.

"You're the last of a line the Crown has spent three generations trying to starve out, whether you knew it or not." He took one step forward; Marigold stamped, and to his credit he stopped. "Do you understand what's hunting you? Not superstition. Not mob fear. *Policy.* The Heartroot is waking for the first time in a century, and every power in the land wants to be holding the leash when it does."

"Then destroy it," Dominic shot back. "Isn't that the doctrine? Magic is corruption, the wood is wicked, burn it all? Isn't that what you people sing at your little bonfires?"

"It was." The word landed strangely. "*Was.*"

Above them, the crow shifted, and its voice slid into Dominic's mind with the smooth certainty of something that had watched men lie for centuries. *Ask him what the Commander intends. Ask him what the orders say now. He carries the paper in his breastplate. He has read it forty times and each time it has weighed more.*

It was reckless. It was possibly suicidal. She opened her mouth anyway.

"If you're such an honest instrument," she said, "read me your orders. All of them. Word for word. Or does the White Sun let its knights skip the inconvenient parts?"

Aldric's jaw tightened. For a long moment the only sound was rain ticking off iron and the crow's claws shifting on wet stone.

Then, slowly, deliberately, he sheathed his sword, drew a leather dispatch case from inside his cloak, and broke its seal in full view of her.

"Commander Haleth's directive, countersigned by the Lord Chancellor," he said. "Read it yourself, if you can read. If not, I'll read it aloud and watch your face while I do."

He read aloud. And with every sentence, Dominic watched the man's voice flatten further, as though the words were being dragged out of some deep place he'd been trying to bury them in.

*"Upon recovery of the Heartroot, the artifact is not to be destroyed. Severance protocols are suspended. The Heartroot's resonance is to be bound to a vessel — candidates: condemned speakers currently in custody — and conveyed to the capital for integration into Crown armaments. The Order will be compensated with lands reclaimed from the Weeping Wood."*

Silence. Even the rain seemed to lean closer.

"Condemned speakers currently in custody," Dominic whispered. Her mind went white-hot and then very, very cold. "Marta."

"They think your gift lives in the blood," Aldric said. "They think they can bleed it into a vessel and point it like a cannon. They've been trying for thirty years. Every speaker they've burned—" He stopped. His throat worked. "Every speaker we've burned was tested first. Those who produced no resonance were killed as failures. Those who did—" His eyes went to the middle distance, somewhere terrible. "There's a facility under the Citadel of Bells. I signed the requisitions for its chains. I told myself it was containment."

*Now he sees it,* the crow murmured. *See how the coal burns him. But a burning man can still set fires, speaker. Choose carefully.*

"You didn't know," Dominic said slowly. It wasn't forgiveness. It was cartography — marking the shape of the thing in front of her.

"I suspected," Aldric said. "There is a difference, and it shames me more than ignorance would." He folded the directive with hands that were not quite steady. "I swore an oath to end magic because I watched it take a girl I—" He cut himself off, jaw hard. "Because I watched it destroy someone. I believed the Crown wanted the same thing I wanted. An ending. Mercy. Silence." He looked up, and for the first time she saw how young his eyes were behind all that scarring. "They don't want silence. They want a weapon that screams."

The rain filled the pause between them.

Dominic looked at the map hidden inside her coat, at the dying courier's blood-crusted seal, at the words he'd spent his last breath on: *the Heartroot wakes.* She thought of Marta in chains beneath a citadel, of goats and wolves and crows speaking terror into her skull, of a valley built — she was beginning to suspect — not as sanctuary but as cage.

If she gave him the map, it went to Haleth, and Marta died screaming into a vessel of Crown steel. If she ran, they hunted her down and the same thing happened slower. If she fought—

She couldn't fight. Not alone.

And neither, she realized, studying the ash-grey man in front of her, could he. A knight alone, disobeying sealed orders, was not a knight anymore. He was a fugitive with excellent armor.

"There's a third option," she said.

"Don't," Aldric warned, but softly, the way a man warns himself.

"Truce." She stepped forward, close enough that Marigold's warmth pressed against her leg. "You don't give Haleth the map. I don't run. We go to the Heartroot together — and when we find it, *we* decide what happens. Not the Chancellor. Not your commander. Us."

"That's treason."

"Yes," Dominic said. "I've noticed it runs in the family. Apparently mine kept bees and yours kept chains, and we've both decided to stop."

A sound escaped him — half breath, half laugh, entirely incredulous. He looked at her for a long moment, this mud-spattered goat girl with a dead man's map and a crow's blessing, and whatever he found in her face seemed to settle the argument he'd clearly been having with himself since the courier died.

"If you betray me," he said, "I'll bring you in myself. No trial. No pyre. I'll put you somewhere they chain you and forget you, and I'll live with it the way I live with everything else."

"Fair," said Dominic. "If *you* betray me, the wolves get you. All of them. At once. And they hold grudges far better than I do."

The crow laughed — an actual sound, harsh and delighted, rattling down off the wet stone. Aldric glanced up at it, then back at her, and she watched the last of his resistance die not in surrender but in recognition: the look of a man finding, in the most impossible place, the only other person alive who understood exactly how much the world had lied to them both.

"Dawn," he said. "We move at dawn. The northern pass, before Haleth's outriders seal it." He hesitated, then added, gruffly, "Your goat may come."

"She was going to," said Dominic. "She doesn't ask permission either."

* * *

Aldric took first watch because he did not trust himself to sleep beside her yet, and because sleep, lately, had become a country he visited mostly to be ambushed.

She was curled against the shrine wall under his spare cloak, the map rising and falling with her breath, the crow roosting above her head like a gargoyle that had chosen a religion. The goat slept standing up, one eye open, fixed on him with what Aldric could only describe as professional skepticism.

He turned the directive over in his hands. One tear of the wax and it was ash, and then there would be no proof, no record, nothing but his word against the Lord Chancellor's — and his word, he knew better than anyone, was worth exactly what the Crown had paid for it.

Instead he folded it small and tucked it back against his heart, where he kept the other things he couldn't put down.

Across the dead fire, the girl stirred. "Vane." Her voice was thick with half-sleep. "The girl you watched magic destroy. On the pyre."

His chest closed like a fist. "Go to sleep."

"Was she a speaker?"

The rain ticked. The crow's eye gleamed.

"Yes," Aldric said finally, into the dark. "She was a speaker. And I lit the pyre myself. That's why the Order gave me this command — they think hatred keeps best in a man who's already burned his own mercy." He looked at Dominic across the embers, and did not look away. "They were wrong about that. And tomorrow I intend to prove it."

Dominic held his gaze a moment longer, something unspoken passing between them across the coals — suspicion not dissolved, but shelved, like two armed nations signing a ceasefire neither fully believes in.

Then her eyes went wide. She sat up sharply, hand flying to the map, and the crow dropped from the lintel to her shoulder with its feathers bristling.

"What is it?" Aldric was on his feet, sword clearing leather. "What's wrong?"

Dominic was staring north, into the rain-black trees, and her voice, when it came, was hollow.

"The wolves just told me," she said, "that Haleth's army crossed the ford an hour ago. And Marta's chains are with them."
