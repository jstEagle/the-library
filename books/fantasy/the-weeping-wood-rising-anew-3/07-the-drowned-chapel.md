## 7. The Drowned Chapel

The rain found them an hour past dusk, and the ruin found them an hour after that.

Dominic heard it before she saw it — or rather, she felt it, the way one feels a missing tooth: a hollow in the world where the wild things had gone quiet. They had been following the river road north for two days, keeping to the reed-banks and the alder thickets, and all the while the birds had been thinning around them, the fox-sign growing old, the whole living tapestry of the land unraveling thread by thread as they walked. Now, in the dark and the rain, the silence ahead of them was so complete it rang.

"There," Aldric said, and pointed through the sheets of water.

She saw it then — or the shape of it. A tower rising out of what might once have been a village green, its crown collapsed into a broken ring like a snapped finger. Below it, half-swallowed by black water and willow, stood the long bones of a building: arched windows blind with silt, a doorway drowned to its lintel. A chapel, or something built by people who had wanted very badly to speak with heaven and had been answered with a flood.

"The Drowned Chapel," Dominic murmured. "The goats told me about places like this. Well—" she caught herself, hearing how it sounded, "—they would have, if I'd asked."

Aldric said nothing. He had said almost nothing since the crossroads, since the moment a stag's voice had come out of her mouth and told him his commander intended to harvest the Heartroot rather than destroy it. He walked like a man carrying something breakable that he could not set down, and Dominic had decided — not unkindly — to let him carry it.

They waded in through the doorway. The water came to their knees inside, cold enough to make her gasp, and clear as glass where the rain hadn't stirred it. Moonlight fell through the broken roof in pale columns, and in those columns she saw what the flood had preserved: rows of stone pews furred green, their tops just breaching the water like the backs of sleeping cattle. An altar at the far end, raised on a dais above the waterline, dry as bone. And everywhere, on every wall, carvings.

"Gods," she breathed.

Not gods, actually. Beasts. Stags and wolves and herons and hares and serpents, all of them carved in flowing relief, all of them turned toward the altar as if in worship — or toward whatever had sat behind the altar, in a niche now empty, its edges worn smooth by centuries of hands.

"People used to pray here," Aldric said quietly. His voice echoed strangely off the water. "Before the Crown taught them what to pray to."

"You sound like you remember it."

"I read." He climbed onto the dais, wrung out his cloak, and hung it over a fallen roof-beam with the mechanical care of a man who had slept rough in a hundred ruins. "I was educated for this work, Dom. You cannot hunt a thing unless you know its shape. That was how they justified the archives, the histories, the burning of everything that didn't fit the shape they wanted." He paused. "I'm talking too much."

"Yes," she said. Then, because the silence between them had grown heavy enough to drown in: "Don't stop."

He looked at her then — really looked, the first time since the crossroads — and something in his face shifted, some gate left unlatched.

* * *

They made camp on the dais, which was dry and defensible and gave sightlines to both doorways. Dominic dug the last of the bread from her pack and divided it without being asked; Aldric produced a strip of dried venison from somewhere in his armor and added it to the pile with the gravity of a man paying a debt. They ate sitting on the altar steps, boots off, feet in the warm wool of their cloaks, while the rain played its endless fingers on the water below.

It should have been unbearable, sitting beside him. He was the Crown's hound; he had crossed half the kingdom hunting her; three days ago he had put a blade to her throat at the crossroads and only the stag's voice had stayed his hand. But exhaustion does strange arithmetic, and fear burns fuel, and somewhere in the walking and the wet and the watching-for-pursuit, her terror of him had worn down into something else — wariness, yes, but wariness is a kind of attention, and attention is the first cousin of trust.

"Why did you believe me?" she asked.

He chewed slowly. "Which part?"

"The stag. What it said about your commander. You've spent your life believing people like me are liars, or worse."

"I've spent my life believing people like me are liars," he said. "That's different." He tore the venison in half and offered her the bigger piece without appearing to notice he'd done it. "I've seen witch-speakers confess under questioning. Confess to anything, everything, whatever the questioner wanted. It proved nothing except that pain makes liars of us all. But a beast has no reason to lie to me. No reason to speak at all." His jaw worked. "And it knew things it shouldn't have known."

"What things?"

"It named the commander's second. A man I watched die at Thornfell six years ago." He said it flatly, but she saw his hands go still. "No one alive knows that name. Whatever spoke through you had been listening to things men whisper when they think the world isn't listening."

Dominic pulled her cloak tighter. Outside, the rain kept on. Somewhere far off, an owl called — the first wild voice she'd heard in hours — and she felt its presence brush against the place inside her where her gift lived, faint as warmth through a wall. *Safe,* the owl's mind murmured, in the wordless way of birds. *Small hunter safe.* She closed her eyes and let it be true for a moment.

"When I was in the valley," she said, "I thought the silence there was mercy. Magic can't follow you in, everyone said. Sanctuary. My mother told me we were keepers of a quiet place." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "But it wasn't sanctuary, was it? It was a cage with the door painted shut. And I was the thing in the cage."

Aldric was quiet a long moment. "What did your mother tell you about the Heartroot?"

"Nothing. She died before I was old enough to ask the right questions. My father—" She stopped. Her father had told her many things, most of them lies dressed as lessons: *never speak where neighbors can see, never touch the goats' minds where anyone might mark the stillness that comes over you, never, never answer when something wild calls your name.* "My father taught me to hide. That was the whole of my inheritance."

"Hiding keeps people alive," Aldric said. There was something underneath the words, something old and raw. "I used to think it was cowardice. I don't anymore."

She turned to look at him. Firelight from their small flame moved across his face, and up close, without the armor of distance, she could see how tired he was — tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix, tired down in the foundations. "You lit a pyre once," she said. Not a question. The beasts along the road had whispered it to each other as they passed — *the ash knight, the ash knight, he burned one of us and walks the world smelling of smoke* — and she had carried the knowledge for days, unsure whether to use it as a blade or simply hold it.

His face did not change, but everything behind it did. "Yes."

"You don't have to tell me."

"No," he agreed. "I don't." And then, after a silence long enough for the rain to fall ten thousand times: "Her name was Maren. She was nineteen. I was twenty-two and very sure of myself, and the village was very frightened, and frightened people will give you anything you ask for if you ask in the right uniform." He looked at his hands. "She spoke with crows. That was all. Crows. And I stood there and did my duty, and the wood went silent around that village for a year afterward, and I told myself for six years that the silence was grief, when really it was accusation."

Dominic did not say *I forgive you*. She wasn't sure she did, yet, and she was too honest with herself to pretend otherwise. But she reached across the space between them and took one of his hands — the scarred one, the one that had held the torch — and held it, briefly, the way you'd steady a man on a rope bridge.

"That's why you hunt the hunters' maps now," she said. "That's why you hesitated at the crossroads."

"That's why I'm here," he said, "in a flooded church eating goat-herder's bread with a woman who talks to animals. Yes." He almost smiled. It transformed his face so completely that she had to look away. Love, she thought, alarmed, would be an inconvenient thing to begin in a drowning chapel, and yet the thought arrived anyway, quiet as water finding cracks.

* * *

Sleep would not come, so they worked instead.

Aldric spread his cloak over the altar steps to make a dry workspace, and Dominic unwrapped the courier's map from its oilcloth with fingers that trembled — partly from cold, mostly from what she already sensed coming. She had studied the map a dozen times in the firelight of the road: the mountains rendered in careful ink, the river systems, the pass through the Teeth, and at the center, in a grove marked only by a spiral, a single word in a script she couldn't read. The rest was ordinary cartography, beautiful but mute.

"Here," she said, laying it flat. "This word. And these marks along the border — they're not decoration. I've stared at them until my eyes bled. They mean something."

Aldric leaned over the map, and she watched him go still the way he went still before violence — total attention, breath slowed, the whole man become an instrument. "Old script," he said finally. "Pre-Crown. They burned the grammars, but I stole time in the archive at Vellamere once, before I understood what I was reading." His finger traced the border-marks. "These aren't decoration. They're wards. Or — no. Invitations. Look at the repetition. It's a liturgy. This map isn't drawn to be *read*, Dominic. It's drawn to be *spoken*."

"Spoken by whom?"

"By someone who can hear the answer." He glanced up at her. "Read it aloud. Just the sounds. Don't try to understand them — I'll translate as you go."

She bent over the map. The central word waited in its spiral, ink gone brown with age, and when she let her eyes unfocus the way she did when listening to the goats — letting meaning arrive sideways, through feeling rather than thought — the shapes began to loosen, to breathe, to arrange themselves into syllables like birds settling on a branch.

"*Hael-en-varra... doth-riel... en-sommar...*"

As she spoke, the candle-flame leaned toward the map though there was no draft. The water in the nave shivered into rings. And beneath her voice, so faint she might have imagined it, something vast and slow and terribly weary sighed — the sound of a heart beating once in a long illness.

Aldric's hand closed over hers, stopping her. "Again," he said hoarsely. "*Hael-en-varra*. Say it again."

"*Hael-en-varra.*"

"Root-of-the-green-heart," he translated. "Or more truly — *the Heartroot, which lives*. Doth-riel — *which fails*. En-sommar — *and calls*. The grammar's archaic but it's unambiguous." He sat back, and in the candlelight she saw something she had not expected to ever see on the face of a witch-hunter: awe, and beneath the awe, grief. "The Heartroot isn't a place. It's a *being*. Alive. And it's dying."

"And calling," Dominic whispered. Her own voice sounded far away. "Calling for what?"

He traced the final phrase, the smallest words, tucked into the spiral's center like a seed in its shell. "This one I'm less sure of. *Vessa-dun-hael.* 'To the warden who remains' — or possibly 'to the warden who remains *to come*.'" He looked at her, and the candlelight made his eyes very bright. "The map isn't a treasure chart. It's a letter. Someone wrote it knowing exactly who would be able to hear it, and no one else. It's been waiting for you."

The silence that followed was filled by the drip of water, the tick of the cooling flame, the distant patient call of the owl. Dominic looked down at the map — at the spiral, at the dying word at its heart — and felt the full weight of it settle onto her shoulders: not a burden carried, but a burden *becoming*, the way a seed becomes a tree whether the ground consents or not.

"My family," she said slowly. "The valley. The wardenship. It was never accident, was it. We weren't hiding *in* that valley. We were planted there."

"Prisoners make poor wardens," Aldric said. "But prisoners' children make excellent ones. They grow up loving the walls."

She should have bristled. Instead she found herself laughing — a low, exhausted, rueful sound — because he was right, and because laughter was the only tool she owned sharp enough for the moment. "You're insufferable."

"I'm frequently told." He rolled the map carefully, returned it to its oilcloth, and handed it back to her with both hands, formally, like a knight returning a sword. "We reach the mountain passes in four days if the weather holds. Six if it doesn't. Either way—" he nodded toward the dark beyond the broken roof, toward the north, toward the Teeth — "we walk into the Crown's own country to find a god that's bleeding out and asking for you by name."

Outside, the owl called again, closer now, perched on the chapel's broken crown. And beneath its voice — beneath the rain and the dripping eaves and the small sounds of their ruined sanctuary — Dominic heard, or dreamed she heard, that vast slow sigh again, rising up through the floodwater, through the stone, through the soles of her bare feet:

*Come, little warden. Before the last leaf falls.*

She met Aldric's eyes across the altar. "Someone knows we stopped here," she said. "The birds went quiet an hour ago. Something's tracking us — two-legged, moving fast, from the south."

Aldric was already reaching for his sword.
