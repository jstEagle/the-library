## 8. Wolves at the Ford

The ford at Marrow Creek was a bad place to die, and Aldric Vane had known enough dying places to recognize one on sight.

It lay in a shallow bowl of land where the old king's road dipped toward the water — a gray ribbon of river braided over stones, flanked by alder thickets that grew dense and dark on both banks. There was no high ground. No wall to put your back against. The crossing itself was knee-deep and slick with weed, which meant that anyone caught mid-ford would be fighting from below, off-balance, half-drowned before the first blade touched them.

He had said as much that morning, when they'd broken camp under a sky the color of dirty wool.

"There's a bridge two leagues north," he'd told her, watching her cinch the straps of the courier's satchel across her chest. "We can take the long way."

Dominic had looked up at him with those level hazel eyes — the eyes of someone who had spent her whole life reading weather in animals and had no patience left for men who prevaricated. "Two leagues north is two leagues closer to wherever your brothers-in-arms are riding," she'd said. "I'll take the ford."

And now, standing on the southern bank with the water muttering at his boots, Aldric understood that she had been right, in the way she was so often right about things he wished she were not. The bridge would have been watched. The ford was watched too — but by men who expected prey, not resistance.

He smelled them before he saw them. Woodsmoke, tallow, wet leather. The particular sourness of men who had ridden hard for days without washing.

"Dominic." He kept his voice low, pitched beneath the creek's murmur. "Downstream. Three, maybe four, in the alders on our side. And I'd wager their horses are picketed behind the rise."

She didn't ask how he knew. That was one of the strange mercies of traveling with her — she never wasted breath doubting him. Instead she went still, head tilting slightly, the way she did when she was listening to something no one else could hear. Her lips parted. Her eyes lost focus for a heartbeat, then sharpened into something cold and bright.

"Six," she said quietly. "Four in the trees, two mounted behind us on the road. They've been following since dawn — the crows told me an hour ago. I thought we might outrun them." She exhaled through her nose. "I was wrong."

Aldric's hand found his sword hilt out of pure habit, and just as quickly released it. Six against two, with a river at their backs. Steel solved nothing here; steel only determined *how* it ended. He turned slowly, cataloguing the road behind them: the two riders sat their horses with the loose, deliberate ease of men who wanted to be seen. One wore the mud-brown cloak of a freelance bounty man. The other wore no livery at all, which was its own kind of answer.

"Not the Crown's men," Dominic murmured.

"No."

"Then who?"

"Anyone." He kept his gaze forward, refusing to give the riders the satisfaction of being studied. "The Crown posted a reward for the map's recovery — discreetly, through brokers, because officially the map doesn't exist. Every hedge-knight, poacher, and debtor between here and the capital has heard whispers of it. A purse full of gold for a piece of wax-sealed parchment." His mouth twisted. "They don't even know what it leads to. They'd sell it back to the witch-hunters, or open it themselves and find nothing but ink, and kill you anyway to keep the secret."

"And you?" Her voice was soft, but there was iron under the softness. "If I handed it to you right now, Ser Aldric, what would you do?"

He looked at her then. Water-light rippled across her face, and the wind coming off the creek moved a strand of dark hair across her cheek, and he thought: *a month ago I would have cut her down without slowing my horse.* He thought: *what a strange, slow poison mercy is.*

"I'd burn it," he said. "And then I'd burn myself, probably, because I've earned worse." He meant it as gallows humor. It came out sounding like testimony.

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, brief and unguarded. Then it died, because the alders downstream rustled, and four men stepped out of them with the unhurried confidence of wolves who had finally stopped pretending to be shadows.

* * *

Their leader was a broad, gray-bearded man with a crossbow held low and easy, and a scar that split his chin like a second mouth. Aldric knew the type intimately — had been the type, once, before the white cloak and the oath. Bounty work attracted two kinds of men: the desperate and the devout, and the desperate ones were far more dangerous, because desperation did not hesitate.

"Well now." The graybeard's voice carried easily across the water, friendly as a market-day greeting. "Ser Aldric Vane. Ashes himself. Didn't expect to find *you* walking this road with a pretty stranger, and her wearing a dead courier's bag like it's Sunday clothes."

"You know me," Aldric said evenly. He shifted, putting himself half a step ahead of Dominic, and saw the movement registered — four pairs of eyes recalculating, the crossbow's angle drifting a degree. "Then you know what I am, and you know what happens to men who stand between me and the Crown's business."

"That's just it, ser." The graybeard smiled around his ruined chin. "Word come down from the capital three days past. You're relieved. Suspended pending inquiry. There's a new warrant out — says the woman traveling with you is to be taken alive, and any knight found aiding her is to be taken with her." He spread his free hand, almost apologetic. "So you see our difficulty. Are you aiding her? Or arresting her? Either way, friend, you're worth more to us than she is."

The world went very quiet and very simple, the way it did before violence. Aldric felt it settle over him like a familiar coat — and felt, for the first time in fifteen years, that the coat no longer fit.

Because here was the truth he had been circling for weeks, avoiding the way a tongue avoids a broken tooth: these men were his allies. Not his friends, perhaps, but his *side*. The same side he had served since the pyre outside Thornmere. The same banners, the same warrants, the same righteous machinery. And they were going to take Dominic — alive, if she was lucky; the word *alive* doing terrible work in a bounty man's mouth — and deliver her to the same inquiry that had already decided what she was before she opened hers.

There was no side anymore. There hadn't been since the night he'd first heard a goat speak through her lips and hadn't reached for his sword.

"The woman is under my protection," Aldric said. Each word came out flat and heavy, like stones laid on a grave. "By my authority as a knight of the realm, still mine until the Crown takes it from my hands personally. Turn back."

The graybeard sighed theatrically. "Knew you'd say something like that. Shame." He lifted two fingers.

Behind them, hooves splashed into the shallows. The two mounted men urged their horses into the creek, cutting off retreat southward, and the four from the alders began to fan out along the bank — two upstream, two holding center with the crossbow. It was well done. Professional. Aldric gave them that much.

"Dominic," he murmured, barely moving his lips. "When it starts, run north along the bank. Don't fight. Don't—"

"No," she said.

"*Dominic—*"

"They'll ride you down before you reach the treeline, and I'll be running alone with a thing on my chest six men are willing to kill for." Her hand found his forearm — brief, warm, steadying. "Besides. I didn't come this far to die at a ford." And then, in a voice that was not raised at all but somehow carried across the whole breadth of the water, ringing off the alders like a struck bell:

"Greywind! *Now!*"

For one heartbeat, nothing. The bounty men glanced at each other. The graybeard barked a laugh — "Calling for help, girl? Who's going to—" — and then the laugh strangled in his throat.

They came out of the northern woods like a gray flood given teeth.

Aldric had fought beside war-dogs, had seen cavalry break peasant levies, had witnessed the disciplined horror of a witch-hunter column at full charge. Nothing had prepared him for wolves moving with *purpose* — five, six, eight of them, big timber-country wolves, flowing down the slope in perfect silence and perfect concert, and at their head a male the size of a yearling calf with frost-pale fur and eyes like chips of green glass. They did not snarl. That was the worst part. They made no sound at all, and the silence was more terrifying than any chorus of howls, because it meant they were not defending territory or answering instinct. They were *obeying*.

The formation dissolved in four seconds.

The crossbowman got one bolt off — wild, high, his nerve gone before his finger tightened — and then a wolf hit him from the side and bore him down into the shallows, jaws closing on the wrist that held the weapon, shaking it with the casual brutality of a terrier with a rat. The man's scream bounced off the hillsides. The two upstream broke and ran; the wolves let them go, exactly two of them peeling away to herd rather than hunt, turning the flight into a funnel. The graybeard stood his ground longest, crossbow swinging wildly between the wolves and the woman who had called them, and Aldric watched the exact moment arithmetic failed him — six men against two travelers had been good odds; six men against two travelers and the forest itself was ruin.

"Yield!" Aldric drew his sword at last, not to attack but to plant its point in the mud before him, a boundary made visible. "Drop the weapons and walk away. *Now.* Last kindness you'll get today."

The mounted men had wheeled their terrified horses in circles in mid-creek; one bolted entirely, crashing through the shallows southward. The other threw down his sword and slid from the saddle with his hands already raised. The graybeard stared at Aldric — at the planted sword, at the frost-pale wolf sitting motionless ten feet away with Dominic's blood-warm scent in its nostrils and its green eyes fixed on the bounty man's throat — and whatever he saw in that arrangement of things, it decided him. The crossbow clattered onto the stones.

"Witch," the graybeard spat. But he said it while backing away, and there was no fire in it, only fear. "You know what she is, Vane. You're standing next to it. Defending it. When they burn her, they'll burn you on the same wood, and every man on this road will say you asked for it."

"Probably," Aldric said.

They retreated — the wounded crossbowman cradling his ruined arm, the others backing into the alders with their eyes never leaving the wolves — and the forest swallowed them, and then there was only the creek talking over its stones, and the breathing of tired horses, and eight wolves sitting in a rough crescent around Dominic Winterbourne as if she were a hearth and they were winter.

* * *

Afterward, Aldric sat on a fallen alder at the water's edge and tried to make his hands stop shaking, and failed, and stopped pretending it was the fighting that had done it.

The battle itself had been nothing — ninety heartbeats of chaos, most of it the wolves' work. What shook him was everything the ninety heartbeats had revealed. She had called them by name. *Greywind.* She had known their positions, their temperaments, their willingness. She had orchestrated an ambush with beasts she had apparently befriended somewhere in the weeks before their paths crossed — and they had come at her word across miles of country, into a fight against armed men, for no reason Aldric could comprehend except that she had asked.

He had spent his career among people who believed beast-speaking was the foulest of the forbidden gifts. The Church taught that it was communion with the unreasoning flesh, a door left open for things to walk through. The Crown taught that it was sedition — a power that could turn a kingdom's horses, hounds, and war-beasts against it in a single night. He had burned a girl for less than what he had just witnessed. For exactly what he had just witnessed.

And the wolves, having won her battle, were now sprawled around her like hounds at a fire while she moved among them, touching ears and ruffs, murmuring thanks and scolding the youngest one — a gangly yearling who had nearly chased the fleeing men too far — with the exasperated fondness of a sister. The great pale male, Greywind, leaned his entire weight against her hip, and she staggered, laughed, shoved back, and the sound of her laughter carried across the water and did something to Aldric's chest that he refused to examine closely.

One of the wolves noticed him watching. It rose, ambled over, and sat down directly in front of him, close enough that he could count the scars on its muzzle, and studied him with amber patience.

"I'm not going to hurt her," Aldric said quietly. He wasn't sure why he was explaining himself to a wolf. He wasn't sure why it felt necessary.

The wolf's ear flicked. It made a low sound, half rumble, half question, and Aldric had the sudden vertiginous certainty that he was being *assessed* — weighed by some standard older and stricter than any oath he'd ever sworn.

"He means it," Dominic said, coming up beside them both. She was mud-splattered to the knees, a scratch on her jaw he hadn't noticed during the fight, and she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read. "Mostly. Greywind thinks you're a strange creature. Loyal to a pack you were born to betray, and betrayed the pack instead." She tilted her head. "Wolves don't really understand knights."

"No," Aldric said. "Neither do I, lately."

She sat down beside him on the log, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and for a while neither spoke. Across the ford, the abandoned horse grazed the bank grass with the serene opportunism of its kind. The wounded bounty man's blood had already washed away downstream. Somewhere north, two leagues away, the safe bridge waited, and beyond it the road to the capital, and beyond that the Heartroot, and beyond that whatever ending was written for them both.

"You heard what he said," Aldric said at last. "The warrant's changed. I'm named in it now. Aiding and abetting." He laughed, short and airless. "Fifteen years of service. Pyres lit. Heretics delivered. And it took one woman and eight wolves to make me an outlaw."

"Does it feel how you thought it would?"

He considered that with genuine care, because she had asked genuinely. "Lighter," he admitted. "Which frightens me more than chains would."

Dominic nodded slowly, as though he'd confirmed something she'd long suspected. Then she reached into the courier's satchel and drew out the map — wax seal cracked now, parchment softened by handling — and held it out to him.

"What's this?"

"You should look at it again. Really look." Her voice had changed; the lightness was gone from it, replaced by something careful and grim. "While the wolves rest. Because Greywind brought me news this morning, before the ambush. Word passed beast to beast down from the mountains — ravens talk to foxes, foxes talk to wolves, and none of them care about human borders." She met his eyes. "There's an army gathering at the mountain passes, Aldric. Your commander's army. Moving toward the Heartroot. And the wolves say they're driving captured beasts ahead of them in chains — stags, bears, things with old blood in them." Her hand closed briefly over the map. "They're not hunting the source of magic to destroy it anymore. They mean to *use* it. And they're taking the road through Thornfell, which means everything we thought we had time for—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "We don't have time anymore."

Aldric took the map. In the flat silver light of the afternoon, the faded lines seemed to shift as he studied them, the old cartographer's hand revealing paths he'd sworn weren't there before — and beneath the paths, faint as breath on cold glass, words in a script he almost recognized.

Behind them, Greywind lifted his head and gazed north, toward the mountains, and began, very softly, to howl.
