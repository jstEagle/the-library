## 9. The Price of the Neighbor

The news came with the crows.

Dominic had learned to read birds long before she could name what she was doing — before she knew that what moved through her was a gift at all and not simply a strange, private weather. In the valley it had been only a faint pressure behind her eyes, a sense of animals as half-lit thoughts, muffled by whatever old enchantment slept in the Weeping Wood's stones. But here beyond the boundary, three days out from home with a disgraced knight riding at her side, every wingbeat arrived like a shout. So when the crows came down from the north in a ragged black ribbon, croaking in their flat, carrion-minded way about *dead thing, cold thing, man-thing on the road*, she understood the shape of it before they spoke a single useful word.

She stopped so suddenly that her mare bumped her spine.

Aldric drew rein beside her, one hand already drifting toward his sword — habit, not threat; he had stopped reaching for it around her sometime during the second day, and she had noticed that he'd noticed, and neither of them had said anything about it. "What is it?"

"The crows." Her voice came out strange. Flat. "There's a dead man on the mill road. Back the way we came. They say he—" She swallowed. "They say he was asking for someone before they found him. A woman. A neighbor."

"Which neighbor?"

"They don't know names." But some part of her, some cold and precise part she did not recognize as her own, already knew. The valley had perhaps forty souls who would have spoken up when the witch-hunters came through demanding to know who had touched the dying courier. Forty souls, and most of them had spent Dominic's whole childhood looking at her sideways, remembering the pyre, keeping their distance the way sensible people keep distance from a well that once drowned a child.

Only one of them had ever brought her bread without being asked. Only one had stood in her doorway the night after the hunters first came through, planting herself there like a fence post against the wind, saying *she was with me, she was with me all evening* — a lie told loudly enough to be remembered, generously enough to be believed by everyone except the people it needed to fool.

"Maren," Dominic said. The word came out of her like a tooth coming free. "It's Maren."

"You can't know that."

"I know it." She turned her mare north, back along the road they had sweated two days to put behind them, and Aldric's hand closed on her bridle before she could spur.

"Dominic." He said her name the way he said everything now — carefully, like a man walking across ice he himself had cracked. "Think. If they've taken someone from your valley, taking them was the message. The commander doesn't waste riders chasing a rumor into the hills. He wants you to hear about it. He wants you to come back grieving and stupid."

"And if I don't come back, what does that make me?"

"A survivor."

"That's not an answer, Ser Aldric. That's a description."

He held her bridle a moment longer. This close she could see the gray coming into his temples like frost creeping across a window, and the scar at his jaw she had not yet asked about, and something else — the particular stillness of a man who has seen this exact grief before and lit the fire under it himself. Then he let go, because he was not her keeper and knew it, and because some part of him, she suspected, wanted very much to see what she would do.

They rode north.

* * *

Maren Odell had kept bees.

That was how Dominic thought of her first, when thinking became possible again: not the way she looked at the end, but the way she had been on summer mornings, veiled and gloved and utterly unafraid, lifting the lids off her hives while the air roared gold around her. *You don't fight them, girl,* she'd said once, when Dominic was small and terrified of the humming boxes at the edge of her garden. *You listen. Everything that stings you tells you first what it wants. Most folk just never bother hearing.*

Dominic had been eleven. She had gone home and pressed her ear against the goat-shed wall and listened until the goats' slow cud-chewing thoughts resolved, faint and warm and dim as voices underwater, into something like speech. Maren had given her that. Maren had handed her the key to the locked room inside herself and never known it, and then twenty years later had planted her broad body in a doorway and lied to armed men for her.

And they had taken her anyway.

The crows led them to a wayside shrine half a mile off the mill road, a leaning stone saint with moss for a face, and beneath it — no. Not Maren. A man in hunter's livery, or what had been a man; the wolves had been at him and the crows were offended about the competition. Aldric dismounted, turned the body with his boot, and went very still.

"This is Veyra's man," he said. "One of hers. Scout named Pell."

"Then where—"

"She wouldn't leave her own dead at a roadside shrine unless the shrine was the message too." Aldric straightened, scanning the treeline with the flat professional sweep of a man reading terrain the way monks read scripture. "Look at the offerings."

Dominic looked. At the saint's feet, among the usual stubs of tallow candles and wilting flowers, someone had arranged a row of objects with terrible neatness: a woman's shoe. A wooden hair comb with a broken tooth. And a strip of linen, bloodstained, pinned to the stone with a hunting knife.

Not a message for her. A message *from* someone. From whoever had taken Maren and left this calling card for any hunter who followed — or for the knight who had gone traitor, whose habits they knew, who might be counted on to pass this way.

"Pell wasn't killed by beasts," Aldric said quietly. "That's a knife wound. Someone in the company is leaving breadcrumbs."

"Or someone wants us to think so." Dominic slid down from her mare. Her legs felt wrong, distant, like borrowed things. She knelt by the shoe. It was Maren's — she'd know that cracked heel-leather anywhere, those shoes had walked to Dominic's door a hundred mornings carrying bread still warm enough to fog the cloth — and beside it, caught on the stone's rough edge, a single strand of coarse grey wool. Wool from Maren's own spindle-spun yarn. Maren had been here. Alive. Recently enough to bleed on this stone.

Or recently enough to die on it.

The thought arrived whole and merciless, and with it the memory she had been outriding for three days: Maren in her doorway, arms crossed, chin up, telling the hunters *you'll not search my house, I've nothing to hide and less patience* — and the tall officer smiling his thin smile and saying *madam, everyone has something to hide; we're merely professionals about finding it.* And Dominic slipping out the back with the map against her ribs while Maren's voice held the door like a beam holds a roof.

She had known, even then, in the animal part of her that never stops counting costs. She had chosen the map over staying. She had let an old woman stand in a doorway against soldiers because the map mattered more, because the mission mattered more, because *someone* had to carry the Heartroot's plea east and Maren had volunteered her doorway without being asked—

"Dominic." Aldric's voice, careful again. "Whatever you're building in your head right now, stop building it."

"They took her because of me."

"They took her because they're butchers and it's what butchers do. The difference matters. One of those things you can answer for. The other you'd be carrying a debt that isn't yours, and it will drag you under before we ever see the mountains."

"Don't tell me what I'm carrying." She heard her own voice go low and strange and did not care. She stood. The world had narrowed to a hot point somewhere behind her sternum. "Don't you dare tell me what I'm carrying, Ser Aldric. You weren't there. You didn't eat her bread. You didn't watch her lie for you."

"For me? She never met—"

"*For what I am.* Same thing." The fury came up in her like floodwater, and for once she let it, because holding it down hurt worse than drowning. "I spent my whole life hiding. Half-buried. Safe. Do you know what safe cost? It cost her. Every year I stayed quiet, I was spending her. Spending all of them. And now the bill's come due and you want me to ride past it to the mountains because *the mission matters*—"

"I want you alive."

"Well, I want her *back*." The words tore something on the way out. "Or I want the men who took her. Is that so monstrous? They put a shoe on a stone like a trophy. They interrogated a sixty-year-old beekeeper in a village that hasn't seen violence in a generation, and when they're done — when they've wrung whatever they think she knows out of her — what happens? You know what happens. You've *done* it. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me what the Crown does with old women who lie for witches."

Aldric said nothing. The silence was its own answer, and they both heard it.

* * *

They made camp late, in a hollow where alders choked a dry streambed, because Aldric insisted on putting a ridge between them and the mill road and Dominic was past arguing about anything that delayed her less than an hour. No fire. Cold rations. The horses hobbled and dozing. Above them the stars came out hard and indifferent, and somewhere in the dark an owl asked its one question over and over, and got no answer worth giving.

Dominic sat with her back against an alder and held the strip of bloodied linen she had taken from the shrine, though Aldric had advised against it — *don't carry relics of grief, they grow heavy* — and turned it over and over in her fingers. The blood had gone brown. Old blood, or recent; there was no telling. That was the torture of it, the exquisite cruelty: not knowing. Somewhere south of here Maren was either in a cell being asked questions by patient men, or she was past all questions, and both possibilities were unbearable in different directions, and Dominic could not stop swinging between them like a bell struck by wind.

"I keep thinking about the bees," she said finally, because the silence had become a weight on her chest. "When I was small. She taught me to listen. She didn't even know what she was teaching. She just — she was kind in the specific way. The useful way. Everyone else in that valley was kind the way you're kind to a stray you won't take in. Feed it, don't name it. Maren named me."

Across the fire-ring, Aldric sat sharpening a blade he did not need to sharpen, the whetstone whispering in slow strokes. He had been doing that more often since the ford — since he'd put his body between her and his own allies, since the moment his old life had ended with witnesses. A man keeping his hands busy so his mind wouldn't report on his situation.

"If she's alive," he said, "she's in the company's baggage train. Interrogations travel with the column. Which means she moves slowly, which means—" He stopped himself. She watched him decide whether to finish the sentence, watched him choose honesty over mercy, because that was the bargain between them now, unspoken since the chapel: *no more softening.* "Which means she's likely already given them everything she has. Which means they have no further use for her. The Crown doesn't feed prisoners it's finished with on a moving campaign."

"So she's dead."

"So she may be dead. Or she may be in a cage wishing she were. I don't know, and I won't pretend to. What I know is what the commander wants." He set the whetstone down. In the starlight his face was all planes and shadow, a face carved for grim news. "Veyra wants you grieving. Grief makes people fast and stupid. She's burned villages for less than a map, Dominic — do you understand what she'd burn for the chance to take you in the act of rescuing a prisoner? She'd write the ballad herself. *The witch dies trying to save the crone.* It would end every question in the ranks about whether burning women works."

"Then I'll be fast and clever instead."

"No. You'll grieve, and then you'll ride east, and we'll finish what the courier died for." His voice was gentle, which made it worse. Gentle was the tone he used when he was being immovable. "I know what it looks like from where you sit. It looks like I'm asking you to abandon her twice. I'm not. I'm asking you not to give Veyra the only ending that lets her sleep at night. Maren Odell lied to armed men to buy you time. Don't spend that time buying her a pyre."

"My justice isn't a pyre."

"It always starts as one." Something moved behind his eyes — that old guilt, the one he carried like a stone in his boot, the one she had glimpsed at the ford and at the chapel and never yet been shown plainly. "I've stood where you're standing. Wanting the law to reach where it doesn't reach. Wanting fire because fire is the only justice that feels proportional when someone's taken something you can't get back. And I'll tell you what I learned at the cost of—" His jaw worked. "At cost. Fire doesn't distinguish. You light it at the guilty man and it spreads to the field, the barn, the family that sheltered him. You light it at an institution and it burns the prisoners with the judges. Justice outside the law isn't a clean sword, Dominic. It's a fire, and you are standing in a dry forest."

"Then what would you have me do?" She was on her feet without deciding to stand. "Swear the oath again in my heart? Trust the Crown that strung up a girl in your story and calls it order? Trust *you*?" She regretted it before it landed, saw it land, saw the flinch he mastered so quickly it was almost invisible — almost. But she was too far into the flood to turn back. "You hunt people like me for fifteen years and then discover one conscience and I'm meant to build my grief a house on it?"

The whetstone lay still. For a long moment the only sound was the owl, asking, asking.

"Yes," Aldric said at last. Quietly. Without heat. "That's exactly what you're meant to do. Because it's the same thing I'm doing with you." He rose, unhurried, and stood close enough that she could see the white scars on his knuckles, earned one at a time over fifteen years of work she was only beginning to imagine. "Every day I ride with you, I'm betting my life that the beast-speaker the Church warned me about is worth trusting past everything I was taught. You want to call my conscience a thin foundation? Fine. Look at yours. You've known me a month. You're betting Maren's death — and your own — on the idea that I'm not marching you to the Heartroot in chains the moment it suits me. We are both building on thin ground. That's what trust *is*. Now sit down, drink your water, and sleep, because tomorrow you're going to make a decision, and I want you making it rested."

"What decision?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he reached into his coat and set something on the flat stone between them.

The hunting knife. The one that had pinned the linen to the shrine.

"I pulled it before you saw," he said. "While you were kneeling with the shoe." He turned it so the starlight caught the maker's mark stamped at the guard's base: a tower split by lightning, struck through with a bar. "That's not a Crown armory mark. That's a guild sign. The Grey Company — bounty hunters, ex-soldiers, the kind of men who sell their knives to whichever side pays, including, lately, ours. Veyra hires them for dirty work she doesn't want her knights' names on." His voice dropped. "Including interrogation work. If Maren is alive, she's not with the army. She's with *them*. In a camp that moves slower than a column. Guarded by sellswords, not soldiers."

Dominic stared at the knife. The flood in her chest went very quiet, the way a river goes quiet before it finds its new course.

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because I promised myself, at the ford, that I'd stop lying to you by omission. And because—" he exhaled, and it sounded like something in him tearing loose, "—because if I refuse to help you and you go anyway, you'll die alone in the dark, and I find I've lost the appetite for that outcome. So. Tomorrow, at first light, you choose. East to the Heartroot, and Maren's death becomes fuel instead of a debt. Or south, after the Grey Company — knowing it's what Veyra wants, knowing it may be a trap, knowing that every hour we spend on it is an hour the Heartroot bleeds."

He picked up the knife and held it out to her, handle first, and his eyes in the starlight were steady and sorrowful and absolutely clear.

"But understand what you'd be choosing, Dominic. Not vengeance. Rescue. If we go south, we go to bring her out alive, and if she's already dead—" his voice did not waver, and somehow that was the worst kindness anyone had ever done her, "—then we turn around, and we bury her properly, and we ride east, and you do not light a fire you can't put out. Swear it. Swear that, and I'll cut our way into that camp myself."

Dominic took the knife. Its weight was nothing and it was everything. She thought of Maren's hands, gloved and calm amid the roaring gold. She thought of a doorway held like a beam against a storm, and a lie told loudly enough to be remembered.

"I swear," she said. "Rescue. Not vengeance."

But deep in the animal country behind her ribs, where the wolves and crows and owls murmured their ancient gossip, something older and less forgiving stirred — and far to the south, carried faint on the night wind, a chorus of dogs began to bark in the rhythm she had already learned meant *soldiers, many, moving slow.*

South. Moving slow.

Alive, then. Perhaps. Possibly. The bell in her chest began to swing again — but now it swung toward a camp full of sellswords, and a door she intended to open, and a price she was suddenly, terribly willing to pay.
