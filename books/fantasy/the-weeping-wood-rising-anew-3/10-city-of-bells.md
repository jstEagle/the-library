## 10. City of Bells

Dominic had never imagined a city could be so loud.

Bells, first of all — a dozen towers all ringing at once as they came through the south gate, the sound tumbling over itself until it was less music than weather, something that pressed on her chest and made her teeth ache. Then the voices beneath it: hawkers crying oysters and hot wine, a preacher howling about the end of days, children shrieking through an alley game that involved throwing stones at a chalked circle with a goat's head drawn inside it.

She flinched at that last one, and Aldric's hand found her elbow — brief, impersonal, the touch of a man steering his sister through a crowd. They had agreed on the story in the cart on the road down from Thornfell: a wool merchant's clerk and his wife, come to the capital for the autumn fair. His hair was cropped close now and greyed with ash; hers was bound up under a plain coif, her face scrubbed of everything but tiredness. He wore no sword. It nearly killed him to leave it behind, she could tell. The knife at his belt sat like a phantom limb.

*Don't look up,* he'd said at the gate. *In the city, people who look up are looking for trouble, or making it.*

So she looked instead at what was level with her eyes, which was somehow worse. Cages. Small ones, hung from brackets beside the gatehouse like decorative birdcages, and in three of them — things. A crow with too many joints in its wings. A ferret whose fur moved against the wind. Something in the third cage that might once have been a cat, curled tight around its own wrongness, breathing very fast.

"They leave them there to die," Aldric murmured. "It's cheaper than feeding them. And it's a lesson."

"To whom?"

"Everyone." His voice did not change at all, and she understood suddenly that this flatness cost him something, that he had learned it the way she had learned silence — one day at a time, until it set like scar tissue.

The cages swung gently in the bell-wind. The thing that had been a cat opened its eyes, and Dominic felt the ghost of a voice brush the edge of her mind — thin, starved, less words than a single sustained note of *wrong* — and then it was gone, whether because the creature had nothing left or because the city itself smothered such voices the way snow smothers embers.

She kept walking. She did not look up.

* * *

Aldric knew the capital the way a man knows a house he grew up in and has tried for years to forget. Every street had a name and every name had a memory attached, most of them bad. The Fountain of Saint Verena where he'd been knighted — the water in it ran rust-red now; they said the pipes were old, but he remembered the color arriving the same season the trials had intensified, and he did not believe in coincidence. The Chancery Row with its white arcades where writs were signed. The Bellfounders' Quarter, where the great bells were cast, and where, if you knew which foundry and which cellar door, you could descend into the Undercroft of the Office of Inquiry — the archive, the ledger-hall, the place where the Crown kept its half of the world's sins written down.

Including, if the courier's map was to be believed, half of a map.

"The trial court sits tomorrow," he told Dominic in the rented room above a dyer's shop, where the ceiling beams were stained every color grief can be and the air smelled permanently of vinegar. "Which means tonight the clerks will be working late preparing the dockets. Tomorrow morning they'll be exhausted and the hall will be near-empty during the session. Either way we go in when they're not looking. But I want to see the court first. I want to know who's sitting on the bench."

"Why?"

"Because if Commander Veyre is attending, the Heartroot writs will be in his personal custody, not the archive, and this whole errand dies before it's born." He said it lightly, practically, and watched her face while he said it, because he had learned in the weeks since the ford that watching her face told him things his own instincts no longer could.

Her face now was doing something complicated. "You keep saying *the writs*," she said. "Like it's paper. Like it's procedure."

"It is paper. That's the horror of it." He sat down on the bed's one stool, which put him lower than her, and found he didn't mind. "Every fire they light starts as ink. A name entered into a ledger. A warrant under seal. I used to think the paperwork was just — the machinery. The ugly gears behind the real work." He turned his hands over, looked at the palms as if checking them for soot. "Then one day I understood the paperwork *was* the work. Everything else is just delivery."

She was quiet a moment. Outside, the bells rang the hour — six strokes, muffled by walls — and in the pause between them he heard her say, softly, "Then tomorrow we go watch the delivery."

* * *

The trial court was held in the old granary hall, and it was full despite the hour, because executions drew crowds the way spilled honey drew flies.

They stood at the back among the fishwives and apprentices and pious widows, and Aldric kept his hood up and his eyes forward and hated himself with a thoroughness that had long since stopped feeling like anything at all. On the raised dais sat three inquisitors in white — white, always white, the Crown's chosen color for purity, though after ten years he mostly associated it with ash — and before them, in a pen of iron lattice, stood a woman perhaps fifty years old with her hands tied in front of her and her chin up.

"She's accused of harboring," the clerk droned, reading from the docket. "Harboring a marked beast within her dwelling. The beast having been found in her cellar, concealed under sacking."

"What manner of beast?" asked the middle inquisitor, a soft-voiced man named Corvane whom Aldric had once drunk wine with and liked.

"A hen, reverend sir."

"A hen."

"Marked, reverend sir. Six toes upon the left foot."

There was a stirring in the crowd — not outrage; appetite. Aldric felt Dominic go rigid beside him, felt the heat of her fury come off her like heat off a stone in summer, and he shifted, very slightly, so that his shoulder blocked her view of the dais. She didn't move away. She also didn't stop shaking.

The woman was asked if she had known the hen to be marked. She said she had bought the hen at market. She was asked if she had examined its feet. She said — and here her voice cracked for the first time, and the crowd leaned in like grass bending to wind — she said, "Reverend sir, who looks at a hen's feet?"

The laughter that went around the hall was good-natured, almost warm, and that was the worst part. That was the part Aldric knew would live behind his eyes for the rest of his life: the laughter, warm as harvest, while the clerks wrote *confessed under questioning* and the sentence was read out in the same tone used for tax assessments.

He got Dominic out before the sentence was carried out. There was no law saying spectators must stay; there was no need of one. Everyone stayed. Everyone always stayed. But he took her arm and steered her down a side corridor toward the privies, which were empty, which were always empty during sentences, and there, in a stone room that stank of lime, Dominic Winterbourne bent double and was quietly, comprehensively sick.

When it passed she straightened, wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist, and looked at herself in the little polished plate nailed by the basin — a cracked oval of tin that gave back a grey ghost of a face.

"Six toes," she said. Her voice came out scraped hollow. "Aldric. They're going to burn a woman for a *hen*."

"Yes."

"And everyone laughed."

"Not everyone." He thought of the faces he'd catalogued without meaning to, the habit of a decade: the widow who hadn't smiled. The apprentice who'd looked at his shoes. The fishwife who'd spat, though at whom was unclear. "But enough. Enough is the number that matters. It only ever needs enough."

She turned to face him, and in the tin's warped reflection he saw both their ghosts at once, and something in him cracked along an old seam.

"You sat on that bench," she said. Not accusation. Worse — curiosity. Genuine, patient, terrible curiosity, the kind that waits for an answer rather than demanding one. "Didn't you."

"Twice." The word fell out of him like a tooth. "Early years. Junior assignments. You sit, you listen, you write your concurrence, and you tell yourself the sentence was decided before you arrived and your dissent would change nothing except your own prospects, and that your prospects matter because someone has to be inside the machine who knows it's rotten." He laughed, short and joyless. "I told myself that for nine years. Do you know what I built with it? Nothing. A beautiful room of nothing. And outside the room, the fires."

The bells began again — vespers, the whole city ringing at once, and under the brazen roar he heard her say, "And now you're stealing paper from it."

"Now I'm stealing paper from it." He met her eyes. "It isn't enough. I've known for years it isn't enough. But it's what my hands can hold tonight."

For a moment neither of them moved. The bells filled the stone room like water filling a hull. And then she did the thing he was learning to dread more than any ambush: she reached out and laid her palm flat against his chest, over the heart, over the place where a sword should have been, and left it there, warm through the dyer's-smelling wool.

"Tonight," she agreed. "Your hands can hold tonight. So can mine."

* * *

The Undercroft opened off a foundry cellar, behind a door that required Aldric's face and a spoken phrase and an iron key sewn into the hem of a dead friend's coat, and the fact that all three still worked told Aldric something sad about how the Office treated its presumed-dead traitors: carelessly.

Inside, the archive breathed cold. Rows of pigeonhole shelves rose into darkness beyond the lamp's reach, each hole stuffed with rolled dockets tied in ribbon — red for executed, black for pending, white for *released*, a color so rarely used the white holes looked like missing teeth. The air smelled of tallow and mouse and, faintly, of smoke that had soaked into the paper over years until the whole room had become, in some slow alchemical way, a record of burning.

"Third gallery," Aldric whispered. "Geographic writs. If Veyre filed the northern survey, it'll be under Weeping Wood or under *Severance*, depending which clerk catalogued it. Clerks are the only honest people in this building. Find one who hates his work and you find truth."

They split — his insistence, her grudging consent, thirty feet apart among the shelves so they could cover twice the ground. Dominic ran her fingers along the labels, mouthing them silently: *Wendwater. Wexley. Woldmere.* The names of places where things had happened, each roll a village, each village a family, and somewhere in this dark the roll that concerned her valley, her goats, her mother's grave —

She stopped. Her breath stopped with it.

The label read: **SEVERANCE — VALLEY PROVISIONS — RESTRICTED — EYES OF THE SECOND SEAL ONLY**.

And the pigeonhole behind it was empty. Not cleared — emptied recently. The dust showed the clean rectangle where a fat leather case had lain for years, and beside it, dropped in haste or left deliberately, a single sheet of cheap paper bearing four words in a clerk's careful hand:

*Moved. Veyre carries it.*

Behind her, thirty feet away in the dark, Aldric said quietly, "Dominic. Don't come here. Just tell me what you see."

She picked up the note. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were perfectly steady.

"He doesn't have it yet," she said. "Veyre carries it. Which means tomorrow, at the trial — he'll be on that bench. Won't he."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, faint through forty feet of stone, the city's bells rang out the midnight change, and Dominic stood alone in the dark among the records of the burned, holding a piece of paper that meant the map was closer than it had ever been — and farther out of reach.

"Aldric," she said. "How do we steal something off a man's body in front of three thousand witnesses?"
