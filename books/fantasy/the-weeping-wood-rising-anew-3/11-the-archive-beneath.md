## 11. The Archive Beneath

The bells of Vael Corin never stopped.

They rang the hours, they rang the trials, they rang the executions — and beneath all of it, so deep in the city's bones that only someone listening for it would ever hear, there was a lower sound: the groan of stone settling on stone, of a city pressing down upon its own foundations like a man leaning his weight on a sleeping thing's chest.

Dominic heard it because she had spent three days learning to listen past the bells.

She stood now in a corridor that no map of the palace admitted existed, at the foot of a spiral stair that had gone down far longer than any building should have been tall. The walls were older than the walls above them. That was the first wrongness she'd noticed, when Aldric led her through the door behind the wine cellar with the key he'd taken off a dead man's belt and swore he'd never expected to use again: the masonry changed as they descended, pale dressed blocks giving way to grey fieldstone, fieldstone to something darker still, fitted without mortar by hands that had trusted nothing but gravity and patience. The air smelled of dust and tallow and, faintly, of sap.

Sap. Under a mountain of stone, in the driest place she had ever stood, something green was breathing.

"You've been here before," she said. It wasn't a question. She kept her voice low anyway; the stair drank sound the way dry earth drinks rain.

"Once." Aldric's torch threw his shadow long up the wall ahead of them, a black giant walking backward into the dark. "When I took my oath. They bring every knight down here once, to show them what we fight for. What we fight *against*." His jaw worked. "They showed us weapons. Seized grimoires. Chains forged from cold-iron that could hold a shapechanger. They did not show me whatever's at the bottom of this stair."

"There's more than one way to teach a lie," Dominic said quietly. "You don't have to say anything untrue. You only have to decide where the light falls."

He glanced back at her, and in the torchlight his face was stripped of the commander's mask he wore above ground. Three days in the capital, moving by night through a city that had burned her name onto three separate warrant boards, had done that to him. Or perhaps it had been the trial court they'd walked past, the one where a woman not much older than Dominic had been led out between two rows of white-helmed knights while the crowd sang a hymn about cleansing. He hadn't spoken for an hour after that.

The stair ended.

Not dramatically — there was no door, no seal, no warding sigil flaring against them. The steps simply ran out into a floor, and the floor opened into darkness so complete that the torch seemed to shrink inside its own flame. Then Aldric lifted it high, and the dark stepped back just far enough to reveal what it had been hiding, and Dominic forgot how to breathe.

Shelves. Shelves rising out of sight, rank upon rank upon rank, receding into the black in every direction, each one crowded with books and scroll-cases and tablets and things she had no names for — bundles of feathers tied with red thread, jars of seeds labeled in a script that hurt to look at directly, a child's shoe resting on a velvet cushion like a relic. The ceiling, if there was one, was lost somewhere above the reach of the light. The room did not feel large. It felt *deep*, the way a well is deep, the way roots are deep.

"The royal archive," Aldric said. "Or what the archivists call the Undervault. Officially, it stores the records of extinguished lineages. Unofficially—" he turned slowly, and the torchlight slid across ten thousand spines, "—I think it stores everything the Crown has ever wanted forgotten."

"It's warm," Dominic said.

He frowned. "It's cold. Stone this deep—"

"Not the air." She pressed her palm flat against the nearest shelf, against wood gone silver-grey with age, and felt it: a pulse, faint as a heartbeat heard through a barn door. "Something under us is alive."

*And it is afraid,* said a voice at the edge of her hearing, thin as thread, and Dominic's whole body went rigid. Here, in the deepest heart of the city that had built itself over magic like a tomb built over a grave that wouldn't stay filled, her gift had not gone silent. It had gone *whispering* — muffled, strained, a shout through six feet of packed snow — but it was there. Something below was speaking, or trying to, and every word cost it what a drowning swimmer's words cost him.

Aldric saw her face change. "What is it?"

"Move the light away from me," she whispered. "Slowly. There's something — I need to follow it."

* * *

They went deeper.

The shelves gave way to open ground — actual earth, dark and soft, though no sky had ever touched it — and the pulse beneath her palms became a pull beneath her feet. Dominic walked it the way she'd once walked toward a wounded ewe crying out in the night fog: half-blind, certain, sick with dread. Aldric followed with the torch held low, and neither of them spoke, because the whispers were getting stronger and they were not kind.

*—starved—*

*—the line thins, the line thins, warden's daughter, warden's daughter—*

*—so long in the dark, so long cut from the green—*

"Warden's daughter," Dominic repeated aloud, and the word felt like a hook going through her cheek. "It called me warden's daughter."

"Your family kept goats."

"My family kept goats *here*." Her voice cracked on it. "In a valley where magic can't follow. Where my gift sleeps. Where I spent twenty-two years thinking I was broken, or cursed, or lucky, depending on the day." She stopped walking. The whispering pooled around her ankles like cold water. "Aldric. Why does a valley forget?"

He had no answer. She watched him understand, watch the same terrible arithmetic run behind his eyes that was running behind hers, and for a moment they simply stood there in the living dark, two people arriving together at a door neither wanted to open.

Then she made herself walk through it.

At the center of the Undervault, where the earthen floor began to rise into a low mound, they found the table. It was a single slab of heartwood older than any tree she had ever seen, polished by centuries of hands, and on it lay documents arranged with the neatness of long habit: rolls of vellum, a ledger bound in white leather gone ivory with time, and a map — a copy, the wax seal of the original still ghosted in brown beside it — that she knew the way you know your own reflection in a stranger's window.

The courier's map. The valley. Her home, drawn in a clerk's careful hand.

Beside it lay a second document, older, its ink faded to the color of old blood. Dominic reached for it, and her fingers were steady, which surprised her; she had thought they would shake.

*Charter of Severance,* read the heading. *In the Year of the Long Frost.*

She read. And the valley that had raised her — the safe valley, the quiet valley, the valley that had held her like two cupped hands hold water — turned over beneath her, and showed her its other face.

* * *

She read aloud, because some things are too heavy to carry silently, and because Aldric needed to hear it as much as she did. Perhaps more.

*"Let it be recorded that the Weeping Wood, so named for the grief of the last Heartroot, shall be maintained in perpetuity as a place of severance. No road shall enter. No spell shall cross its bounds. Let its people be simple, and many, and bound to their holdings, for a speaker-line starves quickest among the humble, who marry near and die near and forget far."*

Her voice caught on *forget far*. She made herself continue.

*"Let the descendants of Warden Eirlys be settled within the bounds, and let no record of their charge travel with them. Let them be given beasts to tend, for a speaker-child who grows amid beasts will feel the lack of speech as an ache without a name, and will learn to hide it, and hiding is half our work done for us. Let no priest instruct them. Let no scholar visit. When a child shows the gift, report it to the Office of Roots, and the Office will arrange a marriage, or a move, or a mischance, as the case requires."*

*A mischance.* Dominic set the page down very carefully, the way you set down something that might otherwise make you scream. "My grandmother's brother," she heard herself say. "He drowned in a stream knee-deep. Everyone said. Everyone always said how strange it was."

"Dominic—"

"And my mother left the valley exactly once, to be married, and came back within the year, and never spoke of why." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "*Hiding is half our work done for us.* They didn't even need hunters. They built a farm and handed my family a hoe and waited for us to bury ourselves."

Aldric had gone grey. She watched him read the passage she'd already read twice, the one that had driven the breath out of her body like a blow to the sternum, and she watched him arrive at it, and she watched the knight of ashes finally find his own pyre.

*"Note, for the Office's private record: the severance holds because the Heartroot draws its strength from the speech of its wardens. Each generation born within the bounds and barred from the green weakens it further. Within four generations, by the Office's reckoning, the Heartroot will fail entire, and wild magic will pass from the world, and the Crown's long war will be won without another fire lit."*

"They didn't want to destroy magic," Aldric said hoarsely. "They wanted it to *die of loneliness*."

"No." Dominic shook her head slowly. "Worse. Look further. Keep reading — the later entries, the ones in fresh ink."

He unrolled the newest vellum, and she watched his eyes track down it, and she watched the last of his loyalty to the white cloak die somewhere around the third line.

The recent entries were brief, clerical, obscene in their tidiness. *Severance weakening; subjects in the valley reporting boundary phenomena. Subject D.W., female, twenty-two years, gift latent but present. Courier intercepted carrying map to Heartroot grove; map recovered? No — map escaped the kill-line. Recommendation: the Heartroot must not be permitted to wake. But if waking cannot be prevented, the Office recommends harvest. A dying god, properly bled, yields enough power to end the northern war, break the eastern leagues, and secure the dynasty for a thousand years.*

"They're not coming to destroy the Heartroot," Dominic said. The whispering beneath her feet had risen to a keening she could feel in her teeth. "They're coming to slaughter it and sell the meat."

* * *

For a long time afterward, neither of them moved.

Dominic stood with her hand flat on the heartwood table, and the table — the *table*, alive too, everything down here was alive, clinging to life the way ivy clings to a wall — pulsed against her palm like a frightened animal's flank, and she understood suddenly, wholly, in the place where understanding stops being thought and becomes marrow:

She had never been hiding from her destiny.

She had been raised inside it. Raised in a cage built by clerks, fed on the lie of safety, groomed by four generations of quiet arrangements into exactly this: the last speaker of a murdered line, standing in the dark beneath her enemy's city, holding the only map out. The valley hadn't sheltered her. The valley was the muzzle. Every neighbor who'd ever side-eyed her strangeness, every winter she'd bitten her tongue rather than answer a goat's complaint aloud, every night she'd lain awake aching toward the treeline like a limb gone numb and burning — none of it was accident. It was architecture. She had lived her whole life inside someone else's blueprint, thanking the walls.

"I thought I chose to leave," she said at last. Her voice came out strange and calm. "On the road. After they took Maren. I thought, *this is the first free choice I've ever made.*"

"And now?"

"Now I know the cage was bigger than I thought. That's all." She looked up at him, and found his eyes wet, and was absurdly, furiously comforted by it. "But knowing the shape of a cage isn't nothing, Aldric. Because now I know which bars to break."

The ledger in white leather still lay open where she'd turned its pages, and as she looked down at it one final time, her eye fell on the last written line — not clerical, this one, but hurried, scratched by a different pen, a different hand, decades ago. A confession, or a warning, buried in the middle of the machinery that had made it necessary:

*To whoever finds this: the severance was sold to the Crown as mercy. It is not mercy. It is starvation with good manners. If any of the warden's blood reads these words — the Heartroot is not a weapon and never was. It is a wound that was never allowed to close. Go to it. Speak to it. End this.*

Below the words, pressed flat and brown and ancient, was a leaf. Oak, by its lobes. From the grove.

The whispering beneath the floor rose all at once into something almost like a word — *warden* — and then, beneath the city, beneath the bells, beneath a thousand years of stone and lies, something vast and starving turned over in its sleep and began, very slowly, to hope.

Dominic picked up the leaf, and the map, and turned for the stairs.

"Aldric," she said. "We're leaving tonight. Before they learn we've been down here."
