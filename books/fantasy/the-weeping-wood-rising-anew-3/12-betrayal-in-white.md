## 12. Betrayal in White

The summons came at dusk, folded inside a hymnal.

Aldric found it waiting in his cell at the Chapterhouse of the White Vigil — a narrow room of whitewashed stone and a cot too short for him, the kind of cell they gave knights who had fallen from grace but not yet far enough to be cast out entirely. Three days he had slept there, or lain awake on its thin mattress, listening to the bells of the capital toll the hours and telling himself that this was still his life. That the white cloak hanging by the door, travel-stained though it was, still meant what it had always meant. That he had come back to the fold, and the fold would hold him.

The hymnal lay on his pillow. He had not left it there.

Inside, between the pages of the Litany of Severance, was a slip of vellum bearing a seal he knew better than his own heartbeat: the crowned antler of Ser Maren Voss, Knight-Commander of the Vigil, the woman who had taught him to hold a blade and to hate in equal measure.

*The Belltower. Ninth bell. Come alone. Come clean.*

He read it twice, then fed it to the candle flame and watched the words curl into ash. Outside his window, the city glowed with ten thousand lamps, and somewhere beneath all that light, in the archive beneath the Archive of Bells, Dominic Winterbourne was reading stolen pages by the light of a shuttered lamp, learning truths about her family that the Crown had buried for three generations.

He should have felt like a knight again. He was back within these walls; his sins had been, if not forgiven, at least overlooked; the old machinery of duty had closed around him like familiar armor. Instead he felt like a man wearing a dead man's clothes.

Ninth bell came too soon.

* * *

The Belltower stood apart from the Chapterhouse, a needle of grey stone where the Vigil's commanders took confession from their knights and issued orders no one else could hear. Aldric climbed its spiral stairs slowly, each step ringing hollow under his boots, and found Ser Maren Voss standing at the open arch at the top, watching the sun drown itself in the western hills.

She had not changed in six years. Grey had come to her temples, but she wore it like campaign honors, and her posture was still the posture that had made a hundred young knights straighten their spines: shoulders square, chin level, hands clasped behind her back as though the whole world were a parade ground she was patiently inspecting.

"Aldric." She did not turn. "You look terrible."

"I've been traveling."

"You've been running." Now she turned, and her eyes — pale, unblinking, the color of frost on a pond — went over him with the thoroughness of an armorer checking for flaws. "Six years chasing rumors through the borderlands. Burning nothing. Hanging no one. Do you know what the men say about you? They say you hunt witches the way a monk prays. Lips moving, heart elsewhere."

"Perhaps my heart is simply quiet."

"Your heart," Maren said, "is guilty." She let the word sit in the air between them like a drawn blade. "I read your reports, Aldric. All of them. Every year they grow thinner. Every year you find fewer fires worth lighting. This spring you wrote me four pages about a woman in Harrow Fen who talks to her hens, and concluded that she is 'harmless and possibly mad.' Four pages to tell me to do nothing. That is not diligence. That is a man begging to be relieved of his oath without having to ask."

Aldric said nothing. The wind off the hills smelled of woodsmoke and coming rain.

Maren crossed the tower floor until she stood close enough that he could see the fine lines around her eyes, the only place age had touched her face. "And now," she said softly, "you come home. You request reinstatement. You tell me you have located the courier's map and the beast-speaker who carries it — the last of the warden line, delivered into our hands by Providence herself." Her voice warmed, and the warmth was worse than the cold. "I believed you, Aldric. I told the Lord Chancellor we had her. I promised him the Heartroot by season's end."

"You have her name wrong," Aldric heard himself say. "She isn't *it*. She isn't a delivery."

"No?" Maren smiled, and it was the smile she'd worn over a hundred pyres, patient and almost tender. "Then let us speak plainly, knight, since plainness seems to be the fashion between us. The Crown knows you brought her into the city. The gate wardens logged your faces. My riders have watched the Ropewalk lodging house for two days." She paused. "They watch it still. Which means that everything — your pardon, your rank, your white cloak returned unstained — depends on one thing only. Bring me the woman. Tonight. And all of it is restored."

The pardon hung before him like a door thrown open onto his old life. His name cleansed. Ashwick forgotten — the village, the pyre, the girl whose screams had taught him the true weight of the word *witch*. Six years of exile ended with a single act.

All he had to do was hand them the person he loved.

"The Heartroot," Aldric said carefully, "is to be destroyed. That has always been the doctrine. Severance eternal. So why does the Lord Chancellor want it delivered intact?"

Something flickered behind Maren's eyes — surprise, quickly mastered, but not quickly enough.

"You've been reading archives," she said.

"I've been *listening*." He stepped closer, and six years of careful neutrality burned away like fog. "I carried the Vigil's sword for fifteen years, Maren. I know how doctrine bends when power is close enough to grasp. The Chancellor doesn't want the Heartroot severed. He wants it *harvested*. A weapon no kingdom can match — magic on a leash, wielded by whoever holds the chain. And every witch burned from here to the coast was never heresy suppression at all. It was pruning. Clearing the field."

"You are tired," Maren said quietly, "and imaginative—"

"I lit a pyre for a girl who could speak to wolves." His voice cracked on it, finally, after six years of holding. "She begged me to spare the wolves. She said they were *her friends*, Maren. She was fourteen." The wind gusted through the arch, carrying the tolling of the ninth bell across the city. "Tell me the doctrine explains her ashes. Tell me the harvest needed her."

For a long moment the Knight-Commander of the White Vigil looked at him, and something ancient and weary moved through her frost-pale eyes — regret, perhaps, or its well-rehearsed counterfeit.

"They'll burn her, Aldric," she said at last, gently, as if explaining death to a child. "Whatever she is, whatever you feel for her — the moment she stands before the Chancellor, she burns. That is mercy compared to what the harvesters would do. Give her to me. Let it be quick. Let it be *clean*. And let yourself come home."

Home. The word landed in him and broke.

He thought of Dominic's face lamplit in the drowned chapel, ink-stained fingers tracing the map's living script. Thought of her laughing once — just once, at the ford, mud-splattered and wild-eyed with wolves around her like a dark crown — and how the sound had rearranged something in his chest that had been calcified since Ashwick. Thought of her voice when she spoke with beasts: not a witch's incantation but a homecoming, a woman greeting a world that had been waiting for her.

"No," Aldric said.

Maren closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, the tenderness was gone, filed away like a report.

"So be it," she said. "Kneel, then, Aldric of the Weeping Wood, and hear your sentence rendered."

"I don't need to kneel. You haven't the authority to pass it."

"I have witnesses." For the first time, genuine sorrow touched her voice. "You were followed up the stairs, knight. Standard practice, when a brother returns from long absence with a suspect's blood on his boots. Ser Callen has heard every word."

Aldric turned.

Ser Callen Roth stood in the shadowed mouth of the stairwell, torchlight sliding off his polished breastplate, his young face carefully blank. Twenty-three years old, maybe. Aldric had held his oath-sword at his knighting, five winters past. Had taught him the low guard. Had drunk with him in this very chapterhouse and told war stories sanitized of all their shame.

Callen's hand rested on his sword hilt. Not drawing it. Resting, the way a man rests his hand on a door he hasn't decided whether to open.

"Ser Aldric," the young knight said, and his voice was steady, barely. "You are under arrest for treason against the Crown and heresy against the Vigil."

"Callen." Aldric kept his voice low, even. "Look at me. Look at me and tell me what you saw in my reports. Tell me what the Vigil has made you burn, these five years, and whether any of it ever once felt like holiness."

"Don't—" The boy's jaw tightened. "Don't make it complicated. It isn't complicated. She's a witch. You're a traitor. The doctrine is clear."

"The doctrine is a leash," Aldric said, "and someone else holds it."

"Enough." Maren's voice cut like a whetted edge. "Ser Callen, summon the watch. Six men to the Belltower stairs, none above. Move."

Callen hesitated — one breath, two — and in that hesitation Aldric saw everything: the doubt, the fear, the terrible machinery of a young conscience trying to reconcile the man who'd taught him swordwork with the traitor in front of him. Then the boy turned and started down the stairs, boots ringing, and the moment closed like water over a drowning man.

Aldric looked at Maren. Maren looked at the sky.

"You could still take the pardon," she said. "Walk down those stairs with your sword surrendered and your honor intact. I will tell the Chancellor you resisted. No one needs to know you refused."

"And Dominic?"

"Dominic Winterbourne dies either way. The only question is whether you die beside her." Maren finally met his eyes, and for one unguarded instant, Aldric saw the woman who had once pulled him, bleeding, from a river in the Thornmarch and carried him three miles on her back. "I am offering you your life, Aldric. Take it. It's the last gift I'm permitted to give you."

He unbuckled his sword belt.

Her eyebrows rose — hope, or its mask — and then he reversed the scabbard and held the blade out to her flat across both palms, the old gesture of a knight surrendering his service, and said:

"This was sworn to the realm. To its people. All of them. Somewhere along the road, the realm stopped meaning that, and started meaning *the Crown*, and the Crown started meaning *the Chancellor's ambition*." He laid the sword on the tower floor between them, steel ringing on stone like a bell tolling. "I renounce it. I renounce the oath, the cloak, and the Vigil. Judge me however you like, Maren. But I won't deliver her. I'd sooner deliver myself to every fire you light."

Silence, except the wind and the far-off bells.

Then Maren did something he had never seen her do. She reached down, picked up his sword, and set it against the wall rather than claiming it.

"Go," she said quietly. "The watch takes six minutes to muster. The south postern gate is watched, but the tannery drain on the river wall is not — I checked it myself this afternoon, because some part of me hoped you'd refuse, and some part of me hoped you wouldn't." Her voice roughened, just slightly. "You were the finest knight I ever trained, Aldric. Don't make me send good men to die catching you."

"Maren—"

"*Run.*"

He ran.

* * *

The Ropewalk lodging house was three streets from the Belltower, and Aldric covered them at a pace just short of panic, white cloak abandoned, hood up, weaving through the evening crowds of fishwives and lamplighters. His mind moved faster than his feet, laying the map of the city over itself: which routes the Vigil's riders would cordon first, how long until Maren's restraint frayed and the Chapterhouse declared him openly, how many minutes remained before Dominic's sanctuary became a trap.

Two days. They had watched the lodging house for two days, and Maren had waited — why? To give him the chance to betray Dominic himself. A cleaner capture. Less mess. The Commander of the White Vigil had gambled on his guilt, and lost, and now the clock was running for both of them.

He took the back stairs three at a time. Their rented room stood at the end of the corridor, and he rapped the signal — twice, pause, once — and heard, from within, the soft scrape of the window latch.

Dominic opened the door herself. She must have heard him coming, must have known his tread among a hundred others; she had that uncanny ear now, half-trained, half-inborn. Her hair was loose, her sleeves rolled, ink still on her fingers from the archive pages spread across the table behind her — pages covered in her own frantic annotations, arrows and circles in charcoal, the warden-line genealogy she'd spent three days reconstructing.

She took one look at his face and knew.

"How long?" she asked.

"Minutes. Maybe less." He was already crossing the room, sweeping the pages into her satchel. "Voss knows everything. There's a knight named Callen Roth who heard me refuse — he'll report it, or he won't, but either way we assume the worst. South postern is watched. The river wall has a drain."

"A drain."

"A tannery drain. It will be the worst thing you've ever smelled, and it comes out under the wall on the reed flats." He slung the satchel across his chest, then hers, then caught her shoulders and made himself look at her. "Dominic. Listen to me. If we're separated—"

"We won't be."

"If we're separated, you go east to the marsh-folk at Saltmere. Show them the warden sign — your family's knot, from the map's border. They owed the wardens passage once."

"Aldric." Her hands came up and framed his face, calloused and warm, and the fear in her eyes was not for herself. "What did it cost you? Back there. What did you have to say?"

He almost lied. Fifteen years of discipline rose in him like a tide, offering the smooth evasion, the deflection, the soldier's habit of carrying wounds privately. And then he remembered the tower, the sword lying on the stones, Maren's face as she set it against the wall instead of taking it, and he understood that the man who would lie to Dominic Winterbourne was a man he had already killed on that tower floor.

"I renounced my oath," he said. "In front of my commander. In front of a brother knight. I put down the sword, Dom. Everything I was — the Vigil, the white cloak, the fifteen years, all of it — I set it on the floor and walked away." His throat worked. "There's no going back to it. Whatever happens tonight, wherever we run — I'm not a knight anymore. I'm just a man who chose you over everything, and I'd do it again, and I don't even know yet if that makes me free or damned."

Dominic didn't flinch. She kissed him — brief and fierce, tasting of lamp-oil and sleepless nights — and when she drew back her eyes were bright and absolutely certain.

"Free," she said. "It makes you free. Now get us to that disgusting drain before I have to find out how I feel about smelling it."

* * *

They went down through the lodging house's kitchen, past a fat cook who took one look at Aldric's coin and developed a sudden fascination with her stockpot. Out the yard gate, into the alley maze behind the rope-walks, where the city's honest business of tar and hemp gave way to its dishonest business of smugglers' cuts and doors that opened onto other buildings' roofs.

The bells began as they ran.

Not the hour bells — the *alarm*, the great brazen voice of the Cathedral of Bells rolling out across the rooftops, and beneath it, thinner and sharper, the Chapterhouse horn answering. Aldric had heard that particular duet twice in his life. Both times, it had meant the same thing: *hunt declared. Kill or take. The Vigil rides.*

"They've declared it already," Dominic gasped, keeping pace at his shoulder. "That fast? Your commander said six minutes—"

"She bought us those six minutes. She used them up." He vaulted a stack of barrels, landed, hauled her over. "Maren wanted me to run. Question is how far she lets us get before duty overrides sentiment."

They burst from the alleys onto Tanner's Lane and the smell hit them like a wall — the reeking chemical stench of lime pits and dung and old blood, so thick Dominic gagged and clapped a sleeve over her mouth. The tannery yard sprawled before them in the dying light, rows of stretching frames hung with hides like flayed shadows, workers long gone home. Beyond it, the river wall loomed, black and massive, and at its base, half-hidden behind a mounded heap of waste, the iron grate of the drain seeped its trickle into the mud.

Behind them, hoofbeats. Many. Coming up Tanner's Lane at the canter.

"There," Aldric breathed. "The grate's rusted — I can break it. Go, go—"

He got the grate half-loose when the first rider rounded the corner, torchlight splashing across the tannery frames, and a voice rang out that froze the marrow in Aldric's bones:

"THERE! The drain — cut them off!"

White cloaks. Five of them, six, spilling into the yard, swords out, torches high. And at their head, riding down into the reeking yard with his visor up and his young face sick with resolve, Ser Callen Roth.

"Ser Aldric!" Callen's voice cracked across the yard. "Surrender! I — you taught me the low guard, you *taught me*, don't make me—"

"Callen, go home," Aldric shouted back, hauling at the grate. "You don't want this. None of you wants this — you're hunting a woman who talks to animals while your Chancellor builds a weapon from a god's corpse! Ask your commander what the Heartroot is *for!*"

"LIAR!" One of the older knights spurred forward. "The witch speaks through his mouth now — see it, brothers? See the sorcery—"

And then the horses screamed.

All of them at once — six warhorses rearing, bucking, shrieking in a chorus of animal terror as something moved in the darkness beyond the tannery wall. Aldric spun, sword-hand empty and useless, and saw them come over the wall in a flowing tide of grey fur: dogs, wolves, feral cats, a brace of deer, even rats pouring from the drains themselves — dozens upon dozens of the city's hidden creatures, silent as a held breath, forming a living crescent between the knights and the drain.

At their center, eyes reflecting torchlight like scattered coals, sat a huge scarred mastiff, and every creature in that yard was looking — Aldric could see it, could *feel* it — at Dominic.

Her hand was pressed flat against the air, fingers spread, and tears ran freely down her face.

"They followed me," she whispered. "Since the archive. Strays — the city's strays — they've been walking behind me for three days and I thought — I thought I was imagining it—" Her voice broke, and rose, and it was no longer entirely her own voice, and every knight in that yard went rigid as the mastiff stood and growled a sound like the earth shifting. "*They are done being hunted. All of them. Go home.*"

The knights' horses would not advance. Would not be urged, whipped, or coerced — six trained warhorses planting themselves like statues, rolling their eyes at the darkness full of teeth. And in that paralyzed moment, Aldric wrenched the grate free, seized Dominic's hand, and pulled her down into the drain's black throat as the first crossbow bolt splintered against the wall above them.

* * *

The reed flats were freezing and stank of the tannery's leavings, and Aldric had never loved a smell more.

They waded through waist-high rushes under a rising moon, the city burning orange with torchlight behind them, alarm bells still tolling. Somewhere ahead, the river widened toward the marshes, toward Saltmere, toward the east road and the mountains beyond — toward Thornfell, and the long dark, and whatever waited for them there.

Halfway across the flats, Dominic stopped, breathing hard, and looked back at the walls of the capital glowing against the night.

"They'll follow us," she said. "All of them. The whole Vigil, the Chancellor's riders. We've made ourselves the most hunted people in the kingdom."

"Yes."

"And your commander — she could have taken us in that yard. She held them back. Why?"

Aldric watched the moonlight on the water and thought of frost-pale eyes, of a sword set against a wall, of the finest knight he'd ever known spending her last gift on a fugitive's head start.

"Because some part of her remembers what we swore to," he said, "before it curdled. It won't save us twice." He took Dominic's hand — ink-stained, mud-caked, trembling slightly with cold and adrenaline and something else, something that felt terrifyingly like hope. "East, then. Through Thornfell. It's cursed, it's lethal, and it's the only way to the Heartroot that an army can't follow."

Dominic laughed — a wild, ragged, wonderful sound, swallowed quickly by the vast dark country opening before them.

"Cursed forest it is," she said. "Lead on, traitor."

And behind them, faint across the water, the bells of the city tolled on and on, ringing out the death of everything Aldric had been — while ahead, deep in the eastern hills, something old and patient stirred in the dark of Thornfell, and waited for them to enter.
