## 13. The Long Dark of Thornfell

The wound in her side had stopped bleeding an hour ago, which should have been a mercy, and instead felt like a verdict.

Dominic knew enough of field-craft to understand what a cold, quiet wound meant. It meant the blood was staying where it belonged, pooling instead of flowing, and that the fever would come next — the sweet, sinking warmth that made a person want to lie down in the ferns and let the forest have her. She pressed her palm harder against the makeshift bandage, a strip of Aldric's shirt soaked through twice already, and kept walking.

Thornfell did not want her to keep walking.

The trees here grew wrong. She had crossed a hundred miles of ordinary woodland since fleeing the city, and ordinary woodland had rules: light fell from above, roots rose from below, and distance behaved itself. In Thornfell the light came sideways, green-gold and sourceless, as though the sun were somewhere it ought not to be. The roots rose like knuckles from black soil. And the distance — the distance lied. She had been walking toward the far edge of the forest since dawn, or what she thought was dawn, and the trees ahead looked no thinner than the trees behind.

Behind her, faint as a memory of sound, she could still hear the horns. The commander's riders had driven them into Thornfell deliberately. *Let the cursed wood do the Crown's work*, Aldric had snarled, dragging her through the bracken with an arrow buried in his shoulder, and then the trees had closed between them and the hunt, and then — she could not remember exactly when — the trees had closed between them and each other.

She had called his name until her voice cracked. The wood had swallowed it whole. No echo. Thornfell ate echoes the way a spider eats flies: quietly, patiently, leaving nothing but the empty husk of the sound.

"Keep to the path," she muttered to herself, because speaking aloud was the only thing keeping the edges of her vision from going soft and dark. "There is always a path."

But there wasn't. There had not been a path since noon, if noon existed here at all. Only the trees, silver-barked and leafless in defiance of the season, their branches hung with pale lichen that stirred without wind. Between them, the undergrowth breathed. Not metaphorically — she could hear it, a slow tidal rush of leaves expanding and contracting, as though the entire forest floor were the flank of some enormous sleeping animal.

And perhaps it was. The beasts had told her stories of Thornfell, back when she could still hear them. Back before—

No. That was wrong too. She could still hear them. She had to be able to. The gift was the one thing the valley had never managed to burn out of her, the one thing the Crown's archive had failed to name, the one thing that was hers.

She stopped, laid her hand against the nearest trunk, and reached out the way she had reached out ten thousand times before — down through her palm, into the living grain, listening for the small bright voices that lived in all growing things.

Silence.

Not the silence of absence. The silence of a room full of people holding their breath.

"They're watching," said a voice at her feet.

Dominic's heart slammed once, hard, and then she saw it: a fox, sitting in the bracken three paces away, its coat the color of rusted iron, its eyes the color of nothing at all. Not amber. Not brown. They were the grey of fog over water, of old mirrors, of things that had forgotten what they used to reflect.

"You're not a beast," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Or you are, but you're not *only*—"

"I am what Thornfell permits me to be." The fox tilted its head, and the gesture was almost comically vulpine, and that was the most unsettling thing about it — the way it performed fox-ness the way an actor performs grief. "You are bleeding on our ground, speaker. Our ground remembers blood. Would you like to know what it does with it?"

"No," Dominic said honestly.

"It grows thorns." The fox smiled, which foxes cannot do, which this one did anyway. "Come. The court will hear you now, because you're dying, and the court loves a dying petitioner. We get so few honest ones."

* * *

It led her downhill, which frightened her more than anything else that day, because there had been no downhill. There had been no up. The terrain of Thornfell arranged itself around the fox's trot like water around a keel, and Dominic stumbled after it, one hand clamped to her side, the other trailing along trunks she no longer touched for comfort but for balance.

The trees changed as they went. The silver bark darkened. The sourceless green light thickened until it moved like something liquid. And then, without transition — one step she was in forest, the next she was in a clearing so vast that its far edge was a rumor — she arrived.

The clearing was full of beasts.

They sat in concentric rings, facing inward, and none of them moved. Stags with antlers hung in moss. Wolves with frost still clinging to their ruffs despite the season. A bear the size of a hay-wagon, its breath stirring the grass in long slow gusts. Crows perched on the antlers of the stags, which should have caused riot and instead caused nothing at all. At the center, ringed by white stones that might once have been standing stones, stood an ancient oak so vast that its trunk alone was wider than her mother's cottage had been, and in its lowest hollow —

In its lowest hollow, two eyes opened.

They were not eyes. They were pools. Green-black, depthless, and when they turned their attention on her Dominic felt it physically, a pressure against her chest like a hand pressing her backward.

"*Speaker.*"

The voice came from everywhere. From the rings of beasts, from the roots, from inside her own skull, resonant as a bell struck underground.

"I'm told I should kneel," Dominic managed. Her legs were shaking, half from blood-loss and half from the sheer animal weight of the attention fixed upon her. "I'd fall over and not get up, so I hope the court will accept a bow."

A sound moved through the rings — not laughter, exactly, but the place where laughter lives in creatures that do not laugh. The great eyes narrowed by a fraction, which she chose to interpret as amusement.

"The last speaker who stood in this circle came four hundred years ago. She asked us for rain." A pause. "We gave it to her. Do you know what we took?"

Dominic's mouth had gone dry. "What did you take?"

"Her name. Every soul in her village forgot it within a season, including her mother. She lived out her days called 'woman' by everyone who loved her." The voice was not cruel. That was the horror of it. It was simply *true*, the way weather is true. "Such is the law of Thornfell, little speaker. Nothing is given. Everything is traded. You come to us wounded, hunted, and lost, and you will ask for passage to the mountain road beyond our northern edge. Ask, and then listen carefully to what we require."

"How do you know what I'll ask?"

"Because we watched you stumble past our border three times today. Because the men with horns wait at our southern eaves and dare not enter. Because you are dying, and the dying ask for roads." The eyes pulsed, slow as a heartbeat. "Ask."

Dominic gathered herself. The clearing swayed gently, like a boat at anchor. She put a hand on one of the white stones to steady herself and found it warm, faintly, like the shoulder of a sleeping animal.

"I ask passage," she said. "Safe passage through Thornfell to the northern edge, for me and—" the word caught, "—for Ser Aldric, if he yet lives, if he comes after me. Safe from your thorns, your turning paths, your hunger. And safe from the men at your southern eaves, should they grow brave enough to follow."

The silence stretched so long she began to fear she had given offense. Then the pressure of attention lifted slightly, and the fox beside her sat up straighter, ears forward.

"A generous petition," said the Heart of the wood. "Two travelers. One wounded. Passage granted — and protection, even against iron men. This is a heavy thing to carry across our dark. The price must weigh the same." The eyes fixed on her. "You carry three currencies, speaker. Your gift. Your years. Your memories. The first is already promised elsewhere — we can taste the Heartroot's claim on you, and we will not poach another's bargain. The second we have little use for; time is the one thing a forest has in plenty. So." The voice softened, and the softness was worse than any threat. "We will take a memory. One piece of home. Chosen by us, not by you. Paid in full the moment you accept."

Dominic's stomach dropped through the warm earth of the clearing.

"Not my memories of—" She stopped. Of what? Of Aldric? He barely counted as home; he was new, raw, unfinished. "My family? My valley?"

"We do not negotiate terms. Only consent."

The rings of beasts watched her with their hundreds of patient eyes. Somewhere south of here, men with horns and torches waited for the forest to finish her. Behind her lay a burned bridge of a city, a dead neighbor whose face she could no longer look at directly, a map sewn into her jacket lining that the whole machinery of the Crown wanted back. Ahead of her lay the Heartroot, dying, calling her name in ways she could feel but no longer hear clearly.

And the price was a piece of home. A single piece.

"What kind of piece?" she asked, hating how small her voice sounded.

"That," said the Heart of Thornfell, with what might have been gentleness, "is rather the point."

* * *

She tried to hold out. She was proud of that afterward, in the dim, incomplete way she would be proud of many things about that night — she made them give her water, and she drank it slowly, and she sat with her back against the warm white stone and counted her reasons for refusing.

The valley had made her. Every hardship and every hiding hour of it. Her mother's hands kneading bread. Her father whistling off-key at the goats. The smell of the forge smoke in autumn, the specific crookedness of the fence post she'd leaned against every evening of her childhood while the sunset did whatever sunsets did behind the western ridge. If she gave up a piece, the rest would never sit right again. She knew this the way you know a torn page leaves a book forever unwhole.

But the map would die with her out here. The neighbor would have died for nothing. The Heartroot would gutter out in its hidden grove, and the world would stay the gray, magic-starved place the Crown had spent three generations making it, and Dominic Winterbourne's perfectly complete memory of home would be buried under the ferns with the rest of her.

"Will it hurt?" she asked at last.

"Yes," said the fox, speaking now for the court, its grey eyes reflecting nothing. "Afterward more than during. During feels like falling asleep in summer grass. Afterward is when you'll notice the shape of the hole."

"And I won't know which memory it was?"

"You'll know the shape of the hole," the fox repeated. "You'll walk around it for the rest of your life the way you walk around furniture in the dark."

Dominic closed her eyes. She thought of her mother's kitchen. She thought, deliberately, cataloguing, hoarding — the bread, the whistle, the crooked post, the forge smoke, Aldric's face by firelight saying *I have done terrible things* — pressing each one like flowers between the pages of her mind, as if naming them could protect them.

Then she stood, swaying, blood seeping warm between her fingers, and faced the ancient oak and its pool-dark eyes.

"I consent."

The forest exhaled.

She felt it happen the way she'd been warned — not as pain but as sleep. Warmth rising from the ground through the soles of her boots, up her spine, sweet and heavy, the sensation of lying down in June grass with the sun on her face and no reason in the world to rise. The clearing blurred at its edges. The rings of beasts became smears of color, gold and brown and white. She heard the Heart say something — words shaped like *it is paid* and *walk north* and *the wound will close by morning, the wood mends what it takes from* — and then everything tipped gently sideways into green dark, and Dominic Winterbourne slept in the heart of Thornfell like a child in a cradle.

* * *

She woke on moss, in morning light that actually came from above, actual sun, actual sky showing in honest gaps between ordinary leaves. Her side was a dull ache wrapped in clean linen she didn't remember tearing. Someone had bound the wound — someone with field-surgeon's hands and a knight's economy of motion — and when she turned her head she found Ser Aldric sitting against a tree three feet away, watching her with an expression she couldn't read, his shoulder freshly dressed, his sword across his knees.

"You found me," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears. Thinner. As though she'd been talking less than she remembered.

"The wolves carried word." He hesitated. "Well. You carried word. Through the wolves. Half-asleep, apparently, from inside the wood itself. I've decided not to think too hard about it." He studied her face. "How do you feel?"

"Fine. Rested. Better than fine, actually, the wound's nearly—"

She stopped.

Something was missing. Not a thing she could name — a thing she could feel the *absence* of, the way you feel the missing stair in the dark, the way the tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. She ran quickly through herself. Mother. Bread. Father. Whistle. Goats. Fence post, crooked, west side of the yard. Forge smoke. The boundary stones where her gift first stirred. The courier dying with the map in his fists, whispering *the Heartroot wakes*. All there. All intact.

And beneath the inventory, a hole. Smooth-edged. Warm. Shaped like home.

"Dominic?" Aldric had gone still, the way he went still when he sensed danger. "What did they take from you?"

"I don't know." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, frayed at one end. "That's the whole cruelty of it. I don't know. There's just—" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, against the smooth-edged nothing. "There's a place where something used to be, and I can feel the shape of it, and the shape is *home*, Aldric. Something about home. Something about the valley. And it's gone, and I'll spend the rest of my life walking around it in the dark."

He crossed to her then, slowly, and sat beside her on the moss, and did the thing he was still learning to do and doing better each week: he said nothing. He simply stayed. After a while her hand found his, and neither of them remarked on it.

When she finally spoke again, the sun had climbed and the northern edge of Thornfell showed as a thinning of trees ahead, honest daylight beyond it, the mountain road they needed.

"There's one more thing," Aldric said quietly. His jaw worked, as it always did before confessions. "About what waits north of here. About why the commander wants the Heartroot so badly. There's something I've never told you — something I should have told you before the ford, before the city, maybe before the crossroads. And after what you paid in there, you deserve to choose whether you travel another mile with me knowing it."

Dominic turned to look at him. His face in the green-gold light was drawn and older than his years, and his grey eyes held something she had only seen glimpses of — the pyre-light she'd caught once, twice, and never asked about.

"Then tell me," she said.
