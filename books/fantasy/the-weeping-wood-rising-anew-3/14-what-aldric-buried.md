## 14. What Aldric Buried

The cave mouth faced east, and the fire was small enough that it might pass for a camp of ordinary travelers — charcoal-burners, perhaps, or shepherds caught out by the early dark. Aldric had chosen the hollow with a soldier's eye: one approach, stone at their backs, a stream's whisper close enough to mask voices. He had not chosen it with a lover's eye. He did not yet know if he had any right to that.

Dominic sat across the fire from him, her knees drawn up, her hands wrapped around a tin cup of broth she was not drinking. The light moved on her face in slow tides. She had been quiet since Thornfell had let them go — quieter than grief, which at least had a sound to it. This was the silence of a woman turning something over and over in her mind, waiting for him to say it first.

He had promised her the truth at the ford, when he'd put his body between her and the bounty hunters' crossbows and felt his old life fall away like ash from a burnt log. *You deserve to know what you're trusting.* He had been saying it for three days now, in his head, in fragments, rehearsing it like a squire rehearsing an oath. And every rehearsal had failed, because there were no words in any knightly litany for what he had done.

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, water dripped from stone to stone, counting out the hours.

"Dominic." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "There's something I have to tell you. And when I'm done, you may want to walk out of this cave and never look back. I'll not stop you. I'll point you toward the safest road myself."

She set down the cup. Her eyes — gray-green, the color of the valley he was beginning to understand she'd been stolen from before she ever left it — did not waver. "Tell me."

He looked into the fire instead of at her. It was easier to confess to flame. Flame, at least, already knew.

* * *

"You asked me once," he began, "why a man who hates magic as thoroughly as I claim to would spend eleven years hunting it. You asked why I never took the pardon they offered after my tenth year. Most hunters burn out. They drink, or they desert, or they start believing their own sermons. I kept going. You wanted to know what kept me going."

"I remember."

"It was a girl named Wren."

The name came out of him like a splinter that had lodged too deep to draw clean. In the firelight he could see her again without trying: seventeen, maybe eighteen, barefoot in the trampled snow of a village square in the hill country north of Marrowmere, her wrists bound with bell-cord because the Church held that iron corrupted the gift but bells silenced it. Small bones. A face that even terror could not make plain. And around her neck, on a leather cord the soldiers had missed or hadn't thought worth taking, a little carved fox on a thong — the kind of charm a child whittles, the kind of thing beast-speakers' families gave their daughters so that even far from home, some animal might know them.

"She was the third speaker I ever helped take," Aldric said. "I say *helped take*, because that's what we told ourselves we were doing. Escorting. The Crown's writ said the gifted were to be brought to the trials, examined, and only the dangerous ones condemned. I was twenty-three years old and I believed the writ the way you believe the ground under your feet. My commander — Veyra, the woman who hunts us now — she was gentle about it, then. She used to say we were surgeons cutting away rot. That most speakers were harmless and would be released with a mark and a pension." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "Two hundred trials. Do you know how many were released with a mark and a pension?"

"How many?" Dominic's voice was very soft.

"None."

The word fell into the cave and lay there.

"Wren was different, though. That's the part I've never told anyone, the part that's mine alone to carry. The others — I stood at the edge of those pyres and told myself the writ was just, that the judges knew things I didn't. But Wren spoke to me. Not through a beast — she'd been bound too long, her animals scattered, and besides, the square was full of soldiers and dogs that would've gone mad. She spoke to me with her own mouth, while they were stacking the faggots around her legs. She looked at me — not at Veyra, not at the priest, at me, because I was the one holding the torch — and she said, *You have a dog. Back at the fort. He's old and he misses you, and he dreams about the river.*"

Aldric's hands had curled into fists against his knees. He made himself open them.

"I had a dog. Bramble. He was twelve that winter and I'd been away eight months. No one in that village had seen me before; we'd ridden in that morning. There was no way she could know. Except that she did, the way you know weather coming. And I looked at her, and I knew — not suspected, *knew*, the way you know a wound is deep — that everything the writ said about these people was a lie we told so the burning would sit easy in our stomachs."

He heard Dominic's breath catch. He didn't look up. If he looked up, he would stop.

"And I lit the pyre anyway."

The drips counted. The fire breathed.

"Why?" Dominic asked. It wasn't an accusation. That was somehow worse. It was the question of someone who genuinely needed to know whether there was an answer.

"Because Veyra was watching. Because forty men were watching. Because if I refused, she'd have handed the torch to the man beside me, and Wren would have burned all the same, and I'd have thrown away the only position I had from which to help anyone else. That's what I told myself." He finally raised his eyes to hers. "It's what I still tell myself, on good nights. On bad nights I know it's simpler. I was afraid. I chose my fear over her life, and I dressed the choice in strategy afterward because I couldn't live with it naked. And then I spent eleven years hunting her kin, telling myself that every speaker I steered away from the trials, every map I mislaid, every report I softened — that it balanced the scale. It doesn't balance anything. Scales don't work that way. You can't weigh a life against a life against a life and call the arithmetic mercy."

Dominic had gone very still. In the firelight her face was unreadable, and he understood suddenly that this was the moment he had been dreading since the ford — not Veyra's blades, not the pass, not the army gathering below the mountain. This. Her silence.

"There's more," he said quietly. "The part that concerns you."

* * *

"After Wren burned, I went back to the fort and I got drunk for two days, and on the third day I went to the archive and pulled the trial records. All of them. Every speaker taken in the last fifty years. I told myself I was looking for patterns — where they came from, how they were found. And there was a pattern, but it wasn't what the Church claimed. It wasn't random villages, random sinners. The speakers clustered. Along the river valleys. Around certain old forests. And densest of all—" he drew a breath "—around a valley in the western hills that appeared in exactly none of our maps. A blank space. When I asked about it, I was told it was poor hunting ground. Barren. Magic can't survive there, they said, so why waste riders."

He watched her understand it arrive in her like cold water.

"The Weeping Wood," she whispered.

"The Weeping Wood. I requested assignment to the western circuit six times over four years. Denied every time. Veyra denied the last one personally, and she looked at me — that long, measuring look she does — and said, *Curiosity is a disease in our profession, Ser Aldric. Burn it out before it spreads.* So I stopped asking. But I kept the records. I copied them. Names, villages, family lines. And when I began steering speakers away from the trials — the ones I could save — I sent them toward that blank space on the map. Toward your valley. Because somewhere in my gut I knew the blankness wasn't emptiness. It was concealment. Someone had built a hiding place for these people, and then someone had buried the knowledge of it so deep that even the hunters had forgotten why."

"That's why you found us," Dominic said slowly. "At the treeline. That wasn't chance."

"No. I'd traced the courier's route for three days, and every road he might have taken funneled past your valley. I thought he was carrying the Heartroot map *to* someone hidden there." He paused. "I was right. Just not the way I thought. I thought I'd find a cell of rebels. Instead I found—" He stopped himself. Found what? A woman whose goats gossiped about her. A woman who fed wolves by hand and argued with hawks. A woman who had taken a dying stranger's burden onto herself with no promise of reward, because that was simply what her hands knew how to do. "Instead I found the last of Wren's line, as near as makes no difference. Her people. The people I swore, kneeling in the snow with her smell still in my nose, that I would spend my life protecting in whatever crooked way I could."

"And instead," Dominic said, and her voice had an edge now, thin and bright as a blade drawn slowly, "you hunted me. You tracked me. You cornered me at the crossroads with a sword drawn."

"Yes."

"You put your hand on my shoulder to bind me."

"Yes."

"And then a mule talked to you through my mouth, and you decided I was useful alive."

"Dominic—"

"Don't." She unfolded from the fire and stood, and the shadow she cast climbed the cave wall behind her like a second, darker Dominic. "Don't soften it. You want to give me your confession whole? Then take mine in trade: I have spent every day since the ford telling myself that the man who defended me from those bounty hunters was real, and the man who hunted me was the mask — that the mask slipped off and the true face showed. But you're telling me it's the reverse. The hunter is the true face, and the defender is the new thing, the untested thing, three weeks old. Why should I bet my life on three weeks?"

"Because I am betting mine on it." He rose too, slowly, keeping his hands loose and visible, a soldier's habit that he hated in this moment. "Because everything I was, I built to survive the Crown, and everything I am now, I built to survive myself, and only one of those is worth anything. You want proof? Here is the last piece. The night we reached Thornfell, while you slept, I took Wren's fox charm out of my saddlebag where I've carried it for eleven years like a stone in a wound, and I buried it. Dug into the frozen ground at the forest's edge and put it under a stone, because I finally understood what I was really doing all these years. I wasn't atoning. Atonement serves the dead. I was hiding. As long as I carried her, I could tell myself I was still serving her somehow. Burying her was the first honest thing I've done since I was twenty-three years old." His voice cracked on the last words and he let it. "I'm not asking you to forgive me tonight. Forgiveness isn't a feeling you wait for. I learned that much from watching the priests. It's a choice, made in the dark, over and over, without proof it's deserved. I'm only asking you to make it with your eyes open — or to refuse it with your eyes open. Either one is honest. Either one I'll carry."

* * *

For a long time she said nothing, and the cave filled with the small sounds of the mountain settling, and Aldric discovered that a confession fully given leaves a strange emptiness behind, like a field after harvest. There was nothing left to hide behind. Whatever grew next would grow in the open.

Finally Dominic crouched and fed a stick to the fire. Watched it catch.

"When I was nine," she said, "there was a dog in the valley — a shepherd's brute, half-wild, that bit anyone who came near the flock. Everyone said it should be destroyed. I could hear it, even at nine, even with my gift half-asleep in that dead air. It wasn't vicious. It was afraid, and in pain — an old injury in its hip that festered every winter. It bit because biting was the only language anyone had ever spoken to it." She looked up at him, and the firelight swam in her eyes. "I sat with it for a whole summer. I didn't tame it. Taming wasn't the word. We just... agreed, slowly, to stop being afraid of each other. It lived fourteen years. It slept on my feet every winter until it died."

"Dominic—"

"I'm not finished." Her voice shook, but she held it. "Here is what I know about fear that bites. It doesn't get forgiven in a night, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. What I feel right now is fury at you, and grief for that girl in the snow, and something else I don't have a name for yet — because I have watched you for three weeks, Aldric Eriksen, and I watched you stand in front of crossbow bolts for a woman you'd met days before, and I watched you bury your god today, apparently, in the dirt at the edge of a cursed wood. Those are not the acts of a hunter wearing a mask. They might be the acts of a penitent. I haven't decided. I may not decide tomorrow either."

He nodded. His throat was too tight for speech.

"But here is what I will give you tonight, because you gave me the truth whole and unsweetened, and I know what it cost — I could see it cost, your hands haven't stopped shaking since you started." She crossed the fire to him, and stood close enough that he could see the pulse beating at her throat. "I choose to stay. Not because the feeling is settled — it isn't, it may not be for a long time. I choose it the way you said. In the dark. Over and over. Starting now." Her jaw tightened. "And starting now, you tell me everything. Every record you copied, every name, every line of Wren's kin you tried to steer westward. If some of them survived in my valley — if some of them are the neighbors the hunters dragged out last week — then I need to know which ones, and you need to be the one who helps me find out what happened to them. That's not forgiveness. That's terms."

"That's fair," he managed. "That's more than fair."

"And Aldric." She reached out and took his shaking hand in both of hers, steadying it, the way you'd steady a frightened animal. "Wren's fox. Where you buried it. After this is over — if we live — take me there. I want to stand over that stone with you. Not to dig it up. To tell her, if anything of her is still listening anywhere in this waking world, that her people are rising, and that the man who lit her pyre carried her name to the end of it."

Something broke open in his chest then — not pain, or not only pain — and Aldric bowed his head over her hands like a man at an altar, and let himself weep for the first time in eleven years, silently, in the firelight, while the mountain dripped its patient count above them.

They were still sitting that way, side by side, when the wolves began to sing.

Not the distant, wandering song of wild packs. This was close, and coordinated, and rising from the slopes below the cave in layered voices — and Aldric, who had spent a lifetime learning the grammar of beasts secondhand through reports and dread, understood it instantly for what it was. Not song. Warning.

He was on his feet with his sword half-drawn before the first note faded. Far down the mountainside, where the pass opened onto the moonlit scree, torches were kindling — dozens of them, then scores, a river of fire winding up toward the high country with the terrible unhurried discipline of an army that knows its quarry has nowhere left to climb.

White cloaks. Veyra's vanguard.

"They found the pass," Aldric breathed. "Three days early. Gods — she must have pushed them night-marches." He turned to Dominic, and the words came out stripped of everything but fact. "By dawn, this mountain is hers."
