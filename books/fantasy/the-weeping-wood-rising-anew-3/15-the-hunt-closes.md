## 15. The Hunt Closes

### Aldric

The pass announced itself long before they reached it — a gray scar between black shoulders of stone, where the wind came down off the heights carrying the smell of iron and old snow. Aldric had known the moment he saw it that they would not cross unchallenged. There was only one road through the Spine of Thornfell, and Vayne Corvasser was many things, but never a fool.

He counted the banners from behind a shelf of rock, Dominic crouched at his shoulder, her breath steaming in the cold. Twelve riders at the mouth of the pass. More on the ridgelines above — he could see the glint of helms among the scree, and the pale smudge of smoke from cook-fires tucked into hollows where they would not be seen until it was too late.

"Thirty," he said quietly. "Perhaps forty."

"And us?" Dominic asked.

He looked at her. Her face was thinner than when they'd met, carved down by the road to its essentials: dark eyes, a stubborn jaw, a small scar across her brow from the ford that she touched sometimes without knowing she did it. She held the map's case against her chest like a second heartbeat.

"Two," he said. "And whatever you've got left of Thornfell."

Her hand went to her throat, where the memory-price still showed as a faint silver mark beneath the skin. The beast-court of Thornfell had taken a piece of her home for their bargain — the smell of bread in her mother's kitchen, gone forever; the sound of goats bleating at dawn, traded away. In exchange they had promised passage. *When you call,* the stag-queen had said, *we will come.*

"But will they come *here*?" Dominic murmured. "This is the edge of their ground. The court's law doesn't cross stone like this."

"They honored their word once already." He kept his eyes on the banners. "They'll honor it again."

"You don't know that."

"No," Aldric admitted. "I don't."

Below them, a horn sounded — one long note rolling up the valley walls and breaking against the rocks like surf. A rider detached from the formation and walked his horse forward into the open ground, and even at this distance Aldric knew him by the white cloak and the way he sat a saddle as though it were a throne.

Vayne Corvasser. Commander of the Crown's Order of the Ashen Vigil. The man who had given Aldric his first sword and his first pyre, and who had spent fifteen years teaching him that mercy was a debt magic always defaulted on.

"He'll want to talk first," Aldric said. "He always talks first. It's how he tells himself he's better than the thing he is."

"What does he say to traitors?"

Aldric smiled without any humor in it. "He offers pardons. That's the worst part, Dom. He means them."

* * *

### Dominic

She stood on the open ground because Aldric asked her to, and because hiding had stopped working for her somewhere back in the drowned chapel, when she'd first read the Heartroot's dying plea and understood there was no version of her life left that fit inside a shadow.

Vayne Corvasser reined in ten paces away. Up close he was not what she'd imagined — not cruel-looking, not hard. He had a scholar's face, mild and lined, with tired gray eyes that took her measure the way a man measures timber before deciding what to build with it.

"Dominic Winterbourne," he said. Her name in his mouth sounded like an entry in a ledger. "Warden-line. Last of it. Do you know what your grandmother died protecting?"

"The same thing I'm going to protect," she said. "Whether you burn me for it or not."

"Nothing so dramatic." He dismounted, unhurried, and that frightened her more than drawn steel would have. Men who rushed were men who feared. This man feared nothing here, and why should he? "I've read the archives, girl. I know what the Heartroot is. A wound in the world that leaks power into anything that kneels beside it. Beasts that speak. Girls who hear them. Kings who want armies that can't be killed." His voice stayed gentle, almost sorrowful. "The Crown doesn't mean to destroy it anymore. We mean to *contain* it. Bottle the leak. End the speaking, end the witchcraft, end every horror your gift has ever visited on some poor village that didn't ask for it."

"And the people who can hear beasts?" Dominic asked. "The children born with it, year after year? What happens to them under your containment?"

Corvasser's face did something very small and very honest — a flicker, quickly buried, of the thing he refused to look at.

"They are regrettable," he said. "As are all casualties of necessary wars."

Behind him, the riders waited. On the ridges, crossbows lay leveled along the stones, and Aldric stood apart from her with his hand nowhere near his sword because both of them knew the arithmetic of thirty crossbows against one knight.

"I'm offering you a clean death if you refuse," Corvasser said, "and a pardon if you yield. Those are the only mercies I carry. Choose."

Dominic closed her eyes. And in the dark behind her lids, she did the thing she had done every dawn of her life in the valley, and every night since leaving it: she listened.

Not with her ears. With the older listening, the one that ran through soil and root and blood. She felt the mountain around her — the marmots asleep in their burrows, the eagles riding thermals above the pass, the thin cold patience of stone itself. And beneath all of it, far below and coming closer, something vast moving through Thornfell's deep places. Many somethings. Antlered and clawed and winged, flowing downhill through the forest like water finding its level.

They had come. They had actually come.

Her eyes opened. "I choose," she said clearly, so the riders could hear, so the ridge archers could hear, "the third option."

"There is no third option."

"You're right," said Dominic Winterbourne, last of the warden-line, and felt the silver mark at her throat burn like a brand. "There never has been. Only the one nobody offers."

She put two fingers to her lips and whistled — three notes, high and wild, the shape of the bargain she had paid for with a piece of her childhood.

The mountainside answered.

* * *

### Aldric

What came out of the treeline was not a charge. It was a tide.

Stags first, huge ones, gray as slate with crowns like winter branches, pouring down the scree at angles no horse could hold. Behind them wolves — dozens, then more than dozens, a gray river of them — and above, a screaming darkness of ravens and hawks and one enormous eagle owl whose wingspan blotted out the sun as it stooped toward the ridge archers.

"Loose!" someone shouted on the heights, and the crossbows spoke, but the bolts found fur and feather and air and little else, because the tide had already broken over the pass.

Aldric moved without deciding to move. He caught Dominic around the waist and pulled her behind him as a stag swept past close enough to touch, its antlers catching a rider's shield and hurling man and horse both into the rocks. The world became noise and motion — screaming horses, the wet percussion of bodies striking stone, ravens diving into the faces of men who had been taught all their lives that beasts were dumb meat waiting for the knife.

And the beasts were not fighting like animals. They were fighting like an army with a general.

He saw wolves take the high ground in relays, driving archers off the ridgeline in disciplined waves while others harried the cavalry below. He saw the eagle owl strike a sergeant's helm and rise again unhurt, a shadow with talons. And at the center of it all, untouched, calm as a queen reviewing troops, a white hind stood on a shelf of rock with her eyes fixed on Dominic — the stag-queen of Thornfell, keeping her side of a bargain made in a cursed wood.

"*Aldric!*" Dominic's voice cut through the din. "The commander — he's falling back to the narrows —"

Corvasser had not fled. That was the terrible thing. Aldric watched his old commander rally a wedge of surviving riders around the white banner and drive *forward*, into the chaos, straight at the rock shelf where Dominic stood — because Corvasser understood, as Aldric understood, that the whole impossible battle turned on one girl, and battles could be ended the way they began: by killing the right person.

"Dom, *move*—"

But the hind was faster than either of them. She came down the rocks like snowfall, silent and total, and put herself directly in Corvasser's path. His horse reared, screaming. His sword flashed. And Dominic — gods help him — stepped *between* them.

"No!" Aldric's shout tore his throat.

The blade never fell. A wolf hit Corvasser's arm from the blind side, not killing — *maiming*, taking the sword-arm at the wrist in a single red arc, and the Commander of the Ashen Vigil dropped his steel in the trampled snow and stared at the stump of his career with an expression not of pain but of pure, astonished betrayal, as though the world itself had broken faith with him.

Then the tide carried everything sideways. Riders broke and scattered into the trees. The banner went down. Somewhere a horn blew retreat, three ragged notes, and the great gray river of beasts parted around the fleeing men like water around stones — letting them go, Dominic's bargain holding even now, *passage granted, passage denied, but slaughter owed to no one* — until the pass held nothing but snow, blood, feathers settling, and the breathing of the living.

* * *

### Dominic

Afterward she could not stop shaking, and it was not fear.

She knelt in the churned snow before the white hind, and the stag-queen lowered her great head until their foreheads nearly touched, breath warm as summer meadows against Dominic's frozen face. Around them the wolves sat in a wide circle, patient, tongues lolling, and the ravens walked among the dead with terrible gentleness, closing the eyes of fallen men as though performing last rites no priest would ever give.

*You paid,* the hind said. Her voice was not sound so much as weather, moving through Dominic's mind. *We keep what is promised. Always.*

"Thank you," Dominic whispered. "Some of those men would have — they're just following orders, most of them, they didn't choose —"

*Neither does the flood choose,* said the stag-queen, *and yet it knows which fields to spare.* She pulled back, and her dark eyes held something that in a human face Dominic would have called sadness. *The wood judges, little warden. It has begun already. You will see. But that judgment is not yours to carry today.*

The hind turned, and the herd turned with her, and the wolves rose and poured back toward the treeline like smoke returning to a fire — and then, at the forest's edge, they paused. Every one of them. Dozens of yellow eyes swinging back to look at her one last time, not as a mistress looks at a servant, nor a weapon at its wielder, but the way family looks back from a doorway.

Then the trees took them, and the pass was quiet.

Aldric limped to her side and sank down into the snow beside her, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. His armor was scored and bloody. One eye was swelling shut. He looked, she thought, like a man who had been taken apart and put back together in slightly the wrong order, and he was smiling.

"We lived," he said.

"We lived." She started laughing then, or crying, or both — some ragged animal sound that had been building since Thornfell, since the chapel, maybe since the day a dying man bled out at her feet — and Aldric pulled her against his shoulder and let her shake until it passed.

When she finally lifted her head, she looked east, past the pass, toward the high country and the hidden grove where the Heartroot lay dying under centuries of silence.

"They'll regroup," Aldric said, following her gaze. His voice had gone quiet and hard. "Corvasser won't report a defeat. He can't. He'll send for everything the Order has left — and worse. If we're going to reach the grove, it has to be now, ahead of him, through country that gets crueler with every mile."

"Then we walk," Dominic said.

She got up. Her legs held. Far below, the wounded commander was being carried from the field, and somewhere behind them the sun was going down over Thornfell in bands of green and gold, and the whole darkening world smelled — for the first time in her life — like a place that had chosen her back.

Two days' climb to the grove. Two days ahead of the storm.

She shouldered her pack, took Aldric's hand, and started up into the pass.
