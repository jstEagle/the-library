## 16. Ascent to the Heartroot

The mountain did not want them.

Dominic had understood that from the first hour of the climb, when the path — if it could be called a path — narrowed to a shelf of wet stone barely wide enough for her boots, with the world falling away on her left into a gray-green nothing where the clouds lived. The wind came up the face of the mountain in long, slow breaths, like something enormous sleeping below and exhaling in its dreams, and each breath carried a smell she had never encountered anywhere in her life: cold stone and old rain and, beneath it, faint and unmistakable, the green scent of growing things that had no business growing this high.

The map had not lied. But it had not told the whole truth either, and she was beginning to understand that it never could have. Ink on calfskin could show a line of ascent, a notch between two peaks shaped like a broken tooth, a spiral marked in faded gold at the summit's shoulder. It could not show her the way the mountain watched them climb.

"It knows we're coming," she said, for perhaps the tenth time that day.

Aldric, a half-step above her with his hand flat against the rock, did not answer at once. He had stopped talking much in the last two days — not the silence of anger, which she knew well now, but the silence of a man rehearsing something in his head and not liking any version of it. He looked down at her, wind whipping his graying hair across the scar at his temple, and said only, "Everything up here knows we're coming. The question is whether it minds."

Behind them, the beasts of Thornfell had fallen back at the tree line, as the beast-court's bargain required. Passage, not company. But she had felt them at her back for two days like a warmth, like hands between her shoulder blades, and now that they were gone she felt the absence the way one feels a missing tooth — the tongue returning again and again to the gap. The last to leave had been the old wolf with the clouded eye, and before turning down the slope he had pressed his broad head once against her knee.

*Warden,* he had said. Not her name. Not *Dominic*. Just the word, heavy as a stone dropped in still water, and then he was gone.

She had not told Aldric. She did not know how to tell him that the beasts had stopped calling her by her name somewhere below the snow line, as though the mountain had already decided what she was, and names were a thing for people who might still turn back.

* * *

They climbed through the night, because neither of them could bear to stop. The moon rose fat and white over the eastern peaks and turned the snowfields into a second sky, and they walked across the stars. Aldric led when the way was steep; she led when the way was strange. Twice the path simply ended — a wall of rock, a crevasse breathing cold — and twice the map's gold spiral seemed to shift when she looked at it sideways, the ink rearranging itself with a soft rustle like a fox turning in its sleep, and a new line appeared.

"It's alive," Aldric had said the first time, staring at the calfskin as though it might bite him. "The map is *alive*."

"It was never a map," Dominic said. She heard the words come out of her and did not know where they came from, only that they were true the way the goats' warnings had always been true, arriving whole and certain from somewhere below thought. "It's a hand. Reaching down the mountain. Waiting for someone to take it."

He had looked at her for a long moment in the blue moonlight. Then he had folded the map away with a gentleness she had never seen him use with any object, any weapon, any living thing — and climbed on.

Now the sky was turning the color of a bruise healing, gray shading into rose, and the broken-tooth notch was above them at last, and beyond it —

Dominic stopped so suddenly that Aldric, behind her, nearly walked into her back.

Beyond the notch there was no summit. There was a bowl, a vast shallow cup of stone cradled between the peaks, and the cup was full of green.

Not the green of the valley she had been born in — the Weeping Wood's careful, cowed green, willows and alders hunched against the wind as though forever braced for a blow. This green was old. This green was *confident*. Trees she had no names for rose from the bowl in tiers, their crowns silvered with frost yet heavy with leaves, and between them ran water — she could hear it, a hundred small voices, streams threading down through the grove and vanishing into the earth as if the mountain drank them. And at the center, rising above every crown, stood a single tree so large that her mind refused it for three full breaths before it agreed to believe.

It was not the largest tree she had ever imagined. It was the largest *living thing* she had ever imagined, and it was dying.

She saw it the way one sees a sickness in a face one loves — not in any single feature but in the whole. The tree was vast, silver-barked, its branches spread wide enough to shade a village. But its leaves were graying from the tips, whole boughs standing bare in the heart of summer's height, and where the bark had split she could see the wood beneath was not white or brown but a deep, dull red, dark as old blood, dark as a wound kept too long from the air. The streams that fed it ran thin. The frost on its branches did not melt even where the rising sun touched it.

"Oh," Dominic said. It was not a word. It was the sound a person makes when a thing they have carried for months finally shows its true weight.

Aldric came up beside her. She heard his breath catch — a small, involuntary sound, a knight's sound, the sound of a man who had burned things and was now looking at the most beautiful thing he had ever seen and understanding what burning meant.

"That's it," he said quietly. "The Heartroot."

"It's starving." Her voice came out flat with shock. "Aldric, it's *starving*. Look at it. It's been starving for — " She tried to count the years and the number would not come; it was not a number of years, it was a number of *generations*. Of centuries. Of speakers born and hidden and burned and buried, each one a cup of water withheld from a dying tree.

The valley. The Weeping Wood. The place where magic cannot follow, where her gift lay dormant, where she had spent her whole life half-buried and calling it safety.

"It wasn't sanctuary," she whispered. "It was a *severance*. Someone cut the Heartroot off from its wardens and called the wound a refuge."

* * *

The way down into the bowl was easier than the way up, as though the grove had decided, finally, to let them in. The trees closed behind them with a sound like a held breath released. The air grew warm — impossibly, gently warm, the warmth of a sickroom kept close against the cold — and the smell of green deepened until she could taste it, sweet and bitter at once, like medicine made from a garden.

The beasts' voices, which had been a low murmur at the edge of her hearing since the tree line — the mountain goats grumbling, the hawks trading gossip on the thermals, all of it distant and thin — fell silent here. Completely silent. Not because the creatures were absent. She could see them: a fox sitting motionless beneath a fern bank, a white hare with its ears up, a pair of ravens on a low bough, all of them watching her with the fixed, waiting attention of a congregation.

They were silent because here, at the root of all their voices, there was nothing left to say. Or rather — there was only one voice, and it was using everything it had to keep breathing.

Dominic walked toward the great tree as though wading upriver. The ground softened under her feet. Moss, deep and springing, and then something finer, a grass silvered like the bark, and then the roots themselves — vast buttressed walls of root rising higher than her head, forming a grove within the grove, corridors and chambers in living wood. And in the heart of it, in a hollow where the roots parted like hands opening, she saw the Heartroot itself.

Not the tree. The *root*. The tree above was only the crown of it, the visible fraction. Here, in the earth's own chamber, the true root descended — a column of wood wider than a well, banded red and silver, and it pulsed.

Slowly. Faintly. The way a candle gutters.

She had expected — she did not know what she had expected. Fire. Radiance. The wild magic of the stories, the thing the Crown wanted to harvest and the witch-hunters wanted to end. She had braced herself all the long climb for a power that would look at her and judge her.

What she found was a patient in its last illness, and the patient knew her name.

*Warden.*

The voice did not come through her ears. It came up through the soles of her boots, through her bones, through the marrow the beasts had always spoken to — but where the beasts' voices had been bright and quick as birdsong, this was deep and slow and frayed, a great bell cracked but still ringing.

*I had given up hope of the line surviving. The last warden's line — I felt it gutter, year by year, in the valley they built to bury it. And then a courier died at your boundary, and a girl with the old blood took up his burden.*

Dominic sank to her knees on the silvered grass. She was not aware of deciding to. Beside her, Aldric had gone still, his hand on his sword out of old habit and then, deliberately, deliberately, letting go of the hilt.

"I'm not a warden," she said. Her voice sounded small and stupid in the vast quiet. "I'm a goatherd. I hide. I've spent my whole life hiding. I don't even know what a warden *does*."

*You have been doing it since the night you took the map,* the Heartroot said. *A warden carries. A warden stands between the gift and those who would end it, or use it, or both — for they are always the same men in the end. You carried when carrying meant the pyre. You climbed when climbing meant leaving the only home you had. Do not tell me what you are not, child. I have watched wardens for a thousand years, and I know the shape of one.*

The pulsing light in the root's column dimmed, steadied, dimmed again. And with each pulse Dominic felt something pour into her — not power, not yet, but *knowledge*, arriving the way the goats' warnings arrived, whole and undeniable. She saw the grove as it had been: the tree in full green glory, its light running down the root and out through the earth in a thousand thousand threads, waking magic in every forest and river and den and burrow across the world. She saw the severance come — not an army, not a battle, but a decision, cold and administrative, made by men who drew a boundary around a valley and called it mercy and it was a noose. She saw the speaker-lines thinned by fire, by flight, by silence. She saw the Heartroot rationing itself, pulse by pulse, century by century, spending its last strength to keep a single grove alive and waiting.

And she saw — with a clarity so terrible it stopped her breath — what the map was.

Not a treasure. Not a weapon. Not even, truly, a map.

*A plea.* A dying thing's last letter, written in gold ink that rearranged itself, sent down the mountain hand over hand through courier after courier, most of whom had died for it, until it reached the one person in the world who could hear it read aloud.

*The Heartroot wakes,* the courier had whispered with his last breath. He had been wrong. Or he had been right in the only way that mattered — because it was waking now, waking to her, spending its final reserves to speak, and a thing must wake to die.

"Dominic." Aldric's voice, low. His hand hovered near her shoulder without touching. "What is it saying?"

She found that her face was wet. She had not noticed the cold up here, or her own tears freezing at the corners of her eyes, or the wind, or anything at all except the slow failing pulse of the light.

"It's asking," she said. "Aldric — it's been asking for a hundred years, and everyone who came before me either couldn't hear it or came to *take* instead of *give*." She looked up at him, and the awe in her own chest frightened her more than the witch-hunters ever had, because awe like this did not leave room for anything else. "The map was never a treasure. It was a plea for a new warden. And the warden it's asking for — "

She stopped. The knowledge sat in her like a stone she had swallowed, cold and smooth and undeniable, and she understood that she had known it since the notch in the mountain, since the wolf had called her *warden*, since perhaps the very first night a dying man pressed wax and calfskin into her hands and changed the shape of her life.

*The gift was never yours,* the Heartroot said, gentle as snowfall. *It was lent. All of it — every beast's voice, every warning, every word — it was my light passing through you. And the light is failing, and I am too weak to carry it down the mountain myself, and so I must ask the vessel to become the spring.*

"What is it asking?" Aldric asked again, and now there was fear in his voice, the specific fear of a man who has stood at enough deathbeds to know when the dying begin to make their requests.

Dominic looked at the great tree, gray-crowned and patient, and at the fox and the hare and the ravens waiting in the green silence, and at the man beside her who had renounced everything he was to walk this far beside her — and she understood, with a terrible and perfect clarity, exactly what was being asked of her, and exactly what it would cost, and that she was going to say yes.

"It's asking for a warden," she said. "The last kind of warden. The kind who doesn't keep the gift."

The pulse of light in the root guttered — and held — and guttered again, like a heart deciding.

And far below them, down the mountain, down through Thornfell and the ford and the long roads behind, a horn sounded. Thin and cold and unmistakable.

The commander had reached the tree line.
