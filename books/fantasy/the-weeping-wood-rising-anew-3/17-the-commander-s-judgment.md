## 17. The Commander's Judgment

The horn sounded again, closer now, and Aldric felt it in his teeth.

He had heard that horn a thousand times across twenty years of service. It was the voice of the Order of the White Thorn, the call that meant *assembled and advancing*, that meant the King's will made flesh in steel and torchlight. He had blown it himself at sieges and burnings. He had taught young knights to answer it without hesitation, because hesitation, he'd told them, was where mercy crept in, and mercy toward magic was how villages caught fire with their children still inside them.

He had believed every word of it. That was the worst part. Not the cruelty — cruelty could be seen and named and hated. But belief. Belief had let him sleep after the pyres. Belief had been his blanket and his absolution both.

Now he stood on the shoulder of the mountain with the Heartroot dying above him and the horn calling him home to a thing he could no longer be.

"Aldric." Dominic's hand found his arm. Her fingers were cold; the climb had cost her more than she would admit. She had gone gray around the mouth since reading the map's final script, gray the way stone goes before it crumbles. "There must be another way down. A path they won't—"

"There isn't." He turned from the tree line and looked up the slope, through the black pines, to where the grove waited hidden behind its veil of old enchantment. The air above the Heartroot shimmered faintly even from here, a heat-haze wrongness that no army would find easy to cross — but enchantments starve like everything else, and the root was starving. "They'll find the way. Vess has the Crown's half of the map. He's had it for years. He only needed ours."

*Ours.* The word sat strangely in his chest. Three days ago it had been *hers* alone, and his duty had been to take it from her. Now the possessive came as naturally as breath.

"How long?" she asked.

"He marched from the ford two days ago if he pushed hard." Aldric calculated it the way he once calculated supply lines and retreat routes, that cold officer's arithmetic rising unbidden. "Four hundred men. Two score knights. The arbalests. Maybe the fire-carts, if he means what I think he means."

"Fire?" Dominic's voice went thin. "Against a living tree?"

"Against the last living tree of its kind." He met her eyes. In the grey light filtering through the canopy she looked carved from something older than her years — the warden's daughter who had never known she was a warden, standing on the bones of her family's long vigil. "Vess doesn't want to destroy it, Dom. You heard what the wolf said. He wants to cut it, bleed it, bottle what comes out. And if the bleeding kills it—" He shrugged, and hated how easily the gesture came, the soldier's shrug that meant *collateral*. "Then the Crown holds whatever it harvested, and the world holds ash."

The wind moved through the pines above them with a sound like distant voices. Aldric had learned, these last weeks, to tell when the wood was listening. It listened now.

"You should go," he said quietly. "Not away. Up. Into the grove, behind the veil. Whatever you mean to do there, do it, and don't come out until it's finished. Let me deal with what's coming."

She stared at him. "Alone? Four hundred men?"

"I'm not going to fight four hundred men." Something that might have been a smile touched his mouth and died quickly, because it wasn't truly a smile; it was grief wearing one's face. "I'm going to stand in front of them and say words I've been avoiding saying my whole life. And then we'll see which of us they follow."

"They follow you," she said simply. "Half those knights trained under you. They called you—"

"Don't." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "They called me the Knight of Ashes. Yes. I know what they called me. I lit that name myself."

Below them, faint but growing, came the sound he knew too well: the creak of harness, the jingle of bit and stirrup, the low murmur of an army moving through trees that did not want it. Branches snagged at banners. Roots seemed to rise under hooves more often than chance allowed. The wood was fighting them the only way it still could — slowly, sullenly, a dying body resisting the hands that dragged it.

It wouldn't be enough.

Dominic gripped his arm harder. "Aldric. Whatever you're planning—"

"Dominic." He said her name carefully, the way one sets down something breakable. "I have spent twenty years being exactly what they made me. Today is the first day I get to choose what I am instead. Don't take it from me by arguing me out of it."

For a long moment she looked at him, and he saw her weighing it, saw the warden in her understanding what he was offering in a way the woman might not. Then she rose on her toes and pressed her forehead to his, briefly, fiercely — a touch like a seal pressed into wax — and stepped back.

"If you die," she said, "I will find some beast who can speak, and I will have it haunt you forever."

"That sounds like a threat you're uniquely qualified to keep."

"Damn right it is." But her voice cracked on the last word, and she turned away quickly and began to climb, quick and light and terrible, toward the grove where the Heartroot guttered like a candle deciding whether to go out.

Aldric watched her go until the veil took her. Then he buckled his sword belt tight, straightened the white-and-black surcoat he had worn so long it had become a second skin, and walked down the mountain to meet his past.

* * *

They emerged from the tree line into the high meadow below the grove's path just as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and Aldric stopped where the ground narrowed between two outcrops of granite — the only approach, the place where one man with a sword could be more than one man, though he had no intention of drawing steel.

Commander Malden Vess rode at the head of the column, and time had not improved him. He had been gaunt when Aldric last saw him in the capital; now he was gaunt the way famine makes men gaunt, hollow-cheeked beneath his polished helm, with the feverish brightness of a man who has stared at a prize so long the prize has begun staring back. Behind him stretched the Order in full array: ranks of pike, the arbalest lines, the mounted knights in their white-and-black, banners snapping in the wind off the peaks. The fire-carts were there too, tar-black and iron-banded, and seeing them settled something in Aldric's chest. Fear, maybe. Or its opposite.

"Ser Aldric." Vess reined in ten paces away, and his voice carried the flat pleasantness of a man performing calm. "You're alive. That will simplify certain reports."

"Will it, Commander?"

"You were declared dead at Thornfell. Deserted, some said. Traitor, others." Vess's gaze flicked past him, up the slope, hunting. "Where is the witch?"

"Gone."

"Gone." Vess smiled without warmth. "Ser Aldric. Twenty years I have known you. You are many things, most of them useful, but you are a poor liar. She's up there. With the root." He swung down from his saddle, unhurried, and gestured, and the column halted with a sound like a wave pausing mid-break. "Which is convenient, actually. Because the King's writ extends to both of you, and I'd rather not spend the day climbing."

"The King's writ," Aldric said, "is about to become irrelevant. To all of us."

Something flickered across Vess's face — not doubt, never doubt, but impatience sharpening into contempt. Around him the front rank shifted, and Aldric recognized faces in it. Ser Brandt, who had wept at his mother's funeral and laughed at witch-burnings. Young Corren, whom Aldric had knighted himself six years ago, whose oath Aldric's own hands had fastened. They were looking at him the way men look at a beloved teacher who has begun saying unforgivable things: with pain, with denial, with the desperate hope that he will stop.

He would not stop.

"Before your arbalests decide this conversation," Aldric said, pitching his voice to carry, the parade-ground voice he had drilled into three generations of knights, "I want every man here to hear what their commander intends. Not the writ. Not the official language. The truth."

"Careful, Aldric."

"The truth," Aldric went on, louder, walking forward along the line of granite so his words rolled down the whole front rank, "is that the Heartroot is not a weapon. It is the wellspring. Every gift the Crown has burned for two hundred years — every beast-speaker, every hedge-healer, every child taken from her bed for talking to crows — all of it came from that tree. And it is dying. Has been dying since the first severance, since your grandfathers' grandfathers built the valley that starves it. Commander Vess does not intend to destroy it. He intends to harvest it. To cut it and bleed it into bottles and sell pieces of the world's soul back to the world. And when the bleeding is done, when the last of it is spent, there will never again be magic anywhere, ever — and the Order will have nothing left to hunt, and so it will turn inward, as such things always do, and begin hunting itself. I have watched it begin already. So have you."

Silence, except the wind. Somewhere in the ranks a horse stamped.

Vess's face had gone very still. "Ser Aldric," he said softly, "you are describing treason while wearing the Order's colors. Take off the surcoat or take up a sword, because you cannot stand there being both."

And that — that was the moment. Aldric felt it arrive like weather. Twenty years of oaths, of pyres, of a girl's screaming face behind his eyelids every night for a decade; twenty years of believing that the law and the right wore the same face, and learning, inch by inch, mile by mile, dead neighbor by dead neighbor, that they did not.

He reached over his shoulder and drew the sword his father had given him, the blade he had carried through every burning, and drove it point-first into the turf between the granite outcrops. Then he pulled the white-and-black surcoat up over his head, and stood in his plain mail in the cold wind, and let the Order's colors fall onto the stones.

"Ser Aldric of the White Thorn," he said, and his voice did not shake, though everything in him was shaking, "renounces his oath. I was sworn to guard the realm against magic. I was wrong to swear it, and worse to keep it, and I will keep it no longer. What I am sworn to now needs no ceremony." He looked down the rank of faces — Corren's stricken young face, Brandt's hardening one, Vess's cold bright one — and made himself finish. "The law says that tree is property. The law says that woman is a witch. The law built the valley that is killing the world. I declare before every man here that there is a justice higher than the Crown's, and it lives in that grove, and I will defend it with everything I am — not as a knight of the White Thorn, but as a man who finally knows better."

No one moved. The silence had a texture to it, dense as wool.

Then Vess sighed, the sigh of a man disappointed in an old hound, and drew his own sword, and the sound of it rang down the column like the first stone of an avalanche.

"Take him alive if you can," Vess said. "Dead if he makes it tedious. And form the assault lines. We climb within the hour."

Aldric bent and picked up his fallen sword from the turf — not the ceremonial surrender, only practicality; he intended to need it — and set his back to the granite, alone against four hundred men who had once been his.

Above him, beyond the veil, the Heartroot pulsed once, weakly, like a heart gathering courage.

And from the deep woods below the meadow — from Thornfell, from the dark places where he had bargained and bled — came an answer: a howl, then ten, then a hundred, rising out of the earth itself, as the beasts of the cursed forest came to honor a debt owed to a woman who spoke their language.

The commander's judgment had arrived. But it seemed the mountain had brought a judgment of its own.
