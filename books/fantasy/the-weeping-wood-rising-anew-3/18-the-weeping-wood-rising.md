## 18. The Weeping Wood Rising

The grove at the top of the world was smaller than Dominic had imagined.

She had carried the Heartroot in her mind as something vast — a cathedral of living wood, a pillar that held up the sky. What she found instead was a single tree, ancient and stooped, standing alone in a bowl of stone no wider than a churchyard. Its bark was grey as old ash. Its branches were bare in the height of summer. And beneath it, where its roots plunged into the mountain's heart, the ground was cracked and dry, fissured like the skin of an abandoned hearth.

It was dying. She had known it would be — the map had told her, the drowned chapel's script had told her, and some older part of her, the warden's blood in her veins, had known all along. But knowing was nothing against seeing.

"Oh," she said softly, and the word came out in every language she owned: in speech, and in the tongue of goats, and wolves, and crows, and the small blind things that tunneled under mountains. "Oh, you poor thing."

*You came,* said the Heartroot.

Its voice was not a voice. It was a pressure behind her eyes, a warmth in her back teeth, a memory of green that arrived whole in her mind the way a scent arrives before its source. It sounded exhausted. It sounded like her grandmother's last winter.

"I came," Dominic answered aloud, because the Heartroot was too weak to hold her thought-language long. She knelt in the dry grass at its roots and laid both palms against the furrowed bark. It was cold. Trees in high summer should never be cold. "I'm sorry it took so long. I'm sorry they starved you."

*Not your fault, little warden.* A flicker of something almost like humor moved through the contact, faint as light through deep water. *Your grandmother's fault. Her mother's. All the well-meaning hands that built the valley to keep me safe until the burning stopped — and then forgot to unbuild it. A prison is still a prison when the jailers love you.*

Behind her, boots on stone. Aldric came around the tree's far side with his sword already drawn, his grey eyes moving constantly across the pass below — the only way up, and the way their enemies would come. He had not spoken since Thornfell. He had not needed to. Whatever he had decided by that firelight, whatever he had buried and finally dug up and shown her, he had made his choice on the mountain road when he turned his back on the white cloaks and did not look again.

"They're coming," he said now. "An hour, perhaps less. The commander rides at the head." His jaw tightened. "He'll want you alive, Dominic. He wants the Heartroot more, but he wants *you*, because without a speaker the root is only wood."

"Then he can't have either." She kept her hands on the bark. The Heartroot's weakness seeped into her like cold water into wool, and beneath the weakness, deeper down, she felt the other thing — the vast dark reservoir of everything magic had ever been, drained nearly empty but not empty, waiting for one final hand to open the sluice. "Aldric. When they come—"

"When they come," he said, "you will be doing what you came to do, and I will be standing between you and every sword in that army. That was always the shape of this. Don't insult me by arguing."

She hadn't been going to argue. That was the terrible thing. Somewhere between the ford and Thornfell and the firelight confession, arguing with Aldric Vane had stopped being something she knew how to do. She loved him the way she loved the valley — helplessly, against her own judgment, with full knowledge of what such love cost.

So instead she said, "Kiss me first. In case there isn't time after."

He crossed the grove in four strides, sheathed his sword, and took her face in both gauntleted hands with a gentleness that had no business living in a man built for war. His kiss tasted of ash and pine smoke and the iron-tang of the altitude, and it was brief because it had to be, because an hour was not very much time at all.

"Say it back," he murmured against her mouth.

"I'm coming back," she lied, gently, and saw him know it was a lie, and saw him let her keep it anyway.

* * *

They came up the pass like a tide of white and steel.

Dominic felt them before she heard them — through the stones, through the thin stubborn grasses of the scree, through the bones of the mountain itself. Three hundred men. Pikes and crossbows, the commander's black banner with its silver axe, and at the edges, leashed and muzzled, the hunter-hounds whose bloodline had been bred for one purpose: to kill things that spoke.

*Aldric,* she warned, and he was already moving, planting himself at the mouth of the pass with his sword bare and his white cloak — his former colors — torn from his shoulders and left lying in the dust behind him like a shed skin.

"HOLD!" His voice cracked off the stone walls like a hammer blow. "In the name of Ser Aldric Vane, Knight of the Ash, who led your vanguard at Redmere and Cael Hollow — hold and hear me!"

The column slowed. Stopped. Dominic watched from the grove's edge as three hundred faces turned upward toward the lone figure barring their road, and she understood suddenly what he was doing, what it would cost him. He was spending himself. Every year of service, every scar, every man who'd slept safe because Aldric Vane stood watch — he was spending all of it in a single breath, buying her minutes with the only coin he had left.

"You were told you come to destroy a weapon," he called down. "You were lied to. You were told magic is a sickness. You were lied to. Your commander means not to burn the Heartroot but to *harvest* it — to cut it open and pour what's left of the wild into the Crown's engines and cages. I have seen his orders. I have seen the wagons. Ask him. Ask him why the iron wagons follow you. Ask him why there are chains sized for a tree."

A murmur ran along the column like wind through wheat.

And then Commander Halev rode forward, unhurried, immaculate in white enameled plate, and the murmur died.

"Ser Vane." His voice carried effortlessly; it was the voice of a man who had spent thirty years being obeyed. "Step aside. You are ill, or bewitched, or bought. Any of the three can be cured. Step aside, and the Crown will remember your name kindly."

"The Crown," Aldric said, "can remember me however it likes. I renounce my oath. Here. Before these witnesses. Before the sky." He flung his sword arm wide. "I was sworn to protect the realm from sorcery. I have learned, too late and too dearly, that the realm includes the sorcerers. Includes the beasts. Includes the tree. If protecting them makes me a traitor—" his voice broke, just once, and steadied, "—then let the record show Ser Aldric Vane betrayed his oath gladly, and would do it again."

For one suspended moment, Dominic believed. She saw doubt move through the ranks, real doubt, men looking at their neighbors, pikes wavering.

Then Halev sighed, the sigh of a patient father, and drew his blade, and said: "Kill him."

* * *

What happened next, Dominic would spend the rest of her life failing to describe.

There was no time to think. There was barely time to scream. The front rank surged up the pass, crossbows leveled at the single figure in their path, and Aldric set his feet and raised his sword and became, for the length of one impossible heartbeat, everything a knight was supposed to be and almost none of what knights actually were.

Dominic pressed both hands flat against the dying Heartroot and opened herself completely.

She had spent her whole life damming the gift — clamping down on the whispers at the valley's edge, swallowing the words of goats, pretending deafness while the world talked and talked and talked. Now she reversed it. Now she tore the dam out by its roots.

Her voice poured into the Heartroot like light into a cracked vessel.

She gave it the goat-speech first, the oldest layer, the babble she'd heard before she could walk — and felt the tree drink. Then the wolf-tongue, hard and loyal, learned on the road from a pack that had bled for her. Then crow and hawk and hare and the slow granite patience of the mountain sheep. Then the deeper strata, the ones she hadn't known she carried: her grandmother's voice speaking to a mule forty years dead. Her mother's laughter answering a barn cat. Generations of wardens' stolen conversations, hoarded in her blood like coins sewn into a hem. All of it. Everything. The Heartroot drank and drank and *shuddered* —

and woke.

Light came up out of the ground.

Not firelight, not sun. Green light, the color of the first leaf of spring seen through closed eyelids, rising out of the fissures around the roots, racing outward through the stone, down the mountain, into the earth of the whole wounded world. Dominic heard it go. She heard it the way you hear a wave strike a shore — everywhere at once, immense, inevitable. Magic, severed for two hundred years, came flooding home through every root and river and buried seed, and where it went, things answered.

Grass erupted through scree. The bare branches of the Heartroot exploded into leaf so fast and so loud it sounded like applause. And far below, at the treeline, at the valley's edge, at the Weeping Wood itself — the severance shattered. She felt it break like ice going out in spring, the great silence that had smothered her childhood tearing open from end to end, and the valley's trees lifted their heads after two centuries of sleep, and began, all together, to sing.

But she paid.

Oh, she paid. The gift went out of her the way blood leaves a wound — not all at once but unstoppably, warmly, forever. She felt each tongue depart. Wolf went first, then crow, then goat, each one a door closing in a house she'd lived in all her life. She clawed at them and they slid through her fingers like water, like names of the dead. By the time the green light reached the pass, Dominic Winterbourne was kneeling under a blazing tree in a world suddenly, absolutely silent — silent of the voices that had been her secret, her shame, her self, for as long as she could remember.

She was screaming. She didn't know for how long.

*Little warden.* The Heartroot's voice, once more, tender as a closing hand. *Thank you. Sleep if you can. But look — look what you've done.*

* * *

She looked.

The battle below had stopped being a battle.

The commander's hounds had gone mad — or sane; it depended on the telling. Their muzzles split like husks as the green light touched them, and the dogs stood among the soldiers and howled, and the howl was *language*, and half the ranks heard it and dropped their weapons rather than listen further. Roots moved underfoot with deliberate slowness, tripping shields, dragging pike-butts into soft earth. The mountain grass thickened around ankles like a rising tide.

Commander Halev fought through it. She watched him climb toward the grove, cutting at branches that reached for him with something horribly like intelligence, his white armor smeared with sap and green-gold light. Aldric met him ten paces from the tree, and the two of them clashed in the falling gold-green rain of Heartroot blossom, and it was brief and ugly and human — steel and fury and thirty years of shared oaths curdled into hate.

Aldric lost his footing on the slick new grass. Halev's blade rose.

The wood did not permit it.

A branch thicker than a battering ram came out of the Heartroot's crown with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and took the commander the way a hand takes a fly — gently, almost, and then completely. Dominic heard the crack of plate, the wet end of a shout. The tree lifted him high, held him up before his own army like a banner run up wrong-side-out, and then drove him down into the earth at the Heartroot's feet, and the roots received him.

The green light pulsed once, like a swallow.

Three hundred men looked at their dead commander, at the living tree wearing spring like a coronation, at the knight kneeling bloody in the grass with his sword point-down and his head bowed — and the army of the witch-hunters broke. Not in panic. In something quieter and far more fatal to the old order. They simply lowered their weapons, all together, and sat down on the mountainside amid the new flowers, like men waking from a fever, and no force on the mountain could have made them take those weapons up again.

Dominic slid down against the Heartroot's trunk, hollowed out, deaf to everything except human sound — Aldric's ragged breathing as he climbed to her, the wind, her own heart.

"It's done," he said, kneeling, gathering her against his chest. His hands were shaking. "Dominic. Look at me. It's done."

"I can't hear them anymore." Her voice came out small and ruined. "The birds are singing right now, right above us, and it's just — it's just singing. It's nothing. I'm nothing to it. I'm just—" 

"No." He took her face in his hands, forcing her eyes to his, and his own were wet and fierce. "You are the woman who fed a dying god with her own voice. You are the loudest thing this world has seen in two hundred years. Silence in you isn't emptiness, Dominic. It's the shape of what you gave away."

She wanted to believe him. Below them the army sat scattered in the blossoms like penitents, and the Weeping Wood, far down in its valley, was rising green and awake and singing to itself in a language she could no longer translate — and somewhere in that vast rejoicing, she realized, someone was climbing the pass alone, unarmed, weeping openly, and Aldric had gone rigid against her as he recognized the white-and-mud figure of the man who had once been his squire.

"Aldric," the young man gasped, falling to his knees beside them. "Aldric — you have to come — the city — they're saying the trials won't stop, they're saying the Regent's ordered a purge of everyone who ever *stood near* a trial — there are hundreds locked in the bell-tower dungeons right now, and the garrison's gone mad with fear—"

Dominic looked at the Heartroot, at its new leaves turning slowly against the sky, at the commander's broken body already being folded into moss and root.

The work, it seemed, was not finished.
