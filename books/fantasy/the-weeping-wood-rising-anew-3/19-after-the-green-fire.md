## 19. After the Green Fire

The silence came first.

Aldric Vane had stood in the heart of a hundred battles, and he had learned that the end of a battle was never quiet — it was a chaos of groans and orders and men weeping into the mud while their officers screamed them into ranks. But this was different. This was the silence of a world that had just remembered how to speak, and had chosen, for one long held breath, to say nothing at all.

The green fire was dying above him. It had not burned like flame; it had risen like light given weight, ribbons of emerald streaming up from the shattered grove and out across the sky in slow rivers, and where they passed, things changed. The dead bracken greened. The blackened trunks of trees felled by the commander's siege-engines budded with leaves out of season. And the Weeping Wood — the valley he had been raised to fear, the prison built by his own Crown and dressed up as sanctuary — simply *opened*, like a fist unclenching after three hundred years. Magic ran off it in visible streams, silver-green and endless, spilling down the mountainsides toward the lowlands, toward the cities, toward every corner of the world that had been starved on purpose.

Below him, on the terraced slope before the grove, the army of Ser Garren Holt was breaking.

Not retreating — breaking. Aldric watched from the ridge as knights who had spent their lives burning beast-speakers dropped their weapons and fell to their knees, not in surrender but in something closer to worship. Some wept openly. A sergeant tore the white-and-gold badge of the Order from his surcoat and ground it under his heel. Others fled, stumbling over roots that seemed, now, to be reaching for them — though whether to catch or to claim, Aldric could not say. The wood itself moved among the soldiers like a slow tide: vines curling around abandoned pikes, pulling them down into the soil; saplings rising in minutes where the army's campfires had scarred the earth; and everywhere, everywhere, the animals. Wolves walking between kneeling men without fear. Deer stepping out of the treeline to stand beside soldiers who had hunted their kind for coin. Crows settling on the shoulders of officers who had once ordered aviaries burned.

They were not attacking. They were *witnessing*.

And at the center of it all, where the Heartroot had blazed and dimmed and blazed again, Dominic Winterbourne lay in a bed of new grass, and she did not move.

Aldric went down the slope at a run.

He shouldered past wolves who regarded him with amber eyes that held no malice and no welcome — only assessment, the way one beast assesses another's claim on territory. He did not stop. His armor was scorched; his shield arm hung useless, dislocated at the shoulder when the commander's vanguard had broken against the beasts' charge; blood from a cut above his brow had dried to a crust that cracked when he clenched his jaw. None of it mattered. Nothing had mattered since the moment the light had gone up out of the grove and he had seen her fall forward into the roots of the Heartroot like a puppet whose strings had been cut.

He reached her and dropped to his knees in the wet grass.

She was breathing. That was the first thing, the only thing, the thing he would carry for the rest of his life: *she was breathing*. Shallow and slow, but steady. Her face was turned toward the sky, and it was the same face he had come to know across a thousand miles of road — stubborn, sharp-boned, freckled from years under a valley sun — but emptied somehow, the way a house is emptied. Everything that had made her *her* had been poured out into the world, and what remained was the shell of her, beautiful and terrible and still.

"Dominic." His voice came out ruined. "Dominic, wake up."

Around them, the mourning and the celebration went on. He could hear it now — hear it because he could not understand it. The wolves were keening, a low harmonic sound that rolled through the grass like distant thunder, and the birds had begun to sing again after days of silence, and somewhere near the grove a stag bellowed the great bellows-call that stags make in autumn, except it was spring, and the call was answered by six other stags from six directions, and the whole wood rang with it like a cathedral of voices. They were grieving her gift and hailing her sacrifice in the same breath, he understood that much even without the words. The beasts knew what she had done. The beasts knew what it had cost.

And she could no longer hear a single note of it.

That was the cruelty of it. That was the wound beneath all wounds. She had given everything so that magic could live, so that the speaking-world could be reborn — and the first act of that reborn world was to mourn her loss in a language she would never translate again. She had bought the conversation of all creation with her own place in it.

"Dominic." He took her hand. Her fingers were cold, dirt-stained, the nails broken from the climb. "Come back. Whatever you gave — whatever's left — I need you to come back."

Her eyelids fluttered. For a moment there was nothing behind them, the blankness of deep sleep or deeper exhaustion. Then her eyes opened, and they found his face, and they focused, slowly, painfully, like someone surfacing from very deep water.

"Aldric." Her voice was hoarse, scraped thin. "It worked."

"Yes."

"Is it—" She stopped. Swallowed. He watched her try to sit up and fail, watched her try again with his arm behind her shoulders, and succeed, and then go very still, her head tilting, listening to something that was not there.

The wolves had drawn closer. A dozen of them ringed the clearing now, sitting in a half-circle like a congregation, their eyes fixed on her with an intensity that would have been frightening if it had not been so obviously tender. One of them — the grey bitch with the torn ear, the one who had led the pack at the ford, the one who had fought for her twice now — crept forward on her belly and laid her great head in Dominic's lap.

Dominic stared down at her. Her hands rose and hovered over the wolf's skull, trembling.

"She's saying something," she whispered. "Isn't she. I can see it — I can see she's *saying* something—"

Aldric said nothing. There was nothing true and kind that could be said.

"They're all—" Her voice broke. She looked up, and her gaze swept the clearing: the wolves, the deer at the treeline, the crows, the stag standing sentinel on the ridge with his antlers silhouetted against the fading green light. "They're all talking. I can see their mouths moving, I can see— oh gods, Aldric, they're *mourning*, aren't they? They know. They know what I did, and they're trying to tell me, and I can't—" 

Her composure, which had held through witch-hunter raids and ambushes and the death of her neighbor and the confession of what he himself had been — her composure went all at once, like ice off a roof in thaw. She bent over the wolf's head and sobbed, great racking sobs that shook her whole body, and the grey wolf whined and pressed closer and licked her tear-streaked cheek, and could do nothing else, and neither could Aldric, and so he did the only thing left to him: he wrapped both arms around her and held on, and let her grieve into the scorched leather of his breastplate until the light failed and the stars came out over the reborn wood.

* * *

They made camp that night among the ruins of the enemy's lines.

There was a strange practicality to it — the army's tents still stood, its cookfires still smoldered, its supply wagons sat loaded with bread and salt pork and good wine — because the army, such as it was, had ceased to be an army. Perhaps two hundred of Holt's fifteen hundred remained within sight of the grove by full dark, and most of those were wounded or dazed or simply unwilling to leave. Word had gone out ahead of them, borne by fleeing riders and, Aldric suspected, by faster and stranger messengers: the Heartroot lives, magic is loose in the world, the Weeping Wood has woken. By morning there would be pilgrims instead of soldiers. By midsummer, perhaps, a city of them.

He built a small fire inside the commander's own pavilion — a gesture of theft so petty and so satisfying that he almost smiled doing it — and brought Dominic water and broth and, eventually, when she could keep it down, wine from the commander's stores. She sat propped against a saddle with a blanket around her shoulders, staring into the flames, and the grey wolf lay across her feet like a living hearthstone, and four more wolves had taken up stations outside the tent flaps, silent and immovable as statues.

"They won't leave," she said at last. Her voice was steadier now, scraped but functional. "I told them to. Well — I tried to tell them. I gestured." A ghost of her old dry humor flickered across her face and died. "They looked at me like I'd lost my mind. Which, fair."

"You saved their entire world," Aldric said. "I suspect they've decided you're theirs now."

"I don't want to be anyone's." It came out raw. Then she closed her eyes. "No. That's not true. I just want — I want to understand them again. Twenty-nine years, Aldric. My whole life I thought the silence was the price of safety. Turns out it was just practice." She laughed, and it was the worst sound he had ever heard from her, and then she stopped laughing and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I can feel where the door used to be. Isn't that obscene? The gift is gone — poured out, spent, every drop of it — but I can feel the shape of where it lived. Like an amputated limb. Like a tooth."

Aldric knelt by the fire and fed it a stick, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say, and because some griefs are not helped by words. Outside, the wolves began to sing again — softly this time, a low weaving chorus, and he saw her whole body tense toward a meaning she would never hold again.

"I keep thinking," she said quietly, after a long time, "about the valley. My mother. The goats." Her hands lowered. In the firelight her eyes were red-rimmed and terribly clear. "Everything I was running toward when I left home, I was actually running away from. The wardens. The severance. All of it built to keep me ignorant and safe and useless. And now the severance is broken, and the valley will change — it's already changing, probably. Magic pouring in. Beasts waking up and remembering. And my mother is there, and she doesn't know anything yet, and I—" Her breath caught. "I can't even send a bird to tell her. Not anymore. Birds don't carry messages for me now. They carry them for everyone else."

"Then we'll go ourselves," Aldric said. "When you're strong enough. We'll walk into that valley together and knock on your mother's door like civilized people, and I'll introduce myself, and she'll take one look at me and bar the door."

That startled a real laugh out of her — small, wet, but real — and something in his chest loosened for the first time since dawn.

"And what will you tell her you are?" she asked. "Witch-hunter?"

The word landed between them like a dropped blade.

"No," Aldric said. And found, examining himself in the silence that followed, that he meant it completely, and had for some time — perhaps since the ford, perhaps since Thornfell, perhaps since the pyre he had lit at nineteen and never stopped carrying. "Not anymore. I'm going to write to the Order tonight. Formal renunciation. Every oath, every rank, every pension. They'll strike my name from the rolls. They may well put a price on it instead."

"The war's over, Aldric. Holt is dead. The wood killed him — you saw it. Who's left to hunt?"

"The Order doesn't end because its purpose ended. Institutions outlive their reasons — you of all people know that; the valley was one." He met her eyes across the fire. "There are commanders in the south who will call today a catastrophe. Who'll say the Heartroot must be destroyed before the magic spreads, that beast-speakers must be purged before they organize. There will be more Holts. There may be worse ones. And every one of them will remember that Ser Aldric Vane, the Knight of Ashes, the man who lit more pyres than any officer of his generation, stood aside and let it happen. My name will be useful to them as a traitor's name. A warning to the others."

"So run," she said. "Go east, past the marches. Disappear. You've earned—"

"That's the thing." He reached into his breastplate and drew out the folded parchment he had written by firelight while she slept, the ink still faintly shining. "I'm not running anywhere."

She took it. Read it. He watched her lips move slightly over the formal language of renunciation, the phrases he had memorized as a boy taking the white cloak and now unmade in reverse, and when she reached the final line her reading slowed and stopped.

*"…and having surrendered my oaths entire, I surrender also my country and my kin, and take as my remaining duty none that any crown or order shall set, but one alone, freely chosen: the keeping and companionship of Dominic Winterbourne, for as long as she will suffer me, in whatever place she chooses to stand."*

She read it twice. When she looked up, her eyes were bright and wary at once, the way they had been at the crossroads, the way they had been in the drowned chapel, every time he had offered her something true and she had weighed it for traps.

"You wrote this before you knew I'd live," she said.

"I wrote it hoping. Hoping isn't a strategy I recommend, but I'm told it occasionally pays."

"Aldric." She set the parchment down carefully, as if it might bruise. "You're giving up everything. Your name, your order, your country. For me. For a goat-herd who can't even hear the wolves anymore, who spent the last month dragging you toward a destiny that cost me the only gift I ever—"

"Yes," he said.

"—and you didn't even ask—"

"There was nothing to ask." He moved around the fire and knelt in front of her, close enough that the grey wolf lifted her head and studied him with grave yellow eyes, close enough to see the exhaustion carved into every line of her face. "Dominic. Listen to me. I spent nineteen years serving a law that burned children. Every morning I woke knowing it, and every morning I told myself the alternative was worse, and the telling wore me down to nothing — you've seen what's left. Duty without love is just a machine that grinds people. I've been that machine. I will not go back to being it. Not for a pardon, not for a country, not for anything." He took her cold hands in his. "You are the first thing I have chosen freely in my entire life. Let me keep the choice."

For a long moment she didn't answer. The fire crackled. Beyond the canvas walls, the wolves sang their low unresolved song, and far off across the reborn wood, something enormous — something that might have been the Heartroot itself, dreaming its first dream in three centuries — sighed through the branches.

Then Dominic Winterbourne leaned forward and pressed her forehead against his, and stayed there, breathing, and said, in a voice worn smooth as river stone:

"As long as I will suffer you. Which, I warn you, knight, may be a very long time."

"I'm told I'm durable," Aldric said, and kissed her, and outside the tent the grey wolf thumped her tail once against the ground — a judgment, he suspected, though of what kind, and in what words, he would spend the rest of his life wondering.

* * *

Dawn came up green and gold through the canopy of a wood that had not looked like this in living memory.

Aldric stood at the mouth of the pavilion with a cup of terrible army coffee and watched the light come down the mountain. The last of the green fire had faded in the night, but its work remained: the slopes below the grove were clothed in growth that had no business existing after winter, and the air itself felt different — thicker, sweeter, charged the way air feels before lightning, except constantly. Magic, loose in the world. He kept catching himself holding his breath, waiting for it to feel wrong, and it never did. It felt like a room where a window had finally been opened.

Movement at the treeline. Not wolves this time.

Riders — a dozen, maybe more, coming up the army's old approach road at a walk. His hand went to his sword before his mind caught up, and then caught something else: they flew no colors. Their armor was mismatched, travel-worn, and several of the riders were not armored at all, and at least two of them were women in the plain wool dresses of the lowland villages, riding astride like frontier folk. Refugees, he thought. Pilgrims. The first of the tide that would follow.

But the man at their head carried a banner after all — furled, tied down, and when they halted at the edge of the camp and the man unwound it and let it rise on its pole, Aldric's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

White and gold. The Order's colors. But not the Order's arms — the field was wrong, altered, and stitched across it in fresh black thread was a device he had never seen and understood instantly, the way one understands a blow: a tree, bare and black, struck through with a sword.

The rider at the head removed his helm. He was young. Younger than Aldric had been at the pyre. He had a fanatic's clear eyes and a herald's trained voice, and it carried across the silent camp without effort.

"I seek Aldric Vane, called the Knight of Ashes!" he called. "By authority of the Grand Commandery of the South, acting in continuity of the Crown's commission! Let him come forth and answer for his treason!"

Behind Aldric, inside the tent, he heard Dominic stir, and the wolves begin, very softly, to growl.

The young knight's eyes swept the camp, found him, and narrowed.

"And be advised," the herald called, and now his voice dropped, and the whole morning seemed to lean in to listen, "that the Commandery knows what was given up in that grove. It knows the beast-speaker spent herself empty. And it has word — word from the south, word already spreading — of another like her. Another speaker-line the archives missed." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man delivering a sentence he believed to be mercy. "The war is not over, Knight of Ashes. It has only changed its aim."

Aldric set down his coffee, very carefully, and reached for his sword.
