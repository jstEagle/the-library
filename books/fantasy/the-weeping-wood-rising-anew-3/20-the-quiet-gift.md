## 20. The Quiet Gift

The road home was not the road she had left.

Dominic felt it before she saw it — a loosening in her chest, like a knot untied after years of pulling. The valley had been sealed against magic for as long as anyone could remember, a hollow place where the world held its breath, and she had grown up inside that held breath without knowing it was one. Now, cresting the last ridge with Aldric's cloak around her shoulders and her body still aching from what she had given away, she looked down into the Weeping Wood Rising and wept without shame.

Green fire had passed through here. She could see its fingerprints everywhere. The birches along the river stood taller than any tree had a right to stand, their white bark shot through with veins of living gold. Moss climbed the stones in patterns too deliberate to be accident — spirals, sigils, the slow handwriting of something ancient learning to write again. And the air. Saints, the air. It moved through the valley like water through a net, catching on everything, brightening everything, so that even the shadowed places seemed to hold light of their own.

"Are you certain you're ready?" Aldric asked beside her.

He had barely left her side since the grove. Since the green fire. Since she had poured everything she was into the Heartroot's failing heart and felt it drink her down and rise. He had carried her half the way down the mountain when her legs gave out, and he had not once spoken of his renounced oath, his scattered order, the Crown that would call him traitor until the end of his days. He simply stayed, the way stone stays.

"I don't know what ready means anymore," Dominic said. "But my goats are down there. Or their grandchildren are. And I'd like to stop being afraid of my own front door."

They descended together.

* * *

The village had changed less than the wilds around it, which somehow made the changes stranger. The same crooked lanes. The same smoke-stained eaves. Old Marta's herb garden had overrun its fence entirely, and no one had cut it back, because the feverfew had begun blooming in colors that did not have names. Children ran past them barefoot through puddles that glittered faintly, chasing each other, laughing at things Dominic couldn't see.

And the people — they watched her pass. Not with the old narrowed suspicion, not with the memory of pyres behind their eyes, but with something rawer. Wonder, and beneath the wonder, guilt. They knew now what had been taken in her name. They knew what Maren had suffered in the witch-hunters' cells while the whole valley pretended not to hear.

An old woman stepped out of the bakery as they passed and pressed a loaf into Dominic's hands without a word. Her eyes were wet. Dominic took it, and nodded, and could not speak either. Some debts could only be acknowledged, never paid.

It was at the gate of her own croft that the first of them came.

She heard no voice. That was the thing she kept forgetting, the wound that kept reopening: she listened, out of habit, the way a woman who has lost her sight still turns toward windows. And there was nothing. No goat-speech grumbling about rain. No crow commentary on the state of the world. The silence where her gift had lived was vast, and it had her own shape.

But the goat came anyway.

A young doe, white-flanked, one horn spiraled wrong — she recognized her, distantly, some granddaughter of Brindle who had never known her. The animal trotted up the lane, stopped three paces away, and looked at her with those horizontal amber eyes. Then it crossed the distance and pressed its forehead against Dominic's knee and stood there, breathing.

"She can't hear you," Aldric said quietly, crouching nearby. "Can she?"

"No," Dominic said. Her voice cracked on it. "I can't hear you. Whatever you're saying. I'm sorry."

The goat didn't leave. If anything she leaned harder, a warm weight against Dominic's leg, chewing thoughtfully on nothing, utterly unbothered by the absence of conversation. And as Dominic stood there with her hand resting on the animal's neck, she began to notice other things. Small things, arriving one by one like guests through an unlocked door.

The way the doe's ears angled toward her before Dominic made a sound. The way the sparrows on the fence did not startle when she moved, though they scattered for Aldric. The way the whole lane seemed to arrange itself, gently, around wherever she happened to be standing — a fox watching from the hedgerow without hunger, a pair of crows walking rather than flying, keeping pace.

"They trust you," Aldric said. He said it carefully, the way men handle broken things. "Even without the words."

"They *follow* me." Dominic's throat tightened. "I'm not commanding anything. There's nothing left to command with. But they—" She laughed, and it came out ragged. "Aldric, they're treating me like I'm one of them."

* * *

That evening she sat in her own doorway, the door she had bolted for twenty years against a world that burned women like her, and the croft was full.

Not crowded — filled. Goats dozed against the walls of the byre. A badger she had never met sat at the edge of the lamplight, washing its paws, dignified as a magistrate. Two owls perched on the lintel above her head and showed no interest whatsoever in eating the mice that ventured boldly across the floor. The mice themselves seemed to understand the terms of the peace. Everything had agreed upon something, some old law older than language, and she was written into it now — not as mistress, not as speaker, but as kin.

"It doesn't hurt," she said, surprised. "I expected it to hurt more."

"And instead?"

"Instead it feels like—" She searched. "You know how you told me about your mother's kitchen? Before the Order took you? You couldn't remember the recipes, but you remembered how it *felt* to stand there. Warmth at your back. The smell of bread. That's what this is. I lost the words, and what's left is just... belonging. Raw belonging. Like the animals decided the contract stands even though the signature's gone."

Aldric turned his cup slowly in his hands. Firelight caught the grey at his temples, deeper than it had been a season ago.

"The Heartroot didn't take everything," he said. "It kept a seed. Or you kept a seed. I'm not sure there's a difference anymore."

"No," Dominic said softly. "I'm not sure there is."

Outside, the night was loud in ways it had never been in the old silent valley. Wolves sang somewhere upriver — not the hungry song she remembered from Thornfell's borders, but something longer, looser, threaded through with harmonies no wolf alone could make. The trees creaked in conversation. The river ran silver with reflected stars that seemed, impossibly, slightly brighter here than anywhere else.

She should have been grieving. Part of her was, and would be always — grief for Maren most of all, whose grave she would visit tomorrow with bread and salt and the story of what her death had purchased. But grief, she was learning, was not the opposite of this. Grief was the price tag still attached. The gift itself was real.

A fawn wandered out of the dark and folded itself at her feet like a dog, and Dominic put her hand on its spotted back, and felt — not words, not thoughts, but something adjacent. A warmth with direction. An attention. As if a hundred small lives had turned their faces toward her at once, wordlessly asking nothing, offering everything.

"You're crying," Aldric observed.

"I know." She wiped her face with her sleeve and laughed at herself. "It's just — I spent my whole life thinking my gift was my voice. That if I lost it, I'd be nobody. A hollow woman in an empty valley." The fawn shifted closer. "Turns out the voice was never the point. The point was that they *wanted* me. All this time. Even when I had nothing to offer but hiding."

* * *

Later, when Aldric slept inside and she kept the doorway vigil she suspected she would keep for the rest of her life — some habits outlive their reasons — she saw them coming up the lane.

Beasts. Dozens. Drawn out of the risen wood by whatever thread still connected her to the Heartroot's great green heart. Deer and hares, a fox with winter-thick fur, a black bear moving with the unhurried gravity of a pilgrim, and overhead a ragged skein of geese that settled onto the roof tiles like snow deciding to stay. They arranged themselves around the croft in a wide, patient circle, and none of them spoke, because none of them needed to, and Dominic sat among them in the doorway of her rebuilt life with tears running freely down her face and a smile she couldn't have stopped if she tried.

The valley that forgot had forgotten how to forget. Magic threaded the fences now, pooled in the well-water, hummed in the honeycombs. And she — hollowed, remade, half of herself poured into the roots of the world — was fuller than she had ever been.

It was near dawn, the beasts drowsing around her like a congregation after midnight mass, that she saw the rider on the far ridge. One figure, mounted, bearing a banner she recognized with a lurch: the white stag of the reformed Court of Judgment, the survivors' council that had begun summoning the old hunters to account. And the rider was not riding toward the village.

The rider was riding toward her, carrying a sealed letter, and the wax bore the mark of someone she had hoped never to hear from again — the commander's seal, torn open by another hand, forwarded with a single line visible even at this distance:

*He lives. And he remembers your name.*
