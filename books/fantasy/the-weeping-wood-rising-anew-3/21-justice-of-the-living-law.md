## 21. Justice of the Living Law

The wood had grown a court.

Dominic felt it before she understood it — a pressure in the air like held breath, a hush that rolled outward from the Heartroot's grove in slow green waves. Wherever it passed, things changed. Brambles that had strangled a well for thirty years drew back and bloomed. A millpond choked with ash and silt cleared overnight, its fish returning silver-bellied as if they had only been waiting to be asked. And along every road, at every crossroads, the trees leaned inward, forming archways, thresholds, doors.

The people called them judgment gates, though no one had built them, and no one could say what waited on the other side.

She stood at one now, on the road below Thornfell where the mountain pass opened onto the river country, and watched the survivors come.

They came on foot, mostly. Some drove carts piled with everything they owned; some carried nothing but the clothes they'd worn when the green fire swept through their villages and left their houses standing while the witch-hunters' garrisons burned. They came because word had gone ahead of them, carried by crows and foxes and children too young to lie: *the wood is calling names.*

At the gate's edge stood a birch older than any of them, its white bark scarred with letters no hand had carved. Names. Hundreds of them, pressed into the living wood in script that shifted like light on water — and beside each name, a mark. Dominic had learned to read them in the weeks since the rising. A leaf for those the trials had wronged and lost. A root for those who had hidden neighbors in cellars and haylofts and paid for it. And for others — the ones the wood summoned here, to this gate — a knot. A closed fist of grain in the bark, tight and dark.

Aldric stood beside her, out of his white armor at last, wearing a plain wool coat the color of wet slate. His sword hung at his hip out of habit more than need; she suspected he would carry it until the day he died, not because he trusted himself with it but because he didn't.

"You don't have to be here," he said quietly.

"Neither do you."

"That's different."

"It isn't." She touched his sleeve. "We're both answers now, Aldric. The wood grew us for this."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh. Below them, the crowd had stopped moving, all of them staring up the road at the birch, and Dominic understood why. The name carved there was fresh. It gleamed like sap.

*Ser Maren Voss,* it read. And beneath it, smaller, in the wood's own patient grammar: *Come. Be seen. Be weighed.*

* * *

Maren Voss came at dusk.

She was not what Dominic expected. She had imagined the commander of the witch-hunters arriving with an army, or in chains dragged by the very people she had hunted, or not at all — fled over the sea, perhaps, to some country where magic was still a story told to frighten children. Instead the woman walked up the road alone, unhorsed, unarmed, her white cloak traded for a beggar's gray, and behind her at a distance of fifty paces came perhaps three hundred of her former soldiers, walking the same road without orders, without banners, drawn the way moths are drawn and men are drawn to their own reckoning.

Dominic's hands shook. She made fists of them.

This was the woman who had burned the courier's body into a message. Who had interrogated Marta Hale in a cellar in the valley while Dominic ran, and kept at it after the map was gone, because cruelty, once begun, does not know how to stop. Who had marched an army up the sacred mountain to cut the Heartroot out of the world like a tumor, or a pearl.

Beside her, Aldric went very still. "I know her," he said. "She trained me. When I was nineteen and still believed the white meant something clean." He swallowed. "She taught me how to build a pyre so the wind wouldn't carry the smoke back into the town."

"I know," Dominic said. "You told me. Under Thornfell."

"And I keep telling myself it was confession." His voice was rough. "But standing here, watching her walk toward that tree, I think maybe I was only asking permission to hate her properly. It's easier to hate someone who taught you your worst self than to admit you were a willing student."

The crowd parted for Maren Voss the way water parts for a stone. No one spat. No one threw anything. That frightened Dominic more than violence would have — this silence, hundreds of people holding their grief like drawn bows and waiting to see what the wood would do.

Voss stopped beneath the birch. Up close she looked smaller, gray-faced, her eyes rimmed red as if she had been weeping for days or hadn't slept since the mountain. She looked up at her own name glowing in the bark, and then — slowly — she turned and faced the crowd.

"I won't kneel to a tree," she said. Her voice carried; it was a commander's voice, built for courtyards and battlefields. "I'll say what I did, and I'll say it once. Then whatever grows out of me can grow."

Someone in the crowd sobbed aloud.

"I burned forty-one people." No flinching. No softening. She counted them off like casualties in a report, and Dominic realized with a lurch that this, too, was a kind of courage — the terrible, useless courage of a soldier refusing to look away from her own ledger. "I ordered the burning of the village of Ashenford, knowing there were no witches in it, to draw out the courier. I gave the order that killed Marta Hale. I marched on the Heartroot intending to take it for the Crown and make magic a weapon that never runs dry." She paused, and something in her face finally cracked — not remorse exactly; something rawer. "I believed I was ending a sickness. I have learned since the mountain that I was the symptom."

The silence stretched. The birch leaves stirred without wind.

Then a man stepped forward from the crowd — a farmer, broad-shouldered, sunburnt, with the calloused hands of someone who had spent his life pulling things out of the ground. Dominic knew his face from the trial lists she had memorized in the capital's archive: *Hale.* Marta's husband. He had survived the raid on the valley because he'd been at market two towns over, and he had spent every day since learning what his wife had endured in his absence.

He stopped an arm's length from Voss. Everyone breathed shallowly. Beside Dominic, Aldric's hand drifted toward his sword and then deliberately away.

"You took her in the night," Hale said. "My boys heard the dogs. They were seven and nine. They heard her all night, and in the morning the house next door was empty, and we were told to be grateful it wasn't us." His voice didn't rise. It dropped instead, down to something quiet and bottomless. "I have imagined a hundred ways to answer you. Every one of them ends with blood, and none of them brings her back, and all of them makes my sons into men who solve grief with a knife."

Voss met his eyes. "Then do it anyway," she said. "I won't stop you. The wood won't either. That's what its justice is — it doesn't shield anyone. It only makes sure everyone is seen."

"No," said the birch.

Not a voice. Not words. But every person in that crowd heard it, the way Dominic had once heard goats and wolves and the low murmuring grammar of the wild — meaning arriving whole, without language, straight into the chest. The sound of it moved through the gathered people like a shudder, and several fell to their knees, and old women began to weep, and Dominic herself gripped Aldric's arm as the wood's will washed over the clearing:

*No.*

The branches dipped. From the birch's high crown, seeds came loose — pale-winged, drifting down through the dusk like snow that refused to land. They settled on Voss's shoulders, in her hair, on the farmer's hands, on the ground between them, and where each seed touched earth it sank and vanished, and where they touched skin they left no mark at all except warmth.

And Dominic understood.

*It isn't asking him to forgive her. It isn't even judging her yet. It's planting.*

"The wood doesn't do blood," she whispered, half to Aldric, half to the gathering dark. "It does roots. It takes the worst of us and puts us in the ground to see what grows."

Hale stood trembling before the commander, seeds in his hair like a blessing neither of them had asked for, and his face worked through fury and grief and something harder than both — choice. Long seconds passed. The crowd did not breathe.

Then he reached out, took a handful of soil from the verge, and pressed it into Voss's palm, closing her fingers over it.

"Bury yourself somewhere useful," he said hoarsely. "That's your sentence. Live where they can watch you work. Start with my farm — I've got fields that haven't grown right in years, and my boys need feeding, and I am not going to pretend that having you there won't be hell. But it'll be honest hell." His jaw tightened. "Better than the other kind."

Voss stared at the soil in her hand. For a moment she looked as though she might refuse it — pride was armor on that woman even now — and then her shoulders dropped, all at once, like a wall coming down, and she bowed her head over the dirt as though it were a relic.

"Yes," she said. "All right. Yes."

Around the clearing, the three hundred soldiers stirred, and Dominic felt the crowd's hunger turn and sharpen toward them — the rank-and-file, the torchbearers, the men who had held the ropes and lit the faggots and guarded cellar doors while women screamed. Justice, the easy kind, wanted them all. Wanted a reckoning wide enough to drown in.

Aldric stepped forward before the hunger could find its voice.

* * *

He walked down into the crowd alone, and Dominic followed because she could not bear not to.

"I was one of them," he said, and his voice, pitched for a single listener under Thornfell's pines, somehow reached every ear in that clearing. "Eleven years in the white cloak. I lit pyres. I dragged families from their beds. I stood guard outside interrogation rooms and told myself the screams were necessary." He stopped in front of the rank-and-file soldiers, men and women who would not meet his eyes, and pulled his sword from its sheath.

The crowd tensed. Someone cried out.

Aldric drove the blade point-down into the earth between himself and them, and let go of it.

"This is the last oath I ever kept," he said. "The Crown's law said magic was a crime and I enforced it. The Crown's law is dead now — the wood has buried it under a birch tree with all our names in the bark. So the question isn't whether you'll be judged. You will. By everyone you wronged, and by the thing that's growing under our feet, and most of all by yourselves, which is the judge none of us survives lying to." He turned slowly, addressing the villagers now, the grieving, the ones clutching tokens of their dead. "But hear me. I have stood where vengeance stands. I built my whole life on it — hunting the kin of a girl I burned, telling myself it was penance. It ate eleven years and gave back nothing but ash. If you spend these people the same way — if you hang every soldier and salt every field — you'll wake in ten years with full graves and empty hearts, and the wood will still be growing, and it will ask you what you planted."

Silence. Wind moved through the birch, and the names in its bark shimmered.

An old woman pushed forward from the crowd — Dominic recognized her, one of the Ashenford survivors, her face seamed like cracked clay. She looked at the soldiers for a long moment. Then she pointed at a young man near the front, barely twenty, still wearing half his white uniform like a man who'd run from a fire without stopping to undress.

"You held my door shut," she said. "While they took my son."

The young man's legs buckled. He went to his knees in the road. "Yes," he whispered. "Yes, mother. Yes."

"I'm not your mother," she said, and her voice broke on it, and she stood over him shaking, and the whole clearing waited to see whether grief would choose the knife or the plow.

She chose, as Hale had chosen. "Wheat," she said raggedly. "You'll plant wheat. My son was a farmer's boy. Somebody has to learn his rows." She turned away before the young man could speak, shoulders heaving, and the crowd parted around her, and one by one, hesitantly, haltingly, the pattern caught — villagers claiming soldiers the way creditors claim debts, naming tasks instead of punishments: *the granaries, the wells, the burned chapel roof, my father's forge, the orphaned children need hands.* Not forgiveness. Forgiveness was a private country some of them might never reach. This was something else — work. Restitution with dirt under its nails.

Dominic watched it happen and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the cellar in the valley, since Marta Hale's name first entered her life as a debt of blood. *Justice of the living law.* The wood had shown them the shape of it: not the axe, not the pyre's mirror-image, but the long slow labor of putting wrong things right with your own hands, in sight of the people you wronged, for as long as it took.

Some would refuse. Some among the soldiers slipped away into the dusk rather than be claimed, and Dominic marked their faces without hatred — the wood saw them too, and its trees had patience measured in centuries. And some among the villagers could not let go; one man screamed curses at the soldiers until his family led him weeping from the clearing, and no one blamed him, least of all Dominic, who had buried a neighbor and could not promise that in his place she would have chosen wheat.

But most stayed. Most knelt, or stood, and let themselves be seen, and were given burdens instead of graves.

Aldric came back to her side at last, swordless, hollow-eyed, wrung out. She took his hand.

"You gave up your blade in front of three hundred people who had every reason to want you dead," she said.

"They weren't my reason to be afraid." He looked down at her, and the ghost of his old grim humor flickered through the exhaustion. "You were. I've gotten used to being the man whose worst chapters you've read. I couldn't stand becoming one again."

"You're not." She squeezed his fingers. "You're the man who drove his sword into the ground and told a mob the truth. That's a chapter worth keeping."

Above them the birch rustled, and new letters surfaced in its bark — not judgments, Dominic realized, but records. *Planted,* said the marks appearing beside name after name. *Planted. Planted. Planted.* The wood keeping its own ledger, patient and exact, and somewhere in that green arithmetic even Ser Maren Voss's knot of grain was loosening, unfurling, becoming — not absolution, nothing so cheap — but possibility.

They walked back up the road together as full dark came down, past fields where freed soldiers were already being handed lanterns and seed-sacks by people whose grief walked beside them like a third figure, silent, unavoidable, real. Behind them the judgment gate glowed faintly, a threshold of living white wood, and above it the stars were coming out in a sky that magic had re-entered only months ago and already seemed reluctant to leave.

"We should sleep," Aldric said. "Tomorrow we ride for the valley. The spring planting won't wait, and neither will—" He stopped.

Dominic had gone still, her head tilted, listening to something he could not hear — or almost could not. In the year since the Heartroot had taken her voice among beasts and left her only its seed, she had learned the difference between hearing and *knowing*, and what she knew now arrived cold and clear as starlight: a summons, wordless, urgent, rising from the deep places of the wood.

From the Heartroot itself.

"Aldric," she said quietly. "Something's happening at the grove. The old growth is waking — all of it, tonight." She turned to look north, where the mountain stood black against the stars, and felt the seed of her gift pulse once, hard, like a second heartbeat. "And it's asking for us."
