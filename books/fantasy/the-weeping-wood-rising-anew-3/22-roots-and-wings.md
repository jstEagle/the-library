## 22. Roots and Wings

The valley had learned to weep again.

Dominic felt it before she saw it — a change in the air as she crested the ridge above her old home, the way the wind carried something more than rain now, something green and listening. Below her, the Weeping Wood spread like a spilled cup of light. Birch and rowan stood where bare ground had been. Streams ran silver where they had run brown. And everywhere — in the hedges, in the stones, in the very soil under her boots — that faint hum she had once known only at the valley's edge, the pulse of wild magic threading back into the world like water finding an old channel.

A year. A whole year since the grove. Since the green fire had poured out of her and into the dying Heartroot, since she had given up the voice that had been both her curse and her truest self.

She still reached for it sometimes. That was the strange grief of it — not missing the power, exactly, but missing the *conversation*. She would pass a crow on the fence rail and open her mouth to greet it, and nothing would come out but ordinary human sound, and the crow would look at her with its flat dark eye and fly off, unbothered, unknowing.

But the crows came anyway.

That was the seed the Heartroot had left in her. Not command. Not speech. Something quieter and stranger: trust. Beasts simply gathered where she was. Foxes trotted beside her cart without fear. The goats she'd raised from kids — old Bramble among them, gray-muzzled and dignified now — still followed her up the mountain paths as if she were one of their own. They did not tell her things anymore. They simply chose her, and somehow that was enough.

"You're stalling," Aldric said behind her.

"I'm admiring," she corrected. "There's a difference."

He came up the trail with the sapling cradled against his chest like a sleeping child — a heartwood seedling, barely knee-high, its leaves a deep impossible green that seemed to hold light the way water holds the sky. He had carried it all three days from the grove, refusing help, refusing even to set it down at night, sleeping propped against his pack with his hand resting on the pot's rim like a man guarding a wound that was also a gift.

A year ago he had been Ser Aldric Vane, Knight of Ashes, witch-hunter, oath-sworn destroyer of everything she was. Now he wore a plain brown coat with patched elbows, and his sword hung on a peg in the cottage they had built together below, and when he looked at her there was no ash in his eyes at all. Only morning.

"The ground's ready?" he asked.

"Since dawn." She knelt and pressed her palm flat to the earth where she had dug the hole that morning, wide and deep and lined with river stones. "It wants this. I can't hear it say so, but—" she smiled, rueful, "—I know it anyway."

"That's not nothing," Aldric said quietly. "Knowing without hearing. Might be the truer kind."

She looked up at him. He was grayer than he'd been a year ago — the siege at the pass, the winter of trials, all of it had written itself into him — but he stood easier now, shoulders unbowed, as if some weight had finally been set down rather than merely shifted. The wood had judged him along with everyone else, in those first strange weeks when the trees grew eyes and the rivers spoke in voices like rustling leaves. It had weighed his pyre against his protection, his guilt against his choice, and found — not innocence. The living law did not deal in innocence. It had found *remnant*, and remnant was enough to build on.

The same, she supposed, could be said of her.

"Hand me the spade," she said.

* * *

They planted it together, which was the point, though neither said so aloud.

Aldric lowered the roots while she guided them straight, and the moment the young tree touched the soil of the Weeping Wood, the ground itself seemed to sigh — a shudder through the earth, a ripple through the grass, and every bird within a mile went up at once, wheeling overhead in a slow spiral that was not alarm but something like applause. The goats pressed close, breathing warm against her neck. Somewhere down the valley, a bell began to ring — the chapel bell that Marta's daughter had insisted on hanging after they rebuilt it, rung for births and plantings and homecomings now instead of burnings.

Dominic's hands shook as she packed the soil around the stem.

Not from cold. From the sheer unbearable rightness of it. This was the ground her grandmother had walked as warden, the ground her mother had died defending without ever telling her why, the ground where a neighbor named Hetta had been dragged away in her place and never come home. Peace earned, not given — that was what the Heartroot's last whisper had promised her, in the wordless instant before the green fire took her voice, and here it was, dirt under her fingernails and a bell ringing for a planting instead of a pyre.

Hetta's name was carved into the new chapel door, along with forty-one others. Every year on midwinter's eve, the whole valley came to read them aloud. No one was required to attend. Everyone did.

"It'll grow crooked," Aldric said, stepping back, wiping his hands on his coat. "You know that. Heartwood always grows toward whatever it loves. In the grove they leaned toward each other for centuries. Out here it'll lean toward—" he glanced at her, and the corner of his mouth moved, "—well. Toward you, most likely."

"Crooked is fine." She stood, knees damp, and looked at the little tree standing in its ring of river stones, leaves trembling in a wind she couldn't feel on her own skin. "Crooked means it chose."

* * *

In the afternoon the valley came.

They came the way they always came now, unhurried, bringing bread and cider and news — the miller's second daughter, who had discovered she could coax flame to bend; the two brothers from the ford whose hounds had begun dreaming aloud; old Tam, who swore the river told him where the fish were running and had stopped apologizing for it. Magic was threading through the world again, uneven and wild and mostly small, and the Crown had learned — slowly, bitterly, after the commander's fall and the scattering of the hunter-orders — that small wild magic could not be burned out any more than you could burn out singing.

There was a school now, in the rebuilt longhouse. Dominic taught there two days a week. Not spells — the Heartroot's gift had not passed to others in anything so tidy — but how to listen. How to sit by a stream until the stream trusted you. How to know a thing without being told it. The children called it the quiet lesson, and they were better at it than she had ever been, because they had never been taught to be afraid of it first.

Marta arrived with cider and her daughter, who was seven and fearless and had already befriended a hedgehog that rode on her shoulder like a small armored captain. The girl looked at the seedling with enormous solemnity.

"Is it true it's the Heartroot's baby?"

"A cutting," Dominic said. "The first one brave enough to leave the grove."

"Where will the others go?"

"All over," Dominic said. "North to the coast. East past the mountains. Wherever someone's willing to dig a hole and mean it."

The girl nodded as though this settled the matter of the world's future entirely, and went off to torment the hedgehog with affection, and Marta squeezed Dominic's arm — she did that often these days, wordlessly, a whole apology and forgiveness exchanged in the pressure of fingers — and followed her toward the cider.

Aldric appeared at Dominic's elbow with two cups. He had gotten good at appearing at her elbow. He had gotten good at a great many quiet things: mending fences, sitting silent through her worst nights, saying *I lit that fire* when memory took him and letting her answer *and you built this one*.

"To the crooked tree," he said, lifting his cup.

"To chosen ground," she answered.

* * *

Evening came down gold through the birches, and the guests drifted home in twos and threes, and Dominic stayed by the seedling as the stars came out over the valley that no longer forgot.

She thought about the woman she had been two years ago — the one who counted fence posts to keep from counting the years, who swallowed words meant for animals, who mistook safety for a life. That woman had believed the valley protected her. She knew now it had been a cage built of love and lies, a prison shaped like a sanctuary, and that her whole childhood had been a wardenship she never consented to. She had spent a long bitter season being angry about that. She was done with the season. Anger, she had learned, was a country you could visit but shouldn't farm.

The beasts moved around her in the dusk — Bramble dozing at her feet, a doe drinking at the near stream without once looking up in alarm, an owl trading soft calls with something in the dark beyond the fence. She couldn't understand a word of it. She understood all of it.

"There you are," Aldric said, coming down the path with a lantern he didn't need — the seedling gave off a faint glow now that full dark had come, green-gold, patient. He sat beside her on the flat stone, shoulder warm against hers. "I've been thinking about names."

"For the tree?"

"For us, actually." He said it carefully, the way he said all the hardest true things. "We've been calling this rebuilding. Starting over. But I don't think that's right anymore. You don't rebuild on chosen ground. You just — live on it. Grow on it. Let it get into your bones." He turned the lantern down. "I keep waiting for the Crown to come take it back. Some part of me always will. But nobody's coming, Dom. The exile papers are real. The cottage deed is real. Tomorrow is real."

She leaned into him. Below them the valley lay in its bowl of hills, lamplit and smoke-scented, threaded with the faint silver shimmer of returning magic — visible now to anyone who cared to look, the world's old blood flowing again through channels centuries dry.

"Then let's call it ours," she said. "Not rebuilt. Ours."

The owl called again, closer, and landed on the seedling's single stake, and folded its wings, and kept watch — not because she asked, not because she commanded, but because that was what owls did for people the wild world had decided to keep.

And far away, deeper than distance, in the hidden grove where the great Heartroot pulsed green and strong beneath its canopy of centuries, Dominic felt — not heard, never heard again, but felt, the way you feel sunlight through a wall — something turn toward her. Something vast and ancient acknowledging the small crooked cutting planted in its name.

*Grow,* it seemed to say, in no language at all.

*Grow, warden. Grow.*

She smiled, and put her hand on the young trunk, and above the valley the stars wheeled on, and the Weeping Wood rose anew around them, root by root, wing by wing, into a world that would never again mistake a gift for a crime.

Tomorrow there would be seeds to carry north, and a school to teach, and letters to answer from distant villages where hedges whispered and wells sang. Tomorrow the work went on.

Tonight there was only the lantern-light, the owl, the man beside her, and the small green heartbeat in the earth — beginning.
