## 1. Charms for a City That Forgot

The baker's hands shook so badly she could barely count out the coppers.

"Please," Marta Voss said, glancing over her shoulder at the shuttered window of her shop. Flour dusted her knuckles white as bone. "I don't want anything strange. Nothing that could get me hanged. Just — luck. A little luck. The inspectors come tomorrow, and they've been closing bakeries all down Candlemaker's Row, and my husband says it's the taxes but I know it isn't, I *know*—"

"Marta." Wendell Yarrow kept her voice low and even, the way Mother Quill had taught her. Calm sells better than magic ever did. "Breathe."

She reached into the false bottom of her satchel and drew out the luck-knot she'd worked the night before: three strands of red thread braided around a core of rowan wood, each loop sealed with a whisper of intention so faint that even a zealous inspector with a divining rod would find nothing but craft. It was honest work, in its way. Trinket-work. Charm-seller's work.

It was also a lie she told with her whole body, every day, because what lived in her blood could crack the sky open.

"This is just thread and wood," Wendell said, pressing the knot into Marta's floury palm. "And a wish stitched into it, which is not against any law in Greyhollow, whatever the proclamations say. Hang it above your oven door. When you knead the morning dough, think of your husband's face. That's all it asks."

"That's all?"

"That's all." Wendell smiled, and hated how practiced it felt. "Luck isn't lightning, Marta. It's attention. You're paying someone to pay attention on your behalf."

Marta laughed — a wet, startled sound — and pressed an extra copper into Wendell's hand. "You're kind. For a charm-seller, you're kind."

*For a charm-seller.* As if the trade itself were something to be kind *despite*. Wendell pocketed the coin and didn't correct her. There was no correcting anyone in this city. Greyhollow had outlawed magic three years ago, burned the groves, closed the schools, and taught an entire generation to flinch at their own shadows. The city had not merely forgotten how to use its gifts; it had forgotten it had ever *had* them, and called the forgetting wisdom.

Wendell remembered. That was the problem. Remembering was the only crime she'd never been able to stop committing.

She packed up her satchel beneath the awning of the abandoned chandlery where the underground kept its market stall, and Mother Quill materialized beside her the way she always did — silent as spilled ink, small and round and sharp-eyed behind spectacles thick enough to read the future in candle smoke.

"You gave the Voss woman a knot," Quill observed.

"She needed one."

"I know what she needed. I'm asking what *you* needed." The old woman's gaze was uncomfortably perceptive. "You wove intention into that knot stronger than the last four combined. I could feel it humming from across the square. Careful, girl. Grief leaks."

"Grief is all I've got to weave with," Wendell said, and shouldered her satchel before Quill could answer.

* * *

The night market on Lanternbridge opened at dusk, when the legal markets closed and the illegal ones woke up. By full dark the old aqueduct arches were strung with paper lanterns in colors no respectable shop would dare — saffron, indigo, a green like deep water — and the canal below ran gold with reflected light. Fortune-tellers shared tables with pickle-sellers. A man sold bottled birdsong. Somewhere a fiddle played a tune that had been banned under the Cultural Purity Edicts, played loudly, played defiantly, and nobody stopped it.

Wendell arrived early, which was itself an admission of hope.

She'd met Ivo Senn eleven days ago, when he'd come to the stall asking after charms for a survey expedition — nothing flashy, he'd said, just something to keep his compasses honest in the marsh fog east of the city. He had ink stains on his fingers and a way of looking at maps like they were letters from friends. He'd paid full price without haggling, then lingered, then asked, with the abrupt courage of a man jumping off a ledge, whether charm-sellers ate dinner.

She'd said yes before she could talk herself out of it.

Now she stood under the third arch, wearing her good coat — the charcoal one with the silver buttons, the one that made her feel like a person rather than a fugitive — and watched the crowd for him, and hated herself a little for watching the crowd for him.

Three years since the storm. Three years since the trial, the exile, the name struck from the rolls of her home province. Three years since she'd last let anyone close enough to be lost. She had built her life in Greyhollow out of small safe things: knots, whispers, trinkets, the found family of charm-sellers who never asked about the scar on her left palm or why she went white at thunder. She had told herself that was enough. Some nights she'd almost believed it.

Then Ivo Senn looked up from his maps and smiled at her like she was a place worth charting, and the walls she'd built turned out to have doors in them after all. Doors she'd forgotten she'd installed.

"There you are." He came through the crowd carrying two skewers of grilled plums and a paper cone of spiced chestnuts, as if feeding her were a mission he'd prepared for. His hair was wind-mussed from the bridge walk, and there was a smear of blue cartographer's ink at his jaw he clearly didn't know about. "I got here early too. We're both liars."

"We're both early," Wendell corrected. "Lying would require intent."

"Oh, I intended." He handed her a skewer. "I intended very hard. I stood by the fountain rehearsing conversation topics for twenty minutes. Ask me what they were."

"What were they?"

"I've forgotten every single one." He grinned, and the lantern light caught him, and Wendell felt something in her chest crack along a seam she'd sealed years ago — not painfully. More like ice going out on a river in spring. Like something moving underneath that she'd thought was frozen solid.

They walked the length of the market slowly. He told her about the survey — the marsh fog, the drowned chapel whose bell still rang underwater some mornings, the way old roads remembered where they used to go even when the maps forgot. She told him about the stall, the knots, the little wishes people carried. She did not tell him about the storm. She did not tell him about Tam.

*Tam,* she thought, tasting plum and smoke and guilt. *My brother, who I cannot say aloud to a man I'm trying to love.*

That was the shape of it, she was learning. Hope didn't erase the guilt; it just gave the guilt somewhere new to live. Every laugh came with a ledger entry. *You are laughing while he is dead. You are eating plums while he is dead. You are letting a stranger make you feel real again while he is dead, and worse, you are glad.*

"You've gone somewhere," Ivo said gently. Not pushing. Just noticing. Cartographers, she decided, were dangerous people. They noticed where things were missing.

"Just the usual," she said. "Exiles carry a lot of luggage between the ears."

"I know the feeling. My mother's been gone six years, and she still takes up half a room in my head." He said it simply, without performance, and Wendell understood it was a gift — a hand extended across the gap, saying *I have rooms like yours.*

They stopped at the fortune-teller's table because Ivo insisted, laughing, that no first date at a night market was complete without a fraudulent prophecy. The old woman turned a card — the Tower, storm-struck, crown toppling — and Wendell felt the smile die on her face before she could catch it.

"Bad card?" Ivo asked.

"It's nothing. It's theater." But her palms had gone cold, and the old scar itched the way it did before weather changed.

The fortune-teller looked at her a moment too long, eyes gone oddly distant, then flipped the deck face-down. "On the house," she murmured. "No charge for either of you. Go on, then. Enjoy the market. Enjoy—" she paused, head tilting, listening to something none of them could hear, "—enjoy tonight. Tonight especially."

They walked on. The fiddle music swelled. Ivo bought her a paper lantern and wrote a wish on it before releasing it over the canal, shielding the words with his arm like a schoolboy, and Wendell laughed — really laughed — and for one whole hour the ledger stayed closed.

They ended the night at the rail of Lanternbridge itself, shoulder to shoulder, watching the last of the lanterns drift downstream. His hand found hers on the cold stone. She let it.

"I'd like to do this again," he said. "Tomorrow, if you're free. I have to show you something anyway. I found it today, cataloguing the back room of my shop — a folio, old, leather rotted right through. Prophecies, or the closest thing this city allows to them anymore." He laughed a little, embarrassed. "Probably nonsense. Probably a hundred years of nonsense. But one page — Wendell, one page described *tonight*. This market. The lanterns. Even the plums, if you can believe it. And it ends with a line I can't get out of my head."

The scar on her palm itched. Overhead, far past the edge of the city, clouds were gathering where the evening had been clear.

"What line?" she asked.

Ivo looked out at the black water, and the lantern light made his face flicker like a flame deciding what to become.

"*The weeping wood rising anew,*" he recited. "*And the storm returns to Greyhollow.*"

Far off, beyond the walls, thunder answered.
