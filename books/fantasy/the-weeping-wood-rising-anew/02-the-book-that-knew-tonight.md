## 2. The Book That Knew Tonight

Ivo Senn had measured every street in Greyhollow twice — once for the guild commission and once because he could not sleep — and he still did not know how to measure the distance between a man's hand and a woman's when both were pretending not to reach.

The night market gave him an excuse to try. Lanterns strung on fishhooks swayed above the stalls like a sky someone had stitched badly, throwing orange light over jars of pickled eels, bundles of dried feverleaf, a fortune-teller's table crowded with chipped teacups. Wendell walked beside him with her hands in her coat pockets and her chin up in that way she had, as if daring the city to be interesting enough for her.

She'd won him a copper ring at the ring-toss booth by cheating — he was almost certain she'd cheated; the rings had bent mid-air in ways that made his eyes ache if he looked too long — and now it sat on his smallest finger, warm as if it remembered being held.

"Cartographer," she said, tasting the word. "You draw places that exist."

"And charm-sellers," he said, "sell luck to people who already made their own choices. We're both in the business of pretending the world has rules."

That earned him a laugh, quick and low, gone almost before it arrived. He wanted to build something out of that laugh. A room, maybe. A life. He was aware this was absurd — they had known each other three weeks, most of it across the counter of Quill & Compass while she haggled over ink — but Ivo had spent his whole life reading maps, and maps taught you one thing above all others: you can tell which roads matter by how often people return to them.

Wendell returned. To his shop, to his bad tea, to his opinions about projection styles she clearly didn't share. She returned, and so tonight he'd done something reckless.

He'd cleaned the back room.

Not the whole thing — the whole thing would take a season — but he'd swept the floor where the folio presses stood, cleared the worktable of the half-finished survey of the Saltmarsh, and set out two chairs instead of one. Above the table hung the lamp with the green glass shade, the good light, the light he used only for work that mattered.

"I want to show you something," he said, and immediately hated how it sounded, and immediately loved how she looked at him when he said it — one eyebrow raised, amused, waiting.

* * *

The shop smelled the way it always smelled: paper dust and iron-gall ink and the tallow of old candles soaked into the shelves until the shelves themselves seemed made of time. Wendell trailed her fingers along the spines while Ivo climbed the rolling ladder into the dark upper stacks, where the things nobody commissioned lived. Ledgers of vanished trade routes. Surveys of districts that had burned before the surveys could be sold. And, wedged flat beneath an atlas of a country that no longer appeared on any map, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark —

He knew the weight of it before he knew the shape. Heavy. Wrong-heavy, the way a stone is heavy in a dream.

"What's that?" Wendell called up.

"I don't know," he said, and the honesty of it startled him. "I've had this shop six years. I've never seen this before."

He brought it down into the green lamplight. Oilcloth peeled away like skin off fruit. Beneath it: a folio bound in leather so dark it was nearly black, cracked along the spine, corners worn round as river stones. No title. Just a stamp pressed into the cover, tarnished silver, small enough to miss.

A tree, weeping. Branches bent low under a burden of drops.

And beneath the tree, worked into the roots: a crown.

Ivo's mouth went dry. He knew sigils the way other men knew faces. That was the royal mark — or close enough to make his neck prickle. The king's seal showed the crown plain, unburdened. This older version, this *burdened* version, belonged to engravings a century dead.

"Wendell," he said quietly. "Look at the cover."

She looked. Whatever crossed her face, she folded it away fast, the way she folded everything away, and said, "Open it."

The folio resisted, then yielded. Inside, pages of heavy rag paper, hand-inked, the script cramped and urgent in some places, ceremonial and slow in others. Prophecies. Dozens of them. Most written in the looping cipher of the old augurs, useless without a key.

Except the last entry.

The last entry was not in cipher at all.

* * *

Later — much later, when the lanterns outside had burned down to coals and the market noise had thinned to the occasional drunk singing to a closed door — Ivo would try to remember the exact moment the wonder left the room. He never could. It didn't leave. It *curdled*, slowly, like milk, like weather turning, so that the same words that had made his chest lift made his hands go cold, and there was no clean line between the two feelings anywhere in the night.

Because first, it was wonderful.

First, he read it aloud, and his voice shook with delight: *"On the eve of the frost-market, when the fishhook lanterns sway, the cartographer will bring the storm-caller to the green-lit room, and between them the book will open."*

He laughed when he read it. Actually laughed. "Frost-market. Tonight. Fishhook lanterns — Wendell, we walked under them not two hours ago." His finger trembled on the line, tracing the faded ink. "Green-lit room. Look up." The lamp hummed above them, its shade casting everything in moss and jade. "It knows my lamp. It knows my *shop*. Someone wrote this a hundred years ago and described tonight like they were standing behind me taking notes."

Wendell hadn't laughed. He'd been too busy being delighted to notice that.

*"The storm-caller will have hidden her name in a false name, and the cartographer will not ask for the true one, because he is kind, and kindness will be the hinge."*

His laugh died somewhere in the middle of that sentence. Not because it was wrong. Because it was right. She had introduced herself as *Paloma Wren* the first week, and he had known — known the way you know a map's north arrow is wrong, by feel, by everything else refusing to fit around it — and he had never asked. He'd told himself it was respect for privacy. It had also been fear. Fear that asking would end whatever fragile thing was growing between them.

He looked up at her then, finally, and saw her face had gone the color of the paper.

"Storm-caller," she said. Her voice came out flat. Careful. "That's specific."

"Wendell—"

"Read the rest."

There wasn't much rest. The entry ended in five lines, the hand deteriorating, letters gouged deep as if the writer had been pressing through fear or fury or both:

*"The wood remembers what the city forgets.*
*The wood has learned to wear the forgetting like a face.*
*When next the moon dies and is reborn, the weeping wood rises anew,*
*and the city's yesterday will be its root-water.*
*Only the storm-caller's dearest wound can buy tomorrow."*

Silence took the shop. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the lane a cat knocked over a bottle, and the sound was enormous.

"The eclipse," Ivo said. His own voice sounded foreign to him. "The lunar eclipse. That's — the almanac says—" He stopped. He knew exactly what the almanac said. He'd drawn the tidal charts himself. Eleven days.

Wendell reached across the table and turned the folio toward herself, and her fingers — steady, always steady, he'd watched her sell luck-knots to trembling strangers with those steady hands — her fingers were not steady now. "This sigil." She touched the stamped tree-and-crown on the inside cover, then flipped to the frontispiece, where a larger version was inked in silver that had gone grey. "I've seen it before."

"Where?"

"In a place I'm going to need you to trust me about." Her jaw tightened. "Ivo. Who owned this book? Where did it come from?"

"I told you, I've never—" But even as he said it, doubt crawled in. Six years in this shop. He'd catalogued everything. Hadn't he? He tried to picture the upper stacks, and found them suddenly vague, the memory of them soft-edged, like a coastline drawn from rumor rather than survey. When had he last actually *looked* up there?

"It wasn't here," he said slowly. "Or — it was always here, and I never saw it. Those aren't the same thing, and I hate that they aren't the same thing."

"That's how it works," Wendell murmured. She was staring at the final lines. "Things hide in plain sight from people who aren't meant to find them yet." Then she caught herself, as if she'd said too much, and pressed her lips together.

"Hid from me. Not from us. Not tonight." Ivo stood, paced to the window, looked out at the dying market, the lanterns swaying on their fish-hooks exactly as the book had promised. "It waited a hundred years for me to sweep a floor and put out two chairs. Don't you see what that means? Tonight mattered. *We*—" He couldn't finish. The tenderness of it, the audacity of it, that some ancient augur had looked down the long throat of time and seen this — him, her, the green lamp — it should have been the most romantic thing that ever happened to him.

Instead it felt like a trap snapping shut around something small and warm.

"Dearest wound," Wendell read aloud, very quietly. She said it the way you'd touch a bruise to check if it still hurt.

"You don't have to explain anything," Ivo said. "Whatever it thinks you are, whoever you really are — you're the woman who cheated at ring-toss and insulted my meridian projections and drank my terrible tea twice. That's what I know. That's what counts."

For a moment her composure cracked — not broke, cracked, like ice over moving water — and beneath it he glimpsed something vast and grieving that made his chest ache.

Then she straightened, and the crack sealed, and she said: "There's someone we need to talk to. Mother Quill. Tonight, Ivo. Before the sun comes up."

"Quill? My landlady's cousin sells charms from Quill's stall, everyone knows Mother Qui—" He stopped. Reconsidered the woman's knowing eyes, the way certain customers came to the back door after midnight, the whispers he'd always filed under *colorful neighborhood lore*. "Oh," he said. "Oh. *Magic.* You're saying magic, actual magic, outlawed magic—"

"I'm saying a hundred-year-old book just described our date," Wendell said, already pulling her coat tighter, already moving toward the door, "and ended with a crowned tree drinking a city's memories during an eclipse. Whatever you believe about the world, Ivo Senn, believe this: we have eleven days, and I am not letting anything else get taken from me."

She stood in his doorway with the lamplight behind her and the frost-market cold rushing in, and Ivo understood that the woman he'd hoped to build something with was carrying something enormous, and that the book had known that too.

He grabbed his coat, and the folio, and followed her into the dark.
