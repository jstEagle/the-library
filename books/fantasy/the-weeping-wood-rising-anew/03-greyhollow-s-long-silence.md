## 3. Greyhollow's Long Silence

The charm-sellers' underground did not have a door, because doors could be kicked in.

Ivo learned this the hard way, following Wendell down three flights of stairs behind a shuttered dyer's shop, through a corridor that smelled of lye and old rain, until she stopped before a wall of wet stone and pressed her palm flat against it. Nothing happened. She pressed again, harder, and murmured something under her breath — not a word so much as a habit of words, a shape her mouth made when it was asking permission.

"Quill's wards don't answer to knocking," Wendell said. "They answer to need. You have to want in like you mean it."

"I want in," Ivo said. "I've wanted in since a book told me my date was destiny."

"That's the spirit." She glanced at him sideways, and even now, with dread sitting between them like an uninvited third guest, there was a flicker of the woman he'd met at the night market — dry-humored, watchful, her laughter kept somewhere deep and locked. "Stand close. The ward reads intent better than faces. If we're touching, it can't tell whose need is whose."

He took her hand. Her fingers were cold. He catalogued that, the way he catalogued everything — coastlines, river bends, the small cold fact of a woman's hand — because cataloguing was how he kept the world from sliding.

The stone exhaled. It didn't open; it *forgot* to be a wall, and they stepped through into warmth and lamplight.

* * *

Mother Quill's hall had been a wine cellar once, then a smugglers' den, and now it was neither and both: low vaulted ceilings furred with candle smoke, shelves sagging under jars of bottled luck, bundles of dried rue hanging from hooks like sleeping bats. Charm-sellers worked at long trestle tables, knotting thread, whispering over copper pins, grinding things that glittered and things that stank. A child no older than ten sat cross-legged on a barrel, sorting buttons by the sound they made when dropped.

Every head turned when Wendell entered.

Not at Ivo — at *her*. And not with suspicion, exactly. With recognition. With relief. The way a room looks at someone who left and came back.

"You're late," said Mother Quill.

She was a small woman built like a knotted root, silver-streaked hair bound up in a scarf, spectacles pushed into it like a bird nesting. She stood at the far end of the hall stirring a cauldron of what appeared to be soup and possibly was not. She did not look up from it.

"The city was watching," Wendell said. "We took the long way."

"The city is always watching. That's why we're underground." Quill set down her ladle and finally looked at them, and her gaze went first to Wendell's face, then to Ivo, then — strangely — to their joined hands, which they hadn't let go of. Something moved across her expression too fast to name. "So. The folio found you."

"It found him," Wendell said. "I was just standing next to him when fate decided to be rude."

"Fate isn't rude, girl. Fate is punctual." Quill pulled off her spectacles and polished them on her scarf with the deliberateness of a woman buying time. "Sit. Both of you. Tam!" She snapped her fingers at the button-child, who materialized at her elbow with startling speed. "Bring the good tea. And the honey, not the stuff Brannick waters down."

They sat at a table tucked into an alcove behind a curtain of beads. The beads clicked softly whenever anyone passed, and Wendell realized, with a small cold shock, that each bead was a charm — dozens of them strung together, a curtain woven out of small protections. This was how these people lived. Layered in charms like scales. Hiding in plain sight beneath a city that had outlawed everything they were.

Tam brought tea in mismatched cups and lingered, openly staring at Wendell.

"She's the storm-caller," Tam whispered, not quietly enough, to another seller passing by.

"I'm *nothing*," Wendell said automatically, and hated how quickly the lie came, how worn smooth it was from use.

Quill waited until the tea was poured and the curtain of beads had stopped clicking. Then she folded her hands on the table — hands spotted with age and scarred with old burns, the hands of someone who had made ten thousand charms and drunk the cost of every one — and said:

"Tell me what the book said. Word for word. Words matter tonight."

Ivo recited it. He had clearly read it so many times the lines lived in his mouth now: *the cartographer and the exile shall break bread beneath the lantern moon, and the weeping wood rising anew shall claim its due.* He got to the sigil — the crowned tree dripping sap — and Quill closed her eyes.

"Three years," she said. "Three years I've been waiting for those words to walk through my door."

"You knew?" Wendell leaned forward. "You knew about the prophecy?"

"I knew about the Wood." Quill opened her eyes. They were pale grey, almost colorless, and very steady. "What do either of you actually know about the Weeping Wood? Not the nursery version. Not 'don't go near the trees or they'll drink your name.'"

"They take memories," Wendell said slowly. "Everyone knows that much. Travelers come back wrong. Hollow-eyed. Some don't come back at all."

"They come back *lighter*," Quill corrected. "That's the cruelty of it. You lose something heavy and you feel *better*. For a while. The Wood doesn't eat memories for hunger, girl. It eats them for company. It's lonely the way only something immortal can be lonely." She drank her tea, grimaced at it — Brannick had watered the honey after all — and continued. "And three years ago, it found a way to stop being lonely forever. It found a king."

The candle flames guttered, though there was no draft.

"The real King Aldous went hunting near the Wood's edge in the autumn three years past," Quill said. "He rode out with six guardsmen and came back with none. Or rather — *he* came back. What walked into Greyhollow wearing his face and his crown was not Aldous. It was root and sap and stolen memory shaped into a man. The Wood has been ruling us ever since. Through him. As him."

Ivo's cup rattled against its saucer. "The edicts. The memory-taxes. The—"

"—the census of recollections, yes. Every citizen required to record their family histories in the royal archive, 'for preservation.' Preservation." Quill laughed without any humor in it. "It's a larder. The Wood is stocking its pantry with our pasts, one archive-wagon at a time. Whole streets have forgotten their own dead since it began. There's a woman in Tanner's Row who lights a candle every seventh day and cannot tell you for whom."

Wendell's stomach turned over. She thought of the baker that morning, clutching his luck-knot, terrified of forgetting his daughter's face. She'd thought it was ordinary fear. Grief. The kind of fear that needed no magic to explain.

"And the eclipse," she said. "The book said the Wood would claim its due at the eclipse."

"The eclipse is when the veil between remembering and forgetting wears thin. When the Wood takes, it takes *big*." Quill set down her cup. "In nine days, if nothing is done, it will reach through that thin place and drink the city whole. Not one memory. All of them. Everyone in Greyhollow will wake the morning after and know only what the Wood leaves them. Names without faces. Lives without beginnings. A city of people who can't remember why they're grieving."

Silence held the alcove. Somewhere beyond the bead-curtain, the charm-sellers worked on, knotting luck against a doom most of them couldn't name.

"Why me?" Wendell asked. It came out rougher than she intended. "Why would the book name me? I'm nobody here. I sell knots and blessings and I haven't called so much as a gust of wind in three years. Why would a century-old prophecy have my *date* in it?"

Quill looked at her for a long moment. And Wendell saw it coming the way you see a wave coming — the stillness before it, the light going strange.

"Because it wasn't just the king the Wood took three years ago," Quill said gently. "It was everything that might threaten it. Every storm-caller in the realm. There were four of you that I knew of. One died mysteriously in the spring. One vanished from a locked room. One fled across the sea and hasn't written since." She reached across the table and covered Wendell's hand with her own scarred ones. "And one was exiled. Convicted of killing her own brother with a called storm. Stripped of her name, her standing, her right to practice. Cast out of the very city where the Wood's puppet holds court — far from the palace, far from the groves, far from anything that might remember what storms are *for*."

The words landed one by one, like stones dropped into still water.

"No," Wendell said. "No, that was — the tribunal, there were witnesses, I—"

"There were witnesses," Quill agreed. "Witnesses placed by the crown. Judges appointed by the crown. A trial scheduled two days after your brother died, before you could even stand upright from grief. Girl, I've been in this city sixty years and I know a rigged scale when I see one. Your exile wasn't justice. It was *gardening*. Someone was clearing the ground of every person who could call a storm — right around the time something in that forest learned to wear a king."

Wendell pulled her hand free. Stood up. The beads clicked wildly around her.

"You're saying I didn't—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the weakness, hated the hope more. Three years she had carried her brother's death like a stone sewn into her chest. Three years of penance, of trinkets and hiding, of never calling so much as a breeze because the last time she'd called weather, Tam's age, maybe younger, her brother had drowned in the flood of it. "You're telling me it was all arranged? That I've spent three years punishing myself for a murder that was *staged*?"

"I'm telling you the timing stinks of rot," Quill said. "I'm telling you that whoever wrote that folio wrote your name in it a hundred years before you were born, and that prophecies are not kind, but they are rarely *wrong*. And I'm telling you that if the Wood feared you enough to unmake your life, then you, Wendell Yarrow, are the most dangerous thing in this city — and it's time you started acting like it."

The candles flared. Every flame in the alcove stood up straight and tall, burning blue-white at the tips, and the air pressure shifted the way it does before lightning — that tasted-metal hush. Wendell felt it rise in her chest, the old thing, the terrible thing, answering her anger like a dog hearing its name, and she slammed it down, shoved it deep, wrapped it in the same iron discipline she'd worn for three years.

But she didn't get it down fast enough. Tam, in the doorway, was staring at the candles. At her.

"See?" Quill said softly, into the ringing quiet. "It remembers you, even if you won't remember it."

Ivo rose and stood beside her — not touching her, just *there*, solid, present, a fixed point on a map gone mad. "Then we need to know more," he said, and his voice was steadier than Wendell felt by half. "About the Wood. About what it wants. About what it was before it was — this."

Something flickered across Quill's face at that. *Before.* An old grief, briefly surfaced.

"Aye," she said. "Before. That's a longer story than tea allows. But you should hear it, because the thing wearing your king was not born wicked." She drained her cup and stood, joints cracking like green wood. "It was born a guardian. And we are the ones who drove it mad."

* * *

Later, much later, Wendell lay on a pallet in the corner of the workroom the sellers had given her — *given*, as though it had always been hers, as though she'd simply been late coming home — and listened to the sounds of the underground settling. Knots being tied by candlelight. Low voices. Tam humming a song about a fox and a ferryman.

Ivo sat nearby, sketching by lamplight, because he was the sort of man who processed catastrophe by drawing it. She watched his pencil move over the page: the arch of the cellar ceiling, the bead-curtain, her own face turned toward the dark.

"They meant it to be home," she said quietly.

He looked up.

"The exile. Whoever arranged it. They meant it to cut me off from everyone who could help me. Instead—" She gestured vaguely at the warm, smoky room, the strangers who had fed them and armed them with tea and truth. "Instead it led me here. To people who hide in walls and bottle luck and sort buttons by sound. To you."

Ivo considered this with the seriousness he gave everything. "Prophecies aren't kind," he quoted, "but they're rarely wrong. Maybe the same is true of exile."

She almost smiled. Then the ache surfaced — her brother's name rising in her throat like a tide, and with it the new, unbearable question that would not stop gnawing: *if they staged it, what really happened in that storm? Who was standing in that field, and what were they trying to bury?*

Nine days, Quill had said. Nine days until the eclipse. Nine days until the Wood drank the city's past.

And tomorrow, Wendell thought, staring at the candle until her eyes burned, tomorrow they would go to the Wood itself — and ask the trees what they remembered about her.

Outside, far above, Greyhollow slept under a sky already bruising toward the dark of the moon, and in the royal palace, something with roots where a heart should be dreamed of rain.
