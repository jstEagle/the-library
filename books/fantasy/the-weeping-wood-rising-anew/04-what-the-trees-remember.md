## 4. What the Trees Remember

The maps in my shop had never shown the Weeping Wood.

I'd drawn Greyhollow a hundred times over — its crooked streets, its drowned harbor, the ring of hills like cupped hands — and every survey ended at the tree line with a cartographer's confession: *terra incognita*. Here be nothing, said the blank space, though everyone knew there was something there. Something that made the king stop riding his own borders three years ago. Something that made the crown outlaw magic and then pretend it hadn't been afraid.

Now I walked toward that blank space with Wendell Yarrow at my shoulder, and I understood at last why no mapmaker ever came back to fill it in.

We'd left before dawn, taking the pilgrim road north through fog so thick it seemed less like weather than like the city exhaling us out. Mother Quill had given us instructions written in a cipher of tea stains on butcher paper: *Follow the birches until they stop being trees. Do not touch what drips. Do not answer any voice you recognize.* She'd pressed the paper into my hand and held my wrist a moment longer than necessary.

"The Wood takes the tender ones first," she'd said, looking not at Wendell but at me. "You look like a man who keeps his griefs polished."

I'd laughed it off. Cartographers are trained to believe that everything can be measured, and what can be measured can be survived.

The birches thinned around noon. That was the first wrongness — not that the forest thickened, but that it *thinned*, as if the trees themselves were reluctant witnesses, standing back from whatever happened deeper in. The ground went soft. The light went green-gold and slow, like honey poured through leaves. And then we heard it: water, where no river ran.

"It's not rain," Wendell said quietly. "It's coming from the branches."

She was right. Each tree wept from its highest twigs, fat drops gathering and falling with the patience of centuries. But the drops didn't splash. They hung in the air beneath the boughs, suspended, hundreds of them, thousands, each one catching the green light like a lens. Like eyes. Like something waiting to be looked into.

"Memories," Wendell breathed. "Mother Quill wasn't speaking in metaphor."

One drop drifted past her face — close enough that she flinched — and for half a heartbeat I saw inside it: a kitchen, steam rising off a pot, someone laughing. A stranger's ordinary joy, stolen and preserved like a fly in amber.

"Don't look too long," she said, and her voice had an edge I hadn't heard before. Not fear. Recognition. "It's how it feeds. You look, it looks back, and then it knows which door of you to knock on."

"And if it knocks?"

"Then you'd better hope you're holding something heavier than grief." She touched the satchel at her hip, where Mother Quill's charm-salt rattled — iron filings blessed backwards, salt cut with graveyard ash. Insurance, supposedly. Against me, I suspected, as much as against the Wood.

We walked on. The drops grew denser, drifting through the air like snow that had forgotten which way was down, and I kept my eyes fixed on the path, on Wendell's boots, on the compass in my hand whose needle spun in slow, drunken circles. North meant nothing here. The Wood had its own geography, and I began to suspect it was drawn in longing rather than distance.

Then I heard my mother call my name.

"Ivo."

Not loudly. Just clearly, the way she used to call up the stairs when supper was ready, the way she'd called across the market square the last morning I ever saw her alive. My whole body turned before my mind could object.

There, hanging between two black pines at the forest's edge, was a single drop larger than all the others. And inside it —

Inside it was the last day of her life.

I knew it instantly, the way you know your own handwriting. The gray morning light in the apothecary shop. Her sleeves rolled to the elbow, flour on one wrist because she'd baked before opening, always, even on the day she would die. She was grinding feverfew, and she paused, and she looked directly out of the memory at me — impossible, impossible — and smiled.

"You're growing into your father's shoulders," she said. "Come taste this. Tell me if it's too bitter."

*I know this day,* I thought wildly. *This is the day the plague carts came. This is six hours before—*

"Ivo." Wendell's hand closed on my arm.

But I was already leaning forward. The drop rose to meet me. Or I rose to meet it. The distinction stopped mattering. The green light folded around me like water closing over a stone, and I was *in* it — in the shop, smelling feverfew and bread and lamp oil, watching my mother's hands move with the surety of thirty years' practice. She was alive. She was humming. Outside the window, the city went about its business, unaware that in six hours she'd be carried out feet-first with a cloth over her face.

*Stay,* said the forest, in a voice that came from everywhere, from the roots under my feet and the sap in the walls. *Stay here. It doesn't have to end. We can hold this hour forever. We can hold every hour.*

And oh, I wanted to. That was the horror of it — not that the Wood forced anything, but that it simply *offered*. Every stolen memory hung in those branches like fruit, and the picking cost nothing but everything. I could live in this morning forever. I could learn the recipe of her feverfew tonic. I could hear her laugh again, properly, not the worn ghost of it my own mind had begun to distort. Three years of grief had rubbed her edges smooth; here she was whole, and sharp, and *mine*.

Somewhere far away, Wendell was shouting my name.

Somewhere far away, my hands were burning.

The charm-salt. I had a fistful of it clenched against my chest without remembering drawing it, and it bit like nettles, and through the sting I saw the truth of what I was doing: I was choosing a memory over a life. My mother had lived six more hours after this moment, hours of pain and fear I'd never witnessed, and the Wood was offering me the mercy of forgetting that — the mercy of amputation dressed as preservation. It wasn't keeping her alive. It was keeping me dead. Standing in this shop forever, tasting nothing, growing nothing, a map with one place on it and no roads leading anywhere else.

Memory could be a wound, I understood then. And wounds, left open, could be *weaponized* — by whoever found them first.

"No," I said aloud, and my voice cracked the vision like ice.

The shop shattered. The green light spat me out. I hit the forest floor on my knees, retching, salt still biting my palms, and Wendell was there, both hands on my shoulders, her storm-grey eyes wide and furious with fear.

"You were gone four minutes," she said. "Four minutes. Your eyes went white and you started walking toward the deep trees and you were *smiling*, Ivo. You were smiling like—"

"Like someone home," I finished hoarsely.

She didn't let go of me. Around us the weeping drops drifted, indifferent, patient. An ocean of other people's yesterdays, and somewhere in it — I realized with a lurch — hers. Somewhere in these branches hung a storm, and a brother, and the worst night of her exile. She was standing in a forest full of the thing she'd sworn never to touch again, and she hadn't looked at a single drop.

"Haven't you wanted to?" I asked. "To see him again? Even once?"

Her jaw tightened. When she spoke, each word came down like a nail. "Every hour since it happened. Which is exactly why I don't dare." She pulled me to my feet. "The Wood doesn't give back, Ivo. It lends, and the interest is you."

We stood a moment, breathing. Then she stiffened, head tilting, listening — and I heard it too. Beneath the dripping, beneath the wind, a sound like a great slow heartbeat rising from the heart of the Wood. And threaded through it, faint as smoke, a voice that was many voices: children, old men, kings, all saying the same thing in unison, soft and terrible as a lullaby sung by a crowd.

*Bring her closer.*

Wendell's hand found mine. Hers was cold.

"It knows who I am," she whispered. "It's been waiting for me."

Ahead, where no path had been a moment before, the trees drew apart — slowly, deliberately, like curtains — revealing a corridor of weeping pines leading down into a green darkness that pulsed with that vast, patient heart.

* * *

We did not go down it. Not yet.

We retreated instead to the ridge above the tree line, where the fog of morning had burned off and Greyhollow lay below us like a scatter of grey dice, small and mortal and forgettable. I spread my field kit on a flat stone and did the only thing I knew to do with terror: I mapped it. The edge of the Wood. The density of the drops. The corridor. My hand shook, but ink is honest work, and by the time the sun touched the hills I had the beginnings of the first true chart of the Weeping Wood ever made.

Wendell sat beside me, sharpening a knife she showed no sign of needing.

"There's a pattern in the drops," I said, showing her. "They're densest along the old royal road — the one that used to run to the palace. As if the Wood has been drinking from the city for years. From the *crown* especially."

She studied the sketch a long time. Then she said, very quietly: "Three years ago the king outlawed magic. Three years ago I was exiled. Three years ago the Wood took him." Her knuckles whitened on the knife. "Ivo, I don't think we came here to understand the Wood. I think we came here because it wanted us to come. Because it's been arranging things since before either of us was born."

Below us, in the gathering dusk, the forest wept on. And on the wind, barely, I heard it again — that many-voiced whisper, closer now, curling up the ridge like fog returning:

*Bring her closer, and you may keep your mother's morning. Bring her closer, and I will give you back every hour you've lost.*

I looked at Wendell. She was looking at the darkening city, and her eyes were full of storms.

"We need Mother Quill," she said. "Tonight. Before it starts making offers we're too tired to refuse."

I packed my maps with hands that wouldn't quite stop shaking, and together we turned our backs on the weeping trees — knowing, both of us, that the trees had already turned toward us first.
