## 5. The King Who Is Not a King

*Wendell*

The masque was a lie dressed in ten thousand candles.

Wendell felt it the moment she crossed the palace threshold, Ivo's arm warm under her gloved hand, both of them wearing faces borrowed from Mother Quill's strongest glamour-charms — hers a hawk-nosed merchant's widow, his a minor lord with wine-stained teeth. Around them, Greyhollow's nobility spun and glittered in the Grand Hall, feathers and gilt and lacquered smiles. And beneath it all, beneath the fiddlers and the fountain of sugared plums, something *green* breathed.

She'd hidden her power for six years. She knew how to be small. But the palace was not small, and neither was what lived in it.

"You feel that too," Ivo murmured against her ear. He smelled of cedar shavings and the cheap cologne he'd clearly overapplied for his disguise. It should have made her laugh. It only made her chest ache.

"Like walking into a cellar," she said. "Cold and growing things."

They moved through the crowd as she'd taught him to move — never stopping fully, always with purpose, two guests looking for the wine. The charm-sellers had spent three days preparing: Mother Quill had pressed a seeing-glass into Wendell's palm before they left, a lens of smoked crystal no bigger than a coin. *Look through it when you doubt your eyes,* she'd said. *The Wood can wear a face. It cannot lie to true glass.*

Above the hall, on the dais beneath the great stained sun of the royal window, sat the king.

King Aldous Vane held court at a masque the way a statue holds pigeons. He was beautiful in the way old coins are beautiful — worn smooth, all edges gone. His crown sat low over brows that never furrowed. When courtiers approached, he inclined his head at precisely the right angle, smiled at precisely the right moment, laughed — Wendell watched it happen — exactly four seconds after the joke landed.

"He's not laughing at anything," Ivo whispered. "He's laughing *on schedule.*"

Wendell drew the seeing-glass from her sleeve, cupped it behind her fan, and looked.

Through smoked crystal, the hall changed. The candlelight went thin and gray. The dancers became flickering shapes, half-erased. And the king —

The king was a husk.

She saw it plainly, horribly: the man-shape seated on the throne was mostly empty, like a glove with no hand in it, and through the gaps in him — the hollows of his eyes, the parted seam of his smile — she could see the green. Fine pale roots laced up his throat like a lady's necklace. Sap gleamed wetly along his jaw where skin should have been. Something vast and patient wound through him the way a hand winds through a puppet's strings, and though the roots were delicate as thread, they ran down, down, through the marble floor, through the foundations, out toward the dark hills east of the city.

Toward the Weeping Wood.

The glass trembled in her grip. She made herself lower it, made her face stay bored, a widow grown tired of masques. Her heart was slamming so hard she was sure the whole hall could hear it.

*I'm looking at the truth,* she thought. *Three years the city has bowed to a tree wearing its father.*

Ivo's hand found hers under the fan and squeezed once. He couldn't see what she'd seen — the glass answered only to her — but he read her face the way he read coastlines, patiently, finding the shape of things.

"Well?" he breathed.

"We tell Quill we were right." She turned them, slowly, toward the terrace doors. "And then we get out."

That was when the music stopped.

Not ended — *stopped*, mid-phrase, every violin choking silent at once. In the ringing hush, the king rose from his throne.

* * *

*Ivo*

Later — much later, when Ivo had time to be afraid properly instead of in installments — he would remember the sound the hollow king made when he stood. Not cloth, not armor. A creaking, like an old ship in weather, or a floorboard bearing weight it wasn't built for.

The court went to its knees in rippling waves. Ivo grabbed Wendell's elbow and knelt too, dragging her down beside him, his heart a fist against his ribs. From this angle, beneath the dais, he could see the hem of the king's robe — and the thing the robe hid. A single root, thick as his wrist, trailing from beneath the royal cloak and vanishing into a seam in the marble, glistening where the candlelight caught it.

*There,* he thought, sick with certainty. *That's the string.*

"My children," the king said.

His voice was warm. That was the worst part. It was a father's voice, a grandfather's voice, pitched to fill a nursery or a cathedral, and the court sighed with love for it. Only Ivo and Wendell knew there was nothing behind the warmth but sap and hunger.

"The eclipse comes," said the king, "in nine days."

A murmur through the kneeling court. Somewhere a woman began softly to cry — joy, Ivo thought numbly. They thought he was announcing a blessing.

"On that night, Greyhollow will give back what it has borrowed. Every debt of memory comes due. You will forget nothing, my children—" the smile widened, seams straining, "—you will forget *everything.*"

Applause. God help them, they applauded. The nobles rose weeping and clapping while their king stood above them, empty as a lantern with no flame, drinking in the sound.

Ivo's eyes burned. He thought of the map shop, the prophecy folio open on his desk, the ink that had known tonight before tonight existed. *The weeping wood rising anew.* He thought of his mother, whose face the trees at the forest's edge had shown him like bait on a hook. He had almost stepped off the path toward her. Some nights he still heard her voice calling from between the birches.

Beside him, Wendell had gone very still.

He knew that stillness. He'd seen it once before, in the shop, the night they learned what the Wood was — a stillness like the air before lightning, like the whole sky holding its breath. Her gloved fingers were pressed flat against the marble floor, and beneath them —

Beneath them, the floor was *humming.*

No one else felt it. The court was rising, turning back to wine and dance. But Ivo felt it through his own knees: a low vibration climbing out of the stone, answering something in Wendell like a tuning fork answers its twin. A faint blue light kindled between her fingers where they touched the ground. Static lifted the fine hairs on his arm.

"Wendell," he whispered. "*Wendell.*"

Her eyes were fixed on the king. On the root trailing from his robe. And her face — her disguised, borrowed face — had cracked open with something terrible and bright, grief and fury fused into a single white heat.

"It's *him*," she breathed. "It's been him the whole time. Three years of that thing smiling down on us, and every law, every burning, every exile—" Her voice shook. The blue light spread, crawling up her wrist beneath the silk of her glove. "Every person they took. It signed the orders, Ivo. With *his* hand."

The nearest chandelier guttered. Just one candle, just a flutter — but the fiddler glanced up, and a footman turned, and across the hall the king's head began, slowly, smoothly, to rotate toward them.

* * *

*Wendell*

She had sworn an oath in blood and rain: *never again.* Six years she had kept it. Six years of luck-knots and warming-stones, of making herself a woman who sold small comforts because she could not trust herself with large truths.

Now the storm woke in her bones without permission.

It came up through the palace stone — the deep roots of the Wood threading under the city, carrying water and memory and old wild voltage — and it found her the way a struck bell finds its note. She hadn't called it. She hadn't wanted it. But her magic recognized the Wood the way she might recognize, across a crowded room, the voice of someone she'd tried her whole life to forget.

The chandelier flames bent sideways. The blue light was past her elbow now, and she could not make it stop, and the king's blank beautiful face was turning toward her like a compass needle finding north.

*Ivo's hand closed around her wrist.*

"Look at me." His voice was low and steady, a rope thrown across a gap. "Not the king. Me. Tell me about the night market."

"What?"

"Tell me. Now."

"Ivo, the king is *looking* — "

"*Tell me about the night market.*"

And because his voice did not shake, hers didn't either. "You bought me roasted chestnuts and burned your tongue," she said. "You pretended you didn't. There was a fiddler playing 'The Miller's Lament' badly and you tipped him anyway. You said — " the light crept backward, dimming, " — you said you'd never seen anyone haggle with such bad grace as me, selling luck I didn't believe in."

"I meant it," Ivo said. "The best luck in that market was already sold. To me."

The storm sank. The candles straightened. Wendell dragged air into lungs that had forgotten how, and when she dared lift her gaze to the dais, the king had turned away — but slower than before, reluctant, the way a man turns from a window.

He knows, she thought. He doesn't know *what*, but he knows *something*. Something in this hall hums at his frequency.

They left during the next dance, unhurried, a widow and a lord in search of fresh air, and no one stopped them, and Wendell hated how easy it was. Hated that she could walk out of the house of the thing that had orchestrated her ruin while half the city starved on outlawed magic. Hated that hiding had become so practiced it fit her like her own skin.

In the terrace shadows beyond the doors, cold air hit her face and she finally let herself shake.

"I can't do this anymore, Ivo."

"Do what? The glamour? Because honestly the nose — "

"*This.*" She pulled off her glove. Her fingertips still crackled faintly blue, sparks dying like fireflies. "Hiding. Shrinking. Selling trinkets to people who need real charms while that *thing* plans to eat everything they are." Her voice broke and she let it. "Six years I've told myself staying small was penance. But it isn't penance. Penance would be standing up. Penance would be *paying.*"

Ivo was quiet a long moment. Below the terrace, the palace gardens lay silvered and still — and threaded through their beds, she saw now, everywhere, pale white roots surfacing like veins beneath skin. The Wood was already here. It had been here for years, drinking the city in by inches.

"When we get back," Ivo said finally, "Quill needs to hear everything. And then—" he hesitated, and she watched him choose honesty over comfort, which was the thing she loved most in him, "—then you need to decide what you're willing to give, Wendell. Because that thing in there doesn't negotiate. It *trades.*"

She looked down at her sparking hands, at the storm sleeping in them, patient as grief.

"I know," she said. "I think it's already chosen what to ask."

Far off to the east, beyond the walls, beyond the rooftops, the Weeping Wood stood black against the stars. And as she watched, every tree in it seemed to lean — gently, terribly, attentively — toward the palace.

Toward *her.*
