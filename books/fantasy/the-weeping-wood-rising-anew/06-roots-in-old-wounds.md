## 6. Roots in Old Wounds

Mother Quill had buried three names in her life — her husband, her daughter, and the woman she'd been before Greyhollow made her a smuggler of small magics — and she had learned that grief, like ink, never fully dries. It only sets. So when Wendell Yarrow and Ivo Senn came down the crooked stair into the Undermarket that night, rain still beaded on their shoulders and prophecy in their eyes, she recognized the look before either of them spoke. She had worn it herself, forty years ago, standing in a burning grove.

"You knew," Wendell said. It wasn't a question. The girl had storm under her skin; Quill could feel it prickling the air like the charge before lightning, could see it in the way the candle flames leaned toward her as if she were the wind's own mother. "The book, the king, the Wood. You knew what was coming."

Quill poured three cups of willowbark tea instead of answering. The Undermarket smelled of tallow and dried lavender, of charm-sellers' wares stacked in crates: luck-knots and hush-bells, memory-moss pressed between pages of hymnals, love-charms that worked only if both parties already suspected the truth. Her people moved among them quietly, pretending to inventory stock they all understood might soon be evidence.

"I knew the Wood had gone quiet," Quill said at last. "Quiet like a held breath. That is worse than loud." She set the cups down. "Sit. Both of you. What I have to tell you is older than your griefs, and it will need somewhere to land."

Ivo sat. Wendell remained standing, arms crossed, one thumb worrying the knuckle of the other hand — a tell, Quill noted, of someone who had spent years keeping her hands from doing what they wanted to do.

"Three years ago," Quill began, "the same year you were exiled from the Stormreach coast, the king of Greyhollow walked into the Weeping Wood at midsummer and did not walk out. The court announced a pilgrimage. A retreat for prayer. The queen — may her ghost forgive the lie — went along with it because she was given no choice and because the thing wearing her husband's face kissed their children goodnight exactly as he always had." She watched Wendell's breath catch. "Yes. You were exiled the same season. You did not imagine the coincidence. There are none in this city. There never were."

"The crown arranged it," Ivo said slowly. He was a mapmaker; Quill could see him drawing lines in his head, connecting coasts. "The exile. They sent her here — to the city at the edge of the Wood — deliberately."

"They sent a storm-caller to a forest that eats memories," Quill said. "Or perhaps they sent bait. I have spent three years deciding which, and I still don't know. The old council feared magic so much they outlawed it, burned it, drowned its practitioners in the harbor. But fear is a strange kind of reverence. When the eclipse was foretold — the true eclipse, the one coming in nine days — someone in that palace understood what would rise hungry enough to use it. And someone decided a weapon should be standing nearby when it did."

Wendell's cup cracked in her hand. Not thrown — simply crushed, the way ice cracks on a warm pond. Tea spread across the table like a small dark flood, and the candle flames stood up straight, quivering.

"I didn't ask to be a weapon," Wendell said.

"No," Quill agreed gently. "No one ever does. That is how weapons are made."

* * *

She took them deeper, past the charm-crates, down a corridor where the walls wept mineral stains in long brown streaks, into the room the charm-sellers called the Rootcellar. It had been a wine cellar once, then a chapel, then nothing. Now its far wall was covered in paper — hundreds of sheets, pinned and pasted, maps and fragments and copies of copies, all concerning the Weeping Wood.

Ivo stopped dead. "These are mine."

"Some are," Quill said. "Your grandfather's, mostly. Your family has charted the Wood's edge for four generations. Did you never wonder why a cartographer's line of work kept them poor? The crown paid them to keep mapping a forest no one was allowed to enter. To keep watching it. Your grandfather noticed something, near the end. Look at his marginalia."

Ivo crossed the room like a man walking into water. His finger found a note in faded sepia: *The trees lean away from the palace road now. As if ashamed.*

"He thought the Wood was dying," Ivo said quietly.

"He thought the Wood was grieving. He was more right than he knew." Quill let the silence hold a moment. Then she told them.

* * *

"Before Greyhollow had its name, there was a grove where the river bends north. The oldest trees in living memory, and older than memory — that is not a figure of speech, girl, so listen. The spirit that lived in that grove was called the Warden. It kept things. That was its nature and its joy. It kept the river's course and the salmon's return. It kept the paths safe for travelers. And above all, it kept remembering. Every person who slept beneath its branches left a little of themselves behind — not stolen, given, the way you leave a coin at a shrine. The Warden held those memories the way soil holds rain. Farmers swore their grandmothers' recipes to it. Lovers swore their vows. The dying walked out to the grove so that someone, something, would remember they had been here at all."

Wendell had finally sat. Her hands lay open in her lap, empty of everything. "And then?"

"And then the crown needed a treasury, and timber, and above all obedience. Magic was declared an abomination — do you know why? Because the Warden would not kneel. The priests said a power that answered to no crown could answer to no god. They cut the grove in a single week. Two hundred trees. The oldest living thing on this coast, ringed and felled while it screamed in a language half the city could still understand, back then." Quill's voice stayed level; she had practiced this. "They salted the stumps. They built the first prison from its wood. And the Warden — what was left of it, which was root and grief and the terrible weight of every memory ever entrusted to it — went into the deep forest and did not die. Dying requires letting go. And it had been made of holding for a thousand years."

"So it holds," Ivo whispered. "That's what the trees drip. All those memories—"

"It cannot stop. It is a wound that hoards. Every mind it touches, it keeps a piece of, because keeping is the only thing left in it. It filled itself with other people's pasts the way a starving man fills himself with stones. And now it has found a bigger hollow to fill — a king, an entire court, and soon, if the eclipse comes and it rises fully, a whole city's remembering. Greyhollow would go on breathing, going to market, marrying and burying, with no idea who any of them had ever been." Quill looked from one young face to the other. "It is not evil. I need you to hear me. It is mad the way a mother is mad whose child was taken. Madness is only love with nowhere to go."

Wendell was crying without noise, the tears simply present, as though they had been waiting in line. "Then it can heal."

"Wendell—" Ivo began.

"It can *heal*. If it's a wound, wounds close. If it's grief, grief — " her voice broke and rebuilt itself, storm-gray, "grief can be carried by more than one person. We don't kill it. We can't kill it. Everything it holds, everyone it took — killing it kills them twice."

"Or," said a voice from the stair, "we burn it before it burns us."

Dessa stood at the bottom step, Quill's fiercest runner, a hush-bell still swinging from her belt where she'd come in fast and quiet. Behind her crowded others — Tomlin with his luck-knots, gray-haired Sefa, the twins. Word traveled fast underground.

"You weren't there forty years ago," Dessa said, coming forward. "You didn't watch them drag the memory-takers out of their houses. Now you want us to feel sorry for the thing that's wearing our king like a glove and eating this city from the inside? It took Marisol's brother last spring. Walked him out of his own wedding. It isn't a wound, Mother. It's a mouth."

"It was a guardian before it was a grave," Quill said, rising, and felt every one of her seventy years arrive at once. "And if we answer a thousand years of cruelty with a bonfire, then the crown wins after all. It will have taught us its lesson perfectly."

"Compassion doesn't un-eat anyone!" Tomlin shouted, and the room fractured — voices overlapping, Sefa pleading for caution, one of the twins saying the word *eclipse* like a curse, Dessa's hand on her knife not as threat but as habit. Wendell stood apart in the middle of it, very still, and Quill saw that the girl had stopped listening to the argument. She was listening underneath it.

When the shouting wore itself into sulking silence, Wendell spoke.

"The trade," she said. "It offered me a trade. My worst memory for the king. I dreamed it two nights ago and told myself it was a dream." Her eyes found Quill's. "It wasn't asking to take. It was asking to *trade*. Why would a thing that only takes offer anything back?"

Quill's heart turned over slowly, like a key in an ancient lock. "Because part of it remembers being a guardian," she said. "And guardians keep bargains."

"Then it can be reached." Wendell's hands had begun, faintly, to glow — static crawling along her knuckles like summer lightning trapped under skin, the first Quill had ever seen her fail to hide it. "Nine days. The eclipse. I'll call the storm it's been starving for — a storm-caller's whole sky, everything I swore off — and I'll give it what it really wants. Not a scrap of my memory. An ending. Someone to witness what was done to it, and grieve it, and mean it."

"You'll burn yourself out," Ivo said hoarsely. "Storm-calling nearly killed you once—"

"It killed my brother," Wendell said, flat and terrible. "Nothing left in me can be frightened of it."

The room held its breath. Dessa's knife-hand loosened. Somewhere above them, through stone and earth, the city bells rang the midnight hour, and Quill thought she heard — beneath the bronze, beneath the rain beginning again on the streets — a low creaking groan from the direction of the northern woods. Like a great tree leaning. Like something vast turning over in its sleep to listen.

"It heard her," Sefa whispered. "Saints preserve us. It *heard* her."

Quill looked at the girl with lightning in her knuckles and a dead man's name she hadn't yet spoken aloud, and understood that the choice she had dreaded for forty years had finally arrived wearing a young woman's stubborn face.

"Then we have nine days," Mother Quill said, "to teach a mad god how to be forgiven — or die trying. Get me every memory-moss charm in the city. We're going to need to show it what it used to be."
