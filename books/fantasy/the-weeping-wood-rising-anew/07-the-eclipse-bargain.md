## 7. The Eclipse Bargain

Sleep did not come gently that night. It came like a hand closing around her ankle.

Wendell had gone to bed in the loft above Mother Quill's charm-house with Ivo's voice still in her ears — *don't do anything rash, don't go near the Wood alone* — and the taste of ink and candle smoke on her tongue. The others slept around her: Sable curled against the wall with her knife-hand under the pillow, Fen breathing in the slow rhythm of a man who could sleep through a siege. Somewhere below, the wards hummed their low, patient note.

The dream took her before she'd finished a single thought.

She was standing barefoot in the Weeping Wood.

Not at its edge this time — not the trembling boundary where Ivo had nearly drowned in his mother's last day — but deep inside it, where the trees grew so close together that the sky was only a rumor of grey between black branches. The ground was soft as flesh. The air tasted of rain that hadn't fallen yet, and of something older underneath it: rot and honey, grief kept so long it had turned sweet.

The trees were weeping. They were always weeping. Sap ran down the bark in slow amber threads, and each drop held something — she could see them now, could see *into* them the way you see into a struck bell. A woman's wedding day. A boy learning his father's trade. A city's whole history, dripping from ten thousand branches, pooled ankle-deep around her feet.

"Take off your shoes," said the Wood.

Its voice came from everywhere. It was not cruel, exactly. It was vast and tired and hungry the way a starving animal is not cruel — hunger is simply what it is made of.

"I'm dreaming," Wendell said.

"You are *visiting*. There's a difference, storm-caller. Dreams are yours. This is mine." The sap around her ankles stirred, warm as bathwater. "You've come to my edge before. You stood there while your cartographer wept for his mother. You wanted to step in. Don't pretend otherwise. I felt the wanting."

Her throat tightened. She had wanted to. That was the horror of the Wood, the thing Mother Quill had tried to make them understand: it didn't take memories by force. It *offered*. It showed you your dead and let you choose to drown.

"What do you want?"

"Ah." The trees leaned in. The whole forest leaned in, a slow groaning creak, ten thousand tons of wood bending toward one small woman standing in its memory-pools. "That is the question, isn't it. What does anything want? To be full."

A branch descended before her, unhurried, ceremonial. At its tip hung a single drop of sap, larger than any other, glowing faintly gold — and inside it she saw the king. The real one. Younger than she'd expected, hollow-cheeked, sitting in a cell made of woven roots, alive, his eyes open and fixed on nothing. Three years of nothing.

"The eclipse comes tomorrow night," the Wood said. "When the moon dies, I will drink Greyhollow. Every memory in it. Every name, every grudge, every lullaby. The city will wake the next morning with no past at all, and I will finally stop being empty, and it will be beautiful, and no one will even know what they lost." A pause, long enough for the sap to drip somewhere far away. "Unless."

"Unless," Wendell repeated. Her heart was doing something strange — not racing, but slowing, going heavy, the way it did before a storm broke. Some part of her had known this was coming since the night Ivo opened that folio. Fate didn't drag you to the table. It set the table and waited for you to sit.

"Here is my offer, storm-caller. Take back your king. I will release him — truly release him, root from bone — and I will turn from the city. Tomorrow night I will sleep instead of feed, and Greyhollow will keep its past, every scrap of it, even the ugly parts. Especially the ugly ones." The golden drop swayed. "In exchange, you give me one memory."

"Which memory."

The forest went very quiet. Even the dripping stopped.

"You know which one."

* * *

She woke with salt on her lips and her hands full of blanket, gripping it like rigging.

The storm. Of course it was the storm. There had never been another candidate; the Wood had simply been polite about naming it.

She lay in the dark and let the memory come because refusing to look at it now felt like cowardice, and if she was going to trade away the worst thing she owned, she would at least hold it properly first.

Her brother Tam. Nineteen years old, laughing, standing in the wheat field behind their father's house with his arms spread like he was greeting an old friend, because he had never once been afraid of her power — he'd thought it was *wonderful*, he'd begged her to show him, and so she had, that one time, that single indulgence, calling up a little thunder just to watch his face light —

— and the sky had answered too eagerly. The sky had always loved her too much. It came down on the field like a judgment, and she remembered the smell afterward, and she remembered that she had called it, that the lightning had used her hands, and she remembered her father's face at the funeral, and the tribunal, and the word *exile* spoken in a courtroom where everyone looked at her like weather itself.

Three years ago. The same year the Wood had taken the king.

She sat up slowly in the dark. That coincidence had been nagging at her for days, and now, with the Wood's voice still ringing in her skull, it stopped being a coincidence and became a shape. Her exile. Its theft. The same season, the same wound. Mother Quill had hinted at it — *the crown outlawed magic and burned its grove, girl, and everything since has been fallout* — but Wendell had not let herself follow the thread all the way down.

If she gave up the memory, she would forget Tam entirely. Not just the storm — *him*. His laugh. The way he'd chipped his front tooth falling out of the pear tree. The fact that she had ever had a brother at all. The Wood would take the guilt, yes, and the grief, and the reason she flinched when it rained — and it would take the love too, because love and guilt lived in the same room in her chest and shared a wall, and you couldn't evict one without the other hearing the noise.

And she would become someone who had never done it. Someone clean. Someone who hadn't killed anyone, hadn't been exiled, hadn't spent three years selling luck-knots to frightened bakers while her hands itched with stolen sky.

Penance or peace. The Wood was offering her both, and they were the same price.

* * *

Ivo found her on the roof at dawn.

He climbed the ladder with two cups of chicory and the particular careful expression of a man who has noticed something wrong and decided not to lead with it. He handed her a cup and sat beside her on the warm shingles, shoulder almost touching hers, and for a while they watched Greyhollow wake below — chimney smoke, cart wheels, the baker already lighting his ovens, the one whose fear she'd soothed with a luck-knot a lifetime ago, back when the biggest thing in her life was whether Ivo Senn would kiss her at the end of the night market.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"No."

"The dream?" He said it lightly, but his knuckles whitened around the cup. He'd been to the Wood's edge. He knew how it knocked.

"It offered me a trade." She told him plainly, because he deserved plainness: the king, the eclipse, the city spared. "One memory. Mine to choose." Her voice didn't shake until the end, and then it shook everywhere. "There's only one it wants."

Ivo set his cup down very carefully, as though it might detonate.

"No," he said.

"Ivo —"

"*No.*" He turned to face her fully, and she saw that he hadn't slept either, that there were shadows under his eyes shaped like maps of places neither of them wanted to go. "Wendell. Listen to me. I have watched that forest work. I stood at its edge for ten minutes and it gave me my mother — gave me her *last day*, her voice, the exact weight of her hand — and I would have walked in. I know I would have, because some part of me is still standing there wanting to. That's what it does. It doesn't bargain. It doesn't need to bargain. It finds the wound and it pours itself into it and calls the drowning a gift."

"This isn't that."

"Isn't it? Think about what you're actually trading." His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "You'd give it Tam. All of him. You wouldn't just lose the storm, Wendell, you'd lose — you'd lose the person who made you brave enough to be what you are. You told me about him. On our date. Before any of this. You told me he was the only one who wasn't afraid of you." His eyes were bright and furious and terrified. "You don't get to erase the only person who ever loved the truest thing about you. Not even to save a king. *Especially* not to save a king."

"And if I don't?" She gestured at the city below, at every rooftop and every sleeping stranger. "Tomorrow night it drinks all of it. Every mother. Every wedding day. Everything you mapped, everything Greyhollow ever was — gone, and no one left who knows it was gone. You saw what losing one memory did to you at the tree line. Now imagine a whole city."

"So we find another way! Mother Quill is digging into the old records, Fen knows people in the palace —"

"We don't have another way. We have one night, Ivo." She heard her own voice go flat and calm, the voice she used with customers when the trinkets were lies and she needed them believed. "It offered fairly. That's the worst part. It offered *fairly*, and I think — I think it wants me to say yes. I think it's been waiting three years for me specifically, because I'm the one person in this city whose worst memory is also a weapon. My guilt is worth more to it than anyone else's. Because I'm a storm-caller. Because my grief is the same kind of magic it used to be."

Ivo was silent for a long moment. Below them, Greyhollow went on being innocent — a dog barking, a door slamming, the ordinary music of a place that didn't know it was mortgaged.

"If you do this," he said finally, very quietly, "you won't know me. You won't know why you're crying when someone says his name. You'll be a stranger wearing your own face, and I'll have to introduce myself to you every morning for the rest of my life." He took her hands. They were cold; his were shaking. "So here is what I'm asking. Not begging. Asking. Give me tonight. Let us try to unbind the king *with* you — all of you, memory intact — and if the eclipse comes and there's truly no other road, then..." His jaw worked. "Then we'll stand at that tree line together, and I'll carry the remembering. Me, Quill, Sable, Fen, all of us. You don't have to hold him alone. But don't *hand him over*. Please. Don't give that forest the satisfaction."

She looked at their hands, tangled together on the shingles, and felt the terrible arithmetic of it turning over and over: one brother against one hundred thousand strangers, one grief against a city's whole past, penance against peace, and no version of the sum that didn't cost everything she was.

"I haven't said yes," she said.

"You haven't said no."

"I'm going to see it again tonight. Before the eclipse. It asked me to come to the tree line at moonrise and answer." She swallowed. "Ivo — when I call the storm tomorrow, if I call it — I need you to do something for me."

"Anything."

"Hold onto me. Whatever happens, however loud it gets — don't let the sky take me back. It loves me too much. It always has." She met his eyes, and inside her chest something vast turned over in its sleep and opened one eye. "Because I think I'm going to have to say yes *and* fight it. And I don't know yet which one of those I am."

Far to the north, past the rooftops, past the walls, the Weeping Wood stood silent under the fading stars — waiting, patient as roots, for the moon to die.
