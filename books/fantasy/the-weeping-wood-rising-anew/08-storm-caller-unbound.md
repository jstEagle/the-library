## 8. Storm-Caller, Unbound

**Wendell**

The eclipse began as a bite taken out of the sun.

Wendell stood at the edge of the Weeping Wood where the trees leaned back from a clearing of blackened earth — the old grove, burned three years ago, its stumps still weeping sap like open eyes. Above her, the sky dimmed from gold to bronze to the color of old bruises. The whole city of Greyhollow held its breath behind her, ten thousand windows going dark as people shuttered themselves against whatever came next.

The Wood was already singing.

It wasn't sound, exactly. It was pressure, a hum in the marrow, in the water of her blood. *Give it to me,* the song said without words. *Your worst hour. I will carry it so gently. I have carried thousands.*

Behind the treeline, at the heart of the Wood, something vast shifted — and through the gaps in the canopy she could see it: the king's body, suspended in a lattice of roots like a moth pinned in amber, his crown grown into his skull, sap running gold down his slack face.

*Ivo,* she thought, and her chest clenched around the name like a fist around a knife.

"You don't have to do this."

He was beside her. He had been beside her for an hour, and every minute of it he had been talking — quietly, relentlessly — because they both knew that if he stopped, she might hear the song too clearly.

"I know," she said.

"Mother Quill is still trying to find another way."

"There isn't one." She turned to look at him, and the dying light made his face strange — half shadow, half ember. "The Wood only opens its hands for a trade. It told me what it wants. There's no bargaining with grief, Ivo. You taught me that when you showed me your mother's day in those trees."

His jaw worked. "Then let me say it again," he said, "so you remember what you're trading away. Not just the storm. *Him.* Your brother. Every—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. "Don't list him. If you list him, I'll start holding on, and if I hold on, the roots win, and the king dies, and the city forgets everything it ever loved. That's the arithmetic. I hate the arithmetic, but it doesn't stop being true because I hate it."

The eclipse deepened. The last sliver of sun went out, and the corona bloomed — a ring of white fire in a black sky, cold and beautiful and wrong. The Wood's song rose to a crescendo. In the clearing, the ground split, and roots came up out of the scorched earth like fingers uncurling, reaching for her, patient, almost tender.

*Come,* sang the Wood. *Come and be empty with me.*

Wendell stepped into the clearing.

"Wendell—" Ivo caught her wrist.

She looked back at him. And because she could not bear for the last thing he said to her — the last thing the woman who walked out of this clearing would remember of this night — to be a warning, she smiled.

"When I come back," she said, "I'm going to be someone who doesn't know why you look at me like that. So tell me anyway. Tell me everything. Even after. Promise me."

"I promise," he said, and his voice broke clean in half. "I'll tell you until you're sick of it."

"Good." She squeezed his hand once, hard, memorizing the shape of it — no. Not memorizing. Letting go of even that, before it could be taken. "Hold my anchor, cartographer. Don't let me drift."

And she let the storm in.

* * *

**Ivo**

He had seen her magic exactly once, three years ago, secondhand — the stories that crossed the border with her name attached, whispered in charm-sellers' kitchens: *the Yarrow girl called down a storm that killed her own brother.* He had imagined lightning, thunder, spectacle.

What actually happened was silence.

Wendell stood in the center of the clearing with her arms at her sides and her head bowed, and the air around her simply *emptied*. Sound drained out of the world. The eclipse-light bent toward her. Every hair on Ivo's arms lifted, and his teeth ached, and the roots reaching for her stopped — recoiled, actually, the way a hand pulls back from a stove — because they recognized what she was carrying.

Not a storm. The *storm*. The one she had swallowed whole and carried inside herself for three years like a stone in her belly, feeding it nothing, letting it grow.

"Oh," Ivo breathed, to no one. "Oh, Wendell."

She raised her head. Her eyes were white as the corona.

The first thunderclap did not come from the sky. It came from *her* — a crack that flattened the grass in a hundred-yard ring and sent the nearest trees shuddering, raining stolen memories down like leaves. He saw them fall: a child's birthday, a wedding dance, a sailor's last sight of home, glinting as they dropped and sank into the soil.

The roots surged. Dozens of them, thick as temple pillars, rising out of the burnt grove to wrap her — and the storm met them. Lightning sheeted off her skin in branching rivers, and where it struck, roots charred and split, screaming a sound Ivo felt in his fillings. But there were always more. The Wood was vast, and it was grieving, and grief does not tire.

Wendell staggered. Her knees buckled. The white in her eyes guttered —

—and Ivo understood, with the terrible clarity of a man watching the person he loves drown, that she was losing herself not to the Wood but to her own fury. The storm wanted the brother-killer dead. The storm wanted *everything* dead.

He ran into the clearing.

Roots whipped past him close enough to tear his sleeve. The air tasted of copper and ozone. He planted himself six feet from her, inside the ring of light, where any sane person would have run the other way, and he shouted over the roaring:

"WENDELL YARROW! THE NIGHT MARKET ON CANDLEMAS LANE!"

Her head turned. The white eyes fixed on him. Lightning crawled up his arm and he didn't flinch, because flinching would break the thread.

"You wore your green coat!" he bellowed. "You haggled a luck-knot down from four coppers to two and then paid four anyway, because the baker looked frightened! You said fear is the one thing worth overpaying for!"

The storm stuttered. The lightning slowed, circling her instead of striking.

"You bought roasted chestnuts and burned your tongue and pretended you didn't! I counted — you ate nine, I ate eleven, and you stole two of mine when you thought I wasn't looking!"

A root lashed across his shoulder and he grunted, spun, kept talking. Blood ran warm down his arm. The world was going strange and bright at the edges.

"You laughed at my map of the harbor — you said no one needs a map of somewhere they've never left! And I said everyone's leaving somewhere all the time, even standing still! And you went quiet, and you looked at me — Wendell, you looked at me like I'd handed you something you didn't have words for!"

The storm was changing. He could feel it — the fury still there, but folding itself now around his voice like a vine around a trellis. She was anchoring to him. To *tonight*. To the small human hours before prophecy got its teeth into them.

"The folio was on the counter between us," he shouted, voice shredding. "Dust motes in the lamplight! Your thumb had ink on it from the charts! When you read the sigil of the royal house you went pale as paper, and I — I reached for your hand before I even knew why, and you let me hold it, and that was the moment, that was the moment I knew—"

He choked. Said it anyway.

"—that I would follow you into anything. Even this. Especially this."

* * *

**Wendell**

Somewhere above the storm, someone was calling her name.

No — not her name. A night. Candlemas Lane. Green wool. Chestnuts, nine and eleven and two stolen. A lamp, dust motes, ink on her thumb. A hand holding hers.

She clung to each detail like a drowning woman to planks. The storm raged through her, huge and howling, full of rain and the taste of her brother's name — and Ivo's voice threaded through it, small and stubborn and *present*, and she built herself along that thread plank by plank.

*Here*, the details said. *This is real. This happened tonight. This is yours.*

The Wood shrieked beneath her — a vast, hollow grief, a guardian with a hole where its heart should be, hoarding ten thousand memories and still starving. She pitied it. Gods help her, even now, she pitied it. But pity was not surrender.

The roots had the king. The roots were coming for her. And the crown — the crown of Greyhollow, iron and oak, fused to a living man's skull by three years of patient sap — sat at the center of it all like a spider in its web.

*TRADE*, the Wood howled, in a voice that shook ash from the burnt stumps. *GIVE WHAT WAS PROMISED.*

"I promised," Wendell said, and her voice was thunder made articulate. "I keep my promises."

She raised both hands to the eclipsed sky and did the thing she had sworn never to do again: she called the storm *down*.

It answered like a loyal dog finally unleashed. The corona flared; the clouds spiraled; and lightning came down in a single column, white-out, world-ending, striking the crown of Greyhollow where the roots gripped it.

The scream was not the Wood's. It was the roots' — burning, unwinding, retreating from the iron band in smoking coils. Sap boiled gold. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a man's voice gasped its first free breath in three years:

"...cold... it's so cold..."

"Hold on, Your Majesty," Wendell snarled through the roar. "Help is coming. Mother Quill — *now!*"

From the treeline, the charm-sellers poured into the clearing with their trinkets blazing — luck-knots and truth-bells and wards stitched from common thread, humble magics suddenly magnificent, cutting the king free root by root while Ivo's voice went on and on, an inventory of one perfect ordinary evening, and Wendell held the storm like a bow drawn to the breaking point.

The last root tore loose. The king fell into waiting arms. The eclipse reached totality — and the Wood, cheated of its trade, its puppet, its purpose, gathered itself for one final surge—

—and Wendell opened her hands and let the storm go. All of it. Upward, harmless, spent, a fountain of light against the black sun.

Silence rushed back in like water into a footprint.

She swayed. The corona was fading; dawn-colored light bled at the sky's rim. Ivo caught her before her knees gave out, and she was crying, though she couldn't have said — even then, even in the last moment she possessed it — couldn't have named what she was mourning.

"It's done," he whispered into her hair. "You did it. You're here. You're *here*."

"Yes," she said slowly. The word felt far away. Something in her felt far away — a room in her mind standing empty, door swinging, and she could not remember what had been kept in it. Only that it had mattered. Only that it had cost everything.

"Ivo?" Her voice sounded young. Frightened. "Why am I crying?"

Above them, in the healing dark, the Weeping Wood began — very softly — to drip.
