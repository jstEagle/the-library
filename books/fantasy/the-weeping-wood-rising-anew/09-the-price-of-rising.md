## 9. The Price of Rising

The rain stopped the way a held breath is finally released.

Wendell felt it go — the storm sliding out of her bones like water through open fingers, leaving her hollowed, ringing, kneeling in the mud of the palace square with Ivo's voice still in her ears. *You bought a luck-knot from a woman with ink on her fingers. You laughed at the name of her cat.* He had recited their date like a litany, like rope thrown to a drowning woman, and she had held to every word while the sky tore itself apart around her.

Now the clouds were unraveling, and through the gaps the eclipse was already dying, the moon's dark bite easing off the sun's face. The light that came down was thin and strange, like light seen underwater.

At the center of the square, where the masque had become a massacre of roots and splintered masks, the king lay crumpled among the wreckage of his own crown. The golden circlet had split clean in two, sap-blackened, and from its shattered band curled pale tendrils — root-fibers, fine as hair — retreating now, withdrawing, slithering back toward the tree line beyond the walls as if called home by some patient tide.

"Is he—" Ivo started, beside her, his hand still gripping her shoulder hard enough to bruise.

The king breathed.

It was a small thing, a terrible ordinary thing: the rise and fall of a chest under ruined velvet. Then his eyelids fluttered, and Wendell watched three years of stolen time come back into a human face all at once — confusion first, then horror, then a grief so vast it seemed to press him flat against the stones. He opened his mouth. What came out was not a royal decree but a sound, cracked and small, like a man surfacing from deep water.

"He's waking," Mother Quill said. She had come out of nowhere, as she always did, her shawl dark with rain, her hands steady as she knelt by the fallen king with the practiced calm of a woman who had patched up worse than kings. "Someone fetch sweetwater. Not wine — sweetwater, he'll need his head clear."

The charm-sellers moved. They had been pouring out of hiding since the storm broke — faces Wendell knew from the night markets, from the tunnels beneath Greyhollow, from years of whispered commerce and shared risk. Old Tam with his weather-charms. The twins, Sefa and Brannic, who sold love-knots that mostly worked. Marisol, who had once hidden Wendell from a magistrate for three days and never asked why. They gathered around the king not with reverence but with the practical tenderness of people who had spent a lifetime tending what the city refused to see.

And Wendell knelt in the mud and waited for relief to come.

It didn't.

There was something wrong with her. She knew it distantly, the way you know your hand has gone numb before the pins and needles start. The bargain had been struck; the storm had been called; the price had been paid. She should feel lighter. She should feel *clean*. Three years of exile, of guilt like a stone sewn into her chest, and she had finally given the Wood what it asked — the worst night of her life, the storm, the—

The storm.

The storm that killed—

She reached for it and found nothing.

Not darkness. Not fog. A *shape* of absence, the way an amputated limb aches in the air where it used to be. She knew there had been someone. She knew it the way she knew there was a room behind a bricked-over door. Someone she had loved. Someone whose death had driven her across half the world to this city of outlawed charms, whose memory she had carried like a wound she chose not to close because closing it would have meant admitting how deep it went.

His name was gone.

She reached further, desperate, scrabbling through her own mind like a woman searching pockets for keys she could hear jingling somewhere just out of reach. A face — no. A voice — no. A childhood, a shared bed during storms, a hand holding hers — nothing, nothing, only the terrible smooth blankness where a whole person used to live, and ringing around it, faint and maddening, echoes without sources: the smell of rain on hot stone and grief like a knife between her ribs. Grief with no object. Mourning aimed at nothing.

"Wendell." Ivo crouched in front of her. His face swam into focus — the ink stain on his left knuckle, the small scar through one eyebrow, the eyes that had looked at her across a night market table two weeks and a lifetime ago. "Hey. Look at me. It's done. You did it."

"I did something," she said. Her voice came out strange, scraped. "Ivo. There was — before my exile, there was someone. My brother."

"Yes." He said it carefully, gently, the way you'd handle a bird with a broken wing.

"What was his name?"

Ivo went very still.

* * *

They carried the king inside on a litter made of charm-sellers' cloaks, and no one stopped them, because half the palace guard had spent the masque watching their sovereign stand motionless among the revelers with sap running from the corners of his mouth, and they were in no state to argue about who had the right to carry him. Wendell walked beside the litter because walking was easier than thinking. Because if she stopped moving, the blankness would catch up.

Behind them, beyond the walls, the Weeping Wood was changing.

She saw it from the palace steps — everyone did, the whole city turning out onto balconies and rooftops as if drawn by the same string. The trees at the forest's edge, which had stood for three years like mourners, dripping stolen memories in silver beads from every branch, were lifting. Not growing — *straightening*, the way a bent old woman straightens when a weight is finally taken off her back. The perpetual weep of memory-dew slowed. Stopped. And then, in the failing light of the dying eclipse, the first green flush ran through the canopy like a word being remembered.

"It's giving them back," Sefa whispered beside her. "Look — the memories. It's letting them go."

Down along the forest road, figures were emerging. Greyhollow folk who had wandered too near the trees over the years and come back diminished, missing pieces — a woman who'd forgotten her wedding day, a soldier who'd lost his mother's face. They stumbled toward the city now, clutching their heads, weeping or laughing, whole again or nearly. And above them the Wood groaned and settled, its branches unfurling, and if Wendell strained she could almost hear something in that sound that wasn't madness anymore. Something older. Something almost like relief.

*Guardian,* Mother Quill had told them, back when all this was a riddle instead of a wound. *It was a guardian once, before the crown burned its grove. It hoarded memories because it was empty. Now it isn't.*

Now it wasn't. And Wendell stood on the palace steps with her hands empty and her mind full of nothing where everything used to be, and understood that the Wood hadn't been cruel. It had been fair. It had asked for her most painful memory, and it had taken exactly that — all of it, cleanly, the way a surgeon takes a limb. The pain was gone. So was the person the pain had been about.

"You're shaking," Ivo said. He draped his coat over her shoulders. It smelled of ink and cedar and the night market, and it was the only anchor she had.

"I can't remember him." The words came out flat, factual, worse than screaming. "I know I had a brother. I know I loved him. I know he died because of me, and I can't remember his *name*, Ivo. I traded away the only part of me that—"

"Don't." His voice cracked like the crown had cracked. "Don't finish that sentence. Don't you dare say it was the only part of you worth keeping. I've known you two weeks and I've got more of you than that."

"Then tell me," she said, turning on him, and her voice rose, storm-touched, frightening them both. "Tell me who he was. You read the prophecy book. You heard Mother Quill's story. You know things about me I don't know anymore, so *tell me*—"

"I don't know his name either." He said it steadily, though his jaw was white. "But I know people who do. Tam. Marisol. Half the underground — you talked about him, Wendell. Over years. Over cups of tea at three in the morning when the markets closed. You think charm-sellers forget anything anyone tells them? We make our living remembering what other people want kept safe."

* * *

Mother Quill found her at midnight, sitting in the kitchen of the charm-house — the cramped, candle-lit warren beneath a chandler's shop that had been home for three years — staring at a cup of tea she hadn't touched.

The old woman set something on the table between them: a scrap of paper, soft with age and handling. On it, in a child's uneven hand, was a single word.

*Cassian.*

Wendell's breath caught. Nothing came with it — no face, no voice — but the word landed somewhere in her chest and *ached*, sweet and terrible, like pressure on a bruise.

"Cassian," she repeated. It fit her mouth strangely. It fit the shape of the absence perfectly.

"You wrote that yourself, six months ago. Drunk on plum wine after the solstice market. You told us about him — all of it, over the years. Storm-calling runs in families, you said. You said he'd have made a better storm-caller than you, gentler hands." Mother Quill sat down across from her, folding her gnarled hands on the table. "We wrote it all down, girl. Every story you ever told us. Tam keeps them in a box under his bed and would die before he let a moth near them. You gave us your memories a piece at a time for three years and never knew you were building an archive."

"Why?" Wendell's eyes were burning. "Why would you—"

"Because that's what family does." Mother Quill's voice was iron wrapped in wool. "You thought you were alone in this city. You were never alone. And tomorrow — or the day after, when you can stand it — we're going to sit you down and tell you about your brother until you know him again. Not the way you knew him. But known. Cassian Yarrow will not be a hole in you, Wendell. He'll be a story we all hold together, and stories can be told as many times as needed."

From the doorway, Ivo cleared his throat. Behind him crowded Tam, Marisol, the twins — the whole ragged congregation of them, drawn by some silent alarm, carrying candles and bread and one of Tam's absurd weather-charms that made it rain indoors, which nobody ever fixed because it made the charm-house feel like a place where impossible things were allowed.

"We also voted," Ivo said, "while you were on the palace steps having your moment. The repeal petition goes to the restored king at dawn. First act of his reign. Magic decriminalized in Greyhollow." He paused, and his voice softened. "And there's a clause in it. A memorial provision. A grove, planted outside the walls, where the new guardian Wood can take root again — dedicated to everyone the old ban destroyed." He met her eyes. "Cassian's name goes first on the list. If that's all right."

Wendell looked around the table at these people — these smugglers and hedge-witches and sellers of small mercies, who had spent three years collecting her brother in scraps without her knowing, who had carried her grief when she could no longer find it herself — and the hollow ache in her chest didn't close. It wouldn't, maybe ever. But around the hollow, warm and stubborn, something else took root.

"That's all right," she said.

Outside, far past the walls, the Wood stood straight and green under the returning stars, and somewhere in its depths, unburdened at last, something ancient began — slowly, branch by branch, season by season — to remember how to be kind.

And at dawn, when the petition reached the young king's desk, he would ask one question of the exhausted charm-seller standing before him: *"Who is Cassian Yarrow?"* — and Wendell, waiting in the corridor, would realize she was about to hear her brother's story told aloud for the very first time.
