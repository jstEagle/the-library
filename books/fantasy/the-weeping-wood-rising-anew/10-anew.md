## 10. Anew

The thaw came to Greyhollow all at once, the way forgiveness does.

One morning in early spring, Wendell Yarrow woke in her narrow room above Mother Quill's charm-shop and heard water running in the gutters — a sound the city had forgotten it could make. Ice that had crouched on the rooftops since autumn let go all at once, sheeting down into the streets like applause. She stood at the window with her hands pressed flat against the cold glass and watched people come out of doorways to look up, as if checking whether the sky had changed while they weren't paying attention.

It had.

The king's proclamation had gone up on every gate three weeks ago, written in his own hand beneath the royal seal: *The ban on magic is repealed, beginning with the district of Lowmarsh.* Not everywhere. Not all at once. The scribes said he insisted on beginning small, in the poorest quarter, where the charm-sellers had always been hung first and pardoned last. There would be trials of the new law, hearings, a slow unwinding of a century of fear. But when Wendell walked through Lowmarsh now, she saw luck-knots hanging openly from door lintels, bright as festival ribbons, and old women selling weather-charms from trays without looking over their shoulders, and children chasing each other under awnings where a hedge-witch's salve-smoke curled sweet into the wet air.

No one had arrested anyone in three weeks. It was such a small thing. It was everything.

She still flinched sometimes when a guard's boots rang on cobblestones behind her. Ivo had noticed. He never said anything; he just moved to walk on whichever side of her the sound came from, as though he could stand between her and a century.

* * *

Ivo Senn spent the winter drawing maps no cartographer had ever been paid to draw: maps of what was remembered instead of what was.

They covered the back room of his shop now, pinned edge to edge, overlapping like feathers. The Weeping Wood rendered not in trees but in names — each stolen memory plotted where it had been taken, a constellation of griefs laid over the forest's dark heart. In one corner, marked with gold ink, a single point glowed: *the king, taken three winters past, midwinter night, alone in his garden.* And beside it, in red, another mark he'd made the day the Wood released its hold: *returned.*

The restored king had come to see the maps himself in the second week of spring. Ivo had expected a procession, trumpets, the whole theater of a crown. Instead a tall man in a plain grey coat arrived with two guards who waited outside, and stood before the wall of names for a long time without speaking.

"I remember being in the garden," the king said at last. "Then nothing. Then waking in my own bed, and my daughter grown two years taller, and everyone looking at me as if I might break." He touched the map lightly, below the gold mark, where Ivo had drawn the city itself — Greyhollow whole and unburned. "You kept the record while I could not. That is a kind of rule-making, I think. Better than mine."

"Your majesty—"

"Call me Aldric." The king smiled, and it was a real smile, unsteady at the edges, the smile of a man relearning how his own face worked. "I am going to plant a grove," he said. "Where the old one burned. I would like you to draw it. What it was. What it should be again."

Ivo looked at the wall of names — the Wood's long theft laid out in ink — and thought about what Mother Quill had told them by candlelight, months ago: *it was a guardian once, before we taught it that guarding gets you burned.* He picked up a fresh sheet of paper.

"It will take some research," he said. "I'll need to speak with the trees."

* * *

The new grove was planted on a grey morning in late spring, on the scarred ground where the crown's soldiers had burned the guardian's heartwood a hundred years before.

Aldric came without armor. He knelt in the ash-grey soil himself, sleeves rolled, and set the first sapling's roots into the earth with his bare hands, and the crowd that had gathered along the ridgeline went quiet enough to hear the wind move through the valley beyond. The Weeping Wood stood at the far edge of that valley — greener now than any of them had seen it, its bark no longer weeping sap but something clearer, running thin and silver down the trunks like the trees were learning, slowly, how to cry ordinary rain.

Wendell stood at the front of the crowd with Ivo's hand around hers and felt the storm magic stir under her skin — not rising, not called, just awake, like a dog lifting its head. Since the eclipse she had felt it differently. Less like a wildness she had to cage. More like a muscle she had refused to use until it cramped. She was learning to hold it gently. Some days she only managed to hold it at all.

Mother Quill stood on her other side, sharp-eyed and straight-backed despite her years, wearing her good coat with the charms sewn into the lining — worn openly now, for the first time in forty years. On Quill's other side stood the whole strange family of the underground: Ferro with his ink-stained fingers, little Maren clutching a jar of fireflies she swore were luck-spirits, old Tam with his bad knee and his worse singing voice. When the king pressed the soil down around the first sapling and rose with dirt on his knees, Tam started humming an old planting song, off-key, and half the crowd joined in because nobody dared laugh at a man crying next to a king.

"Magic is no longer a crime," Aldric said, when the song faded. His voice carried without effort, the way it must have before the roots got into him — or perhaps stronger for it, for the hollow years. "Not here. Not anywhere in this kingdom, as fast as law can be unwritten. To those who hid for generations: you are welcome out of the shadows. To those who were exiled:" — and here his eyes found Wendell, deliberately, in front of everyone — "you are welcome home."

Wendell had planned, all week, to say something dignified if this moment came. What came out instead was a wet, undignified laugh, and then she was crying into Ivo's shoulder while the crowd cheered and the saplings caught the wind and bent, resilient, green.

* * *

They wrote the book in the evenings, in the warm back room of the charm-shop, with the kettle going and the rain starting against the shutters.

It had been Maren's idea first — Maren, who noticed everything and said nothing until it burst out of her sideways. She'd found Wendell standing in front of the shelf where the household keepsakes lived, staring at a space on the wood with a frown, touching it like a woman probing a missing tooth.

"What goes there?" Wendell had asked.

And Maren had gone white, and looked at Ivo, and Ivo had come and put his arm around Wendell and said, gently, "Tamir does. Your brother. That's his carving kit."

She knew the name. That was the cruelty and the mercy of it both — the name remained, and a shape around it, a silhouette of love with nothing inside. She knew she had had a brother. She knew she had loved him. She knew the storm had taken him and that she had traded the remembering to free a king, and she did not regret it, and she grieved anyway, endlessly, for someone she could no longer see.

So they began to write it down. All of them. A ledger bound in green leather that Mother Quill supplied without being asked, because Quill understood that some charms are made of paper.

Ivo wrote first, because he had met Tamir only in stories and maps, and so he wrote the stories as he'd received them: *Tamir Yarrow, born in the river country, could whistle so precisely that birds answered him. Once convinced a flock of starlings to spell his sister's initial over the harvest fair. Drew maps in the mud of everywhere he wanted to go. Wanted to go everywhere.*

Ferro wrote about the summer Tamir had apprenticed briefly with a tinker and come home having fixed every broken hinge in the village, whether the owners wanted it or not. Old Tam wrote a page of terrible verse. Maren drew pictures in the margins — Tamir laughing, Tamir whistling, Tamir with Wendell, two children on a fence, drawn from descriptions, from photographs of memory held by people Wendell had never met who loved her brother too.

But the pages that mattered most, Wendell saved for last. One evening in April she sat at the table with the green book open before her and the pen trembling in her hand, and Ivo sat across from her and did not fill the silence, which was its own kindness.

"I don't remember him," she said. "But I remember that I don't. Is that strange? It's like — there's a room in me with the door bricked up, and I know the room is there. I know I built the wall myself, and why."

"You're allowed to knock," Ivo said.

So she wrote. Slowly. Not memories — she had none — but the truth around their absence:

*I had a brother. His name was Tamir. I loved him the way you love the ground under your feet: without thinking about it, without ever believing it could be gone. I killed him by accident, with a storm I didn't mean to call, and I have spent every day since wishing it had been me. I gave away my memory of him to save a stranger, and I would do it again, and I hate that I would do it again. But this much I still know, even with the door bricked shut: whatever Tamir was, he was worth more than a kingdom. And whoever I was when he was alive — I want to be her again. Help me learn her.*

She read it aloud. Her voice broke twice. Nobody minded.

When she finished, Mother Quill took the pen, turned to a fresh page, and wrote at the top, in her fierce, spidery hand: *Chapter Two: What We Remember For Her.* Then she passed the book around the table, and everyone wrote, and the rain came down soft on the roof of the house where Wendell Yarrow had finally stopped hiding.

* * *

On the last evening of spring, Wendell and Ivo walked out past the city walls to the young grove.

The saplings had taken. They stood in their long rows catching the low gold light, small and stubborn, and beyond them the Weeping Wood filled the valley — taller now, fuller, its silver tears dried, its branches moving in a wind that seemed, to Wendell, less like grief than like breathing. Sometimes at dusk she thought she felt it notice her: the old guardian, mending, acknowledging the storm-caller who had unmade its madness. It never spoke. But it never looked away, either.

"Read me something," she said, settling against Ivo's side on the warm grass.

He had the green book with him; he always did now. He opened it to the newest pages and read her the story of the starlings spelling her initial over the harvest fair, and then Ferro's account of the hinges, and then his own page, which he'd rewritten eleven times and still wasn't satisfied with. She closed her eyes and listened, letting the words build her brother up out of paper and love, brick by careful brick, a boy she would spend the rest of her life meeting.

Above the grove, the first starlings of the evening wheeled together into the darkening sky — and for half a heartbeat, before scattering, they held a shape against the sunset.

An arrow. A direction. *Everywhere,* said the shape. *We wanted to go everywhere.*

Wendell laughed, startled, delighted, and squeezed Ivo's hand hard enough to hurt.

"Did you see that?" she whispered.

"See what?" he said — and then he saw her face, and the starlings wheeling home, and understood exactly what he was allowed to believe.

They stayed until full dark, two small figures among the new trees, while overhead the sky went on turning, and the wood breathed, and the city behind them lit its thousand windows one by one — a place that had forgotten itself, learning, street by street and season by season, to remember.
