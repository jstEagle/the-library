## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The stars had always been Jude Quintana's first employers.

Before the guild seals, before the coin, before the name she'd made for herself on the high roads of Thornfell — before all of it, there had been the sky. She had learned to read it the way other children learned their letters: crouched on her mother's shoulders in a frost-bitten orchard, tracing the Wagon, the Weeping Sisters, the long pale river of the Thorn that spilled across the dark like something poured and never wiped away. Her mother used to say that smugglers were just sailors who'd run aground, and that the stars were the same either way — indifferent, ancient, honest. A star never lied to you about where you were. That was more than could be said for any man.

So when the stars went out, Jude Quintana lost more than light. She lost her bearings in every sense the word allowed.

* * *

It began as a wrongness she felt in her teeth.

She was walking point, four lengths ahead of the caravan, with the mule-train strung out behind her along the shelf-road above the Greywater gorge. Nine wagons, twenty-two souls, a cargo manifest that officially did not exist: salted eels from the lowland fisheries, Vaelmoorish glass, three crates of ward-tokens whose provenance the guild master had been very careful not to ask about. The autumn air was thin and clean and smelled of pine-resin and coming snow. Below them the gorge breathed its cold mist upward, and the road was good — dry shale, well-graded, one of her own routes, scouted and paid for over two years of careful bribery.

And yet something in her jaw ached. A deep, subsonic hum, the way she imagined a struck bell must feel to the metal itself.

"Quintana." Brannoc Hale's voice came up the line, low and rough. The caravan master rode a dun mare he didn't deserve, and his hands were too big for his reins, and he trusted her the way drowning men trust rope. "You feel that?"

"I feel it."

"Earthshift? We should—"

"It's not the ground." She held up a fist and the column halted behind her, wheels creaking into silence, mules stamping. She turned her face up toward the sky out of pure habit, to check their position by the Weeping Sisters — the twin stars that hung over the pass at this hour, close together, like two figures standing shoulder to shoulder at a grave.

They were gone.

Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Gone.* And as she stared, understanding arriving slowly and horribly, like blood through bandages, she saw that the whole sky was going. Star after star after star, not falling but simply ceasing, as if some vast hand were snuffing candles down a corridor. The Thorn river vanished. The northern constellations that marked the Vaelmoor border vanished. The darkness came down over the world not like night but like a lid.

"Hale," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, flattened. "Get the lanterns lit. All of them."

"What in the hells—"

"*All of them.* Now."

He moved, because Brannoc Hale was many things but slow was not one of them, and within a minute the caravan glowed like a string of festival lights along the cliff-road — six lanterns on the wagons, torches at the flanks, her own storm-lamp unhooded and held high. It wasn't enough. Nothing would have been enough. The dark above them was total in a way darkness had never been before, a blackness with weight and texture, pressing down like water. Somewhere in the mule-line a beast began to scream and would not stop.

"Saints," whispered Tam Wicker, the youngest of the drivers, sixteen and freckled and holding his torch so hard the pitch cracked. "Where'd they go? Where'd the *stars* go?"

Nobody answered him. There was no answer. There was only the sound of the gorge breathing, and the mule shrieking, and — beneath it, beneath everything, rising now through the soles of Jude's boots and up her spine and into the roots of her teeth —

the Wood.

* * *

Everyone in Thornfell knew about the Weeping Wood, the way everyone knows about weather or taxes: it was there, it was old, it was enormous, and you did not speak of it carelessly. It stood at the heart of the border country, leagues of silver-barked trees whose leaves wept resin in the rain, whose roots went down further than any surveyor had dared map. It had no priesthood anyone respected and no miracles anyone could prove. But every smuggler who worked the Thornfell passes left an offering at its edge — a ribbon, a coin, a lock of hair — because the roads ran strange near the Wood, and because the Wood listened. Everyone agreed it listened. It had never once, in living memory, answered.

Jude had heard it exactly once, years ago, and even then it had not been words. She had been seventeen, bleeding out into the moss of a cut-off trail with a warden's arrow in her side, and something had come through the trees — not a figure, not a presence, but a *leaning*, the sense of an immense attention bending toward her like a branch heavy with snow. It had looked at her. She could not describe it any better than that. It had looked at her, and she had lived, though the wound alone should have killed her twice over. Afterward she had left offerings like everyone else, but hers were larger, and she had never told anyone why.

Since that day, she had carried a silence with her. Not emptiness — a listening silence, the kind that lives inside a held breath. On bad nights she had thought of it as her god being far away. On worse nights she had wondered if she'd imagined the whole thing, if the arrow-fever had simply shown her what a dying girl wanted to see.

The silence ended on the shelf-road above the Greywater gorge, under a sky with no stars in it, at approximately the eleventh hour past sundown.

It did not end gently.

The scream hit her like a physical blow. She was moving before she knew she'd fallen — she found herself on her knees on the shale with her storm-lamp rolling away and both hands pressed flat against the ground, against the mountain itself, as if she could brace the whole world. The sound was not in her ears. It bypassed her ears entirely, bypassed everything, pouring straight into the marrow of her: a sound like ten thousand trees tearing loose from their roots at once, like a forest-sized throat opening, like grief given volume. The Weeping Wood — patient, silent, listening for longer than kingdoms had existed — was screaming.

And it was screaming *her name.*

Jude. Jude. JUDE. The syllables came wrapped in the scream like stones in a flood, unmistakable, urgent, terrified. No god should be terrified. That was her first coherent thought, surfacing through the white noise of agony: whatever else this was, whatever power or horror or miracle, the thing she was hearing was *afraid*, and it was calling for her as if she were the only hand it could reach.

"Quintana!" Hands on her shoulders — Hale, hauling her upright, his face a lantern-lit smear of alarm. "What's wrong with you? What is it?"

She tried to answer. What came out was: "It's awake."

"What's awake?"

She didn't know how to say it. *The thing I've carried in my chest since I was seventeen. The silence I thought was mine.* She looked east, toward where the Wood lay beyond three ranges and a hundred miles of broken country, and saw — or thought she saw, or her screaming nerves showed her — a glow on the horizon that was not dawn and not fire. A sickly amber shimmer, pulsing, like light through sap.

Then the wards broke.

She felt that too, and hated that she felt it, because it meant she was bound up in all of this past any sane hope of denial. The border wards of Thornfell were old magic, older than the kingdom, older than the feud with Vaelmoor — a lattice woven between the peaks, maintained by the wardens' colleges, humming along the high ridgelines the way current hums along a wire. Smugglers like Jude made their living finding the gaps in that lattice. She had spent years mapping its weak points; she knew its texture the way a pickpocket knows the weave of a purse. And now she felt the whole fabric *tear*, a single catastrophic rending that ran from the southern marches to the northern crags, and where the lattice had been there was nothing. Not silence. Absence. A hole in the world shaped like a border.

Above the pass, where the wards had always burned their faint cold blue along the ridge, there was nothing at all.

And then the second wave of the scream crested, and this time it took her vision with it. For one blinding instant she was somewhere else — she was *underground*, or felt like it, in a dark full of roots and pressure, and something vast was wrapped around her, chains or coils or cables of grey iron-cold will, and she was suffocating, and the last of her strength was going into one final act: reaching. Reaching outward through earth and stone and miles of frozen air, reaching for the only thread she had ever touched, the small bright mortal mind that had once bled on her moss and lived —

Then it let go, and Jude Quintana was on her back on the shelf-road with snow beginning to fall into a starless sky, and the caravan gathered around her in a frightened ring of lamplight.

"She's bleeding," someone said. "Her nose — saints, she's bleeding."

Hale knelt over her, big hands hovering, useless. "Quintana. Look at me. Talk to me."

She talked. It took her three tries. "Cut us loose."

"What?"

"The cargo. The manifest. Everything." She sat up, wiped her nose with the back of her wrist, and saw the blood there, black in the lamplight, absurdly ordinary. "We're done running cargo tonight. Tonight we get off this road."

"We're eight hours from the waystation. In the dark. With no stars." Hale's voice climbed. "You want to abandon the route because you fainted?"

Because my god screamed my name and then reached for me like a drowning man reaches for a rope, she did not say. Because the borders of two realms just tore open like wet paper, and I felt it happen inside my own body, and I think — I think something has the Wood, Hale, something has it *chained*, and whatever it is, it knows my name.

"Every warden in Thornfelt is going to be on these roads within the hour," she said instead, "and they'll be looking for someone to blame. Do you want to be caught mid-route with three crates of forged ward-tokens when they find the wards are dead?" She got her feet under her. The ground seemed steadier now, or she had simply decided to stop trusting it and walk anyway. "We cache the crates in the scree field below the switchback. We take the mules down the shepherd's track to Lowmark. We move *now*, while the dark still confuses them more than it confuses us."

Hale stared at her for a long moment. Around him the drivers waited, torches guttering, faces hollow-eyed. Snow hissed softly into the flames. Above them all, the sky was a solid roof of black, absolute, without one single point of light — and Jude watched Tam Wicker look up at it and begin, quietly, to cry.

"Move," Hale said at last, hoarse. "You heard her. Move!"

* * *

They made the scree field by midnight, or by what Jude guessed was midnight, because time itself had gone strange without the sky to hang it on. The caching went quickly, hands working fast and silent, and by the time the caravan turned onto the narrow descent of the shepherd's track, the panic had settled into the grim mechanical efficiency of frightened professionals. Jude walked point again. She refused the offer of a horse. She wanted her boots on the stone, her own weight telling her true.

But the world she navigated by had stopped making sense, and she knew it, and every step forward was a step taken on faith.

Without the stars, direction was guesswork. Without the wards, the mountains had no voice — she hadn't known until tonight that she could hear them, that faint lattice-song she'd mistaken all these years for wind through high passes. Without the sky, north was just a word. She steered by the gorge's sound on her left, by the lean of the pines, by memory, and prayed silently to a god who had finally spoken and whose only message had been terror.

Perrin — the toll-village boy they'd picked up at the last crossing, hired to carry water and tend mules, all elbows and watchful brown eyes — fell into step beside her on the second mile. He kept glancing up.

"There's supposed to be stars," he said. It wasn't a question. It was a protest, the protest of a child against a rule broken.

"There are."

"Where'd they go, mistress?"

"I don't know." She looked down at him. His face was pale in the swinging lantern-light, but his eyes were steady, and something about that steadiness steadied her. "But I know someone who does. Or knew. Maybe." She touched her own sternum, where the silence had lived and where now, faintly, something else lived instead — a tautness, like a wire pulled to singing pitch. Like a thread. Like the far end of a rope thrown to a drowning woman, held from the other side.

Because that was the truth she hadn't given Hale, the truth she was still turning over as they descended into the lowlands: the scream had ended with the reaching, yes. But after the reaching, in the last instant before contact broke, she had felt the Wood do one more thing.

It had let go of everything else. Every root, every leaf, every league of listening dark — it had released it all, folded it inward, spent its whole imprisoned self on a single act. And what remained, what it had pressed into her marrow with the force of a dying man's last grip, was not a warning and not a prayer.

It was directions.

South and down, the feeling said. South, and down, and follow the old salt road, and *hurry.*

Behind them, far up the pass, a horn blew — once, twice, three times. Warden-signals. The law, waking to a broken world, already looking for someone to blame.

Jude Quintana turned her face south, into the dark, and started to run.
