## 2. Ash on the Toll Road

The lowlands came up to meet them like a held breath finally let out, and what they exhaled was smoke.

Jude smelled it before she saw it—two days' descent from the pass, through fog that clung to the caravan like wet wool, and then the wind shifted and brought the smell of burning thatch, of grain stores gone to ash, of something else underneath it all that she did not want to name. Meat, maybe. Or hair. Her mare, Bracken, tossed her head and snorted, nostrils wide, and Jude tightened her grip on the reins and said nothing.

Behind her, the caravan of nine wagons creaked and groaned its way down the switchbacks. Old Tammos drove the lead wagon after hers, and he'd been quiet since the stars went out—quieter than a man who talked to his mules more than his own children had any right to be. The others were quieter still. Three nights without stars did something to people. They kept looking up as if the sky owed them an apology, and the sky gave them nothing back but a flat, starless black, like the lid of a coffin.

"The toll village is over that rise," Tammos said at last, his voice rough as gravel under wagon wheels. "Verren's Crossing. Good folk. We've paid their gate-tax twenty years running."

"I know," Jude said. She knew every toll between Thornfell and the Vaelmoor border the way other people knew the lines of their own palms. She had walked these roads half her life, first as a child runner carrying messages no one would entrust to a grown man's saddlebag, later as a guide, selling safe passage through passes that wardens couldn't patrol and smugglers couldn't map. She had sold those roads so long she sometimes felt they belonged to her, the way you feel you own a thing you have betrayed enough times.

She had also, three years ago, sold a map of the Verren road to a warden-captain with soft hands and a hard smile. She tried not to think about that. She thought about it constantly.

They crested the rise, and the caravan stopped, all nine wagons, as if some hand had reached down and closed around them.

Verren's Crossing burned. Not all of it—the granary still stood, blackened on one side, and the mill wheel turned in the river with nobody to mind it, creaking its slow idiot patience. But the toll-house was gone, collapsed into a heap of glowing rafters, and the row of cottages along the east bank had been put to the torch in a line, neat as beads on a string. Ash fell on the road like grey snow. It settled on Bracken's mane, on the canvas of the wagons, on Jude's hands where they rested on the reins. It got into everything. It would get into everything for weeks.

And in the square, in front of what remained of the chapel, there was a pyre.

It wasn't built yet. It was being built—a stack of fence rails and broken furniture and door planks pried off houses whose owners were probably watching from the treeline, and on top of the stack sat a boy.

He couldn't have been more than ten. He was small for even that; he sat with his wrists lashed to a stake driven between the rails, and his feet didn't reach the edge of the platform, and he swung them slightly, absently, the way children do when they are frightened but trying very hard not to show it. His face was smudged with soot. There was blood on his temple, dried brown. He was watching the men below him with an expression Jude recognized from battlefields and slaughterhouses: the look of someone who has decided that screaming will do no good, and who is therefore spending their last strength on dignity instead.

Around the pyre stood eight wardens in the black-and-ochre of the Tollwarden Compact, and their captain stood closest, reading from a scroll by the light of a lantern because there was no other light left in the world worth reading by.

"—by confession witnessed and sealed," the captain was saying, his voice carrying flat across the square, "the accused did traffic in forbidden ways, did carry contraband past lawful gates, and did—by his own admission—know of the fallen wards before their falling, which knowledge he concealed."

"He's a child," Tammos breathed beside her. "Gods' teeth, Quintana, that's Hesta Maren's boy. That's Perrin. He runs messages. He can't be more than—"

"I heard," Jude said.

Her voice came out strange. Level. Cold in a way she hadn't chosen. Something was happening inside her chest that felt less like decision and more like weather—a pressure building, a change coming, and she understood distantly that it had started two nights ago when the stars went out and her silent god had screamed, and it had not stopped since, and now it had found somewhere to go.

Because here was the arithmetic of it, laid out plain as a ledger: the wards fell, the stars vanished, and the world needed someone to blame. Not the powerful. Never the powerful. The wardens could not admit their wards had failed them—that their great covenant-magic, the pride of Thornfell, had cracked like cheap crockery—and so they needed a sin small enough to punish. And who was small? Smugglers. Toll-village children who ran messages along roads the law pretended didn't exist. People like her.

People she had made vulnerable, three years ago, when she sold that map.

"Quintana." Tammos caught her arm as she swung down from the saddle. "Whatever you're thinking—there's eight of them, and we've got cargo, and the guild doesn't pay us to start wars with the Compact."

"The guild pays me to know which fights are worth having." She pulled her arm free. "This one's worth having."

"It isn't justice, what they're doing. But it isn't ours either."

"Then whose is it?" She looked at him, and old Tammos—who had buried a wife and two sons and most of his illusions—took a step backward. "Nobody's going to come save him, Tam. The law is the ones holding the torch. So either justice belongs to whoever's willing to pick it up, or it belongs to no one, and we might as well all burn too."

* * *

She walked into the square alone.

Later, she would try to reconstruct how she'd decided to do it, and she never could. There was no plan worth the name. She had her sword, her knife, her bow with fourteen arrows, and a face the wardens of three counties knew by reputation if not by sight. Eight against one was poor odds in the open. In a square full of smoke and confusion, with a caravan at her back and night that no longer had any stars in it—odds were just a number men told themselves so they could sleep.

"Hold there!" The captain looked up from his scroll. He was young for the rank, broad through the shoulders, with the ruddy, well-fed look of a man who had never once been hungry on principle. "This is Compact business. Lawful execution, witnessed and sealed. Stand off, or stand witness, but stand aside."

"Whose confession?" Jude asked. "A ten-year-old confessed to trafficking?"

"The boy admitted to carrying goods on the forbidden ways. He named names. He—" the captain hesitated, and in the lantern light Jude saw something flicker behind his certainty, quick as a fish turning, "he implicated others. The Compact requires example. The wards are fallen, the heavens are dark, and the Compact's charter empowers summary judgment in times of crisis."

"Your wards fell," Jude said, walking closer, boots crunching on ash. "Not his. He's what, ten? Eleven? What forbidden way did a child carry goods on—the road to his mother's kitchen?"

"His mother harbored smugglers." The captain's jaw tightened. "Hesta Maren kept a house that trafficked in untaxed goods for years. She fled before we could question her. The boy remains."

"So you're hanging your failure on a child because his mother got away."

"I am upholding the law!"

His voice cracked on the word, and that crack told Jude everything. He was afraid. They all were—she could see it now, the white-rimmed eyes, the hands gripping halberds too tightly, the way none of them would look up at the empty sky. The stars had gone out and the world had come off its hinges and these men needed, desperately, to do one thing today that made sense, that followed a rule, that ended with a clean result they could write in a ledger. Burn the smuggler's boy. Restore order. Go home and drink until the sky made sense again.

She almost pitied them. Then she looked at Perrin, swinging his feet above the kindling, and the pity burned away like fog in sun.

"You want an example," Jude said. "Fine. Here's one."

She drew.

The fight, such as it was, lasted eleven seconds, though she would remember it for the rest of her life in pieces that refused to stay in order. A warden rushed her and met her pommel with his teeth. Another swung a halberd in a wide, terrified arc and she stepped inside it—too close for the weapon, exactly where trained men panic—and broke his wrist against her hip and put him down. The captain had a sword after all, and he wasn't bad, but he fought like a man fighting a document: correct forms, expected angles, nothing in him ready for someone who had learned blade-work in mountain passes where there were no rules except survive. She cut the sword out of his hand at the wrist-guard, spun him with a boot to the knee, and put her knife to his throat as the remaining wardens froze, weapons half-raised, ash drifting down around them all like the world quietly coming apart.

"Cut the boy down," Jude said.

Nobody moved.

"Now," she said, "or I start collecting fingers as evidence."

One of them—older, grey-bearded, with the tired eyes of a man who had joined the Compact to feed a family and never expected the world to end on his watch—moved first. He dropped his halberd, climbed the pyre, and sawed through the ropes at the boy's wrists. Perrin slid down the pile of rails and furniture and landed badly, in a crouch, like a cat or a soldier, and stared at Jude across the smoking square with eyes so dark and so steady that she thought, absurdly: *that one will live a long time, or die for something that matters.*

"Get to the wagons," she told him.

He didn't run. That was the remarkable thing. He picked himself up, looked at the captain—at the wardens—with a hatred so complete and so calm that it seemed almost architectural, something built to last, and then he looked at Jude and nodded once, and walked to the caravan at a pace that dared anyone to shoot him in the back.

Nobody shot.

"You'll hang for this," the captain rasped under her knife. His throat moved against the flat of the blade. "The Compact doesn't forget. Every road, every crossing, every market—you'll have wardens behind you the rest of your life."

"They were already behind me," Jude said. "At least now I'll know to check."

She stepped back, swept the captain's sword into the ash, and backed toward the wagons with her knife low and her eyes moving. The wardens parted. The old one with the tired eyes looked at her—looked at her the way you look at a door opening onto a room you'd forgotten existed—and said nothing, and did not raise the alarm, and Jude filed that away in the place where she kept debts both owing and owed.

Tammos had Perrin up on the lead wagon before she reached them, wrapped in a blanket, a mug of something hot shoved into his hands. The boy held it in both hands and drank like it was a task assigned to him, methodical, thorough, and watched the burning village recede over the girl's shoulder—he watched it the whole time, Jude noticed, not the wardens, not his rescuers. The village. As if memorizing it. As if intending, someday, to hold someone accountable.

"What's your name?" she asked, riding alongside.

"Perrin." His voice was hoarse, unused. "Perrin Maren."

"Maren. Your mother ran a toll-house on the Verren road?"

"She kept the gate." His chin lifted slightly. "Kept it honest. Charged fair rates, fed travelers free who couldn't pay. Your lot called it smuggling because we wouldn't pay the Compact's tax on top of the crown's tax on top of the guild's tithe." He looked at her then, directly, and there was no fear in it at all. "You're Jude Quintana. I've carried messages with your seal on them."

The words landed harder than any blow in the square. Because it was true. She remembered the boy now—dimly, a small figure sprinting along hedgerows with letters in his shirt, always first to arrive, always asking for the wax-stamped token as payment because he was saving up for something. And she remembered, too, the map. The soft-handed captain. The raid on the Verren road two winters ago that had scattered half the gentle smuggling network she'd spent a decade weaving, that had gotten four people imprisoned and one—she'd heard—dead.

Had it led here? To this? To a toll-house burned and a woman fled and a child tied to a pyre? She would never be able to trace the thread cleanly, and that was the worst of it. Guilt that can be traced can be paid. Hers was a debt with no ledger line, smeared across years, impossible to total and impossible to discharge.

"Yeah," she said finally. "That's me."

"My ma said you were the best guide on the mountain." He took another swallow from the mug. "She also said you'd sell anything if the price was right and your conscience was sleeping." He said it without malice, entirely as information, and somehow that was worse. "Is your conscience awake now?"

Bracken's hooves crunched in ash. Somewhere behind them, Verren's Crossing went on burning, and the mill wheel went on turning, patient and idiotic, grinding nothing for no one.

"Not yet," Jude said. "But it's stirring."

Perrin nodded slowly, as if this answer satisfied some private standard of honesty, and burrowed deeper into the blanket, and within minutes was asleep sitting up, the way children and soldiers can sleep, anywhere, after the worst day of their lives.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow off the salt road, wagons drawn into a circle, no fires larger than a cupped hand. There was little point in darkness discipline anymore—there was no light left to hide from—but old habits die slower than gods, apparently.

Jude took first watch because she didn't trust herself to sleep. She sat on the wagon bench with her sword across her knees and watched the starless dark press down on the hills like a lid, and she turned things over in her mind the way a gambler turns cards.

Eight wardens. One of them would talk. The Compact would have her name attached to an assault on lawful officers within the week, and her face would follow after that, sketched on warrants nailed to every toll-gate from here to the coast. Twenty years of careful work, of being useful to everyone and owned by no one—burned in eleven seconds in a square full of ash.

She found, examining the feeling carefully, that she could not make herself regret it.

That frightened her more than the wardens did.

"You could have ridden past."

She hadn't heard Perrin climb down. He stood at the edge of the firelight, blanket around his shoulders like a cloak, soot still in the creases of his neck. Children his age should have been sobbing or screaming or hollowed out. This one stood like a small man reviewing his losses.

"You should be sleeping."

"Can't. When I close my eyes I'm back on the pyre." He said it simply, without self-pity, reporting a fact about the terrain. "So. You could have ridden past. Everyone else did. The miller saw them building it and locked his doors. The ferryman crossed to the far bank. Why didn't you?"

Jue considered lying to him and understood immediately that he would know, the way dogs know rain. "Because I've spent three years paying for roads I sold," she said, "and the interest just came due."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "But it's the only true one I've got."

He came closer and sat on the ground by the wheel, folding the blanket around his legs. For a while neither spoke. The dark pressed close. Somewhere far off, something howled—not wolf, not dog; the sound had a wrongness in it that made Bracken stamp and the horses on the picket line tremble. Since the stars died, the nights had begun making noises they'd never made before. Things were waking up in the gaps where the light used to be.

"I took something from the village," Perrin said suddenly. "Before they caught me."

"Contraband?" She meant it lightly. He didn't smile.

"From our house. From the hearth. Ma kept her mother's keepsakes in a box behind the third stone, and when the wardens came I grabbed what I could." He worked something out of his shirt—a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string, worn smooth as if he'd been handling it for days. He unwound the covering with careful fingers.

Inside lay a shard of stone no bigger than a man's thumb. It should have been cold—it was night, it was autumn, the boy had carried it against his skin through capture and terror and a near-execution—but it gleamed faintly, warmly, like a coal that refused to die, and when Jude's eyes touched it something behind her ribs *turned over*.

Her god screamed again, distant now, wordless, and for the first time since the stars went out, the scream sounded less like grief.

It sounded like recognition.

"What is it?" Perrin whispered, watching her face.

Jude stared at the shard, and at the boy holding it, and felt the road ahead bend toward something she could not yet see—an altar, a frozen lake, a sister she didn't know she had—all of it waiting somewhere down the dark like lamps being lit one by one in windows of a house she'd lived in and forgotten.

"I don't know," she said truthfully. "But I think it knows us. Keep it warm, Perrin. Whatever happens—keep it warm, and tell no one."

He rewrapped the shard and tucked it back against his heart, and nodded, solemn as a vow, and Jude looked out into the starless dark and understood that she had just picked up something on this road that could not be put down again.

Two days south, at the river crossing where the old ford ran, someone was waiting for her. She didn't know it yet. But the shard glowed a little brighter whenever she faced south, like a compass needle finding not north, but *family*.
