## 3. The Inquisitor in Grey

The Verren crossing at Duskwater was the last honest ford on the old smuggling roads — honest meaning only that its ferryman took coin from both sides of the border and asked no questions of either. Jude had crossed there a dozen times in her years running Thornfell goods south: salt wool and emberglass going down, Vaelmoor tin and stranger things coming up. The river ran slow and brown-green between reed banks, wide as a lie, and on its far shore the grey country began — hedgerows trimmed by law instead of weather, roads that went where the wardens wanted them to go.

She came down to the water alone.

That had been Brannic's idea, or rather his fear dressed up as strategy. After the night the stars went out, the caravan wanted no part of the open lowlands, and Jude couldn't blame them. They'd made cold camp in a hollow above the toll road with no fire, because fire drew eyes, and because without stars overhead the darkness felt less like an absence and more like something leaning close to listen. Perrin hadn't spoken since she'd pulled him off the pyre. He sat against her pack with his knees drawn up and watched her the way you watch a door you're not sure is locked.

"The ferryman knows me," she'd told Brannic. "I go down, I pay for the whole company's passage, I come back before moonrise."

"There is no moonrise," Brannic said, and that was true too. There was no moon at all now. The sky was a black lid, seamless, pressing down like a hand over a mouth.

So Jude walked the last mile to Duskwater alone, boots quiet on the frost-stiffened mud, one knife at her hip and another in her sleeve, and told herself the crawling feeling along her spine was just the cold.

* * *

The ford was wrong.

She knew it before she could name how. Ten years of reading country — that was the trade she'd actually mastered, more than blades, more than bargains — and every instinct she owned stood up at once and shouted. The ferry rope hung slack and dripping on both banks, but there was no boat. The reeds were bent in long combed rows, as if something heavy had been dragged through them toward the water. And the birdsong — there wasn't any. Not the silence of empty marsh, which hums and ticks and breathes, but the silence of held breath.

The god's silence, she thought, and hated herself for it. Three days since the scream, and she still caught herself listening for the Weeping Wood the way you listen for a heartbeat under your palm after it stops. She had never heard it speak before that night. Now she couldn't stop hearing the fact that it didn't.

"You're late, Quintana."

Jude turned toward the voice, blade already clearing leather.

A woman stood at the edge of the alder stand where the toll path met the bank. Grey — everything about her was grey, the travel coat, the hood thrown back, even the horse waiting behind her with a saddlecloth the color of slate and rain. Vaelmoor cut, Vaelmoor make, the high collar and the silver chain of office that marked an officer of the Inquisition. The chain bore no sigil. That was the thing people said about the Grey Order's inquisitors: their badges were blank, because what they served needed no name.

The woman was perhaps thirty, tall, dark-haired, with a face that —

Jude's breath stopped in her chest like a bird hitting glass.

She had seen this face before. Not in life. Somewhere deeper than life. The set of the mouth, the faint asymmetry of the left eyebrow, the small pale scar at the hairline — she knew all of it the way you know the layout of a house from a dream, every detail correct and no memory of ever walking through it. Her own reflection looked back at her from a stranger's face, older by some years, harder by many, and familiar in a way that made her stomach drop as if the ground beneath her had quietly opened.

"Who are you?" she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"Sera." The woman said it simply, like handing over a document. "Sera Corvane. Inquisitor Third Rank, Order of the Grey Veil." She stepped forward, and the frost did not crackle under her boots — or if it did, the sound was swallowed by that terrible hush. "But you can call me sister. Everyone else does. You used to."

"I don't have a sister."

"No," Sera agreed, and something moved behind her eyes — quick, involuntary, like a fish turning under ice. "You don't. That's rather the point of you, isn't it? Jude Quintana, born nowhere, enrolled nowhere, mercenary of the Thornfell roads, no family on any ledger in either realm." She smiled, and it was a professional smile, polished and cold, but it didn't reach the scar at her hairline. "And yet here you are, carrying something that belongs to my Order. Something that belongs to *me*, if we're being precise, and the Grey Order is always precise."

Jude circled slowly leftward, keeping the alders out of Sera's line to the water. "If this is a toll, say the amount."

"This isn't a toll." Sera lifted her chin slightly, nostrils flaring, like a hound catching a scent on air Jude couldn't smell. "It's a shard. Covenant-stone. Old work, older than either realm, and it's been singing to me across forty miles of dead sky. Do you feel it?" Her head tilted. "Of course you do. It's why you came down here alone. Why you left your caravan in the dark. You told yourself it was the ferryman, but you've been walking toward me since the wards fell, because the stone wants to be found and you're the one holding its leash."

"I'm holding nothing—"

"Your left sleeve."

Jude's arm moved before her mind authorized it — a twitch, barely, a flinch of muscle — and Sera saw it. Of course she saw it. Inquisitors were made of seeing.

"That," Sera said softly, "is very interesting."

* * *

They fought at the water's edge, and afterward Jude would remember it in pieces, the way you remember lightning.

Because Sera gave her the choice first. That was the strange part, the part that kept snagging in her mind like a splinter too deep to dig out. The woman drew no weapon when she said, "Give me the shard and walk away, Quintana. This is the only mercy my Order authorizes, and I am spending it on you for reasons I don't fully understand. Take it."

"You could just tell me why."

"I could." A pause. The brown river slid past between them, carrying leaves that had no light to reflect. "I've spent eleven days asking myself why. Since the wards broke. Since the sky—" For half a breath the professional mask slipped, and underneath it Jude glimpsed something raw and sleepless and afraid, and it was *her own* fear looking back at her, worn by another woman's face. Then the mask came down again, seamless. "No. Reasons are above your rank. The shard, or the steel."

Jude had survived ten years of smuggling roads on exactly one principle: never give up the thing they want until you know why they want it. So she said, "Come take it," and threw herself sideways into the reeds as the first crossbow quarrel hissed out of the alders and buried itself in the mud where she'd been standing.

There were two more in the trees — grey-cloaked, silent, moving with the drilled economy of Vaelmoor regulars. Jude registered them in flashes: a shape dropping from a branch, the flat whistle of a weighted cord, Sera's blade coming out of its scabbard with a sound like a word being unsaid.

The fight was short and ugly and Jude lost most of it.

Sera Corvane fought like someone who had been taught by people who never intended their students to survive the teaching. No flourish, no anger, no wasted motion — angles and endings, every strike a question with only one safe answer, and she asked them faster than Jude could reply. Jude gave ground, gave blood — a line opened along her forearm where the parry came a finger's width late — gave the reeds, gave the bank, backing step by step toward the brown water with her lungs burning and her mind doing cold arithmetic. Two crossbows covering the shallows. One inquisitor who read her feints before she finished making them. No boat. No stars. No god answering.

*Where is the ferryman?* The thought surfaced absurdly amid the violence. The ferryman who knew her, who owed her, whose hut stood thirty paces downstream — where was he, on a morning when the whole world had gone wrong?

Then her heel found the ferry-rope, slick and taut across the stones, and the arithmetic changed.

She dropped.

The river took her like a fist. Cold closed over her head, brown and total, and the current grabbed her pack and spun her downstream with the indifference of something ancient. Above her — muffled, warped, filtered through three feet of murky water — she heard shouting, and a splash, and the twang of a crossbow firing at nothing.

She let the river decide. It was the only ally at Duskwater that hadn't yet shown its price, and it carried her a quarter mile on its shoulder before her boots found bottom and she came up gasping behind a fallen willow, hair plastered to her face, one knife gone, the other miraculously still sleeved.

The shard was warm against her forearm. Warmer than her skin, warmer than the water, warm as a sleeping animal. When she peeled back the wet linen, there it lay in the little pocket she'd sewn years ago and forgotten the purpose of: a sliver of stone no longer than her thumb, veined with light the color of dawn through fog. It pulsed once, twice, like a heart remembering its business.

*I've been carrying this,* she thought. *How long?*

And beneath the thought, colder: *Why don't I remember sewing the pocket?*

* * *

She got clear of the river by full dark — full dark being no different from any other hour now — and holed up in a charcoal burner's hut three miles north, a turf-roofed cell that stank of ash and old rain. She built no fire. She bound her forearm with strips of shirt and sat with her back to the wall and her knees up, and she stared at the shard in her cupped palms while it breathed its slow fog-colored light onto her face.

Footsteps in the frost outside. Soft ones. Deliberate enough to be heard.

Jude was on her feet with the knife out before her mind caught up, and then stopped, because the door was already open and Sera Corvane stood in it alone — unarmed, hands visible, hood down, frost melting on her shoulders. Behind her, far off across the fields, two lanterns bobbed away toward the ford, her crossbowmen called off or dismissed.

"You missed me at the river," Jude said.

"You missed me at the river," Sera agreed. "Deliberately. On both counts." She looked at the knife, at the shard glowing in Jude's other hand, and made no move toward either. "May I come in, sister? Just to talk. I'll leave my sword outside. I already have."

"Don't call me that."

"Why not?" And there it was again — the mask slipping, the fish under the ice. "You flinch when I say it. Not the flinch of a stranger. Strangers look confused. You look *wounded*. Like I've said a word you've been trying not to hear for years." She stepped inside uninvited, and the hut felt suddenly smaller, the way rooms shrink around grief. "I pulled your file, Quintana. All of it. It's four pages long. Four pages, for a woman who's run the Thornfell roads for a decade — no origins, no kin, no debts, no grave. Do you know what the Order calls files like yours?"

"No."

"*Anchors.* Files that hold everything else down. Someone paid very well, very early, to make you weightless. To make you nobody." Sera's voice stayed level, inquisitorial, but her hands — her hands had curled slightly, unconsciously, as if reaching for something across a table. "And I should report that I found you. I should put the shard in a lead box and you in a wagon and deliver you to the Chapterhouse at Greymere, and by every oath I swore, that is what I am going to do. Tomorrow. At the crossing. With witnesses."

Jude's grip shifted on the knife. "But tonight?"

"Tonight—" Sera exhaled, and for a moment she was not an inquisitor at all, just a tired woman in a cold room with frost in her hair. "Tonight I want to ask you something I cannot ask on the record." She looked directly at her, and the lamplight-less gloom made their faces twins, shadow for shadow, scar for scar. "When you dream — and I know you dream it, because I dream it — do you see a cottage? Stone walls, red door, a garden gone wild with feverfew? Two girls in the yard. A woman's voice calling them in for supper. Does the woman's face—"

"—never resolves," Jude whispered.

Her heart was slamming. Because she had dreamed it. Every night since the stars went out, and — the vertigo hit her like cold water — *before*. For years, she realized. A recurring dream she'd never mentioned to anyone, dismissed as smuggler's superstition, a mind making houses out of hunger. Stone walls. Red door. Feverfew. Two girls, one always turned away, one always almost turning—

"And in the dream," Sera said softly, "when the girl turns around—"

"You wake up," Jude finished. "Every time. Before you can see her face."

"Not her face." Sera shook her head slowly. "*My* face. That's why you wake. That's why I wake. The dream won't show us what we already know." She laughed, one note, entirely without humor. "Eleven days ago I was a loyal instrument of the Grey Veil and the sky was full of stars, and now my Order has sealed its archives, recalled its field agents, and posted a standing order regarding a fugitive named Quintana — capture the Anchor, recover the shards, terminate resistance — and I stood at a riverbank today with a clean shot at you and watched my own quarrel miss on purpose, and I don't know anymore which of us is the weapon and which is the wound."

Outside, the wind moved through the bare alders, and in the spaces between gusts the silence pressed in — the god-shaped hole in the world, vast and patient.

"Why did you let me go?" Jude asked. "Really. The truth, inquisitor. You'll get no second chance at me."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Frost ticked on the turf roof. The shard pulsed its fog-light between them, and in its glow their faces were so alike it hurt to look at either.

"Because when you dropped into the river," she said finally, "you didn't swim for the far bank. You didn't run downstream. You surfaced once, behind the willow, and you looked back — not at my crossbows. At the alder stand. At the *south*. Toward the wood." Her jaw tightened. "Everyone in both realms is looking up right now, Quintana. At the empty sky. Asking what took the stars. But you looked at the ground. At the roots. As if you already knew where the answer was buried." She turned for the door, then paused, silhouetted against the starless dark. "The shrine at Hollowmere. If you're wise, you'll never go near it. If you're what I think you are, you're already halfway there. The priestess there — Mother Ivell — she'll know your name. She knew our mother's."

"Our mother's," Jude repeated. The words tasted like iron and feverfew.

"Ask her about the red door," Sera said. "And then decide whether you'd rather I find you first, or the rest of the Order does. Because I miss on purpose, sister. They don't."

She was gone into the dark, footsteps fading on frozen ground, leaving only frost-smell and the shard's slow breathing light.

Jude stood a long time in the empty hut, knife still bare, heart still hammering a smuggler's rhythm — *run, run, run* — but her eyes kept going south, the way they had at the willow, the way they had, she understood now, for her whole weightless life.

Three miles east, past the burned villages and the pyre-ash and the boy she'd saved, the Weeping Wood stood root-bound and silent on its hill. Waiting, she thought, for the first time in ten years, like a door she'd always known was there.

At dawn she started walking toward it.
