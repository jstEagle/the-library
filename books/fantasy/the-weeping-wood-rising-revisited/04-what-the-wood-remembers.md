## 4. What the Wood Remembers

The shrine road was older than the kingdom that had paved over it.

Jude knew this the way she knew most things about Thornfell — not from books or priests but from the soles of her boots and the habits of smugglers. The old paths ran beneath everything like veins beneath skin: the toll roads the wardens claimed, the salt roads the guilds kept secret, and beneath both, older than either, the pilgrim ways that had gone unmarked for a generation because no one wanted to remember what they led to.

She had not walked this one since she was small enough to ride on a carter's shoulders. She was not sure she remembered it at all. And yet her feet kept finding it — the flat stones under the peat, the way the path bent east around a hill that every map called by a different name, as though the land itself were embarrassed by what stood at its heart.

Perrin walked behind her, still smelling faintly of smoke. She'd tried to leave him at the last waystation with a woman who dried mushrooms for the guild, and he had simply appeared on the road an hour later, walking in her shadow with the stubbornness of a creature that had decided where it belonged. She hadn't the heart to try twice. Or perhaps she hadn't the courage. A boy pulled from a pyre was a debt she didn't know how to carry, and letting him follow was easier than thinking about why.

"You're going the wrong way," he said, for the fourth time since dawn. "The lowland camps are south."

"I'm not going to the camps."

"Then where?"

Jude looked up. Through the grey weave of cloud, where stars should have been — where *everything* should have been, moon and constellations, the whole stitched ceiling of the night — there was nothing. Not cloud-cover nothing. Nothing-nothing. A black so complete it made the daylight seem like a lamp lit in a sealed room.

"Somewhere I hoped I'd never have a reason to go," she said.

The boy was quiet after that. He was learning, she thought, the first lesson of the borderlands: some questions you ask only when you're ready to be lied to, and some lies are kindnesses.

* * *

The forest announced itself before she saw it.

It began as a smell — sweet and wrong, like honey poured over iron. Then a sound, or rather the absence of one. The woods around them had been loud all morning: jays, wind, the tick and shift of frost letting go of branches. But as they climbed the last ridge, the noise fell away from them like water off oiled cloth, and Jude realized the silence had a shape. It was coming from ahead. It was pouring downhill toward them like cold air, and where it touched, the birds stopped mid-phrase.

Perrin's hand found the back of her belt. She let him hold on.

They came over the ridge together, and Jude stopped breathing.

The Weeping Wood stood in a bowl of cleared ground, alone, the way a wounded man stands in a crowd. It was enormous — a single tree grown vast and strange, its trunk broad as a chapel, its bark pale grey and smooth as skin that had never seen sun. Its branches spread wider than any crown she'd ever seen, and from every branch hung long trailing filaments, willow-like, except these were not leaves. They were threads of sap, slow-falling, glinting, each one catching what little light the day offered and holding it like a bead of glass.

Her grandmother had told her about the Wood once, when Jude was seven and still believed the borderlands had gods in them the way rivers had fish. *It weeps for us*, the old woman had said. *That's how you know it loves us. Everything that loves us weeps.* Jude had forgotten the words for twenty years. They came back now whole, in her grandmother's voice, in the accent of a village that no longer existed.

And the Wood was weeping.

But not clear. Not the bright beads of legend.

The sap running down its trunk was red.

Not red like rowan berries or red like paint. Red like something that had been inside a body and should never have been outside it. It sheeted down the pale bark in slow ropes, pooled in the roots, soaked the moss at its feet until the whole clearing smelled of copper and sweetness, and the silence pouring off it was so total that Jude could hear her own pulse in her ears, could hear Perrin swallow beside her.

"It's hurt," Perrin whispered.

"Everything is hurt," Jude said. "Look at the sky."

"No." The boy's voice cracked with a certainty she envied. "Everything else got hurt *around* something. This—" he pointed at the trunk, at the dark wound high up where the red flowed thickest, a gash in the bark wide as a door— "this is where it started."

Jude wanted to tell him he was being a child about it. She opened her mouth to do exactly that, and then she felt it.

The scream.

It did not come through her ears. That was the horror of it — she would have preferred ears. Ears could be covered. This came up through the ground, through her boots, through the bones of her legs, a sound made of pressure and grief that bypassed every part of her that was a mercenary and a smuggler and a woman who had sold roads, and struck something underneath all of that, something she had no name for. Her knees buckled. She went down on one hand in the wet moss, and the moss was warm, and she understood with a lurch that it was warm because of what had soaked into it.

Beside her, Perrin said, "What? What is it?" and his voice sounded far away, underwater, because the scream was still going — no, not still going. It had only happened once. Three nights ago, the night the stars went out. She had heard it then too, alone on a ridge above the toll road, and had told herself it was wind in the wards, stress, exhaustion, the mind making ghosts out of a bad night.

But now she stood inside the thing that had screamed, and the silence here was not silence. It was the shape a scream leaves in a room afterward. It was the ringing bell after the strike. The Wood had screamed three nights ago and had been screaming inwardly ever since, and the silence was the sound of it, and her body recognized it the way a dog recognizes its own name spoken in another room.

"Jude." Perrin crouched next to her. "You're crying."

She touched her face. He was right. She hadn't noticed. The tears weren't hers — or they were, but they hadn't come through grief or fear; they'd come the way tears come when you yawn, reflexive, hydraulic, the body answering a call the mind never received.

"Stay close," she said. "Don't touch the sap. Don't touch anything."

* * *

The priestess was at the roots.

Jude saw her from thirty paces and thought at first she was a heap of offerings — the old shrines used to collect ribbons, bread, carved tokens, little braided charms from travelers asking favor of the Wood. But the heap shifted, and became a woman kneeling, forehead pressed to the bleeding bark, hands flat against the trunk as if she were trying to hold it shut. Her robes had once been white. They were stained rust-brown from hem to shoulder. Her hair had gone entirely white as well, though her face was not old — fifty, maybe, lined the way stone is lined by water rather than by years.

"Priestess," Jude called.

Nothing. The woman prayed on, murmuring into the bark, words worn down to syllables, a prayer said so many times it had been sanded smooth of meaning.

"Priestess!" Louder, and the silence drank even that.

So Jude did the thing her instincts screamed against and her feet decided anyway: she crossed the clearing, stepped over the root-veins of red, and put her hand on the woman's shoulder.

The priestess turned with a speed that had nothing human left in it.

Her eyes were the worst part. Not mad, exactly — Jude had seen madness, in soldiers, in miners after cave-ins, in herself after certain jobs. Madness scattered. These eyes were gathered. Focused. Burning with a terrible attention, like a woman reading a letter by firelight while the house burns around her.

"They took its voice," the priestess said.

"I—"

"They reached up into the sky and they took its voice out of the stars." Her hands left the trunk and seized Jude's wrists, and her grip was astonishing, iron wrapped in paper. "Every star was a word. Do you understand? Every star was one word of its speech, and the sky was its mouth, and for nine hundred years it spoke to us every night, and we forgot to listen, we forgot, we built tolls and laws and borders across its sentences—" Her voice climbed, frayed. "And now it has nothing left but weeping. Now it can only cry where we can see it. So it makes itself bleed where we can see it. Because we stopped listening to anything we couldn't see."

"Priestess, listen to me. The wards fell. Vaelmoor—"

"Vaelmoor." The woman spat the name like a seed. "Vaelmoor is a symptom. Vaelmoor is the hand. Someone paid the hand." Her burning eyes moved over Jude's face, and Jude felt the look like a searchlight, like a warden's lantern swept across a hidden campfire. "You came up the pilgrim road. No one comes up the pilgrim road anymore. Who told you the way?"

"No one. My feet—"

"Your feet." The priestess laughed, and the laugh was worse than the weeping. "Yes. Yes, I imagine they did."

She released Jude's wrists and rose — swaying, joints cracking like green wood in a fire — and stood before her, and up close Jude could see that the woman's lips were stained red too. She had been drinking the sap. Or the sap had been drinking her. There was no telling anymore which direction the exchange went between this woman and this tree.

"You have blood on your sleeve," the priestess said, quieter now. Almost gentle. "Warden blood?"

"A warden was going to burn a child."

"And you stopped him."

"Yes."

"With violence."

"Yes."

The priestess nodded slowly, as if ticking items off a list only she could read. "And before that? Before the stars went out. What were you?"

"A guide. A smuggler, if you want the ugly word. I moved goods along the old roads."

"What goods?"

"People, sometimes. Cargo, mostly. Things the tolls taxed too high and the law forbade outright." Jude heard her own voice go flat and professional, the tone she used at negotiations, and hated it. "I'm not proud of all of it."

"No," the priestess agreed softly. "You wouldn't be. That's the point of you."

Something cold slid down Jude's spine. "What's that supposed to mean?"

The priestess didn't answer. She had turned back toward the Wood, and she lifted both arms toward the great bleeding trunk the way a conductor lifts toward an orchestra, or a drowning woman toward a boat, and she said — clearly, formally, in the cadence of a rite:

"Hear me, Root-of-the-Border. Heartwood. Elder of Elders. The Anchor walks in the world again. She stands in your shadow. She hears you."

The effect was immediate and Jude would deny to her grave how it undid her.

The trailing filaments of sap — thousands of them, hanging motionless since she'd entered the clearing — began, all together, to sway. Not with wind. There was no wind. They swung toward her, all of them, every thread in that vast grey crown turning like compass needles, until the whole tree seemed to lean, seemed to regard her, and the silence changed key. It was still silent. But it was a listening silence now. A held-breath silence. Jude had spent her life reading rooms, reading ridgelines, reading the micro-expressions of men deciding whether to pay her or kill her, and every skill she owned told her the same impossible thing:

The tree was looking at her. And it knew her.

"Stop it," she said. Her voice came out small. "Whatever this is — priestess — stop it. I don't know what an anchor is. I'm not anything. I'm a caravan guide with a knife and a bad reputation. I've never sworn to your god. I've never prayed. I stole from shrines when I was hungry." Her breath hitched. "Why would it know me?"

The priestess lowered her arms. And Jude watched, in real time, as the madness in the woman's face gave way to something infinitely more frightening: recognition. Grief-stricken, wondering, horrified recognition — the look of someone finding a face they'd buried.

"Oh," the priestess whispered. "Oh, you *did* it. You actually did it. You paid the whole price."

"What price? What did I do?"

"You don't remember."

"No."

"Not any of it."

"*Any of what?*"

The priestess reached out and touched Jude's cheek — gently, the way you touch something you're afraid will break, or something you're afraid will break you — and her hand was slick with sap and it was warm, blood-warm, and Jude could not make herself pull away.

"The covenant," the priestess said. "The pact between the worlds, girl. The treaty written in living stone that holds Vaelmoor on its side of the dark and Thornfell on ours and keeps the sky sewn shut above both. It broke. Years ago. It was breaking, and the two realms were coming apart at the seam, and someone had to seal it, and sealing it required an Anchor — a living soul, willing, bound to both sides, who would give the covenant everything she was to hold it closed." Her thumb traced Jude's cheekbone. "Someone volunteered. Someone paid. And the payment was memory. All of it. The covenant ate her whole life and left a hollow woman standing on a smuggling road with quick hands and no past, and everyone who loved her was ordered never to speak her name to her again, because a reminder might unravel the seal."

The clearing tilted. Jude sat down without meaning to, right there in the bloody moss, and Perrin dropped beside her, saying her name, saying it twice, three times, and she heard him as if from the bottom of a well.

"That's a story," she managed. "That's a pilgrim's tale. Priestesses tell sad stories to explain bleeding trees."

"Do I look like I have the strength left to invent stories?" The priestess knelt in front of her, robes soaking red, uncaring. "Three nights ago the seal failed anyway. Something tore it loose from the other side — something that has been feeding on the Wood's silence, growing fat in the dark it made. The stars went out because the covenant's anchors went dark, all but one." She took Jude's hand, turned it palm-up, and pressed it flat against the mud, against the ground itself, and leaned close, and her whisper was almost tender.

"But the last anchor isn't a stone, child. Stones can be stolen. Stones can be broken. The last anchor is the woman who made the bargain. The covenant kept a piece of her back — it had to, a seal needs a keystone — and that piece has been walking around buying bread and sharpening knives and pulling children out of fires without knowing why she couldn't stop herself."

Jude's hand, against the earth, was trembling. Beneath the mud, faint as a heartbeat under a floorboard, something pulsed. Once. Twice. Patient. Enormous. Waiting.

"Say it," Jude whispered. She didn't know what she was asking. She asked anyway. "Say whatever it is you keep looking at me like you need to say."

The priestess bowed her head over Jude's hand, and her white hair spilled forward, and when she spoke, the formal rite-cadence was gone and only the woman remained, exhausted and certain and afraid.

"You are the Anchor, Jude Quintana. Last pillar of the covenant. The Weeping Wood's own. And the moon is turning its thirteenth turn, and when it completes it, the seal dies with it unless you finish what you started — and I don't know if you can, because I don't know if what's left of you is enough, and neither does the Wood, and it is *terrified*, can't you feel it, it has been terrified for three days—"

She stopped.

She swayed.

"Mother Root—" Perrin caught her elbow. "Priestess—"

The woman's eyes rolled back. Her grip on Jude's hand tightened once — a message, a plea, a farewell, Jude couldn't parse it — and then she collapsed sideways into the red-soaked moss, and the great tree's thousand threads of sap shuddered all at once, like a flinch, like a gasp.

"Priestess!" Jude was on her knees, pressing fingers to the woman's throat. A pulse — fast, thin, moth-winged, but there. "She's alive. Help me turn her—"

Beneath her palm, against the ground, the heartbeat in the earth beat again.

And this time, Jude felt it form a rhythm. Two beats, pause. Two beats, pause. Deliberate. Insistent. The rhythm of something that had once had a voice made of ten thousand stars, reduced now to knocking on the floor of the world, hoping the deaf woman kneeling on top of it would finally, finally listen.

Two beats. Pause. Two beats.

*Come,* said the Wood, in the only language it had left.

*Come and remember.*
