## 5. Thirteen Turns

The priestess did not wake.

For two days Jude carried her, slung across the mule's back like a sack of grain that occasionally moaned. The woman's weight was wrong for her frame — too heavy, as though grief had a mass and it had settled into her bones. Her lips moved constantly, shaping words with no sound behind them, and when Jude leaned close she caught fragments: *anchor*, *turns*, and once, clearly enough to raise the hair on her arms, *thirteen*.

Perrin walked beside the mule and didn't complain about his feet, which from Perrin was its own kind of alarm. The boy had been quiet since the shrine. Quiet in the way of someone who had watched an adult world crack open and was carefully noting where the pieces fell.

"Where are we even going?" he asked on the second evening, as the road bent down toward a smudge of lamplight in a fold of hills. "You know this place?"

"I know the man who owns the light." Jude shifted the priestess's slack weight. "Orrin Vask. He used to run star-charts up the Salt Road before the wards went sour. Smuggler first, astronomer second — or the other way around, depending on which cargo paid better."

"You trust him?"

"No one on these roads trusts anyone. But I've sold him honest charts and he's never sold me to the wardens, and in Thornfell that's nearly a marriage vow."

The light belonged to a waystation built into the shoulder of the hill, half dugout, half tower — a squat stone base crowned by a wooden observatory whose roof split and rolled back on greased runners. Even now, under a sky stripped bare, the slit of the roof gaped upward at nothing, the way a dog keeps watching a door its dead master used to come through.

Orrin Vask opened the door before Jude knocked. He was a narrow man, dried out like driftwood, with a lens on a chain around his neck and eyes that had spent sixty years learning to see in the dark. He looked at Jude, then at the priestess, then at the sky, in that order.

"Quintana," he said. "I'd heard you were guiding caravans through the end of the world."

"The world's not ended."

"Not yet." He stepped aside to let them in. "But it's started ending. Come in before something notices you're out there."

* * *

The inside of the tower smelled of ink, tallow, and old brass. Charts papered every wall — not maps of roads but of skies, decades of them, each one a night pinned down like a moth specimen. Orrin cleared a bench with his forearm, spilling instruments, and helped Jude lay the priestess down. She had stopped murmuring. That frightened Jude more than the words had.

"She was at the shrine," Jude said. "The Wood is — bleeding. Weeping sap like blood, and the stars are gone, and she kept saying my name like she knew me."

"Everyone on the border knows your name lately, Quintana. You're the smuggler the sky died on." Orrin lit a second lamp, then a third, filling the room with overlapping circles of light. "Though if you're asking whether she knew you before this week—" he paused, lens swinging gently against his chest, "—that's a different chart entirely."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means sit down. It means I'm going to show you something I've shown no one, because until four nights ago I thought I was a mad old man who talked to lamps." He pulled a rolled chart from an iron tube bolted beneath the bench, unrolled it, and weighted the corners with brass spheres. "And it means you're going to want to be sitting when you understand what you're looking at."

Jude sat. Perrin crowded in beside her, close enough that she could feel him trembling — not fear, exactly. The specific shiver of a boy trying very hard to be brave in a room full of adults being afraid.

The chart was a record of the moon. Not one drawing but hundreds, small silver circles marching in rows across the vellum, each annotated in Orrin's cramped hand: dates, positions, phases. And threaded through them all, drawn in red ink so fine it looked like stitching, a line — a spiral, tightening, turning inward on itself like water finding a drain.

"This is the moon's path over the last year," Orrin said. His voice had gone flat, the voice of a man reading numbers he wished were lies. "Every night since the wards fell, I've recorded it. And every night it has been moving wrong. Not drifting. *Spiraling.* Each turn of the moon around us comes slightly short of the last. Closer. Faster. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Jude stared at the red thread winding inward. "No."

"Neither did I, at first. So I went into the old records. Pre-covenant charts — there are a few, locked away, older than the border itself." He tapped a column of annotations. "And in those records, there is a term. *Turn.* Not a phase. Not a month. A *turn* is one complete circuit of the moon around the twin realms, measured against the fixed stars. Twelve turns to a cycle. Twelve turns, twelve months of the old reckoning, and the covenant between Thornfell and Vaelmoor was renewed at the completion of each twelfth turn — renewed by the Anchor, at the covenant site, with the stones."

"Renewed by who?"

"By whoever held the covenant. By the Anchor." Orrin looked at her for a long moment, lamp-light swimming in his old eyes. "That was always a title, girl. I thought it meant a family. A lineage. Some order of priests keeping a calendar nobody else remembered." He exhaled slowly. "Then four nights ago the stars went out, the wards fell, and I stayed up all night doing arithmetic with shaking hands."

He turned the chart toward her fully. At the center of the red spiral, where the lines converged, he had written a date. One season hence. Midwinter's edge.

"One season from tonight, the moon completes its thirteenth turn. There has never been a thirteenth turn. The count stops at twelve; the covenant renews; the count begins again. That's the shape the worlds were built in. But without renewal—" his finger traced the tightening spiral, "—the moon doesn't reset. It finishes the thirteenth circuit and then it comes *down*. Or the realms tear apart along the old seam and everything drains out through the wound. The astronomers of Vaelmoor knew this. Why do you think their inquisitors are already on our side of the border? They're not hunting smugglers, Quintana. They're hunting whatever's left of the mechanism."

Jude's mouth had gone dry. "The priestess called me something. Before she collapsed. She said—" the word came out of her unwillingly, "—she said *Anchor*."

Orrin nodded slowly, unsurprised, which was somehow worse.

"And the stones?" Jude pressed. "There are three stones. Covenant-stones. How do you know that?"

"I don't. *She* does." He nodded at the priestess. "Or your god does, speaking through whoever it can still reach. But I'll tell you what I know, which is this: the old records say the covenant isn't a wall or a treaty. It's a knot. Three cords, tied together — memory, oath, and root, the scholars named them — and tied by a living hand. When the Anchor renewed it each twelfth turn, she touched all three stones and the knot held and the thirteenth turn never came." He straightened, joints cracking like green wood. "Your stars didn't go out because someone attacked the border, girl. Your stars went out because the knot is untied, and everything the border was holding up — the lights, the wards, the sky itself — was hung on that knot."

Outside, wind moved through the dark with nothing above it to make the wind visible. No cloud-shadows racing over the land. No moonlight silvering the grass. Just wind, moving blind.

"So I find the stones," Jude said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "Before midwinter's edge. All three. And I tie the knot."

"If you're the Anchor." Orrin gathered the chart with careful hands, rolling it the way other men handled relics. "If you're anything. If the title doesn't require things of you I can't read in these records." He paused at the iron tube. "Because here's the last thing, and you'll want to hear it sitting down too, though you already are. The records say the renewal was made *willingly*. Every time. Freely given, freely renewed. Whatever the price of it was—" his old eyes found hers, and held, "—it was never paid twice by force."

Jude looked at the priestess, silent on the bench. At Perrin, who had gone very still, listening the way children listen when they understand more than they let on. At the walls of charts, a lifetime of nights pinned down by a man who had watched the sky die alone and kept taking notes.

One season. Thirteen turns rising.

"All right," she said, to none of them, to the wind outside, to the thing beneath the earth knocking its two-beat rhythm. "Then we'd better move fast."

* * *

Four hundred miles east, in the grey halls of the Inquisitorial Bastion at Corvane's Crossing, Sera Corvane stood before her superiors and lied.

"The smuggler escaped me at the river," she said. "She crossed into the hill country. I lost the trail in the rain."

It was not entirely a lie. There had been rain. She simply omitted that she could have crossed it too, and had chosen, in a moment she still could not account for, not to pursue.

First Inquisitor Halden sat behind a desk of black Vaelmoor oak, and behind him the window showed the same starless dark that showed everywhere now. Grey-robed clerks worked by lamplight in the ranks beyond, transcribing interrogations, and the sound of their pens was the only weather the Bastion allowed.

"You let her keep the shard," Halden said.

"The shard was worthless without the others. I judged pursuit more valuable than recovery."

"Did you." Halden folded his hands. He had a scholar's face and a headsman's patience, and Sera had watched men walk into his judgments certain they were innocent and walk out understanding otherwise. "Inquisitor, you will tell me why your report describes the fugitive's face in detail unnecessary to identification. You described her eyes twice."

Sera had known the question was coming. She had written the report herself at three in the morning, hands steady, mind not, and had read it over at dawn and understood that she might as well have signed a confession.

"She resembled someone," Sera said. "A figure from early training memories. Irrelevant to the mission."

"Nothing is irrelevant to the mission." Halden rose and came around the desk, and from the sleeve of his grey robe he drew a folio sealed with the Order's sigil — a circle bisected by a line, the mark of the divided realm. "Since you raise training memories. Tell me what you recall of your childhood."

"I was taken in by the Order at six years of age. Records prior to that are incomplete. I was told my family died in the border wars."

"Incomplete." Halden set the folio on the desk between them but did not unseal it. "We have been conducting our own inquiry since the wards fell. The Order's archives contain a gap — forty years old, precisely cut. Someone removed a set of records concerning the covenant site and the family that served it. Someone with access and authority." He studied her. "And in the surviving fragments, Inquisitor, there is a name that appears twice. Once in a list of the dead. Once in a margin note beside the word *Anchor*. The name is Corvane."

The lamps seemed to dim, or perhaps the room tilted; Sera could not have said which. "That is not possible. My name was assigned by the Order."

"Names are rarely assigned, Inquisitor. They are *returned*." Halden pushed the folio an inch toward her, then — deliberately — drew it back. "Here is what concerns me. Our diviners confirm the moon completes its thirteenth turn at midwinter's edge. The First Doctrine is clear: if the covenant cannot be restored under Order control, it must fail entirely. A reforged pact means another century of the border, of the divided realms, of Thornfell's superstitions poisoning the continent. The Order was built to end that. Which means the woman the priestesses call the Anchor must not reach the stones."

"And your orders for me?"

"Find her again. And this time, Inquisitor—" Halden's scholar's face did something almost gentle, which was the cruelest thing Sera had ever seen him do, "—this time, kill her. Bring me proof. Consider it a test of whether your resemblance to irrelevant memories has compromised your instrument."

Sera bowed, the full formal bow, forehead nearly to the floor, and took the dismissal she was given.

Only at the door did Halden speak again.

"One more matter. The exile called the Hollow King has sent riders into the pass country. He seeks the stones as well — though what a man wants with a godless dark, the Order cannot fathom. If you encounter his people, treat them as rivals. The realms may tear apart, Inquisitor, but Vaelmoor intends to be the one holding the knife when they do."

* * *

Sera rode west through the blind dark with the folio unopened on her saddlebow.

She should have burned it. Orders permitted the destruction of documents rather than their capture. Instead she sat her horse through leagues of starless country, feeling the sealed vellum under her gauntlet like a second pulse, and at a crossroads shrine — one of the old ones, a carved post worn smooth by centuries of travelers' touching — she stopped, dismounted, and broke the seal.

Inside were three pages. A fragment of a census: *the Anchor's household, covenant site, two daughters.* A scrap of a duty roster bearing the surname Corvane in an unfamiliar clerk's hand. And the third page was not a document at all but a child's drawing, preserved behind wax paper: two small figures under a large tree, one tall and one taller, holding hands, rendered in charcoal by someone who had been maybe five years old. Beneath it, in a different, older hand: *my girls.*

Sera stood at the roadside shrine in the middle of a dying world and could not remember the tree. Could not remember the mother. Could not remember a sister — and yet when she closed her eyes she saw rain on a river crossing and a smuggler's face turned up toward hers, and some part of her, some part older than the Order, older than doctrine, older than her own assigned name, said with absolute certainty:

*I know you.*

She put the pages away. She did not report their contents. For the second time, Sera Corvane lied to her Order, and discovered that the lie fit her like a glove fitted long ago, waiting for a hand.

Then she turned her horse west, toward the hill country, toward the drowned salt road and the lake called Verren, riding hard through the dark after a woman she had been ordered to kill — and found, examining herself honestly for perhaps the first time in her adult life, that she did not know which errand she was actually on.

Behind her, far to the east over Vaelmoor, where no stars remained to measure by, the invisible moon climbed toward its impossible thirteenth turn, and the season began to run.
