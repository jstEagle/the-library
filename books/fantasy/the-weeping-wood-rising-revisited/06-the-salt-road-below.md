## 6. The Salt Road Below

The lake did not look like a door.

It looked like the rest of the world had come to look: grey water lapping at a shingle beach, reeds hissing in a wind that smelled of iron and old rain, and far out, where the depth began, a darkness so complete it seemed less like absence of light than presence of something that had eaten the light and was still digesting it. Lake Verren. On the smugglers' charts it was marked with a single word in an old hand — *under* — and nothing else.

Jude stood at the waterline with her boots off and her trousers rolled to the knee, because the priestess had insisted, and because she had learned in the last week that the priestess's insistences were not madness but memory wearing madness like a coat two sizes too large.

"It opens for the Anchor," the priestess said. Her name was Maren, though she had stopped answering to it somewhere between the shrine and the mountains. Now she answered only to *we*, as if the Wood spoke through her mouth and she were merely the door it leaned against. "It has always opened for the Anchor. The wardens sealed it behind them when they went down, but seals are just prayers made stubborn, and prayers know their maker."

"The wardens went *down*?" Perrin asked. He was crouched at the edge of the shingle, poking at the water with a stick he refused to put down, the way other children refused to put down blankets or thumbs. "Wardens drowned themselves?"

"They didn't drown." Maren's eyes were the colour of tea left too long, and they did not track things the way living eyes should. They tracked things the way weather does. "They walked down singing the toll-warden's oath, and the oath holds them still, the way a hand keeps holding a sword after the arm is cut away. Law is patient, child. It outlives everything it was meant to protect."

Jude looked out at the dark water and felt the familiar cold settle behind her sternum, the one that had lived there since the night the stars went out. She had crossed Lake Verren a dozen times in her smuggling years — over it, never under. There had been stories about under. Every road had its ghost stories, and the salt road beneath Verren was the oldest one in Thornfell: a trade passage from before the realms split, sunk when the lake rose, kept dry by wards older than any kingdom's name. Smugglers said you could walk from one shore to the other beneath the water and never wet your head, if the road let you. Smugglers also said the road charged tolls of its own.

She had sold roads once. She knew what tolls cost.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," she said, mostly to hear her own voice, which sounded steadier than she felt.

Maren turned her face toward Jude, and for a moment the madness thinned and something ancient and terribly kind looked out through it. "Because the first stone is down there, Anchor. The stone of Memory. Because the moon is turning, and the world is coming apart at the seams, and you are the needle. Because you asked me to tell you, every hour, and I will keep telling you until you stop needing to be told."

"I don't remember asking."

"You ask every hour," Maren said gently. "You simply don't remember that either."

Perrin stood up, brushing shingle grit off his knees with the gravity of a man twice his age and four times his size. He was eleven, maybe twelve, thin as a whip handle, with a burn scar crawling up his left forearm from the pyre Jude had pulled him off of, and he had not let her out of his sight since Ash on the Toll Road. Not out of fear. Out of the particular ferocity of children who have decided someone is theirs.

"I'm ready," he said.

"You're not coming."

"I'm coming." He said it the way he said everything now — flat, final, a small stone wall of a voice. "You saved me off a fire. That means I'm yours till the debt's paid. My gran taught me that. Debts get paid or they rot, and rotted debts follow you." He patted his chest, where something hung under his shirt on a cord — he'd carried it since the burning village, some scrap he'd snatched from the flames, wouldn't say what. "Besides. Somebody has to hold the lamp."

Jude opened her mouth to argue and found, to her irritation, that she had no argument that would survive contact with those eyes. She had faced down warden captains and guild enforcers and one Vaelmoor inquisitor who claimed to be her sister, and none of them had beaten her in an argument. This child did it by standing very still and meaning it completely.

"Lamp stays lit," she said finally. "You stay between me and Maren. If I say run, you run, and you don't look back, and you don't argue."

"I never argue," Perrin said, arguing.

Jude pulled her boots back on. The lake waited.

* * *

The road opened the way Maren promised: they walked into the shallows at the place where three flat stones lay in a triangle half-sunk in the shingle, and the water parted around Jude's ankles, around her knees, around her waist, folding back from her body like a curtain drawn by unseen hands, until she stood on a descending ramp of black stone with a corridor of water rising on either side of her — walls of lake, held vertical by nothing, fish drifting past at eye level like birds in a green sky, light sifting down from above in long trembling columns.

It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen and it made her want to scream.

"Keep walking," Maren said behind her. "Don't touch the walls. The walls remember being sea, and the sea remembers being angry."

The salt road descended in long shallow ramps, wide enough for six ox-carts abreast in its day, and its day had been long ago indeed. Pillars flanked the way, each carved with figures Jude recognized from no temple and no book: tall robed shapes whose faces were leaves, whose hands were roots, standing with palms open toward the water walls. Between the pillars ran grooves worn smooth by centuries of cart wheels — and newer scratches, sharp-edged, recent.

"Wardens' tally marks?" Perrin whispered. His voice behaved strangely down here, flattened, as if the water drank the sound before it could echo.

"No," Maren said. "Counting is a living habit. Those are *prayers*. Scratched by hands that wanted to pray and had forgotten how, so they wrote the wanting instead."

They walked. Jude counted her own steps because counting was a living habit and she intended to stay alive. Two hundred steps. Four hundred. The light from above thinned to a dim green murk, then to a darkness the lamp pushed back in a small gold circle. The air grew cold and mineral, tasting of brine and stone and something older underneath both — the smell she had begun to think of as *the god's breath*, the same smell that had wept from the wounded shrine.

At seven hundred steps the first of them appeared.

It stood at the far edge of the lamplight, where the ramp bent around a pillar, and for one merciful moment Jude took it for a statue: a figure in warden grey, helm on, spear upright, standing at attention beside the road as toll-wardens had stood at every crossing in Thornfell for three hundred years.

Then the lamplight moved, and the figure turned its head to follow it, and the helm had no face inside. Or rather — it had a face, but the face was water. A slow shifting of silt and shadow where features should have been, and two points of pale light deep in it that might once have been eyes, and might still be, in whatever way dead law keeps its eyes.

"Halt," the drowned warden said. Its voice came through the water walls rather than the air, arriving everywhere at once, the way thunder arrives. "State your business on the King's road. State your toll. State your lawful purpose, in the name of the Crown of Thornfell, keeper of the borders, guardian of the—"

It stopped. The pale lights in its face flickered, guttered, steadied. When it spoke again the voice was smaller, frayed, a rope unraveling.

"—keeper of the borders," it repeated. "Guardian of the..." The pale lights drifted, searching. "...I am Warden-Captain Elsen Vay. Third Company. I am waiting for relief. Has relief come?"

Jude's hand was on her knife without her having decided anything. She made herself take it off. Made herself step forward instead, into the full lamplight, hands open.

"We're travellers," she said. "Bound under the lake. We mean the road no harm."

"Travellers require papers." The water-face tilted. "Travellers require... the border is closed. The border has been closed for—" The lights flickered again. "For some time. Forgive me. How long has the border been closed?"

Jude thought of the night the stars went out. Thought of the burned villages, the pyres, the panic dressed up as duty. "A while," she said carefully. "Things have changed up there, captain."

"Changed." The drowned thing considered this. Water slid slowly down inside its helm and did not fall out. "Change requires a writ. Change requires the seal of the Crown. Is the Crown well? Is the Crown—" and here the voice did something Jude had not been prepared for, something that went through her ribs like a splinter. It *hoped*. "Is the Crown coming to relieve us? We have held the road. We have held it faithfully. No smuggler has passed. No toll has gone uncollected. We have held it even after the water came, even after the breathing stopped, because the oath does not end when the breath ends, the oath ends when the *law* ends, and no one has told us the law has ended."

Behind her, Perrin made a small sound. Jude did not turn to look at him. If she looked at him she would look at Maren, and if she looked at Maren she would see the answer written there, and she wasn't ready to read it.

"The law hasn't ended," she heard herself lie. "Hold your post, captain."

"Thank you." Relief moved through the drowned shape like a current, silt swirling, the pale lights softening. "Thank you. It is good to be seen. It has been so long since anyone was *lawful* on this road." It stepped aside, spear presenting itself in a salute three hundred years out of date, and Jude walked past it with her skin crawling and her throat tight, and did not let herself breathe fully again for another hundred steps.

There were more of them. Of course there were more. They stood at intervals along the descending miles, grey sentinels in the dark, each one asking the same questions in slightly different stages of decay. One had lost its voice entirely and communicated by raising its spear, blocking the road until Maren touched its gauntlet and murmured something in a language that made the water walls shiver. One had forgotten it was a warden and asked them, plaintively, whether they had seen its wife, who was surely waiting at the toll-house with supper going cold. One simply wept — great slow clouds of silt bleeding from its helm like ink, endlessly, silently, mourning something it could no longer name.

"They can't leave," Perrin said, somewhere around the thousandth step, in the smallest voice Jude had ever heard him use. "Can they? Nobody ever came and told them."

"No," Maren said. "Nobody came."

"That's not right." The boy's fists closed. "That's not *right*. Somebody should tell them."

"Somebody should," Maren agreed, and looked at Jude, and Jude looked at the floor of the road and kept walking, because there was a stone to find and a moon turning and no time to grieve for every faithful corpse in the dark — and hated herself a little more with each step, the way you do when you realize you've become the kind of person who walks past.

* * *

The bottom of the road was a vault.

The ramp ended at a chamber vast enough that the lamplight couldn't find its ceiling, its walls faced in the same black stone, carved — when Jude raised the lamp high enough to see — with the story of the covenant in a frieze that ran all the way around: two realms rendered as trees, their branches interlocking overhead, rooted on opposite shores of a river of stars. In the centre of the frieze stood a third figure, smaller, human, arms raised, holding the branches together where they strained apart. The carvers had given the figure a face, but the face had been chiselled away — deliberately, recently, the marks still sharp.

Someone had erased the Anchor from the story. Someone had wanted the story to forget her.

And in the middle of the chamber floor, set into a dais of white salt crystal like a wound in the black stone, lay the first covenant-stone.

It was smaller than Jude had imagined — a shard really, a fragment the size of a shield boss, its broken edges glowing with a faint amber light that pulsed, slow and steady, like something sleeping. The air above it shimmered. The water walls of the chamber leaned inward, all around, straining toward it the way flowers strain toward sun, and Jude understood suddenly that the road, the vault, the whole impossible architecture of dry passage beneath the lake — all of it was built around this fragment. The stone wasn't hidden in the road. The road was grown around the stone, like scar tissue around a splinter.

Drowned wardens ringed the dais. Eight of them, closer together than the ones along the road, spears crossed, forming a fence of grey flesh and older grey steel. Their water-faces were all turned toward the stone, and the pale lights in all of them burned brighter than the others', fed on its glow, perhaps, or feeding it — Jude couldn't tell which, and suspected the distinction had stopped mattering centuries ago.

"Captain," Jude called, to the largest of them, the one whose pauldrons bore the scratched remnants of rank. "We've come for the stone. By lawful authority."

The eight heads turned as one. The silence that followed had texture, weight, the pressure of eight dead laws considering one living question.

"There is no lawful authority," the captain said at last, "but the Crown."

"The Crown is gone."

"Then there is no lawful authority," the captain said, without anger, without grief, with the terrible flat patience of a rule that has outlived its reason, "and the stone remains in our keeping. Until relieved."

Jude looked at the crossed spears. Looked at the pulsing amber heart of the vault. Looked at Maren, who stood with her eyes closed and her lips moving, listening to something far away. Looked at Perrin, who had gone very pale and very still, and whose hand had crept inside his shirt to grip whatever he carried against his heart.

"How do we relieve you?" she asked.

The captain's water-face shifted. Something moved in it — surprise, maybe, or the ghost of surprise, the last reflex of a man who had died waiting for exactly this question and never believed he'd hear it.

"You cannot," it said. "Relief must come from the Crown. The Crown is gone. Therefore we wait forever. That is the oath."

"Oaths are just prayers made stubborn," Maren said softly, opening her eyes. "And prayers know their maker." She stepped forward, past Jude, past the lamplight, into the amber glow, and the drowned wardens did not stop her — could not, Jude realized, watching their spears tremble; the priestess was not a traveller, not a smuggler, not a thing their law had words for. "You swore to the Crown of Thornfell. But who crowned the Crown, captain? Who bound the borders before there were borders? Whose law is older than the law you died holding?"

The pale lights flickered. All sixteen of them, guttering like candles in wind.

"The... Wood," the captain said. The word came out of it like a tooth being pulled. "The Wood crowned the kings. The Wood sealed the... the..."

"The covenant," Maren said. "And the Wood's anchor stands before you." She gestured, and her tea-coloured eyes found Jude's across the vault, and there was no madness in them at all now, only a pity so vast it looked like cruelty. "Look at her, captain. Look, and be relieved."

Eight drowned faces turned to Jude.

And the water in them *moved* — all at once, all together, a slow revolving shift, silt settling, shadows rearranging, and for one suspended heartbeat Jude saw what Maren wanted them to see: eight pairs of dead eyes looking at her face and finding, in its lines, in its stubborn jaw and storm-grey gaze, the reflection of something they had sworn to before they'd ever sworn to any crown.

The spears lowered. All eight, in unison, with a sound like a sigh underwater.

"Be welcome, Anchor," the captain said, and its voice cracked down the middle, law and grief in equal measure. "The road is yours. It was always meant to be yours. We only... we only wanted someone to tell us we could stop."

Jude's throat closed. She managed, barely, "You're relieved. All of you. Thank you for holding the road."

The thank-you undid them. She watched it happen — watched the rigid discipline go out of eight dead bodies like string cut from puppets, watched them sag, kneel, bow their helms to the salt-crystal floor. And then, one by one, they came apart. Not violently. Gently. The water of them loosening from the armour of them, draining away through seams and joints, leaving behind eight empty suits of warden grey collapsing softly onto the stone, and a fine bright mist rising through the water walls toward a surface and a sky none of them would ever see again.

Perrin cried. Jude discovered she was crying too, and had been for some time, and hadn't noticed.

* * *

The stone sat in her cupped hands heavier than its size, warm as a living thing, pulsing against her palms in rhythm — and it took her an absurdly long time, standing in that emptied vault, to recognize the rhythm as her own heartbeat.

"Take it up the road," Maren said. "Not yet touching your skin. Wrap it. It will want to give you things, and what it gives, it takes payment for, and you are not ready to learn the price in the dark under a lake."

"What does it give?" Perrin asked, wrapping it in his own shirt without being asked, because apparently that was simply what Perrin did now.

"Memories," Maren said. "It is the stone of Memory. It remembers everyone who ever swore at this altar. Including—" she paused, and the kindness in her face became something almost unbearable, "—including you, Anchor. Especially you."

Jude looked at the wrapped bundle in the boy's arms, glowing faintly through the cloth like a banked coal, and felt the cold behind her sternum shift for the first time since the stars went out. Not colder. Warmer. Reaching.

Above them, far away, through fathoms of held-back lake, the sky was still starless. Somewhere on the roads, an inquisitor in grey was hunting them with her sister's face. And here in Jude's hands, wrapped in a rescued child's shirt, the first of three keys to the end of the world beat gently against her heartbeat, like a fist knocking on a door she had locked herself, from the inside, years ago.

"Come on," she said, and her voice came out rough. "Let's get up top. Before it starts talking."

It started talking before they reached the first bend of the ramp.

And the first thing it said, in a voice like her own voice remembered from outside herself, was a name she had never heard anyone speak aloud, and had been dreaming for weeks:

*Sera.*
