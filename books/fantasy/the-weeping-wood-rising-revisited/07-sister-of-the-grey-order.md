## 7. Sister of the Grey Order

The bells of the Grey Order did not ring in Vaelmoor. That was the first thing they taught you. Bells were for the borderlands, for the soft southern realms where people needed to be told when to pray and when to sleep and when to bury their dead. In Vaelmoor you kept time by the ledger. The day began when the archive opened its iron doors, and it ended when your report was filed, and everything between was measured in ink.

Sera Corvane had been filing reports since she was nine years old.

She sat now in the scriptorium of the Bastion Verane, third window from the left, with a candle burning low and a half-finished dispatch about a river crossing that she could not make herself write honestly.

*Subject encountered at Verrin ford, 14th of the Ash Turn. Subject resisted apprehension. Subject escaped.*

All true. All beside the point.

She dipped her quill, held it over the page, and watched the ink gather at the nib like a small black wound.

*Subject called me sister.*

Her hand did not shake. Her hands never shook; the Order trained that out of you before your voice finished changing. But there was a tremor somewhere deeper, some subcutaneous thing, a hairline crack running through the bedrock of her, and every time she thought the word *sister* she felt it widen by a degree too small to measure and too large to ignore.

She wrote: *Pursuit continues. No further anomalies noted.*

It was the first lie she had ever put into an official report. She knew this with terrible clarity, the way you know the exact moment a rope begins to fray — not because it snaps, but because you feel the fibers part under your palm.

Outside the scriptorium's high windows, Vaelmoor went on being Vaelmoor: grey stone, grey sky, the permanent administrative dusk of a country that had legislated away its weather. Lantern-lighters moved along the bastion wall with the punctual gloom of men who had done the same rounds for thirty years. Somewhere below, in the Hall of Records, ten thousand drawers of indexed certainty waited to tell Sera exactly who she was.

She was going to go down there tonight. She had decided it on the riverbank, with Jude Quintana's blood on her knuckles and the taste of a name in her mouth that no one had ever spoken to her.

*Corvane* was not a family name. It was an assignment name, given to foundlings raised in the Order's crèches, and Sera had never once wondered what came before it. Wondering was inefficiency. Wondering was the first symptom listed in the Inquisitor's Handbook, Chapter One, *On Sentimental Contamination*: *the subject begins to ask questions to which the answer changes nothing.*

But the woman at the ford had looked at her — really looked, the way you look at a door in a house you've lived in all your life that you have suddenly realized you have never opened — and said nothing at all, and her saying nothing had been worse than any accusation. Because Sera had seen her own face do something in response. She had felt her own mouth almost form a word. And the word had been ready. It had been waiting behind her teeth like a coin kept in a cheek for years, worn smooth from turning.

*Jude.*

She hadn't said it. She'd let the woman go, which was itself an anomaly worth reporting, and then she had stood in the shallows of the Verrin while her quarry vanished into the reeds, holding a shard of covenant-stone that wasn't hers, listening to her own heartbeat misbehave.

* * *

The Hall of Records smelled of dust and tallow and the particular cold of stone that has never once been warm. The archivists worked in silence under hooded lamps, moving through the stacks on wheeled ladders like pale, patient birds.

Sera gave her name at the desk. The under-clerk — a boy of perhaps sixteen with ink to his elbows — consulted his ledger without looking up.

"Corvane, Sera. Inquisitor, Third Chair. What records do you require?"

"Foundling intake," she said. "Bastion Verane creche. Twenty-two years ago. Winter intakes."

He led her into the stacks, past drawer after drawer after drawer, each one labelled in the Order's unlovely blockhand. He pulled the drawer, set it on a reading table, lit a second lamp, and withdrew to a respectful distance — close enough to observe protocol, far enough to pretend he wasn't observing her.

Sera opened the drawer.

Intake records were thin things. A date. A place of collection — usually a border village, usually burned or flooded or taxed into ruin, because that was where foundlings came from. A health notation. An assigned name. The Order named children after virtues and instruments, mostly: Temperance, Fulcrum, Ledger, Quill. Sera Corvane, the record said, collected from the hamlet of Alder's Rest during the warden purges of that winter, aged approximately four years, no surviving kin, health adequate, disposition watchful.

*Alder's Rest.* She turned the word over. She had no feeling attached to it. That was normal; four-year-olds remembered nothing. The Handbook said so. There was a whole appendix on it.

She checked the rest of the winter intake anyway. Eleven children. Eleven names. None of them *Jude*. None of them anything like Jude.

"Is there another index?" she asked the under-clerk. "Border collections. Cross-referenced by village rather than year."

"For the same period? Yes, Inquisitor." He hesitated. "Though the Alder's Rest entries were consolidated after the fire."

"What fire?"

"The hamlet burned two years after the intake. Warden action, according to the annotation. Most of the local records went with it." He said it the way he would have said *it rained*, and Sera understood suddenly how a whole childhood could be erased inside an institution whose only sacrament was the record. You didn't need to burn a person out of history. You just needed the right clerk on the right night, and an annotation, and a drawer that closed as smoothly as any other.

She copied the eleven names into her notebook. Then, because the crack in her bedrock had widened enough to let light through, she did something no Third Chair should do: she asked to see the creche rosters. Not the intakes. The rosters — the daily lists of children actually present, year by year, in the Order's own careful handwriting.

The clerk fetched them, frowning faintly, and Sera carried them to the table and began, with a researcher's patience she did not know she possessed, to read.

* * *

It happened the way it always happens, which is sideways.

She was halfway through the fourth year's roster, a page of ninety-odd assigned names in alphabetical blocks, when her eye snagged on a margin note. Small. Faded. In different ink from the roster itself, added later by some supervisor with a fine-nibbed pen:

*C. has again spoken of the "other girl." Corrected. Note persists despite correction. Recommend observation.*

C. There was only one C in the Verane creche that year. *Corvane, Sera.*

She read it again. Read it a third time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the deep stacks a ladder rolled, unhurried, indifferent.

*The other girl.*

And underneath the note, like sediment under floodwater, the memory rose.

Not a memory of documents. A memory of warmth. Of a summer that could not have happened in Vaelmoor, because Vaelmoor did not have summers, only administrative seasons — but there it was: grass going gold on a hillside above a grey wall, and a fence made of woven willow, and a girl on the other side of it with dark hair chopped short by someone who didn't know how, showing Sera how to whistle through a blade of grass so the sound came out ragged and alive. The girl was laughing. Laughing at Sera's failure, laughing in a way that made failure feel like a game rather than a deficiency, and Sera — small Sera, creche Sera, Sera before the Order had finished building her — was laughing too, helplessly, the sound tearing out of her uncorrected.

"You're doing it wrong," the girl said. "You have to mean it. The grass can tell."

"The grass can't tell."

"It can. Everything can. That's the whole problem." And the girl had grinned, gap-toothed, and pressed the blade of grass back into Sera's hands, folding her fingers around it, and her hands had been warm, warmer than anything in the bastion, warmer than—

Sera came back to herself standing up, both palms flat on the reading table, the roster crumpled slightly under her fingers. The under-clerk was watching her from his distance with carefully blank concern.

"Inquisitor?"

"Nothing." She smoothed the page. "A cramp."

She looked down at the roster. Ninety names. She read them one by one, slowly, the way you search a crowd for a face, and of course there was no girl with dark hair and a gap-toothed grin, because there was no such child, because the intake record said *no surviving kin*, singular, alone, collected, assigned.

But the margin note said otherwise. The margin note said *again*. As in: not the first time. As in: a supervisor, years ago, writing down with mild bureaucratic irritation that little Sera Corvane kept insisting she had a sister.

Sera closed the roster. Her hands were perfectly steady. That was the horror of it — the training held, it always held, it would hold until the day it didn't — and beneath the steadiness the crack ran on and on, and through it she could hear, very faintly, a blade of grass whistling ragged and alive on a hillside that did not exist.

*Everything can tell,* the girl had said. *That's the whole problem.*

* * *

The summons came at dawn, which meant it was important, because the Grey Order summoned no one at dawn unless the matter concerned the moon.

Third Chair Corvane was received in the Chamber of Instruments, a round room at the top of the Bastion where the Order kept its orreries and astrolabes and its great armillary sphere, brass rings within brass rings, all of them frozen mid-turn since the night the stars went out. Twelve rings for twelve moons, and a thirteenth ring at the center that no one had ever explained to her, painted black, motionless.

First Chair Ondrell Vasse stood before the dead sphere. He was a narrow man, grey as his order, with the kind of face that seemed designed to be forgotten by portraitists. He did not turn when she entered.

"Do you know why the stars went out, Third Chair?"

"They are covered," Sera said. "By the collapse of the border wards. The covenant—"

"The covenant is a story we tell southerners to make their lawlessness sound like theology." Vasse touched the black ring at the sphere's heart with one finger, gently, the way a man touches a scar. "What actually happened is that something on the other side of the border stopped paying attention to us. The stars are not lights, Third Chair. They are eyes. They are the Wood's eyes, and the Wood has been taken, and while it is taken, the eyes are shut. Do you understand what that means?"

"That the wards—"

"That the wards were never ours." Now he turned. His eyes were the pale, patient grey of old ice. "They were lent. By a god who is currently a prisoner in Vaelmoor's deepest vault, and who is the single greatest strategic asset this realm has acquired in five hundred years. The Directorate intends to keep him." He folded his hands. "Which brings us to the Anchor."

"The smuggler." Sera kept her voice level. "Jude Quintana."

"A smuggler. A mercenary. And also, according to our diviners, the last living anchor of the covenant — the one mortal soul through whom the Wood can still be reached, or freed, or bound more tightly, depending entirely on who holds her leash." Vasse smiled without warmth. "You intercepted her at the Verrin ford."

"I engaged her. She escaped."

"Yes. Your report says *no further anomalies noted*." He let the words sit in the air between them like a dropped knife. "Our diviners say otherwise. Our diviners say that at the ford, the subject looked at you and experienced a recognition event of considerable magnitude. And that you — Third Chair — experienced one as well."

The crack in Sera's bedrock went wide enough to fall through. She did not fall. She had been trained too well for falling.

"Recognition of a wanted fugitive, First Chair."

"Of course." Vasse studied her for a long moment, and Sera understood that he knew — not everything, perhaps, but enough; that the diviners had read something off the ford's wet stones, and that whatever they'd read had been interesting enough to bring here, to the dead stars, at dawn. "Here are your orders, Third Chair. The subject is moving toward the shrine country, seeking the old stones. You will follow. You will close. And when you have her alone — alone, Third Chair, away from witnesses, away from the guilds, away from anything that might make a story of it — you will kill her."

The room was very quiet. Twelve dead moons hung in their brass cages.

"She is the Anchor," Sera said. "I understood you wished to hold her leash."

"We wished to. We no longer can. The Hollow King's riders are also hunting her, and the Hollow King does not leash what he takes — he empties it. If Quintana falls into his hands, the Directorate loses the asset and gains a catastrophe. A dead Anchor is simply a door that stays shut. An emptied Anchor is a door that opens onto whatever he wants." Vasse's voice remained mild throughout, the voice of a man ordering tea. "Kill her cleanly. Burn the body. File the report."

"And if I encounter complications?"

"Then you will resolve them." He turned back to the black thirteenth ring. "You have served the Order faithfully since you were four years old, Third Chair. Collected from a burned hamlet, raised in the creche, no kin, no attachments, no history. You are, in a sense, the perfect instrument for this work. Nothing to compromise you." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "That is still true, I trust."

"Perfectly true, First Chair."

"Good. Dismissed."

Sera saluted — fist to sternum, the grey salute, the only gesture her body had ever been taught to make with conviction — and walked from the Chamber of Instruments with her spine straight and her boots striking the stone in perfect measure.

In the corridor outside, alone, she stopped beside a window and looked east, toward the border, toward the drowned hills and the river crossings and the reeds where a woman with a mercenary's stride had vanished from her life for the second time.

*I have orders to kill you,* she thought. *And I cannot remember your name, and I remember your laugh.*

She took out her notebook, tore out the page with the eleven intake names, and fed it to the window-draft. Then she took a fresh page and wrote, in the Order's blockhand, in the honest grey ink of a lie properly constructed:

*Trail lost at the Verrin marshes. Subject presumed to have crossed into the shrine country via the pilgrim paths. Requesting three weeks' independent pursuit. Will report at each waystation.*

Three weeks. Enough time to find Jude Quintana first. Enough time to stand in front of her and say the word that had been waiting behind her teeth for twenty years, and see whether the other woman flinched, and learn from her flinch whether the hillside with the willow fence was a memory or a madness.

She sealed the request, addressed it to the First Chair's office, and posted it herself.

Then she packed a grey cloak, a grey knife, and the shard of covenant-stone that hummed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and went down through the bastion gates into the administrative dusk, walking toward the border and the sister who did not know her either.

Behind her, in the Chamber of Instruments, the black thirteenth ring trembled on its axis — once, faintly, like a bell struck in another room —

and began, very slowly, to turn.
