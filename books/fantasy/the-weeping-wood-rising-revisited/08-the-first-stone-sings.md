## 8. The First Stone Sings

The drowned road went down farther than light wanted to follow.

Jude walked it with the priestess's blessing-oil still slick on her palms and Perrin's lantern swinging ahead of her, its flame bent sideways by a draft that came from nowhere, from everywhere, from the deep places under Lake Verren where the water had no business being held back by anything but an old promise. The salt walls glistened on either side, crusted white and grey like the ribs of some enormous beast that had lain down here to die a thousand years ago and hadn't finished.

"Keep your hand on the wall," she told Perrin. Not for the first time. "Count your steps. If I say run, you run up. You don't wait. You don't look back."

"You said that already," Perrin said. "Four times."

"Then we're agreed."

He was nine years old and he had walked out of a burning village with soot in his lashes and a secret warm against his heart, and when Jude had asked him — once, only once, on the second night — why he'd followed her instead of running the other way like any sensible child, he had shrugged and said, *You pulled me off the pyre.* As if that were a complete map of the world. As if one act of ordinary decency were a covenant of its own.

She envied him that arithmetic.

Ahead, the priestess moved like a woman walking in her sleep. Maren — that was her name, though half the time she answered to nothing at all — had been lucid this morning for the first time since the shrine, long enough to wash her face in the lake and say, clearly: *The stone remembers what you paid to forget. Be ready to meet yourself. She may not be kind.*

Jude had not asked what that meant. She was beginning to suspect she already knew.

* * *

They heard the singing before they saw the chamber.

It was not sound, exactly. It came up through their boot soles, through the salt, through the water overhead — a pressure, a hum, the way a struck bell keeps ringing in the bones of the building long after the air goes quiet. The drowned wardens had let them pass. That was the horror of it: the guards of the first stone stood in alcoves along the final stretch of road, armored in rust and weed, faces behind visors gone green with age, and they had not stirred. They stood watch over a law that had died with the wards above, loyal as corpses are loyal, which is to say completely, and uselessly, and forever.

One of them, as Jude passed, turned its head. Just its head. The motion made a sound like a hinge in a house no one lives in anymore.

"The law is ended," Maren said softly, to the warden or to herself. "You can rest."

It did not rest. It watched her go with whatever eyes remained behind the green, and Jude thought: *that* is what it is to serve something past its death. She had done it too, in her way. Sold roads to wardens who believed they kept order, while order rotted around them all.

Then the corridor opened, and there was the stone.

* * *

It floated.

That was the first impossible thing. The chamber was a hollowed bell of salt, and Lake Verren lay overhead like a second sky — a ceiling of black-green water shot through with the last grey light of the starless afternoon, fish moving through it slow as thoughts. And in the center of that hollow, waist-high above a basin of clear water, hung a shard of crystal the size of a sleeping child, turning slowly, catching light that had no source.

The Second Stone — no. Jude corrected herself. *A* stone. The First. The stone of Memory. She didn't know how she knew that. She knew it the way she knew her own name, which was suddenly not a comfort, because she was no longer certain her name was her own.

"It's been waiting," Maren whispered.

"Things that float aren't waiting," Perrin said, practical even now, gripping his lantern with white knuckles. "Things that float are about to fall."

But the stone did not fall. When Jude stepped into the basin — because she understood, without being told, that it would come to no one else; she felt its attention settle on her like a hand on the crown of her head — the water rose to meet her ankles, warm as bathwater, warm as blood, and the singing stopped.

The silence was worse.

Then the stone sang again, and this time it sang in her own voice.

* * *

— A cottage with smoke-stained rafters and a door that stuck in winter, and someone laughing in the kitchen, a girl's laugh, bright and unguarded, and Jude knew that laugh before she knew why she knew it —

— A mother's hands kneading bread, scarred at the knuckles, humming a tune in a mode Jude's throat remembered but her tongue could not find, and the hands were beautiful and the hands were terrible and Jude could not stop looking at them —

— Sera. Sera at twelve, hair braided wrong because she'd braided it herself and refused help, standing on a fence rail with her arms out like a tightrope walker, shouting *look, look, look* — and Jude looking, always looking, always caught between terror and adoration —

— Snow on a chapel roof. Two girls carving names into a pew with a smuggled knife. *Jude and Sera, sworn beneath the Wood.* The knife slipping. Blood beading on pale wood. Both of them laughing anyway, because what was a little blood between sisters —

— *Sisters.*

Jude's knees hit the water.

— A night of storm. Her mother's face lit red by torches, by fire, by something worse than fire, saying words Jude's mind flinched away from even inside the memory: *the covenant is a cage, little anchors, and I will see it broken if it costs me everything* — and the two girls pressed together in a cupboard, Sera's breath fast against her ear, both of them understanding, in the way children understand catastrophes, that the world had just changed shape —

— Then white. Then cold. Then her own hands — older hands, scarred differently — pressing shards together over an altar while something vast and grieving poured itself through her, and her own voice saying words in that lost mode, the old tongue, the covenant-tongue: *take what I keep, take what I know, let me be the lock* — and memories leaving her like birds startled from a tree, whole flocks of them, the cottage going, the snow going, the names in the pew going, Sera's laugh going, going, going —

— And the last thing, the very last thing before the white took everything: Sera's face above hers, older now, grey-cloaked, wet-eyed, saying *you promised you'd remember me* —

The stone went silent.

* * *

Jude came back to herself face-down in the basin, water in her mouth, Perrin hauling at her arm and shouting her name — her name, *Jude*, and it sounded strange, like a coat borrowed from a stranger who might come back for it.

She got up. The stone hung before her, dimmer now, its song spent, and when she reached out and closed her hand around it, it was warm. Warm as the shard she suddenly understood Perrin had carried against his heart. Warm as a living thing.

"That's yours, then," Maren said quietly. There were tears cutting tracks through the grime on her face. "That was always yours. You put it in the deepest place you could find, where even you wouldn't go looking."

"Maren." Jude's voice came out wrecked. "The woman in the memories. My—" The word snagged. "Sera. Corvane. The inquisitor. Is she my—"

"She is your sister." The priestess said it plainly, the way you tell someone a limb has to come off. "And your mother is the reason the stars went out."

The lake shivered.

Jude felt it before she saw it — a tremor through the salt floor, a note of wrongness in the pressure overhead. She looked up at the black-green sky of water above them and watched frost bloom across it. Not spreading from the edges. Blooming everywhere at once, feathering outward from nothing, the whole weight of Lake Verren stiffening like a thing deciding, all at once, to stop being water.

"What's happening?" Perrin's voice cracked. "Jude. What's happening?"

"The stone woke up," Maren said. "And the lake remembers who sealed it here. Everything down here remembers." Her mad eyes had gone clear and terrified. "Take your stone and run, Anchor. Run *up*."

* * *

They ran.

The drowned road that had taken them half a day to descend became a nightmare climbed in what felt like minutes — lantern light lurching over salt walls, breath tearing, Perrin's small boots slapping stone, the priestess astonishing them all with a speed that grief and purpose lent her. Behind them the cold came like a tide with intent. Jude could feel it through her soles, climbing after them, patient and enormous, the whole frozen mass of the lake pressing down through the mountain's bones.

In the alcoves, the drowned wardens began to sing.

It was barely sound — a rust-and-water moan, harmonized, dozens of dead throats finding one note — the last order they had ever been given, still faithfully executed after a century: *guard the stone, guard the stone, guard the stone.* And the stone was leaving. Jude carried it against her chest, wrapped in her cloak, and it beat against her sternum like a second heart, and every step away from the basin was a step the wardens' broken law forbade, and they sang their grief at her back the whole way up.

"I'm sorry," she gasped at them, and meant it, and hated how little it meant.

Light ahead. Grey daylight, thin as broth. Perrin reached it first, hauled himself out onto the lakeshore rocks, and turned to pull Jude up — and then froze, staring past her, mouth open.

Jude turned.

Lake Verren was freezing behind them. Not at the edges, not gradually — all at once, a ring of white racing outward from somewhere above the drowned road, ice groaning like a ship breaking apart, waves freezing mid-heave into sculptures of themselves. In the space of ten heartbeats the lake became a field of shattered glass and locked swell, and then went still. Utterly still. A lake turned to held breath.

From beneath the ice, faint and far down, the drowned wardens' note continued. One long chord, fading like a bell in another room.

Perrin sat down hard on the rock. "Is it dead?" he asked. "The lake. Is it dead?"

"No," Maren said. "Sleeping. Or remembering." She looked at Jude, and her voice gentled. "It will thaw when the thirteenth moon turns. Or it won't. That depends on you now."

Jude looked down at the bundle in her arms. Through the cloak's weave, the stone glowed faintly, the way embers glow under ash — banked, not spent. Against her ribs, it pulsed.

And inside her skull, uninvited, the memories were still arriving. Late stragglers from the flock that had fled her years ago, drifting home out of the white: the smell of bread. A fence rail. A girl with badly braided hair shouting *look, look, look*. A knife slipping in a chapel pew, and blood, and laughter.

*You promised you'd remember me,* Sera had said, in the last moment before forgetting.

"I'm sorry," Jude said aloud — to the girl in the memory, to the sister hunting her across two realms, to whoever she had been before she bought the world's survival with herself. "I'm coming. I remember now. Most of it. Enough."

Perrin looked up at her, soot-eyed, shivering, brave. "Coming where?"

Jude turned east, toward the high passes, toward the guild roads she knew better than anyone alive — toward the second stone, and toward the grey-cloaked woman who wore her childhood like a stolen coat.

"To collect the rest of my life," she said. "And maybe to save my sister's. Saddle up. We've got a thirteenth moon to outrun."

Behind them, under a hundred feet of new ice, the wardens' chord faded to silence — and far off across the darkening water, something else answered. Hooves. Many hooves, moving fast along the eastern shore, toward the pass.

Grey riders. Coming for the stone.

Coming for her.
