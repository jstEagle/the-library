## 9. Smuggler's Justice

The cave had a name older than any of the people who used it. The Mawmouth, the guilders called it — a cathedral of stone hollowed out of the mountain's root by water that had given up and gone elsewhere centuries ago. Stalactites hung from the ceiling like the ribs of some enormous drowned animal, and when the torches were lit, their shadows moved on the walls in slow, breathing rhythms, so that the whole cavern seemed to be alive and watching.

Jude Quintana stood in the center of it with her wrists bound in front of her, and she tried very hard not to think about how much the place looked like a throat.

They had taken her weapons at the mouth of the pass — her sword, her knives, even the little hook-blade she kept sewn into her boot cuff, which Old Marla found anyway because Old Marla had been finding things for forty years and did not take kindly to being insulted by amateurs. They had taken Perrin's shard too, or tried to; the boy had fought like a wildcat until Jude told him to let it go, that it would come back to him, that promises made against the heart had a way of keeping themselves. She didn't know if that was true. She was running low on truths she could trust. But Perrin's eyes had gone wide and wet and he had let them take it, and now he sat near the back of the cavern between two guild wardens twice his size, his chin up, refusing to look afraid.

The priestess of the Weeping Wood sat beside him. She had been quiet since the shrine — quieter than grief, quieter than madness. Sometimes her lips moved around words no one else could hear, and once, crossing the high meadow, she had reached out and touched Jude's hair as though checking whether it was real. Jude had not pulled away. She was beginning to suspect there were very few things left she had the right to pull away from.

*You sold the roads,* they had said, dragging her down off the starless pass. *You sold the roads and now the stars are gone and Vaelmoor is coming and the wards are ash, and it started with you, Quintana. It started with your ink and your maps and your clever little bargains.*

She had not argued. That was the strange part, the part that kept her awake through the cold night in the holding tent while the guild debated what to do with her. Four years ago she would have argued. Four years ago she would have lied with such fluency and charm that half the court would have believed her before she finished her first sentence, and the other half would have believed her by morning. Lying was a trade skill, like lockpicking, like reading weather. She had been good at all three.

But that had been before the lake. Before the stone of Memory had poured itself into her like black water into an empty cup. Before she had remembered a cottage with smoke-stained rafters and a mother who hummed while she worked and a girl with grey eyes laughing in the doorway — laughing at *her*, with *her*, the way only sisters laugh. Before she had remembered choosing, kneeling in the dark beneath the Wood, choosing to forget everything so that the covenant would hold.

Before she had remembered that forgetting was the price she had set herself, and paid, and never regretted until now.

So she stood in the Mawmouth with her hands bound and did not lie.

* * *

The court convened at dusk, which was indistinguishable from every other hour now that the sky was a lid of featureless black. Torches guttered in iron brackets along the walls. The guildmasters sat in a crescent on carved stone benches worn smooth by generations of elbows: nine of them, one for each of the old roads, each wearing the badge of their route stitched into their collars. Salt Road. Ash Road. The Verren Causeway. The Starfall Cut. Roads Jude knew the way other people knew their mothers' faces — which was, she thought grimly, no longer a comparison that comforted her.

Old Marla presided. She was the eldest of the nine, keeper of the Salt Road charter, a small woman built like a knot in hard timber, with a voice that carried without effort across the whole cavern as though the stone itself were obliged to repeat whatever she said. She looked at Jude for a long moment before she spoke, and in that look there was something worse than hatred. There was disappointment. There was the particular grief of a woman who had taught a girl to read river-ice and trail-sign and had watched her turn those lessons into a knife.

"Jude Quintana," Marla said. "You stand before the Nine Roads. Do you know why?"

"I can guess."

"Guessing isn't knowing." Marla leaned forward. "Say it plainly. What are you accused of?"

Jude lifted her chin. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, and she was grateful for that, at least — grateful that somewhere in her there was still a mercenary who knew how to stand in front of armed people and not shake.

"In the winter four years past, I copied the guild road-charters — the salt routes, the causeway approaches, the warden patrol schedules — and I sold them to the Thornfell wardens. All of them. Every road. I gave the law the map of everything we'd built in the dark." She paused. "I was paid eight hundred crowns and a pardon for two outstanding warrants. I took both."

The sound that went through the cavern was not loud. It was worse than loud. It was a low murmur, a shifting, like a nest of snakes rearranging themselves, and it traveled from bench to bench and up into the stalactites and came back down again. Several of the guildmasters had gone very still. One of the younger ones, a broad man with the Ash Road knot at his collar, put his hand flat on the stone in front of him and stared at it as though it might tell him this wasn't happening.

"You confess," Marla said. It was not a question, but she waited for the answer anyway.

"I confess."

"Why?"

And here it was — the trap inside the confession, the question she had known was coming since they bound her wrists. Because the honest answers were ugly ones. *Because I was angry.* Because the guild had passed her over for a route-keeper's badge twice, because Marla herself had said she was too reckless, too young, too hungry. Because the wardens had offered her legitimacy, and she had wanted it the way a starving woman wants bread, without thinking about what was in the loaf. Because she had told herself, in the small hours, that the roads belonged to everyone anyway, that the guild's secrecy was just another kind of greed, and that she was merely redistributing what was already common — and then she had taken the eight hundred crowns and bought a horse and drunk very good wine and slept very well.

"They'll want a reason," Sera had said to her once, in a memory that wasn't a memory, two girls lying on a chapel roof counting the stars they were about to lose. "People forgive crimes easier than they forgive reasons."

Jude exhaled slowly.

"There isn't a good one," she said. "I wanted what I hadn't earned. I dressed it up as principle afterward, but that was cowardice, not conviction. The roads were yours because you bled for them — men died keeping the salt routes open in the famine years, and I knew that, and I sold them anyway because I was proud and underpaid and someone finally offered to treat me like I mattered." Her jaw tightened. "That's the truth. It doesn't excuse anything. It's just the shape of it."

Silence held the cavern for a long moment. Somewhere high above, water dripped from a stalactite onto stone, a slow patient ticking, the Mawmouth counting seconds the way it had counted them for ten thousand years.

It was the priestess who broke it.

She rose from her seat at the back, and the wardens flanking her did not stop her — perhaps because she moved like weather, inevitable, or perhaps because some instinct older than their orders told them not to lay hands on her. She walked the length of the cavern floor barefoot, her grey robe whispering, and stopped an arm's length from Jude, and looked up at her with eyes that had gone white as birch bark since the shrine.

"The Anchor speaks true," the priestess said, in a voice like wind through hollow reeds. "The Wood knows the taste of a lie on the tongue. This is not a lie." She turned her blind face toward the crescent of guildmasters. "Hear the rest before you weigh her. The roads she sold were never hers alone to sell — but the road she walks now belongs to everyone, and none of you will survive its ending. Ask her where she goes."

"She goes to the second stone," Perrin said.

The boy's voice cracked across the silence like a whip. He was on his feet, shaking off the warden's hand, sixteen years old and furious and unafraid in a way that made Jude's chest ache. "She saved my life off a pyre your wardens lit. She's carrying the first covenant-stone in her blood right now — ask her, ask the priestess, ask the frozen lake at Verren if you don't believe us. And you've got her standing here talking about *eight hundred crowns* like that matters more than the sky." His fists were clenched. "The stars are out because the covenant broke. And she's the one trying to fix it."

"Perrin." Jude said it quietly, but he heard her, and sat — slowly, glaring, his point planted in the ground like a banner he refused to lower.

Marla studied the boy for a long moment. Then the priestess. Then Jude.

"Is it true?" she asked. "The stone."

Jude opened her mouth — and hesitated, because how did she explain? *A dead god screamed through my skull and a lake full of loyal corpses let me pass and a piece of singing crystal showed me a childhood I paid to forget.* She settled for the simplest truth she owned.

"It's true. I carry the stone of Memory. I don't fully understand how, or why me. But the moon is finishing its thirteenth turn within the season, and when it does, the realms tear apart unless the covenant is reforged with all three stones. Two are still hidden. One lies beyond the Hollow King's territory, on the far side of the pass." She met Marla's eyes. "And I know where it is. Or rather — I'm the only one the road will let through. The old smuggling ways answer to the guild's marks, and the deep ways answer to something older. Both of those things live in me now, whether I like it or not."

"Convenient," said the broad man with the Ash Road badge, and his voice was bitter as ash itself. "Very convenient. A confessed traitor discovers she's suddenly indispensable the moment her neck's on the block."

"Yes," Jude said.

He blinked. He had expected denial, deflection, the old Quintana silver tongue. She watched him recalibrate, and hated that she understood exactly how he felt, because four years ago she would have done exactly what he expected and enjoyed doing it.

"Yes, it's convenient," she went on, into the ringing quiet. "I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Here is what I have to trade, and it's the only thing I have." She raised her bound hands. "Safe passage. Me, sworn and witnessed, guiding any company this court chooses through the starless pass and the deep ways to the second stone — through country where every smuggler trick I ever learned is the difference between arriving and dying. You send your own people with me to hold the rope at every crossing. If I lead you wrong, you leave me in the dark with nothing, and the Mawmouth can keep my bones as payment. If I lead you true—" she looked around the crescent, at the nine badges, at the roads she had betrayed stitched into wool, "—then maybe I start paying back eight hundred crowns' worth of debt. Which I know I can never finish. I'm not asking for forgiveness. I'm asking for work."

* * *

The guildmasters withdrew to deliberate, as tradition required, behind a curtain of woven chain at the rear of the cavern. Tradition gave them until the third bell. In practice, Jude knew, they would take exactly as long as Old Marla decided to take, because Marla had broken in every guildmaster sitting on that bench except perhaps herself.

They gave her a stool and water while they waited. Small mercies. Perrin edged over during the wait, close enough to speak under his breath, and for a while neither of them said anything at all. The torches burned. Water ticked from the ceiling. Somewhere in the stone above them, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, Jude felt the mountain's old road-veins humming — the deep ways waking, restless, hungry for direction.

"You meant it," Perrin said finally. "What you said. About the crowns."

"Every word."

"My da always said a confession's worth nothing unless it costs." The boy picked at a splinter on the stool's edge. "He said anyone can be sorry. Sorry's free. He said watch what a person gives up, and you'll know what they believe." He glanced sideways at her. "You gave up your best weapon in there. The lying. I could see it hurt."

Jude huffed something that was almost a laugh. "You're sixteen. How do you see things like that?"

"Pyres clarify things." He said it lightly, and it wasn't light at all, and they let it sit between them, acknowledged and unmourned, the way soldiers do.

In her chest, faint and constant as a second pulse, the stone of Memory hummed. It had been humming since the lake — sometimes a warmth, sometimes a word, occasionally a full flood: the cottage rafters, the mother's humming, grey-eyed Sera doubled over laughing on the chapel roof. The memories came and went like weather now. She was learning to let them. Grief, she was discovering, was not a thing you finished. It was a thing you carried, and it got heavier or lighter depending on how honestly you held it.

She thought about Sera — out there somewhere beyond the pass, grey-cloaked, hunting her, calling her *sister* with a face that fit a hole in Jude's mind perfectly. She thought about the Hollow King whose territory lay across the road she'd just offered to guide them through. She thought about the way the priestess had said *the Anchor speaks true*, and wondered how many more times she'd get to say anything true at all before the world ran out of season.

The chain curtain rattled.

Old Marla came out alone. She crossed the floor slowly, her boots loud in the hush, and stopped before Jude's stool, and looked down at her for a long, long moment — the look of a woman measuring a rope for weight, deciding whether it would hold or hang.

"The court says yes," Marla said. "Conditionally. You guide. We hold the rope. Six of ours go with you, chosen by lot, and the boy stays behind—"

"No," said Perrin and Jude, simultaneously, and then looked at each other.

"He carries a shard of the broken stone warm against his heart," Jude said, rising. "We don't know yet why that matters. I'm not leaving the only other living piece of the covenant in a cave while I walk into the dark. And he's earned the road. More than most."

Marla's mouth thinned. For a moment Jude was certain the old woman would refuse on principle alone — courts did not amend their rulings for the convenience of traitors. But then Marla looked at Perrin, really looked at him, at the burn scar creeping up from his collar where the warden's fire had licked him before Jude cut him down, and something in her granite face shifted by a degree.

"The boy goes," Marla said. "Under guard." She thrust out her hand — not to shake Jude's, but palm-up, waiting. "Your oath, Quintana. On the roads. The old way."

Jude looked at the offered hand. The old way. Spoken oaths meant nothing among smugglers; written contracts could be forged; but the hand-oath before the Nine Roads, sealed in the Mawmouth where the mountain itself was witness — that was the one bond in the borderlands that no one broke twice. Not because of punishment. Because the roads remembered. Because every guilder who ever swore it believed, in the marrow-deep way people believe the things they learned as children, that the mountain kept accounts.

She laid her bound hands in Marla's palm.

"On the roads," Jude said. "True passage, honest counsel, my life held in trust for the company's safety. From the Mawmouth to the second stone and back, or to whatever end finds us first. So witnessed."

"So witnessed," Marla echoed, and closed her fingers over Jude's knuckles, brief and hard as a shackle closing. When she let go, something in her expression had eased by a single grain — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but the suspension of judgment that passes for mercy among people who live outside the law. "We move at first bell. The pass is crawling with things since the stars went out, and there's talk on the wind of riders in grey and a man with no face buying passage from every bandit king between here and the Verren headwaters."

Jude's blood went cold. "Riders in grey. Buying passage."

"That's the word." Marla turned away, then paused, her back to Jude. "One more thing, girl. The man with no face — they're calling him the Hollow King. And the word is, he's not buying passage north." She glanced over her shoulder, and her eyes were old and tired and certain. "He's buying it *here*. To the Mawmouth. Word is he wants an audience with the court."

The torches guttered all at once, as one, though there was no draft.

Above them, faint through miles of stone, something began — very softly, very patiently — to ring.
