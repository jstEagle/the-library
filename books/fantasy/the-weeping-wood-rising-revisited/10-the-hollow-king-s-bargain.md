## 10. The Hollow King's Bargain

The guild had given her three things at the end of the cave-court: a guide's mark burned into a strip of oiled leather, safe passage through the guild roads for as long as the mark held true, and a warning.

"The Hollow King holds the second stone," Vess had said, folding her tattooed hands on the flat of a boulder that served as the court's table. Her voice had carried in that cavern the way water carries in a well — round, cold, echoing. "He'll trade it. He always trades. That's what makes him worse than a thief."

"Trade for what?"

Vess had looked at her a long time, and in the firelight her eyes had gone the color of old coins. "For whatever you have left that you haven't already given away."

Jude had thought, then, that she understood. She'd given up her memories once — or so the priestess said, so the first stone sang in her blood whenever she let herself listen. She had a sister she couldn't remember, an enemy who called her *sister* anyway, a god gone quiet as a held breath, and a boy named Perrin who followed her like a small shadow with warm hands. What else was there to take?

She would learn better before the day was out.

* * *

The road down from the guild caves wound through country that remembered being green. Now the starless dark pressed close even at noon — there was no noon anymore, only a grey dimness like the inside of a closed fist — and the world grew by lantern-light in fragments: a fence post furred with pale moss, a dead orchard whose branches hung heavy with fruit no one dared pick, a stream running thin and voiceless over stones the color of teeth. Without stars, without moon, distance became a rumor. Sound traveled strangely. Twice Perrin stopped walking and said, "Did you hear that?" and twice there was nothing to hear, which was worse than if there had been something.

The priestess rode behind them on a mule borrowed from the guild, wrapped in blankets despite the season, her madness burned down now to a low coal of lucidity. She spoke perhaps ten words an hour. Most of them were names.

"She's getting worse," Perrin said quietly, when they stopped to water the animals at a ford where the water moved like poured pewter. "The priestess."

"They all are." Jude hauled the bucket up and watched it tremble. "The Wood is dying, or sleeping, or screaming somewhere we can't reach. Everything rooted to it feels that." She handed him the bucket. "Drink. Then we ride. I want walls around us before the dark gets thick."

"What kind of walls?"

"The Hollow King's."

Perrin made a face. He was eleven, and he made his feelings about exiled kings of Vaelmoor very plain. "Vess said he eats people."

"Vess says a lot of things. She also gave us this." Jude touched the guide's mark at her belt. "Which means the guild thinks we can walk into his hall and walk out again. Or it means the guild wants him to eat us and be done with the trouble we carry." She swung up into the saddle. "Either way, we're going."

The priestess stirred under her blankets and said, in a voice like dry leaves, "*He will ask you for the one thing you will not give.*"

Jude reined the horse around. "Then he'll be disappointed."

* * *

His hall had been a border fortress once — she could see its bones under the corruption, the way you could see a drowned man's face under ice. Grey Vaelmoor masonry, squat and stubborn, built to watch the passes back when both realms still pretended to be neighbors. But the Hollow King had been growing things against it for years, maybe decades: pale timber thrust up through courtyards like ribs through skin, galleries of bone-white wood connecting towers that had never been connected, staircases of living grain that creaked and shifted when climbed, so that every step announced itself. A house that listened to itself.

Riders met them at the treeline — tall figures in lacquered half-armor the color of birch bark, faces hidden behind smooth masks carved with closed eyes. They did not speak. They simply turned their horses and walked, and Jude understood that they were expected to follow.

Inside, the hall was lit by lamps of amber resin that gave off a smell like honey and rot. Servants moved in silence — real silence, Jude realized, the silence of people whose voices had been taken or surrendered — laying out a table with food she didn't touch. At the far end, on a throne that might once have been a warden's judgment chair, sat the Hollow King.

He was smaller than the stories. Thin as a winter branch, wrapped in grey robes, and where his face should have been there was only a hollow — not a mask, not a wound, but a concavity, a place where light went and did not come back. Yet when he inclined his head, Jude felt the gesture land on her like a hand on the shoulder.

"Jude Quintana," he said. His voice came from everywhere in the room at once, soft as snowfall. "Anchor of the covenant. Killer of wardens' fires. Seller of roads, redeemer of roads. The smugglers' newest saint." A dry sound that might have been laughter. "And the daughter of my oldest grief. Sit."

"I'd rather stand."

"Of course you would. You always did prefer standing. It makes leaving faster." The hollow of his face tilted toward Perrin, and Jude stepped sideways, deliberately, putting herself between them. "And the child who carries fire in his shirt pocket. Sit too, little ember. No one here will harm you. Harm has a flavor, and yours would be bitter — kindness salted with fear. I don't dine on bitterness."

"You know about the shard," Jude said.

"I know everything that burns in my dark." The Hollow King folded his hands. Up close, she saw they were beautiful hands, long-fingered, a scribe's hands, and terribly still. "Let us not pretend at mystery, Anchor. You want the second stone. The stone of Oath. It sits below this floor, in a vault older than my exile, and I am its unwilling keeper. The guild sent you to me because they lack the stomach to send soldiers. The priestess sent you because she is dying and hopes you are not. And you came because you have no other door." He spread those still hands. "So. Let us speak plainly, as family should."

"We're not family."

"No?" The hollow seemed almost amused. "You wear her face when you sleep. Your sister told me so."

The word went through Jude like a blade finding a gap in armor. "You've seen her. Sera Corvane."

"I have watched her since she was a girl in grey learning to kneel. I have read her reports before Vaelmoor read them. I know that she keeps your first stone's song transcribed in a private notebook, in cipher, though her order forbids it. I know she lies to her superiors now — small lies, growing ones, the way frost grows on a window. I know that when she fought you at the river crossing, she let you go, and she has been drowning in the why of it ever since." The voice softened further, which somehow made it crueler. "I know things about your sister that you do not, Anchor, because you sold your knowing. Shall I tell you what you paid?"

"Don't." Perrin's voice, small and fierce. Jude hadn't heard him move, but he was beside her now, his hand fisted in her coat hem.

"It's all right." It wasn't, quite. Her heart was slamming. "What's your price, King? Say it. I've stood in enough market squares to know when the haggling starts."

"Simple. Clean. Almost kind." The Hollow King rose, and the shadows rose with him, peeling off the walls like cloth. "Deliver Sera Corvane to me. Alive, unspoiled, her memory intact. Do this, and the stone of Oath is yours within the hour — carried to you, sealed, singing, ready for your altar above the clouds. Reforge your covenant. Save your two realms. Raise your thirteenth moon. All of it. Everything you crossed the drowned road and froze a lake for, everything you confessed your sins to a cave full of cutthroats to earn — yours, for the price of one woman."

The silence afterward had texture. Jude could feel Perrin breathing against her side. Could feel the priestess, out in the courtyard with the mule, going very still, as if listening across distance.

"Why her?" Jude asked, and hated how carefully she kept her voice level. "If you know so much, tell me why her."

"Because she is the last piece of your mother that still moves." The Hollow King's hands tightened, just slightly, on each other. "Because the covenant you mean to reforging was written in two hands, and one of them was hers before it was ever yours. Because—" and here the voice frayed, just at the edge, like thread worn thin, "—because some debts are paid in blood and some in silence, and hers has come due, and I would rather collect it gently."

"That's not an answer. That's poetry around an answer."

"All answers are, at my age." He descended the single step from the dais. This close, she could see that the hollow of his face was not empty — far down in it, like a coin at the bottom of a well, something glinted. "You refuse. I can see it assembling behind your eyes. Very well. Refuse beautifully. But understand what you're choosing, Anchor. With her, you save the worlds. Without her — well. The stone stays in my vault. The thirteenth turn comes. The realms tear along the seam, and everything rooted to the Wood goes with it, including a boy with warm hands and a priestess full of names and every toll village that ever lit a lamp against my dark." He paused. "One woman. Two worlds. Even the wardens could do that arithmetic."

Jude looked at him for a long moment. Then she took off the guide's mark — the guild's leather, the guild's trust — and held it out flat on her palm between them, which meant, in smuggler's grammar: *hear me, and hear me clean.*

"I spent years selling roads," she said. "People died on roads I sold. I stood in a cave-court a week ago and said so out loud, and I meant it, and I'm not going to unsay it by selling a person instead of a road. Not her. Not anyone. You can keep your stone and your arithmetic." Her voice cracked, finally, and she let it. "Find another way to pay your debt, King. Or choke on it."

The hollow regarded her. Somewhere far down in it, the glinting thing turned over, slow as a fish.

"So," the Hollow King said softly. "She refuses. As she refused me once before, in another life, with another face." He drew back, and the shadows settled around him again like a robe being gathered. "Take them to the guest tower. Feed them. My riders will escort you to the pass at dawn whether you wish it or not — the dark is breeding worse things than me these nights, and I keep my roads clear. Even for fools."

"And if I never change my mind?"

"Then you never leave with what you came for." He turned away. "But minds change, Anchor. Especially yours. Yours has changed so many times already."

* * *

The guest tower was comfortable, which was its own kind of insult. Warm beds, a fire that burned without smoke, bread still warm from an oven somewhere below. Perrin ate like a soldier and fell asleep mid-chew, and Jude laid him out under the blankets and sat by the window with her sword across her knees and did not sleep at all.

Below, the fortress breathed. Pale timbers creaked. Masked riders walked the walls with lamps that cast no shadows. And beneath the flagstones, somewhere, the second stone waited in its vault, singing a song she couldn't hear, patient as geology.

*Refuse beautifully,* the King had said.

She turned the bargain over and over in the dark, the way you worry a loose tooth. One woman against two worlds. And it wasn't even that — Sera was an inquisitor, Sera had hunted her, Sera had put a knife against her throat at the river crossing and meant most of it. If the arithmetic were honest, handing over Sera Corvane wasn't even cruelty. It was tidiness.

But the first stone sang in her blood, and when it sang she saw things: a cottage with blue shutters. A woman laughing with sap-stained hands. Sera, younger, gap-toothed, daring Jude to climb the sacred oak past the third branch. *Sister,* said the stone, in a voice like water over roots. *Sister. Sister.* And the memory of it — a memory she did not own, lodged in her like a splinter from someone else's hand — hurt more than anything the guild or the wardens or the darkened sky had ever done to her.

You don't sell family twice. Once was the whole of her shame. There wasn't enough of her left to survive doing it again.

Toward what would have been morning, in the old world, hoofbeats came up the mountain road. Many horses, moving fast. Jude was at the window before she knew she'd moved, sword bare in her hand, watching riders in birch-bark armor pour through the gate below — the Hollow King's own outriders, returning.

They were carrying something between them on a litter. Something wrapped in grey wool, bound at wrist and ankle, with a hood of grey silk over the head — and even at that distance, even in that lightless dark, Jude knew the set of the shoulders. Knew the way the figure strained against the bindings, not wildly but precisely, testing each knot in order, an inquisitor's discipline even trussed like game.

A rider dismounted and bowed to the tower. His mask's closed eyes lifted toward her window, and his voice, when it came, carried perfectly through the still air, pitched exactly loud enough:

"For the King's collection. Taken on the river road, alive and unspoiled. The lady needn't lift a finger."

The litter-bearers carried Sera Corvane through doors Jude hadn't known were there, down into the roots of the fortress, toward the vault where the stone of Oath lay singing — and the great doors closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting, like a bell being muffled, like the end of an argument she had already lost.

Jude stood at the window until her knuckles went white on the sill.

Then she went and woke Perrin, and began pulling on her boots.
