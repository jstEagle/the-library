## 11. Prison of Quiet Bells

They did not bind her hands.

That was the first cruelty Sera Corvane noticed, and she had been trained to notice cruelties the way other people noticed weather. The Hollow King's riders had taken her sword, her grey coat with its silvered buttons, the lead case of instruments she had carried across two realms without ever once being asked to open it. They had taken everything that said *Inquisitor of the Grey Order*. What they left her was a plain wool dress the color of old snow, boots that almost fit, and her hands — bare, unbound, useless.

"You won't need rope," the rider who brought her down had said, not unkindly. He was young. He had a scar through his eyebrow like a stitch in cloth. "Nobody runs from the bells."

She should have asked what he meant. She had not asked. That, too, she would remember — or rather, that was the last thing she remembered clearly for a long time.

The prison was a hollow mountain, or the memory of one; the road into it descended through rock that rang faintly under the horses' hooves, a low bell-note that traveled up through her spine and settled behind her teeth. Then gates of black iron, and beyond them a vast cavern where the ceiling was lost in dark and the floor was polished smooth as a frozen lake, and hanging everywhere — hundreds of them, thousands, tier upon tier into the gloom — were bells.

They made no sound.

That was the second cruelty. A cavern full of bells that had never once been permitted to ring. Sera stood on the threshold and understood, with the cold certainty of her order's training, that this was not a silence of absence. It was a silence of *prevention*. Every bell hung in its cradle of chains, every clapper lashed fast with wire, every mouth of bronze stopped with wax and wool, and the whole weight of all that unspent sound pressed down on the air like a held breath. She could feel it against her skin. She could feel it in the small bones of her ears.

"The Quiet Cells," said the scarred rider, whose name, she would learn and then unlearn and then learn again, was Tam. "You'll have a cell to yourself. Comfortable enough. We're not monsters." He paused at the gate, and something moved behind his eyes — pity, or its fossil. "Just don't listen too hard."

*To what?* she wanted to ask. But the guards were already walking her down a corridor lined with small iron doors, each one fitted with a grille no wider than a hand, and through the grilles came no weeping, no cursing, no praying. Nothing. Other prisons sang with the noise of their inmates. This one had been tuned, like an instrument, to perfect quiet.

Her cell held a cot, a stool, a basin, a candle that never burned down — she checked, the first night, marking the wax with her thumbnail, and found by morning the mark gone and the candle whole. Time did not so much pass here as pool.

The third cruelty took nine days to find her.

---

It began with her mother's voice.

Sera had no memory of her mother. This was doctrine: the Grey Order raised its children from the foundling carts, stripped of kin and name and history, because a past was a leash and the Order's hounds must run free. She had been told, at seven, standing in the white cloister while snow fell past the arches, that whatever she had been before belonged to whoever she had been before, and that Sera Corvane belonged to Vaelmoor. She had accepted this the way she accepted the taste of the cloister bread — flatly, daily, without complaint.

But on the ninth day, lying on the cot in the pooled time, she heard humming.

Not from the corridor. Not from anywhere outside her skull. It rose up out of her own chest, a tune in three falling notes, repeated, patient, and it came with a warmth attached — the specific warmth of a kitchen, of flour on a wooden table, of a hand smoothing hair back from a forehead. *Someone hummed this over me,* she thought, and then thought, with genuine alarm: *I don't have a someone.*

She sat up. The candle burned. The bells hung in their thousands above the mountain's heart, silent as stones.

"Stop," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the perfect hush, too loud, a profanity. The Order trained its inquisitors against interrogation drugs, against pain, against the confession-mirrors of the southern covens. Nowhere in her training had anyone mentioned what to do when your own mind began offering you gifts you knew you had never been given.

Because that was the thing. She *knew*. Whatever the humming was, whatever kitchen and flour and hand, it was not hers. It could not be hers. She was Sera Corvane, raised in the white cloister under Magister Ellery's dry voice, and her history began at seven years old in the snow, and anything before that was —

Was what?

She reached for the years before seven and found — not darkness. Darkness would have been fine. Darkness was clean. What she found was a door. Painted blue. With a chip in the paint at knee height, shaped like a crescent moon.

She had never seen that door. She was certain she had never seen that door. She could describe the grain of its wood.

---

On the twelfth day — or a day she counted as the twelfth, since counting was becoming an act of faith — they fed her through the grille, and the scarred rider crouched on the far side while she ate.

"Why am I here?" she asked him. "The Hollow King wants the Anchor. I'm not the Anchor."

"You're leverage," Tam said. "Or bait. Depends which way the wind blows." He glanced up and down the empty corridor, then lowered his voice. "Mostly you're here because nobody knows what else to do with a Grey inquisitor. Can't ransom you — your Order doesn't pay. Can't kill you — bad luck, killing a Grey. So." He gestured at the bells overhead, invisible beyond the ceiling but present all the same, a pressure like deep water. "The mountain does the work."

"What work?"

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face made him older. "You know how you remember things? Little ones, mostly. Your own name first thing in the morning. Where you slept last night. What you're afraid of." He tapped his temple. "Sound does that. Sound is how the mind hangs its coats. Everything you've ever learned, you learned through your ears first — your mother tongue, your prayers, your own name read back to you." He nodded upward. "This place has no sound. And a mind in no sound starts letting go of its coats. One sleeve at a time."

"That's monstrous," Sera said.

"It's merciful," Tam said, and she heard, underneath the words, that he believed it. "People come to the Hollow King wanting to forget. Debts. Crimes. Griefs. He keeps a prison where forgetting happens honest and slow, no knives, no potions. Some of them thank him at the end." He stood, brushing off his knees. "Course, some of them are just in the wrong cell at the wrong time."

"How long," Sera said, "before I forget my name?"

Tam didn't answer. But at the corner, before the torchlight swallowed him, he said, quietly, like a man laying a flower on a grave: "Fight it. Say things out loud. Loud things. It slows it down."

Then he was gone, and the silence closed over the corridor like water over a dropped stone.

So Sera fought.

She stood in the middle of her cell and recited. The Grey litany, all forty-one lines. The capitals of Vaelmoor in alphabetical order. The names of every magister she had served under, every oath she had sworn at nineteen with her hand on the cold glass of the Verity Mirror. She recited until her throat was raw, and the sound of her own voice was the only sound in a mountain built to drink sound, and it was like shouting into snowfall — her words fell, and fell, and made no mark, and the silence took them and gave nothing back, and somewhere beneath the floor she could feel the great bells waiting, patient as hunger, holding all that stolen noise the way a spider holds silk.

The blue door stayed. The crescent-shaped chip in its paint grew more detailed. She began to know what color the hinges were.

---

She dreamed, after that. Or she remembered, there was no longer any difference, and that was the terror of it — the prison did not take memories away so much as it took away the wall between memory and invention, and once the wall was down, everything poured through equally.

A cottage with smoke-stained rafters. Rain on a slate roof, the particular arrhythmia of it, missing every fourth drop because of the loose flashing over the door. A woman's hands kneading bread — broad hands, scarred across the knuckles — and a girl sitting on the table swinging her legs, and another girl beside her, smaller, darker-haired, laughing so hard she knocked over the salt.

*Sera,* said the woman, without turning around, in a voice that fit Sera like a key fits a lock. *Tell your sister if she breaks my crock again she'll be carrying water till midwinter.*

*I don't have a sister,* Sera said inside the dream-memory, and the woman laughed, warm and unbothered, and the smaller girl — the laughing one — turned around, and her face was Jude Quintana's face, younger, softer around the jaw, alive in a way the smuggler's face never quite managed, and she stuck out her tongue and said:

*You always tell on me, Ryn.*

*Ryn.*

Sera woke with the name in her mouth like a stone she'd bitten through. Ryn. Not Sera. Ryn, short for something — Rynne? Serayne? — a child's name, a kitchen name, a name that had been called across a muddy yard by a mother with scarred knuckles, and it was *hers*, it fit her hands and her teeth and the place behind her sternum where grief lived, and she lay in the pooled time of the Quiet Cell and shook, because she understood now exactly what the mountain was doing.

It was not erasing her. It was *un-erasing* her.

The Grey Order had taken a child off a foundling cart — or claimed to; the cart might have been a story; the cart might have been a lie told to a stolen girl — and had sanded her down and named her anew and called the sanding mercy. And now the Hollow King's prison, that great engine of forgetting, was dissolving the Order's work the way water dissolves lime, layer by layer, and what was coming up underneath was not emptiness.

It was a kitchen. It was rain on slate. It was a sister.

"No," Sera said to the candle, to the bells, to the dark. Her voice cracked. "My name is Sera Corvane. I am an inquisitor of the Grey Order of Vaelmoor. My duty is the Verity. My duty is —"

*Duty,* said the dream-kitchen, gently. *Is that what they called it, when they sent you to cut a stranger's face open at a river crossing?*

"I didn't cut her. I let her go."

*Why?*

And Sera, who had filed a report saying she had been overpowered, who had lied to her superiors with a steady hand and a straight pen, lay in the dark and could not produce an answer that wasn't true. Because her face felt like a memory I don't own. Because when our blades locked I knew her grip. Because she fights like someone taught her, and I taught her, except I never met her before that river.

Because she called me *sister*, and some door inside me swung open, and behind the door was a chipped blue paint and rain on slate and I would rather die than let them close it again.

---

The days after that, she stopped fighting the mountain and started listening to it instead, which Tam had warned her against and which was, she suspected, the only way through.

She learned the silence had a texture. It was not one silence but many, layered: the mineral hush of the rock, the felted hush of the wax-stopped bells, and beneath them both a third silence, thin and high and continuous, like the ring that lives in the ear after a strike — except inverted, a ringing inward. The sound of the mountain *digesting*. Somewhere below, she became sure, was the master bell, the one all the others were wired to, the heart of the machine, and every scrap of noise her voice made was draining down toward it, feeding it, feeding *him* — the Hollow King, sustained unnaturally, the priestess had screamed before her mind broke, sustained by the Wood's silence, by silence itself, oh, oh, that was the bargain, that was the whole architecture of the place. He did not merely keep a prison where forgetting happened. He *farmed* it. He ate what the prisoners forgot.

And what Sera was forgetting — the litany, line by line, she could feel the forty-one verses going soft and gray like chalk in rain — what she was forgetting was being sold to him bite by bite.

Good, she thought, with a ferocity that surprised her. Choke on it.

Because here was the arithmetic she kept doing in the pooled time, the sum that would not balance: if the Order had stolen her, then the Order's doctrine was built by thieves. If her childhood had been cut out of her, then *Verity* — the Order's holiest word, the mirror-glass truth they made her swear on — was a word used by liars. And if Jude Quintana was her sister, then every hour Sera spent hunting her was a knife in the only family she had ever actually had, wielded by a woman who had been manufactured specifically to hold it.

Magister Ellery's voice, in memory, dry as the cloister snow: *An inquisitor's strength is that she wants nothing.* And Sera, floating in the bell-hush, answered him aloud for the first time in fifteen years:

"She wants her sister back."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Far below, faint as a heartbeat, the master bell tolled once — the first sound the mountain had made since she arrived — as if something down in the dark had just been handed an unexpected meal, and found it bitter.

---

They came for her on a morning that was indistinguishable from every other pooling hour, which was how she knew it mattered. Tam and two others, keys in hand, faces carefully blank.

"The Hollow King requires you," Tam said. "There's been a development. The Anchor's been asking after you." Something flickered in his eyes — the pity again, or maybe now something warmer. "He thinks you'll be useful. Breaking her."

Sera stood. The wool dress hung off her; she had lost weight; her hands were steady anyway. She walked between the guards down corridors she had memorized in the dark — forty paces, turn, sixty paces, the grille-door, the stair — cataloguing, the way the Order had taught her to catalogue, every bolt and hinge and blind corner, even as the other part of her, the part wearing a kitchen like a coat, whispered: *you'll forget the count by tomorrow, Ryn, you know you will.*

At the stairhead, passing the great gallery where a thousand bells hung in their tiers like a bronze orchard frozen at winter, she saw it.

The master cable.

It was a thing of beauty, and she hated that she could see it as beauty: a single braided line of black steel running from the lowest bell — the enormous one, big as a chapel, its mouth stuffed with wool the size of a grown man — up through the tiers, branching, branching, so that every smaller bell's wire ultimately tied back to it. Cut one link near the top and the whole orchard went free. All that stored sound — years of it, decades, every stolen prayer and name and lullaby the mountain had drunk — released at once into the dark.

She looked away before the guards could follow her gaze. She smiled at nothing. She let them think the mountain had already done its work, that the grey woman walking between them was mostly fog.

Let him think he was getting a weapon.

---

They put her in a room with a table, and the Hollow King's shadow filled it before his body did. She would remember — she fought to remember, writing it on the inside of her own skull in letters of pure will — that his voice sounded like a room where someone had just died. Kind, and ruined, and hungry underneath.

"You've been dreaming," he said. "I can smell it. Kitchens and rain." He leaned close, and his breath was cold as well-water. "Shall I tell you a secret, little Grey? Those aren't dreams. Those are *leavings*. The Wood's memories, caught in the covenant-shards. Your sister carries one. You carry their echo." His smile was a crack in a mask. "Forget the smuggler. Forget the sister. Give me the Anchor, and I'll give you back your Order — clean, whole, the way it was. No kitchens. No rain. Just duty. Doesn't duty sound *quiet*?"

And Sera Corvane — Ryn, Serayne, daughter of a woman with scarred knuckles, sister of a laughing girl who stole salt-crocks — looked at the Hollow King and felt the last of her doctrine fall away like ash off a burnt log.

"No," she said.

His expression barely changed. But his riders shifted, and Tam, at the door, went very still.

"It's funny," Sera continued, conversationally, while her mind ran the count — forty paces, turn, sixty, the grille-door, the stair, the black steel cable singing silently overhead. "You built a machine to make people forget. But you never once considered what they'd remember *instead*." She stood, slow, hands open, harmless. "Take me back to my cell. I'll be no trouble. I'm just a broken Grey who's lost her faith. Ask your bells — they drank enough of it."

For a long moment the Hollow King studied her, that ruined kind gaze trying to find the lie in her, and found — because it was true — nothing.

They walked her back down. Forty paces, turn, sixty. At the gallery she stumbled, apologized, caught herself on a chain pillar — and the wire she'd loosened on the way out slipped free of its binding, soundless, invisible, a thread pulled loose from a hem.

That night, in her cell, she sat cross-legged on the cot and put her lips against the cold iron of the bedframe, so the metal could carry what the air could not, and she hummed the three falling notes her mother had hummed over a girl on a rainy night in a cottage with a blue door.

Then she waited for the guard change. Then she climbed.

The gallery was a cathedral of hanging bronze, and she went up it hand over hand like a thief in her own story, wire-burn opening the skin of her palms, until she reached the master cable where it knotted through the great bell's crown. Below her the mountain breathed its inward ring. Below her, somewhere in Vaelmoor and Thornfell both, a sister was asking after her, and a season was running out, and a thirteenth moon was climbing toward a sky with no stars.

Sera wrapped both hands around the cable, set her boots against the bell's cold shoulder, and thought: *they can have my name. They already took it twice. But they don't get the kitchen. They don't get the rain. And they do not get her.*

She pulled.

Every bell in the mountain rang at once.

The sound was not a sound; it was the end of silence, ten thousand stolen voices released in a single instant — names, lullabies, oaths, screams, prayers in dead languages, a mother's three falling notes — a flood of everything the Hollow King had ever eaten, given back to him at volume, and the master bell split from crown to lip like an egg struck on a bowl's edge, and the pressure of all that returned memory tore through the corridors, and doors sprang their bolts, and in the sudden roaring light of every torch in the mountain flaring alight, Sera dropped from the shattered bell, landed rolling, and ran — past cells flung open, past prisoners stumbling blinking into the first noise they had heard in years, some weeping, some laughing, one old man standing in the middle of the corridor calling a name over and over, *Mara, Mara, Mara*, like a man watering a dead garden.

Tam stood at the gate, keys in his fist. For a heartbeat neither moved.

Then he threw her a grey cloak off a hook — her own, they'd never burned it — and jerked his head west. "Riders go out at dawn to hunt you," he said. "The salt road's faster. Go find your sister, Inquisitor."

"Not inquisitor," Sera said, pulling the cloak around her shoulders, already moving. The mountain roared behind her like a sea finally allowed to be a sea. "Just her sister."

She ran out of the Hollow King's country with the dawn coming up grey behind her and every memory she owned ringing in her ears like bells — and ahead, on the road toward the burnt chapel, dust rising: riders. Too many. And at their center, bound and pale, a figure with dark hair that she knew the way she knew rain on slate.

Jude.

They had her.
