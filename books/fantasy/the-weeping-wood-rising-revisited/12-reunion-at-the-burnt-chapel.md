## 12. Reunion at the Burnt Chapel

The Hollow King's riders had passed through the valley like a scythe through wheat, and what they left behind was not death but something worse — a hush. Jude had walked battlefields before. Battlefields were loud even in their silence: crows, flies, the groaning of the dying. This was different. The village of Aldenmere had been emptied of sound itself, as if the riders had swept it up along with everything else and carried it away in sacks.

She found Perrin waiting where she'd left him, at the tree line above the road, the priestess's grey head pillowed against his pack.

"Anything?" he asked.

"Tracks," Jude said. "Six horses, headed east. And drag marks." She didn't say whose. She didn't have to. Perrin's face did the work for her — that quick collapse of hope he'd never learned to hide, though he was trying, gods help him, he was trying so hard to be the kind of person who could carry bad news without dropping it.

"The inquisitor," he said. "Sera."

"They took her alive. That means they want something from her." Jude shouldered her pack. "Alive is a thing we can work with."

"And the priestess?"

Jude looked down at the old woman. Since the lake, since the first stone had sung its terrible song into Jude's bones, the priestess had been fading — a candle drowning in its own wax. Her breathing was shallow but steady. Small mercies.

"She stays with us. We move slow."

"Slow won't catch six horses."

"No," Jude agreed. "But we don't need to catch them. We need to know where they're going, and I know where they're going, because there's only one place in this whole starless country the Hollow King would bring a Corvane." She looked east, to where the hills folded up against the old border, where a chapel stood — or had once stood — blackened by a fire no one had ever explained to her, because she had never been there before in her life and yet she could draw its floor plan from memory, every pew, every crack in the altar stone.

That was happening more often now. The stone of Memory had cracked her open like an egg, and things kept leaking out. Not memories exactly. Worse than memories. *Certainties.* Knowledge with no origin, arriving in her mind the way rain arrives under a roof with holes in it.

"This way," she said, and hated how sure her voice sounded.

* * *

They traveled through the night because night was all there was now. Without stars, dark wasn't dark — it was *absence*, a pressure on the eyes, a weight you had to push through. Jude navigated the way she always had, by the feel of the land under her boots, by the smell of water and stone, but lately the land itself seemed to know her. Paths opened when she needed them. Brambles leaned away from her hands. Once, crossing a stream, she'd felt the current slow beneath her feet, patient as a dog waiting for its master to finish some errand.

The Weeping Wood was still imprisoned — she felt its absence like a missing tooth, tongue returning again and again to the gap — but whatever she was becoming, whatever the first stone had started in her, it did not sleep.

Near dawn — near what would have been dawn; the sky stayed the color of wet ash — they reached the ridge, and below them lay the chapel.

It had burned long ago. That much was true even of the false knowledge in her head: the walls were char-black, the roof open to the sky, the bell tower a broken tooth. But someone had tended the ruin. Ivy had been trained along the fallen beams instead of allowed to strangle them. The door — a new door, pale unweathered wood — hung slightly open. Someone came here. Someone kept it.

"Why would his riders bring her here?" Perrin whispered.

"Because this is where the records are," Jude said, before she could stop herself. "Because he wants her to forget the right things, and you can't choose what to erase until you know what's written."

Perrin looked at her sideways but said nothing. He was getting good at that. It made her miss him being a terrified boy who asked questions constantly, which was an absurd thing to miss, and she filed it away with all the other griefs she wasn't currently bleeding out from.

They left the priestess hidden in the bracken with water and a knife within reach of her hand — she was mad, not helpless — and Jude went down alone. Perrin wanted to come. She told him someone had to guard the only person in the world who knew what the covenant-stones actually were, and watched him accept the lie because he wanted to believe he was being useful rather than protected.

The new door made no sound when she pushed it.

Inside, the chapel smelled of smoke decades cold and, beneath it, sap. Fresh sap. Her heart stumbled. She knew that smell the way she knew her own blood — and then corrected herself, furiously — *you don't know it, you've never known it* — but her body was already moving down the aisle between ruined pews, following the scent like a thread.

The altar had survived the fire better than anything else. Someone had cleared the debris from it, and on its surface, in a shallow basin of dark wood, sap had pooled. Clear sap, golden in the gloom, weeping slowly from a crack in the altar stone as if the building itself were wounded. As if something underneath the floor were wounded.

And kneeling beside the altar, wrapped in the tatters of her grey coat, hair loose and filthy, was Sera Corvane.

"Sera."

The woman flinched at her name like a struck animal. She turned, and Jude's breath caught, because the face that turned to her was wrong. Not injured — the riders hadn't marked her. *Emptied.* Sera's eyes, which had been hard and grey and full of doctrine, were the eyes of someone standing in a room where all the furniture had been removed. She knew how rooms worked. She couldn't remember what had been in this one.

"You," Sera said. Her voice was rough, as if she'd been screaming, or praying, or both. "I know you."

"Do you?"

"No." A terrible honesty. Sera pressed her hand flat against her own sternum as if holding something in. "No, I don't. There's — they put me under the bells. I can't find my order anymore. I reach for it and my hand comes back empty. I can't remember my vow. I can't remember—" Her voice cracked. "I can't remember my own mother's face. I don't think I ever had one. I think they made that up too. Everything in my head has a seam in it now. I can see the seams."

Jude had imagined this reunion a dozen times on the road, and in every version Sera fought her, or accused her, or called her sister with that hungry, hateful certainty. She had prepared for blades. She had not prepared for this: the most dangerous person she knew sitting in the wreckage of her own mind, looking up at Jude with nothing but question in her face.

"I'm going to help you," Jude heard herself say. "Or I'm going to try."

"Why?" Sera asked simply. "Why would you? You have every reason to leave me here. I hunted you. I tried to take the stone from you. I—"

"Tried to kill me. Yes." Jude knelt, slowly, keeping her hands visible, the way you'd approach a spooked horse. "And then you let me escape at the river. And then your order ordered you to kill me and you didn't. And then the bells ate everything you were, and the first thing left over — the last thing the bells couldn't reach—" she tapped Sera's chest, lightly, over the heart, "—was apparently me. So. That's why."

Sera stared at her. Then, very quietly, in the voice of someone confessing to a crime: "I don't know if that's love or a wound."

"Could be both," Jude said. "In my experience it usually is."

* * *

They searched the chapel together, because Jude could not shake the certainty that had walked in with her: *the records.* The Hollow King — their mother, the word still tasted like poison — had brought Sera here for a reason. Erasure needed a source text.

Behind the altar, where a shrine wall would have held icons, the fire had peeled the plaster away to bare stone. And on that stone, revealed by fire the way bones are revealed by rot, were carvings.

Old ones. Older than Thornfell, older than Vaelmoor, cut deep and worn smooth by centuries of touching hands.

Two figures stood side by side at the center of the wall. Sisters, unmistakably — same height, same stance, mirrored — with their arms raised together toward a tree rendered in such loving detail that Jude could almost hear its leaves. From the tree's branches fell lines upon lines of carved droplets, and each droplet had been given a face: hundreds of tiny faces, generations of them, falling like rain into the sisters' cupped hands.

Below the figures, in a script half Vaelmoori and half a tongue Jude didn't recognize but somehow could read, ran an inscription. She traced it with her fingers, and the reading came not from learning but from somewhere deeper, the way you know your own name:

*TWO SWORN TO THE WOOD, ONE TO HOLD THE DOOR AND ONE TO WALK THROUGH IT. WHAT ONE FORGETS THE OTHER REMEMBERS. WHAT ONE CARRIES THE OTHER BEARS. NEITHER ALONE.*

"There were two of us," Sera whispered beside her. She had come forward without Jude noticing, drawn like iron to lodestone, and her fingers rested on the carved figure on the right. "There were always supposed to be two."

"Yes."

"I'm the one who walks through."

Jude looked at her. "How do you know that?"

"Because you're the one who holds the door." Sera said it with strange calm, tracing the left figure's stance — planted, rooted, immovable. "Look at her feet. They're carved as roots. Look at mine. Mine are mid-stride." She laughed, and it was not a happy sound. "My entire order, my entire life, my vows, my rank, my name — gone, and the thing that survives is that I'm the one who walks through doors."

"That's not nothing," Jude said. "Doors matter. Ask anyone who's ever been locked out."

But Sera wasn't listening. She had moved along the wall, to a section lower down, where smaller carvings clustered like footnotes. Children's heights, marked on a doorframe. A game scored into the stone — some hopping pattern, squares and crosses. Two names scratched over and over, practicing letters: *SERAI. JUDEA. SERAI. JUDEA.* And beneath them, in a steadier adult hand:

*Serai Corvane, born of the Anchor's line, sworn to the Wood at the weeping moon.*
*Judea Quintana, born of the Anchor's line, sworn to the Wood at the weeping moon.*
*Let no crown part them. Let no law unmake them.*

"Quintana," Sera read aloud. She turned. In the dimness, in the gold light of the weeping sap, her hollowed face had something in it again — not memory, not yet, but the shape where memory would sit, like a chair pulled up to a cold hearth. "You kept the name."

"It was mine to keep."

"Was it?" Sera's eyes moved over Jude's face, searching it the way you search a familiar room after a fire — for what survived. "Because I keep almost-remembering. Fragments. A garden with a crooked fence. Someone braiding my hair who complained the whole time about how much of it there was. A woman singing off-key. Is that you? Was any of that you?"

Jude's throat closed. Because yes. Yes, she remembered the garden, the crooked fence, the braid-work and the complaining, the off-key singing — remembered it now with horrible clarity, since the first stone had cracked her open — and the woman doing all of it had been *her*, years ago, in a life she had paid away like a merchant settling a debt. She remembered raising Sera. She remembered swearing the oath beside her. She remembered walking into the Wood's heart with her memories gathered up like an armful of belongings, and she remembered —

No. Past that point the memory sealed itself, smooth as river ice. What had happened at the sealing, why she had done it, who had betrayed whom — that was locked, and the lock lived somewhere in the dying priestess's mouth.

"I think it was me," Jude said carefully. "I think a lot of it was me. But I gave pieces of myself away, Sera, a long time ago, and I'm still finding out which pieces. So I won't swear to you what's true. I won't build us a house on ground I haven't tested."

Sera considered that. Then she did something Jude had never seen her do, not at the river crossing with knives drawn, not in any of their collisions across the burning lowlands. She reached out and took Jude's hand. Her grip was cold and certain.

"You don't have to swear," she said. "I've spent my whole life obeying oaths written by other people. This time I'll just decide." A ghost of the old inquisitor surfaced in her jaw, in the set of her shoulders. "I've decided you're my sister. You can argue with the evidence later."

"The evidence," Jude managed, "is a wall."

"The evidence is a wall," Sera agreed solemnly, "a wall of children's handwriting and a tree with faces in the rain, in a chapel neither of us should remember, which I found by trusting the shape of your back in front of me on the road here. I know how it sounds. I don't care. Some things you know before you can prove."

Outside, wind moved through the ruined tower, and for one moment — one impossible moment — the empty bell above them stirred on its melted chain and hummed, faintly, sweetly, a single note like a struck glass. Both of them froze. The note faded. Somewhere far beneath the floor, beneath the hill, beneath the world, Jude felt something vast shift its weight, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice.

*Soon,* the silence seemed to say. *Hold a little longer.*

* * *

They buried the day in small tasks. Sera showed an eerie competence at ruin-craft — clearing rubble, rigging shelter from the collapsed vestry — muscle memory surviving where narrative memory had died, another life living on in her hands without her permission. Perrin arrived at dusk with the priestess, who had woken enough to walk leaning on the boy's shoulder, and whose sunken eyes fixed on the carved wall the moment she crossed the threshold.

The old woman began, very softly, to weep.

"Not yet," Jude begged her. "Whatever you know — the betrayal, the sealing, the mother — I need it, but not tonight. Tonight we rest."

The priestess looked at her with an expression of such vast, exhausted pity that Jude's stomach dropped. "Child," the priestess said, "you have it backward. You think the truth will break you. But you are already broken in the shape of the truth — that is what an anchor is." Her gaze crawled up the carved wall to the two sisters, arms raised to the weeping tree. "You cannot prepare. You can only stand under it when it falls."

Then her knees buckled, and Perrin caught her, and the moment passed — but it did not pass. It settled over the chapel like snowfall, quiet and inevitable.

Jude sat by the small fire they dared, close enough to the altar that the sap-light mingled with the flame. Sera sat beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, deliberately almost. Perrin slept with the warm shard of the broken stone against his chest, his fist curled around it even in dreams. And on the wall above them, two carved girls held up their hands to catch the falling faces of everyone who had ever trusted the Wood.

Tomorrow, Jude thought. Tomorrow I ask her everything. Tomorrow I learn what our mother did, and what I did after, and why I chose to forget.

She reached out in the dark, and Sera's hand met hers halfway, no hesitation at all, and held on — love without proof, proof without love needing it — while under the floor, in the roots, in the prison of a god, something ancient waited for morning too.

And far to the east, past the hills, the thirteenth turn rose a little closer to full.
