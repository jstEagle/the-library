## 13. The Priestess's Confession

The priestess lasted three more days.

They carried her out of the drowned shrine on a litter of smugglers' canvas and hazel poles, Perrin walking at her side with his jaw set in that stubborn way he had, as though his presence alone could argue death into waiting. Sera walked behind, grey cloak traded for a plain brown one they'd bought at a river market — she had burned the old one without ceremony, and no one asked what it meant when a Corvane of the Grey Order fed her own insignia to the fire. And Jude walked at the front because someone had to choose the road, and choosing roads was the only prayer she'd ever been fluent in.

They made for the high shrine above Thornfell's spine, the mother-shrine, where the Wood's oldest roots drank from snowmelt and the air smelled of resin and cold iron. The priestess — Maren, she had finally given them a name, *Maren, I was called Maren before I was called vessel* — grew quieter with each mile, as if the climb were pouring her out through some slow unseen crack.

On the third evening they reached it: a hollow in the mountain's shoulder where a single great tree stood alone against the sky, silver-barked, its branches hung with strips of cloth and bone chimes and the small desperate offerings of people who no longer remembered what they were praying to. The tree wept. Not sap, not water — something between, amber-dark, sliding down its furrows in slow ropes that caught the last light like cooling wax.

Maren asked to be set down beneath it.

* * *

Jude built the fire while Perrin fetched water and Sera stood watch at the trailhead, though there was nothing left to watch for but weather. The stars were still gone. Nine days now, and the dark overhead was not empty the way night was supposed to be empty — it was *occupied*, a ceiling of velvet pressed so close Jude sometimes felt she could reach up and touch the underside of it. The moon was gone too, swallowed somewhere in that blackness, and every smuggler's tale said the same thing: thirteen turns, and then the seams give.

The fire popped. Maren watched it with eyes gone milky at the edges, grief having eaten outward from the pupils like rot through an apple.

"You should rest," Jude said.

"I have rested for eleven years." The old woman's voice was dry leaves over stone. "Rest is how I let you all forget."

Jude's hands went still around the kindling. "Forget what?"

Maren turned her head, slowly, and looked at her — really looked, the way you look at a door you've been afraid to open.

"Sit down, Anchor. It is time someone told you what you paid."

* * *

Perrin came back with the water and stayed. Sera, sensing — or summoned by some frequency only sisters could hear — left the trailhead and folded herself onto the ground across the fire, close enough that their knees nearly touched, far enough that either could pretend otherwise.

"Tell me about my mother," Jude said.

It was the question she had been circling for days, since the chapel, since the carved record of two sisters sworn to the Wood, since the dreams that had grown teeth. Every night now she dreamed of a woman with her own face grown older, standing in a doorway with lamplight behind her, saying words Jude couldn't hear. She woke with her cheeks wet and no memory of weeping, which felt less like mercy and more like theft.

Maren smiled, and it was the worst thing Jude had seen in weeks, worse than frozen lakes and burning villages, because it was gentle.

"Your mother," Maren said. "Yes. Let us speak of Ysolde Quintana, who was Anchor before you, who held both realms in her two hands, and who decided — in the end — that one hand deserved more than half."

* * *

"She was born here," Maren began. "In this shrine, in the winter the twelfth moon first stuttered. Her own mother served the Wood, and hers before that, back and back until the names wear smooth. The Anchors are not chosen, child. They are *kept*, the way you keep a hearth. Generation upon generation, one life laid against the covenant so the border between Thornfell and Vaelmoor stays soft instead of tearing."

Jude glanced at Sera. The other woman was very still, her profile carved orange by the firelight, listening the way soldiers listen to a report of casualties.

"The covenant needs three things," Maren went on. "Three stones — Memory, Oath, and Root — and one living soul willing to stand between. A bridge does not get to belong to one bank, do you understand? An Anchor belongs to neither realm and both. That is the price. You give up your country. Your name becomes a hinge."

"And my mother couldn't pay it," Jude said quietly.

"Your mother paid it beautifully for twenty years." Maren's voice roughened. "She was magnificent. I loved her. Remember that, when you hate her — remember that she was easy to love, which is how she did what she did. But Vaelmoor courted her. Slowly. Kindly. They showed her the cities across the veil, the towers, the libraries, the grey order with its beautiful terrible discipline. And they showed her what Thornfell was beside it — smuggling villages and toll fires and mud — and somewhere in those years a splinter went in. *Why should I hold both worlds equal,* the splinter whispered, *when only one of them deserves holding?*"

The fire cracked. Somewhere below them in the valley, a wolf sang and got no answer.

"There was a rite," Maren said. "There is always a rite; that is the covenant's cruelty and its kindness — everything can be unmade by the same hands that made it. The Anchor may reforge the pact to favor one realm. It requires the stones, and it requires the Wood's silence — the god must be bound while the reforging happens, or the god will refuse. Your mother spent four years preparing it. Four years of pilgrimages, of offerings, of kneeling at shrines just like this one, weeping prettier tears than the tree itself. And when it was done — when she had lulled every warden and charmed every priest — she took the stones and bound the Wood and began the rite."

"What stopped her?" Perrin asked. His voice was small in the vast dark. Nobody told him to be quiet.

Maren looked at him almost fondly. "Her daughters stopped her. Two girls, sixteen and fourteen, raised in the chapel below the ridge, taught to sweep the floor and tend the candles and never ask why their mother vanished for months at a time." Her gaze slid to Sera, then back to Jude. "Sera, the elder, had already been promised to the Grey Order — Vaelmoor's payment, you understand, a hostage dressed as an honor, taken young enough to forget. But memory is a stubborn animal. And Jude—" the old woman drew a breath that seemed to cost her a rib, "—Jude was never sent anywhere. Jude was *home*. Jude swept the floors. Jude knew every root-path and every hiding hole, because her mother had trained her as a guide without ever telling her what she was being guided toward."

The world tilted slightly. Jude found herself gripping a root at her side, knuckles white, because the ground had begun to feel like a story someone else was telling.

"They followed her," Maren said. "Both girls. To the summit altar on the turn of the thirteenth moon, eleven years ago, when the sky last went wrong. And they found their mother mid-rite, hands full of covenant-stone, the Weeping Wood screaming silent under her bindings. And Ysolde — magnificent, beloved Ysolde — looked at her daughters and offered them a choice, because she was never cruel, only *certain*. Join me, she said. Help me finish it. Vaelmoor will flourish and Thornfell will endure as a vassal and no one need die except a god's freedom."

"And Sera chose Vaelmoor," Jude said. The words tasted like ice.

"Sera chose the only path she'd been shown," Maren corrected, sharp as a whip-crack. "A girl of sixteen, handed to another nation as a pledge before her teeth were done coming in, told her whole childhood that duty wears grey. Do not judge her yet, Anchor. Judge no one yet. Because it was not Sera who stopped the rite."

The silence stretched. Jude heard her own heartbeat, absurdly loud, counting down to something.

"It was you," Maren said. "Fourteen years old, with no order behind you and no doctrine in your mouth, just a mother's face turning strange in the altar-light. You could not fight her — no one could fight Ysolde. So you did the only thing an Anchor's blood can do. You stepped into the circle yourself."

* * *

The fire had burned down to coals. No one moved to feed it.

"You cannot undo a reforging once begun," Maren continued softly. "But you can *complete* it differently. The rite was open. The stones were loose in your mother's hands. And the covenant will accept any living Anchor who gives themselves willingly — it does not care whose future it favors, only that the giving is true. Your mother meant to give Vaelmoor the greater share. You gave it *balance*. Perfect balance, the old balance, the balance that had held for nine hundred years. But the rite demands payment proportional to the mending, child, and the mending was total. Your mother had already bound the Wood's voice to buy her opening. Someone had to pay for the closing."

"My memories," Jude said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, like shouting up a well.

"All of them," Maren said. "Not the past eleven years — those were returned to you, scattered, the way a body returns to a grave. Everything before. Your mother. The chapel. Your sister. Your own name in your mother's mouth. The covenant took the whole of who you had been, sealed it inside the stones, and left you a mercenary with good instincts and no history, because a bridge does not get to remember being a shore." She paused. "You woke in the chapel ruins with no memory, and the first person you met was a toll-warden who asked your name, and you said *Quintana*, because some things live deeper than remembering. Then you walked downhill and became whoever the road needed. Eleven years. You were magnificent too, in your way. You sold roads and saved children and never knew why the sight of the Wood made you weep."

Jude's hand rose to her own face without permission. Her cheek was wet. She hadn't noticed.

Across the fire, Sera had not moved, but something had broken open behind her eyes — Jude could see it even in the coal-light, the exact moment when a lifetime of grey certainty developed a hairline fracture down its center. "I don't remember any of it," Sera whispered. "They took me at six. They gave me new records. New parents on paper. There is no sister in the archives. I checked. I checked *eleven times*—"

"Because the archives were written by the same hand that wrote your orders," Maren said. "Ysolde's bargain included your forgetting, Sera. Vaelmoor got its inquisitor, scrubbed clean, loyal as a blade. It was part of the price your mother paid gladly. Two daughters, two erasures. She called it tidying."

"No," Sera said. It came out flat, then again, louder, cracking in the middle: "*No.*"

And then she was crying — the Inquisitor Corvane, who fought like falling masonry and hadn't flinched at anything Jude had seen, crying silently and furiously, both fists pressed against her mouth as though she could stuff the sound back in. Perrin, bless him forever, simply scooted across the space between them and put his small hand on her arm and left it there, saying nothing, because he had learned from watching Jude that presence was sometimes the whole of what justice could offer.

Jude sat with her own grief, which was stranger. It was grief for a woman she could not remember loving. She searched herself for the cottage from the stone's song, the mother's laugh, the sister's hand in hers — and found only smoke, only the shape of a doorway with lamplight behind it, only the recurring dream-face now wearing, at last, a name.

Ysolde.

*Mother.*

Even thinking it felt like handling a knife by the blade.

"She's still alive," Jude said. "Isn't she. The Hollow King."

Maren's smile returned, and this time it was pitying. "Eleven years feeding on the Wood's stolen silence. Yes. The rite was interrupted before completion — you balanced the realms, but you never finished binding the stones, because your mother fled with the stone of Oath rather than let a child undo her. The covenant has hung broken ever since, held together by your living blood and nothing else. And Ysolde has spent those eleven years becoming something else. Something that eats quiet." The old woman coughed, wet and long. When she finished, her voice was thinner. "She will come for the last turn. She must. With the thirteenth moon rising, the broken rite can be completed by whoever holds all three stones — and she means to hold them, and mean the reforging *her way*, and this time there will be no fourteen-year-old daughter to step into the circle."

"Then we finish it first," Jude said. "We take the second stone. We take the third."

"Child." Maren reached out and took Jude's wrist, and her grip was astonishing, iron wrapped in paper. "Do you understand what finishing it costs? You have paid once already. You will pay again. And next time there will be no scattering — next time the covenant takes the *Anchor entire*. You will not wake in a chapel with a name you kept by accident. You will become the Wood. Root and bark and starlight. You will watch your sister grow old from inside a tree." Her milk-edged eyes bored upward. "Know it now. Choose it with your eyes open, or do not choose it at all."

Jude looked at the fire. At Perrin's hand on Sera's arm. At her sister's ruined face, wet and defiant and *hers*, somehow, across eleven amputated years.

"I already know," she said quietly. "I think some part of me has known since the lake. Since the stone sang. You don't grieve someone you were that hard unless you're planning to bury her properly." She turned back to Maren. "Tell me where the Hollow King keeps the Oath-stone."

Maren laughed — a real laugh, rusty and startled out of her, and it turned into another coughing fit, and when it ended she lay back against the great tree's root, and the amber tears of the Weeping Wood dripped slowly onto her white hair like the tree was trying to mend her and could not.

"Starless Pass," she managed. "Three days' hard ride northeast, past the toll villages, up where the dark breeds things. She keeps it in a chapel of bells, guarded by riders who used to be men." Her eyes fluttered. "Go at once. Go tonight. And Anchor — when you meet her, and you will meet her — remember that she loved you. That is not forgiveness. It is only the truth, and truth is the sharpest blade anyone will hand you on that road."

"Maren?" Perrin's voice, frightened now. "Maren, look at me—"

The old woman's breathing slowed. Her hand slipped from Jude's wrist and came to rest palm-up in the dirt, open, the way you surrender a weapon.

"Thirteen turns," she murmured, to the tree or the dark or no one. "Hold the seam, little bridge. Hold the seam—"

And then the Weeping Wood wept harder, and Maren did not breathe again, and the wind moving through the silver branches sounded — Jude would swear it to her dying day, to whatever day that turned out to be — like a woman exhaling after eleven held years.

* * *

They buried her at dawn beneath the tree, wrapping her in the same canvas that had carried her, Perrin placing a smooth grey stone from Lake Verren in her folded hands. Sera spoke a few words in High Vaelmoori, low and formal, and then, in Thornfell's plain tongue, added only: "She told the truth when lying would have been kinder. That's the best eulogy I know."

Jude stood apart, looking northeast, where the Starless Pass cut its black notch between two peaks.

Three days. The moon's thirteenth turn was closing like a hand around a throat, and somewhere beyond that notch a woman with Jude's own face grown older was waiting with a stolen stone and eleven years of patient hunger. The enemy wore her mother's face. The dream-face. The lamplight doorway.

"Horses are saddled," Sera said, coming to stand beside her. Her eyes were red-rimmed and level. "Perrin's packed. Say the word."

Jude rolled her shoulders, felt the shard of Memory warm against her sternum where she'd kept it since the lake, singing its fragments of a cottage and a laugh.

"We ride for Starless Pass," she said. "Let's go meet my mother."
