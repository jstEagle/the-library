## 14. Riders on the Starless Pass

Jude felt the pass before she saw it — a cold breath sliding down from the heights like something exhaling through a keyhole. She reined her mule to a stop at the head of the guild column and let the dark settle against her face.

There was no moon. There had been no moon for weeks now, not since the stars went out, and the sky had become a single unbroken lid of black that pressed down on the world like a hand on a wound. In that dark, things were breeding.

"Quintana." Old Vess rode up beside her, lantern shuttered to a coin of amber light. The caravan master's knuckles were white around her reins, and Jude could smell the fear-tallow the guild had started rubbing into their gear — rendered fat mixed with ash and iron filings, supposed to keep the dark-things from tasting you first. "You feel it too."

"I feel it."

"The outriders say the high road's moving. Not blocked. Moving." Vess spat over the side of her saddle. "Stone doesn't crawl, Quintana. Stone doesn't have teeth."

"This stone does," Jude said. "The second covenant-stone sits in the hollow of the Starless Pass itself. The old smugglers called it the Oath. My mother—" She stopped. The word still snagged in her throat like a fishhook. "The woman who raised us built roads through here once. Before."

Before was doing a great deal of work in that sentence. Before the betrayal. Before the sealing. Before the priestess had died with the truth half-spoken in her mouth, leaving Jude carrying it like a swallowed stone of her own: *your mother broke the pact; you paid for it with everything you were.*

Behind them, three hundred wagons creaked and groaned — guild caravans hauling salt, iron, grief, and every soul who'd decided that following the Anchor into the mountains beat waiting for the dark to come down and eat them. Ahead, somewhere above the treeline, Vaelmoor riders would be making the same climb from the other side. And somewhere behind everyone, warden remnants — what was left of Thornfell's law after the toll-road pyres — were marching under a banner whose meaning had died with the stars.

Three armies. One road. One stone.

"Light the way-lanterns," Jude said at last. "Full shutters off. If the dark wants us, it knows where we are anyway. I'd rather see it coming."

Vess looked at her a long moment. Then she turned in her saddle and bellowed the order down the column, and one by one the lanterns bloomed along the caravan like a string of stubborn orange flowers refusing the night.

* * *

Sera Corvane heard the bells before she saw the riders — which was wrong, because there were no bells on the pass, and the sound was coming from below the ground.

She pulled up her grey mare at the head of the Vaelmoor column and held up a fist. Forty riders halted behind her, breath steaming, their pale armor drinking the lantern-light instead of reflecting it. Her second-in-command, a broad man named Doss with a scarred lip, drew alongside.

"Inquisitor?"

"You hear that?"

Doss cocked his head. "Wind in the rocks."

"It's ringing." Sera pressed her gloved palm flat against the mare's neck, feeling the animal's heartbeat hammering faster than the climb warranted. "It's ringing *under* us."

Since the bell-prison, since the Hollow King's cells where sound scraped memory off the bone like a knife scraping hide, Sera had been able to hear things other people couldn't. Or perhaps she'd always been able to hear them, and the prison had only stripped away whatever taught her not to listen. Much of her old self had been left behind in those quiet cells — her doctrine, her certainty, the clean architecture of the Grey Order's law. What remained was simpler and stranger: a woman who trusted one person absolutely and an order not at all, riding toward a stone her superiors had ordered her to shatter.

Her superiors believed the Anchor meant to break Vaelmoor. They did not know — or did not care — that breaking the Oath-stone meant both realms tearing apart like wet cloth. They did not know their own Hollow King had gone silent, his riders ranging ahead of her column like wolves running before a fire.

They especially did not know that Sera had spent the last week writing false reports, describing a column that moved slowly, took wrong passes, lost supplies. Treason by ink. It sat in her chest each morning like a coal she'd learned to breathe around.

"Riders ahead!" The call came down from the point, hushed and urgent. "Lanterns on the switchbacks — guild colors!"

Sera stood in her stirrups. Far above, winding up the black shoulder of the mountain like a lit rosary, the guild caravan crawled. And between her column and theirs, on the middle switchback where the road narrowed to two wagons' width above a drop that the eye couldn't find a bottom for—

Something moved.

Not riders. The darkness on the switchback *poured*, thick and slow, like tar finding its level, and where it poured, the road-sound changed. Sera had grown up on these border roads — or someone had grown up somewhere, the memories still came back wrong, patchwork, smelling of woodsmoke and a sister's laugh — and she knew what healthy stone sounded like under hooves. This sounded like chewing.

"Doss. Signal the column. Lanterns high, blades out, and nobody — *nobody* — steps off the road."

"And if the road isn't safe?"

Sera watched the dark pour another foot up the switchback, toward the guild lights above.

"Then pray the Anchor has a plan," she said, "because I don't."

* * *

Jude heard the warden drums an hour past midnight.

They came up the lower road in good order, which impressed her more than she wanted to admit — Captain Hale's remnant, maybe sixty soldiers in patched warden grey, marching in the starless dark with drummers keeping count so the column wouldn't dissolve into the night and be eaten piecemeal. She stood at the roadside as they approached, Perrin a shadow at her elbow with his sling already loaded, and watched the drums grow louder.

"Halt." Hale himself walked forward, sword drawn but lowered, his face a map of everything Thornfell had lost. He'd burned villages once, on orders. He'd put a boy on a pyre once, on orders. He'd also, Jude had since learned, dragged three of his own sergeants before a tribunal for it afterward, and been broken in rank for his trouble. "Quintana."

"Captain."

"We're not here to arrest you."

"That's a relief. You'd need more than sixty men."

A ghost of a smile crossed his ruined face and died. "We're here because the Order of Wardens is dead, the wards are dead, and my people are dying in the dark. Someone told me you were gathering the stones. That you meant to bring the sky back." His jaw worked. "I don't believe in gods anymore, Quintana. But I believe in the sky. And I've got sixty soldiers who'll follow a working sky anywhere."

Behind Jude, the whole guild column had gone quiet enough to hear the drums' echo die among the crags. Three hundred smugglers who'd spent their lives dodging wardens, staring at sixty wardens asking to march with them.

Jude thought about toll-road pyres. About Perrin, tied to burning faggots, screaming her name without knowing it. About justice that lived outside the law because the law had burned itself down around its own ears.

"You march at the rear," she said. "You guard the wagons. You take orders from Vess and me, not your drummers. And when this is done—" she met Hale's eyes— "there will be a reckoning for the burnings. Yours included. Not tonight. But someday."

"Someday's fair," Hale said. "Tonight's more than I hoped for." He turned and barked an order, and the warden column folded into the rear of the caravan like a blade sliding into a sheath that didn't quite fit yet.

Perrin leaned against her arm. "You just made peace with the guy who tried to burn me."

"I made a truce with him. Peace comes later, and it costs more." She looked up the pass, where the guild lanterns climbed toward a darkness that wasn't empty anymore. "Everything does."

"What is that?" Perrin whispered.

The darkness on the middle switchback had begun to move. Not wind. Not cloud. A slow, viscous flood of absolute black, pouring uphill against all sense, and as Jude's god-given senses opened to it she heard what Sera had heard: a ringing beneath the earth, deep and wrong, like a bell struck inside a grave.

And threading through it, faint and terrible, a voice she recognized.

*Come home,* said the Hollow King, from everywhere and nowhere. *Both of you. Come home, little roots.*

Jude's blood went cold. *Both.* The King knew about Sera. The King knew they were converging.

"Vess!" she shouted. "Break out the fire-oil! Everything you have! And get those wagons moving — we're going through the middle of it!"

* * *

The battle, when it came, did not look like a battle. It looked like the night attacking the light.

The first of the dark-things hit the guild column at the third switchback, where the mountain narrowed and the void yawned on the left like an open mouth. They came out of the rock itself — shapes made of the same starless substance as the sky, long-limbed, many-jointed, with faces that kept rearranging as if they couldn't decide whose face to wear. Where they touched the lanterns, the flames guttered blue and died.

"Fire-oil!" Vess roared, and the answer came as a line of burning arcs over the wagons' heads, bottles shattering against the switchback wall, flame sheeting down and catching the creatures mid-pour. They shrieked — a sound like tearing silk and grinding teeth — and recoiled, and for one heartbeat the road was only road again.

Then the Vaelmoor riders came down from above with lances lit, forty points of pale fire driving into the flank of the dark-flood, and the two columns met in the narrow throat of the pass with the enemy on both sides of them.

"Sera!" Jude screamed it across the chaos, standing in her stirrups with her sword alight from a bottle of burning oil poured down the flat. The grey rider wheeled toward her voice, and even across fifty feet of screaming dark Jude saw the recognition crack across that familiar, impossible face. "Your left! The wall — they're IN THE WALL—"

Sera turned, drove her lance into the living shadow seeping from the rockface, and held it there while the thing writhed on the steel like a fish on a gig. Around them the battle became a single knot — smugglers, wardens, Vaelmoor riders, all of them back to back on a road barely wide enough for two carts, fighting a darkness that came from above, below, inside the stone.

Jude fought until her arms burned, and through it all the bell rang under the mountain, and through it all the Hollow King's voice purred like a mother singing:

*Yes. Fight beautifully. Bring me the stone. Bring me my daughters.*

"DON'T LISTEN TO IT!" Jude bellowed, to everyone, to no one. "IT LIES THE WAY OTHER THINGS BREATHE!"

* * *

They fought through the night by burning the mountain.

That was how Perrin described it later, sitting on a wagon tail with his hands blistered from passing fire-bottles up the line. They fed the switchback walls with flame until the rock itself glowed, and the dark-things withdrew up-pass ahead of them like a tide pulling back from a shore it had decided wasn't worth drowning.

Dawn never came. There was no dawn anymore. But the sky's black lightened by degrees to a bruised grey, and in that grey the survivors counted themselves and found, impossibly, that most of them still breathed.

The column reformed on a shelf of road below the final ascent. Guild wagons in the center. Warden greys on the flanks. Vaelmoor riders, dismounted, holding their pale lances like walking staves, their armor scorched black in places. Enemies a week ago. Now they shared water skins and didn't speak about it.

Jude found Sera at the shelf's edge, cleaning her lance with a warden's rag, because a smuggler's cloth was apparently a step too far.

"You lied to your order," Jude said quietly. "The reports. Slow columns, wrong passes. Doss told Vess."

Sera didn't look up. "Does it matter?"

"It matters that you chose."

"I chose a long time ago." Now she looked up, and her eyes were steady and exhausted and certain in a way that scared Jude more than the dark-things had. "In the bell-prison, when everything else was scraped out of me, you were what was left. That's not a choice, Jude. That's just true."

Jude sat down beside her on the cold stone, shoulder almost touching shoulder, the way she was beginning to suspect they'd sat a thousand times in a life neither of them fully owned.

Above them, the last switchbacks climbed into a notch of deeper black — the throat of the Starless Pass, where the Oath-stone waited in its hollow. And in the notch, arranged with terrible patience, stood the Hollow King's riders. A hundred of them. Silent. Unmoving. Their lanterns burning white instead of orange, and at their center a banner Jude had never seen but somehow remembered: a tree, upside-down, roots reaching for a sky with no stars in it.

"They're not attacking," Perrin said, coming to stand behind them, his shard-warmth radiating faintly against Jude's back like a small hearth. From inside his shirt he drew the fragment of broken stone he'd carried since the burning village — the piece of Memory he'd saved from the pyre without knowing what it was. It was glowing now. Faintly, steadily, pulsing like a heartbeat.

"Why are they waiting?" Sera murmured.

Jude understood, and the understanding dropped through her like ice water.

"Because they don't need to fight us here," she said. "Look at the sky. Look at the road." She pointed up through the notch, to where the thirteenth turn was nearly complete — where, in one day, perhaps less, the moon would finish its impossible circuit and the realms would tear at the seams. "The King doesn't want the stone. The King wants *us* to claim the stone. Wants the rite begun. Because the rite needs an Anchor willing to give herself—"

She stopped.

"—and the King intends to be standing there," Sera finished softly, "when it happens."

Far above, at the mouth of the pass, the Hollow King's white lanterns flickered once — all together, like an eyelid blinking — and the voice rolled down the mountain, gentle as snowfall:

*One day, my darlings. Then you can come home, and be whole.*

The shard in Perrin's hands pulsed brighter, answering, and Jude felt the second stone calling from beyond the notch — singing the opening bars of a rite she'd once performed and paid for, a debt coming due at last.
