## 15. The Second Stone Breaks

The pass to the second stone was called the Throat by the smugglers who once ran it, and it earned the name twice over: once for its narrowness, and once because everything that went down it was swallowed.

Jude climbed it in the hour before dawn, when the starless sky was at its darkest and the mountain seemed to lean in around her like a listener. The air up here tasted of iron and old snow. Below, far below, the column she had gathered moved through the switchbacks — guild wagons with their lanterns hooded, warden remnant marching beside them with their weapons still slung out of habit and suspicion both, Vaelmoor riders on pale horses that made no sound on the stone. Three armies' worth of enemies, bound into a single rope of travelers by nothing but the moon's thirteenth turn and the arithmetic of survival.

Sera walked at her shoulder. Half-erased, grey-cloaked, trusting without proof. Perrin trotted ahead, the shard he'd carried against his heart since Ash-on-the-Toll-Road wrapped now in oilcloth and tied to his belt, where it knocked softly against his hip with every step — a small warm sound in all that cold.

*It knows the way,* the priestess had said before her legs gave out and they'd left her at the last shrine with two guildwives to tend her. *The stone of Oath remembers everyone who ever swore on it. It will remember you.*

Jude did not want to be remembered. She wanted, very badly, to get through this next day without learning anything else about who she had been. But the covenant-stone of Oath sat in the high hollow above the pass, and the thirteenth turn rose tonight, and there was no version of the world after tomorrow that did not require her hands on it.

"Stop," Sera said quietly.

Jude stopped. Ahead of them the trail bent between two walls of black rock, and in the gap the wind changed. It had been moving uphill all night, pushing at their backs the way winds do before weather. Now it came *down*, cold as a cellar door opening, and it carried a smell Jude knew from burned toll villages and from the drowned dark under Lake Verren: ash and bell-metal and something underneath both that was older and worse. The smell of a thing that had been waiting a long time to be hungry again.

"He's here," she said.

"You knew he would be."

"I hoped he wouldn't." She loosened her sword in its sheath. "Hoping's a kind of lying. I try not to do either on a job."

Sera's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling — a small tilt, a ghost of someone laughing in a cottage Jude couldn't see. "You've been doing both since the river crossing."

"That was different." They started forward together, boots crunching frost. "At the river I didn't know what I was hoping for."

"And now?"

Now, Jude thought. Now she hoped for an ending. For the stars back. For the scream of her silent god to stop echoing through the marrow of every night. She hoped Sera would remember her own name by spring. She hoped Perrin would grow up in a world with a sky worth navigating by.

"Now I hope we're fast," she said.

* * *

The hollow opened above them like a cupped hand.

It was a place built for oaths. That was the first thing Jude understood about it, standing at its rim in the grey pre-light. The ring of standing stones around the perimeter were not natural rock; they were too regular, each one carved with a band of script worn nearly smooth, and the ground between them was paved in flagstones fitted so precisely that four hundred years of frost could not pry them apart. In the center, on a low plinth of the same seamless stone, sat the second covenant-stone.

It was smaller than Jude had imagined. Smaller than the memory-flooded thing beneath Lake Verren, which had filled a cavern with its singing. This one stood no taller than a man, a rough pillar of grey-green crystal shot through with veins of faint gold light, and it hummed — not a sound, exactly, but a pressure behind the teeth, a feeling like standing near a struck bell that has not yet finished ringing. Oath. Of course it rang. Everything about it said *I am binding; I am held; hold me back.*

And around it, in the hollow's shadows, waited the Hollow King's riders.

They did not charge. That was the first wrongness. They simply stepped out of the rocks where they had crouched all night, thirty of them, forty, their armor matte and lightless, their faces masked in pale ceramic shaped like sleeping faces. Their horses stood motionless at the hollow's mouth, blocking the way back down the pass. Behind Jude, she heard the column coming up the switchbacks — heard it slow, and falter, and understand, all at once, the shape of the trap they had climbed into.

In the center of the hollow, alone, unarmored, wearing a plain grey coat with his hands folded at his waist, stood the Hollow King.

He was taller than his riders and thinner, and where his face should have been there was only a smooth expanse of skin stretched over absence — eyes gone, nose gone, features worn away like a coin rubbed blank by ten thousand hands. Only his mouth remained, and it was a good mouth, patient, almost kind.

"Anchor," he said. His voice reached every part of the hollow without effort, the way a bell's does. "You brought me a congregation."

"I brought witnesses," Jude said. She walked down into the hollow because stopping felt like conceding. Sera flanked her left; Perrin, despite three separate orders to stay behind the stones, drifted at her right with his hand resting on the shard at his belt. "Whatever happens here, they'll carry it back down the mountain. Both realms will know what you are."

"What I *am*," the Hollow King said, and his mouth curved. "Yes. Let's speak plainly, since we're among honest criminals and dishonest lawmen. I want the covenant broken. Not mended. Broken. Permanently. Vaelmoor has lived a thousand years in Thornfell's shadow — your god's shadow, your wards, your borders drawn by a Wood that wept on command. I intend for us to live in the dark instead. All of us equally. A godless night is the only fair kingdom left."

"The dark breeds monsters," Jude said. "I've seen them on the pass. Your riders have fought them too."

"My riders have *fed* some of them," he said pleasantly. "Everything eats in the dark, Anchor. That's rather the point. In the light, things pretend otherwise."

Behind him, the second stone hummed on, gold-veined, patient. Jude looked at it and felt the pull she had felt under the lake — the sense that the stone knew her, had always known her, had been waiting for her the way a lock waits for its key. The stone of Memory had shown her fragments: a cottage, a mother's hands braiding hair, Sera laughing in a doorway. The stone of Oath would show her more. Would show her, maybe, the whole of what she'd paid to forget.

She found she wanted it so badly her mouth had gone dry.

That, she thought grimly, is how he gets you.

"You offered me a bargain once," she said aloud. "My sister for the stone. I refused. Then you took her anyway. So let's skip the negotiation. You know what I'm going to do."

"I do," the Hollow King said. "And I know something you don't yet. Come closer. Look at the stone. Really look."

She shouldn't have. Every instinct she owned said don't, said it's a hook with bait on it, said look at the riders, look at the exits, look anywhere but the thing that wants looking at. But the hum of the stone had found the frequency of her heartbeat, and her feet carried her forward across the seamless flags, past the plinth, until she stood close enough to see that the gold veins in the crystal weren't veins at all.

They were names.

Thousands of names, tens of thousands, written in script finer than spider silk, winding through the stone like roots through soil. Every oath ever sworn upon it. Every promise kept, every promise broken, every hand laid on this crystal in the long centuries when the two realms still trusted each other enough to bind themselves in writing. And threaded through them all, brighter than the rest, running from base to crown like a spine—

Her own name.

Not *Jude Quintana*. The other name. The one she'd had before she paid for silence. It glowed in the heart of the stone the way a hearth glows in a window seen from a dark road, and looking at it hurt worse than any wound she'd taken on any road, because it looked like *home*.

"There she is," the Hollow King said softly, behind her. "There's my daughter."

* * *

The trap did not spring with a shout. It sprang with a word.

Jude never learned afterward what the word was — some syllable of the old covenant-tongue, perhaps, or perhaps just a name, spoken by a mother who had spent years learning how to unmake what her daughters had sworn to keep. What she knew was this: the moment the Hollow King spoke, the standing stones around the hollow's rim lit with pale fire, and the fire wasn't light but *silence*, a visible hush that poured inward across the flagstones like water finding its level, and everywhere it touched, sound died.

She saw the battle begin and could not hear it.

The guild caravan boiled up over the rim of the hollow and met the riders in a collision that was utterly noiseless — horses wheeling, steel flashing, men's mouths open on shouts that never arrived. Sera was suddenly at her side, sword drawn, lips moving, and Jude read her name off those moving lips — *Jude, JUDE* — and grabbed her arm and pointed at the plinth, at the stone, because whatever else happened, whatever this silence was for, it was for *that*. The Hollow King hadn't come to fight a battle. He'd come to keep anyone from reaching the stone while he did whatever he intended to do to it.

They ran. The silence parted around them like a crowd.

Then the riders came for her specifically, and the hollow became a nightmare conducted for the deaf. Jude fought in a world stripped to sight and touch: the shock of parried blades traveling up her arms, the hot spray of blood with no cry attached, Sera's grey back against hers, turning, always turning, the two of them a single wheel of defense rolling toward the plinth. Perrin was somewhere — she caught one glimpse of him small and furious, swinging a smuggler's hatchet too big for him at the knee of a falling rider — and then the press of bodies closed and he was gone, and terror for him rode her shoulders like a rider of its own.

Twenty feet from the stone. Fifteen.

The Hollow King stood beside it now, and he was not fighting anyone. He had simply raised one hand, palm out toward the crystal, and the pale silence flowed into him and out through his fingers into the stone, and Jude watched the gold veins *dim*. Name by name. Oath by oath. He was drinking it. He was drinking the covenant itself, drawing the binding out of the stone the way poison is drawn into a poultice, and where the names dimmed, hairline cracks crawled across the crystal's face like frost forming on a window.

Ten feet. Five.

A rider stepped out of the melee directly into her path — bigger than the others, his sleeping-face mask cracked across the brow, and Jude recognized the stance if not the man: a warden-sergeant from the toll villages, one of the law's remnants, sold or broken or simply hungry enough to take the Hollow King's coin. She had no time to feel anything about it. Steel met steel three times, and on the fourth stroke Sera took him from the side, a short brutal cut that put him down, and then they were at the plinth, both of them, hands on the humming stone—

—and the Hollow King turned his blank face to them, and smiled with his patient mouth, and spoke the second word.

The stone of Oath *broke*.

It did not shatter. Shattering would have been quick, merciful, comprehensible. Instead it split along its spine — along *her* name — with a sound that cut through the silence like a scream through sleep, one enormous tolling crack that every soul in the hollow felt in their teeth and their bones and their history. The crystal fell apart in two great leaning halves, still upright, still joined at the base, but divided, sundered, a door forced open at its hinge. The gold light poured out of the wound like blood from a severed artery. Names guttered and went dark by the hundreds. By the thousands. The hum that had lived behind Jude's teeth since she entered the hollow thinned to a whine, to a whisper, to nothing.

And the silence the Hollow King had brewed rolled into that nothing and filled it, and settled, and stayed.

The battle stopped. Not ended — *stopped*, the way a heart stops. Riders and smugglers and wardens alike stood frozen in the ringing quiet, staring at the broken stone, because every one of them, in their bones, in the oldest animal part of them, understood what had just died up here above the clouds. The stone of Memory slept under a frozen lake. The stone of Oath lay split and bleeding light into the frost. One stone remained in all the world, somewhere in Vaelmoor's deep country behind the Hollow King's riders, and the thirteenth turn would rise in hours, and the path between here and mending had just been cut.

"No," Jude heard herself say. Her voice sounded obscene in the dead quiet, huge and human. She pressed her hands into the crack, into the gap between the halves, as if she could hold the stone closed by force, as if binding were a thing muscles could do. The edges of the fracture were sharp as razors and the last of the gold light leaked out between her fingers and soaked into her skin and was gone. "No — no, *come back*—"

"Anchor." The Hollow King's voice, in the silence, was the only voice. He sounded almost gentle. Almost sorry. "Do you understand now? There is no reforging. There was never going to be a reforging. Your priestess told you three stones, three roads, a season's quest — a story to keep you walking while the moon turned. But a covenant needs *all* its anchors, child. Memory sleeps. Oath is broken. And the third stone is mine, and has been mine for years, and tonight I will feed it to the dark along with everything your god ever promised anyone." He tilted his blank face, studying her the way a scholar studies a specimen. "You were always going to lose. The only question was whether you'd waste the season finding out. And look — you did. Isn't it better to know?"

Jude knelt in the spilled light with her hands full of ruin.

She thought of the lake freezing behind her. Of the priestess collapsing, naming her Anchor with her last sane breath. Of Sera forgetting herself in a prison of bells and choosing, with nothing left of doctrine or duty, to walk toward her anyway. Of Perrin keeping a stolen shard warm against his heart through burning villages and haunted passes because it was *right*, because no one had to tell him, because justice outside law was just kindness that refused to sit down.

All of it, for a broken rock on a mountaintop. All of it, for nothing.

Grief came up through her like floodwater. She let it. She had learned, somewhere on these roads, that fighting grief only teaches it where you live.

Then, through her palms, through the razor edges of the fracture, she felt it.

Faint. Fainter than a dying ember. But rhythmic.

The stone was not dead. Deep in the split, deeper than the Hollow King's silence had reached, something was still beating — the oldest name, the first oath ever sworn on this crystal, buried too far down to drink. Two names, actually, wound around each other like braided hair. She couldn't read them. She didn't need to. She knew whose they were. Some vows go in too deep to dig out.

The stone of Oath was broken.

It was not empty.

Jude lifted her head. Across the ruined hollow, Sera stood over a fallen rider with her sword still bare and her face turned toward Jude — and her expression, half-erased as she was, stripped of every memory but the choosing, was the most certain thing in the starless world. Beyond her, scrambling over the flagstones with his hatchet abandoned and his hand pressed flat to the oilcloth bundle at his belt, came Perrin. And from the bundle, from the shard he had carried since a burning village, warmth was bleeding out into the frost — answering the beat in the broken stone like a pulse answering a pulse.

The Hollow King followed her gaze. For the first time, the patience left his mouth.

"That," he said, "is impossible."

"Yeah," Jude said, and got to her feet, and put her bleeding hand out toward the boy. "We've been getting that a lot lately."
