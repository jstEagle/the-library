## 16. What Perrin Carried

Perrin had learned, in the weeks since the stars went out, that fear made people loud and hope made them quiet. The camp below the Starless Pass was full of loud — guildsmen arguing over the last of the dried plums, warden remnants sharpening blades they no longer had law to swing, Vaelmoor riders sitting apart with their grey cloaks pulled close like men ashamed of their own colors. But up on the ridge, where Jude Quintana sat alone with her back against a wind-scoured pine, it was quiet.

Perrin climbed toward her with his boots silent on the frost-bitten needles, because he had been a smuggler's boy long enough to know how to be quiet, and because there was something he needed to say before the mountain said it for him.

The moon — the thirteenth turn, fat and wrong and pale as a dead man's fingernail — hung low over the pass. It gave just enough light to see by. Not enough light to trust. Perrin hated it. He had grown up navigating by stars, and when they vanished he'd felt like a page torn out of a book. Now this impostor moon hung where the real sky should have been, counting down the days like a merchant counting coin at closing time.

Jude didn't startle when he sat down beside her. She never did. She just glanced sideways, one eyebrow raised in the way that meant *talk fast or don't talk at all.*

"I need to show you something," Perrin said.

"Show me."

His hand went to the cord around his neck. He'd worn it so long now that he'd stopped feeling the weight of it, the way you stop feeling your own name if you say it enough times. He worked the knot loose with cold-stupid fingers and drew out the little leather pouch, soft as an old ear from all the handling.

"It's from Ashen Toll," he said. "The village. The night the wardens burned it."

Jude went very still. He knew why. He'd seen her face in the firelight that night — the moment she'd cut him down off the pyre, the moment she'd turned and looked at what her own roads had bought. Some things you carried whether you wanted to or not. Perrin figured she carried that village the same way he carried what he was about to give her.

"When the pyre went up," he said, "everything was coming apart. People running. Wardens swinging. And I saw something fall out of the sky. Out of the *dark*, which wasn't right, because there weren't any stars to fall." He turned the pouch over in his palm. Even through the leather, even through his own skin, he could feel it: a warmth, faint and steady, like a bird's heartbeat pressed to your chest. "It came down in the well-yard. Nobody else saw. Everybody was looking at the fire, or at each other, or at nothing. But I was looking up, because I kept thinking — *the sky's broken, maybe it'll fix itself, maybe I'll see it fix itself.*"

"You were watching for the stars," Jude said quietly.

"I was watching for anything." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Anyway. Something fell. Small. Glowing faint, like a coal that doesn't want to die. And I picked it up." His voice dropped. "I don't know why. That's the honest truth. I didn't think it was worth anything. I didn't think about selling it. I just — my mum used to say when something falls out of the sky, you pick it up, because the sky doesn't waste its gifts on people who won't bend over."

That got the ghost of a smile from her. "Wise woman."

"She was." Perrin swallowed. He hadn't talked about his mother since the toll road. "She died in Ashen Toll. In the burning. So did everyone else who ever told me to do the right thing. That's kind of why I've been quiet lately."

* * *

The camp noise drifted up faintly — someone laughing too hard at nothing, the way people do when they're scared. Below them, the whole desperate congregation of the pass lay scattered among the rocks: three caravans of guildsmen who'd spent years hating wardens, a handful of warden survivors who'd spent those same years hanging smugglers, and a dozen Vaelmoor riders whose order had commanded their deaths not two months ago. They'd fought side by side on the high road against the dark-born things that bred in the starless nights, and now nobody quite knew what anybody was anymore. Perrin thought that was probably what a world looked like when its covenant was breaking. Everything holding together out of habit, and the habits wearing thin.

He opened the pouch.

The shard inside was smaller than a sparrow's egg, rough-cut, and it glowed — not bright, never bright, but with a deep amber warmth like lamplight behind horn panes. Against the black of the starless night it seemed almost alive. The warmth hit Perrin's fingers first, then his chest, and he felt that familiar pull under his sternum, the one that had been there since the well-yard. Like something on the other side of the world leaning gently against him.

Jude's breath caught audibly.

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "Perrin. Where exactly did it land?"

"The well-yard. Near the shrine-post. The old covenant-marker, the one the priestess—"

"I know the one." Her voice had gone strange and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice that might hold. "It broke when the wards shattered. The stone of Oath cracked, and pieces of it fell across the realms like seed." She reached out, then hesitated, her fingers hovering. "May I?"

He put it in her hand.

The change was immediate. Jude shuddered like a horse shedding rain. Her eyes went wide and unfocused, and for a heartbeat Perrin saw her mouth shape words without sound — and then the glow of the shard flared once, twice, and steadied, and she sagged back against the pine, breathing hard.

"It knows me," she whispered. "Or — I know it. There's a song in it. Half a song. The other half is in the lake stone, the one we already carry, but this..." She closed her fist around it, and the light leaked gold between her fingers. "This is a piece of the second stone, Perrin. The stone of Oath. We thought it destroyed. We thought the Hollow King's trap had broken it beyond mending."

"The big crack in it? At the pass fight?"

"The stone shattered. This is a piece of the shattering." She laughed — a small, disbelieving sound, wet at the edges. "All this time. You picked it up out of a burning yard and carried it against your heart for two hundred miles."

"My mum said the sky doesn't waste its gifts."

"Your mum," Jude said, "was smarter than every warden and every inquisitor and every guildmaster on this mountain." 

* * *

They sat a while in silence after that, and Perrin let himself feel the thing he hadn't let himself feel since the burning: that he had not lost everything in Ashen Toll. That some part of him — some small, bent-over, sky-watching part — had saved something that mattered.

"Can I tell you something?" he said finally. "I keep thinking about the trial. In the cave-court. When you confessed about selling the roads."

"That's an odd thing to keep thinking about."

"It's the only thing I keep thinking about." He pulled his knees up, wrapped his arms around them. "My mum always said justice was like water — it finds its own level eventually, but somebody's got to dig the channel. The wardens had channels, but they were all crooked. And the guilds — the guilds just had holes." He glanced at her. "You dug one. You stood up in front of everybody who had reason to hate you and said *I did this, and here's what I owe.* Nobody made you. No law made you. You just... did it."

"And then the guild gave us safe passage instead of hanging me," Jude said dryly. "Justice outside the law. It works, mostly."

"That's not what I mean." Perrin frowned, hunting for the words. Words were harder than climbing; he'd never had much schooling. "I mean — everything that's happened since. Sera choosing you over her order. The guild standing with us at the pass. Even the drowned wardens, in the end, letting you take the lake stone because you *asked*, because you honored them. All of it started because somebody chose to tell the truth when lying would've been easier. Small things. Load-bearing things." He touched his chest, where the pouch had hung. "Like this. It's tiny. It's warm. It's been keeping me company for weeks and I thought it was nothing. Turns out it's half the reason the world doesn't end next week."

Jude looked at him for a long moment. In the false moonlight, her scarred face was all angles and shadow, but her eyes were soft in a way he rarely saw.

"How old are you, Perrin?"

"Fifteen come thaw."

"Fifteen," she repeated, shaking her head slowly. "Fifteen, and you just explained the whole covenant better than the priestess did, and she trained thirty years for it."

"The priestess is dying," Perrin said, more bluntly than he meant to. It was true, though. Everyone knew it. The mad grief had burned through her like fever through kindling, and what was left was a husk of a woman propped against the caravan's spare cart, whispering to trees that weren't there.

"Yes," Jude said. "She is."

"And you're going to do what she can't."

"I'm going to try."

Perrin nodded slowly. He'd known it for weeks, really — since the lake, since the frozen shore, since the way Jude talked about the reforging like a woman describing her own funeral in loving detail. He wasn't stupid. He was fifteen and unschooled and he'd watched enough good people die to recognize someone measuring herself for it.

"So," he said, and held out the shard on his open palm. The amber light pooled in his lifelines like honey. "Take it proper. Not borrowed. Given."

"Perrin—"

"You said the covenant needs willing giving. Freely given. That's what you told the drowned wardens, and that's what the priestess screamed at the shrine, and that's what the stones are *for*." His voice cracked, and he hated it, and he pushed through anyway. "So I'm giving it. Willingly. Because my mum said pick it up, and I did, and I kept it warm, and now it needs to go where it's going. That's all. That's the whole speech."

Jude took the shard from him. Her hands were steady; his weren't, and he wiped them on his trousers, embarrassed again.

"It'll want to sing," she warned softly. "When it joins the others. Are you ready for that?"

"No," Perrin said honestly. "But I wasn't ready for the stars going out either, and I'm still here."

The shard flared at her touch — recognition, welcome, homecoming — and the note it made was low and sweet, a single held tone like the last chord of a lullaby. It sank into the space between Jude's fingers and the other fragments she carried, and somewhere beneath the mountain, Perrin would have sworn he felt the deep places of the world shift, like a sleeper turning over.

* * *

They came down from the ridge together, and the camp noticed. Of course it did. Word traveled faster than fire in a place this cramped. By the time they reached the central fire, half the smugglers were on their feet, and Sera Corvane was pushing through the crowd with her grey cloak flying, and the dying priestess was being helped upright against her cart by two guildsmen who'd never once been gentle with anything before tonight.

"What happened?" Sera demanded. Her eyes went straight to Jude's hands, to the new light glowing faintly through her closed fist. "What did he—"

"He found a piece of the Oath stone," Jude said. "In Ashen Toll. The night of the burning. It fell when the first stone cracked the wards. He's carried it since."

Sera stared at Perrin. He stared back, trying not to fidget. This was the woman who'd tried to kill Jude at the river crossing, who'd hunted them across two realms, who'd stood in the bell-prison and forgotten her own name rather than forget her sister. She terrified him, honestly. But she'd also cried into Jude's shoulder at the burnt chapel, and she'd carved their names beside the old record of two sisters sworn to the Wood, and Perrin figured terror and tenderness could live in the same person if you let them.

"A child found it," Sera said slowly. "A child carried it. Through warden patrols and dark-born country and the Hollow King's riders. And none of us—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened. "None of *us* thought to look."

"We were all looking at the sky," Perrin offered. "Waiting for it to fix itself."

The priestess made a sound. It might have been a laugh. It might have been a sob. She leaned harder against the cart, and her clouded eyes fixed on the shard in Jude's hand with something like recognition, something like relief, something like the end of a very long vigil.

"*Three,*" she whispered. "Memory. Oath. And the Anchor herself. Three, three, three." Her head lolled, then lifted. "It's enough. Oh, thank every root and river, it's *enough*—"

"Not enough yet," Jude said. Her voice had gone quiet and iron-hard, the mercenary voice, the voice that had guided caravans through passes no one else would walk. "We still have to carry them up. Past whatever the Hollow King has left. Past whatever our mother sends." She looked around the circle of faces — smuggler, warden, rider, priestess, sister, boy. "One season. Less, now. The thirteenth turn completes at the next dark-of-the-moon, and when it does—"

"When it does, the realms tear," Sera finished. "Yes. We know."

"Then we climb tomorrow," Jude said. "First light. All of us who choose to."

Perrin felt it move through the crowd then — not enthusiasm, exactly, nothing so clean. More like the moment on a rope bridge when everyone steps forward at once and the sway becomes something you can ride instead of something that throws you. The guildsmen shouldering their packs. The warden remnant checking blades they'd sworn to sheathe forever. The Vaelmoor riders pulling their grey hoods back, baring their faces to a sky with no stars, as if to say *we're done hiding.*

Small things. Load-bearing things.

That night, Perrin slept better than he had since the burning. The absence against his chest — the place where the shard's warmth had lived — ached a little, like a tooth pulled, like a hand let go. But underneath the ache was something else. Something his mother would have recognized.

The sky doesn't waste its gifts on people who won't bend over.

He'd bent. He'd picked it up. And come morning, he was going to carry the rest of the way up that mountain — not a stone this time, but the knowledge that the smallest person in any story might be the one holding the load-bearing wall.

He was almost asleep when the screaming started.

Not human screaming — though there was some of that, quickly enough. This was deeper, older, a sound like the mountain itself clearing its throat, rolling down from the heights of the Starless Pass where no sentry had reported movement. Perrin bolted upright, grabbing for his knife, and beside the cold fire pit he saw Jude already standing, the shards blazing in her fist like captured dawn.

Above them, at the crown of the pass, the darkness was moving. Gathering. Taking shapes that had no business holding together — shapes like antlers, like roots, like a crown of thorns worn by something vast and patient that had finally, *finally*, decided to stop waiting at the top of the world.

And threading through the mountain's roar, clear and cold and horribly familiar, came a voice Perrin had heard only once before, at a bargain refused:

"Bring me my daughter's stones, little guide. Or bring me my daughters. The mountain will take whichever comes first."

The Hollow King had come down to meet them.
