## 17. The Hollow King Unmasked

The bell-prison had not wanted to give her back.

Jude had felt its reluctance in the rope bridge that sagged between the cliffs — a swaying span of iron chain and salt-crusted planks that hummed faintly with the same wrong note she'd heard beneath Lake Verren. The Hollow King's fortress was built on silence the way other keeps were built on stone. Every step rang hollow, and every ring was swallowed before it could become an echo.

She crossed with Perrin at her back and the priestess carried between them like something breakable. Behind them, the freed prisoners stumbled into grey daylight, and among them Sera — half-erased, blinking at the sun as though it were a language she'd once spoken fluently and now only recognized.

Sera had looked at Jude on the bridge and said nothing. But she had reached out without looking and taken a fistful of Jude's cloak, and had not let go for the whole crossing.

Now they stood in the antechamber of the Hollow King's hall, and the doors opened on their own, and the silence leaned forward to meet them.

* * *

The throne room was not what she expected.

Jude had imagined a tyrant's court: banners, guards, the architecture of fear. Instead there was a long room of black glass, and along its walls hung bells — hundreds of them, thousands — each one wrapped in wool, in silk, in strips of funeral linen, muzzled so thoroughly that not one had sounded in years. She could feel them straining. She could feel the sound they wanted to make pressing against the quiet like water against a dam.

At the far end, seated on a chair that was merely a chair — no gold, no skulls, no ornament — sat the Hollow King.

He — it wore a man's shape, tall and gaunt, robed in grey so pale it was nearly white — rose when they entered. And Jude's first thought, absurdly, was that he moved like someone who had forgotten how bodies worked and was remembering one joint at a time.

"You brought her," the Hollow King said, and his voice was soft and ruined, like wind through a dead orchard. "You brought the Anchor to me. I confess I did not expect it."

"I came for answers." Jude stepped forward, putting herself between the King and the others. Old habit. New meaning. "And I'm leaving with them whether you like it or not."

"Mercenary bluster." He tilted his head, studying her. "You wear it well. You've worn it for years. Do you know how strange it is to watch someone wear your daughter's face like borrowed armor?"

The words landed somewhere below thought, in the place where dread lives before it has a name.

Perrin shifted behind her. The shard against his chest — the fragment of the broken stone of Oath — had begun to glow through his shirt, a warm ember-light pulsing like a second heartbeat. Jude felt an answering pull in the pack at her own hip, where the stone of Memory slept and sang.

"Your daughter," she repeated carefully. "You have no daughters. The records—"

"The records," the Hollow King said, "were written by people who needed there to be no such sisters. Records are just bells that learned to lie."

He raised one hand, and the wool wrappings on the nearest hundred bells began, very slowly, to unravel.

* * *

"You want to know what I am," he said. "So I will tell you, because you have earned truth the hard way, and because truth is the only currency left in this room."

He walked toward them down the length of black glass, and as he walked, he changed. It was not dramatic. That was the horror of it — no burst of fire, no pealing thunder. He simply became smaller. His shoulders narrowed. His hands softened. The grey robe pooled around him like shed skin, and by the time he stood ten paces away, the Hollow King was gone and a woman stood in his place — tall, silver-streaked, with lines around her eyes that spoke of decades spent squinting against weather Jude had never seen.

Jude knew that face.

Not from memory. Memory had been carved out of her; she'd known that since the lake, since the stone of Memory had flooded her with fragments of a life she never lived — a cottage with blue shutters, a mother's hands kneading bread, Sera laughing with grass in her hair. Those fragments had faces. One of them belonged to this woman.

But it was more than the fragments. It was her own reflection. When Jude looked into mirrors she saw this woman's jaw, this woman's brow, twenty years younger. When she dreamed — the rare dreams that survived the wardens' camps and the smuggling roads — she dreamed from inside this face.

"No," Jude said aloud. It came out very small.

"My name," the woman said, "is Maren Iversen. I was the Anchor before you. I held the covenant between Thornfell and Vaelmoor for thirty-one years, and then I did what Anchors do when they grow tired, or frightened, or hungry for a world where their children don't have to die for strangers' peace." Her smile was terrible in its gentleness. "I broke my oath. And when my daughters sealed the covenant in my place — when one of them paid the price I should have paid — I fled into the dark between realms, and I fed on the only thing left that could keep me alive."

The bells. The silence. Jude understood all at once, with a nausea that rose from her boots to her teeth.

"The Wood," she whispered. "You've been feeding on the Weeping Wood."

"On its silence, specifically." Maren spread her hands, and Jude saw that beneath the skin they were faintly translucent, veined with shadow, like paper held up to a candle. "A god screams when it's caged, girl, but gods also sleep, and in their sleep they dream in silence, and silence is *food*, if you know how to eat it. I have eaten eleven years of my god's dreaming. That is why the stars went out. That is why the wards fell. Not smugglers. Not Vaelmoor's inquisitors. Me. Your mother."

Behind Jude, Sera made a sound — small, wounded, wordless. Half-erased Sera, who remembered nothing but trusted anyway, and who had just watched her own face walk around on a stranger.

"Don't," Jude said, and her voice cracked on it. "Don't say *your daughters*. You lost that word."

"I never lost anything." Maren's eyes — grey, the exact grey of Sera's order, the exact grey of stormlight on the pass — glistened. "That is rather the point."

* * *

"Here is what happened," Maren said, and began to walk a slow circle around them, and the unraveled bells trembled in their frames, drinking the words even as she spoke them. "Thirty-two years ago, the covenant between the realms frayed. It always frays; that is its nature. It is not a wall, it is a marriage, and marriages must be tended. I tended it. I gave it years of my life. I gave it—" her voice caught, genuinely or otherwise, "—I gave it everything, and the Wood kept taking, and the wardens kept demanding, and the realms kept forgetting what the covenant was *for*. So I found a way to end the tending forever. A covenant unmade cannot fray. A god unbound cannot demand."

"You tried to kill it," Jude said.

"I tried to *free* it. There is a difference that only slaves can see clearly." Maren stopped circling. She was close now, close enough that Jude could smell her — cold stone and lamp oil and, underneath it, unbearably, bread. Bread baking. The cottage with blue shutters. "The rite required an Anchor's willing surrender. I went to perform it myself. My daughters followed me. They were seventeen and nineteen, and they loved me, and they thought they were saving me." Her mouth twisted. "They were right, which is the cruelest part. They bound the Wood instead of freeing it. They reforged the covenant with a stronger knot than mine. And the price — the Anchor's memories, freely given — they split it between them, because neither would let the other pay alone."

Jude's hand drifted to her temple without her permission.

"But the rite demanded *all* of it," Maren went on softly. "From one. Your sister tried to take the whole price herself. You wouldn't allow it. So you struck her memory of the rite first — stole her recollection of choosing — and took the remainder yourself. She woke believing she had failed. Believing she had no sister, because the records had been scrubbed, because the Order needed her clean. And you woke with nothing at all, and walked out of the shrine, and sold your skills to smugglers because a body remembers trade even when a soul forgets its name."

The room was very quiet. Even the straining bells seemed to lean in.

"That's not true," Perrin said from behind her, fierce and young. "Jude wouldn't—"

"It's entirely true," Maren said. "It is the truest thing in either realm. Ask the stone she carries."

And the stone of Memory, in its wrapping at Jude's hip, pulsed warm against her side — agreement, confirmation, grief.

* * *

"So," Maren said, and now her voice changed, and Jude heard the merchant enter it, the bargainer, the thing that had worn a king's shape for eleven years. "Here is my offer. Listen with the whole of yourself, because I will only make it once."

She raised her hand again, and this time every bell in the hall unwrapped at once — and still made no sound. They simply hung there, mouths open, waiting.

"I can restore you," Maren said. "All of it. The cottage. Your mother's kitchen. Sera at nine years old, missing teeth, teaching you swear words in Vaelmoori. The night of the rite — every choice, every word, including the ones I've just told you, but *felt* this time, lived, owned. Eleven years of yourself, returned whole. No fragments. No vertigo. You would know exactly who you are and exactly what you lost and exactly why." She stepped closer. "And more than that. I can give you Sera's memories too — hers were stolen by the Order, not the rite. I know where they're kept. Between us we could make your sister whole. Both of you entire. Two daughters, restored, standing in the light of everything that was taken from them."

"And the price?" Jude's voice came out flat. She was proud of that. Some distant part of her was taking notes on how flat her voice came out, the way she'd once noted wind direction before a mountain crossing.

"Walk away." Maren said it gently, the way you'd tell a child the honest answer to *is Grandmother coming back*. "Leave the shards here. Let the thirteenth turn pass unreforged. The realms will tear apart at the seam, yes — borders will burn, the lowlands will flood with the dark, thousands will die. I won't lie to you about that; you deserve better than my lies. But you and Sera will live. Whole. Together. On some shore far from both dying realms, with every year of your life returned to you, and your sister beside you knowing your name." Her translucent hand hovered near Jude's cheek, not touching. "Or you can pour what's left of yourself into a tree, and spend eternity weeping sap over two kingdoms that forgot you existed before you ever finished forgetting them."

The silence of the hall pressed down like deep water.

* * *

Jude thought about the cottage.

That was the shame of it, and she let herself feel the shame fully, because she'd promised herself in the drowned dark under Lake Verren that she would never look away from her own heart again. She thought about the cottage with blue shutters. She thought about bread. She thought about a version of herself who had a childhood, who had a mother who wasn't this hollow-eyed creature, who had a sister whose laugh she could summon on command instead of receiving secondhand through a singing stone. She thought about walking out of this hall *whole*, with Sera's hand in hers, both of them stitched back together, leaving the world behind them to tear along a seam they hadn't sewn.

Eleven years. She had been robbed of eleven years, and everyone who'd done the robbing was dead or mad or standing in front of her wearing her own jawline.

"You're crying," Maren observed.

"I noticed," Jude said.

She wiped her face with the back of her hand, the way she did after a bad crossing, businesslike, and looked at the woman who had given birth to her.

"Here's what I remember," Jude said. "Not from before. From *now*. I remember pulling a boy off a pyre because it was right and illegal. I remember a drowned warden under Lake Verren saluting a law that didn't exist anymore, and I understood him, because loyalty to dead things is the only religion I've ever seen practiced honestly. I remember Sera letting me escape at the river when every doctrine she had said to cut my throat. I remember her taking my cloak on that bridge and holding on." Her voice steadied as it went, the way a road steadies after a pass. "Those aren't memories you can sell back to me. They're the only ones I ever made myself. And every single one of them points the same direction."

"Which is?"

"Away from you."

Maren's face did not change. That was the worst thing — it didn't shatter, didn't rage. It simply closed, like a door in winter, and behind the closing Jude glimpsed something vast and starved and sad.

"The boy keeps the Oath-shard warm against his heart," Maren said quietly. "Did you know that? I can hear it from here. A child's kindness, load-bearing. It won't be enough, you understand. Two stones, one broken. The rite requires three whole. Even if you refuse me — even if you climb to the altar and stand there heroically — the covenant cannot be reforged from rubble." She turned away, gathering her grey robe around her translucent frame. "So consider, over the coming hours, what refusal actually purchases. Not salvation. Just a different way to lose."

"The thirteenth turn is in four days," Jude said.

"Three," said Maren. "You've lost time in my halls. Everyone does. Time is the first thing the silence eats."

* * *

They left the throne room in single file, and the doors closed behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released.

In the corridor outside, Sera stopped. Half-erased, stripped of her order, her doctrines, her past — she had heard all of it, every word. She looked at Jude with those grey eyes that were supposed to belong to a stranger, and her jaw was set in a line Jude realized she knew from mirrors.

"She lied about one thing," Sera said.

"The stones? She's right, we need—"

"Not that." Sera's hand found Jude's wrist, gripped it hard enough to leave marks, the grip of someone anchoring themselves to the one true thing in a hall full of muzzled bells. "She said you'd be spending eternity weeping over kingdoms that forgot you." Her voice shook, and she let it. "I didn't forget you. I couldn't. Whatever they took out of my head, they didn't get all of it. Because when you walked into that prison, I knew you. Before you said your name. Before any of it. Something in me stood up and said *there*." 

Her eyes were wet and furious and certain.

"So whatever you decide at that altar — decide knowing somebody remembered."

Jude could not speak. She nodded instead, and Sera released her wrist, and they went out together into the thin cold light of the starless morning.

Above the eastern peaks, where the covenant site waited beyond the clouds, the darkness had begun — very faintly, very wrongly — to pulse. Like a heartbeat. Like a countdown.

Three days. Two broken stones. One road.

Jude shouldered her pack, checked the blade at her hip out of twelve years of habit, and started climbing.

Behind them, faint through the fortress walls, a single bell — one bell, unwrapped, unfed — rang once.

It sounded almost like her name.
