## 18. Thirteenth Turn Rising

### Jude

The pass began where the world's kindness ended.

Jude stood at the mouth of the Starless Pass with the first stone warm against her sternum and the second — cracked, but still singing its broken note — wrapped in oilcloth across her back, and she looked up at a road that climbed out of the dark itself. There was no moon to light it. There had been no moon for a season now, only that swollen absent place in the sky where the thirteenth turn gathered like a held breath, and the stars had never come back, and above the pass the clouds glowed faintly from beneath with a light that had no honest source.

"Hours," Perrin said beside her. He said it the way he'd been saying it all day, each time a little flatter, as if the word were wearing smooth from handling. "The astronomer said hours, not days."

"The astronomer also said the sky would fall on Vaelmoor first," Sera said, tightening the girth on her grey mare. The horse was the last thing she owned that belonged to her order, and she had stopped pretending otherwise three days ago. "So we have until at least the top of this mountain to be wrong about everything."

Jude almost smiled. Almost. Her face had forgotten how, somewhere around the moment she'd learned the woman who'd hunted her was her sister, and the man who'd bargained for both their lives was their mother wearing a crown of borrowed silence.

Below them, far down the valley, torchlight moved. Not scattered, not searching — moving *together*, a long deliberate serpent of fire coiling up the low road. Riders. The mother's riders, the Hollow King's black-liveried column, closing the distance with the patience of something that did not need to sleep.

"They'll reach the switchbacks by dark," Jude said. "Which is now. Which is always now." She flexed her hands inside her gloves. They ached. Everything ached — her palms from the ice of Lake Verren, her ribs from the cave-court trial, someplace deeper than either, from a memory she had chosen once to cut out of herself and could feel now like an amputation healing wrong.

Perrin tugged his coat closed over his chest, over the small hard shape he still carried there. He hadn't shown her the shard again since the cave. He didn't need to. She could feel it the way you felt a hearth through a wall.

"We don't have to take the high road," he said. "There's the old smuggler's cut, under the ridge—"

"—and it ends in a scree chute no horse survives," Jude finished. "I sold passage on that cut myself, eleven years ago. I know how it ends." She said it without flinching, which was new. Confession had done that. Standing in front of the guilds and naming every road she'd sold, every warden's purse she'd fattened — it had burned something away and left her lighter and uglier and more able to walk uphill. "High road. We climb. We climb fast, and we climb mean, and when they catch us—" she looked at Sera, "—we make them regret the catching."

Sera swung into the saddle. In the under-light of the glowing clouds her face was all planes and shadow, the face Jude kept finding in dreams she now knew were memories: a kitchen with a crooked door, a girl laughing so hard she fell off a stool, a mother singing in a language the dreams refused to translate.

"You've gone grim," Sera observed.

"I've gone honest. It looks similar at a distance."

They rode into the pass together, and the mountain swallowed them, and behind them the serpent of torches bent upward like a thing smelling blood.

* * *

### Sera

The oath broke at the third switchback, quietly, the way most true things happen.

They had stopped to breathe — horses lathered, breath hanging in the windless cold, the glow of the cloud-ceiling turning everything the color of old pewter — and Sera found herself standing at the edge of the trail looking down the whole descending spine of the pass. The riders were visible now even without torches, because the dark itself had begun to breed shapes. Things moved along the low road at the column's edges, long-limbed, starved-looking, drawn to the moonless night the way moths drew to flame except these were the moths and the flame was absence. Her order had names for them. Her order had protocols for them. Her order, she understood now, had *built* them — or rather, had spent two hundred years feeding the Wood's silence until the silence grew teeth.

*Inquisitor Corvane.* She heard the title in her own head in her superior's voice, dry as ledger paper. *You are ordered to kill the Anchor. You are reminded that your attachment is a documented vulnerability. You are reminded that you have already lied to this office twice.*

Three times, she thought. Four. She'd lost count somewhere around forging the travel writs.

"Jude." Her voice came out strange to her, formal, the voice of a woman reading charges. Jude turned. Perrin's hand went instinctively toward his knife, then away; the boy had learned which adults were safe the hard way, and he re-learned it every morning, checking.

"When I took the grey," Sera said, "we swore on the Ledger of Quiet Hours. No name, no family, no allegiance above the order. We were told it was a mercy — that forgetting who you were made you free to serve." She laughed, short and joyless, and the sound skittered off the rock. "It wasn't a mercy. It was a theft. They stole us the same way the Hollow King steals memory in those bells, only slower, with paperwork."

Jude said nothing. She had learned, in the weeks since the river crossing where they'd fought and Sera had inexplicably let her go, that silence was a language her sister spoke fluently. So Sera kept going, because if she stopped she would lose the nerve.

"I'm breaking it. Here. Now. Formally, because I know how oaths work, and informally, because I think the formal part matters less than this:" she pulled the grey sash from beneath her cloak — the one mark of her rank she had never removed, not in the lake, not in the battle on the pass, not when the bell-prison had scraped her mind down to bare trust — and she held it out over the drop. "The Order of the Quiet Ledger has no claim on me. My name is Sera. My sister is Jude Quintana, who I tried to kill, who let me live anyway, who is climbing this mountain to give everything she has left to a god that cannot even thank her. And my allegiance is—" the wind pushed at the sash, hungry, "—is hers. Is ours. Is whatever's left after."

She let go.

The grey cloth caught the updraft, billowed once like a wounded bird, and went down into the dark between the switchbacks, and Sera watched it until it was nothing, and found she was shaking, and found that the shaking felt like standing upright for the first time in years.

Jude crossed the trail and put her hand on the back of Sera's neck, forehead to forehead, the way — Sera realized with a jolt — the way you greet someone you were raised with, someone whose skull-shape you know, a gesture with no origin in anything either of them remembered and perfect provenance in everything they'd recovered.

"Took you long enough," Jude said, and her voice cracked on the joke, and neither of them pretended it hadn't.

"Boy," Sera managed, turning to Perrin because if she looked at Jude one more second she would come apart before the summit, "you're witness. That's how it works in the low countries — a child can witness an oath-breaking, makes it binding."

"Already witnessed," Perrin said solemnly. Then, ruining it entirely, the way children ruin things beautifully: "Also I ate the last of the dried plums."

* * *

### Jude

Above the fifth switchback the trees gave up.

That was the only way Jude could describe it. The pines thinned, then stooped, then simply stopped mid-slope as though walking into a room whose purpose they hadn't been told, and beyond them the pass climbed bare through fields of scree and old snow, under the sourceless pewter glow, toward a saddle of rock where — the priestess had told her, mad and dying and certain — the covenant altar stood above the clouds, older than both realms, older than the quarrel that had broken it.

Older than their mother. Older, maybe, than mothers.

Jude climbed with the stones' weight pulling at her spine and thought about weight. The first stone sang constantly now, a thin sweet note of Memory that filled her skull with other people's yesterdays: bread rising on a windowsill she'd never stood beside; a grey-cowled girl practicing sword-forms in a chapel yard while a smaller girl mocked her stance; a woman's voice saying *the Wood does not want your worship, daughters, it wants your attention*. Fragments. Shards of a life she had paid away. She had stopped fighting them. Fighting them cost more than carrying them.

The second stone, cracked across its heart, sang the same note a half-beat late, a broken round, an echo that couldn't quite find its original. Oath. Even shattered it pulled at her — every step uphill felt like walking against a current of promise.

"How much farther?" Perrin asked, and it wasn't complaint. The boy had walked out of a burning village with a stolen shard against his heart and hadn't complained once in four hundred miles; Jude suspected complaint had been burned out of him along with the village, and she meant to spend whatever power she gained making sure the world gave him something back.

"Saddle's the altar," she said. "Cloud-line's just past the trees. Above the clouds, the priestess said. Above the weather. Above the reach of—" she almost said *her*, and changed it. "Of everything below."

"And the riders?"

Jude looked back. The switchbacks descended below them like the vertebrae of some enormous buried animal, and along them, three turns down, the torch-serpent climbed. Faster than she'd estimated. The riders didn't rest. Of course they didn't. Their mistress didn't sleep, didn't age honestly, didn't do any of the ordinary mortal things that Jude had spent her whole mercenary life treating as guarantees.

"Two hours ahead of them," she said. "Maybe less. Maybe—" and the mountain answered her.

It came as a sound first, a groan deep in the rock like a ship changing its mind, and then the glow beneath the clouds *pulsed*, brightening, dimming, brightening, and Jude felt it in the stones against her body — felt both notes bend, sharp, urgent, the way a singer pushes toward a final chord. The thirteenth turn, gathering. The season ending not in days but in hours, exactly as the astronomer had sworn and exactly as she'd hoped he was wrong.

"Run the horses," she said. "We run them until they drop and then we run ourselves."

* * *

### Sera

They lost the horses at the cloud-line, as Jude had known they would, and Sera marked it as the last easy decision of the day.

Beyond the clouds the air changed. It was thinner, colder, and yet easier to breathe in a way that made no physiological sense and complete spiritual sense — as though the air itself were less argued-with up here, less claimed. The pewter glow resolved as they climbed: not weather at all but light leaking upward from the realms themselves, Thornfell and Vaelmoor both, two wounds in the world shining through their bandages. From up here Sera could see what no map showed: that the realms were close. That the border was not a line but a seam, and the seam had been stitched by something living, and the stitching was failing.

And she could see the riders clearly now, unlit by any torch, flowing up the switchbacks like ink poured uphill.

"They're not riding anymore," Perrin said. He lay flat at the lip of the trail, peering down. "Look. They're *pouring*."

He was right. The column had abandoned formation and become something else, a dark flood finding every channel upward at once, and at its head — Sera's stomach turned — a figure on a pale horse that did not move like a horse, ridden by a shape in a crown of antlered iron that she knew from a single glimpse in the bell-prison's courtyard, from the worst hour of her life.

"She's coming herself," Sera said. "Not sending anyone. Coming."

"Good," Jude said.

Sera looked at her sister. Sweat had frozen in Jude's hairline; her lips were cracked; she had the stones and the shards and the guilt and the memories-not-hers and maybe six hours of existence left as a separate person, and she said *good* the way other people said *finally*.

"Explain to me how that is good."

"Because she can't bargain with me through messengers anymore. Because whatever happens at that altar, she has to say it to my face." Jude shouldered her pack. Below them the flood climbed. Above them the saddle waited, black against the leak of light, and Sera thought she could see — or feel, the distinction had gotten unreliable — the altar's silhouette, a table of stone older than argument. "Come on. Both of you. The reckoning's uphill."

* * *

### Jude

The last hour of the climb, Jude stopped being afraid.

She noticed it happening the way you notice a fever breaking. Somewhere around the snowline — real snow now, ancient, blue in the under-light — the fear simply drained out of her and left something else, something that felt less like courage than like *arrival*. Every fragment the stone of Memory gave her now came clear and whole, no longer shards but scenes: the cottage with the crooked door; Sera at eight, solemnly burying a dead sparrow with full military honors; their mother's hands braiding their hair into one shared braid as a joke, *so you can't be separated, see?* — and then the other memory, the one that had surfaced in the priestess's hut and still stopped her breath: herself, younger, kneeling before this very altar, choosing the knife of forgetting, choosing to pay.

*I did this,* she thought. *I chose this. Which means I can choose again.*

The covenant-stones hummed against her body, both notes bending toward unison, and she understood suddenly why the rite needed a willing Anchor, why her mother's stolen immortality had never satisfied the pact, why the whole rotten architecture of the Hollow King's reign leaked silence into the world. A covenant was not a contract. It was a person. It had always been a person, standing between two realms and holding them together the way a keel holds a boat, and her mother had tried to hold the position without paying the price, and the price unpaid had curdled into everything: the fallen wards, the vanished stars, the monsters breeding in the dark.

Jude Quintana had paid once. She could pay again. The knowledge sat in her chest, terrible and calm, like a loaded crossbow pointed at her own future.

"Jude." Perrin's voice, small in the vast white silence. "Look."

She looked up.

The saddle was fifty yards away, and the altar stood against the sky — and above the altar, impossibly, in a heaven that had been empty for a season, there was a *hole*. A circle of clearing darkness, ringed with the first stars she'd seen since the night the wards fell, widening slowly, breathing, like an eye remembering how to open.

And below, pouring up the final slope with the patience of winter, came their mother.

* * *

### Sera

Sera reached the altar first, because she was the fastest climber and because some part of her had decided, without consulting her, that she would stand between her sister and the world for as long as standing worked.

The altar was a slab of black stone veined with silver, and it wept. Clear water, not sap — the priestess's shrine had wept blood, but here at the source the wound ran clean — beading along the silver veins and dripping off the edge into the ancient snow, each drop ringing faintly when it landed, a small wet bell tolling the same note as the stones. She laid her palm on the slab and the weeping water was warm. Alive. *Waiting.*

"Jude. It knows you're here."

Jude crested the rise with Perrin a step behind, and the altar's note deepened, and the hole in the sky widened another degree, and down-slope the flood of riders came on, and at their center the antlered crown caught the starlight from above and reflected nothing back at all.

Then the riders stopped.

All at once, forty yards below the saddle, the whole dark flood halted as if the mountain had spoken a word only it could hear, and the pale horse walked forward alone, and the figure in the antlered iron crown dismounted, and lifted both empty hands in a gesture so horribly like a mother's greeting that Sera's whole body flinched.

"My daughters," the Hollow King called up, and the voice was their mother's voice, the voice from the braid, from the kitchen, from every dream the stones had returned, and it was gentle, and it was starving. "You brought them both to me. How thoughtful. Now — shall we talk about what you remember, Jude? Or shall I tell you what you forgot?"

The stars overhead widened their circle. The altar wept faster.

Jude stepped forward to the altar's edge, and Sera moved with her, shoulder to shoulder, and Perrin's small warm hand found the hem of Jude's cloak, and the reckoning began.
