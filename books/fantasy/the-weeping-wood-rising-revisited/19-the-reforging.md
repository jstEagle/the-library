## 19. The Reforging

The wind above the clouds did not howl so much as *remember* — a long, low moan that came up through the stone of the pass and out of the mountain's throat, as if the whole spine of the world were clearing itself to speak. Jude climbed the last stretch of the starless stair with Perrin's shard warm against her own chest now, having taken it from him at the tree line with hands that would not stop shaking.

"Keep it safe," he had said, pressing it into her palm. It was no bigger than a knucklebone, that fragment of the broken Oath-stone, and it had ridden against a child's heart for half a season while armies gathered around it like weather. "You have to give it back yourself. That's what it's for."

She had wanted to tell him he was wrong, that stones didn't need ceremony, that she was a mercenary and mercenaries knew there was always a cheaper way. But the shard pulsed against her skin like a second heartbeat, and every step upward made it beat faster, and by the time she crested the final ridge she understood that Perrin had understood something she was only beginning to grasp: some things cannot be carried for you. Some things can only be given.

The summit opened before her like a wound in the sky.

There were no stars — there had been none since the night the wards broke — but the darkness here was different from the darkness below. Below, the dark was a blindness, an absence, a thing done *to* the world. Here it was a presence, thick and breathing, pooling in the great bowl of the covenant site the way water pools in a cupped hand. The altar stood at its center: a ring of pale stone older than either realm, older than the roads she'd sold, older than the names Vaelmoor and Thornfell. Twelve sockets circled its rim, one for each of the old moons, and at its heart a thirteenth hollow waited, shaped like nothing so much as a person lying down.

Jude had been walking toward that shape her whole life without knowing it.

Sera came up beside her, grey cloak torn at the shoulder from the pass, her face bloodied at the temple where a rider's flail had grazed her. She carried the first stone — Memory — wrapped in linen against her breast, and when she set it down on the altar's rim it sang, a single clear note that hung in the dead air like a held breath.

"The second is cracked," Sera said. Her voice was steady, but her hands were not. "Even with Perrin's shard, the Oath-stone is—"

"It's enough." Jude heard herself say it and marveled at how certain she sounded. "It doesn't need to be whole. It needs to be *willing*."

Sera turned to look at her then, really look, and Jude saw the question arrive in her sister's eyes before it reached her mouth — the question Sera had been circling since the burnt chapel, since the priestess's confession, since the moment their mother's face had appeared beneath the Hollow King's crown.

"Jude. What does the rite require?"

Below them, faint as surf, the sound of riders climbing.

* * *

Their mother had come up the eastern road, and she had come beautifully.

That was the worst of it, Jude thought later — or thought in the strange timeless way she would think things once time stopped mattering to her. The woman who had worn the name Hollow King wore mortality badly and majesty well. Her riders fanned out across the lower bowl of the summit with the discipline of a hunting pack, torches blooming one by one in the dark like a constellation of false stars, and at their center rode a figure on a pale horse whose face was the face from Jude's dreams — the cottage kitchen, the song hummed over bread dough, the hands that had braided two small girls' hair by a fire that smelled of pine sap and woodsmoke.

*Mother,* said the memory-stone against Sera's chest, unbidden, in a voice like a struck bell.

"I gave you a chance," the Hollow King called up across the bowl, and her voice carried the way voices carry in high thin air, perfectly, terribly. "I offered you wholeness, daughter. Every memory I took from you when you sealed the covenant — your childhood, your sister's laughter, your mother's face. All of it returned. You could walk down this mountain *yourself*, Jude Quintana. Whole. Human. Free."

"You mean I could walk away," Jude called back.

"I mean you could *live*."

"And everyone else?"

Her mother smiled, and it was the smile from the dreams too, and it broke something in Jude to see it worn by the thing that fed on a god's silence. "Everyone else chose their side already. The wardens burned villages. The smugglers sold roads. The Wood let itself be imprisoned rather than fight. Let the realms tear along the seam they cut. You owe them nothing."

"That's where you're wrong." Jude stepped forward to the altar's edge, and the wind pulled at her hair, and below her the torches wavered. "I owe them everything. That's what you never understood about anchors, Mother. An anchor isn't a chain. It isn't a cage. It's the thing that says *here*. Not because it's owed. Because someone has to."

"You're a mercenary," her mother said softly. "You told me so yourself, the day you forgot me. You sell your sword to whoever pays. What makes this different?"

And Jude laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised even her — because the answer was so simple it had been sitting in her chest since a burning toll village, since a boy tied to a pyre, since a river crossing where a stranger's face felt like home.

"Because this time," she said, "I'm paying."

* * *

The rite, when she finally understood it, required almost nothing and absolutely everything.

The priestess's dying words had been riddles; the drowned wardens' loyalty had been a lesson; the singing stone had shown her fragments. But standing at the altar with the shards in her hands, Jude found the knowledge simply *arriving*, the way a route arrives in a guide's mind after years of walking — not learned but known, worn into her like a path into stone.

Three stones. Memory, whole. Oath, cracked but mended with a child's kept faith. And the third —

The third was her.

The thirteenth socket was person-shaped because the covenant had never been sealed with stone alone. Stone held the pattern; flesh held the promise. Someone had to lie down in that hollow and become the hinge between worlds, willingly, with open eyes, trading everything they were for everything the worlds needed. The previous Anchor had done it and then betrayed it. Jude had done it once already — that was the truth under all the truths, the shape beneath the shape. She had lain on this altar years ago and paid with her memories, and the payment had bought a season of quiet, and the season had ended, and now the debt came due again at compound interest.

But last time, she understood, she had done it as duty. Duty was a coin that spent poorly.

This time it had to be a gift.

"Sera." She turned to her sister. The torchlight from below made the grey of Sera's cloak look almost silver. "When I start, it'll pull. Everything I am will start coming loose — the memories I got back at the lake, and the ones I never got back, and the ones I'm making right now, this minute, looking at you. It'll want all of them. I need you to hold the circle and not let go, no matter what I say. No matter who I forget."

"No." Sera's voice cracked clean through. "No, there has to be another—"

"There isn't. There never was. That's what the priestess meant, that's what the Wood's been trying to scream at me since the night the stars went out." Jude took her sister's face in both hands. Up close, Sera's eyes were wet and furious and achingly familiar, and Jude memorized them — the exact grey, the exact anger, the exact love underneath the anger — because she had a feeling she would need the memory where she was going. "You found me when you remembered nothing but trusted anyway. Do that one more time. Trust me. Even when I stop being someone you can recognize."

"Jude—"

"Say you'll hold the circle."

A sob tore out of Sera like something breaking bone. Then she straightened, wiped her face with the heel of her hand the way she must have done as a child, and said, in the flat iron voice of the Grey Order repurposed for a truer oath:

"I will hold the circle."

"Perrin." The boy stood at the altar's edge, small and soot-stained and utterly unafraid, and Jude crouched to his level. "You kept a shard warm against your heart through fire and ice and a frozen lake. I need you to do one more impossible thing. When I call for the third stone, you bring me the shard. Don't think about it. Don't be scared. Just bring it."

"Okay," Perrin said. Then, with the terrible directness of children: "Will it hurt?"

"Yes."

"Will you still be you?"

Jude smiled at him, and found the smile was real. "Better," she said. "I'll be *us*."

* * *

She lay down in the thirteenth hollow, and the stone received her like water.

The first thing she felt was cold — not the cold of altitude but the cold of deep places, lake-bottom cold, root-cold, the cold of the Weeping Wood's buried heart. Then warmth, rising to meet it, the warmth of Perrin's shard against her sternum, and where the two temperatures met inside her chest something kindled that was neither hot nor cold but *awake*.

"Memory," she said, and Sera lifted the first stone and set it in its socket, and it began to sing.

"Oath," she said, and the cracked stone went into its broken socket, and its song came out ragged, wounded, true — a note with a scar in it, and somehow that made it stronger, the way a mended bone heals thicker than it ever was.

The pull began.

It started gently, the way tide starts, barely perceptible — a lightness in her fingertips, a softening at the edges of her name. *Jude.* She held onto it. *Quintana.* Harder to hold. The caravan roads unwound from her first, all those years of passes and tolls and smuggler's lanterns dissolving like sugar in rain, and she let them go, and grieved them, and let them go again.

Then the deeper layers. The shrine. The priestess's mad weeping. The lake freezing behind her as she ran with a stranger's childhood flooding her skull — a cottage, bread dough, a song. Those went next, and she felt herself thinning, felt the borders of *Jude Quintana, mercenary, guide, survivor* going translucent like paper held to flame.

Below, the battle erupted.

She heard it more than saw it — hooves on stone, the shriek of the Hollow King's riders charging the bowl, Sera's voice ringing out in a language Jude suddenly recognized as the old covenant-tongue, the words of warding that their mother must have taught them both before either could walk. Torchlight strobed across the underside of the cloud ceiling. Someone screamed. Steel met steel with a sound like the sky cracking further.

*Hold the circle,* Jude reminded herself, which was absurd, because holding the circle was Sera's task. Hers was to *be* the circle. To lie still while the world fought over her body's empty shell, and to pour herself out into the sockets, and to trust.

Her mother's voice cut through the din, closer now, dismounting, climbing the altar steps — *"Daughter, stop, STOP, you don't know what you're giving up—"*

"I know exactly," Jude whispered, and her lips were already going numb, and she was amazed she could still speak. "That's the difference between us. You fed on silence. I'm choosing it."

Hands seized her shoulders — her mother's hands, the dream-hands, shaking her — and Jude looked up into the face she had mourned without knowing she mourned it, and felt the last of her human grief rise like a tide, and offered it up too. *Take this as well,* she told the rite silently. *Take the wanting. Take the rage. Take the little girl in the cottage who loved her mother. Make it all part of the seal.*

The third stone was not brought to her. She realized, in the last lucid moment, that it never needed to be carried anywhere. It had been waiting in the hollow, in her, all along — the living anchor, the willing gift, the thirteenth moon's seed.

"Perrin," she breathed. "Now."

Small footsteps. Small warm hands. A knucklebone of stone pressed to her lips like communion.

"Oath," said Perrin, in a voice that did not shake at all, "kept."

Jude Quintana opened her mouth and gave back everything she had ever been.

* * *

What it felt like was roots.

Not pain — pain belonged to bodies, and hers was already becoming an argument between stone and tree. It felt like the moment a fallen branch, half-buried in wet earth, decides without deciding to put out white thread-roots into the dark. It felt like the Weeping Wood's long imprisonment ending and beginning in the same breath, the god's silence filling at last with a new voice, her voice, except it wasn't hers anymore, it was *theirs*, it was every prayer ever muttered on a smuggling road and every curse sworn at the border wards and every lullaby sung in two realms under a sky that had forgotten how to shine.

Memories left her in order of weight. The heaviest went last.

Sera's face. Sera's grey eyes. *Trust me one more time.* She held that one until the very end, cradling it like an ember, feeding it to the rite drop by drop so the seal would carry love in its mortar. And as it dissolved she felt it change — felt it stop being *her* memory of her sister and become something larger, something carved into the covenant itself, so that forever after, anyone who stood at this altar would feel, without knowing why, that they were loved by someone they couldn't remember.

That was enough. That was more than enough.

The thirteenth socket filled. The twelve old moons woke in their sockets like eyes opening. And far above the cloud ceiling, beyond the reach of any battle, the first star in a season pricked through the dark — then ten, then a hundred, then a spill of them, pouring back into the sky like light returning to a house.

Jude did not see it. Jude Quintana, at the last, saw nothing at all, and was glad.

But the thing she was becoming felt the stars land on her leaves like rain.

* * *

Below the altar, the Hollow King screamed — a raw, mortal sound, suddenly stripped of everything unnatural that had sustained her, because the silence she fed on had just ended forever. Sera Corvane turned from the circle she had sworn to hold, sap-blooded and starlit, and planted herself between her mother and the rooted, glowing, waking form of her sister.

The riders were still coming. The night was still full of blades.

But the stars were back, and the Wood was rising, and Sera raised her sword with tears freezing on her face and said, in the old tongue, in the plain tongue, in every tongue there was:

"You will not touch her again."
