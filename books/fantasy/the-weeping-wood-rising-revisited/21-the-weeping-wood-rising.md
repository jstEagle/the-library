## 21. The Weeping Wood Rising

The stars came back in the hour before dawn, and Sera Corvane was the only one awake to see them.

She sat at the edge of the summit altar with her sister's body cradled against her chest — what was left of it, which was not nothing and not quite something. The skin had gone pale as birchwood. The veins beneath had darkened to the green-black of heartwood. When she pressed her palm flat against Jude's sternum she could still feel a rhythm there, but it was not a heartbeat. It was slower. Vaster. Like something turning over in its sleep beneath the floor of the world.

Above her, one by one, the stars kindled — not all at once, but in the order they had died, Sera would swear to that afterward, though no astronomer could confirm it. First the Wainwright. Then the Sisters Twinned, close together in the northern dark, so near they seemed to lean on one another. Last of all, low on the horizon where Thornfell's mountains bit the sky, a star she had never seen before: thirteenth, unnamed, hanging exactly over the place where the Weeping Wood had stood.

The moon completed its turn. Thirteen times thirteen shadows crossed its face, and then it simply hung there, full and white and ordinary, as if it had never once considered leaving.

Sera did not weep. She had spent her tears somewhere between the bell-prison and the burnt chapel, and what remained in her now was drier and harder and more useful. She arranged her sister's body on the altar with the care of someone dressing an icon, folded the ruined hands, smoothed the hair back from the brow. The wind moved through the pass and carried the smell of ash and cold iron and, faintly, impossibly, sap.

"Hold on," she told the body, because she did not yet know what else to say. "I'll get you down the mountain. I'll plant you somewhere with good light."

The body said nothing. But beneath her palm, that vast slow rhythm turned again, and for a moment Sera could have sworn she felt it *answer*.

* * *

They came up through the clouds at midmorning: Perrin first, limping, his coat scorched and one arm bound in a sling he refused to stop using; behind him a ragged column of guild caravaneers, warden remnants, and even two grey-cloaked Vaelmoor riders who had thrown down their swords somewhere below the snowline and climbed the rest of the way like pilgrims. None of them spoke when they saw the altar. None of them needed to.

The priestess had died in the night — gently, at last unburdened, her mad grief finally given somewhere to go. They buried her at the tree line in soil that was already softening, already waking, and Sera marked the grave with a stone from the shattered covenant-site, because the dead deserved anchors too.

It was Perrin who noticed it first.

"Sera." His voice cracked on her name. He was pointing past the burial cairn, down the southern slope, toward the valley where the Weeping Wood had stood silent and bleeding for a generation. "Look."

Sera looked.

The Wood was rising.

Not growing — she would insist on that word for the rest of her life. Not spreading, not creeping, none of the slow patient verbs of trees. It was *rising*, the way a tide rises, the way a song rises in a throat. New growth moved across the valley floor like a green shadow given weight, birch and rowan and blackthorn unfurling from ground that had been ash since the wards fell, and at the center of it all, where the old shrine had stood, a single tree lifted itself out of the earth taller than any tower in Vaelmoor, its bark pale as the body on the altar, its branches silvered with new leaves that trembled though there was no wind.

And from those leaves, from every leaf, water fell. Clear water. Sweet water. It fell in threads and beads and long bright curtains, and where it struck the ground the ash turned black and rich and living, and small white flowers opened in the space of a breath.

"Weeping," Perrin whispered. He had walked half the length of the salt road beside this god's silence, and he understood before anyone older did. "But it isn't grieving anymore."

Sera descended the mountain without waiting for the others. She walked into the rising Wood alone, through corridors of young birch that parted before her like a crowd making way, until she stood beneath the great tree and looked up into ten thousand leaves each one holding a bead of falling light.

"Jude," she said.

The water fell. The leaves shivered. And then — softly, from everywhere and nowhere, from the roots and the canopy and the space between — a voice that was not a voice at all, more felt than heard, the way you feel rain approaching by the smell of it:

*Sera.*

Her knees nearly went. She locked them, the way the Grey Order had trained her, duty as posture, discipline as spine — and then she abandoned all of it and pressed both hands against the bark, and the bark was warm, and under it beat that vast slow rhythm, and she knew it the way you know your own name.

"You're supposed to be dead," she managed. "Or asleep. Or — I don't know what the rite was supposed to do. Nobody wrote it down."

*The rite made me an Anchor,* said the Wood, and the voice was Jude's — roughened, amused, unmistakably hers underneath the vastness, the mercenary who had once guided smugglers through these passes for coin and kept a knife in her boot and a boy's secret warm against her heart. *Anchors hold things. I'm holding everything. It's a lot of everything, Sera. You have no idea how much sky weighs.*

"Jude—"

*I'm here.* A pause, and the vastness drew back a little, gentling itself the way a large animal lies down carefully among small ones. *I'm here. That's what I wanted to say first. Before anything else. I'm here, and I remember you, and I'm not going anywhere.*

Sera slid down the trunk until she was sitting in the wet grass, laughing and crying at once, water running off her chin that was only partly the tree's doing. Above her the leaves stirred, and one bead of clear sap-water fell precisely onto the crown of her head like a benediction, or like a sister flicking rainwater at her, and she heard — distinctly, dryly, in Jude's cadence —

*You look terrible, by the way. Grey really isn't your color.*

* * *

They rebuilt the shrine that summer, or rather the Wood let them rebuild it, directing the work through dreams and omens and once, memorably, by dropping a branch directly onto the shrine-master's proposed blueprints. The old blood-weeping stones were reset as the threshold. The mad priestess's cell became a hospice. Pilgrims began arriving before the roof was finished — smugglers first, because the roads were open again and the tolls were fair, then farmers, then wardens without badges, then, cautiously, grey-cloaked envoys from Vaelmoor who came under a flag of truce and left, every one of them, looking as though they had been quietly rearranged.

The covenant held. Both realms breathed. Under the thirteenth moon, treaties were signed that no one fully trusted and everyone honored, because the alternative was written plainly in the stars that had come back.

Sera took up residence above the shrine in a small stone house with a view of the border passes. She wore grey still — she could not help it; it was woven too deep — but she had cut away the order's sigil and stitched in its place a single green leaf, and when riders came out of Vaelmoor asking questions about the Anchor, the Wood, the woman who guarded the tree, it was Sera who met them at the tree line with her hand resting easy on her sword and her eyes saying everything her mouth didn't need to.

In the evenings she climbed to the great tree and sat against its roots and talked, because the Wood liked to be talked to — or Jude did; the distinction had stopped mattering — about the caravans and the weather and the letters that arrived from a licensed guide named Perrin, who had taken over the old smuggling routes and run them clean. She read the letters aloud. She complained about the shrine-master's accounting. She argued theology with a tree and lost, regularly, cheerfully.

And in the autumn, on the anniversary of the night the stars went out, she took a cutting.

The Wood offered it — a branch lowered itself, quite deliberately, while she stood beneath it with the pruning knife she'd brought and hadn't dared use. The cut bled clear water instead of sap, and Sera caught it in cupped hands and drank, and it tasted like a river crossing in high summer, like laughter she couldn't place and then could, like a cottage with smoke rising from its chimney and two girls racing through tall grass toward a wood that watched them with love.

"I'm going to plant you on the ridge," she told the cutting. "Where the whole pass can see you. Where the border runs. Someone has to stand watch, and apparently I'm the only one left standing."

The leaves shivered. The voice came gentle, and vast, and hers:

*Then stand well, little sister. And Sera — don't forget to eat. You've gotten thin.*

* * *

She planted the cutting at first light on the ridge above the rebuilt shrine, in soil she had carried up herself, bucket by bucket, because some things should not be done quickly or by proxy. She packed the earth around its roots with her bare hands. She watered it from the spring that ran clear down from the summit. Then she sat beside it in the wet grass and waited, the way you wait with anything newly alive, attentive and foolish and hopeful.

It grew. Not fast — the Wood did nothing vulgarly fast — but visibly, if you knew how to look. By midwinter it stood hip-high. By spring it reached her shoulder, slender and pale-barked, its leaves silvering, and when the mountain wind moved through it, it made a sound like breathing, or like someone humming an old road-song half-remembered.

Travelers began leaving offerings at its base within the first season. Not coins — the Wood had no use for coins, and dropped them pointedly back into people's pockets, which caused no end of theological confusion — but other things. A carved wooden bird from a caravan master whose route had once been lost. A ribbon from a girl who'd survived the pyre-season in the lowlands. Letters, sealed and unsealed, left in the crook of the little tree's lowest branch: confessions, apologies, memories written down and surrendered to the keeping of a god who had once been a woman who had once given up her own memory to keep two worlds from tearing.

Sera read some of them. She burned most, unread, at their owners' request, feeding them to the tree so the words went wherever such words needed to go. On quiet nights she stood on the ridge with the wind in her cropped hair and looked north to Thornfell and south to Vaelmoor and saw, for the first time in her life, not a border but a seam — the place where two pieces had been sewn together, and held.

Some nights, when the moon was right, she climbed the little tree's hill and pressed her hand to its bark, and it was warm, and under it beat a rhythm she knew, and she stayed until the stars wheeled overhead — thirteen constellations now, the astronomers insisted, though the thirteenth appeared only to those the Wood chose to show it to — and she said goodnight to her sister in the language they had never needed words for.

Once, near midwinter, the voice came to her clearly, unbidden, while she stood watch with snow gathering on her shoulders:

*Sera. There's a boy on the lower road. Walking up through the snow. He's carrying something warm against his heart, and he doesn't know why he's smiling.*

Sera turned toward the pass. Far below, a small figure crested the frozen switchbacks, coat flapping, one arm swinging free of its old sling-habit, climbing toward the ridge and the shrine and the tree with the particular determination of someone delivering a letter he had promised to deliver.

She smiled, wiped the snow from her shoulders, and went down to meet him.
