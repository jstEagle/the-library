## 22. A Road Worth Keeping

The pass had a smell again.

Perrin noticed it the way he noticed most things now — slowly, and with his whole body, because that was what a year of guiding taught you. A year ago the high road had smelled of ash and cold iron, of wards burned down to their roots and something underneath them gone sour. Now it smelled of pine resin warming in the morning sun, of wet stone, of the faint green sweetness that came off the Wood when the wind moved through it just right. Like rain about to happen somewhere else. Like a promise being kept quietly, without anyone making a speech about it.

He stopped at the first cairn out of Thornfell and did what every licensed guide on the border roads did now: he touched two fingers to the stone and said his name.

"Perrin Ashfoot. Guide, third mark. Four travelers behind me, all bound for Vaelmoor side, papers in order."

The cairn didn't answer. It never did. But the little sprig of Wood that grew from its crack — every cairn on the reopened passes had one now; nobody had planted them, they'd simply *arrived* — shivered in the windless air, and Perrin felt the small familiar pressure against his thoughts like a hand resting briefly on his shoulder. *Noted*, the pressure said. *Welcome back.*

Behind him, his four travelers shifted their packs and muttered. Two wool merchants from the lowlands who still crossed themselves when the leaves spoke. A young scholar from Vaelmoor with ink-stained fingers and a satchel full of instruments for measuring things that did not want to be measured. And an old woman going home to a village that might not remember her, carrying nothing but a jar.

"Is it true," the scholar asked, catching up to him as he set the pace, "that she hears everything? Every word said on the road?"

"She hears everything given to her," Perrin said. He'd answered this question maybe four hundred times and he still liked answering it. "That's different. You can talk all day about the weather or your bad knees and she won't keep a word of it. But if you *give* her a memory — truly give it, at one of the shrines — then she keeps it. Forever. That's the covenant now. That's what we walk on."

"And what do the memories buy us?" the old woman asked. Not hostile. Just tired, the way people were tired in the first years after the stars came back, when everyone was still learning which miracles were load-bearing.

"Safe passage," Perrin said. "Open borders. The thirteenth moon." He pointed up, because even though it was day and the thirteenth moon was a pale ghost only visible at dusk, pointing was habit and comfort both. "And roads that don't lie to you anymore."

They walked. The road wound up through the rebuilt toll village — new roofs, new warden-house with its door left pointedly unlocked, the old pyre-stump in the square hollowed out and filled with flowers that someone tended every single week and no one would admit to — and past it, into the high country where the trees began to change.

Everyone knew the moment it happened, because the light changed with them. Ordinary pine gave way to silver-barked trees that no forester could name, trees that grew in spirals and wept — not sap, not blood, but clear water, cool and sweet, that gathered in the stone basins travelers had set beneath them over the past year. The Weeping Wood Rising. It had started as a single tree above the old shrine and spread along the passes like a slow green tide, and everywhere it went, the water followed.

Perrin's travelers fell silent, the way everyone did. Even the merchants. Especially the merchants.

* * *

The shrine at Verren Pass sat on a shelf of rock overlooking the lake, and it had been rebuilt three times in a year — once by the guilds, once by the wardens trying to make amends, and once by nobody at all, because after the third rebuilding the shrine had simply begun maintaining itself, the stones fitting tighter each dawn, the roof thatching itself with silver needles. The priestess who had gone mad with grief had died in the spring, peaceful, her hands full of leaves, and the shrine kept her name carved into the lintel in letters that deepened a little every month, as though the Wood were still learning to say it.

Perrin made his travelers wait at the boundary while he went in alone. That was custom too. The guide entered first, greeted the tree, reported the state of the road.

She was bigger this year. That was the thing that got him every time, the thing he'd never quite stopped flinching at and never quite stopped loving. Last autumn she had been a young tree with a human shape still ghosting through her — you could squint and see shoulders, see the line of a jaw, see hair like dark bark falling around a face made of grain and shadow. Now she was thirty feet of spiraled silver and green, roots running down into the shrine floor and out through the mountainside, branches heavy with leaves that caught the light and held it, so that even on grey days the shrine glowed faintly from within, like a lantern behind skin.

But the face was still there, if you knew how to look. And the tears were still there too — except they ran clear now, down channels worn smooth in the bark, into the great basin at her roots, where the water lay perfectly still and reflected a sky that had thirteen moons in it whether the hour allowed or not.

"Morning, Jude," Perrin said.

The leaves stirred. Not wind. Stirring. And the voice came the way it always came — not in his ears but in the space just behind them, in the place where you heard your own thoughts, except these weren't his.

*Good morning, little torch.*

"That's not my name anymore either."

*It is the one I gave you.* A ripple of something like laughter moved through the branches, shaking loose a scatter of silver needles that drifted down into the basin and vanished without ripples. *You were carrying embers in your coat the night we met. You have been carrying embers ever since. Some names are earned by what a person does once, and stay true forever.*

Perrin looked at the water in the basin. Sometimes, if you watched long enough, images rose in it — not prophecies, she was very firm about that, *I am not an oracle, stop asking* — but memories. Offered ones. Given ones. Travelers' last days with their mothers. First snowfalls. The taste of bread in a house that no longer stood. They drifted up through the clear water like fish made of light, and the tree drank them slowly, and kept them.

"You've been busy," he said. "Basin's nearly full."

*I have had a generous year.* The leaves turned toward the boundary, toward his four waiting travelers. *The wool merchant will give me a memory of his brother, whom he has not spoken to in nine years and misses more than he admits. The scholar will give me nothing — she will try to measure my water instead, and I will let her, because curiosity is also a kind of prayer. And the old woman—*

The leaves paused. When the voice came again it was softer, and Perrin felt it settle into his chest like a coal.

*The old woman is going to ask me if her village remembers her. Her village gave everything they remembered about her to me, last winter, every soul of them, so that she would exist somewhere even if she never came home. She is going to kneel at this basin and look into it and see herself as her neighbors kept her. Would you like to know what they kept?*

"No," Perrin said, and his voice cracked on it. "No. That's hers. Tell me when she's done."

*That,* said the Weeping Wood, *is exactly why I chose you to carry messages.*

* * *

He waited outside with the travelers while the old woman went in. She was gone a long time. The merchant sat on a rock and rehearsed under his breath, the way people did, practicing the shape of the memory they meant to give, getting the weight of it right in their mouths before they laid it down. The scholar took measurements of the boundary stones and wrote in her journal in a shorthand Perrin suspected was half cipher and half love letter to whatever theory she was building.

Sera Corvane came up the path from the lake side while they waited.

Perrin saw her before the travelers did and felt the old reflexive tension — the grey coat, the inquisitor's stride — before his mind caught up with his body and reminded him that the grey coat was gone, that Sera wore warden-green now, border-green, guardian-green, that she had spent the winter personally dragging the Vaelmoor orders out of their vaults and burning the parts that ordered people killed for what they remembered. She carried a staff cut from a branch the Wood had shed for her, and she walked the passes like a woman patrolling her own heartbeat.

"Third mark already," she said, falling into step beside him. "They're giving those out fast."

"They're giving them out to people who earn them." He bumped her shoulder with his. "How's the Vaelmoor side?"

"Open. Terrified. Open anyway." She looked up at the tree, and her expression did the thing it always did — the softening, the grief and gratitude braided so tight you couldn't pull them apart. "There's a caravan coming through next week. Two hundred souls. Families that fled the border wars, going home to villages that haven't seen them in a generation. The Hollow King's riders burned some of those villages so thoroughly there's nothing left to go back to. So the Wood is—" Sera gestured vaguely at the shrine. "She's growing houses."

"She's what?"

"Out of herself. Cuttings. Rooted where the villages used to be, grown into walls and roofs by morning. People live inside their god now. Apparently it's very warm and smells like cedar and she hums to the children until they stop crying." Sera's mouth twisted. "The Vaelmoor theologians are having a catastrophic year."

Inside the shrine, the old woman began to weep. Everyone outside pretended not to hear, and everyone heard.

When she came out, her face was wrecked and lit from within, the way faces got. She pressed a copper coin into Perrin's hand — far too much, the standard overpayment of the transformed — and said, "They kept my name. All of them. Even the children. Even the dog, they remembered what the dog thought of me." Then she walked down toward the lake, upright, carrying her jar again, though the jar was empty now and everyone understood it had always been empty, that some vessels are for holding a journey rather than anything you can pour.

The merchant went in next. Then the scholar, who did indeed try to measure the water and emerged an hour later looking like a woman who had met the ocean and lost an argument gracefully, clutching her instruments and grinning.

* * *

Evening found Perrin and Sera on the shrine steps with tea, watching the thirteenth moon rise pale and patient over Lake Verren. Below them the drowned salt road gleamed under the water, visible now that the lake ran clear — the drowned wardens long gone to whatever rest dead law earns, the first stone's resting place a soft golden glow at the lake's heart, singing so quietly only the quietest hours could hear it.

"Do you ever miss it?" Perrin asked. "Being feared?"

Sera considered. "I miss knowing who I was supposed to be. The orders told me every morning. Wake up, doubt nothing, burn what remembers wrong." She drank her tea. "Now I wake up and decide. Every morning, decide again. It's exhausting. It's the best thing that ever happened to me."

The leaves overhead rustled, and both of them felt the voice arrive, gentle, addressing them together the way it did on the rare evenings it chose to simply sit with its friends.

*Sera. There is a cutting ready. South slope, above the second village. It has your mother's laugh in it — the merchant gave that to me this afternoon, he did not know why he remembered it, but he did. Would you carry it home?*

Sera closed her eyes. "Yes," she said. "Tomorrow."

*And Perrin.* The branches dipped, and one silver leaf detached itself and drifted down, landing in his open palm, warm as a held hand. *Next season there is a road none of my guides have walked. Far north, past the old starless pass. Something is stirring there that even I cannot see — roots of mine have gone quiet in that country, and quiet is not the same as safe. I will need my best guide. One who carries embers.*

Perrin turned the leaf over. In its veins, faintly, he could see the map of every road he'd ever walked — and beyond the starless pass, a blank space shaped like a question.

"I'll need a fourth mark for that," he said.

*You will need more than a mark,* said the Weeping Wood, and the laughter in her branches shook loose silver across the whole shrine, bright as returning stars. *You will need a very good coat with very deep pockets. We will speak of it in the morning.*

Below them, the travelers' fires dotted the lakeshore. Above, all thirteen moons climbed. And the road wound north out of sight, waiting to be worth keeping.

* * *

In the morning, the old woman was gone down the south road, the merchant had sent his brother a letter he couldn't explain writing, and the scholar had stayed behind to argue theology with a tree that won every round by refusing to have any. Perrin shouldered his pack, touched two fingers to the shrine's lintel, and started walking.

The road smelled like rain about to happen somewhere else.

Somewhere north, a blank space waited to be filled with footsteps.

He whistled on his way up the pass, and the trees leaned in to listen, and kept it.
