## 1. The Gate That Stays Shut

The gate at the mouth of the Weeping Wood has no lock, because a lock implies something worth stealing, and the valley's only treasure is the absence of magic. I tend it anyway. Every morning I walk its length, touch each of the nine wardstones in their order — birch first, then ash, then the black one nobody likes to talk about — and murmur the maintenance words my mother taught me before she taught me anything else. Not *I love you*. Not *be careful*. The maintenance words. Priorities.

The wards don't hum. They don't glow. If you pressed your ear to a wardstone hoping for mystery, you'd hear nothing but your own pulse and the distant weeping of the trees that gave this place its name — sap running down bark like slow tears, all night, every night. The wood grieves constantly and never says why. I've stopped asking. In thirty years of guardianship, the valley and I have reached an understanding: it keeps its secrets, I keep mine, and neither of us mentions the tremor in my shadow.

Ah. Yes. Let's address that now, while we're being honest with the furniture.

Every person casts a shadow, and every shadow is supposed to be still. Mine shivers. Just at the left edge, just where the shoulder would be if shadows had shoulders — a fine constant trembling, like a moth trapped under cloth. Only visible in certain lights. Only noticeable to certain eyes. My mother noticed it the day I was born, which is how she knew the bargain had taken root before I'd drawn my first breath: her son's soul, sold to the court beneath the mountain, payment deferred until such time as the collectors came calling. She spent the rest of her life buying silence, hiding me inside the one valley on earth where magic cannot follow, where the court's long fingers stop dead at the wardstones like a dog at an invisible fence.

She died four winters ago. Her silences died with her. I've been maintaining them alone ever since, the way I maintain everything else — carefully, daily, without hope.

* * *

It was raining the morning the impossible happened, which felt appropriate. Impossible things should at least have the decency to arrive in weather.

I was crouched at the seventh stone, scraping moss from its face, when I heard the sound. Not a footstep. A drag. Something pulling itself through the ferns along the boundary line, wet and labored, like a man swimming through mud toward shore.

Now, understand: nothing crosses into the valley from outside unless it walks in through the gate on its own two unenchanted feet. Traders do it. Pilgrims, occasionally. Once a very confused tax assessor. But nothing *carried* by magic can cross, because there is no magic here to carry it, and nothing pursued by magic can cross either, because pursuit requires the same. The boundary doesn't keep things out so much as it makes the outside forget we exist. It is not a wall. It is a shrug.

So when a stranger crawled out of the ferns and over the wardline — *through* it, the way water goes through a net, leaving the wards flickering behind him like a struck bell — I did what any seasoned guardian would do.

I fell backward into the mud.

He was young. Younger than me, maybe twenty-five, dressed in courier gray gone dark with rain and worse with blood. There was a wound low in his side that I didn't need to be a healer to read; it had a finality about it, a punctuation. He dragged himself another arm's length, saw me sitting in the muck staring at him, and smiled with cracked lips.

"You're him," he said. "Sable Sandoval."

"Nobody's him," I said. "Nobody lives here."

"The map—" He was fumbling at his coat, fingers slick, and I watched three separate thoughts fight for control of his face: duty, terror, and the particular peace of a man whose body has already voted to stop. "Map for Sable. Son of Marisol. Debt-holder. Dark moon."

My mother's name in a dying stranger's mouth does things to a man's spine.

"How do you know that name?"

"Everyone below knows it." Below. He said the word the way sailors say *storm*. "You're famous down there, keeper's son. The soul that got away." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough came up red. "Got away. That's good. Nothing gets away. It's just... postponed."

I should have run. I want that on the record. Some wiser version of me stands up, backs away, lets the wards do what wards do, and spends the rest of his life tending moss. Instead I crawled forward and caught him as he slumped, and his blood soaked through my sleeve warm as bathwater, and he pressed something into my hands with the desperate strength of the nearly-dead.

A map. Oilskin-wrapped, small as a prayer book. My hands knew it was important before my head did; they went cold around it.

"Dark moon," he said again. His eyes had fixed on something past my shoulder, or through me, or nowhere at all. "Eleven days. The Ledger-Keeper sent collectors to the valley edge last spring. Your mother's silence-bargains expired with her heartbeat, keeper's son. Every year she bought you another year. She bought none ahead. Do you understand? There's nothing standing between you and the collection except—"

"Except the valley," I said. "Magic can't enter. You just proved they can't reach me here."

His smile was terrible with pity. "The valley keeps magic out. It doesn't keep *claims* out. A debt isn't magic, boy. A debt is older than magic. On the dark moon, the claim comes due, and the valley can't tell a collector from a creditor." He gripped my wrist, and his strength was astonishing, the last flare of a lamp before the oil runs dry. "Run. Or go down and settle it yourself. Those are the only doors. Running works for a while — I've carried maps to runners before. But the court is patient. The court has *time*."

"What's beneath the mountain," I said, "that it wants souls?"

"Everything wants what it's owed." His voice was going quiet, going elsewhere. "Take the pass east. Ask for Wren at the memory-house in Corvane. Tell her—" and here he stopped, and something moved across his face like weather, grief or recognition or both, "tell her the courier remembers. Even if she doesn't. Tell her the courier *remembers*."

"Wren who? Remembers what?"

But his hand had gone slack on my wrist, and his eyes had gone from elsewhere to nowhere, and the rain came down on us both with the indifference rain reserves for the endings of stories.

* * *

I buried him at dusk above the treeline, where the valley soil takes its dead without complaint, and I said the maintenance words over the grave because they were the only prayers I owned.

Then I sat on my doorstep with the oilskin map in my lap and did not open it for a long time. Opening it made it real. Unopened, it was just a dead man's luggage, a curiosity, a thing that could still be rationalized. I am very good at rationalizing. It's a survival skill, like the wards.

Around me the valley went about its evening. Smoke rose from chimneys. Someone's goat complained about something. The trees wept their endless sap-tears down the slopes, grieving whatever it is they grieve, and I realized — with the slow cold horror of a man reading his own sentence — that I had always assumed I would die here. That the valley wasn't a hiding place. It was the whole of my life, walls and floor and ceiling, and I had loved it the way prisoners love their cells: sincerely, gratefully, without ever once testing the door.

Eleven days.

Inside the oilskin, I could feel the map's edges, crisp and patient. Somewhere beneath a mountain, a ledger held a page with my name on it, and the ink was drying toward some appointment, and my mother — who had spent her whole life buying me mornings — had run out of coin the day she ran out of breath.

I unwrapped the map.

And there in the corner, in ink that shimmered faintly even in the lightless valley — ink that had no right to work here, ink that made my shadow tremble harder against my will, like a dog hearing its master's whistle — someone had written a single line:

*They know where you sleep.*
