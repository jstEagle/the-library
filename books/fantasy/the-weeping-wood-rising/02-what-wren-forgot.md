## 2. What Wren Forgot

*Sable*

I had lived twenty-six years inside a valley that magic could not enter, and in all that time I had learned exactly one useful skill: how to lie to my own shadow.

Every morning at the gate I checked the wards—salt lines gone gray with rain, iron nails driven into the posts by hands two hundred years dead, the little carved owls whose eyes were supposed to blink if anything crossed the boundary that shouldn't. The wards never stirred for me. That was the joke of it. Magic couldn't reach into the Weeping Wood, so the debt written into my blood just sat there, patient as a stone, marked only by the faint tremor at the edge of my shadow on bright days. A shiver. A stutter. Like a candle flame remembering wind.

I'd spent my whole life pretending not to see it.

The courier saw it immediately.

He came crawling through the boundary at dusk—which was impossible, because nothing carrying magic could cross, and he was so saturated with the court beneath the mountain that he practically glowed, a sick green-gold shimmer under his skin like rot in cheese. The wards didn't stop him because he wasn't crossing *into* anywhere anymore. He was already dying. Magic was simply leaking out of him faster than he was.

"Map," he rasped, and pressed something into my hands—oilcloth, cracked, heavy with something folded inside. "Sandoval's son. Dark moon. They come for you at dark moon."

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said, which was a lie, which meant my shadow trembled, which meant he smiled with bloody teeth.

"Nobody ever does," he said. "That's how they like us."

Then he said three more things, in this order: *the ledger keeps what the ledger holds*; *don't let them weigh you*; and finally, looking past me at the valley I'd never left, "You have no idea what she paid." I assumed he meant my mother. I've had a lot of time since to wonder if I assumed wrong.

He died before full dark. I buried him shallow because the ground was hard and I am honest enough to admit I was also in a hurry.

Here is what I knew by midnight: my mother sold my soul before I drew breath. The bill was coming due. And the map in my hands showed a road out of the only home I'd ever known, drawn in ink that moved when I wasn't watching it directly.

Here is what I did by dawn: packed bread, a knife, the good coat, and left a note on the gatepost that said *gone to settle an account*. Understated. My whole life, understated.

Then I walked out of the Weeping Wood for the first time, and the world outside smelled like—nothing. That's the part nobody warns you about. Inside the valley everything hums, even the silence. Outside, the air was flat as dead water, and my shadow lay still beside me for the first time in my life, and I understood with terrible clarity that the stillness meant it belonged to someone else now. It always had. Outside the valley, the truth of me could stretch its legs.

Three days east, at a crossroads inn called the Drowned Hen—a name that turned out to be accurate to both words—I met her.

She was sitting alone with two cups of tea, and when I came through the door she looked up, and her face did something complicated. Grief, I'd have said, except grief usually knows its own name.

"You're staring," I told her.

"No, I'm not," she said, and pushed the second cup toward the empty chair across from her like she'd been waiting for someone specific and hadn't yet admitted who. "Sit down. You're being followed. Two collectors took the north road an hour ago and they smell like wet pennies and old promises."

"And you know this because?"

"Because it's my job to know things people have forgotten." She turned her cup. Her hands were stained faintly silver at the fingertips—the mark of a memory-keeper, though I didn't know it then. "Drink your tea. Then we're leaving through the kitchen, because the front door has eyes."

I should mention that I went. No argument, no questions. A stranger tells me to flee through a kitchen and I flee through a kitchen, and later, much later, Wren would tell me that this was characteristic—that I'd always been the sort of man who followed her lead without asking why, and she'd hated it and loved it in equal measure. But that night I didn't know her. That night she was just a woman with silver-stained fingers who looked at me like I was a page torn out of a book she used to own.

* * *

*Wren*

Here is the trouble with keeping memories for a living: you learn exactly what you gave away, even if you can't remember giving it.

There's a hollow where he used to be. I have carried it for six years like a stone in the boot. I know its shape intimately. I know that once there was a person who filled it, because the hollow has edges, and edges mean something was cut out—and I know, because I keep other people's memories and I know how trades are made, that I paid something enormous for something small, and that the small thing was worth more.

So when Sable Sandoval walked into the Drowned Hen with his dead man's coat and his trembling shadow, my chest did a thing I have no word for. Not recognition. Recognition requires content. This was the shape of recognition without any of the picture—like hearing a song and knowing you once danced to it but not with whom, or why you stopped.

I helped him anyway. Obviously. You don't argue with the stone in your boot.

We went out through the kitchen past a scullery boy who owed my guild eleven favors, and we walked east through the night because collectors hunt by proximity and distance was the only ward either of us owned.

"The map," he said around dawn, when we'd stopped in a birch stand and he was chewing bread like it owed him money. "It moves. Is that normal?"

"For court-work? Yes. The court beneath the mountain doesn't write things down, it *keeps* them. Everything in those halls is alive in the way debts are alive." I crouched to check the road behind us. Nothing. Yet. "Where does it lead?"

"Beneath the mountain, presumably. There's a hall drawn here with—" he turned it over twice, frowning, "—a lot of little marks. Names, I think."

My hands went cold, and I want it noted that they went cold *before* I knew why.

"Don't read the names," I said.

"Why not?"

"Because some of them might be yours, and some of them might be mine, and neither of us wants to know which before we have to." I stood, brushed off my knees, and discovered him watching me with that particular attention again—the look of a man trying to place a face from a dream.

"Do I know you?" he asked. Directly, the way he asked everything. "Before tonight. Have we met?"

"No," I said, and the word came out steady, because I'm a professional, and professionals can say *no* with a straight face while the hollow in them screams. "You'd remember. Keeping memories is my trade. Forgetting is yours, apparently, if you're sold."

"That's not funny."

"No," I agreed. "But it's true, and true things get to be unfunny."

* * *

*Sable*

We crossed four days of countryside that got steadily worse the farther we got from any wood, any ward, anything holy. The collectors found our trail on the second day—not us, just the trail. We'd find their signs behind us: circles of scorched grass where they'd paused to sniff, and once, hung on a fencepost, a single copper coin. Wren went very quiet at that one and wouldn't explain until dark.

"A probe," she said finally, feeding the fire with one hand and not looking at me. "They leave payment where a debtor might find it. If you touch it, it binds tighter. If you refuse it properly—out loud, with intent—it tells them where your intent lives."

"So I shouldn't have picked it up."

The fire crackled. Somewhere behind us, something sniffed.

"*Sable.*"

"It was a coin! Who leaves a coin on a fencepost? That's basically litter!"

She made a sound I would come to know well over the following days—half laugh, half despair, aimed at some private joke between her and a version of me that no longer existed. "Give it here."

I handed it over and watched her work. She held the coin to the firelight and murmured over it in a language that made the flames lean away, and slowly, like frost retreating, the coin forgot it had ever been a hook. When it was done she flipped it into the dark and it landed somewhere with a sound like a very small bell.

"What was that?" I asked.

"Me, spending something I can't afford." She wrapped her cloak around herself and lay down facing away from me. "Sleep. We reach the mountain's skirt tomorrow, and after that the air gets thick with listening."

I lay awake a while, watching my shadow on the ground—still, obedient, foreign. And I thought about the courier's last words. *You have no idea what she paid.*

"Wren," I said quietly, testing whether she slept. She didn't answer. But I saw her shoulders go stiff, the way a person's do when a question lands too close to the hollow in them. "Whoever I was before the valley—whoever my mother traded—did anyone wait for me? Did anyone pay for me?"

A long silence. Long enough that I almost believed she'd slept after all.

"Everyone pays for everyone," she said at last, muffled by her cloak. "That's how the court designed the world. Sleep, Sable."

Hearing my name in her mouth did something strange to the air between us—strange for both of us, I'd swear it. Like the name fit her voice better than it had any right to.

* * *

*Wren*

On the fifth day the mountain came up over the horizon like a judgment, black-boned against a white sky, and the map in Sable's hands began to pull—gently, patiently, the way a ledger pulls at every name inside it.

He stood looking up at it, jaw set, and I stood looking at him, aching in a place I couldn't point to on any map of myself, and I thought: *whatever happens in there, I will not leave without learning why.*

And then the road ahead filled with collectors—six of them, seven, unfolding out of the thin mountain air like bad news—and the nearest one opened its mouth full of pennies and said, in a voice like a receipt:

"Sandoval's son. The Ledger-Keeper will see you now."

Behind it, further up the slope, I saw figures dragging something between them—a woman-shaped bundle, silver-fingered, struggling.

They'd taken Wren hours ago, back at the stream, while I scouted ahead.

And the collectors had let me follow. All this way. Straight to the door.
