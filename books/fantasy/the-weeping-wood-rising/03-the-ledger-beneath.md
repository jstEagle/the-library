## 3. The Ledger Beneath

The mountain ate the road a mile before we reached it, and I mean that with as little poetry as I can manage. One moment there was a track winding up through grey scree, and the next the stone simply opened its mouth and swallowed us, and Wren and I were walking down a throat of polished dark that breathed warm air against our faces like something deciding whether we were worth chewing.

"Cheerful place," I said, because if I stopped talking my hands would start shaking and I didn't want to watch that happen.

"You've said that four times," Wren said.

"I've meant it four times."

She walked half a step ahead of me, which was her way all through that journey—always between me and whatever came next, though she'd have cut out her tongue before admitting it. Her lantern threw our shadows long against the walls, and I kept catching mine at the edge of the light: a tremor in it, a flicker, like a candle flame someone was trying to snuff. Sold goods, marked for collection. Even my shadow couldn't be trusted to hold still.

The bone halls began where the breathing tunnel ended, and I will not pretend I took them well.

They were not bones scattered about, which would have been merely horrible. They were bones *arranged*. Rib cages arched into doorways. Femurs laid in courses like brickwork, mortared with something pale and glistening. Skulls set into the ceiling in patterns that might have been constellations, if constellations had teeth. And everywhere, threaded through the architecture like veins of ore, ran lines of faint gold script—the ledger's handwriting, written so large it became the world.

"It's all one page," Wren whispered. "The whole mountain. It's one open account."

"How do you know that?"

"Because keeping accounts is what memory-keepers do," she said, "and this is the worst bookkeeping I have ever seen." But her voice cracked on *bookkeeping*, and I understood she wasn't joking at all. She was grieving for the arithmetic.

We followed the gold lines downward for what felt like a day and might have been an hour. Time down there was another entry in someone else's column. The script grew thicker as we descended, older, the letters shifting from the court's current sharp hand into something looped and heavy, and I realized with a lurch that I was reading my mother's name over and over, carved deeper each century, interest compounding in gold.

*Sandovál. Sandovál. Sandovál.*

"She didn't know what she was selling," I said. I don't know who I was telling. The mountain, maybe. "She thought it was a blessing. The peddler told her it was a blessing."

"The court is very good at blessings," Wren said quietly.

Then the corridor ahead went dark—not shadowed but *absent*, as if light had never been invented for that stretch of stone—and when we stepped into it anyway, because the alternative was standing forever in a hallway made of dead people's patience, the Ledger-Keeper was simply there.

I want to describe it to you. I have tried, since, many times, and every attempt comes out wrong, so let me try the truest wrong version: it looked like an accountant. Grey robe, ink-stained fingers, spectacles balanced on a face that had been worn smooth by centuries of not blinking. It held a pen the length of my forearm. Behind it, rising into the dark, stood the ledger itself—a book taller than the mountain above us, its pages turning slowly of their own accord, and every page I glimpsed had a name on it, and every name had a weight beside it in that same gold script.

"Sable Sandoval," it said. Its voice was dry paper. "You came early. Most debtors wait to be fetched."

"I'm told the fetching's unpleasant."

"It is thorough," it agreed. "That is not the same thing." It turned a page without touching it. "Your mother, Lira Sandoval, came to the court's outer market heavy with child and heavier with fear. She purchased a warding against fever. She paid in the only coin the court accepts from the poor." The smooth face inclined toward me, almost gently. "You were the coin."

"I know."

"Do you?" The pen tapped once against the ledger's spine, and the whole hall rang like a struck bell, and every skull overhead rattled in its socket. "You know the shape of it. Do you know the terms? Your soul, held in trust until the dark moon following your twenty-eighth year. At which time you present yourself, and the account is settled, and you walk back out into your valley lighter by exactly one soul." A pause. "Or you are collected. Collected souls arrive in pieces. It saves space."

My hands had finally started shaking, and now they wouldn't stop, so I put them behind my back like a man pretending to be calm in front of a bank manager while his house burns down.

"What does it weigh?" I asked. "A soul. When you weigh them."

"Why?"

"Because you said trade of equal weight. That's the rule, isn't it? Nothing given free under this mountain. So tell me what my soul weighs, because I've brought something to swap."

Wren made a small sound beside me. I didn't look at her. If I looked at her I would lose the thread, and the thread was the only thing holding me upright.

The Ledger-Keeper considered me for a long moment. Then it turned pages—dozens, hundreds, the sound like wings—and stopped, and read, and something in its posture shifted by a degree that on any other creature would have been called surprise.

"A full life," it said. "Memory, love, grief, the whole weight of it. Offered entire, against a single soul." The smooth face did something terrible and gentle. "It balances. Barely. You would leave here hollowed, Sandoval. No past. No wood. No—" and here the pen lifted, indicating, with accountant's precision, the woman standing at my shoulder, "—no her."

"I know what I'm offering."

"He doesn't," Wren said.

And that was when the collectors came.

* * *

I heard them before I saw them, which is backwards, because everything about them was silence—they moved the way a held breath moves. Three shapes unfolding out of the dark behind us, tall as doorways, their bodies stitched from the same gold script that veined the walls, letters crawling over each other in a constant whisper. They did not look at me.

They looked at Wren.

"Wren Aldermoor," said the middle one, in a voice like a ledger closing. "Outstanding balance."

"No," I said.

"She traded memories to buy my mother's silence," I said. "Years ago. She paid. She paid everything, she doesn't even remember—" My voice climbed and cracked and I hated every second of it. "Her account is settled."

The nearest collector extended a hand of woven letters toward her, courteous as a customs officer. "Settled? Let us review the terms."

And the Ledger-Keeper, obliging as death, turned its great pages backward, back past my mother's name, back past decades of entries, and stopped on a page that was newer than mine, cleaner, and read aloud:

"*Received of Wren Aldermoor: seven years of memory, tendered against the silence of Lira Sandoval.*" A pause. "Seven years. Not seven-and-twenty. The silence was bought for seven years, keeper. The contract expired last spring."

The silence in the bone halls was absolute. Somewhere far above, water dripped—the Weeping Wood's tears finding their way down through a hundred fathoms of stone, weeping even here.

"Last spring," Wren repeated. Her voice was very level, the way voices get when the person behind them is falling. "I carried him across three provinces. I starved with him. I fought collectors in the marsh country. For six months past due."

"Interest accrues on overdue balances," said the Ledger-Keeper.

Something went very quiet and very hot in my chest, in the place where I had been carrying my terror all these years like a stone I'd learned to sleep on. Duty, my mother had called it. Debt, the court called it. Terms, conditions, weights, balances—every word in that mountain designed to make theft sound like weather, like gravity, like something you simply lived under.

I looked at Wren. She was watching the collector's hand come for her the way you watch weather, resigned, already calculating how much of herself this storm would take—and I understood that she had known. Some part of her, some keeper's instinct for ledgers, must have known the math was short, and she had walked into this mountain anyway, for me, without saying a word.

Duty curdles, I have learned. In the right company, at the right moment, it turns thick and sour and rises up your throat, and there is only one thing left to do with it.

Spit it out.

"No," I said again, and this time the word came out with the valley in it, with the gate wards and the quiet pines and every morning I had ever spent keeping magic out of a wood that wanted nothing but to be left alone. "No more terms."

The Ledger-Keeper's pen paused above the page.

"Debtor," it said, with the first hint of something that was almost alarm, "the dark moon has not yet risen. You cannot default on an account that is not yet due."

"Watch me," I said, and reached for the ledger's page with both hands.

Behind me, Wren shouted my name—a name she shouldn't have remembered, a name she'd paid away—and I would spend what remained of my past trying to understand how she'd gotten it back. But that was later. That was after.

First, the tearing.
