## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The wolf came to her at dusk, limping along the fence line where the Coldharbour pasture met the birch wood, and it did not come the way wolves came — low, sidelong, all hunger and caution. It came the way a neighbor comes, dragging its left forepaw through the frost-brittle grass and looking at her window with an expression she had no better word for than *please*.

Dominic pulled on her boots before her mother could call her in for supper.

"I'm checking the festival lanterns," she said through the kitchen door, which was true in the way most of her sentences were true: technically, partially, just enough to pass. The Founding Festival was tomorrow. Lanterns had been hung along every eave in Vell for three days, paper and horn-glass, hundreds of them, because the valley liked its light contained and its darkness kept outside where it belonged.

Outside, then. She went.

The air smelled of woodsmoke and the last of the apple harvest, and beneath that the cold mineral scent of coming winter. Frost was already forming in the shadows of the fence posts. The wolf waited for her at the corner of the sheepfold, and up close she could see how bad it was — a snare wound, or something worse, wrapped around the foreleg twice and torn deep where the animal had worried at it. The fur there was matted black. The leg below the injury was swollen tight as a drumhead.

"Who did this to you?" she whispered, though she already knew. Not a snare. Snares didn't leave marks like that — clean-edged, deliberate, almost surgical. This was wire laid by hands that knew exactly what they were doing and wanted the animal to suffer doing it.

*Wardens,* said the wolf, in the wordless way beasts spoke to her — not language, never quite language, but meaning arriving whole in her chest like a swallowed stone. *Grey men. Grey wire.*

Dominic crouched in the frozen grass and looked over her shoulder at the house. Lamplight moved behind the kitchen window. Her mother's silhouette bent over the stove; her father's rose and fell with some argument about lantern oil or tithes or whatever small thing the village argued about the week before the Festival. No one would come looking for her for twenty minutes at least. Twenty minutes was enough. Twenty minutes was always enough, and that was the problem.

"Let me see," she said.

The wolf lowered its leg into her lap without hesitation, and that trust sat heavier on her than any weight of bone and fur. She'd been speaking to beasts since before she could speak properly to people — her mother told the story of two-year-old Dominic screaming at the top of her lungs until someone opened the henhouse and let out the fox-trapped vixen, screaming words nobody understood but the fox clearly did. They called it a child's fancy then. By the time she was old enough for it to stop being a fancy, she'd learned what the Choir's law said about people like her, and she learned it the way children learn anything that will kill them: quietly, completely, without ever being told outright.

Beast-speakers were unclean. Beast-speakers carried *taint*. Beast-speakers went into the white wagons and did not come back, and their families got a visit from men in grey who asked gentle questions and left behind a silence that never fully lifted.

So Dominic healed animals in the dark, in ditches, in the reeds by the millpond, and lied beautifully, and hated herself a little more each year for how good she'd gotten at it.

She cupped the wounded leg between her palms. The flesh was hot under the cold skin. She closed her eyes, found the place inside herself where the gift lived — a warm hollow behind her sternum, like a second heart — and began to whisper.

Not words, exactly. Words were only the surface of it. What she actually did was listen first: to the leg, to the angry red thread of infection climbing toward the shoulder, to the wolf's own body shouting its pain in a hundred small voices. Then she answered. *Ease. Ease. Close. Mend.* She poured warmth down her arms and into the wound, and the warmth wasn't hers alone — it never was. It came from somewhere she couldn't name, up through the ground or down through the sky or sideways out of the world's hidden seams, and it passed through her the way water passes through a channel, and if she held too much of it too long it would scour her hollow, so she held only what the leg needed and gave the rest back.

The swelling sank. The torn edges drew together, pink and new. The wolf made a sound low in its throat that was not quite a whine and not quite a song.

"There," Dominic breathed. "There. Stay off it tonight anyway. Don't argue with me."

*I will run by morning,* the wolf said, smug.

"You'll limp by morning, is what you'll do." But she was smiling, and the smile felt dangerous on her face, the way it always did — like something she'd have to fold away and hide before anyone saw it. That was the arithmetic of her whole life: joy on one side of the scale, terror on the other, and her forever adding small weights to both sides hoping they'd balance.

She should go in. Supper would be on the table. Tomorrow was the Festival, and she had a dress to press, and her father would want help hanging the last lanterns along the barn.

She stayed one more minute, scratching the wolf behind its ears, feeling the great slow engine of its heartbeat against her knee.

That was when the stars began to go out.

It was not like clouds crossing them. Clouds move; they blur and reveal. This was different. High above the eastern ridge, directly over the Warden's Teeth where the border wards stood oldest and strongest, a star simply ceased. One moment it burned, small and white and indifferent; the next it was a hole in the sky. Then another. Then three in a row, going out left to right like windows being shuttered down some endless dark corridor.

Dominic stopped breathing.

The wolf felt it before she understood it. Every hair along its spine lifted. It turned its head upward and made a sound she had never heard from any beast — not fear exactly, but recognition, grief, the sound of an animal seeing a thing it had been promised long ago it would never see again.

"What is it?" she whispered. "What's happening?"

But the beasts had gone strange and silent, all of them at once, all across the valley. She could feel it the way you feel a room go quiet — the sheep huddled mute in the fold, the dogs in the village refusing to bark, the roosting birds packed silent into the birches like held breath. The whole living world of Vell had stopped talking, and Dominic, who had spent her life listening, suddenly heard nothing at all, and the nothing was louder than any sound.

More stars went out. Dozens. The constellations she'd known since childhood — the Ploughwife, the Running Stag, the Seven Sisters who watched over midwives — unraveling, their bright knots picked apart thread by thread. And where the stars vanished, the dark behind them seemed deeper than ordinary night. Not empty. *Attended.* As if something vast had pressed its face against the far side of the sky and was looking in.

"No," Dominic said aloud, stupidly, uselessly. "No, no—"

Then the wards broke.

She saw it happen, and she would see it every time she closed her eyes for the rest of her life. Along the ridgeline of the Warden's Teeth, five miles distant, a line of pale light flared into being — the border wards, invisible all her life, suddenly revealed in their moment of dying. They ran the whole curve of the horizon, a seam of silver-white fire stitched through the mountain passes, and for one heartbeat they blazed brighter than the missing stars had ever managed.

Then they shattered.

Not faded. Shattered — like ice on a pond, like a dropped plate, like glass struck by a stone. Cracks raced along the ward-line faster than lightning, branching, spidering, and where each crack passed, the light didn't gutter but *burst*, flinging sparks that fell upward instead of down, streaming off into the hole-punched sky like embers sucked into a chimney. The sound reached her seconds later: a deep crystalline groan rolling across the valley, followed by a ringing silence, followed by — faintly, impossibly, from miles away — something that might have been singing.

The wolf bolted. It didn't growl or warn her; it simply fled, low and fast, into the birch wood, and every other creature in earshot went with it. Somewhere down-valley a dog finally did bark, one hysterical burst, then cut off as if slapped silent.

Dominic stayed on her knees in the frozen grass with her healing-warmth still tingling in her palms, staring up at a sky with no stars in it, and felt the secret inside her rise like floodwater.

Because here was the thing she could not say to anyone, the thing that had lived in her chest since she was old enough to understand it, and now stood up inside her and demanded to be spoken: *the sky isn't broken.* Broken things fall and lie still. The stars hadn't fallen. They had been *taken* — plucked out cleanly, one by one, by something with intent. And the wards hadn't failed either. Wards were woven things, and woven things fray; these had been cut, deliberately, expertly, from the outside or — and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome and true — from within.

And beneath all of it, quieter and worse: for one instant, in the moment before the wards shattered, Dominic had felt them. Felt the border-wards the way she felt everything alive — as presence, as attention, as a vast slow mind half-asleep along the mountains. They had known her. In that single heartbeat of contact, the wards had recognized what she was, the way the wolf had, the way every beast did.

They had been holding her in. All of them. Her whole life. The valley had never been a refuge.

It was a cage, and she was one of the things it was built around, and tonight someone had opened the door.

Down in the village, the first lanterns of the Founding Festival were being lit, little flames trembling in horn-glass, and people were coming out of their houses to stand in the lanes and stare upward and call to one another in voices pitched carefully casual, the way people speak when they are trying not to scream. Someone rang the meeting-house bell — once, twice, then uncertainly, as if unsure what it was supposed to announce.

Dominic got to her feet. Her legs shook. The warmth of the healing still glowed faintly in her hands, and she shoved them deep in her pockets as if hiding evidence.

Above the dead country beyond the passes, where no star remained, something answered the shattered wards' last note. A harmony. Distant, patient, many-voiced.

It was singing her name.
