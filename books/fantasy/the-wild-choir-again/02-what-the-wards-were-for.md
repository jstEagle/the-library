## 2. What the Wards Were For

The morning after the stars went out, Vell pretended nothing had happened.

That was the valley's oldest trick. Dominic had grown up inside it without ever learning its name—the way the village of Hollowmere folded its griefs into bread and harvest and the small ceremonies of living, so that a funeral became an excuse for a feast, and a failed crop became a reason to plant twice as deep, and the whole valley went on humming its little tune as though the world outside the passes were not a wound in the sky. By noon on Founding Day proper, the market square was full again, the bunting re-hung where the wind had torn it, and old Maren Corvane stood on the well-steps in her good shawl, calling for order with a voice like gravel in a bucket.

"Curfew," she said. "Sundown to first light. Every soul indoors, every beast penned or tethered. The wards have suffered some manner of collapse—no one's denying it—but collapses happen. Old magic tires, same as old men. The elders will send riders to the border shrines at week's end, and until then we keep our heads down and our lamps lit and we do not speak of wild magic."

She said *wild magic* the way you'd say *plague*. Around Dominic, the villagers nodded, grateful to be handed something small enough to fear. A curfew was a thing they could understand. A curfew had edges.

Dominic stood at the edge of the crowd with Bracken's ruff under her hand, and said nothing, because what could she say? *The wards didn't tire. They shattered, all at once, like a window struck from the far side. And something out there is singing my name.*

The wolf leaned against her leg. He'd followed her home before dawn, limping only faintly now, the wound in his flank closed to a pale seam that would have taken any healer in Vell a month of prayer to mend. He looked up at her, and in the private language that was not words but something older than words—a pressure behind the eyes, a taste on the tongue—he said: *They are lying. Not cruelly. Lying the way sheep lie to lambs about the knife.*

*I know,* Dominic answered.

*You don't. You think you do.* Bracken's ears flattened. *Listen to them. Listen to everything, today. Everything is screaming.*

He was right. All morning, as she made her rounds—because the Coldharbours' beasts didn't know there'd been a curfew announced, and neither did anyone else's, and someone had to see to the calving heifer at Dunmore's and the sick ewes at the Tarn—she heard it. The animals of Hollowmere had always talked. That was her curse and her secret both, the gift that would hang her if the Choir ever learned of it: she heard the low brown mutter of cattle, the sharp gossip of geese, the slow stone-deep thoughts of the plow horses who remembered being wild. But today their voices were wrong. Frantic. Overlapping.

*It's gone, it's gone, the hum is gone—*

*My bones feel hollow, why do my bones feel hollow—*

*Something took it. Something came in the night and took it—*

That last was from the Tarns' old ram, standing in his paddock with his head thrown back, nostrils flaring at a sky with no stars in it even in daylight. *Took it,* he repeated, when Dominic stopped by the fence. *Girl. Girl-who-hears. It didn't break. Things don't break like that on their own. Glass breaks because a hand strikes it. The hum was struck.*

Dominic gripped the fence rail until splinters bit her palm. All her life she'd felt the wards the way you feel your own pulse—not as sound exactly, but as a constant beneath everything, a vast patience holding the valley like water holds a fish. She'd never thought about it. You don't think about the shape of the cage when you've never seen outside it. Now the absence of it rang in her teeth like a pulled tooth's ghost, and every animal in the valley felt the same amputation.

"The elders say wild magic did it," she told the ram.

The ram looked at her with one yellow eye, and his contempt was so total it nearly made her laugh. *Elders. Elders are people who got old by agreeing.* He turned back to the empty sky. *Go to the high pasture. Go before dark, whatever they say. There's one still alive up there who needs to tell you something before he goes. He's been calling since dawn. You're the only one left who can hear him.*

*Who?*

*Go,* said the ram, and would say no more.

* * *

She went at dusk, breaking her first curfew within hours of its making, and found it strange how little fear she felt. The rules of Vell had never fit her anyway; they'd been a coat sewn for somebody else, and she'd spent nineteen years pretending the sleeves weren't too long. She climbed the shepherd's track above Hollowmere with Bracken ghosting silent beside her and the last grey light dying off the western passes, and above her, where the stars should have been wheeling up thick as midsummer pollen, there was nothing. A black so complete it seemed less like night than like a held breath.

The high pasture lay in a bowl below the old ward-shrine, and she smelled blood before she saw him.

The stag stood in the middle of the grass with his legs splayed, and even from thirty paces she knew him, though she'd never seen him whole—only glimpsed, once or twice over the years, a white shape at the treeline on the longest nights, and every beast in the valley going reverently quiet when he passed. The ward-stag. His antlers were wrong; that was the first thing. They should have risen like winter branches, but they drooped, cracked along their length, weeping thin threads of light that guttered and went out as she watched. Light leaked from him everywhere—from his eyes, from the split seams of his hide, from the place low in his chest where something had gone through him and out the other side.

*Little speaker,* he said. His voice was vast and very quiet, like wind through a canyon heard from miles away. *You came. Good. Good.*

She dropped to her knees in the frost-stiff grass. Up close she could see what had wounded him: not a blade-wound, not teeth. A hole, clean-edged, burned through muscle and bone alike, and around its rim the flesh had turned the color of old ivory—as though the life had been drawn out of it deliberately, carefully, the way you'd draw thread through cloth.

"Who did this?" Her voice cracked. "The elders say—"

*Your elders say what they were taught to say. They know nothing. Their mothers knew nothing. That was the design.* The stag swayed. Bracken crept forward and pressed his shoulder under the great neck, taking some of its weight, and the stag allowed it. *Listen, little speaker. I was a ward. Do you understand? Not a keeper of wards. A ward. My body is one anchor among hundreds—stag and wolf and salmon and owl, every wild thing in this valley knotted into the working, generation after generation. We hold the walls. We have always held the walls.*

"Holding them against what?"

*Nothing.* The light in his eyes flickered, and something in it was almost amused, almost bitter. *Against nothing. That is the joke, child. There was nothing outside for us to keep out. The Choir built the walls to keep something in.*

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. *Choir.* She'd heard it in chapel lessons, in the Founding stories sung every autumn: the holy order beyond the mountains, the keepers of harmony, the ones whose law said a girl who spoke with beasts was an abomination to be cleansed. A story. A distant, half-mythical thing, like the stars themselves.

"They're real."

*Oh, they are real.* The stag's legs buckled; Bracken took more of the weight; Dominic threw her arms around the white neck and felt the terrible coldness of him, the light running out of him like sand through fingers. *They came in the night. White robes, many voices. They sang the wards loose—I felt every knot unpicked, one by one, patient as thieves—and then they reached into the working and took the heart of it. Took the power itself. Two hundred years of hoarded strength, gathered from this valley like rent from tenants, and carried away singing. What shattered was only the shell. The shell, little speaker. We were never protecting you.*

His breath came ragged now, each exhale dimming him further.

"Then what—" Dominic's throat was tight. "What were we? What was any of it? The festivals, the laws, the—the borders, the passes nobody crosses—"

*A lie of geography.* The stag lowered his great head until his ruined antlers nearly touched her brow, and where the light touched her skin it felt like snowmelt, like truth. *There was never anything wild beyond the hills. There was only everything else. The world. Your mother's mother came from it. Your children's children will walk in it. Vell is a jar with a lid on it, and tonight the lid came off, and the ones who took the honey will come back to see if the bees are dead.*

She was crying without deciding to. "Can I save you?"

*Nothing saves an anchor when the wall is gone. I am only waiting to be finished.* His voice softened. *But listen. Listen while I still have a mouth. They took the power to the Door, beneath the mountains, in the dead country. The Door. Remember that name. And they will come here soon—they always come back to check their locks—and when they come, they will be looking for anything left untaken. Anything wild. Anything that hears.* The yellow light of his eye fixed on her, gentle and dreadful. *They will be looking for you, little speaker. Run from the valley. Find the Door. And find the warden called Sable, if any warden lives—grey coat, one arm, a face that has already confessed everything. She knows the way. She was there.*

"I don't understand—how do you know these—"

The stag smiled. It was unmistakably a smile, and it broke her heart clean in two.

*I have stood at the border of this valley for two hundred years, child. Walls hear everything that touches them.* He lifted his head, gathering himself, and the last of his light rose in his antlers like dawn coming early and wrong. *Now go. Go, go. And when you sing someday—and you will sing, sooner than you wish—sing loud enough for both of us.*

The light went out of him all at once, the way the stars had gone out, and what remained in the grass was only a great white body, ordinary and enormous, cooling under a sky with no stars in it at all.

Dominic knelt there a long time. Below her, Hollowmere was lighting its curfew lamps one by one, obedient little flames in obedient little windows, and the sight of it made her chest ache with love and grief in equal measure—for the valley that had raised her, which had never once been free, and might never forgive her for learning it.

Bracken nudged her shoulder. *We should burn him,* the wolf said gently. *It's what his kind would want.*

"Yes." She stood. Her legs were steady now, which surprised her; she'd expected to shake forever. "And then, Bracken—we're leaving."

The wolf looked at her, and then east, toward the dead country and the passes and the darkness where something many-voiced waited, still patient, still singing.

*Good,* he said. *I was afraid you'd want to stay.*

Behind them, far off across the valley, a bell began to ring—the alarm bell, the one that hadn't sounded in living memory—and down in Hollowmere, doors began to open, and lanterns began to move, and voices began to cry that strangers were coming up the valley road. White strangers. Singing.
