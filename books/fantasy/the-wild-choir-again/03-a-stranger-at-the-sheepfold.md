## 3. A Stranger at the Sheepfold

The dog saw her first.

Sable had been mending the south fence when Bracken went rigid at the treeline, ears forward, hackles lifting in a slow wave from shoulder to tail—not the bark-happy alarm of a farm dog but the deep, silent attention of an animal watching something it could not name. Sable followed his gaze and found nothing for a long moment. Then the nothing moved.

A woman came down out of the pines the way a wounded thing comes down: low, deliberate, spending each step like coin she couldn't spare. Grey coat, or what had been grey once—now torn along one sleeve to show old stitching, warden's braid gone dark with something that wasn't mud. One arm ended above the elbow in a neat, terrible stump wrapped in strips of shirt. The other hand carried no weapon. That was the detail that snagged in Sable's chest like a fishhook. Wardens always carried weapons. Even disgraced ones. Especially disgraced ones.

The woman made it perhaps twenty paces into the dooryard before her legs decided for her. She didn't fall so much as pour herself onto the packed earth by the gate, folding like laundry dropped from a line, and lay there breathing in the shallow, whistling way of someone who has been running far longer than the body agrees to.

Sable stood very still with the fence wire loose in her hands.

She knew that grey. She had worn that grey for eleven years before they took the rank off her shoulders and told her never to wear anything like it again. And she knew what it meant that this woman was alone, unarmed, and bleeding at the edge of Vell three days before the Founding Festival, when every road into the valley should have been singing with pilgrims and lantern-light.

It meant pursuit. It meant the Choir had finally noticed what Sable herself had spent four years pretending not to notice: that the wards were thinning, that the stars over Vell flickered some nights like candles in a draft, that the beasts had begun to speak in their sleep with voices too large for their bodies.

*Don't,* said the careful part of her, the part that had kept her alive since the day Commander Ivo read her name off a list of traitors. *Don't go out there. Don't touch her. Let the valley keep its secrets and let hers be someone else's problem.*

But Bracken was already moving, trotting out through the gate with his tail low and his body curved in that particular arc dogs use when they've decided a stranger is theirs now, and Sable found she had set down the wire and was walking after him before any decision in her head had caught up with her feet.

Up close the woman smelled of pine resin and old blood and, underneath it, faintly, of cold stone—the smell of the high passes where the ward-stones stood in their rings. Her face was younger than her ruin suggested. Thirty, maybe. Grey shot through her black hair not with age but with the particular frost that came from standing watches no one thanked you for.

Her eyes opened. They were the color of wet slate, and they fixed on Sable with the flat, total alertness of a creature deciding in half a second whether this was rescue or the end of the run.

"Water," the woman rasped. "Please."

Sable crouched, keeping her weight back on her heels, and held out the skin from her belt. "You're a long way from anywhere, warden."

"Not a warden." The woman drank like someone who had learned to ration even water, two swallows, then forced herself to stop. "Not anymore. Not anything." She looked down at her missing arm as if confirming the paperwork of her own dismissal. "Sable. Your name is Sable. I know your file."

Cold walked up Sable's spine on small feet. "My *file.*"

"I was stationed at Ashfall archive, before." The ghost of a smile, humorless as a scar. "I read everything. Disgraced warden, left-handed, lost her posting for—" She stopped herself, which was its own kind of courtesy. "They said you'd gone to ground somewhere in the valley provinces. They said it like you were dead."

"And they sent you to check?"

"No one sent me." The woman's gaze slid past Sable's shoulder, toward the tree line she'd come out of, and whatever she saw there—or remembered seeing—made her jaw tighten. "I ran. There's a difference. There's going to be a difference between us too, if you help me up, because I can't do it one-armed and I refuse to die in a sheepyard."

* * *

His mother called him Dominic, so that was his name: Dominic Coldharbour, nineteen years old, tall in the way of someone still surprised by their own height, with ink stains on his fingers from copying festival programs and a habit of looking at animals the way other people looked at people—directly, expectantly, as though conversation might happen.

Sable watched him come across the yard with an armload of straw and stop dead when he saw the stranger propped against the well-house wall.

She watched something else, too. She watched the boy glance at Bracken first—a flicker, barely a heartbeat—and she watched the wolf answer it. Not a sound, not a gesture anyone else would catch. Just a settling, a communication passing between them like light through a window, and then Dominic turned back to the stranger already knowing, somehow, that she was safe.

Sable had seen that look exactly once before, years ago, through the barred window of a transport cart rolling south out of the mountains. A child in a village the Choir had burned, standing in the ash, holding a half-dead crow against her chest and *listening to it*, her lips moving, her eyes gone somewhere no one in the Choir's custody ever came back from. They had put the child in the white wagon themselves. Sable had been assigned to guard the wagon. She had looked away. Looking away was the only rebellion available to a person in warden grey, and she had spent four years telling herself it was enough.

It had not been enough. Nothing was enough. The wards were dying, the stars were guttering, and here in a sheepfold at the bottom of the world stood a boy with a wolf's whole vocabulary in his eyes.

"You're hurt," Dominic said to the stranger, kneeling, all practical concern, straw spilling forgotten from his arms.

"I'm *hunted,*" the stranger said, and Sable saw the boy absorb it without flinching, saw him weigh it and decide it changed nothing. "There are things behind me that don't leave tracks. Inquisitors' hounds, or worse. If they're close, being near me is a sentence."

Dominic glanced at Bracken again. Whatever passed between them made him stand and square his narrow shoulders. "Then we'd better get you inside before they arrive. Ma will want the story anyway; she'll notice blood on the porch stones regardless."

He was lying about the last part—Sable had met his mother, Marta Coldharbour, a woman who could walk past a corpse and see only a floor that needed scrubbing—but the lie was kind, and kind lies were the mortar that held hidden people together. Sable knew. She had laid a thousand of them.

* * *

By lamplight, with the door barred and Bracken stretched across the threshold like a drawn bowstring, the stranger let them see how bad it was. The wound under the ruined coat was a burn, not a blade-cut—a long seared stripe across her ribs, the flesh around it unnaturally pale, as though the injury itself had been *emptied*. Sable had seen wounds like it once, in the last year of her service, on a man who had gotten between an inquisitor's song and its intended target. The Choir did not always kill with steel. Sometimes they simply took what they wanted out of you and left the shell standing.

"They came for the vault records," the stranger said quietly, while Dominic cleaned the burn with spirits and did not ask questions, which was its own discipline. "In Ashfall. I was a copyist. Low clearance, low value—I thought. But I'd read things. About Vell. About what the wards actually *are*." Her slate eyes came up and rested on Sable with terrible gentleness. "About who keeps them fed."

The lamp flame leaned. Somewhere outside, far off, an owl called—or made a sound shaped like an owl call, wrong in a way Sable's skin understood before her mind did.

"The wards aren't stone," the stranger went on, voice dropping. "Stone is just the frame. They're alive, warden. They're *animals*. Great old bound things wearing the shapes of stags and wolves and whales, anchored in the border hills, singing themselves hoarse to hold the valley shut. And the Choir knows. The Choir built it that way." She hissed as Dominic's cloth touched raw flesh, steadied herself. "And something is unmaking them. One by one. Starting at the edges. Which is why I ran here instead of away—because if the wards fall, everything the Choir stole goes back into circulation, and I wanted to see it. I wanted to *witness* it."

"Witness," Sable repeated. The word tasted like ashfall.

"You think I'm mad."

"I think madness would be safer for everyone in this room."

But she didn't believe it, and the stranger knew she didn't, and the knowledge sat between them like a third guest. Because Sable had served the Choir for eleven years. She had stood watch on ward-rings in the high passes and heard, on windless nights, a sound beneath the wind that was not wind—a vast slow breathing, a patience the size of a mountain range. She had filed reports calling it acoustic anomaly and gone to bed and slept. Complicity wore many uniforms. Hers had fit well for a long time.

Dominic tied off the bandage with neat, practiced knots—he was better at this than most village boys, having grown up stitching torn ears and splinted wings—and said, without looking up: "The stag died last night."

Both women went still.

"Down by the river bend. Old, enormous, antlers like winter branches." His voice was steady, but his hands weren't, and he pressed them flat against his knees. "It spoke to me while it was dying. It said—" He swallowed. "It said the wards didn't fail. They were taken. It said to tell anyone who would listen that the *Choir* is coming to collect what it planted." He raised his eyes, and they were not a frightened boy's eyes; they were something older wearing a frightened boy's face. "So either we're both mad, or we're the only ones awake."

The lamp flame stood perfectly straight now, as if listening too.

Outside, Bracken rose to his feet without a sound, every hair along his spine lifted, his nose aimed at the darkness beyond the sheepfold where the snow showed no tracks at all—no tracks, no prints, no broken twig, only a silence arriving with footsteps in it, patient as arithmetic, closer than it had been an hour ago.

Sable reached over and pinched the lamp out.
