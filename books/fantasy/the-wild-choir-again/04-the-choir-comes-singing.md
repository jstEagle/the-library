## 4. The Choir Comes Singing

The white robes came at dawn, and they came singing.

Dominic heard them before she saw them — a thread of sound winding up the valley road, thin as smoke, sweet as water over stones. It slid through the cracks of the Coldharbour farmhouse the way cold slides in under a door, and it made the hair on her arms stand up without her knowing why. Bracken felt it too. The wolf had been restless for three days, pacing the barn's shadow line, and now he pressed himself flat against her legs with a low whine that needed no translation: *wrong, wrong, wrong*.

She climbed to the loft window and looked down the long slope of the barley field to where the Vell road bent around the millpond. They came on foot, twelve of them, robed in white so clean it hurt against the mud of the world. No horses. No weapons she could see. They walked in step, and they sang, and their song had no words she could hold onto — only harmonies that stacked and folded like light through water.

Below her, the village was coming out of its doors. Three days of curfew, three days of elders muttering about wild magic and broken wards, and here at last was an answer wearing white. She watched Tam Wetherby drop his hoe. She watch old Marta Iles press both hands to her mouth. She watched them drift toward the road like leaves toward a current, drawn by the singing, and understood with a lurch that she wanted to go too — that some part of her was already halfway down the hill, aching toward that sound the way a thirsty woman aches toward a well.

That was what frightened her. Not the robes. The wanting.

Bracken growled, low, and the wanting cracked like ice underfoot.

"Dominic!" Her mother's voice from below, sharp with fear. "Inside. Now. Whatever happens, you stay in the hayloft and you don't make a sound."

She didn't argue. She'd learned by now which battles were hers to fight and which were simply weather. She gathered Bracken into the deep straw at the loft's far end, wrapped her arms around his neck, and listened to the Choir come singing into Vell.

* * *

Sable had heard that harmony before, and it had never once meant anything good.

From the ditch behind the Wetherby fence line — a one-armed woman in torn grey is invisible anywhere if she holds still enough — she watched the twelve walk into the village square, and her mouth went dry as old bone. Wardens wore grey. Wardens carried staves and ledgers and the weary authority of men who kept borders. But the Choir's inquisitors wore white, and Sable had seen white robes once before, eleven years ago, on the day she stopped being a warden and started being nothing at all.

They arranged themselves in the square without being asked, without speaking, in a crescent that faced the well. The tallest of them stepped forward — broad-shouldered, silver-haired, with a face like a kind grandfather in a woodcut — and lifted his hands, and the singing changed.

Sable knew the technique. Every warden learned the theory of it, though none but the Choir were permitted the practice: *harmonic compulsion*, the manuals called it, the shaping of sound into the shape of guilt. A voice alone could soothe or stir. Twelve voices braided together in the old intervals could reach inside a person and turn the locks.

It began gently. A weeping, first, spreading through the crowd like damp through cloth. Then movement — villagers sinking to their knees, clutching their own arms, rocking. And then the confessions.

"I spoke harshly to my daughter," Tam Wetherby sobbed, face in the dirt. "I spoke harshly, forgive me, I spoke harshly—"

"I kept back grain from the tithe," said Marta Iles. "I kept it back, I kept it hidden, I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

Grain there wasn't, Sable thought grimly. Marta's stores had been empty since midwinter. That was how the harmony worked; it didn't read your sins, it *supplied* them, offered up whatever shame your heart had lying loose in a drawer, and let you drown in it. She watched the tall inquisitor move among the kneeling villagers like a shepherd among sheep, touching heads, murmuring absolution, and she saw the villagers love him for it. That was the cruelest trick of all. He gave them relief from a wound he'd opened himself, and they would remember him afterward as a healer.

Her eye found the Coldharbour farm on the slope above, the hayloft door shut tight, and her jaw tightened. *Stay hidden, girl. Whatever you hear down there, however it burns you — stay hidden.* Because Sable knew something the villagers didn't yet know, something she'd hoped with a coward's hope wouldn't come to this: the Choir hadn't come to Vell for sinners. They'd come because the wards broke, and when wards break, the Choir hunts for why. And a girl who talked to beasts was the loudest possible answer to that question.

The tall inquisitor raised his hand again. The singing stopped. In its absence the silence rang like a struck bell.

"Children of the valley," he said, and his voice was warm bread, was honey, was every kind thing anyone had ever said to you at once. "You have suffered. Your sky has gone dark. Your beasts cry out in the night. You are afraid." He let the word sit among them, let them nod, let them weep afresh. "Fear not. Where there is taint, we will find it. Where there is corruption, we will cut it out. And Vell will be clean again."

In the ditch, Sable closed her eyes. *Cut it out.* She'd been a warden long enough to know what the Choir meant by clean.

She should slip away now, she told herself. Downriver, over the ridge, gone before they finished their work. Survival was a discipline; she'd practiced it for eleven years.

She stayed in the ditch.

* * *

They took the miller's son on the second day.

His name was Aldous Fenner. He was nineteen years old and pigeon-chested and he used to leave crusts on the windowsill for the crows, which everyone knew and no one had ever minded, because that was just Aldous — soft-hearted, slow-spoken, forever feeding things that couldn't feed him back.

Dominic watched it happen through a gap in the loft boards, and she would spend the rest of her life unable to stop watching it, even in sleep.

They came to the mill at noon, four of them in white, and they were singing again — softly this time, a small and intimate music, the kind you might hum to a child. Aldous stood in the mill doorway with flour to his elbows, blinking at them like a man waking from a nap. Dominic heard the tall inquisitor ask him questions in a voice pitched to carry, gentle as confession.

Had he touched the beasts? Had he spoken to them? Had he felt, perhaps, that they answered?

And Aldous — poor Aldous, honest to the marrow, terrified and flattered to be noticed by such grand folk — said yes. Yes, he talked to the crows. Everyone did, didn't they? Didn't everyone say a few words to the birds? He laughed, nervous, flour-white hands spread wide, waiting for them to laugh too.

The crows themselves went silent. All across the village, every crow, every sparrow, every starling went utterly still, and Dominic alone knew why: they were listening. They understood. And they could do nothing.

"That is beast-taint," the inquisitor said, still gently, and the harmony swelled behind him, and the villagers who had known Aldous since he was in swaddling clothes found themselves nodding, weeping, *agreeing*, the music reaching into them and turning the locks. "The law of the Choir is clear. The tainted must be cleansed, lest the corruption spread."

"They'll confess," Sable had warned her, two nights past, crouched in the dark of the barn while rain hammered the roof. "When the Choir comes, people will confess to anything, and mean it, and hate themselves after, and thank the Choir for taking it away. That's the horror of it, girl. They don't need to force anyone. They only need to sing."

Aldous didn't even struggle. That was the worst part. He looked around at the faces of his neighbors — at Tam Wetherby, at Marta Iles, at his own father held between two white-robed arms — searching for one face that would say *no, this is madness, not our Aldous*. And Dominic, watching through the gap in the boards, saw him find nothing. Saw him understand. Saw the light in him go out before they ever raised the blade.

They cleansed him at the well, in the square, at dusk, with the whole village kneeling and singing the harmony back at the singers, tears streaming down faces lifted in terrible gratitude. Dominic lay in the straw with her hand buried in Bracken's ruff and her teeth locked shut so hard her jaw screamed, because if she opened her mouth she would scream, and if she screamed she would die, and if she died then Aldous would have died for nothing at all — and worse, the wolves and the crows and every wild thing in Vell would lose the one voice they had left.

So she was silent. She was silent as the blade fell. She was silent as the white robes sang a hymn of completion over the body. She was silent as the villagers filed away, hollow-eyed, already beginning to forget, the way you forget a fever.

But silence, she discovered, lying in the dark with Bracken's heartbeat under her palm, has a sound of its own. It sounds like a blade. It sounds like a well. It sounds like nineteen years of crusts on a windowsill, ending.

*I did that,* she thought. *My silence did that.*

Not the Choir. Not the harmony. Her. Hidden in the hay, safe as a seed, while the one boy in Vell who loved birds the simple human way paid for the crime that was actually hers.

Something closed in her chest, then — not grief, or not only grief. Grief was soft. This had edges. It settled around her ribs like a band of cold iron, and it said, in a voice that was entirely her own and entirely new: *never again. Never again will I be quiet while they sing.*

Bracken stirred and pressed his muzzle into her neck, and through the bond between them passed not words but a picture: the white robes, the well, and beyond the village the broken border hills where the stars had gone out — and beyond *that*, everything, everywhere, all the roads out of the cage.

*Yes,* she told him. *We're done hiding.*

Down in the house, floorboards creaked. Footsteps on the stair, quick and uneven — her mother's tread, and behind it a second set of steps she didn't know, heavy, deliberate, stopping at the foot of the ladder to the loft.

"Dominic." It was Sable's voice, low and urgent, stripped of all its usual dryness. "Come down. Now. They're going door to door with the census rolls — and girl, there's a name on the Coldharbour roll that shouldn't be. They're asking about a stranger. One-armed. Grey coat."

A pause, and then, quieter:

"And they're asking about a girl who talks to wolves."
