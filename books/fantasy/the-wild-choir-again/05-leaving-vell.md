## 5. Leaving Vell

They left before dawn, when the valley was still the color of drowned things and the mist lay so thick on the Coldharbour fields that the fences seemed to float.

Dominic had packed the way her mother taught her to pack for market: light, and as if she meant to come back. A change of clothes. Bread wrapped in cloth. The little bone-handled knife. She stood a long moment in her own doorway with her hand flat against the wood, feeling the grain of it, the faint warmth of a house that had held her whole life, and told herself she was only stepping out to check the sheep.

The lie didn't even convince the dog.

Bracken pressed his grey shoulder against her knee, the wound Sable had helped her close now a ridge of healing scar beneath his fur, and looked up at her with those amber eyes that needed no words between them. *You are going,* he said, in the way wolves say things, which is to say without saying anything at all. *I am going too.*

Behind them, in the barn's shadow, Sable waited with her single arm cradled against her chest and her torn warden's grey buttoned to the throat, as if the uniform might still protect her from what it stood for. Her horse—a rangy dun mare she'd found grazing loose near the shrine road—shifted and blew through her nostrils.

"The border," Sable said quietly. "We cross while it's dark. What's broken of it will be quietest then."

"What's left of it?" Dominic asked.

"Enough to notice you." Sable's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "A ward that shatters doesn't die, girl. It bleeds. And bleeding wards remember hands that touched them."

Dominic took one last look back at the farm—the low slate roof, the sheepfold where she'd whispered her first word to a lamb and been understood, the chimney standing up against the greying sky like a finger raised in warning. Then she turned her face toward the hills, toward the place where Vell ended, and walked.

* * *

She had expected the border to look like something.

All her life, the valley's edge had been an idea more than a place—stone markers worn smooth, her father saying *past the high pass there's nothing but wild country*, and everyone nodding as though *wild country* were a kind of weather. But standing now at the top of the shepherd's trail, where the last of Vell's birches gave way to black pine and the air changed taste in her mouth like water from a different well, Dominic felt the border everywhere and saw it nowhere. It was a silence shaped exactly wrong. It was the sensation of walking through a spiderweb.

Bracken stopped so suddenly she nearly tripped over him. His hackles rose in a slow wave from shoulders to skull.

*It hurts here,* he said. *Old hurt. Old singing.*

"Sable," Dominic whispered. "He says—"

"I know what he says." Sable had gone very still, her one hand resting on the mare's neck. "Feel it under your feet?"

And she did—once she listened for it. A hum, deep as a heartbeat heard through a wall, coming not from any direction but from all of them at once. The ground itself was holding a note. The shattered ward hadn't finished dying; its last chord was still ringing down in the stone, the way a struck bell goes on sounding after you've covered it with your hands.

"Walk fast," Sable said. "Don't run. Running tells it we're afraid."

"And if we're not afraid?"

Sable glanced at her sidelong. "Then we're fools, and I've been enough of a fool for one lifetime."

They crossed in eleven strides. Dominic counted them because counting kept her from screaming—on the seventh stride the hum climbed into her teeth, into the wet hollows behind her eyes, and something vast and wounded turned its attention toward her from underneath the world, and knew her name, and let her go.

On the twelfth stride they were through, and the pine forest swallowed them, and Dominic realized she was crying without knowing when she'd started.

"That's the far side of your whole life," Sable said, not unkindly, reining the mare in beside her. "First time I crossed a border I threw up. You're doing better."

Dominic laughed—a cracked, startled sound—and wiped her face with her sleeve, and the valley of Vell disappeared behind them like a word you can't quite recall.

* * *

The trouble began on the second day, or perhaps it had begun earlier and they simply hadn't noticed.

The map was Sable's—a thing of soft leather and faded ink, creased so many times along the same lines that it fell open to certain pages on its own, like an old dog turning to its favorite spot. She spread it across her knees at their first fire, in a hollow ringed with pines that leaned away from the wind, and traced the route with one blunt finger.

"We're here. Or—" The finger paused. Moved half an inch. Paused again. "Here."

"You don't know?" Dominic asked.

"I knew this country twenty years ago. There was a town called Merrow's Ford two days south of the pass. River crossing, good roads, a warden post." She said it the way you recite a lesson learned by heart. "So we'll follow the river south until—"

"There's no river south," Bracken said—or rather, he said it in the way he said everything, and Dominic translated because translating was the oldest habit of her life. "Water runs east here. He smells it. East and down."

Sable looked at the wolf for a long moment, and then, remarkably, believed him. Dominic watched her do it: the small visible surrender of a woman deciding that a talking wolf was no stranger than anything else the world had become lately.

"East, then," Sable said. "Fine. East."

But east brought them, by late afternoon, to a road running north and south—an honest-looking road, gravelled and ditched, the kind of road that implies destinations. They followed it south for an hour before Dominic noticed the birch tree.

She'd seen it that morning. She was sure of it—the lightning-split trunk, the shelf fungus growing from the wound like a pale ear. It stood at the roadside again, split trunk and pale ear, exactly as before, except that now it grew on the *left* side of the road instead of the right.

"Sable."

"I see it." Sable had stopped the mare. Her face, in the slanting light, had the grey look of someone reading bad news twice. "Roads loop. That's old knowledge—wardens used to say the wild country rearranged itself around travelers who carried too much magic." Her voice went dry. "Or around travelers who *were* too much magic. Congratulations, Dominic. You may be a landmark now."

"That's not funny."

"No," Sable agreed. "It isn't. It's also not the worst part." She swung down from the mare and stood looking down the road, into the deepening blue of evening. "The worst part is that the loop wasn't here twenty years ago. The Choir has been tightening this country like a drawstring. Everything loose gets pulled toward the center. Toward the Door."

The word dropped into the clearing between them and sat there.

"Door," Dominic repeated.

"Not a door the way you mean it. Not wood and hinges." Sable crouched and fed a stick into their little fire, watching the flame take it with an intensity that had nothing to do with the fire. "Think about what happened in Vell, girl. Every star in the sky went out in one night. Do you understand how much power that is? How much *magic*? Stars aren't lamps. They're the oldest workings there are—the first songs, still burning. Somebody reached up and *took* them."

"And put them where?"

"In a vault." Sable's jaw worked. "Under the mountains, in the dead country past Ashfall. I was posted there once—young, stupid, promoted for obedience and calling it honor. Warden duty at the Door. We guarded a mountain that sang, and we were told the singing was holy, and none of us—not one of us, not even me, especially not me—ever asked what was being sung *to*." She looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were hard and wet at once. "Magic doesn't vanish when the Choir takes it, Dominic. It's hoarded. Caged in crystal, stacked like coin, sung into silence. Every gift ever burned out of a child. every beast ever driven mad by severed wards. All of it, locked behind the Door, so that a handful of men in white can decide who deserves wonder."

The fire crackled. Somewhere above them, in a sky already going dark, there should have been stars. There were none—only a black so complete it seemed less like night than like a lid.

Dominic sat with her arms around her knees and thought of the ward-stag dying in the bracken, its great head heavy on the earth, its last words spent naming the thieves. *Taken*, it had said. Not failed. Taken. And she had thought she understood. She had not understood at all. The stag had died at the hem of the theft; the theft itself went on and on, deeper than borders, deeper than maps.

"Then that's where we're going," she said.

Sable barked a laugh with no humor in it whatsoever. "Do you hear yourself? You're a shepherd's daughter with a parlor trick and a week of bread. The Door is guarded by the full strength of the Cantorate. I couldn't get within ten miles of it when I had two arms and their colors on my back."

"I healed a wolf," Dominic said quietly. "That's not a parlor trick. That was against their law, in a valley built to strangle it, and it lived." She reached out and laid her hand on Bracken's neck, and the wolf leaned into her, warm and solid as a promise. "And I'm not going alone. Neither are you. So the question isn't whether we can reach the Door." She met Sable's eyes across the fire. "It's whether you can stand to go back to the place you looked away from."

For a long time Sable said nothing. The fire ate down to coals. When she finally spoke, her voice had shed its armor entirely, and what was underneath was very tired and very young.

"No," she said. "I can't stand it. Which is, I'm told, how you know a thing is worth doing."

* * *

By the fourth day, the country had stopped pretending.

Towns appeared when the road wished it—a village at noon with smoke rising from chimneys and not one living soul in the lanes, doors standing open on empty rooms, bread gone green on the table. A chapel whose bell rang though nothing moved it. A signpost with three arms pointing three directions, each carved with the same word: *ASHFALL*. And always, always, the towns remembered different names. An old woman gathering sticks outside one settlement called it Corrie Maw; her neighbor, passing with a bundle of reeds, called it Ferrow; a child chasing a hoop down the street called it nothing at all, because she never once looked up.

"It's not that the maps are wrong," Dominic said, on the fifth morning, watching a river flow uphill past them with a sound like slow applause. "It's that the country forgot what it's supposed to be. Like—" she groped for it— "like a song nobody's sung in so long the verses have slid out of order."

Sable rode in silence for a while. "That's the truest thing anyone's said in my hearing in twenty years," she said at last. "The Choir didn't just cage the magic, girl. They caged the *singers*. The land itself was part of the choir once. Take away its voice long enough and this happens. Roads forget where they're going. Names come unstitched. Towns dream different dreams." She nodded at the uphill river. "Even the water's trying to remember downhill."

And yet—and this was the thing Dominic could not explain to Sable, could barely explain to herself—she was not afraid. Not the way she should have been. Each day the world grew less lawful and more alive, and each day some knot inside her loosened another turn. In Vell, every step of every path had been prescribed: field to fold, fold to kirk, kirk to market, and the wards humming overhead like a hand pressed gently but permanently on the back of her neck. Here, nothing was prescribed. Here, the world was unfinished, unmoored, singing snatches of songs it had forgotten the ends of—and Dominic walked through it feeling, for the first time in her life, the size of her own stride.

Freedom, she discovered, felt exactly like being lost. There was no telling them apart from the inside. Perhaps there never had been, anywhere, for anyone.

Bracken ranged ahead through the bracken—*that's where his name came from, she thought, absurdly, and almost laughed*—and came loping back on the sixth afternoon with his ears pricked and his breath steaming, and something new in his voice.

*Roads,* he said. *Many roads. And iron. And many, many people.* A pause, and then, softer, with the gravity wolves reserve for the important things: *And singing. Far off. Many voices, all the same voice.*

Dominic straightened. Ahead, past the last confused folds of the forgetting country, the land descended in long grey terraces toward a smudge of woodsmoke on the horizon—a city, vast beyond anything Vell contained, its chimneys standing up against the starless sky like a second, darker forest.

"Ashfall," Sable said, and her hand—the hand, the only one—closed unconsciously around her missing arm's phantom weight. "God help us. I hoped we wouldn't come this way."

"The roads chose it," Dominic said. "Every road points here. You said so yourself—the drawstring pulls everything toward the center."

"Yes. And the center," Sable said grimly, nudging the mare forward, "is where the Choir keeps its hands."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the evening air from a hundred thousand throats in perfect unison, the city began to sing.
