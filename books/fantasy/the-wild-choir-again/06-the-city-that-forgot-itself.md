## 6. The City That Forgot Itself

Sable had known Ashfall before the fall, and it had not been called that then.

It had been Corven's Reach, a grey and orderly capital where wardens drilled in courtyards and the Choir's cantors sang the dawn from white towers, and every street had a name and every name meant something. Now she stood at the edge of what remained of it with ash sifting down like patient snow, and the streets had no names she could find, and the people in them looked up when she passed as if she were a rumor come true.

The city had burned once—she could see the story of it in the melted window-glass and the black teeth of rafters—and then it had forgotten how to be anything but what it was now: a place where people waited for light that never came back.

"They're looking at us," Dominic murmured beside her. She had her hood up and Bracken pressed close against her leg, and still every eye on the ruined boulevard seemed to slide toward them like water finding a crack.

"They're looking at the wolf," Sable said. "Keep walking."

"They're looking at *you*." The girl's voice dropped. "You walk like someone who used to have authority."

Sable almost laughed. Eleven years since they'd stripped her grey off her back in a courtyard very much like this one, and apparently her spine still remembered its old posture. She made herself stoop, made herself small, and led them down an avenue where the cobbles had been pried up and replanted with turnip greens, because hunger outlasted ceremony.

The ash thickened as they went deeper into the city. It came from nowhere—or rather, from everywhere, from the sky itself, which had been empty for eleven days now and seemed determined to fill the vacancy with something. Fine grey flakes settled on shoulders and doorsteps and the folded hands of people kneeling in the street.

Kneeling was the thing that stopped her.

They were everywhere, small congregations of them, faces tilted up toward the blank sky. Some had chalked stars on the flagstones beneath their knees—five-pointed, six-pointed, shapes no astronomer would recognize, drawn from memory gone soft at the edges. A woman held a child up toward nothing, patiently, the way you'd hold a child up to see a parade.

"Eleven days," Sable said quietly. "That's all it took."

"All what took?" Dominic asked.

"For them to start praying to the wound." She nodded at the chalked stars. "I've seen this before. Not here—" *not anywhere*, she almost said, because the truth was she'd only ever seen it in reports, in the warden manuals' dry warnings about *devotional collapse in unwarded populations* — "but I know what it is. People can't stand an empty sky. It's like a room with no walls. They'll put gods in the hole faster than you'd believe."

Dominic was quiet a moment. Then: "Or maybe they just miss them."

There it was again, that habit the girl had of saying the simplest true thing in the flattest voice, so that it landed harder than any argument could. Sable didn't answer. She walked.

The temple found them before they found it—or rather, its sound did. A bell, or something like a bell, but wrong: a note that bent in the middle, that seemed to arrive slightly after it was struck, hanging in the air like smoke. Down a street of gutted merchant houses, a crowd was flowing toward a building that had been a granary once and was now hung with hundreds of strips of pale cloth, each one inscribed with a star drawn in charcoal. The cloth moved in a wind Sable couldn't feel, all together, like a field of wheat deciding something.

At the doors stood a man in undyed wool, grey-haired, broad through the shoulders in the way men got from carrying things their whole lives rather than exercising. He was handing out bread. He touched each person who took a loaf on the crown of the head, gently, the way you'd steady a bird.

Sable knew him.

She stopped so abruptly that Bracken's nose bumped her hip. Eleven years, and the face had softened around the edges—the jaw gone slack, the eyes gone kind in a way they had never been in the field—but she knew him the way you know a scar.

"Aldous Vane," she said, before she could decide not to.

The man turned. And Sable watched recognition move through him like weather: first blankness, then narrowing, then something that might have been grief or might have been relief, arriving all at once.

"Sable Wren," he said. Her old name, her warden name, spoken in that low even voice she'd taken orders under for six years. "They told me you were dead."

"They tell everyone that. It's cheaper than a trial."

A ripple went through the nearest worshippers—not fear exactly, but attention, the way a flock shifts when a hawk crosses the sun. *Warden.* The word still had weight here. Sable felt it settle on her shoulders like the ash.

Vane looked past her, and his gaze found Dominic, and stayed.

Sable saw it happen. Saw the priest in him cataloguing the girl in three seconds flat: the hood, the way the wolf leaned into her, the way the wolf's ears kept swiveling back to her as if she were north. Aldous Vane had been the best commander she ever served under precisely because he missed nothing, and she watched him miss nothing now, and understood that everything about this meeting had just changed character.

"You'll come inside," he said. It was not quite a question. "You look like people who haven't eaten in two days, and the girl looks like someone who's been running longer than that. Whatever you're running from, it isn't in here."

*It might be,* Sable thought. But Dominic's stomach chose that moment to growl loud enough that half the congregation smiled, and pride, Sable had learned, was a luxury that got people killed on the road.

They went inside.

* * *

The granary's interior had been transformed. Grain bins torn out; in their place, rows of benches facing an altar that was simply a circle of chalked stars on the floor, unlit candles arranged among them like seeds waiting for a season. The ceiling above had been opened to the sky—a deliberate wound, squared and clean—and through it came the ash, drifting down onto the altar, onto the chalked stars, filling their hollows with grey.

"They feed the stars," Vane explained, catching Sable staring. "What fell on the city the night the sky broke—we gather it. Every flake is a piece of something that used to burn overhead. We don't let it be swept away like dirt." His voice had lost none of its cadence, that measured warden's rhythm repurposed for liturgy. "We call them the fallen. We keep them safe until they can go home."

"That's not worship," Sable said. "That's evidence collection."

"It's both. Everything holy is." He smiled, and it was the same tired smile he'd worn over tactical maps a decade ago. "Sit. Eat. Then you'll tell me why a disgraced warden and a farm girl with a wolf are crossing dead country toward the Door."

The bread was real bread, dense and warm, and there was stew behind it, and Sable ate like the soldier she'd been while watching Dominic do the same with more manners than the girl had shown in weeks. Bracken lay under the bench with his muzzle on his paws, radiating watchfulness.

"The Door," Sable said at last, wiping the bowl. "You know about it."

"I sang its locks shut for nine years." Vane said it without shame, which was somehow worse. "Every warden does, in rotation. You stand in the dark below the mountains and you hold your note and you think about order, and the crystal drinks it out of you, and somewhere in the world a hedge-witch loses her touch, a beast goes quiet, a child born with second sight is born ordinary instead. That was the bargain. That was the peace." He turned his hands over, palms up, as if weighing something invisible. "I believed in it. Do you understand? I didn't just obey it. I loved it. Order is a beautiful thing right up until you see what it's standing on."

"And then?"

"Then the sky went out, and I finally asked myself what we'd been keeping down there that needed *stars* for locks." His eyes came up, gentle and terrible. "But you haven't answered my question, Sable. Why are you going there?"

"To open it," Dominic said.

Sable closed her eyes. Of course. The girl had no talent for lying and had apparently decided to stop trying.

Vane didn't startle. He set his bowl down with a soft click, and when he spoke again his voice was quieter, warmer, and every degree of that warmth was a blade being drawn slowly.

"I thought so. The beasts have been whispering about a speaker for months—did you know that? Word travels even now. *The one who talks.* They say the wards didn't break; they were *called*, by one of their own." He leaned forward. "Child. What is your name?"

"Dominic."

"Dominic." He tasted it. "Do you know what will happen to you if the Choir finds you? Not the inquisitors—they'll kill you, quickly, and count it mercy. The High Cantor himself will come for you. Because a gift like yours isn't a crime to him. It's a *key*. And keys get used." He spread his hands, encompassing the hall, the kneeling city, the ash-filled stars. "Stay here. No one in Ashfall reports to the Choir anymore; we report to the sky. You'll have food, walls, people. The wolf can sleep indoors. You'll belong to something again—I remember what it's like, Sable, don't look at me like that, I remember exactly what it's like to have nothing to be part of."

And Sable did look at him like that, because she could feel the trap assembling itself around them, elegant as harmony. Belonging. He'd chosen the word like a fencer choosing a line of attack. Eleven years outside the grey, and her chest still ached at it, and that ache was the most dangerous thing in the room.

"There's a price," Sable said flatly.

Vane's kindness didn't flicker. That was the horror of it.

"One," he agreed. "Only one, and it's smaller than you think." He turned to Dominic, and his voice went soft as ashfall. "Your gift. The speaking. Here, it would be surrendered to the Choir for safekeeping—there's a rite, an old one, a binding sung into the blood. You'd live free, child. Loved, even. You'd simply never hear another beast again. Never speak to one. The wildness in you, given up so that nothing wild can ever be blamed on you." He paused. "Think of it as taking off armor you never asked to wear."

Under the bench, Bracken lifted his head and made a sound Sable had never heard from him—low, continuous, a dog-shaped word for *no.*

Dominic looked at the wolf. Looked at the chalked stars filling slowly with grey. And Sable watched her weigh belonging against selfhood with the serious, unhurried gravity of someone who had been hunted long enough to consider every exit.

Then Dominic said, gently, "Father Vane. When the stars come home—who do you think they'll ask for first?"

The silence that followed had edges.

Because it was true, and everyone in the hall felt it land: the ash on the altar was settling into the chalk-drawn stars like water finding its level, and the candles—all of them, dozens of unlit wicks in their waxen seeds—had begun, without wind, without flame, to lean.

Toward Dominic.

Vane stared. For the first time in eleven years, Sable watched her old commander's certainty crack, and through the gap she heard, faint and far beneath the city, beneath the mountains, beneath everything—

a single note. Held. Waiting.

The Door knew they were coming.
