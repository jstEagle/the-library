## 7. Beasts at the Gate

They took Sable in the hour before dawn, when the ash-light over Ashfall was the color of old bone and even the priests had gone to their knees for sleep.

Dominic heard it happen from three streets away, curled in the loft of a tannery that Bracken had chosen for them because it smelled of enough dead things to mask a wolf and a fugitive both. What she heard was not shouting. The Choir did not shout. She heard singing—a single line of harmony threading up through the cobbles like water finding a crack—and beneath it, unmistakable even muffled by walls and distance, Sable's voice saying something short and flat and final, the way she said things when she had already decided to lose.

Then the singing stopped, all at once, the way a held breath stops.

Dominic was down the ladder before her mind caught up with her body. She burst into the street with Bracken's fur bristling against her leg and found the lane empty except for frost, except for a smear of grey wool caught on a nail of the tannery fence—warden grey, torn, one sleeve cut away where there had been no arm left to tear it from.

"They didn't run," Dominic said. Her voice came out strange, very small in the enormous silence. "She let them take her."

Bracken pressed his shoulder against hers, heavy as a door closing. In the language that was not words but weight and warmth, he said what he always said: *I am here.* And then, because he was honest the way animals are honest and would not lie to comfort her, he added: *We cannot follow. There are too many.*

He was right. Of course he was right. The Choir had taken the cathedral district two nights past—she had seen it from the rooftops, the white-robed figures moving through the colonnades in slow processional lines, their harmonies rolling out across the city like fog off a lake, making the watchmen lay down their arms and weep, making shopkeepers open their doors and kneel on their own thresholds. You could not fight a song that lived inside your own chest. Sable had told her that much, laughing bitterly: *That's how they took everything, girl. Not with swords. With chords.*

And Sable had walked out into that anyway. Had stood in the tannery doorway an hour ago and said, in the flat voice she used for truths too large to decorate, "If they come for me, don't follow. Whatever you hear, however it sounds—" she had touched Dominic's cheek once, briefly, with her single hand, rough palm against cold skin, "—don't you dare sing back."

The grey wool trembled on the nail in the wind.

*Don't you dare.*

Dominic stood in the empty lane until the frost soaked through her boots, and understood that she had already decided. She had probably decided the night the stars went out. Maybe the night the miller's son died with his hands tied and his eyes searching the crowd for someone—anyone—who would say *this is wrong*. She had hidden in the hayloft then. She had been silent then. Silence was complicity; she had learned that word in Sable's mouth and carried it since like a stone swallowed whole.

But this was not Vell's valley, and these were not its beasts. This was Ashfall, a city of forty thousand souls and none of them hers, and every creature in it belonged to no one—or worse, belonged to everyone, kicked and starved and shooed, the dogs chained behind butcher shops, the rats poisoned in the drains, the ravens shot off the granary roofs by boys with slings who never once wondered what the birds were saying about them.

She had spoken to beasts all her life. She had never once asked them for anything larger than *hold still* or *eat this* or *you're safe now*.

Bracken watched her with amber eyes as she knelt in the frozen mud, laid both palms flat against the ground, and did the unthinkable.

* * *

It began with the rats.

Not because she chose them first, but because they were nearest—always nearest, under everything, in the walls of everything—and because when she opened the door in herself that she had kept shut for twenty-two years, they were the ones already pressed against it. Their minds were quick and narrow and bright, a thousand candle flames in the dark under the city, and when her voice touched them they went still, all together, in every drain and cellar and granary floor from the river gate to the cathedral hill.

*I need you,* she said. And because her gift had never been command—it had never once been command, only speech, only asking—she gave them the truth entire: *They have taken my friend. She has one hand and grey wool and she did not cry out. They keep her in the white place on the hill where the singing comes from, and I am afraid, and I am going anyway.*

Rats could not weep. But forty thousand rats, all at once, lifting their heads out of the dark of the world—that made a sound like weeping, faint and vast, a rustle in the bones of the city.

The dogs came next, because dogs understand loyalty the way stones understand gravity. She felt them wake behind their chains and fences: the mastiff at the butcher's, the three half-starved curs that ran the fish market, the blind spaniel who had loved some dead child and still waited by a door for him. She felt their answer come not as words but as bodies leaning toward her across the whole dark map of Ashfall, a tide of muscle and heart pulling uphill toward the cathedral square.

The ravens simply laughed. That was the thing about ravens—they heard everything, judged everyone, and forgave nothing, and when her voice reached them in their roosts along the eaves of the temple district, their answer came back sardonic and immediate: *We know. We've been waiting all week for one of you two-legs to ask.*

Above the tannery roof, the sky began to move.

Dominic rose. Mud to her wrists, frost in her hair, Bracken's hackles a ridge of shadow beside her. Down every lane of Ashfall, things were coming out of the dark—not swarming, not raging, though rage was there, banked and old in every animal breast—but walking, trotting, flowing uphill with terrible unhurried purpose, cats sliding along walls with their ears flat, pigeons rising off the rooftops in grey sheets, the city's vermin and guardians and forgotten ones converging like iron filings around a lodestone that was a girl standing in a freezing lane with her hands shaking.

"I'm going anyway," she told Bracken, and her voice cracked on *anyway*, and he leaned his whole weight into her side, and they went.

* * *

The cathedral square was white marble and white robes, and in its center, bound kneeling between two braziers whose flames burned without heat—the Choir's clean fire, the fire that purified rather than burned—knelt Sable.

They had not hurt her yet. That was somehow worse. They stood around her in a crescent of forty voices, mid-harmony, drawing the confession out of her the way you draw poison from a wound, and Sable's head was bowed, and her shoulders—which had carried Dominic through the broken country, which had never once shaken—were shaking. Her mouth moved around words she did not want to say. The song made her confess. It made her name her sins, real and invented, the wardens she'd failed to warn, the Door she'd guarded and looked away from, and the Choir received each confession with bowed heads and gentle faces and sang it deeper.

The High Cantor stood apart, watching. Even from the square's edge Dominic recognized him from Sable's description: tall, silver-bearded, his robe unmarked by any color, his expression one of profound sorrowful kindness. He looked like a man about to forgive the world for disappointing him.

Dominic stood at the mouth of an alley with a city's worth of beasts coiled around her in the shadows, and discovered that her plan had ended here. She had called them and now she had them and she did not know the next word. *Attack* was not a word she knew how to say. She had healed a wolf with whispers; she had asked birds to carry messages; she had never—

Sable lifted her head. Across the whole white square, through the wall of singing, her eyes found the alley mouth as if pulled there, and she saw Dominic, and her face did something terrible and simple: relief, first, and then horror, and then she shook her head, once, hard.

*Run,* she mouthed.

"No," Dominic said aloud, to no one, to everyone, to the thousand small burning minds gathered in the dark at her back.

And she sang back.

Not the Choir's music—she had no harmonies, no training, no discipline, nothing but twenty years of talking to creatures in a suppressed valley, nothing but the wild untrained voice of someone who had spent her whole life being quiet. What came out of her was a single note, low and cracked and human, and underneath it the answering chord of everything she had ever spoken to: the wolves of Vell, the ward-stag whose ghost-memory still guided them, Bracken's deep bell-tone, and now this—rats screaming like tiny trumpets, dogs baying in forty throats, ravens adding their dry metallic laughter high above, cats yowling, horses screaming in the Choir's own stable, pigeons thundering off ten thousand rooftops until the sky itself seemed to shout.

It should not have worked. No single voice could hold that chord. But it was not a single voice anymore, and the sound that rolled across the cathedral square was the sound the Choir had spent centuries burying: the wild choir, unbidden, ungoverned, everything alive at once refusing to be quiet.

The harmony broke.

White-robed figures staggered, clutched their ears, dropped their censers. The clean fires guttered. Sable rolled sideways off the brazier-step, got her feet under her, ran—one-armed, graceless, magnificent. A mastiff the size of a pony hit the first inquisitor like a landslide. Ravens fell out of the sky onto white hoods, talons and shrieking. Rats poured up out of the drains in rivers, over boots, into robes, and men who could face heresy without flinching screamed at mice. The braziers toppled. A bell somewhere began ringing—not the Choir's bell, the alarm bell, and then another, chaos propagating through the waking city.

"GO," Sable shouted at her across the madness, blood on her lip, her one hand hauling Dominic bodily backward into the alley. "GO, it's done, GO—"

They ran. Behind them the square dissolved into feathered, furred, howling pandemonium, and Dominic fled through it with her gift roaring in her ears like a floodgate broken, powerful beyond anything she had imagined and sick with it, drunk on it, terrified of how easy it had been, how good it had felt to finally stop being silent.

* * *

It was three days later, in a safehouse above a shuttered dyeworks, that she learned about the child.

Sable told her gently, which made it worse. During the rout, in the stampede of panic and beasts and fleeing congregants, a girl of six had been knocked down on the square's marble steps—trampled, not by any beast, the report said, by human feet, by the crowd the chaos had unleashed. Dead before anyone could reach her. Her mother had been in the choir stalls, singing.

"She would have died anyway, maybe," Sable said, in the flat voice she saved for mercy. "The Choir burns beast-tainted children in that square twice a season. You've read the lists."

"That isn't the point." Dominic stood at the window, looking out at Ashfall smoldering under its new curfews, and saw in the black glass not the city but her own face, pale, lit by the lamp, wearing an expression she did not recognize. Someone powerful. Someone frightened of it. "The point is I didn't think of her. I didn't think of anything. I opened the door and everything came through it and I couldn't—" Her voice failed. "Sable, I liked it. For one moment, I liked it."

Sable said nothing for a long time. When she spoke, she was looking at the same window, the same ruined reflection.

"Then you're ready for the Door," she said. "Because that's what's in there. Everyone's appetite. Including mine." She pushed back her chair. "And we leave tonight. Before the Choir decides a dead child is worth more to them as a martyr than the girl who caused her death is worth as a prisoner. Pack light, Dominic Coldharbour. We go under the mountains."

Outside, on the rooftop opposite, a raven waited in the dark, and when Dominic met its eye it tilted its head and spoke in the voice of the city's whole listening flock:

*They know your name now, speaker. All of them. Even the ones under the mountain.*
