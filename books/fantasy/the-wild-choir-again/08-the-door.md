## 8. The Door

The mountains did not want them there.

Dominic felt it the way she felt weather coming, a pressure behind her teeth, in the roots of her hair. The range above the dead country rose in black ridges like the hackles of some animal too large to name, and every switchback trail they climbed seemed to narrow by deliberate degrees, as though the stone itself were closing its throat against them.

Bracken led. He had led for three days now, ever since they'd crossed the salt flats where the ward-stag's ghost-memory first came to Dominic—not as a vision, not exactly, but as a warmth at the base of her skull, a voice with no breath behind it: *east, and under*. The stag had died naming the Choir, and it seemed death had not finished its sentence. It walked beside her now the way old grief walks beside a person, mostly silent, occasionally impossible to ignore.

*East, and under,* it said again, when Bracken paused at a cleft in the rock no wider than a coffin.

"He wants us to go in there," Sable said. She stood with her single hand resting on her knife hilt, grey cloak stiff with dried sweat, looking up at the mountain's face rather than into the crack. Her eyes were doing the thing they did—reading a place the way other people read a page.

"The stag wants it," Dominic said. "I don't know what I want."

"That's how you know it's honest." Sable shouldered through the gap without waiting, which was how Sable did most things.

The passage swallowed their lantern light whole. The walls were not natural rock; Dominic ran her palm along one and felt grooves cut in spirals, thousands of them, overlapping, so that the stone was less carved than *written on*, some script older than any alphabet she knew. And the walls were warm. Not sun-warm. Body-warm, like pressing a hand to the flank of something sleeping.

"It's breathing," she said quietly.

"Vaults don't breathe."

"This isn't a vault yet."

The stag's memory pulsed agreement, faint and sad, and Dominic followed Sable down into the dark with Bracken's fur brushing her knee, three creatures descending together into the throat of the world.

They heard the Door before they saw it.

It was not singing, exactly—or rather, it was what singing wanted to be when it forgot mercy. A chord held for centuries, sustained by crystal, and underneath it, woven through it, voices. Dominic stopped walking. Bracken pressed against her legs, trembling, and she realized with a lurch that she could *hear names* inside that sound. Not words. Names. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each thread of the harmony was someone, or something—a fox's quicksilver thought, an old woman's lullaby-hum, rain on a roof, a child laughing, all of it pulled out of the world and stretched taut and made to hold a note forever.

"Oh," she said. It came out very small. "Oh, Sable. They're *in there.*"

Sable had gone the color of ash. She stood staring down the final corridor, where the passage opened into a cavern vast enough to swallow Vell entire, and at its heart—

The Door was a door the way a cathedral is a box. It rose from the cavern floor in tiers of white crystal, each tier singing a different register of that terrible chord, and set into the crystal like flies in amber were seeds of light. Stars. Dozens of them. The stolen stars of Vell, dimmed and dormant, drifting slowly in their crystal cells like dandelion seeds caught in ice.

Dominic had imagined rage would come first. What came instead was recognition—the way you recognize your own handwriting—and beneath it a grief so wide it had no edges. Every night of her childhood, the sky over Vell had been a lid. She had lain in the hayloft counting stars and feeling lucky to live somewhere the heavens were so close. They had been close. They had been *imprisoned* forty miles from her bed, singing to each other through crystal, and she had slept soundly under their absence and called it home.

"They took the sky," she whispered, "to keep us small."

"They took everything to keep everyone small," Sable said. "The magic. The beasts' voices. The stars. You don't build a cage this size because you're afraid of wolves, Dom. You build it because you're afraid of *everyone.*"

Bracken growled low, a sound Dominic felt more than heard, aimed not at the Door but at the corridor behind them. Footsteps. Many. The Choir had never been far; the Choir had been *waiting*, the way a spider waits at the center of its own web, patient as architecture.

But before the white robes came around the bend, Sable did something Dominic had never seen her do.

She sat down. Right there on the cold stone, her back against the wall, her one hand open on her knee, and she began to speak—not to Dominic, not entirely, but to the cavern, to the singing crystal, to whatever part of herself she had carried unopened for twenty years.

"I guarded this place," Sable said. "Did you know that? Did I ever tell you why I've got one arm?"

"You said a warden's work takes pieces of you."

"A warden's work takes pieces of you." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as old paper. "That was true, and it was also the lie I hid inside. I lost the arm the day I tried to stop a transfer. There was a stag—you'd have liked him, he was vain about his antlers—they were pulling his voice out to feed the vault. He fought. I fought. A cantor's discipline took my arm to the elbow and my courage with it, and after that—" She looked at the Door. "After that I stood post here for six years. Six years, Dom. I listened to that chord every hour of every shift, and I told myself it was beautiful. That was the trick of it. They make it beautiful so you don't have to hear what's underneath. I heard what was underneath. I heard it every day. And I looked away, because looking is where complicity starts, and I wanted so badly to stop looking."

The chord swelled, or perhaps Dominic only heard it differently now. Inside it, faint as a heartbeat, she found the stag again—the *other* stag, the vain one, the one whose ghost-memory had led her east and under. His note was still in there. He had never left. His voice had been holding up this ceiling of silence for twenty years while the rest of him died in the hills.

"Sable." Dominic knelt in front of her. "You're telling me this now, with them coming, because—"

"Because you need to understand what you're walking into." Sable's grey eyes were clear in a way Dominic had never seen them, scoured clean. "You think the Choir is the Cantor and his robes and his inquisitors. It isn't. It's an appetite, and we all fed it. The villagers who reported neighbors. The elders who built the curfew. Me, standing post. Even you, Dom—even you, loving the beasts in secret instead of screaming it from the rooftops. We were all quiet in our own comfortable ways, and the quiet is what they *eat*. I'm not telling you this to break you. I'm telling you so that when you stand in front of that thing, you know the corruption isn't a villain you can kill. It's a hunger you have to refuse. In yourself first. That's the only place anyone can refuse it."

Boots in the corridor. White hemlines rounding the stone. And ahead, down the long slope of the cavern floor, a figure detached itself from the base of the Door—tall, robed in white shot through with gold, moving with the unhurried grace of a man who has never once been interrupted.

The High Cantor stopped ten paces away, and when he spoke, his voice harmonized with the vault behind him, two notes at once, gentle and appalling.

"Daughter of Vell," he said. "Keeper of the wild tongue. We have waited a long time for you. Come. There is a place prepared beside me, and a world we could keep safe together—orderly, quiet, and *sung.*"

Behind him, the stars turned slowly in their crystal cells, and Dominic felt the stag's memory press close against her mind like a hand on her shoulder, and Bracken's growl rise through the soles of her boots, and somewhere deep in the singing crystal a note wavered—just one, thin and desperate, like a voice calling from a locked room—

and every beast she had ever spoken to, every creature from the valley to the salt flats, lifted its head at once and turned east, toward the door, waiting for her to begin.
