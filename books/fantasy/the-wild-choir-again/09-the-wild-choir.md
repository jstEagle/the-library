## 9. The Wild Choir

The Door was not a door.

Dominic had imagined it for weeks—brass and iron, hinges, a lock, some great keyhole the size of a cart wheel. What she found instead beneath the mountains was an absence: a cavern so vast that her lantern light gave out before it touched any wall, and at its center a pillar of crystal rising from floor to unseen ceiling, singing. Not metaphorically. The stone itself hummed a chord so pure and so patient that Dominic felt it in her back teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the soft wet chambers of her heart. It was the sound the wards of Vell must have made, once, before they shattered like glass over her head.

And inside the crystal, caged in light, were the stars.

She knew them the way she knew Bracken's heartbeat under her palm. They hung in the vault's depths like seeds in amber—hundreds of them, thousands, each one a knot of silver fire no bigger than a fist, drifting slow as dandelion fluff through the singing stone. Some pulsed gently, dreaming. Others beat against their prisons with a rhythm that made her chest ache, frantic and small, like birds thrown against windows.

"They've been down here since before your grandmother's grandmother," said the High Cantor.

He stood on a stair of white marble that spiraled up into the dark, robed in white so clean it hurt to look at, his hands folded, his voice pitched to carry without effort—as if he'd spent a lifetime learning to fill rooms this size. His face was mild. That was the worst of it. He looked like a man who might have fixed fences, or taught children their letters. Behind him, ranked along the stair and the gallery above, stood the Choir: dozens of them, white-robed, silent, their harmonies held in reserve like drawn blades.

Beside her, Sable had gone very still. Her single hand rested on Bracken's ruff, and the wolf's hackles were up, a low growl rolling out of him like distant weather.

"You're the one who took them," Dominic said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm the one who *keeps* them." The Cantor descended three steps, unhurried. "There's a difference, child, and it matters. Do you know what magic is? Truly? Everyone believes it's power. Power is only its shadow. Magic is *voice*. Every wild thing has one—the stag, the crow, the wolf, the girl who listens. Left loose, voices quarrel. Voices war. We have seen what happens when every beast and every gifted human sings their own song at once." He gestured upward, toward the vanished sky. "We gathered them. We harmonized them. We built Vell to keep the loudest voice of all safe from herself."

"Vell was a cage."

"Vell was a *cradle*." His smile was gentle, and it did not reach his eyes, which were busy—always busy, measuring, counting. "You healed a wolf there when you were eleven years old. Did you know we knew? Of course you didn't. The wards reported everything. We watched you grow, Dominic Coldharbour. We watched you speak to sheep and sparrows and never once turn cruel. You are the finest argument for our work that has ever lived—a wild voice raised gentle, in perfect captivity." He spread his hands. "So I offer you what we offered no one else. A place beside me. Order. Safety. A world where nothing wild needs fearing, because nothing wild will ever again be free to fear *us*. Sing with us, and your wolf grows old in a warm barn. Sing with us, and no village will ever burn a gift-marked child. Sing with us, and you will never again have to watch stars go dark and pretend it isn't your fault."

The last word landed like a hook set in flesh.

Because she had wondered. In the dead country, in Ashfall, walking roads that looped back on themselves, she had lain awake and thought: *if I had spoken sooner—if I hadn't hidden—would the miller's boy still be alive? Would Sable still have both arms?*

The Cantor saw it land. She watched him see it, watched the mildness of his face adjust by a fraction, a craftsman checking his work.

"That guilt," he said softly, "is the first honest thing the wild ever gives you. We can take it away."

Beside her, Sable exhaled slowly through her nose. "Don't," she murmured, barely a breath. "Whatever he offers—it's bought with everyone else's silence. I paid that price for twenty years. It doesn't get cheaper."

The Cantor's eyes flicked to her with something almost like pity. "Warden Sable. You guarded this place and looked away. You know better than anyone what the alternative costs. Tell her how many villages burned while you kept your comfortable shame."

"I remember every one," Sable said. Her voice didn't shake. "That's why I'm here. That's why she's going to open your pretty cage, and you're going to stand there and let her, because deep down—in the part of you the crystal can't reach—you're tired too. Aren't you? All those years holding the harmony. Don't tell me you've never wondered what your own voice sounds like alone."

For one heartbeat—one—the High Cantor's composure cracked, and behind the crack Dominic heard something vast and exhausted and terribly lonely.

Then it sealed shut, and he sighed, and raised one hand, and the Choir began to sing.

It came down the stair like a flood. Harmony upon harmony, layered and seamless, the same music that had made villagers weep and confess invented sins, the same that had played beneath the miller's boy's execution while his mother screamed into the muffling air. It wrapped around Dominic's mind like cold water, seeking the shape of her will to dissolve it. Bracken's growl broke apart into a yelp. Beside her Sable dropped to one knee, teeth gritted, pressing her palm flat against the stone as if the mountain itself might hold her upright.

Dominic stood in the middle of the flood and listened.

That was the thing he had never understood—she realized it now, standing in the heart of everything stolen—that was the flaw running through all that perfect architecture like a seam of rotten rock. The Choir sang *at* people. They had spent centuries mastering harmony, but harmony was not the same as listening. They had never once, in all their long history, been quiet enough to hear an answer.

Dominic closed her eyes and spoke the way she had spoken since she was small enough to fit under a ewe's belly. Not words, exactly. Not even quite sound. The old language underneath language, the one that ran through fur and feather and root.

*Bracken. Little sister-rats of Ashfall. Ravens on the mountain road. Sheep of Vell, whose names I know. Stag—who died telling me the truth. Every creature I ever sat beside in the dark and listened to. I need you now.*

The crystal pillar sang on. The Choir's harmony pressed down. And then, from far away—from everywhere—the answer came.

It started as a tremor in the floor. Then a rustle in the black throat of the tunnel behind them: rats, hundreds of them, pouring out of the dark with their whiskers trembling, followed by bats unspooling from crevices like smoke given intent. Somewhere above, ravens began to scream down the mountain passes. And deeper than all of it, fainter, arriving across distances no message should cross—sheep bawling in a valley two hundred miles away, dogs lifting their heads in ruined Ashfall, wolves answering from forests she had never seen. The ward-stag's ghost-memory rose in her like warmth in winter, lending its low bell-tone to the gathering.

They couldn't all come. Most of them never would stand in this cavern. But they could *sing*, each in their own voice, and their voices poured into her the way water pours into dry ground—not harmony, not order, nothing the Choir's trained ears could predict or counter—but a wild chord, dissonant and alive, a chord no single throat could hold and none needed to.

Dominic opened her eyes and walked toward the crystal.

The Choir's song redoubled, trying to bend around hers, and failed, because hers had no center to seize. She put both hands against the singing stone. It was cold and it was burning and inside it the caged stars pressed toward her palms like children at a window.

"You don't understand what you'll unleash," the Cantor called, and for the first time his voice cracked along its polished grain. "Magic without walls! Beast-voices in every human ear! There will be chaos—"

"There already is," Dominic said. "You just buried the sound of it."

She drew breath—and Bracken howled, and the rats shrieked, and the ravens tore the dark above, and Sable, kneeling, added something raw and wordless and human that might have been grief or might have been joy—and Dominic sang the wild choir.

The crystal did not shatter. It *exhaled*. Ten thousand seams of light opened at once, and the stars came out like a flock released, streaming upward past the galleries, past the Choir—who fell silent, every one of them, white faces upturned, caught between horror and something dangerously close to relief—past the High Cantor standing alone on his stair with his empty hands finally, finally unfolded.

The seeds of light poured out through the mountain's veins, through the broken border of Vell, up through soil and stone and the roots of sleeping trees, and burst into the night sky one after another after another until the darkness overhead was stitched silver again, more stars than the old maps had ever named, and one of them—low, near the horizon, unmistakably—pulsed twice, like something winking awake.

Magic went with them. Dominic felt it leave her own chest in a long, shuddering wave—her gift loosening, spreading, spilling into the world like ink into water. Somewhere in Vell a shepherd would wake tomorrow and half-understand his dog's warning growl. Somewhere a child would hear her own name in a crow's caw. Nothing wild would be safe from being understood ever again. Nothing wild would be caged, either.

The cavern rang with silence afterward, a different silence than before—full instead of hollow.

The High Cantor descended the last steps and stood before her, an ordinary man in an ordinary robe, looking older than mountains and lighter than she had expected. He studied her for a long moment.

"What do we sing now?" he asked, and it was not accusation. It was almost hope.

Dominic thought of home—of the valley that had been her cradle and her cage, of neighbors who would forgive her and neighbors who never would, of a farm with a crooked gate and a wolf growing old in the sun. She thought about how a life was not a vault you locked, but a song you chose, note by note, every morning.

"Now?" she said. "Now you learn to listen."

Bracken pressed warm against her leg. Above, through the shaft the stars had climbed, the new sky was coming up like a tide.

They walked out of the mountain together, and behind them, faint but unmistakable, the High Cantor cleared his throat—and began, very quietly, very badly, to try.

* * *

Dawn found them on the high road above Vell, where the land dropped away in folds of grey-green mist and the valley lay below like a cupped hand. Sable leaned on her spear-staff, breathing hard from the climb, watching the sun come up over fields that no longer hummed with anything but wind.

"Well," she said. "No walls. No wards. No idea what happens next."

"That's the point," Dominic said.

Below, at the valley's edge, the first villagers were emerging from their doors, staring up at a sky they didn't recognize, pointing at the star that winked. Someone was ringing the Founding Festival bell out of season—wildly, joyfully, off-key. Bracken bounded ahead down the switchbacks, tail high, and turned at the bottom to wait for them, the way he always had, the way he always would.

Dominic took a breath of the free, unwarded air and started down toward home.
