## 10. First Citizen

Three years after the stars came back, the first thing a traveler saw on the road into Vell was not a wall, because there was no wall, and had not been since Dominic sang.

There was a gate, though. Everyone agreed it was important to have a gate. It was built of pale mountain ash and stood open day and night, wide as two carts, with no door in its frame at all — just the suggestion of one, an arch that said *you may pass* rather than *you may not*. The carpenter who raised it, old Maren Ostrey whose son had died in the Choir's singing, had wept while she carved the lintel. She'd carved into the wood, small enough that you had to lean close to read it: **NOTHING HERE IS KEPT.**

Dominic passed under it every morning on her way to the high pasture, and every morning she touched the lintel with two fingers, the way you'd touch the shoulder of a friend you weren't ready to speak to yet. Some mornings she spoke anyway. "Morning, Maren," she'd say to the wood, knowing Maren could not hear her from the village below, and knowing too that grief like hers didn't need hearing so much as it needed carving.

The valley had changed less than strangers expected and more than its people admitted. The fields were still fields; the sheep still went stupidly up the hill in spring and came down fat in autumn. But where the wards had run — that faint silver shimmer only beasts had ever truly seen, the cage-light that had hummed along Vell's borders for four hundred years — wild things now crossed freely. Foxes trotted down the lanes at dusk without fear or hunger for the henhouses, because the hens, who had their own opinions now, negotiated. A bear had taken up residence in the old millrace the first winter and nobody had moved him out. They'd simply built the new mill upstream and named the bear something dignified — Aldous — and left fish scraps for him on feast days, which he accepted with the weary tolerance of any neighbor of long standing.

The beasts talked more now. That was the strangest part, the part Dominic still hadn't grown used to: magic scattered was magic shared, and the sharing had gone both directions. Not everyone could hear them. But more could each year — children mostly, born after the singing, who babbled to crows before they could walk properly and told their mothers, matter-of-factly, what the rain intended to do tomorrow. The mothers found this either delightful or terrifying depending on the mother. There were arguments about it at council meetings. Real arguments, loud ones, the kind Vell had never been allowed to have when the elders ruled by curfew and silence.

Dominic sat on the council now. She hadn't wanted the seat; she'd been voted into it the way weather is voted into a season, by general agreement and over her objections. Her contribution was mostly translation. When the river said its banks would fail by the willow stand if nobody cleared the gravel, Dominic carried the message down, and the council argued about whether rivers could be trusted, and eventually they cleared the gravel, and the flood came exactly where the river said it would, and after that the arguments grew shorter.

She kept her father's farm, too. Someone had to, and the land remembered her hands. The sheepfold where Sable had collapsed all those years ago — bleeding, one-armed, pursued by a thing that left no tracks — was rebuilt twice its old size now, and in its western corner there was a flat stone where nothing was written but where everyone knew to leave an offering in hard times: bread, a ribbon, once a whole wheel of cheese that the crows found hilarious.

Some neighbors forgave her. Some never would. She had stopped expecting the second kind to become the first, and she had stopped apologizing to them, too — not out of pride, but because she'd learned that some griefs are load-bearing walls, and knocking on them doesn't help anyone. Tam Hollis, whose boy had been taken in the first year of the Choir's occupation, still turned his cart off the road when he saw her coming. He did it silently, without malice, every time, and every time Dominic bowed her head slightly, acknowledging the wound without pretending to heal it. You could not sing someone's dead back to them. She had tried, in the early days, with the whole wild choir behind her voice, and the answer had come back the same way it always comes back: the dead stay dead, even in a world made generous.

But others — most others, if she counted honestly — had found their way to something gentler. The Widow Crane, who had once spat at her feet outside the chapel, now brought her honeycomb every solstice without a word, setting it on the fencepost and walking away quickly, as if embarrassed by her own kindness. Children ran to Dominic in the market to report what the dogs had dreamed. Old men who had spent their whole lives under the Choir's harmonies, weeping through invented sins, now sat on benches arguing cheerfully about whether the wind was gossiping or just showing off.

And the stars. That was the thing she returned to, always, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

The sky above Vell was crowded again — not with the old stars, the tame fixed lights the Choir had stolen and stored as seeds beneath the mountains, but with the scattered descendants of those seeds, flung back into the dark by the chord she and the beasts had sung together. They were brighter than the old sky had been. Wilder. Some of them moved, slowly, on errands of their own. And one of them — low in the northern sky, above the notch in the mountains where the Door lay sealed forever under a hundred tons of deliberate collapse — answered when spoken to.

Dominic discovered it the first winter back, lying in the high pasture with Bracken's grey muzzle resting on her chest, both of them watching the cold stars breathe. She'd said, half to herself, *I wonder if you remember being small*, the way she said everything now, aloud, having spent too many years learning the cost of silence.

And the star had flickered. Not twinkled — flickered, deliberately, twice, the way Bracken flicked his ear when he was listening.

She'd sat up so fast the wolf had grumbled. Then she'd spoken to it properly, in the speech that was not words but meaning, the speech that worked on wolves and stags and rivers and, apparently, on light itself.

*Who are you?*

*Small,* the star had answered, in a voice like frost forming on glass. *Growing. Like you.*

They had talked, after that, on clear nights, when she walked the pasture line. The star told her almost nothing about itself except that it was warm and far away and glad to be out. Glad. That was the word it kept returning to, the way a child returns to a favorite stone. *Glad to be out.* Every star in the new sky said some version of it, if you listened long enough. Four hundred years in a crystal vault, hoarded by men in white robes who believed wonder was safer locked away — and now the whole sky was saying *glad, glad, glad*, in voices too distant for most people to hear.

Dominic heard them. She carried it the way she carried everything: privately, heavily, gratefully.

* * *

Sable came through the valley twice a year, spring and autumn, walking the roads she maintained for wages that were honest and modest and entirely sufficient. The road-work suited her. There was something in the mending of broken surfaces that asked exactly what she had to give: patience, stubbornness, a tolerance for weather, and no requirement whatsoever to explain herself. She traveled with a crew of three other ex-wardens — all of whom had looked away from the Door's cruelty in their time, all of whom had chosen, like her, to spend the rest of their lives making straight what had been crooked.

She stayed at the farm when she passed through. She slept in the loft, still, though the hayloft held no inquisitors anymore and never would. On her first evening she always sat on the porch step with Bracken's head in her lap — the wolf was fourteen now, moving stiffly in the cold, his muzzle gone the color of ash — and scratched his ears and said nothing for a long while. Their friendship had never required many words. It had begun in silence, two survivors recognizing each other across a sheepfold, and silence remained its native tongue.

"You've heard from Ashfall," Dominic said, on an autumn evening three years in, watching the priest's city glow faintly on the horizon where it clung to its cliffs. Even at this distance, on certain nights, you could see the star-shrines burning — not worship anymore, exactly, but gratitude. The gentle priest who had offered sanctuary at the price of Dominic's gift had renounced the price, after the Door fell, and now led a congregation that sang badly and meant it beautifully.

"They're rebuilding the aqueduct," Sable said. "Properly, this time. No vaults underneath." She paused. "They want you to visit. He asks every season."

"I know."

"He'll keep asking."

"I know that too." Dominic turned her cup in her hands. "Tell him — tell him I'm glad the city remembers its own name now. Tell him I'll come when the road brings me, and not before."

Sable snorted softly. "You sound like your star."

"I am like my star."

Bracken sighed enormously between them, and they laughed, and the evening settled around the farmhouse like a bird folding its wings.

* * *

On the last night of autumn, with the first snow already falling soft as ashfall in reverse — falling *up*, almost, in the way of snow seen by firelight — Dominic climbed the high pasture alone. Bracken had stopped making the climb with her this year; his hips were old, and he'd watched her go from the doorway with the patient dignity of an elder seeing off the young, which was its own quiet heartbreak, because she could hear him thinking it and he knew she could.

At the crest she sat on the flat stone where she'd healed her first wolf, all those years ago, on the eve of a festival, under a sky that was about to go dark. She spoke to the north star.

*I'm staying,* she said. *I want you to know that. People keep assuming I'll go — the capital, the coast, wherever the wild things gather thickest now. They think I'm restless. I'm not.*

*No,* the star agreed, frost-voiced, amused. *You're rooted.*

*Is that strange? To spend your whole life breaking out of a cage, and then choose to live inside the same valley?*

The star considered this with the unhurried attention of something that had waited four hundred years in the dark and was in no danger of rushing ever again.

*A cage is a place you cannot leave,* it said at last. *A home is a place you can. The shape is the same. Everything else is different.*

Below her, Vell glowed with lamplight. Somewhere down there Maren Ostrey was carving a second gate for the south road, because the village had grown past the first one. Somewhere Tam Hollis drove his cart home under a sky full of gladness he couldn't hear, and the Widow Crane set out honeycomb for a morning that hadn't come yet, and the bear called Aldous slept in the millrace with fish bones in his beard, and children dreamed in crow-talk, and the river muttered its plans, and the gate stood open, open, open, with nothing behind it that was kept.

Dominic touched the flat stone the way she touched Maren's lintel — two fingers, a greeting, a promise.

"Tomorrow," she said to the valley, to the star, to herself. "I'll decide again tomorrow."

That was the whole secret, she thought, turning back down through the snow toward the lamplight. Not the singing. Not the Door. Not even the chord that had unmade four hundred years of theft in a single breath. The secret was smaller and harder and had to be done fresh every morning, like bread:

Home was no longer a place she was trapped in.

It was a place she chose.

She chose it, and walked down into the light, and the star above the mountains flickered twice — *heard, glad* — and followed her all the way home.
