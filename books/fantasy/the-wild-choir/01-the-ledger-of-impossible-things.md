## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There are three rules to keeping accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is to say: by breaking them.

Rule one: never round down. A griffin feather is not *approximately* a griffin feather, and if you write "twelve" when the manifest says "eleven and a half," some clerk three floors up will assume you've pocketed half a feather, and then you'll spend an afternoon explaining that no one can pocket half a feather because the other half is still attached, in most cases, to a very annoyed griffin.

Rule two: never ask where things come from. The bottled thunder arrives in crates stenciled with a mountain range no cartographer will admit to. The moon-tears come in vials the color of regret. The Guild pays, the suppliers vanish, and the ledger stays clean. Clean ledgers are the whole religion of this place.

Rule three — and this is the one that matters, the one I have kept for eleven years without a single lapse — rule three is: *never let them hear you listening.*

I'll come back to that one.

It was the third of Thawmonth, and the counting house smelled of ink, tallow, and the particular ozone tang of a thunder-jar that had cracked somewhere between the loading dock and my desk. Again. The third one this season. I made a note in the margin — *jar 44, hairline fracture, contents degraded to a sulk* — because even spoiled lightning has to be accounted for. The Guild does not forgive losses; it merely itemizes them.

My desk sits in the corner by the high window, where the light comes through glass thick with salt residue and falls in a gray wash over everything, like the whole room has been left out in weather. From there I can see the tally-boards, the iron cages on their shelf, the long bench where Master Corvane's clerks scratch away like a rookery of very well-fed crows. And from there, if I am honest — and the ledger requires honesty, so let us practice it — from there I can hear everything.

That is my secret, the one under all the others. Not that I steal, or lie, or keep a second set of books (though I could, and better than anyone). My secret is smaller and stranger than that: I can hear animals speak.

Not "hear sounds animals make." Anyone can hear a dog bark. I mean I hear them *talk* — words, arguments, grievances, jokes, the whole chattering parliament of the nonhuman world, arriving in my ears as plainly as Corvane's wheeze or the strike of the noon bell. A horse complaining about its bit. Pigeons debating the architectural merit of a new eave. Rats — always rats — conducting their endless, meticulous audits of the city's garbage, and finding it wanting.

I have never told a living soul. Do you know what the Guild does with impossible things? It doesn't celebrate them. It *acquires* them. There is a ledger for people too, though it isn't called that. There's a shelf in the vault below us with jars on it, and I try very hard not to think about what's in some of them. A girl who hears beasts speak would be an acquisition. A valuable one. She would be catalogued, crated, stenciled with someone else's mountains.

So I keep rule three. I hear, and I do not answer, and I do not flinch, and I balance the books.

* * *

The morning's work was reconciliation — the quarterly inventory against the manifests, which is accounting's version of digging a grave and counting the bones. Griffin feathers: eleven and a half, condition fair, two molted, remainder plucked under protest (the protest was noted in the supplier's hand; the griffin's own commentary was not available to him, poor creature, since his buyer couldn't hear it).

Bottled thunder: forty jars received, three fractured in transit, contents degraded. One jar — jar nineteen — was humming to itself on the shelf. I don't mean buzzing. I mean humming, low and tuneless, the way a man hums when he thinks he's alone. Storms get bored in those jars. Eleven years I've known that, and eleven years I have written *contents stable* in a steady clerk's hand while something ancient and weathered sang to itself three feet from my elbow.

"Mirabel." Master Corvane, at my shoulder, smelling of pepper and disapproval. He is a narrow man, Corvane, like a door that has been shut too many times. "The Voss requisition. Where is it?"

"Being copied now, sir." It was being copied now. It had been being copied for an hour, because Serane Voss's requisitions are always wrong and always furious about being corrected. "She's asked for thirty jars of thunder. We show twenty-six serviceable."

"Then she gets twenty-six and an apology."

"She'll want thirty."

Corvane looked at me over his spectacles with the expression of a man who has spent forty years being right and resents every minute of it. "Then she will learn, as we all did, that the world does not expand to fit her requisitions. Copy it. Initial it. Next."

He moved off toward the tally-boards, and I bent over my page, and that was when the songbird started begging.

* * *

They keep birds in the counting house. Officially: for quality-testing — a canary near the thunder-jars, finches near certain volatile reagents, the old mine-shaft logic. Unofficially, I think someone simply likes having something alive in this building besides clerks. The cages sit on the shelf beneath the high window, and in the smallest cage, in a cage honestly too small for it, there is a songbird the color of a struck match, and it has been talking at me for three days.

Songbirds, if you have never heard one speak, talk the way water runs — quickly, brightly, looping back on itself. This one had been saying, in essence, the same thing for seventy-two hours:

*Small one. Small one with the sad hands. The wire is cold. The seed is old. The others went through the ceiling and I did not fit through the bars, I tried, I tried until my wings ached, small one, open the little door, please, please, the sky is doing something today, I can feel it doing something—*

And I had written *contents stable* and said nothing, because rule three, because eleven years, because the alternative is a jar with my name on the label.

But today the bird had changed its tune. Today it said: *Something is coming. The rats are running the wrong direction. Even the rats are frightened, and rats are never frightened, they're only busy.*

Rats are never frightened. They're only busy. I nearly laughed — a real laugh, the kind that starts somewhere behind the ribs — and I turned it into a cough, and Corvane glanced over, and I bent lower over the ledger.

Here is what concealment costs, and no one tells you this: it isn't the fear. Fear becomes furniture after a while. It's the *answering*. Every word you swallow sits somewhere. I have swallowed ten thousand answers. When a dray-horse curses its driver I nod as if I were thinking about column sums. When the warehouse cats hold court on the loading dock I keep my face smooth as a closed ledger. When the songbird begs me — me specifically, me with the sad hands — I copy out figures in a beautiful, dead, correct hand, and the swallowed words pile up inside me like undelivered letters, and I begin to suspect that a person can drown in her own unposted mail.

I am good at my job. That is the worst part. I am *excellent* at my job, and my job is to make the impossible countable, and I am so very good at making myself countable too — one clerk, value: sufficient, condition: stable — that sometimes I wonder whether there's anything left of me that hasn't been entered in somebody's book.

The bird said: *Please.*

I looked at the cage. The bird looked at me. Its head tipped, that quick mechanical tilt, one eye fixed on me like a black bead of absolute attention, and I swear on the ledger itself that it knew. Birds know things they shouldn't. Perhaps it saw it in my face — the terrible softness I keep folded away with my best pen, brought out only when no one's watching.

*You hear me,* it said. Not a question. *You've heard me for days. Your ears go soft when I talk. Everyone else's ears stay stone.*

"I don't know what you—" I began, under my breath, and stopped.

Because I had answered. Aloud. Three words, barely voiced, barely breath — but aloud, in a counting house full of clerks, with Master Corvane within earshot and the noon bell still twenty minutes off.

* * *

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. Corvane was dictating to a junior clerk; the quills scratched on; the thunder-jar hummed its bored storm-song; the world continued in its orderly, itemized way. Three whispered words are nothing. A cough. A clerk muttering over her columns.

But I sat there with my heart going like a trapped moth, and the bird sat on its perch with its bead-eye bright, and between us stretched the smallest, most enormous silence of my life.

*I knew it,* the bird said softly, and its voice had lost the frantic water-rush. It was almost gentle. *Oh, small one. How long?*

"Eleven years," I breathed. I didn't mean to say it. It came out the way tears come out — without permission, without dignity.

*Eleven years,* the bird repeated, tasting it. *In a cage?*

I looked at my desk. At the ledger. At the neat gray light and the tally-boards and the iron shelf and the door with its lock that locks from the outside.

"Yes," I said. "Something like that."

The bird was quiet for a moment, which for a songbird is roughly equivalent to a philosopher's lifetime of contemplation. Then it said: *Open the little door.*

"I can't."

*You can. Your hands open things. I watch them all day, opening and closing, writing, unlocking the drawer. Hands like yours should be ashamed, keeping themselves locked too.*

"That's—" Absurd. Presumptuous. True. "If I open that cage, they'll notice. A missing bird is a line in a ledger. Everything here is a line in a ledger, and lines get investigated."

*Then don't open it,* said the bird, with terrifying reasonableness. *Just tell me what the sky is doing. You have a window. Describe it to me. That's all. That's a payment anyone can afford.*

So I looked out the window, and I described it. The thaw-mist coming off the roofs. The light going from gray to the color of weak honey. A line of geese far up, arrowing north, and — this I added before I could stop myself — arguing about the route, because they were, the lead goose insisting on the river way and her second-in-command holding out for the coast, and the whole skein rippling with the argument like a ribbon shaken loose.

The bird listened with its eyes half-closed, its throat working, and when I finished it sighed — a small, complete, satisfied sigh, the sigh of a creature who has been given exactly enough.

*Thank you,* it said. *You see? You already pay debts to the wild. You just do it secretly, like a criminal. Like a lover.*

"I'm neither."

*No,* it agreed pleasantly. *Not yet.*

I bent back over the ledger. My figures had gone crooked while I wasn't looking at them — the first crooked figures of my career — and I straightened them, stroke by stroke, and told myself the last quarter hour had been a lapse, an anomaly, a rounding error in the person of Mirabel Sandoval, clerk, value: sufficient, condition: stable.

But my hands weren't quite steady, and the bird began to sing — really sing, the old bright cascade, as if describing the sky had refilled some tank inside it — and beneath the singing it murmured, just for me:

*Something is coming, small one. The rats are running the wrong direction. Whatever it is, it will need someone who listens.*

I wrote *contents stable* beside jar nineteen, and for the first time in eleven years, my pen hesitated over the word *stable*, and I wondered — with the particular dread and hunger of a woman balancing someone else's books — how much longer the accounts of my own life could bear the entries I was forcing into them.

The noon bell rang. The clerks rose for bread. And down at the far end of the building, faint but unmistakable, someone began pounding on the salt door — the back entrance, the one nobody uses, the one that isn't supposed to open for anyone at all.

The bird stopped singing.

*There,* it said. *That. I told you something was coming.*

I put down my pen. Rule three lay in pieces around my chair, and I found — God help me — that I didn't mind.

I went to see who was knocking.
