## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Door

The salt door is called the salt door because it is made of salt. Not metaphorically — the Guild of Impossible Things does not deal in metaphor, which is one of its few honest qualities. Centuries ago, some founding treasurer decided that a threshold of pressed grey salt would keep out anything that shouldn't come in: spirits, debts of honor, and, presumably, people with bad news.

I mention this because on the fourth night of the tenth month, a man with bad news came through it anyway, and the salt didn't stop him, which should have been my first warning that the world was coming apart at its seams.

I was in the counting room, which is to say I was where I always am, hunched over the great ledger with a quill worn down to a nub and a lamp burning oil the Guild charged me for at cost. The autumn inventory was due. That means griffin feathers — eleven crates, three of them molted and therefore worth half — and a cask of bottled thunder from the Stormfields that had gone quiet in storage, which the ledger delicately terms "degraded stock" and which I privately suspected was just expensive, disappointed air.

I'd been reconciling columns for four hours. My eyes had gone to that soft, unfocused place where numbers stop meaning anything and start swimming like minnows. The candle guttered. Somewhere above me, in the rafters, the warehouse mice held one of their midnight parliaments — I could hear the scratch and squeak of it, the little arguments, the little treaties. I understood perhaps one word in ten. Enough to know they were complaining about the cats. Enough to know the cats were winning.

I want to be clear about something, since it matters later: I did not talk to them. I never talk to them. Talking back is the whole crime. Hearing is only half of it, and hearing, I told myself every night for eleven years, is not a thing anyone can prove.

I was lying to myself, of course. I was very good at it. Eleven years of practice.

So there I was, minding my columns, when the salt door groaned.

It's a heavy door. It takes two hands and a shoulder, and everyone who uses it uses it slowly, because salt grinds and complains the way old things do. This door did not open slowly. It banged inward with a crack like the bottled thunder finally making good on its promise, and the cold came in with it, and then the man.

He came through it the way a sack of turnips comes off a cart. He hit the floor. The salt crunched under him. And the first thing I noticed — before the blood, and there was a great deal of blood, I'll get to the blood — was that he was *wrong*. Wrong the way a note is wrong in a song you know by heart. He was soaked to the ribs in fenwater and something darker, his coat was scorched along one sleeve, and every part of him was shouting a different story: drowned, burned, cut, run for miles. He was a man the night had tried to kill in four ways and had only half succeeded at each.

The second thing I noticed was the smell of the fens on him. Marsh rot and reedmace and that green, living stink of the deep water. I knew that smell. I'd grown up two counties from it, before the Guild bought my silence along with my labor.

"Lock it," he said. His voice was mostly gone, scraped down to the wood. "Behind me. *Lock it.*"

I should have screamed for the night porter. I should have run for the wardens' stairs. I did neither, because my body had apparently decided, without consulting me, that this was a night for doing exactly the wrong things, and it moved me across the room and set my shoulder against the salt door and pushed it shut.

The bar was iron, older than the building. I dropped it into its brackets with a sound like a bell tolling for someone.

Then I turned around and looked at him properly, and the looking was not kind.

He was perhaps fifty, perhaps sixty, the kind of face that could be either depending on how much of it the years had eaten. His beard was grey and full of burrs. His hands — I remember the hands — were a mapmaker's hands, ink-stained at the nails, one of them crushed at two fingers, old damage, old story. And the new damage was a wound under his ribs that was soaking his coat black at a rate I didn't need a ledger to balance to know was fatal. He was bleeding out on the Guild's salt floor at roughly the speed of a leaking cask.

"You're the bookkeeper," he said.

It wasn't a question, which unsettled me more than the blood. "I'm the night clerk," I said. "There's a difference. Clerks can't authorize expenditures."

Something that was almost a laugh came out of him and turned into a cough, and the cough brought up blood, because of course it did. "Mirabel Sandoval," he said. "Daughter of Wren Sandoval. Of the Amberdale line, though they'd flay me for saying it."

The lamp flame stood very still. So did I.

My mother's name has not been said aloud in my hearing since I was nine years old. The Guild's records say *deceased, cause unrecorded, effects liquidated.* I have read that line perhaps a thousand times. I have never once believed it, the way you never quite believe a column that balances too neatly.

"Who are you?" I said. "Who told you that name?"

"No one told me." He was trying to get his back against the wall, and failing, and I found myself kneeling beside him and helping, my hands in his blood without my permission, because apparently my body was still making decisions on its own. "I've known it for twenty years. I made a promise to a man who could sing to trees. He said, *if it all goes wrong, find the daughter. The bookkeeper. She counts things. She'll understand what's missing.*"

"I count feathers," I said. "I count thunder. I don't—"

"You count *everything*," he said, and there was such ferocity in it, such sudden blazing certainty, that I shut my mouth. "That's the whole point of you. Someone has to know what was taken. Someone has to keep the account."

He fumbled at his coat with his ruined hand, and I helped him, and what came out of the inner pocket was wrapped in oilcloth and tied with sinew, and it was warm. I want you to understand that it was warm, warmer than his body had any right to make it, warm like something alive had been sleeping against his heart for a long time.

"Take it," he said.

"I can't take—"

"*Take it.*"

I took it. The oilcloth was slick with his blood. Underneath, I saw bone — a sheet of it, thin as vellum but unmistakably bone, scored with lines that were not ink. They were scored from the *inside*, if that makes any sense, as though the map had grown its own geography the way a shell grows its whorls. Rivers I didn't recognize. Mountains with names in a script that made my eyes water to follow. And at the center, a mark like a circle of small figures — I counted them without meaning to, because counting is what I do — nineteen marks, with two gaps, like teeth knocked out of a jaw.

"What is it?" I whispered.

"The Choir," he said. "What's left of it. Where they hid the pieces when the Guild started buying." His eyes were going glassy and I watched it happen and hated that I could do nothing, hated it with a specific, ledger-keeping hatred, the hatred of an error that cannot be corrected. "The Hollow Choir. The pact. The old song between the walls of the world. Your mother didn't die, girl. Your mother was *spent*. Put into the keeping of it, when it began to fail, and the Guild let everyone believe—"

And then he did the thing I will remember until I die, which is he grabbed my wrist with his crushed hand, with strength that should not have existed in him, and pulled my ear down to his mouth, and said:

"The Choir is starving."

Four words. He spent the last of his voice on four words, and then the strength went out of him all at once, like a lamp when the oil's gone, and he sagged against the wall, and his breathing went from ragged to slow to a long, thin whistle, and then to nothing at all.

I knelt there in the salt and the blood with a warm map of bone in my hands and a dead stranger's promise on my shoulders, and I did the only reasonable thing.

I checked his pulse. Twice. Because I am a bookkeeper, and bookkeepers verify.

* * *

Here is what I did not do, in the hour that followed, and I have had a great deal of time to be ashamed of the list:

I did not scream. I did not fetch the night porter, whose name was Hobbes and who slept with a cudgel under his cot and would have raised the whole house. I did not fetch the wardens, whose stairs were forty feet away and whose boots I could hear, sometimes, pacing the upper gallery — *tap, tap, tap*, like a metronome for a song nobody sang anymore.

What I did was sit on an upturned crate with the bone map in my lap and think, with terrible clarity, about what would happen next.

Because I knew what would happen next. Everyone in the Guild's employ knows. A stranger dies in the counting house; the wardens come; the wardens ask questions; and the questions are not the kind you answer. The Guild's enforcers do not investigate. They *audit*. And an audit, in the Guild's vocabulary, is when they count everything you are and everything you have, and decide which parts they get to keep.

I looked at the dead man. I looked at the map. I looked at my own hands, which had his blood on them, which had *always* had blood on them in a way, if you counted the ledgers, if you counted what the ledgers were *for* — the griffin feathers plucked from live birds because plucked feathers regrow and cut ones don't, the bottled thunder that was bottled *screaming* if you knew how to listen, and I knew how to listen, I had always known how to listen, and I had balanced the columns anyway, for eleven years, because the alternative was the road and the road was death for a girl alone with a secret.

Except a girl alone with a secret and a dead man's map is apparently a different animal entirely.

The map was warm in my lap. It had not cooled. I told myself it was residual heat. I told myself a great many things that night.

*The Choir is starving.*

I didn't know what the Hollow Choir was. That's the honest truth — I'd heard the phrase maybe twice in my life, both times from my mother, both times half-dreamed, a lullaby about a song with twenty-one singers where one voice was missing and the whole melody leaned into the gap like a person leaning on a cane. I'd thought it was a lullaby. I'd thought a great many things were lullabies.

But I knew what *starving* meant. Starving is a word from the ledgers. Starving is what happens when the inflows stop and the outflows don't, when you draw down the principal because there's nothing else, and everyone knows — *everyone who keeps accounts knows* — that a thing living on its principal is already dead, it just hasn't filed the paperwork.

And the Guild, the rats in the walls had been saying for months, in their squeaking, gossiping way that I pretended not to understand — the Guild had been *buying*. Buying relics. Buying bones. Buying songs in jars and feathers and thunder and every odd holy thing the fens and the mountains would sell, and locking it all in the deep vaults, and the deep vaults had no windows, and I had never once been permitted to audit them.

I had told myself it was hoarding. Guilds hoard. It's what guilds are *for*.

I was beginning, sitting on that crate in the small hours, to suspect it was something worse. I was beginning to suspect that the Guild was not collecting the impossible things.

It was eating them.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

The boots on the upper gallery had stopped. I hadn't noticed when. That was the thing about the wardens — you never noticed the silence start, you only noticed it had been going on too long, and by then it was too late, and the door was opening, and it was not the salt door.

It was the front door, the iron one, the one that opened from *their* side of the building, and it opened the way wardens open doors: without hurry, because hurry implies the outcome is in doubt.

There were three of them. I knew two by sight — Brannic, who was built like a piece of siege equipment and had once watched me for a full minute to see if I'd flinch (I hadn't; flinching is a ledger entry), and a woman called Ivet with grey eyes and a voice like a filing clerk, which was worse than Brannic, because Brannic hit people and Ivet *catalogued* them.

The third one I didn't know. He was new. New ones are always the most dangerous, because they still believe in the work.

"Night clerk," Ivet said. Not a greeting. A designation.

"Warden," I said. I stood up. I am proud of standing up. My knees were water and I stood up anyway. "There's been a death."

"We know," she said. "The salt door was barred from the inside. The wards on it sang when it broke. We came down the stairs." Her grey eyes moved over the room the way a hand moves over goods, pricing everything. They touched the body. They touched me. They touched the crate I'd been sitting on, and stopped. "You were sitting there."

"I was sitting there."

"For how long?"

"I don't keep accounts of my own time. The Guild doesn't pay for it."

Brannic made a sound that in a lesser building would have been a laugh. Ivet did not look at him. "Who was he?"

"I don't know."

"What did he say to you?"

I had rehearsed nothing. I had had an hour and rehearsed nothing, and this is where I learned the most useful and most terrible lesson of my life, which is that the truth, delivered flatly, in the voice you use for inventory, is the best lie there is. Because I *had* counted. Because I *had* kept the account.

"He said the Choir is starving," I said. "Then he died."

Silence. The new warden shifted. Brannic's hand had gone to his belt.

"Say it again," Ivet said.

"The Choir is starving." I let my voice do what it did over the griffin crates: bored, precise, faintly resentful at being kept from my work. "I assumed he was mad. Madmen come to the salt door sometimes. The salt is supposed to stop them. It doesn't. I've filed a complaint about the salt. Twice."

"Show me your hands."

I showed her my hands. There was blood on them. There was blood on everything, that was the entire situation; I had knelt beside a dying man because I was the only person in the building, which was true, and I let her look at the blood and I let her draw her own conclusions, which were the boring ones, and I watched her draw them, and I did not breathe until she was done.

"And the crate," she said. "You were sitting on a crate. Why?"

"Because standing up for an hour is tiring," I said, "and I was waiting for the wardens, who I could hear on the gallery, and who took," I consulted the ceiling, "a very long time to come down the stairs."

Brannic made his almost-laugh again. The new warden was looking at me with an expression I didn't like, an expression that said he was not yet bored enough by the world to take the boring conclusions. I filed his face away. I file everything away. It's the one gift the Guild gave me that they didn't mean to.

Ivet crouched by the body. She did the things wardens do — the pockets, the boots, the collar — and I watched her find nothing, because there was nothing to find, because the map was warm against my stomach where I had tucked it into my waistband under my apron, warm as a second heartbeat, and no one was going to find it because no one audits the night clerk, the night clerk *is* the audit.

Except the new one. The new one was still looking at me.

"Nothing on him," Ivet said, standing. "Robbery, maybe. Fen-runner. They kill each other over debts." She said it like a column total, and moved to go, and I let my shoulders come down half an inch, and that was when the new warden spoke for the first time.

"The salt door was barred," he said. His voice was young and careful. "Barred from inside. So either he barred it behind himself and then died of a wound he couldn't have gotten in this room, or—" and he looked at me, and finished, pleasantly, terribly, "—someone barred it for him. Someone who was already here. Someone who had time to sit down and wait."

The lamp flame stood very still again. It does that around me sometimes. I have never asked it why.

"I bar the door every night at the second bell," I said. "It's in the duty ledger. Page forty-one. You're welcome to read it." I picked up my quill. I picked up the griffin-feather inventory. I said, with the whole of my dead mother's stubbornness and none of my own, "Are we finished? The autumn count is due at dawn, and unlike the wardens, I answer to it."

Ivet looked at me for a long moment. Grey eyes, grey scales of judgment, weighing. Then: "You'll be available for further questions."

"I'm always available. I live above a counting house. It's not the life I'd have chosen, but the benefits are adequate."

They went. The iron door closed behind them with its soft, expensive click, and I sat back down on my crate, and my hands, which had been steady as a scale the whole time, began to shake so hard the quill chattered against the desk like a small trapped bird.

I let them shake. There was no one to see. That's the other thing I learned that night: a secret is just a performance with an audience of one, and the one is the hardest crowd of all.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the counting room's high window, the way it always does in that part of the city, filtered through salt-dust and the smoke of the tanneries. The dead man was gone by then — the porters had taken him to the cold room, and the cold room's paperwork would say *unidentified, fen-runner, no effects*, because I had written the interim report myself, and I had written it in my best hand, and it was a masterpiece of the true and the incomplete.

I stood at the great ledger with the autumn count open in front of me, and I could not make myself write.

The map was under my apron still. It had cooled at last — or I had stopped noticing the warmth, which amounts to the same thing in a life like mine. Nineteen marks and two gaps. *The Choir is starving.* Your mother was spent.

Above me, in the rafters, the mice were quiet. The cats had won, presumably. Or the mice had simply, like me, learned that some nights the only safe thing to do with your voice is keep it.

I looked at the ledger. Eleven years of columns. Griffin feathers, bottled thunder, everything the wild places had ever been made to give up, all of it neat, all of it balanced, all of it *mine*, my whole self folded into a job that would feed me until it used me up, and then enter me in its own pages as *deceased, cause unrecorded, effects liquidated.*

I thought about the new warden's face. I thought about how long *a very long time* would last before someone came back with better questions.

I dipped my quill. And in the margin of the autumn count, in a script so small that only a bookkeeper would ever find it, I wrote the first entry of a ledger that no one had commissioned:

*One (1) map, bone, warm. One (1) promise, inherited. One (1) Choir, starving. Debtor: the Guild. Creditor: the world.*

*Payment overdue.*

Then I closed the book, and took the map out from under my apron, and began — with the calm of a woman balancing accounts, which is to say with terror wearing a very tidy dress — to plan how a night clerk steals a dead man's secret out of the Guild of Impossible Things without being counted among its losses.

The rats, I thought, would know which way the vaults weren't watched.

The rats, I thought, owed me a conversation.

And I had, at last, decided to have it.
