## 3. What the Rats Overheard

There is a particular art to talking to rats in a building that would kill you for it, and I had been practicing it my entire life without ever once saying a word aloud.

The trick is this: never look at them. Look at your ledger. Lick your thumb, turn a page, sigh like a woman bored by column sums. Let your attention drift the way water drifts — everywhere and nowhere — until the rats decide you're furniture. Furniture with crumbs. The moment you *look*, really look, they scatter, because rats know better than any creature alive what a hungry gaze means.

So I sat in the counting house long after the last clerk had gone home, lamp trimmed low, ledger open, and I listened.

The warehouse beneath the counting house — three floors of vaulted stone that smelled of salt, mildew, and old money — was not empty. It was never empty. But most nights it held only the breathing of stored things: griffin feathers rustling in their cedar crates like sleeping birds, bottled thunder muttering to itself on its shelves, jars of moonlight going slowly stale. And beneath all of that, the small busy sound of the rats.

They had names for me, I'd learned over years of eavesdropping. The Ledger-Woman. The Quiet One. Once, memorably, She Who Drops Cheese. I had dropped cheese exactly twice, both times by accident, and been canonized for it. That is the whole of my legend among rodents, and I confess it pleased me more than any compliment I ever received from humans.

Tonight they were arguing about a smell.

*Old,* said a voice from under the floorboards, thin and piping as a bent nail. *Older than old. Old like the well behind the tannery.*

*It's the bone thing,* said another voice, closer, from inside the wall behind my chair. *The dead man's bone thing. It smells like the deep places. Like before.*

*Before what?*

*Before,* said the wall-rat, with the enormous patience of the very small explaining things to the slightly smaller.

I turned a page. My heart was doing something unsuitable for a woman of ledgers — galloping, if you must know, like an unpaid invoice through a merchant's dreams.

The map against my ribs had not cooled since the stranger died holding it into my hands. If anything it had grown warmer, which bone should not do, and twice tonight I had felt something move beneath its surface — not motion, exactly. More like a pulse remembering it used to be a heartbeat. I had decided, in the firm way one decides not to think about interest compounding, not to examine this too closely.

*The Quiet One's awake,* said a third rat, from somewhere near the cheese cupboard, and my blood went cold. *I can hear her heart.*

Rats could hear hearts. This was news to me, and bad news, because mine had apparently been announcing itself all evening like a town crier.

*I'm awake,* I said.

I want to be clear about what happened next, because nothing happened next, physically speaking. My lips did not move. My throat made no sound. But I thought the words at them, hard and clear, the way I had been thinking words at animals since I was six years old and first understood that the stable cat's opinions about my father were unkind but fair — and every rat within thirty feet went utterly still.

This is the part no one warns you about, when you have a gift you must hide: using it feels like opening a door in a house where you are only a guest. Every time, there is a moment where you stand with your hand on the latch and listen for footsteps upstairs.

The footsteps upstairs belonged to Guildmaster Corvane's night wardens, who walked their rounds at the half-bell, boots ringing on the stairs two floors above. I counted them past. Four sets. Then silence.

*I'm awake,* I said again, gentler now. *And I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer true, the way you answered true for me about the smuggler's crates last autumn. Do you remember?*

A long pause. Then, from the wall behind me, dry as dust and considerably drier than flattery:

*We remember. You didn't scream when we told you there were snakes in the flour. Most humans scream. You just moved the flour and left us the spilled part.* A rustle, the sound of a rat settling in for a negotiation. *What do you want, Ledger-Woman?*

*The bone thing,* I said. *The dead man carried it. The enforcers took his body but they didn't find it, and tomorrow they'll start searching the building. I want to know where the watchers aren't.*

*That's two questions.*

*Rats bargain?*

*Rats survive,* said the wall-voice, with dignity. *Bargaining is how. What will you give?*

I looked down at my ledger — the real one, the official one, the one that recorded the Guild's holdings in impossible things — and an idea arrived that was either brilliant or suicidal, and in my experience those are frequently the same idea wearing different hats.

*Information,* I said. *You tell me about the watchers, and tomorrow I leave the grain-store vents open, all of them, and I write in the wastage column that we lost four sacks to vermin instead of two. Nobody hunts four-sack losses. Two-sack losses get a cat.*

Another pause. Somewhere below, a chorus of whiskers twitched in collective astonishment.

*Four sacks,* said the wall-rat reverently. *You'd falsify a ledger. For us.*

*I've falsified ledgers for worse reasons,* I said, which was a lie, because until this week the worst reason I'd ever had was sparing a junior clerk's feelings about miscounting thunder-jars. How quickly a dead stranger changes your moral arithmetic. *Do we have a bargain?*

*We have a bargain,* said the wall-rat, and then, sounding genuinely puzzled: *Why do you need to know where the watchers aren't, Quiet One? Humans only want that when they're running or stealing. You don't look like a runner. Your heart runs though. Your heart's been running all night.*

*Both,* I said. *Now talk.*

*

And oh, they talked.

Rats, it turns out, are the finest historians in any city, for three excellent reasons: they go everywhere, they remember everything, and nobody has ever thought to lie to one. They do not read reports. They hear the men who write the reports complaining about the reports while the men eat supper badly in the vault office above.

*The bone man came through the salt door eleven nights ago,* said a rat who lived, by her own account, in the warden's boot rack — a detail I chose not to dwell on. *He came wet and bleeding and he asked for the Undercroft Registry, and the night porter laughed at him and said the Registry burned years ago. And the bone man said, "No. It was bought."*

My pen stopped moving. I hadn't written anything in ten minutes; I'd just been making the motions, little loops of nothing, while the rats' voices poured through me like cold water through a sieve.

*Bought,* I repeated.

*Bought. And here is the part you want, Quiet One, so mark your little book:* the boot-rack rat drew breath, theatrical as an opera singer. *The porter sent for Warden Hesk, and Warden Hesk came down with his lantern and his ring of keys, and he looked at the bone man and he said — we heard it through the grate, all of us, all forty of us in that wall — he said: "The Choir has been dying for sixty years, friend. The Guild has merely been helping it along. Every relic we buy is one less voice. Every bell we melt is one less song. When the last voice goes quiet, the wild places stop dreaming, and when they stop dreaming—" *

The rat paused. Even the pipes seemed to hold their breath.

*"—they start forgetting us. And that," Hesk said, "is the safest trade this city has ever made."*

I set down the pen very carefully, because my hand wanted to shake and shaking hands smudge columns, and a woman who has spent ten years being precise does not break precision even when her world does.

*He said that,* I said. *Out loud. To a stranger. In a Guild building full of rats.*

*Humans say everything out loud,* said the boot-rat, not unkindly. *It's why we've never needed to invent lying. You give it away free.*

*Sixty years,* I said, mostly to myself. Sixty years. My mother had been dead — taken, lost, whatever the word was — for twenty-two of them. The Choir had been starving since before I was born, and the Guild had been holding the bowl behind its back the whole time, smiling, selling tickets to the famine. All those crates I'd inventoried. Griffin feathers. Bottled thunder. Jars of dawn-song from a marsh no map named. Relics, they'd called them in the ledgers. Acquisitions. I had balanced the accounts of a murder and called it bookkeeping.

*There's more,* said the wall-rat, and something in its voice had changed — gone quieter, the way voices go quiet around sickbeds. *The bone man ran because of what's in the deep vault. Sub-level three. We've been down there. It's warm, always warm, and there's a sound down there that isn't a sound, and the oldest among us won't go past the second landing anymore. Old Whiskerless — she was in that wall when your grandmother's grandmother was dropping cheese — Whiskerless says sub-level three used to sing. Before the Guild bought the singing. Now it just... hurts. Like a tooth in the world's jaw.*

*What's down there?*

*A bell,* said the wall-rat simply. *A bell that doesn't ring anymore. And chains. And something alive that used to be a song.*

The lamp flame leaned sideways in a draft from under the door, and for one absurd moment I saw the whole counting house as it truly was: a beautiful skull, elegant and orderly, built around something buried and screaming.

*One more question,* I said. *The watchers. Where aren't they?*

*Ah,* said the wall-rat, and I could hear the grin in it. Rats can grin; I've seen it; it's mostly teeth and triumph. *That's the easy part, Quiet One. They watch doors, because humans are door-thinkers. But the salt chute — the old one, behind the brine cisterns, the one that hasn't cleared cargo since the flood year — the chute opens onto the fen road, and the fen road belongs to the foxes now. The Guild doesn't watch the fen road because the Guild is afraid of the foxes. We heard Hesk say so himself. He said, "Nothing that goes into the fens comes back with its mind intact."*

*Charming,* I said. *Thank you. Four sacks. I keep my word.*

*We know,* said all the rats at once, in the eerie unison they sometimes fell into, like a small furry congregation. *That's why we helped. There aren't many of you who keep word with our kind.* A beat. *Be careful on the fen road, Ledger-Woman. The fox talks. And the fox lies beautifully.*

*

I closed the ledger. I blew out the lamp. I sat in the dark with the map warm against my ribs, and I let myself feel, for exactly one minute by the clock I kept in my head, the full weight of what I now knew.

Then I got up, and I did the bravest thing I have ever done, which looked from the outside like nothing at all.

I opened the wastage column. And in my own tidy, blameless, ten-year-perfect handwriting, I wrote: *Vermin loss, grain stores: four sacks. Vents left open for ventilation per maintenance schedule.* Four sacks. My first forgery. My first open act of war against the Guild of Impossible Things, disguised as generosity toward rodents, and I found — this surprised me — that my hand did not shake at all. It wrote the lie the way it wrote everything: clearly, evenly, without flourish.

Because here is what I understood, sitting there in the dark with ink drying under my fingers: they had counted on my silence for sixty years. Not my silence specifically — the silence of everyone, the great purchased hush, bell after bell melted, voice after voice crated and shelved and labeled ACQUISITION. The Guild hadn't stolen the wild's song with armies. It had done it with ledgers. With neat columns. With women like me balancing the books and asking nothing.

Well. I was done asking nothing. I was, in fact, about to take quite a lot: a bone map, a stolen hour, and a future the Guild had not authorized.

I packed the way a night clerk packs, which is to say perfectly: bread, cheese (the good cheese; Quillfeather — no, I didn't know his name yet, but some instinct told me to pack the good cheese), flint, the small knife I used for trimming quills, my mother's ring, and the ledger of impossible things itself — not the copy. The original. Let the Guild wake to a missing book and a missing clerk and try to calculate *that* loss. I wanted them to have to write it down. *One (1) accountant, fled. Value: incalculable.* I hoped it kept them up nights.

The half-bell rang. Wardens' rounds: four sets of boots, stairs, landing, receding. I waited for the echo to die, then slipped out of the counting house and down the back stair, apron off, hair bound flat, moving the way I'd learned to move through my whole concealed life — present, unnoticed, essential to no one's evening.

Sub-level three was on my way to nowhere, and I did not go there. I want that recorded. Whatever sang-hurt in the dark under the vaults, whatever lived chained beside a silenced bell — I was not ready, and the map was not asking, and there will be time, I promised the warmth against my ribs, there will be time. The bone pulsed once, like a patient heart. It did not believe me. Bones rarely do.

The salt chute was exactly where the rats said: behind the brine cisterns, choked with a decade of dust, its iron door swollen shut — for human hands. I pressed my palm to it and thought *open, please, old door, I keep word with your residents,* and whether the door understood or whether I simply pushed harder than I knew, it groaned wide enough for a slim woman with a satchel and a grievance.

Beyond it: the fen road, black under a fat blind moon, smelling of reeds and rain and distance. Behind me, the counting house stood with its lit windows and its orderly shelves, every crate inside holding one more stolen piece of the world's voice.

I looked at it for a long moment.

"Four sacks," I said aloud — aloud! — to the darkness, to the rats, to the sixty silent years. "And counting."

Then Mirabel Sandoval, night clerk, beast-speaker, thief of one bone map, stepped out of the Guild of Impossible Things and into the fens —

— and something with amber eyes, sitting on the drowned fence post directly ahead, began, very politely, to applaud.
