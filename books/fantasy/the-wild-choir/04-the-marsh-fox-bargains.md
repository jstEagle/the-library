## 4. The Marsh Fox Bargains

I want it noted, for the record I was no longer keeping, that I had never intended to end up in the fens. Nobody intends to end up in the fens. The fens are where intentions go to drown quietly and be digested by something with too many teeth.

But the salt road out of Grayharbor had lasted exactly as long as my coin purse, which is to say: not long enough, and not honestly. By the second day the Guild's riders were behind me — two of them at first, then four, then a shape on the horizon that moved like a man who had never once in his life needed to hurry and was therefore the most frightening thing I'd ever seen. So I did what any sensible bookkeeper would do when pursued by an institution that owned the ledgers, the roads, the law, and quite possibly the rain.

I went off the map.

Literally. The bone map in my satchel hummed — a low, wet thrum against my hip, like a struck bell underwater — whenever I turned toward the fens, and flinched like a scolded child when I faced back toward the road. Dead men's maps, apparently, have opinions. The stranger's blood had long since dried into its grooves, but the channels still glimmered faintly, and they all pointed one direction: down, and east, and into the drowning country.

"The Choir is starving," he'd said, and then he'd been a body cooling on the Guild's doorstep, and I'd been a woman holding the most dangerous piece of accounting in the world.

So. Fens.

* * *

The marsh at dusk is not dark. That's the first lie it tells you. It glows — foxfire in the hollows, rot-light on the standing water, a greenish phosphorescence that makes everything look like it's lit from underneath by something patient. Reeds stood ten feet tall in every direction, whispering to each other in a language I could almost — *almost* — catch the edge of, the way you can almost remember a dream if you don't look at it directly. My gift sat under my skin like a splinter working toward the heart. Every frog, every heron, every nameless thing gulping in the mud had something to say, and I could hear all of it, and I couldn't answer, because answering aloud in open country with Guild riders behind me was how you got caught.

Also, possibly, how you got eaten. The two were not mutually exclusive out here.

I'd been walking for hours with water in my boots and the map pulling my hip toward some point ahead, when the reeds stopped whispering.

All of them. At once.

If you've never heard several acres of vegetation decide simultaneously to shut up, I recommend keeping it that way. The silence had texture. It had *intent*. And then a voice came out of it, dry and drawling and far too pleased with itself:

"You're being followed. Badly."

I froze with one foot half-sunk in peat, which meant my options were limited mostly to falling over with dignity or without it. "I'm not—" I began.

"Oh, you absolutely are. Four riders on the causeway behind you, moving at a walk because they know you can't move at anything else. There's a fifth who circled ahead — he's currently lost, which buys you perhaps twenty minutes, though frankly I've seen eels navigate better." A pause, delicately savored. "You're also standing in a bog hole. Left foot. You're welcome."

I looked down. My left foot was indeed sinking with the slow, courteous enthusiasm of peat that had all night and no plans. I hauled it free with an unflattering noise and staggered onto what I hoped was solid ground, which turned out to be marginally less liquid ground, which in the fens counts as triumph.

"Who's there?" I said, which was stupid, because I already knew. Not from sight. From the *shape* of the voice — that sly, layered quality that human throats can't make, words wrapped around a growl like ribbon around wire. I'd spent my whole life hearing animals talk through walls and cage bars and closed doors. I knew the sound the way a musician knows a false note.

"Who's *where*?" the voice replied. "That's the question, isn't it. Very philosophical. You'll go far, if you survive the next hour, which is doubtful."

And then it stepped out of the reeds, and I made the mistake of laughing.

It was a fox — marsh-born, mud-colored, its coat streaked with rust and ash, ears ragged at the edges like a well-read book. But across its shoulders, where a wild fox would carry nothing but weather, this one carried a ruff of feathers. Actual feathers, grey and white and iridescent at the edges, molted and preened into something between plumage and mantle. They lifted slightly in the fen wind, and the fox wore them the way a disgraced officer wears his old uniform: defiantly, and a little too carefully.

"Go on," it said, watching my face with flat amber eyes. "Laugh again. Everyone does. It's why I keep them."

"They're beautiful," I said, before my survival instincts could intervene.

The fox's tail stopped mid-swish. For a moment — just a moment — the sarcasm drained out of its posture entirely, and what was left underneath looked startlingly young. Then the armor came back up so smoothly I might have imagined it.

"Flattery," it said, "from a woman ankle-deep in a swamp with five armed men hunting her. This is either the worst negotiation I've ever conducted or the best, and I genuinely cannot tell."

"How do you know about the riders?"

"I live here. Knowing things is the entire economy of the fens. We trade in it. Information for passage, passage for warnings, warnings for grudges — it's very sophisticated, there are ledgers." Its whiskers twitched. "Speaking of which. You smell like ink and counting houses and someone else's blood. You're the one from Grayharbor. The one carrying the dead man's map."

My hand went to my satchel before my brain could veto it, which told the fox everything it needed to know, which was presumably the point.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"No, of course not. And I'm a particularly large weasel." The fox sat down on a tussock, curling its feathered shoulders with the air of a merchant settling in for a long haggle. "Here's how this evening goes, Mirabel Sandoval, sometime ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. Option one: you keep blundering northeast, and within the hour you meet a man named Corvane, who is patient and thorough and has been paid to bring back your satchel and, failing that, whatever parts of you are easiest to carry. Option two: you follow me, and I take you along paths no rider alive can find, to places the map wants you to go anyway — yes, I know it wants things, I can smell it humming from here, and so can everything else in this marsh, which is why the frogs have gone quiet, and why you should be more frightened than you currently are."

"And option three?"

"There is no option three. Options come in pairs in the fens. It's traditional."

"What do you want?"

"Ah." The fox's eyes caught the foxfire and held it, twin coins of green-gold. "There it is. The right question. I want a debt."

"A debt."

"One debt. Unnamed. Owed by you, to me, redeemable at a time and in a manner of my choosing, within reason — I'm not a monster, I'll let you stay alive and roughly untraumatized while paying it." It examined a claw with tremendous casualness. "Standard terms."

"That's not standard terms. That's a blank check with fur on it."

"Thank you. I've worked hard on the fur."

I should say now, in fairness to myself, that I hesitated. I want the record — the imaginary record, the one I kept in my head after the real ones burned — to show that I understood exactly what I was being offered and what it would cost. I had balanced accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things for six years. I knew what unnamed debts looked like on paper. They looked like small entries that grew. They looked like interest compounding in the dark. They looked like the reason half the Guild's vaults were full of things people had once loved and later regretted.

But I also heard, beneath the fox's drawl, the thing it wasn't saying. Animals wear their loneliness differently than people do — closer to the skin, harder to hide. Six years of listening through walls had taught me that the creatures who talked the most were usually the ones who'd been silent longest. This fox had feathers on its back and a voice full of knives and it had stepped out of hiding to negotiate with a stranger, which no truly self-sufficient creature ever does.

Somewhere behind us, faint as a thought, a horse snorted on the causeway.

"Twenty minutes," I said. "You said twenty minutes."

"Fifteen now. Time moves strangely here; I'd stop wasting it."

"If your debt turns out to be something monstrous—"

"It won't be monstrous. It will be inconvenient, humiliating, and precisely calibrated to be worth exactly what I'm giving you. I'm very fair. Ask anyone." A beat. "Ask anyone who isn't currently avoiding me, which is everyone, which is a scheduling issue, not a character reference."

Against every instinct that six years of double-entry bookkeeping had instilled in me — every instinct that said *never sign anything with a blank line* — I held out my hand, palm up, mud and all.

"On one condition. When the debt comes due, you tell me what it is *before* I owe it. Full disclosure. I don't care when you call it in. I care that I get to know what I agreed to."

The fox looked at my hand. Looked at me. Something moved behind those amber eyes — calculation, certainly, but underneath the calculation, briefly, something rawer, like a creature checking whether the trap it was walking into might, improbably, be a door.

"You drive a strange bargain," it said. "Agreed. Quillfeather, at your service, and considerably less at anyone else's."

I took the offered paw — because it was a paw, and shaking it meant acknowledging that, and I decided in that moment that I was done pretending otherwise, at least out here where the only witness was the fog — and the deal settled over me like a change in the weather. Not unpleasant. Just present. The kind of weight you stop noticing until you try to stand up straight.

"Well, Mirabel Sandoval," said Quillfeather, turning east with his feathered ruff catching the light like tarnished silver. "Shall we go starve a choir?"

I hadn't told him what the map was for.

He hadn't asked.

I filed both facts away in the mental ledger where I kept all the things I'd decided not to think about yet — a ledger growing distressingly thick since a stranger died on a doorstep — and followed him into the glowing dark.

* * *

We walked most of the night. Quillfeather navigated the fens the way I imagine a fish navigates water: without effort, without mercy, and with occasional contempt for those of us built for land. He found paths where I saw only reeds, fords where I saw only drowning, and twice he stopped me with a raised tail an instant before I would have stepped into water that, he assured me cheerfully, "has opinions about warm-blooded things."

"You're enjoying this," I said somewhere past midnight, hauling myself up a root-bank with what little dignity remained.

"Immensely. Do you know how long it's been since I walked with anyone? Centuries of talking to herons. Herons, Mirabel. Do you know what herons discuss? Fish. Exclusively fish. One heron once spent four hours describing a fish. It was a good fish, admittedly, but four *hours*."

"You counted."

"I count everything. It's a curse we appear to share." He glanced back at me, and for a moment his voice lost its lacquer. "The gift. Beast-speech. That's what you carry, isn't it? Under the skin. That's what the Guild would want you for, if they knew."

My stomach dropped. "How—"

"You listen the way listeners listen. Even now, with nothing to hear, you're tilted toward the reeds. Like a door left open a crack." He resumed walking, tail swaying. "Relax. The fens keep secrets better than any guild. It's our chief export."

"And yours?" I asked. "What did the fens keep of yours?"

Quillfeather didn't answer. His feathers lay flat and still, and the silence he offered me was, I realized, the same silence I'd been offering the world for six years — the polite, armored, aching silence of someone who has decided that some questions cost more than the answer is worth.

So I didn't ask again. Instead I walked beside him through the burning dark, two liars keeping each other company, and when the first grey light came up over the drowned country, the bone map against my hip began to hum louder than it ever had before — urgent now, insistent, a pulse quickening toward something ahead.

Quillfeather stopped at the top of the bank. Below us, half-swallowed by the marsh, stood the rooftops of a village that had drowned and refused, stubbornly, to finish dying. Water lapped at windows. A stone well rose from the flood like a throat.

"Three voices," Quillfeather said quietly, all the humor gone out of him. "Sleeping in that well. I wondered when we'd get here."

"You knew the waypoints."

"I knew the maker of your map." His amber eyes slid sideways, away from mine, toward the water. "And if you're who I think you are, little ledger-keeper — then you and I have been circling the same grief for a very long time."

Below us, in the flooded village, something answered the map's humming with a note — thin, wavering, unmistakably a human voice, singing up through forty feet of dark water.

Quillfeather sighed. "Do try not to die," he said. "I have a debt to collect."
