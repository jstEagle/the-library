## 5. Emeric of the Broken Order

I had been a warden of the Covenant for nineteen years, and in all that time I had learned exactly two things worth teaching: how to track a person through country that did not want them found, and how to lie to yourself about why you were doing it.

The second skill was the harder one to lose.

I picked up her trail at the edge of the fens, where the Guild's hounds had lost it and I had not, because the Guild's hounds were trained to follow scent and I was trained to follow sense. A woman alone, traveling light, moving like someone who had recently discovered she was prey. She cut north through the reedbeds instead of east along the salt road, which was stupid, and then doubled back on her own path twice, which was clever, and then forded a channel no sane person would ford, which meant she was frightened or desperate or both.

Frightened people run in straight lines. Desperate ones zigzag. The dangerous ones do both and pretend it's strategy.

Her name was Mirabel Sandoval. I knew it from the Guild's morning dispatches, read aloud by a clerk with a voice like wet paper: *fugitive, wanted for questioning regarding the death of an unknown man at the Salt Door; considered armed with information; recover the object she carries.* The dispatch did not say what the object was. Dispatches never said what the object was. In nineteen years I had recovered forty-one objects for the Guild — relics, fragments, bones of things that used to sing — and not once had anyone above me explained what any of them were *for*. You carried out the order. That was the whole of wardenship. The Covenant's old oath had been three pages long and beautiful enough to weep over. What remained of it fit on a dispatch slip.

The Broken Order, we called ourselves now, when we called ourselves anything at all. Most of my brothers and sisters had stopped calling themselves anything. It is easier to be a tool than a name.

* * *

I found her at dusk on the third day, camped in the lee of a leaning menhir — one of the old standing stones, cracked down its middle like a loaf nobody wanted. She had built no fire, which told me she was smarter than she looked, and she was sitting very still with her eyes closed, which told me either she was asleep or she was listening to something.

I chose the moment she opened her eyes.

"Mirabel Sandoval," I said, stepping into the last grey light. "My name is Emeric Vael. Formerly warden-second of the Order of the Living Chord. I've come for the map."

You will notice I said *formerly*. Even then, some honest part of me was already drafting its resignation letter. The rest of me hadn't read it yet.

She came up off the ground faster than I expected, a knife already in her hand — a ledger-clerk's knife, short and clean, the kind used for trimming quills. Behind her, half-hidden in the shadow of the stone, something rustled and swore under its breath. I caught a flash of russet fur and two bright unimpressed eyes.

"You have a fox," I said.

"I have a fox," she agreed, as if this were a normal thing for fugitives to travel with, as if foxes were standard equipment, like bedrolls and regrets.

"It talks," the fox said. "And I'm standing right here."

I did not flinch. Nineteen years teaches you not to flinch, mostly because flinching gets you killed and partly because after a certain point nothing surprises you anymore. But I will confess — here, in the privacy of my own telling, where no one from the Order can hear — that hearing a fox speak plainly, without glamour or riddle, made something turn over in my chest like a body shifting in shallow sleep. I had heard wild speech before, once, long ago, filtered through covenant-stones and formal channels. Never like this. Never so *casual*, so insultingly ordinary, as if the world had always worked this way and only I had been kept out of the room.

"The map," I said again, because repetition is cheaper than thinking.

"And if I give it to you?"

"I take it to my superiors. They'll want to question you about the dead man. After that—" I hesitated. This was the part of the script where I was supposed to say *you'll likely be released*. It is what I had said to others. Some of them had even believed me.

"After that?" she prompted, knife steady. There was a smear of marsh mud along her jaw and shadows under her eyes deep enough to post letters into, but her chin was up. Clerks, I have found, are the most stubborn class of person alive. You can frighten soldiers. You cannot frighten arithmetic.

"Honestly," I said, "after that I don't know."

Something flickered across her face. Not fear. Recognition, maybe — the look of someone who has spent years balancing other people's books and knows exactly what an entry that doesn't add up sounds like.

"That's not what your dispatch says," she said.

"You read my dispatch."

"I have friends who read your dispatch." The fox snorted. "Rats," she added, by way of explanation, which explained nothing and everything.

I should have taken the map then. I should have done what nineteen years had trained my hands to do — closed the distance, disarmed her, bound her wrists, and delivered both woman and bone-map to the chapterhouse at Grey Ford, where they would have fed her questions until she gave up answers, and then probably fed her to something quieter. That was the work. That had always been the work.

Instead I heard myself ask: "Do you know what happened to the man at the Salt Door?"

"No." And then, quieter: "He died holding my hands. He said the Choir is starving. Do you know what that means?"

I knew what it meant the way you know a word in a language you spoke as a child and have deliberately forgotten. The Hollow Choir. The broken pact. Every warden learned it in the first year, as history, as bedtime story, as warning: there had once been twenty-one voices binding the human realm and the wild together, a song sustained between worlds, and something had gone wrong, and the song had frayed, and the Order had been founded to keep what remained of it safe. Keep it safe. That was the phrase. We were the keepers of the remainder.

Except.

Except that for the last decade, the objects I recovered — the feathers and bells and bottled voices, the splinters of old songs — had not gone to sanctuaries or choir-halls. They had gone to Guild vaults. Sealed ones. And the orders had changed shape, slowly, the way a river changes course, so gradually that no single order ever looked like treachery. *Recover the relic. Suppress the witness. Burn the road behind you.* Never once in ten years had I been asked to restore anything. Only to gather. Only to silence.

I had noticed this, of course. Noticing is not the same as knowing. Knowing requires you to stop lying to yourself, and I had gotten very good at that — champion-grade, medal-winning self-deception, the sort they ought to hang ribbons for.

"Give me the map," I said, and my voice came out strange, "and I swear on the old oath — the real one, the three-page one — that no harm comes to you while I hold it."

"That's not an answer to my question," said Mirabel Sandoval.

"It's the only one I have."

The fox laughed. It was not a kind laugh. "Wardens," it said. "They come in two kinds: liars and liars with oaths."

* * *

Here is what happened instead of the sensible thing.

We sat down at her cold fireless camp, and I told her things I had never told anyone, because she asked questions in the tone of an auditor and I discovered, to my horror, that after nineteen years I had been waiting for someone to audit me.

She asked why a warden tracked a lone woman for three days instead of reporting her position to the hunters' circle. I said I preferred working alone. True, but not the truth. The truth was that I had watched the hunters burn a waystation full of pilgrims in Ashfall Vale because one of them had hummed an old road-song, and I had reported it as *collateral compliance*, and the word had tasted like ash in my mouth ever since.

She asked what the Guild did with the relics. I said I didn't know. Then I said: actually, I did know. Not the purpose, but the pattern. Everything gathered, nothing restored. The vaults at Grey Ford had tripled in ten years. The wild roads were closing one by one, and every closure was filed under *safety*. The birds in the capital had started vanishing from the eaves. The bells rang less each year — officially due to maintenance costs. Maintenance costs. For bells.

She asked me what the Order's founders would have thought of us.

"They'd have burned us," I said. "Slowly. With hymns."

And then — because she just looked at me with those ledger-keeper's eyes, patient as interest accruing — I told her the thing I had sworn I would carry to my grave. Two winters ago, I had escorted a shipment to the deep vault beneath Grey Ford. My clearance was high enough for the outer corridor, not the inner door, but the inner door was old, older than the Guild, and it sang. Just barely. A thread of sound leaking through stone, like a voice through a wall. And I stood in that corridor with a crate of confiscated relics at my back and listened to that thread, and I realized it wasn't music.

It was counting. Something behind that door was counting, faintly, endlessly, like a heart keeping time for a body that had forgotten it. And the count was going *down*.

"Starving," Mirabel said softly. "The Choir is starving."

"And the Guild isn't trying to save it," I said. The words came out of me like teeth being pulled, each one with roots. "The Guild is *feeding on the starvation*. Or causing it. Or both. I don't know which. I've spent ten years not knowing which, and I'm tired, and I'm—"

"Dishonored," offered the fox helpfully.

"—disillusioned," I snapped.

"He means dishonored," the fox told Mirabel. "Disillusioned is what happens first. Dishonored is what you are when everyone else notices."

I decided then and there that I would feed the fox to the next convenient owl, and also that I would probably not, because it was the first creature in years to tell me a true thing to my face.

Mirabel had not moved through any of this. She sat with her knife across her knees, watching me the way she must have watched ledgers — looking for the line that didn't balance. At last she said, "If I give you the map, what will you do with it? Truly. Not the script. The man underneath."

And that was the crack, right there. That was the moment duty went through the ice. Because I opened my mouth to give the correct answer — *deliver it to my superiors, as sworn* — and what came out instead was:

"Burn it. Feed it to the fens. Let the Choir starve quietly rather than let the Guild finish it faster." 

I heard myself say it and felt the strangest sensation, like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was wearing for a decade. My shoulders actually ached with the lightness.

Then I thought about what I'd said, and felt sick, and then thought about it again, longer, and felt worse — because if my first honest instinct was *destroy it*, then I had not been a keeper for a long time. I had been a gravedigger who liked the uniform.

"No," I corrected myself. Mirabel raised an eyebrow. The fox made a noise like a kettle deciding against boiling. "No. Burning it is just another kind of silence. I've been carrying silences for the Guild for ten years. I won't start carrying theirs." I held out my hand, palm open, empty. "So here is my offer, and I warn you it's a bad one. Take me with you. I know the Order's methods, its signs, its hunting patterns, its blind spots. I can get you past my own brothers. And when this is finished — whatever *this* is — you can hand me to whatever justice is left in the world. I'll go willingly. I'd almost welcome it."

"That's a terrible offer," Mirabel said.

"Told you."

"She takes terrible offers," the fox said. "It's her whole method. Ask the rats."

"Nobody asked you, Quillfeather."

"You literally cannot stop me talking. It's one of my finest qualities."

But Mirabel was looking at my empty hand. "Why?" she asked. One word, flat, auditor's steel. "Why defect now? Why for me? You don't know me. People don't ruin their lives for strangers carrying maps."

And there it was — the real audit, the one I couldn't dodge with procedure. I looked at the cracked menhir, at the fen going violet and gold around us, at the first stars coming out over the Ashspine far to the north, and I gave the only honest answer I had left in inventory.

"Because I stood outside a singing door for one hour, two winters ago, and I have not slept properly since. Because every order I've followed for ten years has been a small quiet cruelty wearing a large respectable coat. Because the man at your Salt Door said the Choir is starving, and I think — I *know* — that my hands are on the rope tightening around its throat. And because—" I stopped. Started again. "Because I took an oath once. Three pages long. It said *to listen, to keep, and to mend*. I have done none of those things. You're heading somewhere that might let me do one of them before I die. That's all. That's the whole of my case."

Silence, except the fens breathing around us.

Then Quillfeather padded out from behind the stone, sat down directly in front of me, and looked me over with the thorough contempt of a customs officer. "He'll do," the fox announced. "He's broken, he's guilty, and he's useful. In my experience that's the complete list of requirements for a decent ally."

"That's the complete list of requirements for a decent *person*," said Mirabel, and sheathed her knife, and put the bone map in my hand — put it there herself, pressed it into my palm, which I had not expected and which undid me more than any battle ever had. "Don't make me sorry," she said. "I'm good at holding grudges. I keep records."

"Noted," I managed.

* * *

We walked north that night by starlight, toward the drowned village and the well, though I didn't yet know that was where the map pointed. I walked behind her, one step back and to the left, in the old escort position — except for the first time in nineteen years I was walking *toward* the thing I was guarding instead of away from it, and the difference felt enormous and ridiculous, like learning to breathe through a new hole in your chest.

I defected badly. I want that on the record. No grand refusal, no dramatic tearing of insignia. I simply answered one honest question with another honest answer, and the Order lost a warden, and I gained a debt I intend to spend the rest of my life repaying to a woman who keeps grudges in double-entry.

It was near midnight when I heard it — far behind us, at the fen's southern edge, the baying of the Guild's hounds, and under them, worse, the low thrum of horn-signals. Three notes, repeating. The signal I knew better than my own heartbeat.

*Prey located. Road closed. Nothing gets out.*

Quillfeather's ears went flat. Mirabel looked at me, and I looked at the darkening north, and I understood that my old brothers were not following the map.

They were following *me*.

"They'll have marked my absence by now," I said. "Which means they're not hunting a thief anymore. They're hunting a traitor." I checked the strap of my sword and found my hands were steady, which surprised me. "There's a ford ahead the hounds won't cross. If we move now, we reach it by dawn."

"And if we don't?" Mirabel asked.

"Then we find out what the inside of a Guild cell sings like." I glanced back south, at the horns, at the life I had just amputated. "I suggest we move."
