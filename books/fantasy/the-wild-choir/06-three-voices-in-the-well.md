## 6. Three Voices in the Well

The drowned village announced itself the way grief does — quietly, and then all at once.

We had walked two days through fens that smelled like the inside of a boot, Quillfeather trotting ahead with the smugness of a creature who knew exactly where he was going and intended to make everyone else suffer for it, when the trees thinned and the water rose and there it was: rooftops breaking the surface of a grey lake like the knuckles of a drowned giant. A church spire leaned at an angle that suggested it had given up on standing but hadn't quite committed to falling.

"Thornmere," Quillfeather said. "Or what's left of it. The river took the village forty years ago when the covenant started fraying. Water goes where the old agreements used to hold it back. Nobody remembers how to argue with a river anymore."

"You could have mentioned we were walking toward a lake," I said.

"I could have mentioned a great many things. I find it builds character when you don't."

Emeric said nothing. He'd said very little since the night he'd turned his back on the Wardens, and what little he did say came out clipped and careful, like a man handling his own opinions as though they might be evidence against him later. He stood at the waterline now, studying the rooftops, and I watched him do the thing he did — measuring, assessing, filing everything away in that orderly head of his.

"The map shows the well," I said, unrolling the bone scroll for the hundredth time. The stranger's bloodstains had gone brown and stiff, but the markings still shifted faintly under my fingers, like something breathing. "Under the church, maybe. Or beneath the water itself."

"Beneath," Quillfeather confirmed. "The well is still down there. Wells don't drown, girl. They just get quiet." He flicked an ear. "Which is why you're here. You're the one who can hear quiet things."

I looked at the water. It was cold enough at this distance to make my teeth ache in sympathy.

"And how exactly am I supposed to reach a well at the bottom of a lake?"

* * *

The answer, it turned out, was badly.

Emeric found a fisher's coracle upturned under a collapsed boathouse — a leaky wooden bowl held together by optimism and tar — and insisted on rowing us out over the church while Quillfeather perched on the prow making comments about our collective life expectancy. Emeric rowed the way he seemed to do everything: without complaint, without wasted motion, and with a jaw set like he expected the river itself to file charges.

"You should stay ashore," I told him, for the third time.

"No," he said, for the third time, in exactly the same tone.

"It's my gift. My risk."

"Yes," he agreed, dipping the oars. "And if you go silent halfway through whatever you're about to do, someone has to be close enough to pull you out. That's not negotiation, Mirabel. That's arithmetic."

I didn't have an answer to that, so I kept my mouth shut and my hands on the map. The bone grew warm as we drifted over the spire. Below us, through water browned with silt, I could just make out the dark suggestion of the church roof, and beyond it — deeper than it had any right to be — a circle of older stone. The well. It had been dry once, before the flood. Now it was a throat of black water going down into the hill itself.

"Here?" Emeric asked.

"Here," I whispered, and closed my eyes, and listened.

This is the part I've never known how to describe to people who can't do it. Hearing animals isn't like listening with ears. It's more like... leaning. Leaning out of yourself into a current that runs underneath everything alive, and letting your mind be carried a little way along it. Mother taught me the lean before she taught me my letters. She also taught me the rule, the one carved so deep in me it might as well be bone: *never let them hear you answer.*

But mother wasn't here. Mother was a grave in Ashspine Province, or so I'd believed for twenty years, and the rules were all I had left of her, and I was about to break the biggest one in front of a fox and a defector because a dead man's map told me to.

I leaned.

The water spoke first. Not words — water doesn't bother with words — but a long grey boredom, the endless patience of things that flow. Fish, small ones, startled and scattered from my attention like minnows from a shadow. Then weeds, which think in slow spirals and have nothing whatsoever to say. Then, down where the light gave up —

Voices.

Three of them. They were not animals. That was the first thing I understood, and understanding it made my hands shake on the coracle's rim. These were old voices, human-shaped in their cadence but worn smooth as river stones, and they were singing. Three notes of a chord, held for forty years at the bottom of a flooded well, and the song was thin as cobweb and fraying at every edge.

*Who comes?* said the lowest voice. It sounded like a grandmother who has been waiting a very long time.

*I—* I almost answered aloud. Caught myself. But the voices felt me anyway; the lean works both ways, and hiding inside my own skull is a skill I've practiced since childhood, but these weren't rats or gulls or marsh-foxes with their quick greedy thoughts. These were covenant-voices. Old magic. They saw me the way a lamp sees a moth.

*A speaker,* breathed the highest voice, and there was such naked hope in it that my eyes stung. *A true speaker, come down to the water. Oh, it has been so long. So long since anyone listened.*

"What do I do?" I whispered. My lips barely moved. Emeric watched me with an intensity that would have unnerved me any other day.

*You open the door,* said the middle voice — a younger one, a man's voice, cracked and salt-rimed. *But doors cost, little speaker. You know they cost. Nothing that opens stays shut behind it.*

"I don't understand."

*You will,* said the grandmother voice, almost gently. And then, all three together, a chord that shivered the surface of the whole lake into rings: *Ask us home.*

I didn't decide anything. That's the truth of it, however it sounds written down. Some part of me that was older than the Guild and its ledgers and its rules simply reached out the way you reach for a hand in the dark, and I said — silently, with everything I had — *Come home. Come up. Sing again.*

And the well opened.

Not physically. There was no dramatic eruption, no waterspout, nothing the Guild's enforcers could have put in one of their incident ledgers. What happened instead was that three notes rose out of the deep water and passed *through* me, and for one impossible moment I was a chord. I was the grandmother's patience and the young man's grief and the high voice's hope, and I heard the whole shape of the old covenant for a single heartbeat — twenty-one voices woven around a root-tree at the edge of the world, holding the wild places and the human places in one continuous song — before the vision tore away and left me holding nothing.

What it cost arrived immediately after, the way the bill follows the meal.

Cold. Cold poured into me like water into a boot, and not the ordinary cold of a fen in autumn. This cold went somewhere specific. It went into the place where my gift lived — the warm hidden room I'd kept locked since childhood — and when it finished pouring, I reached inward for that room and found it smaller. Noticeably smaller. A room with a wall moved inward. I could feel the missing space the way you feel a tooth that isn't there anymore: constantly, and worse when I touched it.

My hands had gone white-knuckled on the coracle rim. Somewhere above me Emeric was saying my name, then shouting it, and then the world tilted sideways and the fen water came up to meet me and was, frankly, warmer than I expected, being only ordinary cold.

* * *

Waking up on a peat bank wrapped in Emeric's coat is not the worst way I've spent an afternoon, but it ranks.

"She breathes," Quillfeather observed from somewhere near my feet. "Pity. I had already begun composing the elegy. It rhymed 'Mirabel' with 'farewell,' which took real craft."

"How long?"

"An hour. You sank like a stone and the warden hauled you out like one, and then you slept like one. You have a gift for mineral behavior."

Emeric crouched beside me. He looked terrible — wet to the waist, hollow-eyed, hair plastered to his forehead — and the look on his face was the one I was beginning to recognize as his version of panic, which is to say he appeared mildly inconvenienced by someone else's weather.

"You stopped hearing," he said. "Midway. Your face went — " He stopped, recalibrated. "Empty. Like a house with everyone gone out."

"I heard them." I sat up too fast and the world performed a small revolution. "Three voices. They were in the well — they're *of* the covenant, Emeric, actual pieces of the old pact, not messengers, not echoes. Pieces. And they're free now. They followed the chord up and out — I felt them go, north and east, toward the next—" I pressed a hand to my temple. The memory of the vision was already going soft at the edges, like ink in rain. "There's a tree. At the end of everything. All the voices go back to a tree."

"The Root of Accord," Emeric said quietly. "At World's Edge. Every warden learns the name. We learn it the way children learn the name of a country they'll never visit." His jaw worked. "We were taught the choir failed on its own. Rotted. Died of age. Nobody ever said it was *starving*, that it was calling for speakers, that — " He cut himself off and stared at the lake. "Forty years I wore the Warden's knot. Forty years of guarding a silence I was told was natural."

"Well," said Quillfeather, "the Guild does love its bookkeeping. Why keep accurate records when inaccurate ones are so much more profitable?"

I flexed my fingers and, carefully, reached inward.

The room was smaller. I checked twice, three times, counting my gift like coin — and I am very good at counting, it is the one trade the Guild ever taught me honestly. Before Thornmere, the beast-speech had filled me the way water fills an aquifer. Now I could feel the boundaries of it. Finite. Measurable. Diminishing.

Every use costs. The voices had told me plainly enough: *nothing that opens stays shut behind it.* Each time I opened myself to speak or listen in the deep way, I paid out a measure of the gift itself, and the gift was not bottomless. It had never been bottomless. I had simply never spent enough of it to notice, the way a rich woman never notices her purse until the purse is nearly flat.

And there were, according to the map, four more waypoints between here and World's Edge. Four more wells, or stones, or drowned bells full of covenant-voices wanting to come home. Each one would take another piece of the aquifer.

I did the arithmetic, because arithmetic is what I'm for.

If each opening cost what Thornmere had cost, I would arrive at the root-tree with perhaps a quarter of what I'd started with. If any single waypoint cost more — and the map's markings grew larger and stranger as they climbed toward World's Edge — I might arrive with nothing at all. With silence. With a lifetime of hearing the world talk and then, at the crucial moment, hearing nothing forever.

"Mirabel." Emeric's voice, low. "Whatever you just calculated, stop calculating it. You've got the ledger-face."

"I always have the ledger-face. It's a professional deformity."

"You've got the *bad* ledger-face. The one where the numbers don't work and you're deciding whether to tell anyone."

Quillfeather's ears swiveled toward me with predatory interest. I considered lying to both of them. I genuinely considered it — concealment is a comfortable coat, and I've worn mine a long time. But Emeric had jumped off a cliff for me two nights ago, metaphorically speaking, and Quillfeather had dragged me out of a bog by my collar, also metaphorically, although with him it's hard to say.

"The gift costs," I said. "Using it spends it. Today I spent — some. Enough to feel. And there are four more stops on this map, and I don't know if there's enough of me left to pay for all of them."

Silence, except for the lap of water and the distant commentary of a moorhen, who thought we were all idiots. She wasn't wrong.

"So we spend it wisely," Emeric said finally. "Ration it. No casual listening — no questioning every magpie and hedgehog between here and the mountains. Only the voices. Only what the map requires."

"You realize you're asking me to walk past a world full of talking creatures and ignore all of them. Do you know what that's like? It's like being told to sit in a tavern and not drink."

"Yes," said Emeric, with the ghost of something that on another man might have been a smile. "I imagine it's exactly like that."

Quillfeather said nothing for a long moment, which for Quillfeather is roughly equivalent to a human delivering a eulogy. Then he shook himself, fur bristling, and trotted to the waterline.

"There's a third thing," he said, not looking at me. "About costs. About what happens to gifts that get spent down to nothing." His tail lashed once. "But that's a conversation for a debt that hasn't come due yet. Ask me again when we're closer to the end of the map, girl. Assuming there's a me left to ask."

"That's ominous even by your standards."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them ominous." He flicked water from his paws. "Now. The voices went north and east — you said so yourself, and foxes trust speakers who've just been drowned, apparently, because we're sentimental that way. North and east lies the Ashspine road, three days' walk if the warden doesn't complain about his boots, which he will." He paused. His nose twitched. His whole body went suddenly, absolutely still, in the way of wild things that have heard something wrong.

"What?" Emeric said sharply.

"Smoke," Quillfeather said softly. "Upwind. Fresh. Somebody's burning the fen-drain bridges behind us — green wood, lots of it, meant to be seen for miles." He turned, and for once there was no mockery anywhere in him. "Your Guild friends aren't chasing us anymore, Mirabel. They're herding us. Someone wants us moving fast, along a specific road, tired and predictable." His amber eyes found mine. "Someone who knows exactly where this map leads."

Behind him, across the grey water, the drowned spire of Thornmere leaned its last degree and settled, with a sigh that no one but a speaker would ever have heard, into a slightly easier rest.

Three voices lighter in the world, and heavier on the road ahead. I got up, wrung out my skirts, and tried not to count what remained in the purse.
