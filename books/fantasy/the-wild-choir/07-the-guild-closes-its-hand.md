## 7. The Guild Closes Its Hand

I had been a warden of the Broken Order for nineteen years, and in all that time I had learned exactly one thing worth teaching: the Guild never sends one hunter when three will do, and never sends three when it can send a friend.

We saw the smoke before we heard the horns.

It rose behind us from the direction of the Salt Road, a dirty brown column against the grey morning, too wide to be a cookfire and too patient to be an accident. The Guild was burning the safe roads. All of them, if I knew Aldous Crane — and I did know Aldous Crane, had shared his watch-fire and his flask and, once, in the flooded spring of my third year, his last dry shirt. He would burn every bridge between the fens and the Ashspine not because it slowed us but because it told us he could. The Guild's arithmetic had always been theatrical.

"They're burning the road," Mirabel said, stating it the way she stated everything — like a line in a ledger she disapproved of. "The *road*. That's not even legal. There are toll-keepers' charters on that road older than the Guild."

"There are charters older than the sun," Quillfeather said from her shoulder-pack, where he rode like a disgraced admiral surveying his own sinking fleet, "and the Guild has used most of them to light its pipes."

"We'll lose two days going around through the cut-fields," I said.

"Two days." Mirabel did the math aloud, because of course she did; I had watched her count everything since the salt door — steps, rations, voices remaining, days until solstice — as if the numbers themselves might be argued into mercy. "The well gave us three voices at the cost of—" she stopped. She never said what it cost. Her nose had bled for an hour afterward, and she'd let me believe it was altitude, which was generous of both of us, since there is no altitude in a drowned village.

"The cut-fields it is," I said.

* * *

The cut-fields were peat country, black ground stitched with old drainage channels, and walking them was like walking across a corpse that hadn't decided yet whether to complain. The channels ran in straight lines to nowhere. Reeds stood in ranks like unpaid soldiers. Somewhere beneath our feet lay the roots of a forest the Guild's great-grandfathers had drowned for fuel, and I will swear to my grave — which may be soon, so consider this sworn — that some of those roots still hummed.

Mirabel stopped twice to put her hand flat on the ground and listen. She thought I didn't see. I saw.

"Anything?" I asked the second time.

"Mud," she said. "Mud complaining about the weather. It's very bitter about the drainage." A pause. "It says the Guild promised the water somewhere else to go."

"And did it?"

"No."

Quillfeather snorted from the pack. "A promise the wild remembers and a debt the Guild denies. Where have I heard that song? Oh yes. *Everywhere.* It's the only song left."

"It's not the only one," Mirabel said quietly, "or we wouldn't be carrying the map."

I let that stand, because arguing with hope felt rude, like kicking a candle.

* * *

We made the far edge of the fields by dusk and found the way north blocked by a thing I recognized and wished I didn't: a Guild cordon-stake, iron banded in white enamel, humming with a silence-charm that ate sound within twenty paces. Under its influence the wind went mute, the reeds went mute, and Quillfeather — who had been composing a ballad about my leadership all afternoon — opened his mouth and produced nothing at all.

His expression, I want recorded, was exquisite. If I died tomorrow I should want it carved on my stone.

"A silence-field," I said. "They're ahead of us, not behind. They've ringed the fields."

"How many stakes make a ring?" Mirabel asked.

"For this much ground? Twelve. Twelve hunters minimum. And twelve means—"

"*Crane*," Quillfeather mouthed, soundlessly, with enormous relish.

"Aldous Crane," I confirmed. Senior hunter. Nineteen years a senior hunter, same as me senior warden; we'd come up through the trials together, back when I believed the Order protected things instead of inventorying them. He had a laugh like a gate swinging and a memory like a debt-collector. He also, I now remembered with a lurch, owed me his life twice over, and men like Aldous hate being owed.

"So we go around the ring," Mirabel said.

"You can't go around a ring."

"Then under it. Or through the water. Or—" she looked at the stake, at the dead circle of air around it, and something in her face shifted, the ledger-line hardening into decision. "Or we break it. If the field eats sound, then sound is its food. And I know where to get more sound than it can swallow."

I should have asked what she meant. In my defense, I was busy watching the horizon behind us, where the brown smoke had been joined by a second column, closer, and by lights moving in the disciplined pattern of hunters who had done this a hundred times and enjoyed it perhaps ninety.

"Quickly, then," I said.

What she meant turned out to be the reed-beds. She knelt at the edge of the nearest channel and pressed both palms to the mud and did whatever it is she does — the thing she hides, the thing that is costing her something with every use, though she'd die before itemizing it — and the marsh answered. Not loudly. Not dramatically. But the frogs began, in their thousands, a chorus rising out of the dark water like the ground itself finding its voice after a century of being told to hush, and the silence-field around the stake drank and drank and *choked*, and the charm cracked with a sound like ice leaving a pond, and the noise that came out of that broken circle woke every bird in three counties.

Twelve hunters, wherever they were, now knew precisely where we were.

"That," Quillfeather said hoarsely, as the frog-song rolled over us, "was either brilliant or suicidal, and I genuinely cannot tell, which is my favorite kind of plan."

"It bought us the stake," Mirabel said. She was swaying. I took her arm without asking and she let me, which was new, and I filed that away next to the bleeding nose and the cost she never named.

"Stakes are iron," I managed. "Iron is proof against almost nothing except patience."

"Then we take the patience and run."

* * *

He found us at moonrise, because of course he did. Aldous Crane always found you at the moment you'd just begun to believe the night was yours.

We'd made the shingle flats beyond the cut-fields, a grey waste of stones and shallow braided water, good open ground for walking and terrible ground for hiding, and I was just thinking *we should not be here* when a lantern flared on the ridge behind us and a voice carried across the flats, unhurried, warm as a gate swinging.

"Emeric Vail!"

My whole body knew that voice before my head admitted it. Nineteen years of shared watches sat up in my spine.

"Don't answer," Mirabel whispered.

"I wasn't going to sing him a ballad."

"He might enjoy that," Quillfeather muttered. "Everyone loves your ballads. Especially the part where you're the hero."

Crane came down the ridge alone, as they always do, the ones who mean to talk first. Lantern held high. No drawn blade. His face in the lantern-light was exactly as I remembered it — broad, weather-cut, creased around the eyes from laughing at things that weren't funny — and seeing it did something ugly to my chest that I chose not to examine.

"Warden," he called, stopping forty paces off, close enough for talk and no closer. "You look terrible. Fen-water agree with you?"

"It's the company," I called back. "What do you want, Aldous?"

"What I've always wanted. To bring you home." He set the lantern down on a stone, deliberately, a man clearing a table for honest conversation. "Voss tore up your warrant herself. Did you know that? Not revoked. *Torn.* She wants you back, Emeric. Full reinstatement. Your rank, your pension, your name off the broken rolls. All of it."

Behind me, Mirabel had gone very still. Quillfeather had gone very quiet, which for him was louder.

"And all I have to do," I said, "is hand over the girl and the map."

"All you have to do," Crane said gently, "is stop lying to yourself about what she is. She's a beast-speaker, Em. An unregistered gift. You know what the law says. You know what happens to unregistered gifts." He spread his hands. "You think I like this? You think any of us like this? The silence keeps people safe. I've seen what the wild does when it wakes hungry. We all have. That's the whole point of the walls and the bells and the roads. You walk around with her for another week and you'll see it too, and then you'll wish you'd taken the offer while it was warm."

"The wild isn't waking hungry, Aldous. It's starving. There's a difference, and the difference is us."

He sighed, and it was the sigh of a man who had rehearsed this and hated the script. "Nineteen years, Em. You pulled me out of the Mireholt with a broken leg and a rope made of belt-straps. I haven't forgotten. So I'm asking once, friend to friend, off the record, before the other eleven come down that ridge and it stops being a conversation." His voice dropped, went rough. "Walk away. Let me take her in clean. You keep your name. She keeps her life — Voss doesn't want her dead, you know that better than anyone, Voss doesn't waste assets. Nobody has to burn."

And there it was, laid out fair and square on the shingle between us: my name, my nineteen years, my pension and rank and the small warm rooms of the Order I'd given my life to, weighed against a woman I'd known a fortnight, a fox I actively disliked, and a map made of bone.

It should have been a harder sum than it was.

"You're doing your arithmetic wrong," I said.

Crane's face changed. "Em—"

"She counted the voices, Aldous. Not me, her. Twenty-one in the covenant. Two missing. Three days to solstice, give or take what your friends burned behind us. And here's the thing about ledgers — someone taught me this recently — the entries don't care whose name is on them. The wild gave. We took. Every charter, every bell, every burned road, it's all one long withdrawal, and tonight the account comes due." I stepped forward, past Mirabel, putting myself — without deciding to, the body deciding on its own old warden instincts — between her and the ridge. "You want to take her? Bring the eleven."

For a moment Crane looked at me the way you look at a house you grew up in, burning.

"You always were the best of us," he said. "That's the pity of it."

He whistled.

What came down the ridge was not eleven men in formation. It was nets and hooks and the particular silence of professionals, and I got Mirabel moving — *run the flats, the water slows them* — and then it was knives and stones and lantern-light strobing off wet stone, and I fought the way I hadn't fought since the trials, ugly and close, buying yards with blood I couldn't spare. A hook caught my shoulder and tore. Something heavy found the side of my head. Through it all I heard Crane shouting *alive, take them alive, Voss wants them breathing* and I thought: *good, breathing men pull their strikes,* and pulled none of mine.

Then the frogs came again.

Out of the dark channels, out of the drowned peat, out of every ditch and drain for a mile, the marsh rose in voice — frogs, birds, something vast and wordless underneath it all, the hum of old roots under black ground — and the hunters staggered as if struck, hands to ears, charms crackling, and in that ringing chaos Mirabel's hand found my collar and hauled, and Quillfeather's teeth found somebody's wrist, and we broke east through the shallows with the whole marsh singing cover for us.

We ran until the flats ended. Then we ran because running was all we knew how to do.

* * *

We stopped in the ruins of an old toll-house, roofless, its charter-board still nailed above the door, the letters rotted to ghosts. Mirabel sat me down against the wall and looked at my shoulder and went the color of tallow.

"This needs stitching."

"It needs a drink."

"It needs *stitching*." She was already tearing her sleeve into strips, hands steady the way hands get when they refuse to shake. "You didn't have to do that. Stand between us. He offered you everything back."

"Yes."

"And you said no."

"It seemed impolite to say yes to the man planning to net my colleague."

She tied off a knot harder than strictly necessary. "Emeric."

"Mirabel."

"Why?"

I looked out through the empty doorway at the dark, at the distant smudge of the burning roads, at the stars coming up over the Ashspine we still had to cross. Nineteen years of believing myself loyal to an order that had sold its soul for quiet, and it had taken a fortnight and a bleeding stranger's dying whisper to show me the ledger had been false all along.

"Because someone has to keep the accounts honestly," I said. "And apparently it's going to be us."

She finished the last knot and sat back on her heels, and for a moment neither of us said anything, and the silence between us was — for once — the good kind. The kind that listens.

Quillfeather limped in from the doorway, spat delicately, and said, "Well. That was touching. Now if you're quite done, the fox has counted eleven hunters, one angry Crane, and approximately zero remaining roads north. Which means mountains." He looked at me, and beneath the sardonic armor his eyes were old and tired and afraid. "High ones, warden. With stone-giants in them. Who, I am told, *hum*."

Mirabel touched the bone map at her throat. "Then we climb."

Above the toll-house, far off, the fires on the Salt Road still burned — and past them, faint but unmistakable, riding the wind down from the peaks, came a sound I had not heard since childhood.

Someone — or something — was singing in the Ashspine.
