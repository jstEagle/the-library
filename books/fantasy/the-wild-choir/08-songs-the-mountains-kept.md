## 8. Songs the Mountains Kept

The Ashspine did not so much rise as *refuse*. That was my first impression of it from the foothills: a range that had looked at the sky and declined to be polite about it. Peaks like broken teeth, snow in their gums, and between them passes so narrow that Emeric kept muttering about avalanches with the cheerful authority of a man who had read about them but never been buried by one.

"You're sure this is where the map wants us," he said for the fourth time that morning. He held the bone map at arm's length, tilting it against the light as though it might confess if he caught it at the right angle.

"I'm sure of what it shows," I said. "A notch in the ridge shaped like a closed mouth. Whether the mountain agrees to have one is another matter."

Quillfeather trotted ahead of us on the frozen track, tail high, ears swiveling with theatrical diligence. "For the record," he said, "foxes are not built for altitude. We are built for marshes, henhouses, and morally ambiguous situations at low elevation. If I die up here, I want it noted that my last words were complaints, and they were *justified*."

"Noted," I said.

"Also I'd like someone to tell the story properly afterward. None of this 'the brave fox perished' nonsense. Say 'the fox died mid-sentence, complaining, exactly as he lived.'"

"Quillfeather."

"Yes?"

"Shut up and walk."

He shot me a look over his shoulder that was mostly affection and entirely insulted, and walked.

Three days we had climbed since leaving the drowned village behind, three days since the well-voices had come loose from the dark water and folded themselves into the map's bone like light into glass. Three voices recovered, eighteen to go, and every step north colder than the last. My hands were cracked from the wind. Emeric had taken to walking slightly on my windward side without mentioning it, which was his way of saying things he couldn't say, and which I pretended not to notice because noticing out loud would have required me to say things too, and there wasn't time, and also I would have set fire to my own face doing it.

The notch in the ridge appeared at dusk on the third day — or rather, I heard it before I saw it.

That's the thing about my gift that no ledger ever accounted for. It isn't only speech. It's *song*, sometimes, or the memory of song, humming in things that have no mouths. And as we came around a shoulder of black rock, I felt it through my boot-soles first: a low vibration, patient and vast, like a hymn being hummed by the floor of the world.

"Do you feel that?" I whispered.

Emeric stopped. His hand went to the broken warding-signet he still wore, out of habit more than faith. "I feel the ground moving."

"It's not moving. It's *humming*."

Quillfeather's fur had gone up along his spine in a ridge. "I hate when the small ones hear things I can't," he muttered. "It's very rude. Like whispering at dinner."

* *

The pass opened into a bowl of stone, walled on three sides by cliffs, floored with old snow that had never quite melted even in summer. And standing in that bowl, half sunk into the mountainside itself as if the mountain had grown around them rather than merely holding them, were five figures.

Giants. Stone-giants — not the boulder-trolls of nursery frights, but something older and quieter. They were perhaps forty feet tall, or fifty, shaped roughly like people if people were carved from granite by someone who loved them but worked slowly. Lichen grew on their shoulders in patterns that might have been embroidery once. Their eyes were closed. And from each of their throats came the humming I had felt through my boots — five notes, interwoven, circling, incomplete.

They weren't asleep. They were *waiting*, the way you wait through the long silence after a question.

I knew that silence. I had lived inside it my whole life.

"Don't run," Emeric breathed beside me. "Wardens' counsel. Giants of the old choirs don't hunt what stands still."

"They don't look like they hunt anything," I said. "They look like they've forgotten what they were waiting for."

"One of us should remind them," said Quillfeather, who was already hiding behind Emeric's boot, which was brave of him, considering Emeric's boots could have sheltered six foxes.

I stepped forward into the snow.

The humming changed. Not stopped — changed, the way a room changes when someone new enters it. The nearest giant's head turned. It took a long time, the way continental drift takes a long time, but turn it did, grinding softly, until its closed eyes faced me. Then, with a sound like a quarry deciding something, it spoke.

Its voice was not loud. That surprised me most. A voice that size should have flattened the bowl walls; instead it arrived inside my bones, unhurried, worn smooth as river stone.

"*Small singer.*"

"I'm not—" I began, and then stopped, because arguing taxonomy with a forty-foot granite choir-member seemed unwise. "I'm looking for voices. Voices of the covenant. There were twenty-one once. I've found three."

The giant's eyes opened.

I had expected eyes like cave-mouths, dark and empty. Instead they were pale as frost-quartz, and full — full of an attention so complete that I felt inventory-taken, weighed, and somehow *known*, all at once. Somewhere behind me Quillfeather made a very small noise and pretended he hadn't.

"*Twenty-one,*" the giant rumbled. "*You count like a keeper. Who taught you to count, small singer?*"

"My mother," I said. "And ledgers. Mostly ledgers." I hesitated. "My father drew maps."

Something moved through the giant then — I can't call it a flinch; flinches are quick, and nothing about this creature was quick. It was slower than a flinch and deeper, a settling, the way a whole hillside settles after rain. The other four giants' humming faltered and re-formed around a new note, lower.

"*Maps,*" it said. "*Bone maps? Maps that remember?*"

The cold went through me sideways. I pulled the map from its oilcloth with numb fingers and held it up, and the moment bone met open air the giants all went quiet — truly quiet, the humming dropping away like a tide going out, and the silence it left behind was enormous, and grieving, and familiar.

The nearest giant leaned down. Snow slid off its shoulders. Its breath smelled of deep stone and, faintly, impossibly, of woodsmoke and bread — the smell of somewhere warm, somewhere long ago, and my eyes stung before I understood why.

"*I know this hand,*" the giant said. "*This is Aldous's work. Aldous Amberdale.*"

The name went through me like a splinter finding its depth.

"That's my father's name," I said. My voice came out smaller than I intended. "How do you know my father's name?"

* *

We sat in the snow — Emeric and I sat; Quillfeather sat on Emeric's shoulder, having determined that shoulder was now the strategic center of the universe — while the giant, whose name was Orrenvast but who told us to call him Orren "because the rest is a sound your throat hasn't built yet," lowered himself with tectonic patience onto the bowl floor across from us. Up close he was less like a statue and more like a landscape that had decided to have opinions. His skin was veined with quartz that glimmered faintly in time with the other four giants' resumed humming, softer now, a background chord like a house holding its breath around a difficult conversation.

"Aldous came here thirty-one years ago," Orren said. "Young, then. Your kind stays young so briefly; it always startles us. He carried a map half-made and a question fully formed." The quartz veins brightened. "He wanted to know why the choir was thinning."

"Thinning?"

"The covenant is not a wall, small singer. It is a *song*. Songs need singers, and singers need listeners, and listeners need reasons to stay. For a thousand years your people and ours traded verses across the boundary lines — your harvest-songs for our root-chants, your bell-ringing for our thunder-humming. Then your Guild began buying. Quietly. Relics, bells, boundary stones. Every purchase a verse removed." One massive hand opened, palm up, and in it lay a fist-sized chunk of crystal that hummed faintly when the wind crossed it. "We sang louder to fill the gaps. Loud singing cracks stone. We learned to sing *less*. To hold our verses in our chests and wait." His frost-pale eyes fixed on me. "We have been waiting a long time. Waiting is the hardest verse."

Beside me, Emeric had gone very still. I watched him take this in — the confirmation, in a giant's voice, of what the rats of the warehouse had told me in whispers, of what his own order had buried. His jaw worked. He said nothing, but his hand found the broken signet again and turned it over and over, like a man worrying a wound to check whether it still hurt.

It did. You could see it did.

"And my father?" I asked. "Why did he come? Why does a cartographer climb into the Ashspine asking questions about a song?"

Orren was quiet for a long moment. Above us the peaks had gone violet with dusk, and the first stars came out, hard and close.

"He came because of a woman," Orren said at last. "A woman who sang to rivers."

The world tilted gently, the way a boat tilts when something large moves underneath it.

"My mother," I said.

"She was known here. Not by name — names are yours, not ours — but by her voice. She came up the river valleys in the years before you were born, listening, writing songs down, teaching them back to the water. She was a keeper's daughter, I think, or a keeper herself. She and Aldous found each other the way two verses find each other. They came together, twice. The second time she was carrying you." The giant's voice gentled, which should not have been possible for a voice like gravel and glacier, and yet. "You were very small. You slept against her collarbone while she argued with me about notation."

"I don't remember any of this."

"You were small," Orren agreed. "But she remembered it *for* you. She asked me to keep the memory, in case she ever couldn't." The quartz in his skin dimmed, note by note, like windows going dark. "Keepers think ahead. It is their gift and their grief."

"What happened to her?" The question came out of me before I could dress it in caution. "Everyone says she died. Nobody will say how. There's no grave. There's no—there isn't anything, there's just a hole where she used to be, and my father stopped drawing anything except that map, and then he died too, and nobody—" My voice cracked. Quillfeather's paw pressed briefly, weightlessly, against the side of my neck, and I was grateful and furious at myself in equal measure.

Orren looked at me for a long time. Behind him the other giants hummed, and I realized with a lurch that they had shifted their chord again — softened it, rounded it, wrapped it around the bowl like something meant to hold a grieving creature together. They had been doing it for some minutes. They were good at it. They had clearly had practice.

"The last time your parents came," Orren said slowly, "was nineteen years ago. The covenant was failing badly even then. The anchor was failing. Do you know what the anchor is, small singer?"

Emeric stirred. "There are references," he said carefully. "In the oldest warden texts. A living binding. But the passages were sealed. Even the Grand Chapter's copies are redacted."

"Of course they are," said Quillfeather bitterly, from the shoulder. "Your Guild redacts everything. They probably redacted the weather."

"The anchor is a singer," Orren said, ignoring him, or granting him the mercy of ignoring him. "One voice chosen — or offered — to hold the covenant's root-note. All the other voices hang upon it. When the anchor weakens, the whole song thins. Nineteen years ago, the anchor was nearly gone. Your parents came to ask how it might be replaced." His eyes closed again. "We told them the truth. The anchor cannot be replaced by force or purchase. It must be *given*. Freely. By a speaker whose gift is true and whole. And there were so few left whose gift was true. The Guild had seen to that."

The snow creaked under my shifting weight. My heart was doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional.

"What did they do?" I whispered. "My parents. What did they decide?"

Orren was silent so long that the dusk deepened into true dark, and the quartz veins in his skin became the brightest thing in the bowl, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart.

"Aldous left with the map unfinished," he said finally. "He said he needed to find the voices scattered across the lowlands before any answer could be reached — that a covenant cannot be reforged from the top of a mountain, it must be gathered from everywhere it broke. He was right. He was also followed. Your Guild was already afraid of him." A pause, tectonic. "He never finished the map. But someone did, later, in his hand, in his style. Someone who knew his shorthand. Someone he had taught."

The map sat in my lap like a live coal.

"I thought he made it," I said. "I thought—I found it among his things. I've been carrying it for weeks thinking it was his."

"It is his," Orren said. "And it is hers. And it is *yours*, small singer, or it would not have let you carry it. Bone remembers the family that feeds it." He inclined his great head toward it. "Ask yourself who finished it, and why they never sent it to you, and why you had to find it hidden. Ask yourself what your mother would have had to become, to keep a secret that large from a daughter who loved her."

The bowl rang with silence. Even the giants' humming had stopped.

"Mother's dead," I said. It came out flat. Automatic. Nineteen years of saying it, worn smooth as a stair-stone. "She died when I was six."

"Does she have a grave?" asked Quillfeather quietly, from the shoulder. His voice had lost every scrap of sarcasm, and somehow that was worse.

I didn't answer. There was no grave. There had never been a grave. There had been a story, told gently, endlessly, by neighbors and Guild clerks and my father's silent shrug — *taken by fever, taken by the road, taken* — and I had swallowed it whole because children swallow what they're given, and I had built my whole life on the foundation of a story with no body in it.

"There is one more thing," Orren said. "And I will give it to you whether it breaks you or not, because Aldous gave us truth freely and I owe his blood the same coin."

"Tell me," I said.

"The woman who sang to rivers did not die of fever," said the giant. "Nineteen years ago, when the anchor failed and no replacement had been given, someone climbed the root-tree at the world's edge and offered herself. Freely. As the covenant demands." The quartz veins flared, and dimmed. "The covenant accepted. She has held the root-note ever since. Failing, thinning, holding. She has been the anchor for nineteen years, small singer." His frost-white eyes opened and fixed on mine, ancient and sorry and certain. "And the anchor's name, in the old notation your mother invented and wrote into the margins of every song she ever collected—"

He hummed it.

Two notes. Low, then high. Simple as a lullaby. Simple as a name.

I had heard those two notes my entire childhood, sung under her breath while she cooked, while she mended, while she rocked me — and I had thought they were nothing, a habit, a tune with no words. She had been signing her work. She had been leaving her name in every song, waiting for someone with ears to find it.

The map slipped from my numb fingers into the snow.

"Mirabel," Emeric said softly, and caught my elbow, and I let him, because the alternative was the ground.

Above us, the five giants began again — the same five notes they'd been holding for nineteen years, the fragment of the covenant they'd refused to let die — but this time, for the first time, they added the sixth. Her two notes. Woven in. Held.

Somewhere at the world's edge, my mother was still singing.

And three days' ride south, in a counting house that smelled of ink and caged birds, the Guild had just received word that the Amberdale map had surfaced — I knew it the way you know weather coming, the way rats know a ship is sinking. Whatever Serane Voss believed about silence, she would not let the anchor's daughter walk toward the root-tree unchallenged.

"We leave at first light," I said.

Quillfeather sighed from the shoulder. "Of course we do," he said. "Up the death-mountain, toward the army, in winter. I want it recorded that foxes also die of *ambition*."

But his paw stayed against my neck all night, all the same.
