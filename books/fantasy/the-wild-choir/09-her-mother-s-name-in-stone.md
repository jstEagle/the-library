## 9. Her Mother's Name in Stone

The giant's hall was not a hall the way I understood halls. There were no walls, exactly—only the suggestion of them, vast slabs of mountain that had leaned together sometime before my grandmother's grandmother learned to fear thunder, and a ceiling of hanging stone that dripped in slow centuries rather than fast seconds. We sat—or rather, the giant permitted us to sit—on a shelf of granite worn smooth by what I could only assume were generations of enormous grief.

The fire in the middle of it all burned blue-green and gave off no heat I could feel, only a warmth like being remembered.

"You said you knew the map's maker," I said, for the third time, because my voice kept failing on the way to the end of the sentence. "Bramble-Root. You said he was a friend of my father."

The giant who had spoken first—she had introduced herself as Ondu-Serra, which she pronounced as a sound like an avalanche deciding to be patient—regarded me with eyes like two deep wells with stars drowned at the bottom. When she spoke, it took her a long time, not because she was slow, but because each word seemed to be chosen from somewhere very far away and carried back with great care.

"Bramble-Root was his name among us," she rumbled. "Among your kind, he was called Aldous Amberdale."

The floor did not move. The mountain did not groan. And yet everything tilted, because my father had been dead for eleven years and I had never once heard anyone speak his name in this tone—not grief, exactly, and not reverence either, but something worse: *recognition*. As if he had mattered here, in a place I hadn't known existed until three days ago.

"He was a mapmaker," I said. My voice came out flat and small. "He made maps of trade routes. He drew coastlines. He—" I stopped, because I remembered, suddenly and horribly clearly, the maps he used to draw at night when he thought I was asleep. The ones that weren't coastlines. The ones full of places with names that sounded like songs sung through water.

"Your father walked between worlds," Ondu-Serra said. "He carried the old paths in his ink. It was he who taught the last wardens where the voices slept." Her gaze slid sideways, to Emeric, who had gone very still beside me in the way of a man trying to become furniture. "And it was your mother who taught him why it mattered."

* * *

I should explain something about mothers, or at least about mine.

Lila Amberdale died when I was nine years old. That was the story, told so often and so gently that it wore smooth as a river pebble: a fever, taken quick, buried under the rowan tree behind our house because she'd loved rowans. I remembered her mostly in fragments—the smell of rosemary crushed between fingers, a habit of humming when she thought no one listened, the way she could stand in the garden at dusk and every bird within earshot would go quiet, attentive, like an audience before the overture. I had assumed everyone's mother had that effect. I had assumed a great many things.

"I need you to say whatever it is you're not saying," I told the giant. "Quickly. Like tearing off a bandage, if bandages weighed forty tons."

Quillfeather, sprawled along the granite shelf with one paw draped over his snout in a posture of theatrical boredom he maintained even while asleep, opened one amber eye. "She means it," he said. "I've seen her tear off actual bandages. She has no ceremony whatsoever."

"Hush, fox," said Ondu-Serra, without heat. Then, to me, with heat enough to melt the marrow of the world:

"When the covenant began to fail—when the silence first crept in from the edges, eating the song note by note—the choir needed an anchor. A living voice, freely given, rooted at the covenant's heart, holding what remained together the way a keystone holds an arch. The voices searched. They chose a woman whose song was strong enough to bear the weight." She paused. The blue-green fire guttered, and shadows moved across the walls like slow animals. "Her name was Lila."

The word landed in the center of my chest and stayed there, burning.

"No," I said. It came out very reasonably, almost politely. "No, that's—she died. Of a fever. Under the rowan tree. There's a grave. I *wept* at the grave, I was nine, I have receipts—"

"Graves can be filled with anything," Emeric said quietly. His hand hovered near my shoulder, not quite touching, the way he'd been hovering near touching me for days now, like a man negotiating with a border he wasn't sure he had the authority to cross. "Mirabel. Let her finish."

"Let her finish?" I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, a sound I didn't recognize as mine. "Let her finish telling me my mother is alive? Do you know how long I've been—" But I couldn't finish that sentence either, because finishing it meant admitting how long I'd been carrying her absence around like a stone sewn into my coat lining, how I'd built my whole careful, hidden, ledgered life on top of that weight and called the arrangement normal.

Ondu-Serra watched me with those drowned-star eyes. "She did not die," the giant said. "But hear the whole of it before you hope. Hope is a door that opens both ways."

* * *

Here is what the giants knew, gathered in fragments over years of humming to each other in the dark of the Ashspine, pieced together like a song missing most of its verses.

When the covenant frayed—and the giants would not say precisely why; they looked at Emeric when they said this, and Emeric looked at the fire, and I filed that away in the drawer of things to interrogate later—a living anchor was required. Not seized. Never seized; the covenant's law forbade compulsion, which is perhaps why it failed, since its human half had gotten quite good at pretending laws were decorative. The anchor had to be *given*, freely, fully, knowing the cost.

The cost was everything. The anchor gave her voice, her freedom, her name itself, poured into the root-tree at the world's edge to hold the dying pact together. In exchange—this was the terrible arithmetic—the covenant would survive. Diminished. Starving. But alive, waiting for someone to gather its scattered voices and sing it whole again.

"They chose your mother because her gift was rare," Ondu-Serra said. "Rarer than yours, child. She could hear not only beasts but the land itself. Stones. Rivers. The slow speech of roots." A rumble that might, in a smaller creature, have been a sigh. "You come by your listening honestly. Though hers was deeper, and deeper-cutting, and it is why they asked her and no other."

"And my father?" My voice had gone strange and thin. "Did he give her? Did he let her go?"

"He argued against it for a season and a day," said a second giant, older, whose name I had not caught and whose voice sounded like a glacier clearing its throat. "Then he spent eleven years drawing maps to help undo what could not be undone any other way. Then he died. Grief is heavy cargo, even for couriers."

Eleven years. Eleven years of secret maps and midnight humming and a daughter who thought her father was just a tired man who drew coastlines. Eleven years of him knowing—*knowing*—that my mother was not under the rowan tree, and never saying, because the anchor's location was the covenant's most guarded secret, because a revealed anchor is a murdered anchor, because—

Because he'd been protecting me. And protecting her. And doing both badly, the way we do all our most important things.

"The grave," I whispered. "What's in the grave?"

"Stone," said Ondu-Serra simply. "A stone shaped like sleeping. Your father's own carving. He wanted somewhere to put his hands."

* * *

I don't remember standing up. I remember the shelf of granite under my feet, and the fire's green light, and Quillfeather rising smoothly to all four feet with none of his usual commentary, which frightened me more than anything the giants had said. A silent fox is an omen. A silent fox watching you with something almost like gentleness is a catastrophe wearing fur.

"She's been down there for twenty-two years," I heard myself say. "Rooted. Holding up a dying song with her *voice*. Does she—can she even—does she remember—" My knees informed me they were considering resigning. I sat back down hard on the granite. Somewhere far away, Emeric said my name again, and again, in the low even tone of a man talking someone back from a ledge.

"Hey. Hey. Look at me."

I looked at him. His face was close, gray-eyed, uncharacteristically stripped of warden's composure—he looked, for once, like a man rather than an institution having a bad decade.

"You survived the Guild's counting house for ten years," he said. "You stared down Serane Voss's enforcers with a forged ledger under your arm and lied in flawless bureaucratic prose. You are not allowed to fall apart now, in front of a *giant*, when we finally have something worth falling apart about."

"That's the worst comfort I've ever received," I managed.

"It gets better. Breathe first."

I breathed. It was a struggle, breathing; my ribs had apparently unionized against it. But I breathed, and the fire crackled its colorless crackle, and Quillfeather settled back down against my hip—warm, solid, deliberately heavier than a fox needs to be, the way cats sit on grieving chests.

"Now the worst part," Emeric said, softer. "Ask it."

"Is she *suffering*?"

The question hung there. The older giant answered, and his answer was gentle the way avalanches are gentle if you're standing exactly right.

"An anchor does not suffer as flesh suffers. She suffers as a song suffers—stretched, thinned, asked to hold more than her notes can carry. She has been singing alone for twenty-two years, child, holding twenty voices' worth of promise in one throat. Some nights, the mountain hears her forget which verse comes next. Some nights, the mountain hears her forget her own." He paused. "But some nights—fewer now, as the solstice nears—some nights she sings a lullaby. Old words. Human words. And the wind carries them down out of the Ashspine, and we do not ask whom she is singing them to. Because we suspect we already know."

I had not cried in eleven years. I had rationed it, budgeted it, entered it in no ledger. Now the whole withheld account came due at once, and I wept the way mountains fall—in silence, massively, taking a great deal of scenery with me.

Emeric held me through it. He didn't say anything clever, which may be the single finest thing he has ever done. He just held me, one hand steady between my shoulder blades, his chin resting on my hair, and if his own breath shook a little I was polite enough not to enter that in any record either.

* * *

Later—hours later, or minutes, time being unreliable in giant country—Ondu-Serra brought something and set it before me with a delicacy that would have been comic from any other creature the size of a chapel. A flat slab of slate, the size of a dinner plate, its surface worked with lines so fine they might have been etched with a needle-thin beam of light.

"Your father left this with us," she said. "For the one who came asking after the Choir. For his blood."

I turned it over with numb fingers. It was a map—but not like his others. This one showed a single route, from the Ashspine down through lands I didn't recognize, to a place marked with a symbol I did know, because I'd seen it carved above the well in the drowned village: the sign of the root-tree. The covenant's heart. World's edge.

And beneath the symbol, in my father's handwriting—small, urgent, pressed hard enough into the slate to nearly cut through:

*If you are reading this, you found the voices. Good. Better than good. Listen carefully, little ledger-keeper: the anchor holds, but barely, and the thing that broke the covenant is not finished. It wears promises now. It eats them. Your mother will tell you herself, if any of her is left to tell. Free her if you can. Forgive me if you can't. Both, ideally, in that order.*

"Dad," I said to the slate, uselessly, the way one does. "You absolute, secretive, cowardly, brave *man*."

"He spoke of you," Ondu-Serra offered. "Often. He said you counted things."

"I count things," I agreed wetly.

"He said it was a gift. That a person who counts what others overlook sees the shape of what's missing." The giant's drowned-star eyes blinked, slowly, tectonically. "We believe he was right. We believe you see it now."

I did. Oh, I did. Twenty-two years of missing, laid out in a single line of slate and ink, ending at a tree at the edge of the world where my mother hung suspended between woman and song, forgetting her own name one verse at a time.

Three weeks ago my greatest ambition had been a clean audit.

"We leave at dawn," I said.

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of the giants' outer wall, under a sky so crowded with stars it looked like the heavens had also heard the news and come out to gossip. Emeric took first watch. Quillfeather took second, allegedly. I lay on my bedroll staring upward, running the numbers the way I always ran numbers when the world went sideways: three voices recovered, eighteen remaining, solstice in nineteen days, one mother at the end of the road.

Except she wasn't at the *end* of the road. She was at the *root* of it. And the map said the traitor-voice—the hungry one, the one Voss's memory had hinted at, the one Emeric still wouldn't meet my eyes about—was somewhere along this same path.

"You're doing the face," Quillfeather said, materializing at my shoulder out of nowhere, as foxes do.

"What face?"

"The one where you're adding up figures and one of them keeps refusing to stay in its column." His ears flicked toward Emeric's distant silhouette against the starlight. "The warden knows something. Has known since the well, maybe since before. Watch how he goes quiet whenever the giants mention *why* the covenant broke. Men go quiet about two kinds of things, ledger-keeper: things they've done, and things they've seen coming."

"Emeric defected for me. He took a knife for me."

"Yes, he's terribly noble, it's very tedious. Nobility and honesty are different organs, Mirabel. Plenty of creatures have one and not the other." Quillfeather curled up against my side, tail over nose, and his voice dropped to something almost too quiet for the space between us. "Ask him tomorrow. And then ask yourself why a fox who owes a debt keeps finding reasons to delay collecting it."

I started to ask what he meant.

He was already asleep, or performing sleep with the commitment of a professional, and outside the wall of the mountain, far off in the dark, I heard—or dreamed I heard—a thread of melody riding the wind down from some high place I couldn't name. Old words. Human words.

A lullaby.

I knew the tune. God help me, I knew the tune.
