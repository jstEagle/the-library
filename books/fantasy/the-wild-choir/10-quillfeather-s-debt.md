## 10. Quillfeather's Debt

There are three things a marsh-fox never does: apologize, explain himself, and sleep in the same den twice. I was about to break two of them, and I'd have broken the third if Mirabel hadn't insisted we make camp in the Ashspine foothills instead of pushing through the night.

"It's called rest," she said, dropping her pack with a thud that suggested the ledger she carried had gained weight. Everything gains weight on this journey. The map. The voices. The silences between us when Emeric goes to scout and it's just her and me and the fire.

"I've heard of it," I said. "Rumored to exist somewhere north of stubbornness."

"You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you say six words when you mean a hundred."

I flicked my tail at her — the universal fox gesture for *mind your own business* — and went to check the perimeter, which is what I call walking in a small circle and thinking about things I don't want to think about.

Here's what I didn't want to think about: the debt. My debt. The one I named back in the fens with all the gravity of a fox negotiating his life's one serious contract, and which she accepted without flinching, because that's what she does. She flinches at nothing except kindness offered to herself.

The debt comes due soon. Sooner than the solstice, if the giants' humming was any indication, and stone-giants are terrible gossips but excellent calendars.

* * *

Let me tell you how a chorister ends up a fox, since apparently we're being honest now.

His name — *my* name, the old one, the true one — isn't something I say. Can't say. That's rather the point of the punishment. The Hollow Choir had twenty-one voices once, and I was the youngest, a boy from the fen-country with a voice like a reed whistle and an ego like a cathedral bell. When the covenant began to fray — before your Guild bought its relics, before Voss ever melted a bell — the choir met to decide what to do. And I, in my wisdom, proposed that we abandon it.

Not destroy it. Abandon it. Let the wild places fend for themselves. We were tired, you understand. Twenty-one voices holding open a door between worlds is exhausting work, and I was young and my feet hurt and I wanted to sing songs people could dance to, not hymns that held reality together.

The others refused. So I did it alone. I walked out mid-verse, during the reforging no less, and the covenant — which is not sentimental, whatever the ballads claim — took notice.

It didn't kill me. Killing would have been mercy, and the wild doesn't waste mercy. It asked me a question instead: *If you will not be a voice, be ears.* And it turned me into the best listener in three realms. Fur and teeth and a nose that can smell a lie at forty paces. It set me loose in the fens to hear everything the silence swallowed, every species going quiet, every songbird sold into a cage, and it made me *remember* — remember that I had walked away from the door while it was still swinging shut.

The name was the price of the forgetting clause. A transformed chorister keeps his memory; he loses his name. And a nameless thing, when the covenant finally dies or is reborn, simply... stops. No grave, no ghost. Just a fox-shaped hole where a regret used to be.

Unless someone speaks it for him. At the reforging. Freely, truly, as part of the choir.

Which requires the choir to be reforged. Which requires Mirabel Amberdale to do the most impossible thing in her ledger.

You see my problem.

* * *

Mirabel was sitting by the fire turning her mother's bone map over in her hands when I came back around. Emeric was still out — he scouted longer these days, and came back quieter, which worried me more than the hunters did. A man who stops complaining is either dying or deciding something.

"Your turn to take watch," she said without looking up.

"I don't sleep."

"Everyone says that. Everyone sleeps."

"I am a legendary exception. Ask the frogs. They speak very highly of—"

"Quillfeather." She looked up, firelight in her eyes, and I recognized the expression because I'd seen it on my own face in a polished shield four hundred years ago. The look of someone about to ask a question they already know the answer to. "The debt. What is it?"

Ah. There it was.

I sat down across the fire from her, which gave me the tactical advantage of having flames between us, though against Mirabel that advantage is mostly decorative.

"You know," I said, "most employers wait until the job is done before auditing their contractors."

"We might die at the solstice. I'd like to know what I promised."

"You promised nothing. That's what makes it a debt and not a bargain. You owe me a service of unspecified nature, redeemable at a time of my choosing, within the scope of your abilities." I licked a paw with tremendous casualness. "Standard fen-law. Very binding. The frogs drew up the paperwork."

She waited. She's infuriatingly good at waiting. She learned it from ledgers, I think — you can't rush an account into balancing.

"A name," I said finally.

Whose?"

"Mine. My real one. Not Quillfeather, which is a costume I wear over the wound." The words came out flatter than I intended, stripped of their usual varnish, and I hated how the firelight made my fur look thin. "When the choir is reforged, there's a moment — the giants will know it, the well-voices knew it — when the pact reaches for every voice that ever belonged to it. All twenty-one. And I was the twenty-first, before I left. If my name is spoken then, freely, I'm counted again. I stand human for exactly one breath — the length of the naming — and after that, I choose. Stay fox or return to man or... something else, some third thing, if the choir is generous."

"And if it isn't spoken?"

I examined my claws with great interest. "Then when the new covenant binds, everything outside it unbinds. Including me. I go out like a candle in a strong wind. No afterlife, no haunting, no dramatic final monologue. Just gone."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the slope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which struck me as thematically appropriate.

"Why didn't you tell me?" she said. "In the fens. Why the riddle and the theater?"

"Because," I said, and here I broke my second rule, the one about explaining myself, "if I told you, you'd have said yes. Immediately. You'd have promised anything, because that's who you are, and then you'd have spent every mile since carrying my death in your pack alongside everything else, blaming yourself every time we lost an hour, every time your gift cost you another piece of yourself. And I—" My throat did something undignified. "I have watched enough people carry burdens that weren't theirs. I was one, once. I won't be another."

Mirabel stared at me across the fire for a long time. Then she laughed — short, wet, startled out of her.

"You absolute fraud. Four hundred years of sarcasm and underneath it you're *kind*."

"I will deny that under oath."

"To whom? The frogs?"

"The frogs are notoriously bribable."

But she wasn't laughing anymore. She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and studied me the way she studies accounts — line by line, looking for the entry that doesn't balance.

"There's something else," she said. "You're still hiding the worst of it."

Of course she'd found it. She finds everything. That's her whole tragedy and her whole gift.

"When you speak my name," I said, "you'll need to *know* it. Truly know it, the way the covenant knows things — not memorized, understood. And the only way for you to learn it is for me to give you the memory of it. Directly. Through your gift." I let that settle. "And you've felt what your gift costs. Every voice you free takes something from you. This one will take more. It's not a bird-song or a rat-whisper. It's four centuries of a man's whole life, handed over in a single touch. It might take days off your life. It might take something worse. I won't know until—"

"Until you do it."

"Until I do it. Which is why I haven't. Which is why I was going to keep not doing it right up until the moment at the root-tree when I absolutely had to, at which point I'd hand it over and you'd do the naming and everyone lives happily ever after and nobody audits the contractor."

Mirabel was quiet. Then she reached across the fire — past the flames, heedless of sparks, which is so like her — and touched two fingers to the top of my head, between the ears, where no one had touched me gently in four hundred years.

"Quillfeather," she said softly, "when we get there, you'll give me the memory. But not because the debt demands it. Because I'm asking. As a friend."

*Friend*. The word landed harder than any hunter's blade this season. I looked away and pretended to be fascinated by the dark.

"That word has consequences," I managed. "In my experience, people who call me friend eventually ask me to stop making jokes."

"Heaven forbid."

"They'll ask me to be sincere."

"Terrifying prospect."

"And they'll expect me to stay."

Her hand stilled on my head. Down in the valley, Emeric's signal-whistle sounded — two notes, safe, returning — and neither of us moved for a moment.

"Yes," Mirabel said. "That's generally what friends expect."

* * *

Emeric came back down the slope with snow in his hair and a look on his face that I catalogued immediately as *trouble wearing a calm expression*. He crouched by the fire, fed it a branch, and said, "Guild outriders on the northern pass. Six of them. Moving slow — they're not tracking us, they're herding us."

"Herding us where?" Mirabel asked.

"Toward the capital road. Toward Voss." He glanced at me, and I saw he'd heard more than he let on — he'd been coming down the slope for at least part of our conversation, fox-hearing works both directions and warden-training never quite leaves a man. He didn't mention it. Instead he said, "Whatever you two decided, decide it fast. We have maybe two days before they close the pass, and the drowned cathedral waypoint is beyond it."

Two days. The solstice in nine. The memory of my name sitting behind my teeth like a stone I'd swallowed four centuries ago.

"Well," I said, standing, shaking the snow from my coat, summoning every ounce of sardonic armor I owned and buckling it tight. "Nothing focuses the mind like a deadline. Shall we go be herded? I've always wondered what it's like to be livestock. Very humbling, I imagine."

Mirabel smiled at me — that smile, the one that makes the whole concealment-and-cages world seem briefly survivable — and shouldered her pack.

And as we started up toward the pass, toward the Guild's closing hand, I thought about names. About how mine had been a boy's name once, sung in harmony with twenty others, and how it had rotted in me like a seed in frozen ground, and how tomorrow, or the day after, I would dig it up and press it into the hands of a woman who owed me nothing and was giving me everything anyway.

I only hoped the man I'd been deserved the breath she was going to spend on him.

Behind us, far to the south, faint as a heartbeat, the bells of the capital began to ring — wrong, cracked, half-melted, but ringing. And beneath the sound, so low that only a listener made entirely of listening could catch it, something answered.

Something hungry.

"Walk faster," I said.
