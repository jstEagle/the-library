## 11. The City That Forgot How to Listen

There is a particular kind of silence that a city makes when it has been taught to be quiet. It isn't peace. Peace has texture — wind in it, breath, the small negotiations of people living near one another. This was different. This was the silence of a room where someone important has just said something unforgivable and everyone has decided, all at once and without discussion, to study their boots.

I had grown up in that silence. I had enforced it for eleven years. Standing on the ridge above the capital of Aldermere at dusk, watching the smoke rise from the bell-founders' quarter in long gray ropes, I understood for the first time that I had never once heard my own city.

"Cheerful place," Quillfeather said from his perch on my pack. "The smoke really ties it together. Very festive. Is there a festival?"

"No," Mirabel said quietly.

"Bell-burning, then."

"They're melting them," she said. "The bells."

She had heard it before any of us, of course. She hears everything, which is either a gift or a wound depending on the day, and lately she'd stopped pretending it was only one of those. From three miles off she had heard the foundries working through the night shift — not the hammers, she said, but what the hammers were unmaking. A bell, apparently, remembers its voice even in the crucible. She hadn't slept well since the mountains.

"We don't have to go in," I said, and immediately hated myself for saying it, because we both knew we did.

"Oh, we absolutely do not *have* to," Quillfeather agreed brightly. "We could go around. We could go very far around. I have cousins in the northern fens who owe me nothing whatsoever, which is the healthiest arrangement in my family. We could—"

"The waypoint's under the cathedral," Mirabel said.

"Yes, well. Cathedrals are overrated. Damp stone, poor company, an unhealthy preoccupation with kneeling." The fox's ears flattened, just slightly, the only tell he ever permitted himself. "Fine. Into the city where they melt music. Splendid. If I die, I want it noted that I objected *wittily*."

* * *

Getting into Aldermere was easier than it should have been, and that ease was itself a message. There were four gates. Two were closed. At the Saltgate, where I had passed a thousand times as a warden, the queue of carts stretched half a mile, and every driver was unloading cargo without complaint: crates, coops, cages. So many cages.

"Do you see it?" Mirabel whispered. We stood in the shadow of a tanner's shed, wearing the gray hooded cloaks Emeric-the-warden no longer had any right to and Emeric-the-man couldn't bring himself to burn.

"I see cages."

"Larks. Thrushes. Something with a song like running water — I can't tell what, they've been muzzled so long they've forgotten how to finish their own phrases." Her jaw was tight. "They're not being sold. They're being *collected*. Every singing thing within fifty miles, brought here."

"For what?"

She looked up at me, and in her face I saw the question she didn't want to ask out loud: *for what do you collect voices, if not to spend them?*

I knew the answer. I had carried Guild orders before; I had read the ciphered memoranda with the wax seal of the Second Chair and thought myself a guardian of order while doing it. Order required quiet. Quiet required management. Management required inventory. I had signed inventories. Somewhere in a Guild archive there was a ledger with my signature beside entries for thrushes, and I would carry that to my grave.

"There's a side entrance to the warden's barracks," I said. "Old postern by the drainage cut. It'll get us into the low quarter without passing inspection."

"You know the capital well?" Quillfeather asked, in the tone of a creature filing information away for later use or possible blackmail.

"I grew up eight streets from the cathedral," I said. "My father rang the dawn bell for thirty years. Before—" I stopped.

"Before the Guild decided bells were a security risk," Mirabel finished softly.

Before my father was reassigned to a counting house in a coastal town and came home one evening with a face like a man who had been told his whole life had been a clerical error. He never rang anything again. He took up whittling. He whittled bells, little wooden ones that made no sound, hundreds of them, and lined them on the windowsill, and when I asked him why he said, "So the house remembers what it lost," and I had laughed, because I was seventeen and a fool, and he had looked at me the way you look at someone standing on ice that hasn't cracked yet.

I hadn't thought about the whittled bells in years. Now I could not stop thinking about them.

"This way," I said, and my voice came out rougher than intended.

* * *

The low quarter at night was worse than the gates. Not because of soldiers — there were fewer than I expected, and that absence had teeth in it. It was worse because of the windows.

Every window in the tenement rows was shuttered. Not closed against cold; shuttered the way you shutter against a storm, latched and braced, some of them stuffed with rags. And pasted beside doors, on lintels, on the public pumps, were printed notices, fresh ink, Guild seal at the bottom:

**BY ORDER OF THE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS: REPORT ALL ANIMAL VOCALIZATION TO YOUR DISTRICT WARDEN. SINGING WITHIN DWELLINGS IS PROHIBITED AFTER DARK. COMPLIANCE IS CITIZENSHIP.**

*Singing within dwellings is prohibited after dark.* I read it three times. The words refused to rearrange themselves into sense.

A door opened down the row — an old woman setting out a slop pail — and from inside her house, faintly, came a child humming. Three notes. Nothing. A child amusing herself while her grandmother worked.

Two wardens materialized out of an alley mouth with the smooth, terrible efficiency I recognized because I had been trained in the same school of movement. They didn't shout. They walked to the door, knocked once, spoke briefly. The humming stopped. The old woman went back inside. One warden made a note in a book.

They never raised their voices. They never had to. That was the genius of it, if you wanted to call it genius: you don't need terror when you have habit. You just need enough small corrections, often enough, until people correct themselves. Until mothers hush children before the wardens can. Until shutters become reflex.

"That's your old life," Mirabel said. It wasn't a question. She was watching my face instead of the street.

"That was my old life."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing in a dark alley next to a fox, planning crimes." I exhaled slowly. "It's the best decision I've ever made, and I resent how easy it was."

Quillfeather, mercifully, said nothing at all, which from him was practically a eulogy for my former self.

We moved along the drainage cut toward the cathedral district, and it was there, crossing the foundry road, that we heard the melting.

You can hear a bell die, if you're close enough. It doesn't shatter — they lower them whole into the crucible fires, great bronze throats tipped gently into the flame, and for a moment before the metal softens the bell *rings*. Not struck. Just heat finding the flaw lines, the tuning, the memory. A long, wavering, descending tone, like something enormous sighing out the last of a name.

Mirabel grabbed my arm. Her knuckles were white.

"How many?" I asked.

"Eleven tonight. Twelve tomorrow. Then the cathedral's own set on solstice eve." Her eyes were shining and wet and furious all at once. "Emeric, they're not destroying them. Listen. They're *harvesting*. The resonance — melted bell-metal holds a charge of song, anyone who worked covenant-steel knows that — they're pouring it into *something*."

"What something?"

"I don't know yet." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, angrily, the way she did whenever grief tried to slow her down. "But I know what it costs when a voice goes into the wrong container. I've felt it since the well. Every voice we've freed, I've felt the shape of the thing it was taken *for*. And this city is being shaped into one enormous mouth, and something intends to make it swallow."

Above the foundry roofs, the smoke bent eastward, all of it, every rope of it, bending the same direction — toward the cathedral hill, where the old spire stood dark against the stars. Dark, I realized, because the beacon lamp that had burned there for two hundred years was out. Dark, because a city that has stopped listening eventually stops seeing too.

* * *

We sheltered until the small hours in the crypt of a deconsecrated chapel I remembered from my novitiate days — the Wardens had used it for storage, then abandoned it when even storage became suspect. Dust, broken censers, and one enormous spider whom Quillfeather negotiated with at length and in low tones, emerging from the conversation looking deeply offended.

"She wants payment in flies," he reported. "In advance. The audacity. No wonder this city fell."

Mirabel sat against the wall with the bone map across her knees, though she wasn't reading it. She was listening. She did that more and more now — eyes unfocused, head tilted, hearing things none of us could, and each time she surfaced from it she looked like someone coming up from deep water.

"There are still singers here," she said suddenly. "Under everything. Under the notices and the shutters. People hum in their cellars. An old man whistles through his teeth when he thinks no one can hear. There's a woman on Bellows Lane who sings her baby to sleep in *whisper*, Emeric. A whisper-lullaby. Because it's all she dares."

Her voice cracked on the last word, and she pressed her lips together hard.

"When this is done," I said, "they'll sing however loudly they like."

"You can't know that."

"I can't know anything. I'm a disgraced warden traveling with a fugitive accountant and a debt-ridden fox. Knowing things was never part of my skill set." I sat down beside her, close enough that our shoulders touched, which had become a thing we did without discussing it, the way you stop discussing most true things. "But I've spent eleven years believing order was worth any silence. And I am telling you that watching that woman's notice tonight, I felt the belief finally die, and good riddance. Whatever comes after the reforging — hunger, chaos, wild things at the fences — it will be better than a city that jails its own lullabies. I'd rather defend a messy world with songs in it than a tidy one without."

Mirabel looked at me for a long moment. In the dark I couldn't read her expression fully, only the shine of her eyes and the slight tremor at the corner of her mouth that meant she was feeling too much to speak and hiding it behind almost-smiling.

"Careful, Warden," she said finally, lightly. "That almost sounded like hope."

"Don't tell anyone. I'll deny it under oath."

Quillfeather, curled on a fallen capital like a ginger loaf, opened one eye. "For the record, I hoped first. I've been hoping since chapter— since the fens. Nobody credits the fox."

* * *

We went up toward the cathedral before dawn, through streets gone fully silent now, and it was on the processional steps — wide, empty, designed for thousands — that we saw the machinery entire.

Scaffolding wrapped the bell tower like a cage around a throat. Cranes stood ready. Below, in the foundry yard, the night's harvest cooled in molds: ingots of pale bronze, stacked in rows, each one faintly warm and each one, if Mirabel's flinching was any measure, still faintly *sounding*. Guild clerks moved among the stacks with ledgers open, checking marks, and beside each stack stood a warden with a drawn baton, guarding metal the way treasure is guarded.

And at the top of the steps, reviewing it all with her hands clasped behind her back, stood a woman in a gray coat cut with the precision of a blade.

Serane Voss. Master of the Guild of Impossible Things. I had seen her twice from a distance in my service years and both times had come away with the unsettling sense of having been counted.

Even at this distance, even at this hour, she seemed to be doing exactly that. Her gaze traveled the yard, the scaffolds, the sleeping city — and then climbed the steps, unhurried, and settled on the shadows where three fugitives stood frozen behind a saint's weathered shoulder.

She could not possibly have seen us. It was full dark, we were forty yards off, motionless. It was impossible.

The Guild of Impossible Things, after all, keeps excellent accounts of what is impossible.

Voss held the look for a moment — a long, courteous, appraising moment, the look of a woman confirming a delivery — then turned away and gave some quiet instruction to a clerk, who wrote it down.

"She knows," Mirabel breathed.

"She suspects," I said, and my heart was hammering in a way it hadn't even during the ambush at the ford. "Or she's baiting. Either way, we're done walking in the front of anywhere. The waypoint vault runs beneath the cathedral undercroft — old warden access, sealed since the schism. There's a door behind the reliquary stair that my oath-key might still open. Might."

"*Might*," Quillfeather repeated with relish. "Oh, wonderful. My favorite word. Right up there with 'perhaps' and 'unfortunately.'"

"If it opens," I said, "we're beneath their feet, inside their strongest hold, three days before solstice, with everything they've harvested sitting one floor above us."

"And if it doesn't open?" Mirabel asked.

I looked at the scaffolding, the ingots, the dark tower where my father's bells hung silent in their cage, and felt the anger in my chest settle at last into something harder and more useful than anger. Resolve has less fire in it and much better posture.

"Then we'll improvise," I said. "I'm told impossible things are rather the family business."

Behind us, somewhere in the sleeping city, a single shutter creaked open, and — so faintly that only Mirabel caught it — someone began, very softly, to sing.
