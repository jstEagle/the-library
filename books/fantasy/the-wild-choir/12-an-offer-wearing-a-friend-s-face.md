## 12. An Offer Wearing a Friend's Face

I have been ambushed in fens, ambushed on mountain roads, and once — memorably — ambushed by a goose with opinions about property lines. But I had never before been ambushed by hospitality, and I will tell you now that it is far worse.

We came down out of the Ashspine foothills at dusk, following the bone map's faint silver tracery toward the drowned cathedral waypoint, when we smelled woodsmoke and roasting meat. That alone should have warned us. Nothing on that moor had any business being comfortable.

The inn stood where no inn had stood a week prior — I know because Quillfeather had marked the spot in his mental catalogue of "places to die badly" — squat and warm-windowed, its sign creaking on fresh iron hinges. The sign read THE QUIET HEARTH, which was either the least subtle trap name in history or the most honest advertising I'd ever seen.

"No," said Quillfeather flatly.

"It's an inn," Emeric said.

"It's an ambush wearing an inn."

"You can't know that."

"I *can* know things, Emeric. It's one of my many gifts, along with devastating wit and a tail that expresses my every emotion whether I wish it to or not." His tail, at that moment, expressed profound suspicion. "There is no inn here. There has never been an inn here. Inns do not *sprout*."

But we were three days from anywhere, Emeric's wound had gone hot at the edges despite my needlework, and the smell of roasting meat was doing to my judgment what it does to everyone's. We went in.

The common room was full of people who all looked up at once, which is not what people in a genuinely busy inn ever do. They looked up the way soldiers look up. Then they went back to their suppers with the studied casualness of people who have been told exactly when to look up and when to stop.

A woman sat alone by the fire. She was perhaps fifty, silver-streaked hair cropped short, hands folded on her knees like someone who had long ago finished being impatient with waiting. She wore plain gray wool, unadorned, without a single ring or sigil — which, if you knew anything about Guild masters, meant she had nothing left to prove.

She did not rise. She simply said, "Mirabel Sandoval. Sit down before your warden does something chivalrous and tears his stitches."

Emeric's hand went to his sword anyway. Of course it did.

"Serane Voss," he breathed, and I felt the name land in him like a stone dropped down a well.

"The same." She gestured at the empty chairs across from her. "Sit. Eat. I promise you nothing in this room will harm you tonight. You have my word, and whatever you think of me, you'll find my word has never once been broken. Ask anyone I've ruined."

It says something about our exhaustion that we sat. It says something about me that I took the chair nearest the fire and only then remembered to be afraid.

* * *

They brought food — real food, bread still warm, stew thick with root vegetables and something that might have been lamb and might not have been, given the company keeping us. Quillfeather refused to eat anything not sealed in its own skin, so they brought him a whole roasted quail, which he accepted with the air of a diplomat accepting tribute while planning the annexation.

Voss watched him eat for a moment with an expression I couldn't read.

"You're the chorister," she said. "The one who ran."

Quillfeather paused mid-bone. "I'm a fox."

"You were Elias Threnody, second voice of the Ash Choir, before the Order stripped you and the wild remade you." She said it gently, which was somehow more brutal than saying it cruelly. "I've read your file. All nine pages of it."

Quillfeather set down the quail. For a long moment his tail was perfectly, unnaturally still. Then: "Nine pages. Hm. Did they mention my singing? Because that's the part worth reading."

"They mentioned everything." She turned back to me. "And you are the daughter of Lira Sandoval, anchor of the Hollow Covenant, who the world believes died of fever nineteen years ago. And you are the last living beast-speaker, hiding in a counting house, balancing ledgers for the very organization hunting you." Her eyes were pale gray, steady as a plumb line. "You've been asking questions, Mirabel. The rats told you about the relic purchases. Emeric told you about the burned roads. But nobody has told you *why*. So let me tell you why, and then you can decide what to do with the rest of your evening."

"You could just let us go," I said.

"I could," she agreed pleasantly. "And you'd walk into the fens, gather three more voices, and get yourselves killed by something with too many teeth, because you don't understand what you're reassembling. I'm not going to kill you, Mirabel. I'm going to show you. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

She drew something from her sleeve. Not a weapon. A smooth gray stone, oval, unremarkable — except that the firelight bent around it wrong, the way light bends around grief.

"A memory-stone," Emeric said hoarsely. "Those were outlawed—"

"By the Order. Yes. Along with honesty and several other inconvenient things." Voss held it out to me. "Touch it. Just touch it. What happens next is yours to judge."

Everything sensible in me screamed don't. But I had spent my whole life being sensible, and look where sensible had gotten me: hunted, exhausted, holding a dead man's map and a fox's debt. I touched the stone.

* * *

The memory took me the way cold water takes a swimmer.

I was standing in a village I didn't recognize — older than any village I knew, the houses built of timber so dark it was nearly black, the lanes paved in riverstone worn round as eggs. It was summer. I could feel it on my skin, the particular heavy sweetness of high summer, and under it another smell: blood, and crushed green things.

The covenant was whole. I knew it without being told — the way you know a room is warm. The wild pressed close against the village borders, and it *sang*, a vast polyphonic presence at the edge of hearing, and the villagers moved through their days inside that song the way fish move through water.

Then something at the forest's edge stopped singing.

I turned, and the thing that came out of the trees was made of the covenant itself — vines and antler and river-water and birdsong given appetite. It had been a voice once. I understood that immediately, the way you understand things in dreams. It had been one of the twenty-one, and something had happened to it, some wound or hunger or betrayal, and it had turned.

It ate the village.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. It walked down the lanes and everything it touched forgot how to be alive — crops collapsing into gray dust mid-growth, cattle dropping where they stood with their eyes still open, and the people — I made myself watch the people — the people didn't die. They *hollowed*. One moment a woman was screaming; the next she was standing quietly, mouth open, with nothing behind her face but weather. The ravenous voice drank them the way I'd drink water after a long road. And the other voices of the covenant sang against it, fought it, and lost, and lost, and lost, until—

Until my mother stepped between it and the last standing wall.

I saw her clearly. Younger than I am now, dark-haired, with my own stubborn chin. She raised both hands and *anchored* — poured herself into the failing pact like water into a cracked vessel — and the ravenous voice shrieked and broke apart and fled into the deep places, wounded but not destroyed. Waiting.

Then the vision shifted, blurred, and I was seeing through other eyes, later years: wardens in gray coming to my mother again and again, each time the anchor thinning, each time she gave more of herself, until there was almost nothing left of Lira Sandoval except the holding. And over it all, men and women in counting houses making quiet decisions, adding columns, concluding that the covenant was too dangerous to keep, too hungry to trust, too broken to save. Better silence than appetite. Better a dead song than a devouring one.

The Guild hadn't silenced the wild out of greed. Or not only greed.

They'd done it out of fear.

* * *

I came back to myself with my cheeks wet and the stew untouched and cold.

"Well?" Voss asked softly.

"That thing—" My voice came out wrong. I cleared my throat. "That voice. Is it still out there?"

"Yes."

"And my mother is bound to a covenant that thing belongs to."

"She *is* the covenant, Mirabel. Or the last living thread of it. Every voice you gather, every fragment you restore — you're weaving a rope for something that has already eaten a village once." Voss leaned forward, firelight catching the gray of her eyes. "You think we're monsters. I've read your little rebellion, girl, and it's a good story: brave speaker, loyal fox, defected warden, mother to rescue. I wrote better ones when I was your age. But stories end. Appetite doesn't. Your mother has been holding that door shut for nineteen years with her bare hands, and you want to throw it open and call it freedom."

"The alternative is silence forever," Emeric said. "You melted the bells, Serane. You caged the birds. You bought up every relic of the old pact and buried them in vaults—"

"To buy time!" For the first time her voice rose, and beneath the steel of it I heard something raw. "Do you know what the Order proposed when the anchor began to fail? Nothing. Do you know what the wild offered? Nothing. Everyone stood around admiring the problem while a woman was devoured inch by inch, and when I said *enough*, when I said we would choose humanity's survival over a pretty song, the Order called me a heretic and your superiors called me useful." She looked at Emeric, and there was something terribly tired in her face. "You want reinstatement, boy? It's yours. Warden-commander by spring. Bring me the map, bring me the fox, and walk away from the Sandoval girl before she drowns you in her certainty. This is the only offer I will ever make you, and I am making it because you were the finest student I ever taught."

Emeric had gone very still. "You trained me?"

"Who do you think signed your first commission?"

The silence stretched. I watched Emeric's jaw work, watched the war move through him — duty and betrayal and the awful arithmetic of a man learning his whole life had been steered by hands he never saw. And I confess that in that moment I hated Serane Voss less than I hated how much sense she made.

Because here is the truth I carried out of that memory-stone: I had seen my mother's face, and I had seen the hollow-eyed villagers, and both images wanted the same space in my chest.

"Come with me," I said.

Voss blinked. Of all her calculations, I suspect mine wasn't among them.

"You say the anchor is failing. Fine. Then killing me changes nothing, arresting me changes nothing — the silence wins by default and your mother's sacrifice becomes a footnote. Come with me instead. Bring your memory-stone and your nine-page files and your fear, and help me do this *properly*. If the ravenous voice must be faced, then let it be faced by everyone who helped break the covenant in the first place." I stood. My legs shook. I stood anyway. "Or keep sitting by this fire, Master Voss. Comfort looks good on you."

For a long moment she simply looked at me, and I thought — absurdly — that she might laugh.

"No," she said at last, and stood, and the room full of soldiers stood with her. "No, Mirabel. I offered you a friend's face. I see now you'd rather have the enemy's. That's your right." She nodded to her people, and they began, silently, to clear the tables, extinguish lamps, fold the inn away like a camp. "But hear me, and hear me well: the solstice is eleven days off. When the choir reforges, the ravenous voice wakes with it. Everything it was, everything it hungered for, everything it ate — it comes back through the door you open. And it will remember that your mother is the lock."

She paused at the doorway, gray wool and gray eyes against the dark.

"When you stand at the root-tree, girl, and you finally understand what I showed you tonight — send word. I'll come. Not for you. For the ending." And she was gone into the moor night, and the inn went with her, and we stood in a cold field with a dying fire's warmth still on our faces.

Quillfeather spoke first, quietly, without a single joke in him. "Well. That was terrible. Shall we?"

Emeric took my hand without either of us deciding to. His grip was shaking slightly, though I suspect he'd deny it under torture.

"Eleven days," I said, looking north, toward the drowned cathedral and everything sleeping under it. "Then we'd better find out what else the map hasn't told us."

Behind us, where the inn had been, the moor grass was already growing back over the ashes of the fire — as if the land itself were trying to forget we'd ever been offered a choice.
