## 13. The Cell Beneath the Bells

The Guild, it turned out, did not believe in windows.

I suppose I should have expected it. A window is an invitation to hope, and hope, in Serane Voss's accounting, was a line item that never balanced. The cell they put me in was three paces long, two wide, and lit by a single lamp behind iron slats that someone had thoughtfully angled so the light fell on nothing at all.

They had taken my boots, my belt, the map — *the map*, which Quillfeather had sworn up one side of the fens and down the other was "perfectly safe in your bodice, darling, no one ever searches a bodice" — and my hairpins, which felt like a personal insult. They left me my dress, my shawl, and my ledger-hand. The last was not generosity. It was oversight. Nobody fears a bookkeeper.

I sat on the stone shelf they called a bed and did what I do best: I counted.

Three paces by two by seven feet of ceiling, if my stride held true in bare feet. One lamp. One door, banded in iron, no keyhole on the inside because whoever built this place assumed its guests would not be leaving under their own power. One drain in the floor, grate too narrow for anything bigger than a rat.

One Mirabel, alone, with her gift locked behind her teeth where it had always been, and no animals worth hearing within a mile of solid stone.

Or so I told myself, for the first hour, while despair sat beside me on the shelf and did its usual impression of a friend.

* * *

It was my fingers that betrayed me first. Bookkeepers' fingers are honest; they cannot stop themselves from checking work. I was running them along the wall seam — idle, restless, the way you tap a pen when the sums won't come out even — when I noticed the wall was wrong.

Stone is cold unevenly. That's a thing you learn if you've spent years in counting houses with bad insulation and worse tea. Stone breathes its chill in patches, warmer near any crack that touches the living dark of the earth. This wall was cold everywhere, uniformly, like a thing that had been made rather than grown.

And it was smooth. Too smooth. Dressed stone has tool marks, however fine. This had texture instead — a faint ridging, regular as corduroy, rising and falling under my fingertips in rows.

Rows.

I knelt. I pressed my palm flat against it. And beneath the smoothness, small and hard and countless, I felt them.

Bones.

Not human ones — I want that clear from the first, because there was a moment, kneeling there with my heart going like a hammer in a bottle, when I thought *they walled people into this place*, and I very nearly lost the thread of myself. No. These were small bones. Wing bones. Keels. The delicate wishbones that fortune-tellers snap over two names.

Songbird bones. Pressed, layered, bound with something pale as tallow until thousands upon thousands of them became a single seamless surface. A wall made of everything the city used to hear in the mornings before the Guild decided mornings were better silent.

I sat back on my heels and laughed, actually laughed, a thin unhinged sound in that little box — because of course. Of course this was how Voss would build her prison. Not with fear. Fear leaks. With *silence*. She hadn't just caged the birds of the capital; she'd spent them. Rendered their bodies into mortar so that the walls themselves would drink any song that dared rise inside these rooms. Every note sung here died stillborn. Every prisoner's scream went nowhere. It was the most efficient thing I had ever seen, and I have balanced ledgers for griffin feathers and bottled thunder.

It was also, I realized slowly, sitting there in the lamplight, the stupidest thing she could possibly have done.

Because I am Mirabel Sandoval, and my whole life I have heard what other people's walls keep out.

The bones weren't singing. That was the horror of it — they couldn't. Whatever binding held them pressed together held their voices too, the way wax holds a seal. But bones remember shape. And a bird's keel remembers the shape of a song the way my hands remember columns of figures: involuntarily, endlessly, whether or not anyone asks.

All they needed was permission.

"Hello," I whispered, and then, feeling foolish and past caring, laid both palms against the wall as if taking a pulse. "My name is Mirabel Sandoval. I'm told I'm an impossible thing."

Nothing. Of course nothing. My gift ran along living throats and wild blood, and these were dead things in dead plaster, and I had known that, and I had hoped anyway, which is either courage or idiocy depending on the ledger.

But the rats —

The drain.

I crawled to it, graceless, knees on cold flagstone, and put my lips close to the grate. Somewhere below, water moved in the dark. Somewhere below, the city's refuse ran through channels that no amount of bell-melting or bone-pressing had thought to seal, because who fears a rat?

"I don't know if anyone can hear me," I said softly. "My name is Mirabel Sandoval. I keep accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things, and I am telling you the truth when I say the Guild has been robbing you for years. Your bells. Your birds. Your songs." I swallowed. "I can't offer you coin. I can't offer you anything except this: when the covenant reforges — and it will, I will see to it or die trying — every voice counts. Even yours. *Especially* yours. The wild places forgot the cities long ago, and I don't blame them, but I am asking you to remember that once, all of this was shared ground."

Silence. Long enough that I began composing my own eulogy.

Then, from far down in the dark, small and dry and utterly indifferent:

*You talk a lot for a creature in a box.*

I pressed my forehead against the grate and nearly wept.

"There are more of you," I said.

*A few.* A scrabble of claws somewhere I couldn't see. *The big folk stopped leaving crumbs since the quiet started. We're thin. We're angry.*

"Good," I said. "Anger keeps. Now listen carefully, because we haven't much time, and I need you to do something impossible."

*We're rats,* it said, and I swear the tone was Quillfeather's smugness in miniature. *Impossible is our native country.*

* * *

Here is what I know about escapes, having now performed one: they are not dramatic. They are arithmetic.

The problem, stated plainly: a door of iron and oak, banded and barred from without, opened twice a day by a guard who brought bread and water and no conversation. Walls of pressed bone that muffled every sound. One drain, too small for me.

The solution required only that I stop thinking like a prisoner and start thinking like an accountant. What assets did I hold?

One: the walls were made of birdsong, pressed and starving. Two: the guards walked a fixed route — I had counted their footfalls during my first hours, eleven steps past my door, pause, eleven steps back, every quarter hour, because enforcers are creatures of routine and routine is just another word for a schedule waiting to be exploited. Three: the rats owed me nothing and would therefore do exactly as they pleased, which meant I needed to make what I pleased profitable to them. Four: tomorrow was the solstice-eve. Voss would be distracted. Every eye above stairs would be on the bells — the last of them, the great cathedral bourdon, being melted at dawn in some ceremony of triumph.

And five, the asset I had been sitting on since the moment my fingers found the seams: the Guild believed my gift worked on living things. They had built me a cell of dead things. They had never once considered that a beast-speaker's real talent was never the speaking at all.

It was the listening.

"Bones," I murmured against the wall that second night, palms flat, voice low enough that the lamp barely stirred. "I know you can't sing. I'm not asking you to. I'm asking you to remember *out loud*. Just the shape of it. Let the shape leak."

And I listened.

At first — nothing, the same blank cold. Then, deep in the plaster, something shifted. Not sound exactly. Pressure. The way a room feels before a storm, or the way a chord feels in the chest a half-second before the ear catches it. The bones couldn't produce music, but they could produce the *memory* of music's weight, a hum below hearing, a tremor in the mortar like a sleeper turning over.

It was nothing. Any inspector pressing his ear to the wall would have heard stone settling.

Unless the listener already knew what song it was remembering.

I taught the rats the shape of it. That took most of the night and cost me more than I'll easily admit — each coaxing of the bones' memory pulled at my gift like drawing thread from a hem, and by the grey hour I had a nosebleed and a headache like a cracked bell. But rats learn fast when food's involved, and I had bargained hard: crumbs from the guard's tray, hoarded and passed down through the drain, plus my solemn promise that the new covenant would restore the capital's granaries to the old sharing-rights. The rat spokesman — I named him Ledger, because he counted everything twice — pronounced my terms *barely extortionate* and shook on it with a paw I chose not to think about too closely.

Then I set them loose in the walls of the Guild itself, carrying a song's ghost.

* * *

Solstice-eve. Dawn came to my windowless cell as a change in the footfalls — fewer of them, quicker, everyone drawn upstairs toward the melting of the last bell. My bread arrived late and stale, delivered by a boy who looked twelve and terrified, and who flinched when I thanked him. Everyone in that building flinched. I wondered if Voss knew her empire ran on flinching, or if she called it order.

An hour after the bread, the lights in the corridor guttered.

Not winded. *Guttered* — the flames leaning all one direction at once, as though the whole building had inhaled. Then the hum came, the memory-weight of it, rolling through the pressed-bone walls from somewhere far above: the rats had reached the upper floors. They were doing what I had taught them, pressing their small bodies to the bone-mortar of the great hall and *remembering at it*, dozens of tiny hearts beating the shape of a song into walls built to devour songs.

The walls drank it, because drinking song was what they were made for.

And the walls, being full — being *gorged*, for the first time in years, on remembered morning-sound — began, very quietly, to come apart.

Binding-tallow softens with vibration. I know this because Emeric once lectured me for an entire evening about binding-tallow, in the tone of a man who had been exiled for knowing too much, and I had listened with half an ear while watching his hands, and God help me, apparently the other half had been working the whole time. The mortar between the bone-panels softened, swelled, loosened. In the great hall above, I would later learn, an entire decorative frieze slid off its wall and crushed a table of ceremonial candles. Down here, in my little tomb, the panel beside the drain groaned, tilted —

— and fell outward into the corridor, landing flat with a sound like a struck coffin-lid.

Through the gap: darkness, the smell of tallow and old feathers, and eleven steps away, the empty guard-post, its ring of keys hanging on a nail because tonight, of all nights, the enforcers were upstairs singing Voss's praises around the bell-furnace.

I put on my borrowed dignity, stepped over the fallen wall of murdered larks, and let myself out of the Guild's arithmetic entirely.

Ledger met me at the drain-mouth of the outer yard, whiskers twitching, looking insufferably pleased. *Told you,* he said. *Impossible is our native country. Now — about those granary rights. In writing.*

"You'll have them carved in the root-tree itself," I said, "if I live long enough to reach it."

*Hm,* said the rat, in the exact tone of a creditor extending terms. *See that you do. And Mirabel Sandoval —* he paused, and for one moment the sardonic little voice went quiet and serious — *the fox. The tall sad one. They're holding them in the bell-vault. Under the furnace. The fox has been telling jokes to the guards. The tall one hasn't said anything at all.*

My whole body went cold and then hot.

"They're alive."

*For now.* Ledger groomed one paw. *The furnace is lit for the bourdon melt. When the bell goes in, the vault goes with it. Waste not, says the Guild.*

I looked up at the smoke-stained tower where the last bell of the old world waited to die, and I thought about Quillfeather's voice cracking on a joke, and Emeric saying nothing at all because he was saving his strength, and I understood with perfect clarity what the next hour of my life was going to cost.

"Ledger," I said, wiping my nose and starting at a dead run toward the furnace stair, "how many rats does it take to open a vault door?"

*How many have you got?*

"All of them," I said. "I'm calling in everything. Every debt, every promise, every favor the wild ever did this rotten beautiful city." The stairwell rang under my bare feet like the inside of a drum. "Tonight the Guild learns what I learned in my first week of bookkeeping."

*Which is?*

"That every silence," I said, taking the steps three at a time, "is just a debt that hasn't been collected yet."
