## 14. What Voss Showed Was True

There are three kinds of trouble in this world, my old master used to say: the kind you can fight, the kind you can run from, and the kind that sits down across from you at a table and asks politely for tea. Serane Voss had been the third kind. She'd shown Mirabel a memory in a room made of hush — a vision of the old covenant devouring a village whole, roots and roofs and screaming — and then she'd let us walk out of the capital with our lives, which was somehow worse than if she'd sent hunters after us.

You don't leave an enemy alive. You leave a question alive.

I hadn't slept properly since. Every time I closed my eyes I saw it again: the wild places opening like a mouth. And every time I woke, I heard Voss's voice saying, *Ask your archives, Warden. If you still trust them.*

So here I was, three days later, doing exactly what my enemy told me to do. Which is either wisdom or treason, and after fifteen years in the Wardens I've learned those are usually the same thing wearing different boots.

* * *

The archive beneath the Ashspine waystation wasn't forbidden in the way most things are forbidden — no locks, no wards, no guards. It was forbidden the way a grave is forbidden. Nobody went there because nobody wanted to know what was buried there.

It had been the first covenant-wardens' library, back when the Order was something other than the Guild's leash-hounds. The shelves were grown, not built — living yew that had long since stopped growing, which tells you how long it had been since anyone tended it. Dust lay on everything like snowfall nobody had bothered to shovel, and the air smelled of old paper and older regret.

I'd found it as a young warden, full of doctrine and certainty, and I'd walked through those aisles feeling like a thief in a temple. I'd left without reading anything, telling myself the heretical texts were best left sealed. That had felt like discipline at the time.

Now it just felt like cowardice with better posture.

Mirabel waited outside with Quillfeather, who had insisted loudly that he would rather be flayed than enter "a building where books outnumber mice," though I noticed he positioned himself by the door in a way that meant he could watch both approaches. The fox worried in his own way: sideways, sarcastically, always pretending he didn't.

"Rules," I told him before going in. "If anyone comes—"

"I scream, I bite, I make pithy remarks about their parentage," he said. "Yes, yes. You've given me this speech twice already. Go read your forbidden truths, Warden. Some of us have napping to do."

"You don't nap. You brood with your eyes closed."

"A fox's brood *is* his nap." He curled his tail over his nose. "And Emeric?" His eyes opened a crack, one gold sliver in the grey morning. "Whatever's in there — whatever she showed you — remember that a memory is a knife. It cuts true only from one angle."

I nodded, because I couldn't think of anything clever enough to say, and went inside to find out which angle Voss had cut from.

* * *

The heretical shelf was exactly where a frightened institution would put it: at the very back, behind a case of triumphant histories, spine-out so the titles faced the wall. I turned the volumes around one by one, and there they were — the chronicles the Order had spent two centuries not reading.

*On the Nature of the Twenty-One Voices.* *Correspondence of the Anchor-Keepers.* *The Hungering Year: An Account.*

I sat down on the floor because my legs had opinions about standing, and I opened the oldest one first.

The early wardens wrote plainly, which is the thing modern Guild documents never do. Plainness is honest; honesty is dangerous; therefore plainness must be purged. But these pages had no interest in protecting anyone. They recorded the covenant as it actually worked: twenty-one voices bound into one song, each voice a living speaker drawn from a different people or place or kind. Human choristers. Marsh-speakers. Mountain-hummers. River-cantors. And holding all of it together, underneath everything, the anchor — a single soul who carried the weight of the whole chord, rooted at the covenant's heart, so the pact would never fray even when individual voices failed.

And then, in a different hand — tighter, faster, scared — an addendum:

*In the two hundredth year, one voice began to take more than it gave. We do not know why. We do not know which. The song grew teeth. It fed on a village in the Thornmere lowlands — took the houses, the wells, the names off the graves. Forty-one souls, unmade into music. The other voices starved it back, barely. But the hunger did not die. It only went quiet, and hid itself among us, waiting for the choir to weaken enough to feed again.*

I read the passage four times. Then I read it aloud, quietly, the way you reread something hoping the words will rearrange themselves into better news.

They didn't.

Voss had shown Mirabel a village being devoured. I'd assumed it was fabrication, or exaggeration, or some Guild trick of memory-bending. It was none of those things. It was real. The Thornmere account matched her vision detail for detail — the roots taking the roofs, the wells filling with song, the silence after.

One voice of the covenant had turned ravenous. The wild had, once, truly eaten.

But the chronicle said *one voice*. Not the covenant entire. Not the wild as a whole. One singer gone wrong inside a chorus of twenty-one — which is a sickness, not a nature. You do not burn down the choir because one throat went rotten. You find the rotten throat.

Unless, of course, you are the Guild, and burning down choirs is your business model.

* * *

The second volume was worse, and I mean that in the precise sense that it was worse *for me personally*, because buried in the Anchor-Keepers' correspondence was a name.

Most anchors were anonymous by design — chosen, sworn, and renamed, their old lives folded away like winter clothes. But keepers grieve like anyone else, and grief writes letters. In one, dated a century before my birth, a keeper mourned an anchor lost to the deepening silence, and wrote: *She held longer than any before her. They say the Amberdale line roots deepest — the gift runs in the blood like water in a well. Pray we find another of her line before the pact fails entirely.*

Amberdale.

I sat in that dust-smelling dark and thought about Mirabel Sandoval, ledger-keeper, beast-speaker, daughter of a woman who had died — supposedly — years ago. Thought about the giants in the Ashspine saying the mapmaker had been her father's friend. Thought about the way the covenant's voices had been scattered and bought up, decade by decade, by a Guild that claimed to fear the wild's hunger while systematically dismantling everything that kept that hunger caged.

The anchor was the last voice unaccounted for. Nineteen gathered, two missing — the traitor and the anchor. And if the Amberdale line rooted deepest—

Oh, you stupid, blind Order. Oh, me, most stupid and blindest of all. The Guild hadn't killed the anchor when the covenant began to fail. Why would they? A dying anchor is a lever. A hostage. A battery you keep plugged in and drained. They'd taken her, and hidden her, and let everyone believe she was dead, because a dead woman makes no demands.

And her daughter was carrying that woman's map toward the root-tree with the solstice three days out.

* * *

I came out of the archive into afternoon light and found Mirabel sitting on the waystation steps with her arms around her knees, watching Quillfeather stalk something in the grass that was probably imaginary. She looked up, and whatever was on my face made her stand.

"That bad?" she said.

"Worse than bad. Complicated." I handed her the Thornmere volume, open to the passage. "What Voss showed you was true. There was a village. The covenant did eat it. One voice turned hungry and took forty-one people."

She read it the way she reads ledgers — fast, thorough, lips slightly moving — and I watched her hope and dread arm-wrestle behind her eyes. When she finished, she said, carefully, "One voice. Not all of it."

"One voice. Hidden among the others. The chronicles don't say which."

"But Voss showed it like—" Her hand tightened on the book. "She showed it like the whole wild was the monster. Like the silence was mercy."

"Of course she did." Quillfeather trotted over, all ears and cynicism. "A lie doesn't need to be false, girl. It only needs to be framed. Hang a true picture crooked and most folk will never straighten it." He sniffed the page. "Ugh. Old paper. Smells like guilt."

"There's more," I said. And here is where I learned that knowing a thing and saying it are separated by a distance you cannot cross without breaking something. "The Anchor-Keepers' records. The anchor isn't just any speaker. It's a bloodline. The strongest rooting line ever recorded was called—"

I stopped. Because I'd rehearsed this in the dark between the shelves, and it had sounded survivable there.

"Emeric." Mirabel's voice went very quiet. "Called what?"

"Amberdale."

The word landed between us and neither of us touched it. Somewhere in the grass, a real bird sang, indifferent, and I hated it a little.

"My mother's name was Wren Amberdale," Mirabel said at last. Each word placed like a coin counted out. "Before she married my father. Before she—" She looked down at the chronicle. "The keeper's letter. Read it to me."

I read it. *The Amberdale line roots deepest. Pray we find another of her line.*

"So when the covenant started failing," she said, and her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse, "and the Guild needed someone to hold it together — someone whose blood could bear the weight —"

"They didn't need to look far," I finished. "Your mother may not be dead, Mirabel. She may be the anchor. Bound at the covenant's root this whole time. Held by the same people you kept books for."

Quillfeather, for once in his life, said nothing. He just sat down heavily beside her, tail wrapped around his feet, and leaned his warm fox-weight against her leg — which, from him, was a sonnet.

Mirabel stood very still for a long moment. Then she laughed, one short broken note, nothing like humor.

"All this time I thought I was hiding my gift from the Guild. And the whole time, the Guild was probably hiding my mother from me." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Is she one of the two missing voices? Is that why the count won't close?"

"Nineteen gathered. The traitor-voice and the anchor remain. If your mother is the anchor, then the last voice we need is her — given freely, or taken." I swallowed. "And there's the other thing. The ugly arithmetic."

"Say it anyway."

"The voice that turned ravenous — the chronicles never identify it. But it hid among the others. It's still out there, starving, waiting for the choir to weaken." I met her eyes. "Mirabel, what if the traitor and the anchor aren't two separate voices? What if the hunger has been eating your mother from the inside all these years? What if the thing we're racing to save her from — or save her *as* — is the very thing Voss showed you?"

The silence after that had texture. It had weight. It settled on all three of us like the dust downstairs.

"We could stop," Quillfeather said finally, and his voice was gentler than I'd ever heard it. "Walk away. Let the silence come. The realms go deaf to each other, the wild dims, the city forgets — but nobody gets eaten. No villages. No mothers unmade. It's not the worst ending I've heard proposed."

"No," Mirabel said.

"It was worth mentioning—"

"She's been under there for years, Quill. Alone. Rooted." Mirabel picked up the chronicle and tucked it into her coat like a weapon being holstered. "Whatever's waiting at that tree — a prisoner, a monster, both — she has been carrying it alone while I balanced griffin-feather accounts and felt sorry for myself about a caged bird. I'm not stopping. I'm not slowing down. But now I know what I might be walking into." She looked at me, and her chin came up, and she was so much braver than me in that moment that it hurt. "Thank you for telling me the truth instead of the kind version."

"The kind version was unavailable," I said. "I checked the shelves."

That got half a smile out of her, which felt like winning a war.

We struck camp as the light went long and copper. Three days to the solstice. Two days' hard travel to the drowned cathedral where the voices would gather, and then the road down to the world's edge, to the root-tree, to whatever my mother had become.

I took the rear position, as wardens do, and spent the walk turning one line of the chronicle over and over in my head like a stone with a sharp edge: *the hunger hid itself among us.*

Among *us*.

Not among the wild. Among the voices. Among the singers. And there were twenty-one voices in the original covenant, and we had nineteen, and I kept thinking about the addendum's frightened hand, writing faster and faster, and I kept wondering what its author had known that the next page — torn out, centuries ago — had said.

Because if the ravenous voice could hide among the chorus once, it could hide among our nineteen now. It could be walking beside us, humming along, patient as dust.

And the only person I knew who could hear what a voice really was — the only beast-speaker in a hundred miles — was the woman marching ahead of me, who had just promised to give her gift away forever in three days' time.

"Hurry," I called ahead, and hated how much my voice shook. "Solstice won't wait, and neither, I think, will the thing that's been waiting for us to get weak."

Quillfeather glanced back at me, and for a moment his grin had no humor in it at all. "Finally, Warden," he said, "something we agree on."
