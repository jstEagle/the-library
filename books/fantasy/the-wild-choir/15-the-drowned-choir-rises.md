## 15. The Drowned Choir Rises

The drowned cathedral did not look like a cathedral anymore. It looked like a rib cage the sea had swallowed and then, in a fit of sentimentality, decided to keep.

We came down to it at low tide, when the water withdrew from the nave like a congregation filing out of a bad sermon, leaving the stone arches dripping and black against a sky the color of pewter. Barnacles had colonized the saints. An oyster clung to the Virgin's cheek like a pearl gone feral. Somewhere beneath the silt, I knew, lay nineteen voices — no, seventeen gathered, two missing, but who's counting?

I was counting. I was always counting. It is the curse of ledger-keepers that even at the edge of the world's great unfinished song, we tally.

"Cheerful place," Quillfeather said, trotting ahead of us along the causeway of exposed rock. His tail was up, his ears were swiveling, and every line of him said *I am relaxed* the way a drawn bowstring says *I am at rest*. "Ruins, brine, the distinct smell of an ambush waiting politely for us to arrive. Reminds me of my wedding."

"You've never been married," Emeric said.

"I've been many things you don't know about." The fox paused on a stone shaped like a kneeling monk, worn smooth by four hundred years of tides. "That's rather the point of me."

Emeric walked close beside me. He had been walking close beside me since the Ashspine, since the giants told us my mother had not died but been taken — rooted into the covenant like a nail holding shut a door. Since then he'd stopped pretending our closeness was tactical necessity. He hadn't named it either. Warden training, I suspected: you learn to hold a line, not to cross one.

"Say something," I said, because the silence between us had grown heavy enough to have its own tide table.

"I was going to say the arches are load-bearing miracle-work," he said. "Pre-covenant masonry. They'll hold the resonance."

"Something else."

He looked out at the water sliding back from the transept, exposing a floor of black-and-white tiles laid in a pattern that hurt to follow, because it kept almost resolving into music. "After this," he said quietly, "if it works — if we gather what's here and the choir stands — there will be no more missions. No more waypoints. No more reasons for you to need a warden with a broken order and a bad shoulder."

"Are you resigning?"

"I'm asking whether you'll still want me around when I'm useless."

I stopped walking. The fox, ahead, pretended very hard to inspect a dead crab.

"Emeric of the Broken Order," I said, "you have been useless in at least six genuinely impressive ways since I met you — you can't cook, you can't lie, you apologized to a river once — and I have wanted you around through all of them. Do the arithmetic yourself. You're supposed to be good with numbers."

His mouth did something complicated. "You apologized to a door last month."

"The door had feelings."

"It was a door."

"And yet." I took his hand, which was warm and calloused and shaking slightly, which undid me more than any ambush could have. "Ask me properly. After. When we're not standing in a graveyard for churches."

"After," he agreed, and squeezed my hand once, hard, as if sealing a contract — and I thought later how much that moment would cost, how much I would want it back unspent, the way you wish you could return a coin you spent without knowing it was your last.

Quillfeather made a small retching noise. "If you two are finished negotiating the terms of your eventual disappointment—"

The tide turned.

Not gradually, as tides do. All at once, as if something below had inhaled, the water reversed and came flooding back up the causeway faster than any moon-driven sea has a right to move. And under its sound — under the hiss and rush — I heard it.

Singing.

Or the bones of singing. Seventeen fragments of the old covenant, scattered across three kingdoms, each one carried now in whatever vessel we'd found to hold it: the well-voices in glass jars wrapped in wool, the mountain-hum pressed into a slate tablet, a nightingale's ghost in a locket, a whale's long note scored onto cured hide. They woke together as the water rose, answering the drowned cathedral the way iron answers a lodestone, and the jars began to glow through their wrappings like lanterns behind shutters.

"There," I breathed. "It's calling them home."

"Then let's not keep a choir waiting," Emeric said, and drew his warden's blade — not for enemies, but because the steel would conduct the resonance; we'd learned that much from the giants. "Mirabel, the altar. Quillfeather, the high ground."

"On it," said the fox, and then, softly, in a tone I had never heard from him: "Watch the waterline. Something's wrong with the tide."

There was. Tides do not carry torchlight.

They came out of the sea-caves along the cliff base as the water sealed the causeway behind us — Guild hunters in oilskin, thirty at least, and above them on the cliff path, mounted figures with the long hooked poles the Guild used for taking birds off the wing. And at their head, riding down where a horse should never have managed the descent, came a woman I recognized by her silhouette alone: Captain Ilsabet Crane, the hunter whose hounds had chased us out of the fens in the first week, who had burned the safe roads behind us, who had put a crossbow bolt through Emeric's shoulder at the ford and called it a warning shot.

She raised a speaking-trumpet. Her voice rolled flat across the water, stripped of echo by some Guild device, so that it arrived sounding like words read off a list.

"MIRABEL SANDOVAL. BY ORDER OF THE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS: SURRENDER THE RELICS. SURRENDER THE MAP. THE WARDEN MAY WALK AWAY. THE FOX IS TO BE TAKEN ALIVE."

"Why alive?" Quillfeather muttered. Then, louder, insolent, carrying beautifully over the water: "Madam, if you wanted me alive you should have led with flattery!"

"They need a chorister's body," I said, cold settling into my stomach. "Voss doesn't just want to stop the reforging. She wants to finish the anchor herself. A living voice, bound to the covenant — she'd take it into the Guild. She'd own the wild places outright."

"Then," Emeric said, stepping in front of me with the terrible calm of a man doing arithmetic he hates, "we hold the line until the voices are seated."

"How long?"

"How long can thirty hunters be held off by one disgraced warden and one sarcastic fox?" He glanced back at me, and his eyes were steady, and they were saying everything he'd promised to say after. "Long enough. Sing, Mirabel."

* * *

The altar of the drowned cathedral stood in the crossing, half-sunk, its surface carved with twenty-one empty niches arranged in a ring — the seats of the covenant, waiting. Water swirled around my knees as I knelt and began unwrapping the first jar.

Behind me, the world became noise.

I will not pretend I heard the battle clearly. I heard it the way you hear weather through a wall — the crack of Emeric's blade meeting hooked poles, the fox's voice rising in taunts pitched to draw hunters into deeper water, the wet thunder of the returning tide. What I heard clearly was the voices.

I set the first jar in its niche and opened it, and the well-voice inside — three old souls tangled together like braided hair — poured out in a ribbon of silver light and sank into the stone with a sound like a struck bell underwater. The niche filled. Sixteen to go.

A jar shattered somewhere near me — not mine, one of the spares — and I flinched as a hunter's shadow fell across the altar. Emeric got there first. I saw only the end of it: the man going down into the flood, Emeric standing over him, breathing hard, blood running freely down his left arm where a hook had opened him from elbow to wrist.

"Emeric—"

"Sixteen more!" he shouted. "Sing!"

So I sang. Not with my hidden gift — that I was saving, hoarding, the way the miser hoards the coin she means to spend all at once — but with my plain human throat, reading the resonance-patterns the stone-giants had taught me, humming the covenant's grammar aloud while the voices answered. Each one that settled into its niche made the whole ring brighter, made the water around the altar tremble with harmonics, made the flooded nave feel less like a grave and more like a throat clearing itself to speak.

Twelve. Thirteen. The hunters were wading toward us in force now, and Quillfeather was everywhere — a russet streak across the rooftops of ruin, luring men onto unstable stones, screaming insults with the fluency of a creature who had once, perhaps, been a court chorister with a taste for scandal. I caught fragments: *"Your mother was a turnip!"* and *"Is that the best the Guild pays for? My condolences!"*

Fourteen. Fifteen. The light was blinding now, the whole cathedral lit from below like a lamp through horn.

And then I heard the fox stop talking.

In all the weeks I had known him, Quillfeather had never stopped talking. Silence from that fox meant one thing only, and I turned in time to see it: a hooked pole, thrown — not swung, thrown, with a rope behind it, from the cliff path above — taking him across the flank as he leapt between a hunter and Emeric's blind side. The barbs bit. The rope went taut. And the fox who had bargained with death himself, who owed debts unnamed and collected them with interest, was dragged off the ruined roof and into the churning water between the nave and the sea.

"QUILLFEATHER!"

What happened next happened fast, and I remember it in pieces, the way you remember a storm: Emeric cutting the rope-line with one stroke and going into the water after them both. Me, abandoning the altar with two niches still dark, hauling on Emeric's collar as the tide tried to keep all three of them. The fox in my arms, lighter than he should have been, blood diluting pink into seawater, his fur soaked flat and his eyes — his clever, unbearable, ancient eyes — half closed.

"Well," he said. "That's embarrassing."

"Don't talk."

"Noted. Won't. Talk. Ever again." A bubble of red at his lips. "Starting now."

We got him up onto the altar steps, above the waterline, into the light of sixteen seated voices. Emeric pressed his hands against the wound and did not say anything at all, and his face was worse than any crying. Around us the hunters had stopped. I understood why a moment later: the covenant-light had reached some threshold, and the flooded nave had begun to hum on its own, a chord too vast for fear to stand in front of. Men dropped their poles. Captain Crane, on the cliff, sat very still on her horse, and I swear the woman looked afraid of the water.

"Seventeen," Emeric whispered. "Wait — the count. Mirabel, the count."

I counted. Ledger-keeper to the bone. Seventeen voices seated, glowing, singing their fragment of the chord. Two dark niches remained. The traitor-voice, ravenous, that we must not summon but redeem. And the anchor — my mother — at the root-tree, three days' ride north, if the solstice gave us three days.

But that wasn't what made my hands go numb.

What made my hands go numb was the fox bleeding out on the steps, and the memory of chapter ten of my life — the marsh, the bargain, the debt unnamed — and the words he'd made me promise never to repeat until the reforging: *when the time comes, you'll know my true name, girl, and you'll spend it or I vanish forever.* He'd told me the name once, drunk on elderflower wine in the Ashspine passes, sworn me to secrecy, and I had locked it away in the vault where I kept everything that mattered too much to touch.

"Quillfeather." I took his paw. It was cold. Foxes run hot; his cold was wrongness itself. "Stay with me. That's an order from your creditor."

"Creditor," he murmured. "I like that. Much better than... patient." One eye opened a sliver, found mine, and held it with the ferocity of a creature refusing, on principle, to die before the punchline. "Listen. The debt comes due at the reforging. You know that. So here is my professional advice, free of charge, which should worry you..."

"Save it."

"No. Time is... short, and I have never once in my life left a word unsaid, and I refuse to start now, it would ruin my record." Blood frothed; he coughed; he went on anyway, because he would. "The name. Say it true, at the right hour. Don't waste it early out of — sentiment." His gaze slid past me, to Emeric, kneeling bloody in the water. "Unlike some people, who wait for *after*, which may not come, which is a fool's economy, honestly, wardens, no head for accounting—"

"Quillfeather," Emeric said, raw, "hold on."

"Holding on is *your* job." The eye closed. Opened again. "Mirabel. Girl. Ledger-keeper. When this is done — if I am spared, which I mention purely as a formality — remind me to teach you the fox-word for what you two are. There isn't a human word for it. Humans are very poor at naming things they actually mean."

And Emeric — soaked, wounded, kneeling in a flooded church while thirty enemies watched and seventeen ancient voices sang — looked at me across the fox's body and stopped waiting for after.

"I love you," he said. "Not after. Now. Whatever comes out of tonight — I love you, and I should have said it on the causeway, and if the tide takes me before I get the chance to say it badly at a proper dinner, I wanted it on the record. In the ledger. Write it down."

My eyes blurred. The choir sang. The water rose.

"Written," I said. "In ink. No erasures."

Then the nearest dark niche flickered — and I felt it, the pull of the eighteenth seat, reaching not for a jar or a relic but for something living, something present, something with a voice to give. The traitor-voice stirring in its sleep, hungry, hearing us sing. And far to the north, faint as a heartbeat through stone, something else answered: a woman's voice, threadbare and achingly familiar, singing a lullaby I had not heard since I was six years old.

My mother. Calling the choir home.

Quillfeather's breath rattled once, twice — and held.

"We move at dawn," I said, to Emeric, to the fox, to the singing stone, to the sea. "Both of you stay alive until dawn. Those are the terms."

The tide, for once, obeyed.
