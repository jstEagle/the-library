## 16. Nineteen Voices, Two Missing

We counted the voices the way you count anything that might slip through your fingers: twice, then a third time for spite.

Emeric laid them out on the flat stone at the center of the drowned cathedral's nave — or what passed for laying out voices, which is to say he drew nineteen circles in river-clay and I hummed into each one until something hummed back. The well-voices from the village. The mountain fragments from the stone-giants, who had given them up grudgingly, like old men surrendering letters they'd meant to answer for forty years. A bell-tone salvaged from the melted bells of the capital, smuggled out in Quillfeather's mouth because apparently that was a thing a fox could do if sufficiently motivated and sufficiently insulted by bell-melters. Voice after voice, humming back at me from little circles of clay like a congregation waiting for a sermon that had been postponed for three hundred years.

"Nineteen," Emeric said.

"Nineteen," I agreed, and hated how small the number sounded. "The covenant wanted twenty-one."

"Twenty-two now." He didn't look up from the clay. "It reached for one more when we were at the root-tree. You heard it."

I had heard it. That was rather the problem.

Quillfeather was draped across a fallen pillar with the boneless entitlement of a creature who had once been human and had never entirely forgiven the world for the demotion. "Nineteen of twenty-one," he said, "with three days to solstice, and our remaining two being, respectively, an eldritch hunger wearing my worst memories as a coat, and a woman who may or may not be currently trying to eat the covenant from inside. I've sung in worse choirs. There was the incident with the plague-year and the bishop who could only hold pitch through his nose. But not by much."

"Thank you, Quillfeather," I said.

"You're welcome. I find honesty is easier when it's delivered horizontally."

I sat down on the pillar's base and looked at the nineteen circles. Here is the thing nobody tells you about saving the world: most of it is arithmetic. Not the dramatic kind — the kind where you sit in a ruined building counting things and discovering that the count does not care about your feelings. Nineteen. Three days. Two missing. And one of those missing was my mother, which meant that every hour I spent not rescuing her was an hour she spent half-rooted to a tree, forgetting her own name one syllable at a time.

The other one was the voice that had turned. The ravenous one. Every promise humans broke, given appetite — Emeric had found that much in the forbidden archive, along with considerably more that kept him awake at night, though he pretended otherwise by the simple expedient of polishing his broken warden's badge until midnight.

"So," I said. "Let's do this properly. The traitor-voice first. We know where it will manifest — through her, through the anchor, mid-reforging. Which means we can't rescue Mother without meeting it. And we can't meet it without—"

"Without knowing what it wants," Emeric said.

"It wants everything. It said so. At length."

"And yet." He sat back on his heels. "Voss showed you a memory. What the old covenant devoured. And what I found in the archive confirms part of it — one voice did turn ravenous. But Mirabel." He looked up at me then, and there was that thing in his face he got when duty and conscience finished their argument and conscience won on points. "The archive says the turning wasn't theft. It wasn't a corruption that crept in from outside. The voice turned because it was *taken*."

The word landed in the ruined nave and echoed off wet stone.

"The old wardens harvested it," he went on quietly. "When the covenant needed strengthening, they didn't ask. They bound voices. Compelled them. Drew on them like accounts to be emptied. One of the twenty-one couldn't bear it. And a voice that gives freely becomes something else when it's taken instead. Something that takes back."

I thought about the ledger in my satchel. Not the Guild's ledgers — mine, the one I'd started keeping since we left the city, because apparently nearly dying in fens and mountains teaches you habits. Every impossible thing the wild ever gave freely, entered in my own hand. Griffin feathers, freely molted. Bottled thunder, gifted by the storm itself. A marsh-fox's guidance, offered for a debt unnamed because he trusted me to name it kindly later.

And on the Guild's side of the page, all the entries that were never gifts at all. Taken. Bought. Pressed into bones beneath prison floors.

"Oh," I said.

"Yes," said Emeric.

"That's why it hates us."

"It doesn't hate us. Hate would be simpler." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Hate you can fight. This is *accounting*, Mirabel. It's owed."

* * *

Quillfeather, who had been listening with his eyes closed in a manner I'd learned meant total attention, opened one eye. "So the plan is what, precisely? We arrive at the world's most important tree, free your mother — who is also the failing anchor, which is a phrase I'd like engraved on my headstone — and then negotiate with a cosmic debt-collector? I've met debt-collectors. They lack poetry."

"We don't negotiate," I said.

"No?"

"No. Because I think—" I stopped, and restarted, because some thoughts have to be walked around to before they'll let you look at them straight. "At the root-tree, when the covenant reached for a twenty-second voice. It wasn't reaching to take. I felt the difference. It was asking. The whole covenant, reforged or not, runs on asking and answering. The old wardens broke it by stopping asking. Voss breaks it by forbidding anyone to answer. And the traitor-voice—" My hands had gone cold, so I tucked them under my arms like a sensible person. "The traitor-voice can't be taken back either. It can't be fought down and bound again, because binding it is exactly the crime that made it. The final voice has to be *given*. Freely. Or none of this works, and the silence wins anyway, and we'll have done all this running just to lose politely."

Silence — the ordinary kind, the kind that isn't an apocalypse — filled the nave. Water dripped somewhere with the patience of architecture.

"That's an awful plan," Quillfeather said at last. "No contingency. No leverage. No fallback whatsoever. It relies entirely on the good faith of a starving god." A pause. "Which, historically, is how all the best disasters begin. Count me in."

"I haven't asked you to be in it."

"You fed me cheese in the mountains. In my experience that constitutes a lifetime contract." He stood, shook himself, and for one moment his sardonic mask slipped and underneath it was something older and wearier and terribly human-shaped. "Mirabel. When we get there — when the moment comes — whatever the covenant asks of you, remember that debts unnamed are debts *chosen*. Don't let anyone else name yours for you. Not Voss. Not the choir." His eyes flicked to Emeric and back. "Not even love."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I did what I always do when the world refuses to balance: I counted.

Three days to solstice. Nineteen voices humming softly in clay circles around the nave, a sound like a lullaby sung by a house that misses its family. One fox asleep on a pillar, twitching through dreams I could no longer hear — I'd given up so much already, and the largest giving was still ahead. One warden sitting watch at the door with his broken badge in his hand, pretending not to notice me noticing him.

Two voices missing. One was my mother, and I knew how to reach her, and reaching her terrified me less than what came after. The other was made of every broken promise I'd ever inherited, and I couldn't fight it, couldn't bind it, couldn't trick it. All I could do was open the ledger and tell it the truth: that accounting cuts both ways. That for every entry marked *taken*, there were hundreds marked *given*. That the wild had never stopped offering, even while we starved it.

It wouldn't be enough, probably. Truth rarely balances the books alone; someone still has to pay the difference. But I'd spent six years hiding my gift in a counting house, keeping perfect columns for people who used them to cage songbirds, and I was done with perfect columns that lied.

Tomorrow we'd walk to the root-tree. In three days, the worlds would either knit together in one vast chord or go silent forever, and the difference between those outcomes was going to be whether a starving voice could be persuaded that it was loved before it was owed.

I closed my eyes. Across the nave, Emeric turned toward me in the dark, and I felt him watching, and let myself be watched.

"Mirabel," he said quietly. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong—"

"Then I'll see you in the next verse," I said. "Now sleep, warden. We've an audience with a god tomorrow, and I intend to be insufferably well-rested for it."

Far away, past the drowned hills, something at the edge of the world began — very faintly, very hungrily — to sing.
