## 17. The Anchor at World's Edge

The root-tree was not what I expected, which is a thing you learn to say about everything once you've spent enough time in the company of impossible things.

I had imagined something vast and cathedral-dark, all shadow and gravity, the kind of tree that makes you whisper even when no one has told you to. What we found instead, after three days of walking north past the last maps and into country that had plainly never been mapped on purpose, was a birch.

A birch. White as bone, slender as a girl, standing alone in a bowl of green hills with its roots spread out across an entire valley like spilled ink. The crown of it was lost in low cloud. The roots went everywhere — over hills, under rivers, threading through stone — and every root hummed. Not a sound you heard so much as a sound your teeth heard, your ribs, the wet architecture of your own ears.

"There it is," Emeric said, in the voice of a man announcing a funeral.

"Very dramatic," said Quillfeather, from my shoulder. "The beating heart of creation. A stick."

But his tail had gone still, which for Quillfeather was the equivalent of another creature going to its knees.

We came down into the valley as the sun went long and gold behind us, and the closer we walked, the more the humming resolved into something I knew. Something I had known my whole life without knowing I knew it, the way you know the shape of your own house in the dark.

It was a lullaby.

My mother's lullaby. Four notes, rising, falling, rising again — the one she sang when storms kept me awake, when the ledgers gave me nightmares about numbers that wouldn't balance, when she thought I was asleep and I wasn't. She used to sing it under her breath while kneading bread, and I used to think it was just a tune she'd picked up somewhere, some market song, nothing.

It was the covenant. It had been passing through her mouth into my ears since before I could talk, and I had carried it across half the world without ever once hearing it properly.

"Mira." Emeric's hand found mine. He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. We had gotten very good, he and I, at whole conversations conducted entirely in the pressure of fingers.

Quillfeather hopped down and walked ahead, uncharacteristically quiet, his brush of a tail low.

The roots parted for us. That's the only way I can put it. They lifted — slowly, groaning, like old men rising from a table — and made a corridor through the tangle, and the humming deepened until it wasn't around us anymore but *in* us, in the marrow, in the spaces between thoughts. And at the end of the corridor, where all the great roots gathered and plunged into the earth together, there was a woman.

She stood — or rather, she *was held*, upright and gently, the way you hold a candle flame steady with cupped hands — with her back against the trunk of the birch. Roots had grown up around her legs and waist, pale and smooth as her own skin, so that it was hard to say where the wood ended and the woman began. Her hair had gone white as bark. Her arms hung loose at her sides, and her eyes were open, and they were fixed on the middle distance of twenty years ago.

She was singing. Of course she was singing. She had been singing for twenty years, four notes rising and falling and rising, and would sing until the solstice or until she broke, whichever came first.

"Mother," I said.

The word came out small. Twenty years of rehearsing this moment, of imagining it in the bath, on the road, at gravesides I'd invented because I couldn't stand having none — and all those rehearsals abandoned me at once, and left me a nine-year-old girl in a grown woman's throat.

She turned her head.

It took a long time. Her neck moved like something waking from a very long sleep, and when her eyes finally found me, they were my mother's eyes — brown, warm, faintly amused, always faintly amused, as if the world were a joke she was kindly choosing not to tell — but they were also not. Behind them was a depth of tiredness I had no name for. Behind them was the valley, and the river, and the sky, and every root, all at once, all the time.

"Hello," she said. Her voice was rust and honey. "You have your father's chin."

"I—" My prepared speech, all of it, every word I'd carried three hundred miles: gone. "You know me?"

"You're the one who comes in dreams," she said, and smiled, and my heart broke cleanly in two, like an egg on a bowl's edge. "Every year on my birthday. You sit by a window with a lamp and you do sums, and you look sad, and you don't see me. But today you're looking right at me. That's new."

*Every year on my birthday.* Every year on hers, then. She had been watching me dream my ordinary grief while she stood rooted at the edge of the world.

"It's me, Mama. It's Mirabel. Really me. I walked here. Well — I fell in a marsh several times, and there was a fox, and a warden, and a cathedral that tried to eat us, but I walked here."

Something flickered behind her eyes. Recognition, trying to climb out of a very deep well.

"Mirabel," she repeated, tasting it. "That's — that's my word. I've been holding that word. All this time, holding it, the way you hold—" her brow creased, "—the way you hold the door. So it doesn't slam. So the cold doesn't get in." Her gaze sharpened suddenly, terribly, and focused on me for the first time. "Mirabel. You shouldn't be here. The song needs—"

"The song doesn't need anything else from you," I said, and crossed the distance between us, and took her face in my hands.

Her skin was cool and slightly rough, like birch bark worn soft. Under my palms I felt the humming of her, the endless chord she'd been holding, and I understood with a sick lurch that she wasn't standing at the tree. She *was* part of it now, grown into the anchor-point the way a nail grows into old wood, and pulling her free would be—

Would be exactly what I intended to do.

* * *

Emeric built a fire a little apart, among the roots, because he is the sort of man who believes no crisis so grave that tea cannot improve it slightly. Quillfeather sat on his shoulder and watched my mother with an expression I couldn't read, though later I would understand it perfectly: the look of a man seeing the shape of his own sentence written in someone else's body.

My mother talked. Slowly, in loops, coming back to things she'd already told me, losing the thread and finding it again — but talking, oh, talking, twenty years of silence pouring out of her like water from a cracked jug.

She remembered our kitchen. She remembered the smell of the Guild counting house, which she'd hated, and how she used to pretend the pigeons outside told her secrets, and how I'd gone white as whey the first time she said it, aged six, because I thought she *knew*. She laughed at that, a dry leaf-scrape of a laugh, and I laughed too, and cried, and did both at once, which is a skill.

And then, in a quieter register, she told me the rest.

"I was a speaker," she said. "Like you. Stronger, they said. The Choir chose me young — before the Guild, before the silence, when the wild places still had voices enough to argue amongst themselves. And when the covenant began to fail — when the first voices started going dark — the Choir needed an anchor. Someone who could hold the remaining song together from inside. Someone whose gift was strong enough to be woven into the root itself."

"You volunteered," I said.

"I *ran toward it*," she corrected, with a flash of the old amusement. "They asked for volunteers and I knocked people over getting to the front. Your father was furious. He said it was suicide dressed up as duty. I said—" her smile faltered, "—I said love was also a kind of duty, and I could hold both. I believed it. I still believe it, mostly. Duty, I mean. Love I'm less sure of lately; it's been a long time since anyone brought me any."

"Well," I said thickly, "here I am. Brought you some. It's slightly bruised from travel."

She laughed again, properly this time, and for a moment she was entirely my mother, and the roots were just roots, and the world was a kitchen with bread in it.

Then her face changed. Went still. And she said, in a voice with no amusement in it at all:

"Mirabel. Listen to me carefully, because I only have moments of clarity and this one is spending itself fast. You must release me."

"I know. That's why we came. The reforging—"

"No. Not at the reforging. Now. Tonight. Even if it unmakes everything." Her hands — what hands she had free — gripped mine with sudden fierce strength. "Do you understand what I am? I am not the choir's keeper, child. I am its *stopper*. Like wax in a wound. When the voices began turning — when one of them went ravenous — the Choir panicked, and it bound the hunger into me along with the song. I have been holding both for twenty years. The song and the hunger. And I am *so tired*, Mirabel, and the hunger is patient, and it knows my name now, and some nights I cannot tell which of us is doing the singing."

Cold walked up my spine. "Voss showed me something," I said. "A memory. Of the covenant devouring a village. She claims the wild was always hungry. That the silence protects us."

"She showed you truth," my mother said, "with the context cut out of it, the way you'd cut the heart out of a letter and claim the paper lied. Yes, one voice turned. One. Out of twenty-one. Do you know why?" Her grip tightened. "Because humans stopped listening. Because every broken promise fed it, every burned forest, every caged bird, every bell melted down — it grew fat on betrayal until it forgot it had ever been anything else. Hunger isn't what it *was*, Mirabel. Hunger is what we *made* it. There is a difference, and Serane Voss has staked an empire on never learning it."

"And if I release you now? Before the reforging?"

"Then the pact fails tonight. The realms drift apart. The wild goes silent forever, and your world forgets it ever had neighbors, and maybe that's survivable. Maybe it's even kinder than what's coming." Her eyes were terrible and clear. "Because here is what I haven't told anyone, what I can barely hold onto myself: the hunger is close to the surface now. Three days. Perhaps fewer. And when I fail — and I will fail, Mira, I am failing already, can't you hear it, the lullaby keeps *slipping* — it won't slip away quietly. It will come out wearing my voice. It will come out *hungry*. Everything the Choir does between now and the solstice is a race, and I am the finish line, and I am coming apart."

Above us, the birch shivered in a windless evening. The lullaby faltered — actually faltered, a note dropping out, a hole in the fabric of the world — and every root in the valley groaned, and somewhere far off, I heard, or dreamed I heard, something answer. Something with appetite in it.

My mother sagged against her roots, spent. The moment of clarity closing like a eye.

"Mama," I whispered. "I can fix this. We have nineteen voices. Two more and the Choir reforges whole, and no one has to hold the hunger alone ever again. Give me three days."

"You beautiful fool," she murmured, sinking, her eyes drifting shut. "You count everything. Just like the ledgers. Some things aren't meant to balance, love. Some things are only meant to be *let go*."

And she slept, or rooted deeper, or whatever it is anchors do, and the lullaby resumed, thinner than before.

* * *

I sat by the fire a long time. Emeric sat beside me, saying nothing, which is one of perhaps four things he is genuinely brilliant at. Finally he handed me tea, and I drank it, and it was terrible, and that helped more than I can say.

"If she's right," I said eventually, "releasing her early unmakes everything. All of it. The marshes, the giants' songs, the well-voices, Quillfeather's name waiting to be spoken — all of it, gone, because I couldn't wait three days."

"Yes."

"But if we wait, and she breaks first, the hunger gets loose wearing her face, and Voss's army is three days behind us, and—" I pressed the heels of my hands into my eyes. "There's no ledger line for this, Emeric. There's no column where both sides come out even."

"No," he agreed quietly. "There never was one. That's what I finally understand, after everything — the Guild's whole sin is believing the world should balance. Debts paid, accounts closed, wild things filed away where they can't surprise you. But the covenant was never an account, Mirabel. It was a *song*. Songs don't balance. They resolve. Or they don't. You keep singing anyway."

I looked at him. Firelight, tired eyes, a healing wound he refused to complain about, and a steadiness that had nothing to do with certainty and everything to do with choice.

"When did you get wise?" I asked.

"Somewhere between the ambush and the archive. It snuck up on me."

From the darkness, a familiar sardonic drawl: "If you two are quite finished being moving, the fox has a tactical observation."

Quillfeather padded into the firelight. His usual smirk was absent. He looked, for the first time since I'd known him, genuinely afraid — and trying, with limited success, to make fear look like contempt.

"The lullaby skipped twice while you were talking," he said. "I counted. I count everything; it's a fox thing, we're basically accountants with better tails. Here's the tactical observation: the hunger isn't waiting for her to break. It's *helping*. Something has been chewing at the roots since sundown. Something that knows this place." He paused, and his ears flattened. "And something else is coming up the valley behind us. Heavy. Many feet. Torch-smell and iron. Your friend Voss travels fast when she smells an anchor."

Emeric was on his feet before the fox finished. I stayed sitting a moment longer, looking at my mother — white-haired, root-bound, singing her four notes into the dark, holding a door shut against the cold with twenty years of her one life.

*I ran toward it,* she'd said. *Love is also a kind of duty.*

"All right, Mama," I said softly. "Hold the door a little longer. Your daughter keeps terrible ledgers, but she pays her debts."

Behind us, at the mouth of the valley, the first torchlight bloomed like a red flower. And beneath the ground, beneath the humming, something ancient turned over in its sleep — and smiled.
