## 18. Serane Voss Comes to Sing

I have stood watch in worse places than a tree-line at the edge of the world. I have stood watch in the salt marshes of Kettering with frost in my boots and a dead man's cloak around my shoulders. I have stood watch outside a Guild tribunal while they decided whether my career — or my head — would be leaving the building.

But I had never before stood watch knowing exactly what was coming, how many of them there were, and precisely how little chance I had of stopping it.

There is a particular clarity in that. I recommend it to no one.

* * *

The root-tree rose out of the vale like a question nobody had dared ask for two hundred years. Its trunk was wider than the Guild's counting house, its branches so high they disappeared into a haze that might have been cloud or might have been song — at this point in my life I no longer pretended to know the difference. The bark was scored all over with old lines, like ledger entries written by someone with very large handwriting and very small patience.

Mirabel stood at its base with her hand flat against the wood, and even from thirty paces away where I'd stationed myself, I could see the tree answering her. A faint gold light pulsed under the bark where her palm touched, spreading outward in slow rings, the way sound spreads over still water.

Quillfeather sat on a root the size of a rowboat, grooming his tail with the air of a critic waiting for the second act.

"She's been like that an hour," he said without looking up. "The tree likes her. Trees have terrible taste. Present company excluded."

"Present company noted," I said. "Anything on the wind?"

"The wind," said Quillfeather, "smells like iron, oiled leather, bad decisions, and Serane Voss's particular brand of soap. Which is to say: yes. They're coming. Half a day, maybe less."

"How can you possibly smell her soap?"

"I'm a fox. I smell everything. It's a curse and occasionally a party trick." He stopped grooming and looked at me properly, one amber eye catching the light under the branches. "You're planning something stupid."

"I'm planning something necessary."

"Those are usually the same thing wearing different coats." He flicked his tail. "How many?"

"Voss marched out of the capital with three hundred," I said. "She'll have lost some on the roads, gained some opportunists. Call it two hundred eighty, plus whatever hunters she kept back. I counted forty banners when we saw the dust from the ridge."

"And you intend to hold a tree-line against that alone."

"I intend to *delay* a tree-line against that alone. There's a difference. The difference is about twenty minutes, if I'm good."

Quillfeather was quiet for a moment, which for him was roughly equivalent to a eulogy.

"You know," he said finally, "when I bargained with that woman in the fens, the debt was hers to me, not mine to her. I could have walked away at any point. I want that on the record. In writing, ideally. Do trees take dictation?"

"Noted for the record," I said. "Why don't you leave?"

He looked affronted. "Because someone has to witness your stupidity properly. If you die alone out here it's just a tragedy. If I watch it, it becomes a story, and stories are the only immortality available to foxes." He hopped down off the root and trotted toward the tree, then paused. "Besides. She sings better when she isn't listening for our deaths. I've heard her practice."

That landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there.

* * *

I went to Mirabel while the light was going long and amber through the branches. She had stopped touching the tree and was sitting cross-legged against its roots, the bone map open across her knees, though she wasn't reading it. She was staring at nothing, which I had learned was how she did her most dangerous arithmetic.

"They're coming," I said.

"I know. I felt it in the tree about an hour ago. Everything that touches the ground out here, the ground remembers." She looked up at me. Her face was pale and set, and there were shadows under her eyes from three days of preparation — learning the covenant's verses from her mother's fading whispers, memorizing the order of voices, doing the thing she did best, which was accounting. Twenty-one voices. One anchor. One singer.

One tree-line between her and two hundred eighty people who wanted all of it to stop.

"Emeric." She said my name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "Whatever you're about to propose, I want you to know I've already thought of it, and the answer—"

"—is no, I know. You're going to tell me to come inside the tree-line, or run, or hide behind the giants' stones, and I'm going to tell you that none of those things work." I crouched down so we were eye to eye. "Voss doesn't need to beat you, Mirabel. She needs to reach you. One moment of contact with the anchor while the ritual is open and she can pull the whole covenant's power sideways into the Guild's keeping. You told me yourself — the reforging is a door, and doors open both ways unless someone holds the hinge."

"So hold the hinge from *inside*—"

"There is no inside. There's just you, and the tree, and the song. That's the whole design. That's what your mother built her life into." I kept my voice level, which took more effort than any swordwork I've ever done. "The tree-line bends here, east side, where the ground dips. Two hundred men can't see the base of the tree until they're through it. If I stand at the bend, they have to deal with me first. And Voss won't simply cut me down."

"Why not?"

"Because she thinks she can still buy me. She offered once already, through Halvard. Wardens are expensive to train and she's lost most of us this year." I almost smiled. "Also because I have one thing left to trade, and I intend to spend it badly, on purpose, at the worst possible moment for her."

Mirabel was silent. Around us the great tree hummed, low and patient, like a held breath.

"If you die," she said at last, "the song will know. Midway through. And I'll—" Her voice did something complicated and came back harder. "I will lose the thread. Do you understand? Not metaphorically. The covenant runs on true things, freely given, and you standing out there dying alone for me is either the truest thing in this vale or the cruelest, and I don't get to choose which."

"Then don't listen for me," I said. "Listen for the choir. That's the entire discipline of wardenship, Mirabel — you hold the line so well that the people behind you forget there's a line at all. Give me that. Let me be forgotten for half an hour. It's the finest thing anyone's ever asked of me."

"That's a horrible thing to say."

"Yes," I agreed. "It's also the job."

She reached up and took the front of my coat in both hands, and pulled me down, and kissed me — not the careful kiss from the drowned cathedral, with arrows in the air and Quillfeather bleeding nearby, but a slow one, deliberate, a signature on a document neither of us had read fully and both of us meant anyway.

When she let go, she said: "You come back. That's the term. Freely given means I give it expecting return. That's my price, warden, and I'm told I drive a hard bargain."

"Who told you that?"

"A sarcastic fox."

"He's right." I stood up. My knees cracked. Somewhere far off to the south, too far to hear yet, an army was walking toward us through the dusk. "Half an hour, Mirabel. Sing beautifully."

* * *

They came at first light, because of course they did. Voss was never one for the dramatic cover of darkness; darkness implied shame, and Serane Voss had never been ashamed of anything in her life, including several things that should have shamed her considerably.

I heard them before I saw them — the specific sound of two hundred eighty boots finding a rhythm, and beneath it the creak of harness, and beneath *that*, faintly, the wrongness of caged birds. They'd brought them from the capital. Songbirds in gilded cages, dozens of them, carried on poles like standards. Voss had learned something since the cathedral. She'd learned that silence wasn't just an absence. It was a weapon you could aim.

I stood at the bend of the tree-line with my father's sword and my grandfather's cloak-pin and absolutely no plan beyond the next twenty minutes, and I watched the vanguard come through the morning mist, and I thought: *this is the most sensible suicidal decision of my career.*

The first rank halted when they saw me. Good. Confusion is free; use it early.

A rider came forward — Halvard. Of course Halvard. Broad, gray-bearded, my oldest friend in the Order, the man who'd taught me how to fall off a horse without dignity but with survival. He reined in ten paces away and looked at me the way you look at a wound that keeps reopening.

"Emeric."

"Halvard."

"You look terrible."

"I've been sleeping outdoors and eating fox-caught fish. The fish are fine. The sleeping is the problem."

Something twitched at the corner of his mouth and died there. Behind him, the ranks waited, spears angled, and above their heads the cages swung gently, and inside the cages the birds were silent — not resting-silent. *Made*-silent. I could feel it from where I stood, a pressure like a held note, like the vale itself flinching.

"Last offer," Halvard said. "You know I hate carrying these. Voss reinstates you. Full honors, full rank, the archive keys, everything they stripped. All you do is walk away from that tree. Nobody touches the woman. Voss says she only wants to talk."

"She wants to talk *after* she has the anchor's throat in her hand. I've seen how she talks."

Halvard's jaw tightened. "Emeric. Three hundred of us. One of you. I've watched you fight; you're good, but you're not three hundred good."

"No," I agreed. "But I'm not trying to beat you. I'm trying to bore you. There's a difference, and the difference is about twenty minutes."

He sighed, long and tired, the sigh of a man who had argued with me across a hundred campfires and always lost the arguments that mattered. Then he drew his sword, and the ranks behind him leveled their spears, and somewhere in the back a captain started the cadence call, and that was that.

"Hold!" said a voice like a drawn wire.

Serane Voss rode forward through her own army as though it were furniture. She wore white and gray, Guild colors, and she looked less like a general than an auditor arriving to find the accounts in disarray. She halted her horse beside Halvard's and looked down at me with an expression I can only describe as *appraisal.*

"Warden Thorne. Or should I say fugitive Thorne. Or should I say — and this is the version I prefer — *colleague.*"

"Master Voss."

"Do you know why I brought the birds?" She gestured upward at the swinging cages. "Not to kill the ritual. Killing it would be easy — fire does that. No. I brought them because when I take the anchor's power, I need vessels. Empty ones. The Guild has spent sixty years buying up every relic, every relic-song, every scrap of the old covenant, and do you know what we learned?" She leaned forward slightly. "We learned that the wild doesn't die when you cage it. It *waits*. Power hoarded is power multiplied. Your woman out there wants to give the covenant away — scatter it back into the world, into every beast and bird and stone. Free distribution. Zero return." She said the words like profanities. "I am going to collect it instead. Every voice. Into vessels that answer to the Guild. An asset, Warden, the likes of which history has never audited."

"And the people whose voices you collect?"

"Complications." She smiled, and it was the smile of a woman balancing books. "Which brings me to you. Step aside, and when this is done, I'll give you something worth more than reinstatement. I'll let you keep her. Alive, unharmed, unbound. She walks out of this vale a null — giftless, harmless, ordinary — and you two can go be ordinary together somewhere the Guild doesn't look. That is not a threat, Emeric. That is generosity. I am offering you a life."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave receives me: for one full breath, I considered it. Not because I doubted. Because I was tired, and afraid, and the picture she painted was warm and small and safe, and I am only human, whatever else the Order made me.

Then I looked past her, past the ranks, up through the mist to where the root-tree's crown vanished into light, and I heard — actually heard, thin and far and steady — Mirabel beginning to sing.

It wasn't a performance. It was an opening. The first verse of the reforging, rising through the vale like water finding its level, and every caged bird in Voss's army went rigid on its perch, straining against bars toward the sound.

"No," I said.

Voss's smile cooled by several degrees. "No to which part?"

"All of it. But mostly to 'ordinary.'" I drew my sword. "You've spent sixty years teaching this realm that the wild is a debt to be collected. She's spent her whole life learning that it's a ledger of gifts. I know which entry I'm filing under."

For a moment — one moment — something moved behind Voss's eyes that was almost respect, or perhaps just recalculation. Then she turned her horse and said, in the voice of a woman closing a ledger herself:

"He's yours, Captain. Waste as little time as possible. I want the tree before the third verse."

* * *

What follows, I remember in pieces, the way you remember weather.

The first wave broke against me at the bend of the line, where the ground dipped and the great roots made walls, and I learned that the terrain loved me today. They could only come at me four abreast, and four abreast is a number a trained warden can speak to. I didn't try to win. Winning wasn't on the ledger. I tried to make every exchange cost them ten seconds, and then another ten, and I gave ground the way a miser gives coins — grudgingly, and always with interest collected.

Halvard fought me in the second wave, and he fought me sadly, and sadly is worse than angrily because sad men hesitate and hesitating men die at other people's hands. I disarmed him rather than opened him, and shoved him backward into his own ranks, and shouted — I confess it, I shouted like a recruit — "Go home, Hal! Go home!"

He didn't go home. He got up, and cried, and came at me again, and I loved him, and I put him down again, and the third time his heart wasn't in it and neither was mine, and somehow that saved us both for another minute.

The birds were the worst of it. As Mirabel's song climbed, the caged birds began to sing back — muffled, desperate, harmonizing through gilded bars — and the soldiers near the cages grew uneasy, then frightened, then angry at their own fear. Some started smashing the cages. Whether to free the birds or stop the noise, I couldn't tell, and it didn't matter; freed birds poured upward through the branches in a spiral of sound that joined Mirabel's voice and made the whole vale ring like a struck bell.

Voss screamed orders. The waves came faster. I stopped counting seconds and started counting breaths, then stopped counting altogether, and there was a stretch of time — I honestly cannot tell you if it was minutes or the better part of an hour — that was only steel and mud and the tree-line bending around me like a bowstring, and my arms going from pain to numbness to some third country beyond both.

I fell twice. I got up twice. The third time, a spear took me through the meat of the left shoulder and put me on my knees, and a young soldier stood over me with his blade raised, and he was shaking, and I looked at him and said, "Son, in about a minute you're going to want to remember you hesitated."

He frowned. Above us, the sky opened.

Not literally. But the song crested — Mirabel's voice and nineteen gathered voices and the deep hum of the root-tree and the freed birds and, God help me, even the stone-giants humming from the ridgeline like distant thunder finding a melody — and the sound rolled down the vale and through the ranks of Voss's army and *through* them, and men dropped their spears not because they were defeated but because they suddenly, unmistakably, remembered what music was.

The soldier with the raised blade lowered it. Sat down in the mud. Started, of all things, to laugh, wetly, helplessly.

I knelt in the churned earth with blood running down my arm and listened to the covenant being reborn above me, and I thought: *twenty minutes. I gave her twenty minutes.*

I gave her fifty-five.

* * *

But the chord didn't finish.

It climbed, and climbed, and hung at its peak like a wave that would not break — and through the ringing, through the singing, I felt the vale tilt. Something in the harmony was missing. Two somethings. And coming up the south road through the last of the mist, unhurried now, certain, her white cloak spotless amid the wreckage of her own army, rode Serane Voss — not retreating, not raging, but *listening*, head tilted, eyes closed, drinking in the incomplete song like a woman tasting wine she intended to buy the vineyard of.

Two voices short. The ravenous one. The anchor.

And Voss, smiling, riding straight for the tree.

I got one knee under me. Then the other. My shoulder screamed. My sword lay somewhere in the mud behind me and I didn't go back for it, because I knew — with the strange cold clarity of a man who has done the sums — that I had nothing left to hold the line with except the line itself.

So I stood up out of the mud, empty-handed, and planted myself between Serane Voss and the root-tree one last time.

"Master Voss," I said. "Your accounts are overdue."
