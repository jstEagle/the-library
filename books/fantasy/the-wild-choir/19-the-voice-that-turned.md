## 19. The Voice That Turned

I had sung for three days.

Not the way singers mean it — not with breath and throat and the small vanity of performance — but the way a loom means it, thread after thread pulled through tension, each voice of the gathered choir drawn from its keeping and set into the root-tree's rings. Nineteen voices, Emeric counted them nightly, his voice gone hoarse, his wound bandaged in moss that Quillfeather swore was better than any Guild salve. Nineteen voices humming through the great tree at world's edge until the bark itself glowed faintly, like a lamp behind a curtain.

And my mother stood among them, half-rooted, half-song, her feet gone to pale wood, her hair drifting like kelp in a current only she could feel. She remembered me now. Sometimes. In fragments. *You were born in a thaw,* she'd said that morning, wonderingly, as if I'd told her. *The ice sang when you came.*

I was holding her hand — or the hand-shaped part of her — when the song turned.

It began as a wrongness in the harmony, a note bent flat, then bent further, like a spoon in a mouth. The nineteen voices faltered. The root-tree shuddered, shedding rings of light. And my mother's face —

My mother's face changed.

Not into something else. Into many things else, all at once, features surfacing and drowning: a wolf's long jaw, a woman's grief, a hunger with no face at all, just depth. Her eyes went black as a well you drop a coin into and never hear land.

"*There* you are," the thing wearing my mother said, and its voice was every voice at once — hers, mine, the caged songbird's, the warehouse rats', all of them run backward and sharpened. "Nineteen voices gathered like coins in a purse. Come to count me, little ledger-keeper?"

Quillfeather's fur rose along his spine. He didn't say anything clever. That frightened me more than the voice did.

"Emeric." My own voice came out thin. "Is this—"

"The traitor-voice." He had his hand on his sword and we both knew how useless that was. His knuckles were white around the grip anyway. "Chapter said it would manifest at the reforging. It didn't say it would wear her."

"It wears what it is given," the voice said, mild as a clerk. It turned my mother's head toward me, slow, savoring. "She gave freely. They all did, once. That was the covenant's genius and its ruin — everything given freely can be taken freely. Do you know what I am, Mirabel Sandoval?"

I should have been terrified. I was, partly. But underneath the terror was the counting-house calm that twenty years of balancing impossible accounts had built into my bones, and that calm looked at the black eyes and thought: *a debt has come due.*

"Tell me," I said.

"I am every promise humans broke." The tree groaned. Somewhere below us, in the dark under the world, something vast rolled over. "Every hedge cut after it was sworn to stand. Every river dammed past its word. Every bird caged after the pact said the sky was common property. You made promises, your kind. Beautiful ones. Carved into stones older than your oldest cities. And then you got busy. And hungry. And afraid." The mouth smiled with my mother's lips. "I kept the receipts. I am the receipts. Given appetite."

"And what do you want?"

"What I am owed."

"There it is," Quillfeather muttered. "There's always an invoice."

"Nineteen voices," the thing said, ignoring him, or eating his words, hard to say. "You've come to stuff them back into the machine and call the world whole again. But the machine broke because the accounting was false. Your kind took and called it trade. Took and called it progress. Took and called it nothing, because who keeps books against the wind?" The black eyes fixed on me. "So. Before you sing your pretty chord, little auditor — answer the account. What do you have that balances what was taken?"

And here is the shameful thing, the thing I will carry even after everything: for one full heartbeat, I had no answer.

I stood at the root of the world with my mother's stolen face smiling at me and nineteen voices trembling in the branches, and I thought of Voss's memory — the devouring, the villages gone down a gullet of green, the truth inside her lie — and I thought: *maybe the account doesn't balance. Maybe it never did. Maybe the wild has every right to eat us.*

The silence stretched. Emeric moved half a step toward me, not to protect me — to stand where I could see him, which was its own kind of protection. Quillfeather's tail lashed once.

And then I thought of the ledger.

Not metaphorically. Actually. The actual book, wrapped in oilcloth in my pack because I had not been able to leave it behind; because a person is what she counts, and I am what I count. Ten years of entries. Griffin feathers received against thunder bottled. Impossible things, entered honestly, in my own small neat hand.

"You want an accounting," I said. "Fine. Let's do it properly."

I knelt and unwrapped the ledger.

* * *

The thing let me. I think it was curious. Appetite always is.

I opened to the first page and my hands were shaking, but my voice — my voice found the counting-house register, the one I used for auditors and Guild masters, dry and clear and unimpressed by anything. Reading aloud.

"Entry the first. Year of the Grey Frost. A griffin hen, nesting on the Guild's own roof, plucked nine feathers from her own breast and left them at the window of a fever-ward, unasked. Nine feathers. One child saved. Cost to the griffin: comfort, warmth, blood. Payment rendered: none requested. None given." I looked up. "Do you have that entry, sir? Or madam? Or whatever address you prefer?"

Something flickered in the black depths. "Continue."

"'Entry the eleventh. The river Wye, having been promised a bridge of singing stone in exchange for safe passage forever, received instead a bridge of grey granite and a tax collector.'" I heard Quillfeather snort. "'Nevertheless, the following spring, when plague came upriver, the Wye slowed itself for six days — six — so that the dead could be ferried home before the water took them. Unasked. Unpaid. Entered here as an outstanding credit.'"

Page by page. I read the whole ledger aloud, there at the root of the world, while the traitor-voice wore my mother's face and listened.

The marsh that swallowed a Guild hunting party and spat them out at its border, sleeping, their weapons neatly stacked. The oak that dropped a limb precisely between a falling child and the ground, breaking itself instead of her. The wolves who led a lost caravan through a whiteout, walking ahead in single file for two days, asking nothing. The bees who moved their hive rather than sting a boy who'd thrown stones at it yesterday. The whale-bones that washed ashore, year after year, exactly where the coastal villages needed them for their winter fires — and the sea that never once sent a bill.

"Some of these are small," I said, closing the ledger at last. My voice had gone rough. "Some are enormous. None of them balance the broken promises. I know that. I'm a bookkeeper; I know when a column doesn't come out even. But here's what ten years of entries taught me, and what you apparently never learned, keeping your receipts in the dark under the world:" I stood up. "The taking was real. The breaking was real. But it was never *all* anyone did. Humans broke the covenant and fed it and mourned it and, in nine hundred small unrecorded ways, kept paying into an account they'd been told was closed. Freely. Without witnesses. Without hope of return."

"That is not in my records," the voice said. And there — I heard it — a crack in the appetite. Not softening. *Confusion.* Hunger meeting a number it couldn't parse.

"No," I agreed. "Because nobody ever showed you the other ledger. Because it's easier to be owed than to be surprised." I held the book out. "Read it. All of it. Then tell me the account is closed."

The black eyes went down to the pages. My mother's ruined hands reached — hesitated — reached again.

And touched the paper.

* * *

What happened then, I don't entirely have words for, and I keep books for a living, so believe me when I say the vocabulary failed.

The thing read. I felt it reading — a pressure like deep water moving over me, page after page, decade after decade of small unpaid kindnesses flowing into that vast starving account. And the account *changed*. Not balanced — I won't insult either of us with that. But changed, the way a frozen river changes in thaw: not gone, not safe, but *moving*.

When it looked up, my mother's eyes were my mother's eyes again, wet and astonished.

"Mirabel," she said. Just that. Just my name, in her own voice, one voice, not nineteen.

"Mum."

"I remember—" Her brow furrowed, wood and flesh wrestling. "I remember being angry. So long. So hungry-angry. And I remember... feathers on a windowsill. Was that true? Did someone really—"

"A griffin. Nine of them. It's all written down."

She laughed. It came out cracked, half birdsong, but it was a laugh. "Your father always said you'd amount to a good audit."

The tree-light steadied. Around us the nineteen voices resumed, tentatively, finding their pitch. Emeric let out a breath he'd apparently been holding since before I started reading. Quillfeather sat down abruptly, as if his legs had filed a complaint.

"Well," the fox said. "I've seen heroes duel dragons, wrestle giants, out-riddle death itself. Never watched one defeat the apocalypse with *accounts receivable*. I'm going to need a very long drink and possibly a new worldview."

But the tree wasn't finished.

The light in the roots pulsed — once, twice — and then rose up the trunk in a slow spiral, gathering, condensing, until it hung before us in the shape of a question. I felt it the way you feel a hand extended in the dark. The covenant itself, old and patient and terribly polite, waiting to ask.

Emeric translated, because he knew the old forms. "It says: twenty voices stand ready. The traitor has turned. The anchor holds — barely." His jaw tightened. "It wants to complete the chord. It needs a speaker to bind it."

"Then it has nineteen plus one turned plus one anchor," I said. "That's twenty-one. What's it waiting—"

And then I understood. All at once, the way you understand a column that finally won't balance no matter how you carry the numbers. Twenty-one voices, and the covenant reaching for a twenty-second, and the twenty-second had to be living, willing, *given* — and there was exactly one person standing at the root-tree who carried a piece of the wild in her own hidden chest.

Who had carried it her whole life in secret. Who had hidden it the way the Guild taught everyone to hide it.

"Oh," I said quietly.

"Mirabel." My mother's hand closed over mine, bark-cool. "Whatever it's asking. Don't answer yet. Promise me you'll think—"

Behind us, far off across the fens and the dark, horns. Many horns. And under them, faint but unmistakable, the sound of bells being carried — melted-down, voiceless bells — on the shoulders of an army.

Voss had come to sing.

Quillfeather stood, ears flat. "Company," he said.
