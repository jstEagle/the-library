## 21. The Twenty-Second Voice

The chord was still ringing.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only prayer I know how to say. The covenant had been reforged — twenty-one voices braided into the root-tree's heart, Quillfeather standing among them in his one borrowed breath of humanity already folded back into fur, Emeric bleeding somewhere behind me at the tree-line and alive, alive, alive — and the whole of it hung in the air like a bell that refused to finish being struck.

And then it reached for me.

Not gently. Let no one ever tell you the wild is gentle. It is generous, which is a different animal entirely. Generous things take what they need and trust you to survive the taking.

The root-tree's light turned toward me like a face. I felt it look through my ribs, past the ledgers I'd memorized, past every careful lie I'd told the Guild for nine years, down to the small warm secret I had kept since childhood: the listening. The gift. The thing that made me *me*, or so I had believed.

*One voice remains unbound,* said everything, everywhere, all at once. My mother's voice in it, and the stone-giants', and the drowned choir from the well, and nineteen more I couldn't name. *The pact must be spoken by living lips, held by a living ear. A speaker who hears the wild as the wild speaks.*

"No," I said, because I am honest even in extremis, and honesty begins with the obvious.

*Mirabel.* That one was just my mother. Just her, thin as thread. *Sweetheart. It has to be given. It cannot be taken.*

"I know," I said. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

Let me back up, because there are things you should understand about the shape of the choice before I tell you how I made it. Accounting habit. Everything must balance.

My beast-speech had cost me all along the road. Every voice I coaxed free from its prison — three in the well, the mountain fragments, the traitor-voice answered with the ledger rather than force — each one took something. At first it was sleep. Then color. For one terrible day outside the Ashspine I tasted nothing but ash, and Emeric watched me push food around my bowl and didn't ask, because he knew asking would make it real faster.

By the sunken cathedral I had begun losing names. Not people's names — those I clung to like rope — but the names of things. I stood on the shore of the drowned nave and looked at water for a full minute before the word *water* came to me, arriving late, like a clerk who'd stopped caring about punctuality.

So when the covenant reached for the last of it, I understood the arithmetic better than it perhaps expected me to. It wasn't reaching for a trinket. It was reaching for the room where I kept myself.

"You could ask someone else," I told the tree. "You have twenty-one voices. Surely twenty-one is enough."

*Nineteen were freed,* said the chorus. *Two were given by their owners. The pact was written for twenty-two. One voice must stand inside it and listen forever, so that neither realm can again forget the other exists. That voice must belong to both worlds. Yours is the only door left open.*

"Quillfeather—"

*Is bound as a voice, not a speaker. He cannot hold the door. He can only be part of what passes through it.*

"And if I refuse?"

Silence then. Honest silence, the kind Voss had never once offered me. And in that silence I felt the truth settle: the realms would not shatter. They would simply drift apart again, slowly, the way they had drifted for a century, and the silence would come back patient and permanent, and someday a girl in a counting house would hear a caged bird beg and have to pretend she hadn't, forever, until she died politely of old age surrounded by balanced books.

I had already lived one lifetime of that. I did not fancy a second.

* * *

Emeric found me at the tree's roots. He walked badly, favoring the leg the Guild's crossbow bolt had opened at the tree-line, and he had tied off his sleeve in that terrible competent way he had, as though wounds were simply another entry requiring initialing. He knelt beside me in the root-light, which lit him gold and made him look briefly like a man out of an older, kinder story.

"The voices are waiting," he said. "They're asking for you."

"I know. I'm negotiating."

"With a tree."

"With everything," I said. "It's what I do. I'm very good at it." My hands were shaking, so I pressed them flat against the bark, which was warm, warmer than any tree had a right to be, warm as a sleeping animal. "Emeric. If I do this, I won't hear them anymore. Not the fox. Not the rats who started all this. Not my mother. I'll be deaf in half the world, permanently, and everyone will think I've been cured of something instead of robbed."

He was quiet a moment. He never gave comfort quickly; he gave it accurately, and accuracy takes time.

"When we met," he said, "you lied to me beautifully. You were cornered, hunted, holding a dead man's map, and you lied with such poise I nearly believed you. Do you remember what you said?"

"That I was a simple accountant."

"A simple accountant," he agreed. "And I thought, there is nothing simple about this woman, and nothing accountant-like either, whatever she believes. Because it wasn't the lie that convinced me. It was the way you listened. You listened to me like I was something wild you might understand, if you were patient enough." His hand covered mine against the bark. "Mirabel. The hearing isn't what makes you a speaker. You've been speaking to both worlds your whole life. The gift was only ever the proof."

"That's very moving and also not the point."

"It's entirely the point. You're afraid you'll be less without it."

"I *will* be less without it."

"You'll be *different* without it," he said, "which is what you actually fear, because different means unknown, and you have spent nine years paying for the crime of being unknown to yourself." He leaned closer. "Ask the fox."

"What?"

"Ask Quillfeather. He chose, today. He knows what choosing costs. If anyone here can price it honestly, it's him."

* * *

Finding Quillfeather in a crowd of singing spirits is harder than you'd think, mostly because he insists on narrating. I followed the sarcasm. It carried well.

"—no, the *third* verse, the giants keep skipping to the fourth because they're show-offs with granite throats and no sense of dramaturgy—" He broke off when he saw my face, which is a service he performed perhaps four times in our entire acquaintance. "Ah," he said. "It's asked you, hasn't it. The last chair at the table."

"It wants my gift. All of it. Poured into the pact, permanent."

He sat down. Foxes sit like a verdict being delivered. "Well," he said. "Now we come to it."

"You knew?"

"I suspected when the well-voices sang your name back to you. Names don't travel unless something's collecting them." His ears flattened. "Mirabel, I'm going to say something, and you're forbidden from repeating it, especially to me, later, in a mocking tone."

"Noted."

"I was a chorister. A human man with a human name, and I lost it because I broke my word, and I have spent two hundred years telling jokes about it so I wouldn't have to feel it. Today the tree gave me one breath of my old self. One." His tail curled tight around his feet. "Do you know what I felt? Nothing. Nostalgia, maybe. A coat I'd outgrown. Because I have been a fox so long that fox is not what I lost. Fox is what I *am*. The transformation didn't erase me, girl. It finished me." He looked up, amber eyes steady. "You think giving up the hearing makes you smaller. But I have watched you these weeks. The hearing is not your soul. Your soul is the thing that kept answering the begging birds even when answering got you whipped. Take away the hearing and you're still the one who answers. That doesn't live in your ears."

I sat down next to him in the roots of the world and cried, briefly, efficiently, the way one cries over a bill one has known was coming.

"All right," I said. "All right. But I want conditions."

"You're negotiating with a cosmic pact."

"Somebody has to. Its terms are terrible."

* * *

Here is what I demanded, and here is what was granted, because it turns out the wild honors contracts better than the Guild ever did — it simply writes them in living things instead of ink:

First: that my mother be released from anchoring whole and healed, that her release precede my binding, so that I would never hold the door alone in the dark. Granted, and gladly; I felt her sigh out of the wood like a long-held breath even as we spoke.

Second: that the ledger be entered into the covenant entire. Every impossible thing the wild ever gave freely — every feather, every storm shared, every harvest the marshes yielded in famine years — recorded as debt acknowledged, so that no future Serane Voss could claim the wild owed humanity nothing. Granted. The tree liked that one. I felt it *like* it, a ripple of appetite through the roots, and I understood suddenly why the ravenous voice had turned: it had simply wanted to be counted, and no one had ever counted it.

Third — and this one I almost didn't dare — that the hearing not be destroyed but *given*, which is not the same. A destroyed thing is gone. A given thing lives wherever you send it. I poured mine into the pact itself, so that the covenant would hear with my ears, speak with my tongue, and if ever a child was born in either realm with the listening waking in them, they would find a door already open, held by something that remembered what it was like to hide.

Granted. All granted.

Which left only the doing.

* * *

I will not dress up what happened next, because dressing things up is what cowards call accounting.

My mother held my face in her hands. She smelled of loam and rain and, faintly, of the kitchen in our old house, rosemary and scorched honey, a smell I had carried eighteen years without knowing I still owned it. "You don't have to be brave," she said. "Brave is just what frightened people call each other afterward."

"Mama. What was my name for you? When I was small?"

She smiled, and it cracked her open, and it cracked me. "*Wren,*" she said. "Because you never stopped singing, even when no one could hear you. Especially then."

"Then hear me now."

And I let go.

I don't have a better word. I had braced for tearing, for theft, for the sensation Voss's memory-machine had shown me of being devoured. It was none of those. It was pouring. It was every coin in the strongbox tipped out at once into a bigger strongbox, and the strange grief of watching the box empty, and the stranger relief of discovering how much lighter your hands are when they're not clenched around something you were terrified to lose.

The hearing went out of me like a tide going out — and it did not go silent. That is the miracle I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe. As my gift left me, the whole choir came *in*: twenty-one voices and now my own twenty-second, and beneath them the vast sub-audible hum of the root-tree, and beneath *that* the sound of the realms themselves knitting, a seam being drawn through the world, thread by golden thread. I heard the marshes exhale a hundred miles away. I heard the Ashspine giants take up the third verse at last, and the fourth, and a fifth no one had sung since before the breaking. I heard bells in the capital — real bells, unmelted, ringing themselves awake — and I heard, unmistakably, a warehouse full of rats cheering.

Then it faded. Not to nothing. To ordinary quiet, the quiet of a forest at evening, birdsong without translation, wind without commentary. Deaf in half the world, exactly as promised.

And alive in all of it.

* * *

Voss's power crumbled the way bad architecture crumbles — all at once, and embarrassingly. We learned the details later, but I felt the moment from inside the afterglow: the enforced silence she'd woven through the capital was stitched from stolen covenant-thread, and when the reforged pact claimed its own back, her loom simply came apart. Somewhere across the miles, I imagine her standing amid falling machinery, realizing that you cannot own a song. You can only sing it or refuse to. She had refused for thirty years, and the interest had come due.

But that was later. In the moment, there was only the clearing under the root-tree, and the light going soft and ordinary and mortal, and my mother — released, upright, *whole* — laughing and crying into my hair while Emeric limped toward us wearing the expression of a man trying very hard not to hope out loud.

"Well," said Quillfeather, trotting up, tail high. "Congratulations on your promotion from miracle-worker to unemployed accountant. How does it feel to be merely a person?"

"Liberating," I said. "Terrifying. Ask me again tomorrow."

"You'll want to know," he said, and his voice did something sly and gentle at once, "that I can still talk. Gift's yours now, yes, but speech was always mine. So if you ever need anything translated — gossip, insults, the emotional availability of a certain warden —"

"Quillfeather."

"—I'm available. Rates negotiable. First consultation free."

Emeric reached us. He took my hand — the hand that had signed the greatest contract in history, still smudged with bark and blood — and he didn't say anything grand. He just laced his fingers through mine like balancing a final column, and nodded once, as if to say: *it balances.*

Behind us, the root-tree settled into its long work of holding two worlds together, humming quietly to itself in a voice I could no longer translate and no longer needed to.

We started home in the blue hour, three of us and one ghost of a gift walking invisibly between us, and it was Quillfeather who stopped first, ears swiveling north, and said, in a tone I had never heard him use —

"Someone's coming up the road. Human. Alone." A pause. "Mirabel — she's carrying a ledger."
