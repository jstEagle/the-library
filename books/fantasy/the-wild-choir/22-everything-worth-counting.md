## 22. Everything Worth Counting

The ledger smelled of cedar and ink and, faintly, of marsh — a smell that had followed me home like a stray dog and refused to be put out.

I sat in the Guild's counting house at my old desk by the salt-light window, the same desk where I had once balanced accounts for griffin feathers and bottled thunder while pretending I couldn't hear the songbird begging two shelves away. The desk hadn't changed. The window hadn't changed. The counting house itself had barely changed, except that now there was a column in every ledger marked *Given Freely*, which had started as my idea and had become, to my ongoing astonishment, official policy.

Also, there was a fox asleep on the warm stones near the brazier, and no one pretended he wasn't there.

"Quillfeather," I said, without looking up from the column of figures. "You're on the entry for river-pearls."

"I am *resting*," said the fox, without opening his eyes. "There is a difference. Resting is what happens when one has held a choir together with sheer personality for three weeks. Entry it yourself."

"You're the translator."

"Then translate this: if you write down that I negotiated the pearl-price while hungover from fermented rowan berries, I will tell Emeric about the incident with the goat."

"There was no incident with the goat."

"Exactly." He opened one amber eye. "And there never will be. As long as the ledger stays honest."

I wrote: *River pearls, forty strands, fair price agreed, translator present and insufferable.* He read it upside down, as foxes apparently can, and pronounced it acceptable.

Three weeks since the solstice. Three weeks since the chord that had knitted two realms together so thoroughly that you could now hear the wild singing under the cobbles of the capital if you stood still long enough, and half the city did stand still, daily, just to listen. Three weeks since I had poured everything I was — the secret gift I had spent twenty-two years hiding, hoarding, being ashamed of and defined by — into the covenant until there was nothing left of it in me but the memory of having had it.

People asked me if I regretted it. Voss asked me once, actually, in her new office with its new open windows, in the tone of a woman who had lost an empire and was trying to determine whether she'd lost anything else worth mentioning.

"No," I'd told her. "I kept the ledgers. I know exactly what things cost. That one was cheap."

She hadn't laughed. But she had signed the trade accord, which from Serane Voss was practically a love sonnet.

* * *

My mother came to the city twice a week now, walking up from the root-tree with a basket on her arm as though she were any mother visiting any daughter, instead of a woman who had been half-tree, half-song for eleven years and had come back to herself one voice at a time.

She still moved slowly. The healers said she always would, some of her — the rooted part, the patient part — having stayed in the wood permanently, the way a river keeps the shape of its banks. She didn't mind. She said, in fact, that she rather liked knowing where she ended; she'd spent so long not knowing anything at all that boundaries felt like gifts.

On market days she helped me with the entries. She had been a bookkeeper before the covenant took her — nobody had ever told me that, or perhaps I'd been too young to remember — and her hand was steadier than mine, her figures smaller and more elegant.

"You press too hard," she said, watching me total the moss-contracts. "You always did. Even as a girl. As if the paper might argue."

"The paper usually wins," I said.

"It does when you fight it." She set her cup down. "Mirabel. I want to say something, and I want you to let me finish it."

I braced. Mothers announcing they want to finish sentences are never delivering good news about supper.

"When I was bound," she said, "for most of those years, I didn't know my name. I knew I was *holding* something — the pact, the chord, whatever we're calling it now — and I knew it hurt, and I knew someone would come. I didn't know who. But near the end, when I was mostly root and almost nothing else, I could feel the ledger."

"The—" I stopped.

"Your ledgers. Every impossible thing you'd recorded. Griffin feathers, bottled thunder, all of it. They reached me. All those years you thought your gift was a shameful secret you hid behind arithmetic — and the whole time, your arithmetic was carrying the covenant's memory. You were doing the anchor's work and calling it drudgery." Her eyes were bright and steady, the way hers were, the way mine were, the way I only now recognized in the glass. "So don't you dare tell me the gift was wasted because you gave it up. It was never yours alone to keep. It was always going back to them. You just made sure it arrived honestly."

I looked down at the ledger very hard for a while. The figures swam.

"That's not how accounting works," I managed finally.

"No," she agreed, patting my hand. "It's better."

* * *

Emeric found me at dusk on the counting house roof, which had become our unofficial office, on account of the view and the fact that Quillfeather refused to attend meetings indoors on principle.

He climbed through the trapdoor with the unhurried competence of a man who had done it a hundred times and still checked his footing each time anyway. Covenant-warden habits died hard, even in wardens whose order had been dissolved, formally apologized to, and partially re-founded around him. The New Wardens — his phrase, delivered with the grimace of a man biting a lemon — had their first cohort of six recruits, all of whom adored him, none of whom he permitted to call him *sir*.

Below us, the city glowed. Bells rang the evening hours from towers where bells had been melted for scrap a season ago; the foundries had cast them again, and the bell-founders' guild had voted, unanimously, to add one small bell to every set that rang only when birds passed overhead. It was a sentimental extravagance. It had been my suggestion. I intended to be insufferable about it for the rest of my natural life.

"You're staring at the ledger," Emeric said, sitting beside me. "Again."

"I'm reviewing the quarterly projections."

"You're staring at the ledger the way you stare at things you're worried about."

"How do I stare at things I'm worried about?"

"Like they owe you money."

I closed the book. Below, the evening market was packing up, traders from both realms haggling cheerfully over prices that now included such line items as *storm-permission*, *river-right-of-way*, and, in one memorable contract I had personally drafted, *one (1) apology, oak tree to carpenter, verbal, sincere*. The oak had insisted on the sincerity clause. The carpenter had wept. Everyone had gotten what they needed, which was, I had learned, the entire definition of a working covenant.

"Emeric," I said, because he was quiet in the particular way he got quiet when he'd rehearsed something, "whatever you've practiced saying, say it before Quillfeather gets up here, or we'll never hear the end of it."

"He already knows."

"He always knows. That's not the point. The point is making him *say* it costs him dignity."

Something loosened in his shoulders — the laugh he swallowed, or nearly. Then he turned and looked at me, and the rehearsed speech apparently died in his throat, because what came out instead was plain and unarmored, which from Emeric was worth more than eloquence.

"Stay," he said. "Here. With me. Not — not because the work needs you, though it does, and not because you're the only person alive who can make both a fox and a stone-giant sign the same contract, though you are. Because—" He exhaled. "Because I spent ten years believing duty meant standing between people and the wild. And then I met you, and you taught me it means standing *with*. Both sides. All sides. And I don't want to learn what standing looks like without you in it."

The wind off the fens carried marsh-smell and distant singing. Somewhere below, Quillfeather was loudly informing a pigeon of its shortcomings.

"You know I can't hear them anymore," I said. "Any of them. I walk past the aviary and it's just — birds. Noise. Lovely noise, but noise."

"I know."

"And I'll never get it back. That wasn't a loan. I gave it. All of it."

"I know that too." He took my hand. His was scarred from the tree-line, from Voss's army, from ten years of holding lines alone. "You think I'm asking for the woman who spoke to beasts. Mirabel. I'm asking for the woman who read a dying stranger's map and went anyway. Who faced a ravenous god with a *ledger*. Who gave away the only magic she had and then complained about the paperwork." His mouth curved. "That woman doesn't need to hear animals. Animals will cross realms to talk to *her*."

I looked out at the city, at the towers and the new-old bells and the lights coming on in windows where people were leaving them open on purpose now, so the night songs could get in.

When I had hidden my gift, I had told myself concealment was safety. When I had spent it, I had told myself sacrifice was enough. Both times I had been lying to myself a little, because the truth was simpler and scarier: I had never once believed I belonged anywhere. Not among humans, who might discover what I was. Not among beasts, who weren't mine. Not even among the ledgers, which were only ever borrowed shelter.

And here was a man on a rooftop asking me to belong on purpose. In daylight. On the record.

"Yes," I said.

His breath went out of him like he'd been holding it since the solstice.

"Yes, I'll stay," I clarified, because I am, at heart, a bookkeeper, and ambiguity is an audit risk. "Yes, with you. Yes, in the city, in the house with the crooked chimney you pretend you didn't buy last month. Yes."

"The chimney is characterful—"

"The chimney is a fire hazard and we are having it fixed. I've seen the estimate."

He laughed then, properly, and kissed me, and somewhere below a fox's voice rose in theatrical agony — "*finally*, by the Choir's own name, *finally*" — and the bells rang the hour, and the birds went over, and the small extra bell above them rang its small silver note for no reason anyone could hear except everyone.

* * *

We were married at midsummer on the counting house roof, because neither of us saw the sense in renting a hall when the best view in the city was free, and because my mother insisted on officiating, having discovered that recovered anchors of world-spanning covenants outrank most clergy on technicality.

Quillfeather served as witness, best man, and self-appointed master of ceremonies, in that order, complaining throughout.

It was at the reception — on the roof, at dusk, with the small bird-bell ringing — that he made his announcement.

"I have decided," he informed the assembled company of humans, giants' envoys, well-spirits in formal basins, and one extremely smug rat delegation, "to remain a fox."

"We know," said Emeric.

"You *know*? I labored over this decision. There were drafts."

"You announced it to the pigeon population of the capital last Tuesday," I said. "They spread it around."

He flicked his tail — the fox equivalent of a wounded sigh — but his eyes were bright and unhidden, which for Quillfeather was a full confession of happiness. "For the record," he said, "I stood human for one breath at the reforging. One breath, with my true name ringing in my ears and my old voice in my throat. And I looked at what I'd been, and I looked at what I am, and I chose." He paused. "Also being human involves trousers, and I have standards."

"You'll translate for me," I said. Not a question. We had been over it.

"I will *graciously* translate for you, at ruinous rates, forever." He hopped onto the table between us. "Because someone has to tell you what the rats are saying, and frankly, Mirabel, you're lost without me. You walked past three separate conversations of genuine gossip value this morning and noticed *nothing*."

"What were they saying?"

"Trousers," said Quillfeather darkly. "Everyone's talking about Emeric's wedding trousers."

* * *

Late that night, after the guests had gone and my mother had walked home under the trees with a lantern she didn't need, I opened a fresh ledger and wrote on the first page:

*Inventory of Everything Worth Counting.*

*One mother, restored, who presses too lightly on paper and exactly right on hearts.*
*One husband, warden, who asks plainly and means it.*
*One fox, permanent, insufferable, chosen.*
*Twenty-one voices, reforged, singing.*
*One gift, given away entire — and returned daily, secondhand, in translation.*
*One city learning to leave its windows open.*
*One bell that rings for birds.*

I sat with the pen hovering over the page, waiting for the ache — the place where the beast-speech used to live, the hollow I'd expected to carry like a missing tooth. It wasn't there. Or rather, it was there, but it wasn't empty. It hummed. Faintly. Like a room where music has just stopped and the walls haven't quite finished listening.

I wrote one last line:

*Balance owed: nothing. Balance held: everything. Carried forward: always.*

And far below, under the cobbles, under the fens, under the roots of the world, the Wild Choir sang on — and for the first time in my life, I didn't need to understand a single word to know it was singing about us.
