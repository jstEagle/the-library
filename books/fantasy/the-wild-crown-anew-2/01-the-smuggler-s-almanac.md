## 1. The Smuggler's Almanac

There are three rules to guiding contraband through the Thornfell passes, and I had broken exactly none of them in six years of work, which in the smuggling trade is roughly equivalent to sainthood.

Rule one: know the mountain better than the king's men do. Rule two: never carry anything you can't lie about convincingly. Rule three — and this is the one that keeps people alive — never, under any circumstances, use magic where anyone can see it.

I was breaking rule three at the moment everything went wrong, which felt, in hindsight, thematically appropriate.

* * *

The pass above Hollowmere is called the Widow's Stitch because it looks like a scar someone sewed shut badly and then abandoned. It's a day's climb from the low road if you're a soldier with a map, four hours if you're me with no map and a grudge against maps on principle. Maps are how witch-finders find things. My routes live in my feet.

That night I was moving salt, iron nails, and — though only three people alive knew it — forty pounds of fae-glass candle stubs that burned without flame and couldn't be traced by the King's sniffers. The candles were the profitable part. The salt and nails were the honest part, the part that let me look the toll-wardens in the eye when they stopped us, which they did roughly every third trip, because Toll-Warden Brennick had a memory like a ledger and a face like one too: flat, ruled, and full of small numbers.

"You're out late, Villalobos," he'd said at the lower gate, lantern held up to my caravan of six mules and two nervous cousins from the Vetch farmsteads.

"Salt doesn't haul itself," I said. "And neither do you, apparently, since the crown won't pay for a second gate-man."

Brennick grunted, which from him was a sonnet. He waved us through. I tipped my cap and thought, not for the first time, that the free cities ran on exactly this kind of transaction: everyone slightly corrupt, everyone slightly kind, nobody asking questions that would ruin the morning.

My crew that night was Doss, who could sleep standing up and often did mid-sentence; Marta Vetch, nineteen, all elbows and ambition; and old Tam, who'd guided these roads before King Aldric had finished losing his hair and liked to narrate every landmark as though the mountain were a friend of his who owed him money.

"Chimney Rock," Tam said, an hour up. "Still there. Lazy thing."

"The rock," Marta said, "is a rock."

"It used to lean more."

I let them bicker behind me and kept my eyes on the sky. That's the other half of rule one: the mountain tells you what it's going to do, if you watch. Clouds stacking wrong over the eastern peaks. Wind coming down-canyon instead of up, cold and mineral-smelling, like the breath off a cellar door. A pressure behind the ears I'd learned to read before I'd learned my letters.

"Storm by moonrise," I said. "We make the high shelf before then or we bed down in the Cut and lose a day."

"A day's coin," Doss observed.

"A dead mule's worth more than a lost day," I said, "but not much."

We made the shelf with twenty minutes to spare, which I counted as a personal victory over the weather, a war I had been fighting and mostly winning since I was twelve years old. The rain came down in ropes. We huddled the mules under the leaning lip of granite that Tam insisted on calling the Almanac, because its strata looked like pages, because old smugglers name everything after themselves eventually.

And that's when the courier came out of the storm.

* * *

You have to understand what's wrong with that sentence.

Nothing comes *up* the Widow's Stitch at night in a storm. Not soldiers — they know better. Not animals — the goats had sense enough to be miserable somewhere lower. And certainly not a man running, because running on wet scree is just falling with extra steps.

But a man came, anyway. He came up the switchbacks below us at a pace that told me he wasn't running *toward* something but *away* from it, and he had a lantern shuttered to a slit, which meant he knew enough tradecraft to be trouble, and he was favoring his left side, which meant somebody had already caught him once.

"Marta." I kept my voice low. "Kill the fire down to coals. Doss, get the mules tight against the wall. Tam—"

"Already awake," Tam said. He was, in fact, asleep sitting upright, which was his way of being ready.

The man reached our ledge and collapsed against the Almanac's stone face, and the little light from our banked coals found his face, and I knew him.

Not well. His name was Pell, and he ran letters and small parcels between Hollowmere and the veil-towns on the far side — the quiet trade, the paper trade, the kind of cargo nobody shoots anybody over. I'd shared a table with him twice at the Sable Ewe in Hollowmere. He'd cheated me at cards both times, badly, and I'd let him, because a man who cheats that poorly wants to be caught, wants to be known as a cheat rather than as whatever he actually is.

Whatever he actually was, it was currently bleeding.

"Pell." I knelt. His left side was soaked dark from ribs to hip, and the smell of the wound cut through even the rain-washed air. Not a knife wound. Something cauterized at the edges, seared shut and then torn open again. Witch-fire leaves a mark like that. I'd seen it once before, on a woman the finders dragged out of Corvane's tannery district, and I had spent the following decade working very hard to forget it.

"Villalobos." His teeth were chattering. "Knew you'd — knew you'd take this pass. Everyone says. Everyone says if you want a thing moved right—"

"What happened to you?"

"Finders." He laughed, and it cost him; I heard the wet rattle underneath it. "Down on the low road. Burning the ferry villages. Said there was a — a vessel. Said the prophecy named a date." His hand shot out and grabbed my wrist with a strength that surprised us both. "Named *tonight*, Villalobos."

The word *prophecy* does strange things in the mountains. Half the free cities think the old texts are fairy nonsense from the veil-side, stories the fae told to keep humans polite. The other half think the prophecies are why the covenant's failing, why the orchards near the veil-line come up bitter, why the rivers run gray some mornings. Both halves are probably wrong. Both halves would burn you for disagreeing.

"I don't traffic in prophecy," I said. "I traffic in salt."

"You traffic in whatever's in this bag." He let go of my wrist and fumbled at his coat, dragging out a package wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord, and sealed — I saw it clearly even in the coal-light — with wax stamped by a signet I recognized.

Everyone in the Thornfell trade recognizes that signet. You don't learn whose it is. You just learn to fear it, the way you fear weather. It belonged to Master Odile Ferrant, hedge-witch of the upper valleys, herb-seller, letter-writer, the woman who had taken in an orphan child twenty-odd years ago and taught her sums and stars and how to hide what she was so well that sometimes the child managed to hide it from herself.

My mentor. Dead three years now, or so I'd believed, which is a different thing from knowing.

"Pell," I said slowly, "where did you get this?"

"She sent it. Before she—" He coughed. "Before. Said hold it. Said give it to the Villalobos child when the almanac turned, when the storm came up the Stitch on the night the stars stood wrong. I asked her what stars stand wrong, and she said *you'll know*, and I didn't, and then tonight I looked up and I did, and I've been climbing ever since."

I looked up. The clouds had torn open along the eastern ridge, and through the gap I could see the sky, and the stars were — fine. The stars were fine. They hung where stars hang, except that three of them, in a triangle over the peak Tam called the Old Woman, were brighter than they had any business being, bright as nail-heads, arranged with the tidy malice of something placed rather than born.

I am not a superstitious person. This is a professional necessity. But my hands went cold, and it wasn't the storm.

"Take it," Pell said, pushing the package into my arms. It was heavier than a book had any right to be, dense as a paving stone. "She said — she said you wouldn't thank her. She said tell you that. She said, *tell Yara they won't thank me, and tell them to open it anyway.*"

Nobody outside Odile's kitchen had called me Yara since I was fifteen years old. To the trade I was Villalobos, the guide, the quiet one with the good routes. Hearing my given name in a dying stranger's mouth felt like someone reading my diary aloud in a market square.

"How do you know my—"

"Witch-finders!" Marta's voice cracked down from the rocks above, sharp with fear. "Lantern light, low on the switchbacks! Three — no, four!"

Pell's eyes cleared for one moment, one terrible lucid moment, and he gripped my sleeve. "They're not after me anymore. They're after what I carried. Get it over the pass. Don't read it by firelight. Don't read it alone if you can help it, and if you can't—" He smiled, and it was a horrible smile, gallows-brave and young. "If you can't, then read it angry. She always said you read best angry."

Then the light found us.

* * *

Here is what I will say about the next five minutes, and I will say it plainly, because everything after depends on it: I did what any competent guide would do, and then I did one thing no competent guide would do, and the second thing is the reason I'm telling you this story at all.

The competent part: we killed the coals completely, got the mules into the deep notch behind the Almanac's shoulder, and went still. Four witch-finders don't climb the Widow's Stitch for salt runners. They climb it when they're tracking something specific, and their tracking isn't dogs and lanterns — it's worse. They carry caged moths, veiled ones, gray-winged things bred in Aldric's own nurseries that fly toward magic the way other moths fly toward flame.

I felt the cage before I heard them. A wrongness in the air, a tugging behind my sternum, faint as a fishhook set in cloth. Somewhere below, a moth was smelling me.

"Villalobos." Doss's whisper was very calm, the calm of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. "Is there something on this mountain they'd want? Because if there's something on this mountain they'd want, I'd like to know if it's mine."

"Not yours," I said.

The lantern light climbed. Voices drifted up — the flat, liturgical cadence of finder-blessings, the words they mutter over their cages. Closer. Closer. The hook behind my sternum pulled tighter, and I felt my own magic stir in answer, waking like a cat that hears its name, warm and eager and utterly forbidden, rising toward the surface of my skin where those gray moths would find it and scream about it.

And Pell — Pell, dying ten feet away in the wet — groaned.

It was small. It was human. It was the most incriminating sound in the world.

The lantern stopped. A voice below us, amplified by the stone: "Something up there. Light the cage."

They were going to open the moth-cage. When they did, it wouldn't just find me — it would find Pell's wound, witch-fire-touched, saturated with the finders' own craft gone sour, and it would find the book in my arms, which radiated something old and patient and *loud*, like a bell that had been ringing for a century in a room I'd only just entered. Three signals, stacked on one ledge. We were dead. All of us, and the mules, and probably Marta's cousins' farm downstream, because finders don't leave witnesses, they leave ash.

So I broke rule three.

I put my palm flat against the wet granite of the Almanac, and I reached into the mountain the way Odile taught me — gently, apologetically, the way you borrow a cup of sugar from a neighbor you intend to repay — and I asked the stone to remember it was a mountain. All of it at once. Every stratum, every page of Tam's beloved book, every ton of indifferent granite from the summit down to the roots.

Mountains are not magical. That's what makes them useful. A moth hunting a candle-flame cannot see the sun rise.

Power moved through me like a flood through a dry channel — my little hedge-magic, the sparks and wards and weather-sense I'd spent a lifetime keeping smaller than a candleflame, suddenly asked to be an ocean for eleven seconds. The whole ledge hummed. Rain fell upward for the length of a heartbeat. And every enchantment within a mile — the moth-cage, the finders' blessing-wards, the sick aura around Pell's wound, the deep slow bell-tone of the book itself — drowned in it, blanketed, erased, a lamp buried under noon.

Below us, confused shouting. The lantern swung wildly. "Lost it — the trail's gone — the whole mountain's *gone*, it's like the cage is blind—"

"Fall back to the low road," another voice snarled. "Send to the garrison. Whatever crossed here, it crossed masked."

Their lights retreated down the switchbacks, slower now, angrier. I sagged against the stone and discovered that my nose was bleeding and my legs had opinions, none favorable. Eleven seconds. It had cost me eleven seconds of work and it felt like I'd donated a pint of blood to a leech the size of a cathedral.

"Yara." Marta crouched beside me, and in the darkness her face was very pale and very careful. "What was that?"

"Rockfall," I said. "Up-valley. Echoes strangely here. Ask Tam."

"It's true," Tam said loyally, from wherever he was. "Strange echoes. Famous for them."

Marta looked at me for a long moment. Nineteen years old, all elbows and ambition, and sharp enough to know when a debt was being quietly created. "Right," she said finally. "Strange echoes." She went back to the mules, and I understood that she would keep the silence, and that she would also remember it, and that someday one of us would call in what the other owed. That's the trade. That's always been the trade.

Doss found Pell. There wasn't anything to be done; there hadn't been since the low road. He died quietly while the rain closed back over the mountain, and his last act was to turn his head toward me and mouth one word. I'm fairly sure it was *angry*. It might have been *sorry*. With Odile's business, the two were usually synonymous.

* * *

We buried him at dawn above the tree line, under a cairn Tam built with more care than he'd ever shown a landmark. Marta said the rites for travelers, the short version, the honest one: *He walked far. He carried heavy. The mountain knows his weight now.* Then we split the load, hid the fae-glass candles in the cache at the Cut for another night, and started down toward Hollowmere with a dead man's parcel strapped under my coat, against my spine, where it pressed between my shoulder blades like a second heart.

Heavy as a paving stone. Warm, faintly, in a way stone shouldn't be. And humming — I'd convinced myself I imagined it until we were two miles down, and then I stopped convincing myself.

Odile Ferrant was dead. I'd stood at her grave. I'd argued with the priest about the words. And yet here was her seal, fresh enough to crack, on a package she'd apparently mailed across three years and one grave, addressed in instructions to a dying stranger: *give it to the Villalobos child when the stars stand wrong.*

I wasn't her child. I was thirty-one years old, I paid my taxes, I cheated at cards competently, and I had spent my whole adult life making myself too dull to be anyone's destiny.

By the time Hollowmere's chimneys rose out of the valley fog, I had talked myself into a plan of great wisdom: I would not open it. I would take it to the Sable Ewe, sit in my usual corner, drink one cider, and decide with a clear head whether to burn it, bury it, or drop it down the deepest mine in the Thornfells. A book that heavy was probably full of ledgers. Debt records, maybe. Old recipes. Nothing that concerned a salt-runner with sore shoulders and a reputation for strange echoes.

The Sable Ewe was warm and smelled of tallow and spilled ale, and Brava the innkeep had my corner swept and my cider poured before I'd gotten my hood down, because I tip well and complain rarely, which in hospitality is the whole secret of the universe.

I lasted four minutes.

Four minutes of the parcel humming against the bench-back like a hive of very polite bees. Four minutes of Brava asking if I was ill, because I'd gone gray, because my hands wouldn't stop shaking, because angry — I realized, with Pell's last instruction circling back around like a carrion bird — angry was the only thing holding the fear down, and the fear had gotten loose the moment I sat still.

I took the parcel up to my rented room, barred the door, lit no lamps — *don't read it by firelight*, he'd said, fine, the window gave me gray daylight and the last of the storm-light, silver and sourceless — and cut the cord.

The wax seal cracked along Odile's signet, and underneath the oilcloth was a book bound in green leather gone black with age, its title stamped in gold that had faded to a ghost:

*An Almanac of the Thornfell Years.* Someone had added, in a later hand, smaller: *and the Years After.*

I opened it at random, because I am a coward, and cowards rip off bandages quickly.

A page of weather notes. Frost dates. Planting tables for veil-side barley. Utterly mundane — a farmer's almanac, a century old. I almost laughed.

Then the pages began turning on their own, slowly, patiently, like a hand leafing through to find its place, and stopped on a date.

The date was tomorrow.

And beneath the date, in ink that looked fresher than the page it sat on, in a script I knew better than my own — because it was the script Odile had drilled into me at a kitchen table, *loops like this, child, always loops, your hand should be a fence nothing climbs* — was written:

*On this night the vessel crosses the Widow's Stitch in storm. On this night the stars stand wrong over the Old Woman. On this night the key comes home to open what was locked, and the finders smell the lock turning.*

I turned the page with fingers that did not feel like mine.

There was a drawing there. Small, quick, done by someone who knew exactly what they were doing. A figure with a pack and a hood, standing on a mountain ledge. And on the figure's right hand, rendered in three economical lines, a scar across the knuckles — the scar I'd gotten at nine years old, reaching into Odile's woodstove for a pan I'd been told twice not to touch.

The margins of that page, and the next, and the next fifty after, were filled edge to edge with Odile's handwriting. Notes, corrections, pleas, arithmetic. And at the bottom of the first margin, underlined twice, in ink that had dried decades before I was born:

*Forgive me. I had no right, and I did it anyway, and I would do it again. Open to the last page when you are somewhere with a locked door and no witnesses. Burn nothing. Trust the knight — not yet. But soon.*

I sat in the gray light with a century-old book describing my evening in the past tense, and outside, far down in the town, a church bell began to ring — though I would swear, then and after, that it rang one stroke too many, and that the mountains, listening, said nothing at all.
