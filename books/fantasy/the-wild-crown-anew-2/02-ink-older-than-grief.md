## 2. Ink Older Than Grief

The waystation at Hollowmere had a roof, half of a door, and a reputation for being forgotten by everyone except the kind of people who needed forgetting. Which made it perfect.

I got the fire going small — hedge-witch's trick, drawing heat out of the stones instead of burning wood that would smoke and signal my location to every bandit within three miles. Then I sat across from the dead courier's satchel for the better part of an hour, eating hard cheese and pretending I wasn't staring at it.

Here's a thing about me: I don't open other people's packages. Not because I'm honest — honesty is a luxury item in the smuggling trade, right up there with boots that fit — but because opening other people's packages gets you killed. The last man who peeked into a sealed crate on one of my runs found out it contained a sleeping basilisk hatchling. He woke it up. The basilisk did not apologize, and neither did I, because I'd told him twice not to touch it.

But this wasn't a crate. This was a book pressed into my hands by a dying man who'd said my *name*. My real name, which exactly four living people knew, and three of them were smugglers who couldn't spell it.

"You're being ridiculous," I told myself, because talking to yourself is free and therapy isn't. "It's probably a ledger. Probably debts. Probably he was delirious and thought you were his nephew."

The book didn't answer. Books never do until it's too late.

I set down the cheese. Washed my hands in the snowmelt bucket, which was stupid ritual behavior and I knew it, but some part of me had decided this was going to be important and wanted clean hands for it, the way you dress up before a funeral or a confession.

Then I broke the wax seal.

* * *

The first thing I noticed was the smell. Old paper has a smell — dust and vanilla, if the paper was good. This smelled like something else underneath. Cold stone. River water. A sweetness like flowers forced to grow in the dark. My skin prickled from wrist to elbow, and every hair stood up like it had been paid to.

The second thing I noticed was the handwriting. Not the main text — that was cramped, formal, someone else's hand entirely. The margins. The margins were full of notes in a script I'd have known anywhere, even blindfolded, even drunk, even dead.

Master Oren's handwriting.

My mentor had been dead eleven months. Fever, they said, though I'd always privately suspected he'd finally eaten something he shouldn't have out of scientific curiosity. I'd burned him on a hillside outside Corvane with my own hands and kept the ashes in a clay pot for a season because letting go seemed rude. His handwriting was as familiar as my own reflection, and seeing it here — in a book that predated his birth by fifty years, judging by the binding — was like hearing a dead man whistle.

I turned pages with fingers that had stopped feeling like mine.

The main text was a prophecy. Of course it was. It's always a prophecy; nobody seals a cookbook in wax and dies delivering it. It was written in the formal cadence the old covenant-scribes used, all balanced clauses and ominous repetition, the sort of thing designed to sound inevitable:

*When the veil grows thin as winter ice,*
*and the crown lies broken in three parts,*
*a child shall walk between the worlds unmarked —*
*two-blooded, hidden, scarred upon the left hand,*
*born under the dark moon in the year the river froze.*

And then a date.

Not a description. An actual date, written out in the old reckoning, and I did the conversion in my head twice because the first time I must have miscounted somewhere, arithmetic being the least magical thing about me.

Tonight.

It gave tonight's date. A century ago. Written by a hand long turned to dust in a book that had apparently spent those hundred years traveling toward me through channels I couldn't begin to guess at.

"Okay," I said aloud, to the empty waystation, to the fire, to whatever god handles clerical errors in prophecy. "Okay. Coincidence."

Except the next verse went on.

*A vessel chosen ere its choosing,*
*bound at birth by blood and word,*
*key to the covenant, lock and hinge,*
*who bears the wild crown's weight alone*
*and pays the covenant's price in full.*

Bound at birth. Key to the covenant. Price in full.

I read it four times. Each time, the words rearranged themselves slightly, the way words do when your brain refuses them and they refuse back. I noticed distantly that my breathing had gone shallow and deliberate, the way it does when you're trying very hard not to panic in front of witnesses, except there were no witnesses, unless you counted the book.

Then I looked down at my left hand.

There's a scar across my knuckles. Three knuckles' worth, thin and white, from when I was six years old and Master Oren caught me stealing plums off a high branch by climbing a tree that had opinions about children. I fell. The branch helped. I've had the scar ever since, and I'd never once thought about it, because everyone has scars and mine weren't even interesting ones.

*Scarred upon the left hand.*

The vertigo hit me like cold water poured down the back of the neck. Not fear exactly — something older than fear. The floor of the world tilting. Because here's what I had built my entire life on, the whole rickety architecture of it: I was nobody. That was the point. That was the *design*. Master Oren had drilled it into me for twenty years — be small, be dull, be forgettable, keep your magic folded up small as a coin and never, never let anyone see the shine. Hedge-craft only. Tricks. Nothing that would make the witch-finders look twice.

Invisibility as a survival strategy. And it had worked. Twenty years of working.

And now a hundred-year-old book had reached across a century, tapped me on the shoulder, and said: *you. We mean you. We have always meant you. Your whole life of hiding was itself part of the hiding we wrote down before you were born.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, more bark than laugh, bouncing off the waystation walls.

*Hidden.* The verse said hidden. Which meant the hiding wasn't my idea. Which meant Master Oren —

I flipped to the margins, and there he was, filling every blank space in that cramped, slanting hand I knew better than my own heartbeat.

*She bound the child herself,* one note read. *Before dawn, before witnesses. I argued. She said there was no one else. She was right, which is the worst thing about her.*

Another, further in, ink faded to brown: *The girl asks too many questions. Good. Questions will keep her alive longer than obedience.*

*I have moved the family again. Third time. She sleeps soundly in the cart and doesn't know these roads are not the roads she knew. May she never need to know.*

*May she never need to know.* Written over and over, actually, like a prayer worn smooth with repetition, appearing in margins throughout the book whenever the prophecy's verses came close to naming things outright. He'd annotated around the dangerous parts the way you step around ice on a mountain path.

I sat with that for a long time. Long enough that the fire burned down and I fed it another knot without looking away from the page.

He'd known. He'd known since before I could talk, and he'd spent his whole life — and mine — building the lie of my ordinariness, brick by brick. Every lesson in concealment. Every lecture about keeping my head down. Every time he'd moved us in the night and lied to me about why. All of it wasn't caution.

It was containment. I was a locked box, and he was the lock, and he had died still holding the key shut behind his teeth.

And the worst part — the part that curdled in me slow and cold — was that reading his marginalia, I couldn't even find it in me to be purely angry. Because the handwriting shook in places. In the later notes, the ones dated closest to his death, the letters wobbled and doubled like a man writing by candlelight with hands that wouldn't obey. And beside the final verse, the one about the price, he'd written only five words:

*I am sorry. There was no better.*

No explanation of what the price was. No instructions. Just apology, in a dying man's hand, pressed into a book and sent rolling through a century toward a girl who'd trusted him with everything.

"There was no better," I repeated to the fire. "That's it? That's the whole defense? You bound me before I drew breath, hid me my entire life, and died leaving me a book with an apology in it?"

The fire crackled. The wind worried at the half-door. Somewhere out on the mountain, a fox screamed, and I flinched hard enough to knock over the cheese.

I gathered the book up, meaning to throw it in the fire. I want that on the record. I genuinely meant to burn it, seal and prophecy and margin-notes and all, and go back to being nobody, because nobody was safe and somebody was hunted, and I had survived twenty years by understanding that equation absolutely.

Instead I held it over the flames and looked at Master Oren's handwriting one more time, and my arms wouldn't move.

Because there was one more note. Tucked against the spine, where the binding met the page, written smaller than the rest, so small I'd missed it:

*If she is reading this, then the courier found her, which means the date has come, which means I am dust and she is alone, and I have failed at the only thing that mattered. The chapel at Vaelmoor. Start there. Trust no knight in red, but if you meet one in grey, listen. And Yara —*

And then nothing. The sentence just stopped. Either the ink ran out or the strength did, and either way, my name hung there unfinished, the only time in twenty years he'd ever written it down.

Yara.

Not "the child." Not "she." Not "the vessel." Me.

I closed the book. Sat down on the floor, because standing had stopped being a service my legs offered. Pulled my knees up like I was six again and the tree had opinions about me.

Vaelmoor. The drowned chapel. I knew the name — every smuggler knew it, the sunken village at the reservoir's edge where the old faith had gone under with everything else. Half-legend, half-haunting, fully flooded.

Which meant Master Oren, from beyond the grave, had just handed me a destination. And destinations are the opposite of hiding.

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it — faint, absurd, impossible at this altitude and this hour — I heard bells. Far off, muffled, like church bells rung underwater. Vaelmoor's bells, if the stories were true, though stories also said the village was haunted by a tax collector, so I tried not to put much stock in acoustics.

But my left hand had started to ache. Just the scarred knuckles, deep and rhythmic, pulsing in time with the far-off bells like the scar itself was listening.

I looked at it in the firelight. Thin white line, three knuckles' worth, six years old and utterly ordinary.

Except nothing about me was ordinary. Nothing ever had been. I'd been a secret written down a century before my birth, a key cut before anyone asked me what door I wanted opened, and the life I'd mourned losing tonight — the small, dull, invisible life — had never actually existed. I hadn't lost my freedom tonight. I'd learned I'd never had it.

"Fine," I told the book, the fire, the dead man's unfinished sentence. "Vaelmoor. But I'm doing this angry."

The bells rang again, closer now, though that was impossible, and my scar pulsed once, hard, like a knock at a door.

I snuffed the fire, shouldered my pack, and stepped out into the dark — and nearly walked into the point of a sword.
