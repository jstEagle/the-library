## 3. The Knight at the Ford

They took my spurs on a Tuesday, which I have always thought was an insult to Tuesdays.

Not the sword — a knight-captain stripped of rank keeps her sword, apparently, because a sword is property of the Crown and so am I, in the way that a hammer is property of the carpenter and may be used to drive any nail His Majesty pleases. Not the armor either. Armor, it turns out, is also a nail. What they took were the spurs, the silver rank-braid, and the name *Serah Dain, Knight-Captain of the Third Lance* from every roster in Vhalcrest, replacing it with *Serah Dain, Warden-Errant*, which is a title that means nothing except that you still have to follow orders.

I had questioned King Aldric's arithmetic. That was my crime, if you can call it a crime to stand in a war council and ask, politely, with charts, where exactly he intended to feed forty thousand soldiers once the covenant with Thornfell finished failing and the granaries of the borderlands turned to gray rot. I asked it twice. The second time, Chancellor Merek stopped writing things down and looked at me the way a man looks at a horse he's decided is lame.

So: Tuesday. Spurs off. And three weeks later, here I was, riding up the old smuggler roads into the Thornfell mountains with a warrant signed in the king's own hand and a mission that made me want to bathe in cold river water just thinking about it.

*Find the prophesied vessel,* the warrant said. *Detain or destroy.*

Vessel. As though a person were a jug.

My orders came from the witch-finders' chapterhouse, which should tell you everything. The witch-finders are Aldric's answer to a question nobody asked, and they travel in threes, in black-and-scarlet, with censers full of something that smells like burnt hair and a doctrine that fits on a coin: magic is treason, treason is fire. They do not detain. Detention implies the possibility of acquittal, and acquittal would imply the whole apparatus might be wrong about something, and the apparatus has never been wrong about anything, ask anyone, preferably not out loud.

The vessel they wanted was, according to the intelligence brief I'd been given — four pages, mostly redactions, one of which appeared to redact a number — someone young, small-framed, traveling light through the Thornfell passes, possibly in company of smugglers, carrying or *being* some artifact of the old covenant. A century-old prophecy, recently recovered from a burned archive, described them down to their scars. The witch-finders' prior captain had gone up this same road a fortnight ago with six men and come back as ash and rumor.

Which meant either the vessel was dangerous or the mountains were. In my experience those two categories overlap more often than command finds convenient.

I rode alone. Command had offered me men; I declined, citing speed. The truth was I didn't trust myself to watch what my men might be ordered to do, and I trusted the witch-finders even less, which was why I'd left Vhalcrest a day ahead of the chapterhouse's own hunting party and told no one my route. If I found this vessel first, then whatever happened next would happen under my judgment and no one else's. That wasn't mercy. I told myself it wasn't mercy. It was jurisdiction.

The mountains didn't care what I told myself. Thornfell in autumn is a country built by something with more ambition than patience — ridges stacked like folded linen, pines going black at the treeline, and everywhere the smell of coming snow underneath the woodsmoke. The smugglers' roads don't appear on any map because maps get confiscated, but they exist the way water exists, finding its level, and the locals know them the way you know the back of your hand: without looking.

I'd served two postings on the border before my demotion. I knew the ford at Marrow Water.

*

Marrow Water is not deep, but it is fast and cold enough to stop a heart that's already considering stopping, and it runs milky-green over stones the color of old teeth. There's a crossing below the gorge where the river spreads wide and shallow, and there's a shrine on the near bank — a fae waymarker, one of the old covenant-stones, its carved face worn nearly smooth, its little niche empty now where offerings used to sit. Someone had chalked a crude crown symbol on it recently. Someone else had tried to scrub it off and only half succeeded, so the stone wore both marks like a face caught between expressions.

I reached the ford at dusk with my horse blowing hard and my ribs aching from two days of bad trail, and I did what any competent warden-errant does: I got off, checked my position, and waited. Because if you're hunting someone on a road, fords are where roads become honest. Everyone crosses eventually. You can take the high ridge or the low tunnel or the smuggler's cut, but the river runs through all of it, and rivers keep appointments.

The light went from gold to copper to that particular bruised purple that means you've maybe got twenty minutes of seeing left. My horse grazed. I chewed dried apple and rehearsed the arrest in my head — *in the king's name, come quietly, I'd rather not* — and noticed, around the fourth rehearsal, that I kept editing out the part where the witch-finders arrived afterward. Because they would arrive afterward. I was the leash, not the hand. My job was to hold the vessel until the censer-men came up the road, and then step aside and watch, and file a report.

I found I couldn't finish the rehearsal past that point. It kept snagging, like a boot heel in mud.

Here is what I had seen in nineteen years of service, most of it honest: I had seen the covenant work. Not the poetry of it — the plumbing of it. Granaries that didn't rot. Wells that stayed sweet within a day's ride of the veil. Border villages where the fae left milk on doorsteps in exchange for bread, a trade so old neither side remembered starting it, and children who grew up thinking the world simply *worked* that way. Aldric's war council talked about the covenant like a treaty clause, leverage, an inheritance to be seized. The farmers I'd garrisoned among talked about it like weather.

And the weather was turning. Everyone felt it. The gray blight creeping up the valley bottoms. Birds flying wrong. My charts in the war council hadn't been treason; they'd been bookkeeping. But Aldric had looked at my bookkeeping and seen a rival's fingerprints, because a man who takes a crown by force reads every ledger as an accusation.

So when the figure finally came down to the water — small, hooded, leading a loaded pack-mule, moving with the careful economy of someone who knows these stones personally — I stood up out of the rocks with my sword drawn and said the words.

"In the king's name. Don't run."

They ran.

Of course they ran. Nobody in history has ever heard *in the king's name* and thought, *ah, safety.* They dropped the mule's lead and bolted along the bank toward the shallows, hood flying back, and I got my first look at the prophesied vessel, terror of the Crown, scourge of the witch-finders:

A kid. Maybe nineteen, maybe twenty. Sharp-faced, dark-haired, mud to the knees, with the build of someone who hauls contraband over mountains for a living and the expression of someone doing arithmetic very fast — *how many steps to the water, how deep, how fast is the rider.* I'll grant them this: the arithmetic came out right. They hit the shallows at a dead sprint, angling for the far bank where the pines closed in dark, and if I'd been ten seconds slower mounting up they'd have been smoke.

But I've chased deserters across half the Marches, and I know how a ford breaks a runner's stride. I splashed in after them, horse plunging, water to my stirrups, and caught them at mid-river where the current shoves hardest — got a fist in their collar and hauled, and we both went sideways into the freezing churn, and I'll say this for the vessel: they fought like a wet cat and cursed like a dockworker, which under other circumstances I'd have respected.

I got them pinned against a boulder, arm across their chest, sword loose in my other hand mostly for weight. Up close their eyes were brown and furious and terrified, and there was a scar across the knuckles of the hand clawing at my vambrace — a thin white line, old, exactly where the redacted brief said it would be.

"There," I said, breathless. "That wasn't so hard."

"Let go of me, you armored — " a word I won't repeat even in my own head, though I noted it showed initiative — "I haven't done anything!"

"Nobody's ever done anything. It's remarkable. An entire kingdom of innocents." I got a wrist-cord on them — standard issue, I'm not proud of the efficiency — and hauled them up the bank to the shrine-stone, where I sat them down with their back against it and stood over them catching my breath while my horse drank and gave me the reproachful look horses reserve for people who take them into rivers after sundown.

The kid glared up at me. Water dripped off their hood. Their hands were bound in front of them and they were already, I noticed, working the cord with their thumb in a way that suggested practice, so I re-tightened it without comment and they had the grace to look mildly abashed.

"What's your name?" I asked.

Silence. Then: "What's yours?"

"Serah Dain."

"The one they stripped." Something flickered across their face — surprise, then recalculation, then, infuriatingly, a kind of hope. "You're the one who asked about the grain."

Word travels. Even uphill. Even about disgraced women. "I'm the one who asked about the grain," I confirmed.

"They hung your effigy outside the chapterhouse in Corvane. It had a pig's head." A pause. "It was a very good likeness otherwise."

"I wept for days. Name."

Another silence, longer. And here's the thing I've turned over a hundred times since: they almost gave it. I saw the decision move behind their eyes like a fish under ice — *this one asked about the grain, this one's not one of the burners* — and then the fish dove, and they said, "Yara. Just Yara," which I later learned was true and also completely beside the point, because Yara Villalobos had never been *just* anything since the night they were born, a fact their entire life had been arranged to conceal from them.

I didn't know that yet. All I knew was that I'd caught the Crown's quarry, that my orders were to hold for the witch-finders, and that the witch-finders were less than a day behind me.

I sat down on a rock opposite the kid and ate another piece of dried apple and hated my life with a quiet professionalism born of long practice.

*

Dawn came up gray and smelling of frost, and with it came the smoke.

I smelled it before I saw it — woodsmoke, but wrong, too thick, with a chemical sweetness underneath that I knew and wished I didn't. Censer-smoke. Witch-fire doesn't burn like ordinary fire; it burns like a *verdict*, all heat and no comfort, and it carries for miles in a mountain valley.

Yara smelled it at the same moment. I watched them go still against the shrine-stone, nostrils flaring, head tilting like a deer's. "What is that?" they asked, and their voice had changed — smaller.

"Smoke," I said, which was cowardly, and we both knew it.

I climbed the rise above the ford, hand shading my eyes, and looked back down the valley along the road I'd come. Three miles off, maybe four, tucked into a fold of the hills where the river bent, sat a village — a scatter of slate roofs and sheep pens that I recognized dimly from a patrol years ago, Harrowmere or something like it, forty souls, a mill. Or there had been a village there. Now there was a column of black-orange smoke standing straight up in the windless morning air, and beneath it, moving between the buildings, figures in scarlet and black, unhurried, methodical, going door to door with the terrible tidiness of people following a checklist.

Three of them. The chapterhouse party. They'd found the village before they'd found us — or rather, they'd found the village *instead*, because the village had sheltered smugglers, and smugglers carried goods, and goods sometimes carried traces, and traces justified pyres. The doctrine fit on a coin. So did the arithmetic: one village weighed against the certainty of the realm, and the realm always heavier, always, because the realm is the thing holding the scale.

One of the figures raised an arm. Even at four miles I saw the gesture, because I'd seen it before, in the Marches, in the purges after Aldric's coronation, in places whose names I'd made myself forget so I could sleep. The arm came down.

A second column of smoke joined the first. Then the sound arrived — faint, cracked by distance, unmistakable. Screaming. Thin, many-voiced, rising and falling like birdsong, like something you could almost pretend was birdsong if you worked at it.

Behind me, Yara had gotten to their feet, bound hands out for balance, staring down the valley with their whole body leaned forward. "Those are *people*," they said. "Those are — you're the king's. You're *his*. Tell them to stop. Tell them — " They rounded on me, and their face was wet, and furious, and about sixteen different kinds of betrayed by a woman they'd met yesterday. "You knew they were coming. You're waiting for them. That's why you're sitting at a ford in the middle of nowhere with your sword out — you're the *leash*."

I hadn't told them that. I filed away later, in the long account of that week, that they'd deduced it themselves, and that deduction would save all our lives more than once. At the time, all I could do was stand there on the rise with the screaming in my ears and the smoke climbing the sky and my orders folded neat in my breastplate like a knife laid flat against my sternum.

*Detain or destroy. Hold until the chapterhouse arrives.*

The chapterhouse had arrived. Just not at our ford. Just at a village of shepherds who'd done nothing worse than sell wool to men who moved it at night.

"Untie me," Yara said quietly. "Or untie me after you ride down there and make them stop. Either way. I don't care about the order."

"I can't make them stop. Three finders with writs direct from the king — I'm a warden-errant, I'm nobody, I have no rank, no authority, I *questioned the grain arithmetic* — "

"So did they!" Yara's voice cracked upward. "So does everyone! You all just — you write it down and file it and let the burning happen somewhere over the hill, somewhere you can almost call it birdsong, and you stand here holding *me* because a book says I matter more than they do — " They stopped. Breathed. When they spoke again their voice had dropped into something flat and cold that sounded, unsettlingly, older than they were. "Is that duty? Is that what they took your spurs for failing at?"

The worst thing, the thing I still can't set down cleanly even now, is that no part of me argued. Some part of me had been arguing since Tuesday — since before Tuesday, since the Marches, since the first pyre I watched from a distance I'd chosen so I wouldn't see faces — and it had lost every argument, and here on a hilltop above a ford it finally, finally won.

I drew my knife and cut Yara's wrists free.

Then I whistled my horse down off the rise, swung up, and turned him down the valley toward the smoke, and said the only order I gave that day that I have never once regretted:

"Stay behind me. And if I fall off this horse, steal him and run."

"You're insane," Yara said. But they were running for the mule, hauling it around, scrambling up bareback, and keeping pace with me as we thundered down the valley road together, side by side, toward three witch-finders and a burning village — the Crown's prophesied vessel and the Crown's discarded hound, allied by nothing except that we had both, in our different ways, been ordered to stand still and watch.

The nearest finder heard the hoofbeats and turned, censer swinging, and I saw his face change as he recognized my armor, and I heard myself shouting something about the king's law and the sanctity of subjects and other words that were technically true and entirely beside the point, and behind me Yara raised their bound-scarred hands toward the burning chapel at the village's edge and *the flames leaned*, just slightly, like grass under wind, bending away from the houses and toward the river —

And that was the moment I understood, with the clarity that only arrives at a gallop toward disaster, that everything the prophecies said about the person behind me was true, and that I had just committed treason in front of witnesses to keep them alive.

The finder lifted his censer. The fire answered.

I put my heels to my horse and went to meet it.
