## 4. What the Mentor Buried

The knight's name was Serah Dain, and she walked like a woman who had been told all her life that her posture was a weapon. She also walked behind me with a drawn sword for the first hour of our flight, which I felt was a bit ungenerous given that I'd saved us both from the ford by doing the one thing I had sworn never to do in front of witnesses: magic.

"Put it away," I said, not turning around.

"You put *that* away," she said. "Whatever it was. The light out of your hands."

"That was self-defense."

"That was a hedge-witch boiling a river."

I glanced back. "Boiling? It was barely warm."

"It scalded two men through their armor." She said it flatly, but her eyes kept going to my hands as if they might do something without consulting me first. Honestly, fair. They'd surprised me too.

The Thornfell road we were on didn't deserve the word *road*. It was a smuggler's track, the kind I'd been guiding contraband along since I was fourteen — a seam of loose shale and pine root stitched along the mountainside above the Vaelwater gorge. In daylight you could walk it and never know it existed; at night, with cloud cover, even the mountain seemed to forget. That was the point. That was always the point of everything I did: be forgettable. Be small. Be nobody the world bothered to write down.

Which was difficult now, because apparently the world had written me down a century before I was born.

The book rode against my ribs inside my coat, wrapped in the courier's oilcloth. I could feel its weight the way you feel a bruise — not pain exactly, just a constant reminder that something was wrong underneath.

We stopped at midday in a hollow beneath an overhang where a spring seeped out of the rock and froze into a curtain of gray icicles. Serah built no fire. Smart. I ate hard bread and goat cheese and pretended not to watch her clean her sword, and she pretended not to notice me pretending.

"You're going back for the book," she said.

"I'm going to read the margins properly this time. There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed. "One of them ends with you hanged."

"The witch-finders hang hedge-witches on principle. The prophecy part just adds decoration." I pulled the book out anyway, because my hands had decided again, and unwrapped the oilcloth.

*Ink Older Than Grief*, the title page said, though that wasn't the original title — someone had pasted a new flyleaf over the old one, and under certain light I could see the ghost of older lettering bleeding through like a scar through paint. The prophecies inside were set down in a formal hand, careful and cold as a ledger. The margins were another matter entirely. The margins were *Maren*.

My mentor's handwriting. Slanted, impatient, forever running out of room at the edge of the page so that words climbed sideways up the paper like ivy. I had copied letters for that woman for eleven years. I knew her ink habits the way I knew my own heartbeat — she pressed harder when she was afraid, lighter when she lied.

The margins were pressed very hard indeed.

Serah watched me turn pages. To her credit, she didn't reach for the sword. "Read it aloud," she said finally. "If I'm to die for this, I'd like to know what 'this' is."

I found the passage I'd fled from last night — the one with the date, the scar on the knuckles, the phrase *the vessel shall come by the smuggler's roads wearing grief like a coat*. And then, for the first time, I made myself look at what Maren had written around it, in the margin, small enough that I'd needed daylight to see it:

*Borne to Ilva Villalobos on the night of the covenant's renewal. Bound before breath. Forgive me. There was no other way to hide a key except to make it look like a child.*

I read it twice. Three times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

"My mother's name," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, like sound heard through ice. "Ilva Villalobos. This says—" I stopped. Started again. "This says I was bound *before breath*. Before I was born. Someone tied me to this — this covenant, this crown, whatever it is — before I drew my first breath, and then—"

"And then hid you," Serah said quietly.

"And then *hid* me." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too loud against the frozen spring. "Eleven years. Eleven years of sweeping floors and copying letters and learning cantrips small enough to fit in a thimble, and she knew. She *always* knew. Every time I asked why I couldn't learn more, every time she flinched when the fae came up in conversation, every time she checked my knuckles like she was looking for—" I stopped. Looked at my own hand. At the thin white scar across the second and third knuckle, the one I'd gotten at nine falling out of an apple tree, the one a dead scribe had written about a hundred years ago.

"She was checking," I said. "She was checking whether it had started."

Serah said nothing. She was good at that, I was learning. Most people rush to fill silence with comfort, and most comfort is just noise wearing kind clothing. Serah let the silence sit there until I could breathe around it.

"Maren Voss," I said. "You buried me alive and called it love."

* * *

We walked until dusk. I didn't remember deciding to walk; my legs simply reported for duty and went. Serah followed, which I chose not to examine too closely, because examining it meant asking why a disgraced knight-captain of King Aldric was following a runaway apprentice instead of dragging them back in chains, and I suspected neither of us had a good answer yet.

At dusk we sheltered in a waystation — one of the smugglers' caches, a hollowed space behind a waterfall curtain where the Vaelwater fell off a shelf of black stone. The roar covered conversation. The spray kept fires invisible. Old smuggler wisdom: wet walls make safe rooms. I got a peat fire lit anyway, small, shielded, because my hands were shaking and I wanted them to have a job.

The book lay open on my knees. I'd stopped reading it like a prophecy. Somewhere in the afternoon it had become clear that the margins weren't commentary — they were a correspondence. Maren hadn't written *in* the book; she'd written *back* to it, answering questions some other hand had asked a century ago. Page after page, the same exchange, decade after decade, her answers getting shorter and angrier while the original text stayed maddeningly serene.

"What are you reading?" Serah asked. She sat across the fire, armor off, sleeves rolled. Without the plate she looked younger and more tired, which I suppose is true of everyone.

"A fight," I said. "A very slow fight between a dead scribe and my dead teacher. The scribe keeps saying the vessel must be prepared. Maren keeps saying the vessel is a person." I turned a page. "It goes on for forty years. Same argument. Neither of them wins."

"Who wrote the original?"

"A covenant-keeper, by the signature. There were three of them once — human, fae, and one who was both, back when such things were allowed. They made a bargain to keep the veil stable. The bargain needs... maintenance." I touched the page where the old hand described it: *a life freely offered, each hundredth year, to bind the crowns anew.* "That's the maintenance."

Serah was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved on her face. "A life," she repeated.

"One life per century. Freely given, the text insists, three separate times, which makes me think it wasn't always." I turned pages faster now, hunting. "And here's the part that's making me want to put my fist through a century-old book. Listen. *The vessel bound at birth shall carry the key within their blood, and at the appointed season shall be led to the drowned chapel of Vaelmoor, where the first shard sleeps beneath the altar bell.*"

"Drowned chapel?"

"Vaelmoor was a town before the last covenant-price was paid. When the old vessel died, the veil shivered and the valley flooded. The chapel's still down there. Divers used to steal the lead off its roof." I closed the book. Opened it. Closed it again, like that might change the words. "Maren knew. She knew where the shard was, she knew what I was for, and her entire plan — her brilliant, loving, unforgivable plan — was to keep me ignorant and keep moving, year after year, hoping the season would never come."

"Would you rather she'd trained you for it?" Serah asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely asking, the way soldiers ask tactical questions.

"Yes," I said. Then, honestly: "No. Both. I'd rather she'd *asked* me. I'd rather she'd sat me down at fifteen and said, Yara, here is what you are, here is what it costs, choose. Instead she gave me a childhood built on a lie with a trapdoor in the floor, and now the floor's gone and I'm falling, and the worst part—" My voice did something embarrassing. I pushed through it. "The worst part is I can't even hate her cleanly, because I *know* why she did it. I've read forty years of her handwriting. She loved me. She loved me so much she decided my whole life should be a hiding place."

The fire popped. Water roared somewhere behind the wall of it.

"Grief does that," Serah said at last. "It builds houses out of lies and calls them shelters." She looked into the fire, and for a moment her face had a door in it too, something behind it I wasn't invited through. "I questioned Aldric's succession because of a village called Harrowmere. You won't have heard of it. Nobody has. That was rather the point."

I didn't push. We were, after all, two people sitting in a waterfall trading wounds like currency.

Instead I turned back to the book and made myself do the thing I'd been avoiding: follow Maren's final marginal note. It was on the page after the Vaelmoor passage, written smaller than any other entry, the pen pressed so lightly it was nearly embossed rather than inked — written, I realized, by someone who was dying, conserving strength, or writing something they hoped no one would ever find.

*If you are reading this, little spark, then the season found you after all, and I am sorry, and I am proud, and those are the same sentence. Go to Vaelmoor. Take the shard. Do NOT let the king's people take you whole — they will try to use the binding backwards, and what they wake will not be a crown, it will be a mouth. Trust the knight, if she is worth trusting. You'll know. And when the price comes due —*

There it ended. Mid-sentence. The rest of the margin was blank, and the blankness was somehow louder than everything written before it.

"*When the price comes due*," I read aloud, "and then nothing. She died mid-sentence, Serah. Or someone made sure the sentence died."

"Or," Serah said, "she stopped herself. Some warnings you finish and some you don't." She reached over, and — carefully, the way you touch something that might startle — turned the book toward the firelight and pointed at the bottom corner of the final page. Faint, water-stained, nearly gone: a sketch. A chapel bell, cracked down the middle, with three marks beneath it like claw-scratches. A shard shape. A crown shape. And a third mark I didn't recognize — a circle with a line through it, crossed out twice.

"A warning about the third mark?" I asked.

"Or a warning *from* whoever owns the third mark." Serah leaned back. "Witch-finders burn that sign into doors. I've seen it on half the villages in the marchlands. It means *witnessed*. It means Aldric's people have already been where you're going."

I looked at the little scratched circle for a long time. Outside, the Vaelwater thundered down its shelf of black stone, patient as everything in these mountains, patient as a prophecy, patient as a lie told out of love.

Vaelmoor was four days east, down the gorge, through country that had started crawling with witch-finders the moment the courier died in my arms. The first crown-shard slept under a drowned bell at the bottom of a flooded valley, guarded — according to a book older than my grandmother — by "the patience of the deep and the debts of the drowned," which sounded charming and would almost certainly not be.

"So," I said, wrapping the book back in its oilcloth and tying the knot with fingers that had mostly stopped shaking, "how do you feel about diving?"

Serah looked at me across the small defiant fire, and something in her expression shifted — duty chafing loose, curiosity winning a round. "Terrible," she said. "I sink like doctrine."

"Wonderful. Between the two of us, one of us floats and one of us fights. That's a rescue team."

"That's a eulogy waiting for a date."

I stood, doused the fire, and slung the book against my ribs where its weight had already become a fact of my body, like the scar on my knuckles, like the binding in my blood I hadn't agreed to and would — I was beginning to suspect — be renegotiating on my own terms or not at all.

Behind us, far down the gorge, a horn sounded. Once, then again. Witch-finder calls, the long rising notes they used for tracking, and they were coming up the smuggler's roads like smoke.

"They've found the waystation trail," Serah said, already buckling her breastplate.

"Then let's give them a chase," I said, and discovered, to my considerable surprise, that part of me meant it — that under the grief and the anger, something in me that had spent eleven years being small had stood up, stretched, and taken an interest in the direction of the flood.

We ran east, toward the water and the drowned bell and whatever Maren hadn't lived to warn me about.
