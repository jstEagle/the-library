## 5. The Usurper's Arithmetic

I have never once in my life wanted a thing I did not intend to pay for.

That is the difference between me and my brother, if you want to know it. That is the whole of it, really. Corin wanted glory and expected the world to hand it over wrapped in silk because he smiled when he asked. I want order, and I am prepared to bleed for it — mine, mostly, though history has rarely let kings choose whose blood does the bleeding.

The ledgers on my desk tonight are the kingdom's blood, rendered down to columns of ink. Grain receipts from the Vale of Arden. Tallow counts. Iron. Boots — you would be astonished how much of ruling an army is boots. And at the bottom of every column, like rot working up through the floorboards, the same notation in my spymaster's careful hand: *shortfall attributed to covenant decline.*

Covenant decline. As though the land itself were merely a merchant who'd taken ill and might recover with rest and warm wine. Master Vessic writes it that way deliberately. He believes that if he phrases the catastrophe in accounting language, I will treat it as an accounting problem. He is not entirely wrong. Numbers are the only prayer I've ever trusted.

* * *

Let me tell you about the arithmetic of Thornfell, since no one else in this palace will say it aloud.

A century ago — ninety-seven years, if we're precise, and I am always precise — the last free king of the old line sat where I sit now and made a bargain with the fae queen of Thornfell. The mountains keep their magic; we keep our borders; and the land between feeds both realms. The fields grow fat. The rivers run sweet. The veil holds. In exchange, the covenant takes its tithe: one life, freely given, each century. A single death to purchase ten thousand harvests.

My brother was that death.

Corin walked into the covenant-stone circle twenty-two years ago with his chin up and his hair combed, because that is what Corin did even walking into his own sacrifice, and the mountain took him, and the harvests came, and I inherited a throne and a wound that has never once scabbed over.

They told me he went gladly. They told me it was an honor. I was fifteen years old, and everyone who said those words to me looked at the floor while saying them, which is how I learned that honor is something we ask other people's children to feel.

So understand me correctly: I do not hate the fae. Hatred requires passion, and passion is a luxury for men who can afford to lose. What I feel toward Thornfell is closer to what a farmer feels toward weather. It is vast, indifferent, and it kills people I love according to terms written before I was born. I simply intend, unlike every king before me, to do something about it.

* * *

Vessic arrived at the tenth bell, as he always does, smelling faintly of lamp oil and other people's secrets. He is a thin man, my spymaster, the sort of man who could hide behind a broom and still see everything in the room. He set down the night's dispatches and stood there with the particular stillness he uses when he has bad news and wants me to extract it personally, so that later he can say I was fully informed.

"Out with it."

"The witch-finders burned a village in the eastern passes today," he said. "Harrowmere. Six dead. They were hunting the prophesied vessel."

"And?"

"And they missed." He laid a paper on my desk. A sketch — a face, young, sharp-boned, drawn by someone who had seen its owner briefly and under poor light. "An apprentice hedge-witch named Villalobos. Smuggler's guide out of the Thornfell roads. The courier network calls them *the Wren*, apparently, because they go everywhere and weigh nothing."

I studied the sketch. Ordinary. That was what struck me — not beauty, not menace. Just a tired young person with a scar on one knuckle, the kind of face you forget before the door closes behind it. Somewhere beneath that ordinary skin was supposed to live the binding that held two realms together.

"You disapprove of the witch-finders' methods," I said. It wasn't a question. Vessic disapproves of everything except results, and only conditionally of those.

"I disapprove of burning villages we will need to feed us in eighteen months. Harrowmere was a granary village, Your Majesty. Its millstone alone took forty years to quarry." He paused. "And I disapprove more strongly of the premise. May I speak plainly?"

"You always do. It's why I keep you. Everyone else here has learned to nod, and nodding tells me nothing."

"The prophecy cannot be controlled, sire." He said it the way a physician says *it has spread*. "I know what the Grand Inquisitor promises. I've read his reports. He speaks of the vessel as though it were a chest to be opened and its contents inventoried. But the covenant was not built by men, and it will not be unmade by them along lines convenient to a throne. Every attempt to seize the mechanism risks breaking it faster than neglect already is. And it *is* breaking. The Vale's yields are down a sixth over three years. The Thornfell herds miscarry. My people inside the free cities report wells turning bitter near the veil-marches. Whatever the crown-shards hold together, it is coming loose, and shaking the box harder will not slow it."

I let him finish, because I respect Vessic, and because the argument deserved its full length before I cut its throat.

"You've concluded," I said, "that the correct response to a failing covenant is patience."

"I've concluded that it is the least catastrophic response."

"Patience." I rose and went to the window. Below, the capital lay in its grid of lamplight, ten thousand hearths, each one fed by grain grown in soil the covenant keeps generous. Beyond the walls, dark. Beyond the dark, the mountains. "My brother was patient, Vessic. He waited his turn in the succession. He accepted the terms. He walked into that stone circle because a century-old contract said the mountain was owed, and everyone praised him, and the mountain took him anyway. Patience is what we call it when the price falls on someone else's family."

"That is not—"

"It is exactly what it is." I turned back. "You warn me the prophecy cannot be controlled. Good. I believe you. I have never intended to control it. Control is a fantasy for priests and poets. I intend to *own* the pieces of it. There is a difference. A man cannot control a fire, but if he owns the flint, the kindling, and every well within thirty miles, he decides what burns."

He watched me with those grey eyes that miss nothing and approve of less. "Three shards," he said. "One drowned in a chapel at Vaelmoor. One in the fae court, around the captive queen's neck like a leash. And the third—" He stopped.

"On my hand," I said.

I turned the signet ring on my finger as I said it. Cold gold, older than my dynasty's claim, won in the civil war that put me on this throne — or taken, depending on whose account you read, and both accounts are true, which is the honest thing about civil wars. I did not seek the third shard. It sought me. It was fused to that ring when I pried it off a dead pretender's hand, and the moment it touched my skin, it began to whisper.

I have never told anyone what it whispers. Not Vessic. Not the Grand Inquisitor, who believes the shards are holy relics and would found an entire liturgy on the whispering if I let him. What it whispers is simple, and patient, and terrible in the way only simple things are terrible:

*Mine. Mine. Bring me to the others.*

I have worn it eleven years. I have never once obeyed it. But I have listened, because a wise commander learns the enemy's voice, and because — I will admit this here, in the privacy of my own skull, where no biographer can follow — some nights the whisper sounds less like hunger and more like grief. It lost the others too, whatever the others are. We have that in common, the shard and I.

"Your Majesty," Vessic said quietly, "when the Grand Inquisitor's men take this Villalobos, and the ritual is performed by force rather than freely given — do you truly believe the covenant will simply accept the substitution? Freely given was the term. The old texts are clear."

"The old texts were written by people who benefited from the arrangement," I said. "Of course they insist the terms are sacred. Every contract insists on its own sanctity right up until someone strong enough reads the fine print aloud."

"And the fine print says?"

"The fine print says the land dies without a covenant. So we give it a new one. One with a crown at the top of it instead of a knife. One where the tithe is paid in obedience instead of children." I came back to the desk. "The free cities will resist. Let them. Resistance gives me a list of names, and lists of names are the beginning of governance. The fae court is already broken — a captive queen, an exiled daughter, a realm going grey at the edges. When the shards are gathered and the ritual is done, both realms will have one law instead of two, and neither law will require that anyone's brother walk into a stone circle."

Something crossed Vessic's face then — quickly, gone before it finished arriving. I have known the man twenty years and I have catalogued perhaps six expressions on him, all variants of skepticism. This one was new. Pity, possibly. Or recognition. Men who serve power closely enough eventually see themselves in it, and flinch.

"And Corin?" he said.

I did not throw him out. That is worth noting. A younger Aldric would have thrown him out, or worse. Instead I sat down and folded my hands and gave him the truth, because I hoard truths the way misers hoard coin and I dislike spending them, but Vessic had earned this one.

"Corin believed the bargain was just," I said. "That is the tragedy of him. He died content. If I break the machine that killed him, then his death becomes a cost, and costs can be weighed, and weighed honestly, it was too high a price. If I leave the machine standing, then his death remains a *sacrifice* — noble, necessary, eternal. Do you understand? Every year I leave the covenant intact, I agree to the murder of my brother. Every year I work to end it, I tell myself he died for nothing." I looked at the ledger columns, the shortfall, the rot climbing through the numbers. "I have chosen that he died for nothing, Vessic. It is the harder grief, and the only honest one. Now get out. Send word to the Inquisitor that the vessel escapes him again and I will have his badge and his teeth."

* * *

Alone, I did what I do every night. I took off the signet ring and set it on the black marble, and I watched it.

It does not move. It does not glow. To any observer it would be a ring on a table. But the candle flames leaned toward it, all four, like courtiers leaning toward a throne, and in the silence I heard it thinking at me — that dry rustle, that patient *mine*.

"Not yet," I told it.

Eleven years of telling it *not yet*, and it has never argued, because it knows what I know: that I will bring it to the others. That everything I have built — the armies, the spy web, the witch-finders with their badges and their torches, the slow strangling of the free cities' independence — all of it converges on the day the three shards touch. The whisper and the king want the same thing. We differ only on who wears whom afterward.

Outside, far to the east beyond the lamplight, a runaway apprentice with a scar on one knuckle was reading words written before either of us was born. I did not know their name yet. I knew only that the prophecy had finally produced its piece, that my Inquisitor's hounds had the scent, and that the game was now fully on the board.

I picked up the ring. It warmed against my finger like something grateful.

"Soon," I corrected myself, and blew out the candles, and in the dark the shard whispered *mine*, and I thought: *yes — but whose, old thing? Whose?*

In three days, the witch-finders would corner a smuggler's guide at a river ford called Greywater, and a disgraced knight would stand between them, and neither the knight nor the apprentice nor the Inquisitor nor I had any idea yet how the board was about to tilt.

I almost envy them that. Almost.
