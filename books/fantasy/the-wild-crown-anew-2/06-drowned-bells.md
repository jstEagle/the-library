## 6. Drowned Bells

The chapel of Vaelmoor had been drowning for two hundred years, and in my professional opinion it was taking its time about it.

We came down to the lake at dusk, when the water turned the color of old pewter and the mountains behind us went blue-black, their peaks still holding the day's last light like a secret they weren't ready to give up. The bell tower rose out of the middle of the lake — just the top third of it, a stone finger pointing at a sky that couldn't hear what it said. Once, I supposed, the bells had rung across the valley every hour. Now the only thing they rang was drowned.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Serah said.

She stood at the water's edge with her boots off and her sword still on, which told me everything I needed to know about how she felt about swimming. She'd rolled her breeches to the knee with the grim precision of someone preparing for surgery. Somewhere on the ride down from the ridge she'd stopped being my captor and started being something harder to name — a person who kept handing me her own rations without being asked, who'd taken first watch three nights running, who hadn't once mentioned the word *orders* since the ford.

"Because the book says so," I said, patting the satchel where the almanac rode against my hip. "And because the margins say so. And because my dead mentor's handwriting says so. Three sources is practically scholarship."

"Your dead mentor also bound you to a covenant before you could walk."

"Yes, well. Nobody's perfect." I crouched and tested the water with two fingers. Cold enough to make my teeth ache in advance. "He did hide me for twenty years, though. That's got to count for something. Half of something."

Serah didn't laugh. She'd been not-laughing more often lately, which I was beginning to suspect was her version of laughing. "Yara." My name in her mouth still sounded strange — deliberate, like she was setting each syllable down carefully so it wouldn't spill. "What's down there?"

I looked at the bell tower. Under the waterline, somewhere below all that gray-green murk, was a chapel roof, and under the roof an altar, and under or near or around the altar — if the almanac's map and my mentor's scrawled marginalia agreed, which for once they did — the first shard of the wild crown. The covenant's keystone. The thing King Aldric wanted, the thing the witch-finders were burning villages to find, the thing that apparently had been waiting for me my entire life without the common decency to send a letter ahead.

"I don't know," I admitted. "A piece of rock that decides who lives and dies every hundred years. Something like that."

"And you're going to swim down and pick it up."

"That's the general shape of the plan, yes."

"You have a truly alarming relationship with plans."

"Thank you. I've worked hard on it."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then her eyes went past my shoulder to the ridge we'd crossed an hour ago, and the smile died wherever it lived.

Smoke. Thin, far off, but smoke — and beneath it, faint as a memory, that particular wrongness in the air that I'd learned to feel through my skin before I ever learned its name. Iron and ash and something sweeter underneath, like flowers rotting fast. Witch-fire.

"They're close," she said.

"Then let's be quick about it."

* * *

The water closed over my head and the world became a green cathedral.

I'd swum smuggler's routes before — cold rivers, black tarns, one memorable canal full of things I preferred not to think about — but nothing like this. Vaelmoor's lake wasn't just deep; it was *occupied*. The drowned village spread out beneath me in stages, roofs first, then upper windows, then doorways standing open onto nothing, doors long since rotted off their hinges. Fish moved through the streets like they owned them, because they did. And rising from the center, growing more solid as I descended, the chapel: slate roof furred with weed, walls furred thicker, and the bell tower reaching up behind me like a spine.

The bells themselves hung maybe ten feet above my head, still in their frame, green with centuries. As I kicked downward past them, my passage stirred some slow current in the tower, and one of them — the smallest, cracked clean down its middle — shifted on its rope and gave a sound.

Not a ring. Not really. More of a groan, a bronze complaint, muffled by all that water into something felt rather than heard. It went through my chest like a hand pressing flat against my sternum.

*I'm here,* it seemed to say. *I've always been here. Why has no one come?*

"Sorry," I said into my breathing air, bubbles streaming up past my ears. Talking to bells now. Excellent. Next I'd be apologizing to furniture.

The chapel doors stood open — of course they did, the water had been coming in for two hundred years, there was nothing left to keep out — and I swam through the gap where the doors had been into the nave. Pews in rows, soft-edged with silt. A stained-glass window at the far end depicting some saint whose face had been eaten by time and algae both, so that all that remained was a figure in blue and gold with no expression at all, arms raised in blessing or surrender, impossible to tell.

And there, at the altar, a light.

It wasn't torchlight. It wasn't witch-fire. It was thin and silver and patient, leaking up through the altar stone itself like dawn through a shutter, and when I got closer I saw the source: a seam in the rock, hairline-fine, glowing. Below the altar. Below the floor. The shard, buried in the chapel's foundations the way a seed is buried in soil.

The margins had said *beneath*, and my mentor had underlined it twice, and drawn a little sketch of hands, and written something I hadn't been able to read until this exact moment, when the glow lit the words I carried in a wax-sealed pouch against my ribs:

*It will want to know you. Let it.*

Great. Wonderful. I love an artifact with opinions.

I pulled the iron pry-bar from my belt — smuggler's tool, church-desecrator's tool, apparently prophet's tool — wedged it into the seam, and heaved.

The seam widened. Silver light spilled out and up, and the water around me went warm — actually warm, impossibly warm, like swimming into a held breath — and the light found my face, my hands, the scar across my knuckles that a century-old book had described with unsettling accuracy, and the light *recognized* me.

I don't know how else to put it. Recognition isn't seeing; it's being seen. Every part of me that I'd spent twenty years hiding — the hedge-magic, the blood-binding, the whole buried architecture of who I was — lit up under that gaze like a house with every window suddenly uncovered. And underneath the recognition, something older and vaster stirred, and I felt it begin to rise toward me through the chapel floor, through two hundred years of drowned quiet, and it wanted —

It wanted to know me.

*Let it,* said the dead man's handwriting, and I was reaching down into the widening seam with both hands when the world above me went orange.

I saw it first as a reflection — the surface of the lake, thirty feet up, flickering with firelight that didn't belong to any sunset. Then shapes on the shore. Lanterns in a line. The particular disciplined geometry of people who burn things for a living.

Witch-finders. At least eight. Maybe more coming.

And on the shore, sword drawn, standing between them and the water — Serah.

* * *

Serah Dain had broken orders exactly twice in her life, and both times had involved Yara Villalobos, which she was choosing not to examine too closely.

The first time had been the ford. Standing there with her blade leveled and her captaincy already forfeit, watching the witch-finders' fires eat the village behind her while the prophesied vessel stood waist-deep in the river looking at her with those too-honest eyes. She had lowered the sword. She had not yet decided whether that was treachery or sense.

This, she suspected, would be less ambiguous.

"Last chance," said Witch-Finder Corvane — no relation to the city, though he had the family look of someone who'd never once doubted himself. He was a broad, gray-templed man with kind eyes and hands that had done terrible things with great care. That was the worst of them, Serah had learned. The ones who were gentle about it. "Give us the vessel. You'll be reinstated. Full rank, full honors. Aldric doesn't forget service."

"He forgets inconvenient questions," Serah said.

"He forgets *wrong* questions. There's a difference, and you've had months to learn it." Corvane glanced at the lake, at the bell tower, at the silver glow beginning to stain the water from below like moonrise happening in the wrong direction. His kind eyes narrowed. "It's waking. Do you understand what's down there? What it does to a body that isn't prepared for it?"

"No," Serah said honestly. "But I've watched what your order does to bodies that aren't prepared for *you*, and I find I prefer the mystery."

Corvane sighed, and it was almost fatherly, and that was the most frightening thing about him. "Take her," he said. "Gently, if she allows it."

Four men started forward, and Serah did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for weeks. Four trained witch-finders, armored, fresh. One disgraced knight, exhausted, out of position, protecting a lake instead of holding ground. No cavalry. No reinforcements. No plan.

She'd fought worse odds. She'd fought worse odds *recently*. But she'd never before fought them knowing that the person depending on her was thirty feet underwater and couldn't hear her shout.

So she shouted anyway. Foolish. Human. "YARA! COMPANY!"

The glow under the lake pulsed, once, like a heartbeat answering.

Then the first witch-finder reached her, and there was no more time for anything but work.

She'd trained in the Temple yards at Corvane under masters who believed in economy of motion, and even stripped of rank and half-starved on smuggler's roads, her body remembered everything. Parry, pivot, pommel-strike. The second man came high and she took his legs out from under him; the third was smarter and waited, circling, making her turn, keeping her back to the water where she couldn't see the fourth.

That was the mistake. Theirs, not hers — because while she couldn't see the fourth man, he made the error of glancing at the lake, at the light rising in it, and whatever he saw there made him hesitate, and hesitation against Serah Dain was a luxury purchased at interest.

She put him down hard and turned to find Corvane watching her with his head tilted, kind eyes gone thoughtful.

"There it is," he said softly. "There's the knight-captain. You know, I argued for you, once. In the early councils. I said Dain's doubt is the honest kind — she doubts because she looks, and looking is half our work." He drew his own blade, and it was a beautiful thing, temple-forged, and his stance was textbook-perfect, and Serah understood with cold clarity that this was the fight she might not walk away from. "I was right, wasn't I? You looked. That's all you ever did. You *looked*."

"And then I reported what I saw," Serah said. "Which is more than your order can claim."

They came together, steel ringing over the water, and behind them the lake began to shine.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about drowning: it's the recognition that kills you first.

The shard came free of the chapel floor in a rush of silver and warmth, and it rose into my hands, and it was small — smaller than a fist, a jagged splinter of something that wasn't quite stone and wasn't quite glass, and it fit my palm the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. And the moment it touched my skin, the binding woke.

Every thread my mentor had tied into my blood at birth pulled taut at once. I felt the covenant the way you feel a rope you've been harnessed to all your life the moment someone finally puts weight on it: sudden, total, inescapable. A century of held breath moving through me. Voices that weren't voices. The land on both sides of the veil, sick and starving, leaning on me like a drunk leaning on the only wall left standing.

And I sank.

I don't mean I chose to sink. The weight came from everywhere — the water, the shard, the binding, the sheer gravitational pull of a destiny I'd never signed for — and my limbs went heavy and slow and stupid. The pry-bar slipped away into the dark. My air left me in a silver stream. Above me, the surface was very far away and getting farther, a ceiling of trembling firelight, and I thought, with the strange clarity of the nearly dead: *so this is the price, then. This is what the prophecy meant. Not a knife, not an altar. Just this. Water, and silence, and everyone going on without you.*

The bells groaned overhead. All of them at once, this time, the whole drowned carillon shifting on ancient ropes, and the sound rolled down through the water like grief given a voice.

*No,* said something. Not the bells. Deeper than the bells. *Not like this. Not yet. Not unless—*

Hands caught me.

I knew them before I saw them. I'd watched those hands for weeks — breaking camp, sharpening steel, once, memorably, pulling a splinter out of my palm with a gentleness that had rearranged something in my chest that I was still refusing to name. Serah's hands closed on my collar and hauled, and I felt her kick, felt her strength go into it, felt her choose — actively, expensively, against four armed men and her king and every order she'd ever sworn — to be here, underwater, dragging me up.

My vision was going gray at the edges. The shard was still in my fist. I couldn't open my fingers. I didn't try.

The surface broke. Air — sweet, cold, outrageous air — and I coughed half the lake back out and heard, absurdly distant, the ring of steel on the shore, and then Serah's arm locked across my chest and she was swimming, dragging me backward through the water toward the reeds at the lake's edge, away from the fight, and she was shouting something over the water that I was too busy not-dying to parse.

"—told you—" *splash* "—plans—" *splash* "—ALARMING—"

"You—" cough "—heard that?"

"I hear EVERYTHING YOU SAY, Yara, that's the PROBLEM—"

The reeds took us. She hauled me into the shallows and I lay in six inches of freezing water with a shard of the wild crown clenched in my fist and the sky wheeling overhead, and Serah knelt over me, soaked, bleeding from a cut above her eye, breathing like a bellows, glaring at me with an intensity that could have curdled milk at forty paces.

"You went down," she said. "You went down and you didn't come up."

"The shard—"

"I don't CARE about the shard."

"It's the covenant, Serah, it's the whole reason—"

"I said—" and here her voice did something I'd never heard it do, cracked straight down the middle like the little bell in the tower, "—I don't care about the shard. I care that you went down and you didn't come UP."

I looked at her. Water ran off her jaw and dripped onto my collarbone, and her hands were shaking — Serah Dain's hands, steady as a mason's level, shaking — and somewhere in the ringing aftermath of nearly dying I thought, with the calm of a person whose judgment is thoroughly compromised: *oh. Oh no. This is a problem.*

"I came up," I said quietly.

"You came up because I *got* you."

"Yeah." I lifted the hand that wasn't welded around the shard and touched her wrist, just briefly, just to check that she was real. "Thanks for that."

Behind her, on the shore, the fight had gone quiet in the specific way that meant someone had won, and I craned my neck to see which someone.

Corvane lay on his back in the shallows, disarmed, temple-blade ten feet away in the mud, and Serah's boot-print was drying on his breastplate. The other witch-finders were scattered — two unconscious, one limping away into the trees, one sitting in the reeds looking deeply reconsidering his career. And Corvane himself, pinned and beaten, was smiling up at the darkening sky with those kind, terrible eyes.

"Do you know what you've done tonight?" he said conversationally, to the sky, to Serah, to me. "You've shown him where it is. The vessel, the shard, all of it. He'll come now. Not his finders. *Him.*" He turned his head with effort and looked directly at me, and there was no cruelty in it, which was somehow worse. "He lost a brother to the last covenant's price, boy. Do you understand? Everything he does, he does because the crown took someone from him. And you're going to take the crown apart in front of him."

Silence, except the lap of the lake.

Then Serah knocked him cold with the pommel of her sword — gently, because she was who she was — and turned back to me, and her face in the last light was unreadable except for everything it was saying.

"Aldric," she said. "In person."

"In person," I agreed.

I opened my fist. The shard sat in my palm, small and jagged, pulsing with slow silver light — and warm, warm as a living thing, warm as a held breath finally released. Two down. Two to go, counting the one inside a grieving king's signet ring, though I wouldn't learn that for a while yet. All I knew then was that the shard hummed against my skin like it knew my name, and the mountains to the east had gone dark, and somewhere out in that darkness a usurper was about to stop sending other people to do his hunting.

Serah hauled me to my feet. Her hand stayed on my arm a moment longer than balance required.

"We move tonight," she said. "Before he comes."

"Through the veil," I said. "Nyssara next. If we can find her."

"If," said Serah, and shouldered her pack, and for the first time since I'd met her at the ford, she smiled all the way. It was brief and tired and slightly mad, and it was aimed at me, and I felt it land somewhere beneath the binding, beneath the covenant, in the place that was still, despite everything, entirely mine.

The lake behind us groaned once more — all the drowned bells together, a long bronze sigh, like something letting go after two hundred years.

Then we climbed into the dark, carrying the first piece of a crown that had cost us the surface of the world, and behind us, far off along the ridgeline, watchfires began to bloom — one, then three, then a dozen — moving toward Vaelmoor like a constellation deciding to descend.

Aldric was coming. We had perhaps a night's lead, and a shard that whispered, and each other.

For a fugitive, an exile, and a prophecy neither of us had agreed to, I supposed it would do.
