## 7. The Queen Who Waited

Crossing the veil, I learned, feels like being read aloud.

That was Serah's description, anyway, offered in her flat knight's cadence as we stood at the threshold — a birch tree older than any kingdom, split down the middle by lightning a century ago and never allowed to die. She'd crossed once before, on some diplomatic errand back when she still had a rank and a king worth serving. I had crossed never. I'd grown up two valleys east of Thornfell and been told my whole life that the veil was a story smugglers used to explain why their cargo sometimes arrived humming.

"It's like standing in a room where someone is reciting your worst decisions," she said, "in your own voice. Slowly."

"Wonderful. Anything else?"

"You might cry. Most people do. Don't be ashamed of it."

"I'm not going to cry."

I cried. Not at the worst decisions — those came fast, a blur of every lie I'd told the caravan masters, every coin I'd shorted, every night I'd let hedge-magic bleed out of my fingers when I thought no one watched. No, what undid me was the moment the reading stopped. The veil finished with me, went quiet, and left me standing in a forest where the light came from everywhere and nowhere and the trees had opinions about my posture.

The silence afterward felt like being set down. Like something enormous had held me up to its face, examined me, and said: *ah, this one.* And then put me back.

Serah handed me a strip of dried venison without comment. She had cried too, I noticed. Neither of us mentioned it. That was becoming the foundation of our entire arrangement: an unspoken treaty of mutual non-mention, ratified somewhere between the ford and the drowned chapel of Vaelmoor, and holding up better than most treaties I could name.

"Which way?" she asked.

I turned in a slow circle. The forest did not have paths so much as permissions. Every direction looked identical — silver-trunked trees, moss like spilled candlewax, a hush so complete I could hear my own blood conducting business. But there was a pull. Low in the sternum, right where the first shard sat wrapped against my ribs in oilcloth and prayer, something tugged like a fish on a line.

"That way," I said, pointing. "The shard wants to go home, apparently. It's dragging me along like luggage."

"Convenient."

"Suspicious," I agreed. "Everything about this book, these shards, this whole prophecy — suspicious. But it's the only map we've got, and witch-finders are burning villages behind us, so we walk toward the tug and hope the tug isn't lying."

We walked.

* * *

The fae realm of Thornfell was dying. You could see it in the way the light fell — or rather, didn't. Where the covenant still breathed, the forest glowed from within, every leaf lit like a lantern. Where it failed, the light simply stopped a few feet short, leaving pools of gray that swallowed sound and warmth. We passed through a grove where half the trees were glass. Actual glass, transparent and ringing faintly when the wind touched them, as if the wood had forgotten how to be wood and settled for the next best thing.

"The poisoning," Serah murmured. "It's worse than the reports said."

"What reports? Aldric doesn't care about this side of the veil."

"His quartermasters care. Half his grain comes through the veil-tithes. If Thornfell fails, the free cities starve by winter, and he knows it." Her jaw worked. "It's why he wants the shards. He thinks he can hold both realms hostage with them."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I sat in his war councils for six years before I made the mistake of having a conscience." She said it lightly. It wasn't light. "Yara. When we find whoever holds the second shard. What exactly are you planning to say to them?"

"I hadn't gotten past *please*."

"We should work on that."

But the forest answered the question before I could. The gray pools thickened ahead of us into a true dead zone — a clearing where nothing grew, where the ground was pale as old bone — and in the middle of it stood a pavilion. Not fae-made; I understood that immediately. It was canvas and iron, military issue, King Aldric's colors hanging limp off the corner pole. A tent from the human world, pitched here like an insult, like a flag planted in a wound.

And beside it, waiting, was a woman who was very clearly not human and very clearly not surprised to see us.

She was tall the way winter is tall — all vertical lines and patience. Her hair was white as the glass trees, her skin the color of birch bark, and her eyes were the exact amber of the light in the living parts of the forest. She wore traveling clothes that had once been magnificent and were now patched with human cloth, and around her neck hung a chain from which dangled a signet ring. I knew that ring. I'd seen it pressed into wax seals on the courier's book, on warrants, on the edicts nailed to Corvane's gates.

Aldric's seal. On a fae woman's neck. Worn the way you'd wear a leash.

"Serah Dain," the woman said. Her voice was low and musical, but frayed at the edges, like a song sung too long. "Stripped of rank for asking inconvenient questions. And Yara Villalobos, the living key, walking around loose with a piece of a crown in their shirt." Her gaze dropped to my chest as if she could see through the oilcloth. "My mother waited a hundred years for you. She's still waiting. She just has less choice about it now."

I looked at Serah. Serah looked at me. Her hand had found her sword hilt, which was, given our location, roughly as useful as bringing a knife to a flood.

"You know who we are," I said.

"I've known about you since before you were born. Everyone in Thornfell who mattered knew. Your mentor made sure of that — she wanted witnesses, in case things went wrong." Something moved across the woman's face that might have been grief or its fossilized remains. "They went wrong. My name is Nyssara. My mother is Queen Vessandra of the Seelie Court, keeper of the second crown-shard, and for the last three years, a guest of your king. In the sense that a bird is a guest of a cage."

* * *

She fed us, which I resented and then appreciated and then resented again. Bread and honey and something like tea that tasted the way the living forest smelled. We sat inside the pavilion — Aldric's pavilion, because Nyssara had stolen it, which told me a great deal about her before she said another word — while she explained, in the tone of someone recounting a funeral they'd attended alone.

"My mother went to treat with Aldric three winters ago. A parley. The covenant was failing; she wanted to negotiate a new tithe, buy time." Nyssara turned the signet ring on its chain, around and around. "He took her. Not killed her — he's not stupid. He keeps her at court, comfortable, honored, displayed. And because she is bound to the crown-shard she carries, and the shard answers her will, and her will is bent under his—" she lifted the chain slightly, "—he effectively holds the second shard of the Wild Crown. He wears its key on a leash and calls it diplomacy."

"He wears *that*?" Serah nodded at the ring. "His signet? I saw him seal orders with it a hundred times. It never seemed—"

"It whispers," Nyssara said. "You'd have noticed if you'd sat close enough and were the sort of person it speaks to. Ambitious people hear it as encouragement. It's very good at flattery. That's how crowns get people killed."

I wrapped my hands around the tea bowl and tried to make my thoughts line up. They kept refusing. "So Aldric has one shard already. Effectively. We have one. Which means—"

"There's a third," Nyssara said. "Yes. And no one knows where it is, except possibly the prophecy, and the prophecy, as I understand it, has recently taken a personal interest in you." She smiled. It was not a warm smile, but it was honest, and after everything I'd learned about my own life in the last week, honesty hit harder than warmth. "Here is what I propose."

"Here's what I propose," I said, before I could stop myself. "You tell us why you're camped in the middle of a dead zone wearing your enemy's seal, instead of, say, rescuing your mother."

The smile flickered. "Because rescuing her requires the shards. Because the shards require the living key. Because I have spent three years watching my mother's court rot from the edges inward, watching the glass take the trees, watching children born in the gray come out *quiet*, Yara — born without voices, without reflection. The covenant is dying and taking both realms with it, and the man who could stop it would rather own the corpse." She set down her cup. Her hands were steady; her voice almost wasn't. "I am camped in a dead zone because this is where the veil is thinnest, and I have been waiting eleven days for the tug of the first shard to cross my mother's border, because I promised myself that if the key ever actually came, I would be the first thing it met. So that no one else gets to decide what happens to it."

Silence. The glass trees rang somewhere far off, faint and mournful, like a bell tower underwater.

"All right," I said slowly. "Say I believe you. Say we're allies. What do you want?"

"A promise." She leaned forward. Up close, in the lamplight, I could see the age on her — not wrinkles, nothing so mortal, but depth, like rings in a tree. "When the crown is reforged — if it is reforged — the new covenant must not be the old covenant. No more tithes of lives. No more keys bred in secret and bound at birth without their knowledge or consent." Her amber eyes fixed on mine. "No more Yaras. Swear it. Swear that whatever bargain remakes the veil, it will be made openly, by choice, by all three peoples — human, seelie, unseelie — or not made at all. Swear it, and I will get you to my mother. Swear it, and I will spend whatever's left of me getting that ring off her neck."

"And if I don't swear?"

"Then I'll still help you. Because the alternative is everyone dies." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "But I'll help you hating what we build. And crowns built by people who hate them have a way of falling on the grandchildren."

I looked at Serah. Serah was watching me with an expression I was beginning to catalog — the one that meant *this is your call, vessel, I'm just the sword* — but underneath it, something else. A warning, maybe. Or a question: *do you trust yourself to promise this?*

Here is what I knew about myself at that moment, sitting in a stolen king's tent in a dying world, holding tea I hadn't earned: I had been bound to a covenant before I drew breath, by the one person I had loved without reservation, without my knowledge or consent. Every disaster of my life traced back to a promise made on my behalf by someone who decided they knew better. And here was Nyssara, kind and desperate and entirely reasonable, asking me to promise — sincerely, bindingly — that no one would ever do to anyone else what had been done to me.

It was the easiest oath in the world.

And I couldn't say it.

Not because I didn't mean it. Because I had opened a century-old book and read my own face in it, and learned that the last people who made promises about the covenant — wise people, loving people, my mentor above all — had built a machine that required a death every hundred years and called it peace. I had learned that promises about great workings have a way of growing teeth between the signing and the keeping. Whatever the reforging demanded — and the margins of that book had gone dark and evasive whenever I neared that page — I did not yet know the price. And I had sworn to myself, in the flooded dark of Vaelmoor with Serah's arm around my chest hauling me toward air, that I would never again agree to terms I hadn't read.

"I swear it," I said, "with one amendment."

Nyssara's eyes narrowed. "Amendments are how kings ruin oaths."

"Or how people avoid ruining each other. Here it is: no more Yaras — yes. Open covenant, freely chosen, all three peoples — yes. I'll swear all of it, gladly, tonight, on whatever you hold sacred." I set down the cup. My hands were shaking, and I let them. "But I swear it *now*, as a promise between us, and I reserve the right to swear it again at the reforging — after I've seen what it costs. If the ritual demands something none of us have read, I want the right to stop. To refuse. Even at the end. Even if refusal breaks everything." I swallowed. "You watched your mother lose her freedom to a bargain she walked into voluntarily. Imagine how much worse it is to be born into one. All I ask is that whatever we build, we build it so that anyone standing where I stand can say no."

For a long moment Nyssara said nothing. Outside, the gray pools crept another inch across the clearing, patient as arithmetic.

"You realize," she said finally, "that what you're describing is not a covenant. It's a conversation that never ends. Every generation, renegotiated. Every century, reopened. Nothing final, nothing sealed, nothing safe."

"Yes."

"That's terrifying. That's the least throne-like thing I've ever heard."

"I've noticed thrones aren't going well for anyone lately."

And then Nyssara laughed — a short, startled, genuine laugh, the first living sound I'd heard in the dead zone — and stuck out her hand, which was a human gesture and clearly cost her something, and I took it, and the oath passed between us skin to skin, and I felt it settle into place somewhere beneath the shard, a second small weight joining the first.

Serah exhaled. I hadn't realized she'd been holding it.

"So," I said, because someone had to. "Your mother. How do we get a captive queen, a hostile court, and a shard out from under Aldric's nose?"

Nyssara's laugh died. She reached up and touched the signet ring at her throat, and for a moment she looked very young, and very tired, and exactly like someone's daughter.

"We walk in the front gate," she said. "Because Aldric has summoned the court. All of it. He's announced a celebration for the solstice — and he's commanded my mother to preside over the crowning of his new heir."

"Heir?" Serah said sharply. "Aldric has no children. He's never—"

"No," Nyssara agreed quietly. "He hasn't. Which is precisely what everyone at court is asking. And the invitation lists arrived yesterday, sealed in black wax." She turned the ring once more, around and around, and the amber light of the dying forest caught it and made it glow like an ember. "One of them was addressed to a runaway apprentice of the late Marguerite Villalobos. By name. With a note."

She drew a folded paper from her coat and slid it across the table, and even before I broke the seal I recognized the handwriting, because I'd spent a week drowning in it — cramped, slanting, ink older than grief, filling the margins of a book that had ruined my life:

*Yara. Come to the solstice. Come to the court. I have waited a hundred years, little key, and I am running out of ways to wait gently.*

It was signed: *Vessandra.*

Nyssara watched my face as I read it. "My mother," she said, "has never written to anyone in your family in her life. Someone taught her that handwriting. Someone wanted you to feel safe coming." Her voice dropped. "So the real question, living key, is not whether you'll attend a celebration thrown by the man who leashed my mother. It's this: who else is waiting for you there — and are they writing to you as an ally, or as bait?"
