## 8. Free Cities, Fenced Hearts

Corvane announced itself the way old soldiers do — not with trumpets but with a smell. Tallow smoke and river mud and the faint sourness of a city that had survived three sieges by being too stubborn to burn cleanly. I'd sworn an oath on its walls once, kneeling in the rain with nine other cadets while the Marshal of the Free Companies read out the words like a grocer reading prices. *To hold. To shield. To ask no crown's permission.* I'd meant it then. I'd been nineteen and stupid, which is nearly the same thing.

Now I rode back through the Southgate with a fugitive hedge-witch under a hood at my side, an exiled fae princess wrapped in a traveling cloak that did nothing whatsoever to disguise her, and a warrant with my own name on it folded in my saddlebag like a bad memory.

"Cheerful place," Yara said.

"It's home," I said.

"I didn't say it wasn't."

Nyssara said nothing at all. She'd been quiet since the ford at Vaelmoor, quieter since her mother's name first came up between us like a knife someone kept setting back down on the table. She looked at Corvane's walls the way you look at a house where a funeral happened — politely, and from a distance.

The gate guards waved us through without a second glance. That was my first mistake of the day: believing it would be easy. Serah Dain, disgraced knight-captain, should have known better than anyone that cities don't look twice until they do, and then they never stop.

* * *

We went first, as one always does in Corvane, to the Council of Nine — or rather to the antechamber of the Council of Nine, because no free city ever made you wait less than half a day for the privilege of begging. The antechamber was a long hall paneled in river-oak, hung with the banners of every company that had ever defended the city, including mine. Mine still hung there. Nobody had taken it down. Somebody had, however, turned it slightly so the embroidered stag faced the wall, which struck me as a very Corvane solution: not removal, just rotation. Denial by interior decorating.

Yara pulled back their hood the moment we were inside, which I should have expected and somehow wasn't ready for.

"What are you doing?" I hissed.

"Hiding got my mentor killed. Hiding got Vaelmoor burned." They flexed the hand with the scar across the knuckles, the one the prophecy had drawn a century before they were born. "I'm done hiding."

"You're done hiding in a room full of council clerks who report to—"

"To whom? Aldric?" Yara smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and I realized I was seeing something new: not the wary smuggler from the mountain roads, not the drowning child I'd hauled out of the chapel flood. Something with edges. "Let them report. Let them write it down. *The prophesied vessel sat in your antechamber eating an apple and nobody stopped them.* Let the witch-finders choke on it."

An apple appeared in their hand from somewhere. Smugglers. Honestly.

Nyssara settled onto a bench with the unhurried grace of someone who had waited out decades in exile and considered a council's antechamber beneath comment. "They will be afraid," she observed, to no one in particular.

"People are always afraid," Yara said.

"Yes. But here it will have a different flavor." Her eyes, silver as thaw-water, moved along the banners. "Your cities built their freedom on one promise: *never again a crown.* And we are bringing them a prophecy about a crown. Watch how fear curdles into arithmetic. It is the most human thing there is."

I wanted to argue. I was, by profession, good at arguing. Instead I watched a junior clerk cross the hall, glance at Yara, glance again, and walk — not run, walk, carefully, the way you move when you don't want to startle anything — toward the far door.

"There goes the arithmetic," I said.

* * *

The Council received us at dusk, all nine of them arranged behind a horseshoe table like judges who'd heard our crime already and were merely deciding the sentence. Speaker Halvard sat in the center — Halvard, who had drunk wine with me after the Border action, who had wept openly at Captain Oren's funeral, whose face now wore the pleasant blankness of a man performing neutrality so hard it left dents.

"Serah Dain." He said my name like a coin he was checking for counterfeit. "You come to this council under a cloud."

"I come to this council under a king," I said. "The cloud is his. He burns villages in Thornfell. His witch-finders took Harrowmere two weeks ago. The road south is littered with refugees who are going to arrive at your gates by winter whether you invite them or not."

"Aldric's quarrel is with the mountain folk and their superstitions," said Lady Mereen, third seat, whose family had made its fortune trading with the fae before the trade soured and whose hatred of the subject was therefore both personal and profitable. "Corvane has treaties."

"Corvane has treaties with everyone," Halvard murmured, "and honor with none, which is why it stands." A ripple of dry appreciation around the horseshoe. "You propose what, exactly, Knight-Captain — forgive me. Former knight-captain."

The title came off his tongue with a small cruelty he probably didn't even notice anymore. That's the thing about institutions: they don't need malice. Malice takes effort. All they need is procedure.

"I propose you hear them out." I stepped aside.

And here is what I will remember, when I'm old — if I'm old, which given recent events is a wager I wouldn't take — here is what I'll remember: the silence. Yara walked forward into that horseshoe of power carrying nothing but a dead mentor's book and a scar, and told the truth. The drowned chapel. The shards. The covenant failing, the land souring, the veil thinning until things were getting through that had no business getting through. Nyssara spoke after, measured and terrible, and when she let her glamour slip — deliberately, I think, a gift of honesty rather than a threat — the gasp that went around the chamber was not awe.

It was fear. Clean, simple, municipal fear. The kind that votes.

"The prophecy speaks of a vessel," Mereen said at last, delicately, as if discussing drainage. "A sacrifice."

"The prophecy can go hang," Yara said. "That's rather the point."

"And yet." Mereen folded her hands. "The last time human and fae bound themselves together, the price was a life every hundred years. You ask us to trust a covenant written in blood because this time — what? This time you promise it will be different?"

"This time I promise I'll fight to make it different," Yara said. "Which is more than the last vessel got."

Halvard looked at me then, just briefly, and I saw the whole calculus behind his eyes: Aldric's armies to the north, winter coming, a refugee crisis, and now a girl with a prophecy standing in his hall asking him to gamble the city's survival on a broken oath and a fae princess's word. I knew what he'd choose before he opened his mouth. Not because Halvard was a coward — he wasn't, once. Because councils are not people. They are averaging machines, and averages always round down.

"We will take counsel," he said, "on all matters raised."

Which in Corvane means: *no*, delivered with a bow.

* * *

They came for Serah Dain at the inn, which flattered me — I'd assumed I'd rate a squad. Six men, plainclothes, the particular plainness of men trying very hard to look unremarkable. I recognized three faces from the Fourth Company. One of them, Brandt, had served under me at Grey Ford, where I'd dragged him out of the water with an arrow through his shoulder. He wouldn't meet my eye.

"Captain." He used the rank like an apology. "Warrant says you're to come answer questions about the fugitive. Unofficial-like, if you cooperate."

"Brandt." I set down my cup. Around the common room, patrons had developed a sudden fascination with their drinks; inns learn fast which way trouble points. "You're arresting me in a tavern because the council doesn't want to do it in daylight."

"Warrant's a warrant."

"It isn't, though. That's the whole point of a warrant." I stood. Slowly. Six men, and I was tired and underfed and my sword arm hadn't been right since Vaelmoor, and I put my hand on the hilt anyway, because some lessons you teach with your body or not at all. "Read me my charges. In front of witnesses. Name the magistrate. Or go back and tell whoever sent you that Serah Dain doesn't sneak out of rooms."

For a moment — I want to believe for a moment — Brandt's jaw worked like he might actually do it, might remember Grey Ford and oaths and the difference between law and orders. Then the innkeeper's daughter screamed from the yard, and everything went sideways at once.

Because Yara, bless them and curse them, had been in the stables. And when the second team — the ones sent around back, the ones Brandt didn't know about, because that's how these things work, the left hand ignorant of the right — came for the fugitive, Yara did not hide. Yara *glowed.*

There's no other word for it. Light came up out of them like dawn through a cracked shutter, gold-green, the color of the old covenant, the color of spring insisting itself through frost, and every horse in the stable knelt. Every horse. And the four men sent to seize them stood in that light and found, I am reliably informed, that they could not make their hands close. Not paralysis. Something worse: their hearts refused. You cannot arrest a sunrise.

By the time I fought my own six to a standstill — mostly by knocking over the big one and looking angry enough to make the rest reconsider their pensions — half the street was watching. Word travels in Corvane faster than plague and with better manners. By morning there were chalk drawings of Yara on doors. By evening, songs. Bad ones. The free cities hadn't had a living symbol in a century, and it turns out a symbol was exactly the itch the whole population had been scratching at under Aldric's shadow, waiting for something to rally around that wasn't another tax.

Yara stood in the stable yard, light guttering off them, surrounded by people pressing closer instead of away, and I watched their face and understood that this was not the triumph it looked like.

"They're making me into a flag," they said quietly, when I reached them.

"Yes."

"I didn't agree to be a flag."

"No," I said. "Nobody does. That's how flags work."

Behind us, Nyssara leaned against the stable door with her arms crossed and her glamour firmly back in place, wearing the expression of someone watching a play she'd seen performed in several courts and never once ended well.

* * *

We left the inn that night through a smuggler's cellar route that Yara swore they'd mapped as a teenager and that I chose not to ask follow-up questions about. We surfaced in the necropolis district, among the terraced tombs of the old merchant families, where the candles burned low and greenish and the air smelled of cold stone and older grief.

"Why here?" I whispered.

"Because the council won't act," Yara said. Their voice had changed since the stable yard — harder, and underneath the hardness, something raw. "And because if the living won't tell me the truth about the covenant, maybe the dead will. My mentor's buried in Corvane. Third terrace. I checked the rolls while you were busy threatening your former soldiers."

"You checked the burial rolls."

"I'm a thorough person."

"That's one word for you."

They almost smiled. Almost. Then they looked up the terraces toward the tombs, where the candlelight pooled like something drowning, and whatever they felt, they swallowed it whole.

"Tomorrow night," they said. "I'm going to ask a dead man why he chained me to a prophecy before I could walk. And Serah—" They paused, and in the green dark of the necropolis their eyes caught the light strangely, the way the shard had caught it underwater. "If what he tells me changes what has to happen next — if it makes everything worse before it makes anything better — I need to know you'll still be standing next to me when it does."

I thought about Brandt's face in the tavern. About Halvard's averaging machine. About a city full of people drawing a stranger on their doors because hope, apparently, is something you can requisition.

"I've stood next to worse odds," I said. "I stood next to you at Vaelmoor."

Below us, in the lowest terrace, a tomb-candle flared — once, hard, against the windless dark — and neither of us said anything, but we both saw it, and we both knew the dead of Corvane had heard every word.
