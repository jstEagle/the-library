## 9. A Date With the Dead

Corvane's necropolis sat on the east side of the city like a guest who'd overstayed his welcome — a terraced city of the dead above a living one, marble balconies for people who no longer needed to see anything. The living called it the Quiet Quarter, and they said it with the particular cheer of people who had decided not to think about something.

I liked it there. Dead cities didn't ask questions. Dead cities didn't look at you twice and whisper *that's her, that's the prophesied vessel* behind their hands, which is what half of Corvane had been doing since Serah and I arrived with Nyssara in tow and a drowned chapel's worth of trouble under our belts.

The dead were also, as it happened, the only ones who could tell me what my mentor had been hiding from me for twenty-two years. So: family reunion.

"You don't have to do this tonight," Serah said. She stood at the necropolis gate in her borrowed cloak, armor hidden beneath it, looking exactly like what she was — a disgraced knight pretending not to be on guard duty. She'd been doing a lot of pretending-not-to since Vaelmoor. I was learning to read it. The set of her jaw meant *I'm worried*. The way she checked the street over her shoulder every eleven seconds meant *the same thing but louder.*

"The moon's right," I said. "Waxing gibbous. Third night after the veil-crossing. Maerlin's notes are very specific."

"Your mentor's notes got you bound to an ancient covenant at birth."

"And also taught me to read, cook passable stew, and pick locks. Let's not throw out the whole ledger." I pulled my hood up. "Besides, if I wait for a better night, I'll talk myself out of it. I'm very good at talking myself out of things. Ask anyone. Ask me."

She almost smiled. It didn't survive the worry underneath. "Yara. If the answer in that tomb is bad—"

"Then at least I'll know which shape of bad to dread. That's practically optimism."

She let me go. That was the thing about Serah Dain — she argued with you until she was sure you'd heard her, and then she trusted you with whatever came next, even when trusting you visibly cost her. I felt her eyes on my back all the way up the first terrace stairs.

* * *

Maerlin's tomb was nothing like him. He'd been a hedge-witch of modest means and immodest opinions, a man who wore patched sleeves and lectured about dignity. His resting place was a marble vault with his name cut deep — MAERLIN OF THE THORNFELL ROADS, KEEPER OF SMALL TRUTHS — because apparently he'd saved money his whole life specifically to be buried more impressively than he'd lived. I could hear him saying it. *A man should leave a good door behind him, little bird. People knock on doors longer than they remember faces.*

"Hello, old man," I said to the stone.

The antechamber held what I'd come for: a brazier of black iron, three candles of grave-wax, a bowl for offerings, and enough floor space to draw a circle without kneeling in someone else's. The rite itself was old hedge-craft, older than the covenant, older probably than Corvane. You didn't summon a ghost — ghosts went where they went — but you could open a door and leave a lamp in it, and if love or guilt or unfinished business was strong enough, they'd come stand in the light.

Maerlin had left me a lifetime of unfinished business. I figured we were rich in the necessary currency.

I drew the circle with ash and salt and a thread of my own magic — carefully, carefully; since Vaelmoor the power under my skin had been sitting up and paying attention in ways that made my teeth ache. I lit the candles. I filled the bowl with honey, bread, and a splash of the plum brandy he'd hoarded like a dragon with excellent taste. Then I sat down inside the ring, put Maerlin's grimoire in my lap, opened it to the page where his marginal handwriting turned strange and hurried, and spoke his name into the dark.

Nothing happened, for a while. That was normal. The dead keep their own hours.

Then the candle flames leaned all together toward the brazier, like flowers turning toward a sun that wasn't there, and the air in the vault went cold in a way cold hadn't gone before — a *specific* cold, an attentive cold.

And Maerlin stepped through the door I'd opened.

He looked the way he had the last morning I saw him, which had been four years ago and also, somehow, this morning: gray beard trimmed badly, sleeves patched, eyes sharp as a fish-hook. Ghosts are made mostly of memory, and his memory had clearly decided to dress for the occasion.

"Well," he said. "You found the book."

"That's it? That's your opening? I perform a solemn rite, I offer honey and *good brandy*, and you open with 'you found the book'?"

"I considered 'hello, little bird,' but I thought you'd throw something."

"I might still." My voice cracked on the last word, which ruined the effect entirely. Four years of grief, two weeks of prophecy and pursuit and nearly drowning, and it all came apart at once, standing in front of a ghost in a marble room. "You're dead. I never — I didn't get to say—"

"I know. I know. Sit down, child. You're swaying."

I sat. The dead can be right in ways the living find insufferable.

For a moment neither of us spoke. The candles burned straight again. Somewhere above us, a night bird complained about existing.

"I've watched you," he said quietly. "Since Vaelmoor. Since the ford. Ghosts drift, mostly, but I — I anchored myself here so I could watch. I saw you dive into that flooded dark after a piece of a crown you didn't even understand. I saw the knight catch you." A pause, dry as his humor ever was. "She's a better influence than I was."

"She's a worse one," I said. "She makes me want to be brave. You only made me want to be careful."

"Careful kept you alive."

"Careful kept me *ignorant*." And there it was. The anger I'd been carrying up the terrace stairs, through the gate, across the city — it stood up in my chest and introduced itself. I opened the grimoire to the marked pages and turned them toward him, though I knew he could read them from anywhere. "What is this, Maerlin? What did you write the night I was born?"

He looked at the margins full of his own hand. His ghost-form flickered — just once, like a candle in wind.

"You were born under the conjunction," he said. "You know that part."

"The prophecy. The vessel. The living key. Yes, I've had some time to review."

"Then you know the covenant between the free cities and Thornfell requires a keeper. Someone born on both sides of the veil's shadow — human blood, fae-touched birth-hour. Someone who could hold the treaty's terms in flesh and blood, carry them, renew them when the century turns." He met my eyes. "That was always going to be you, little bird. I didn't make that true. I only recognized it. Your mother knew. She agreed. It was the price of crossing safely out of Thornfell when Aldric's father started the purges, and she paid it knowing what it bought."

My mother. Who I remembered as hands and humming and the smell of rosemary, and then not at all. "Agreed," I repeated. "Everyone keeps agreeing to things on my behalf. It's a popular pastime."

"Yes," he said simply. "It is. And it's shameful, and I won't dress it up. But that binding wasn't the secret, Yara. The binding was the mercy. The secret is *why the covenant needs a key at all.*"

The cold in the room deepened. The candle flames shrank to blue points.

"The crown-shards," I said slowly. "The three pieces. The ritual to reforge—"

"Every hundred years, the covenant must be renewed at the covenant-stone, by the keeper's hand, with the crown made whole. And renewal has a price. It has only ever had one price." His voice didn't waver. I would learn later that he'd rehearsed this confession to my memory for four years. "A life. Freely given. The land doesn't run on treaties, child, however we dress it up. The veil runs on sacrifice. One life, freely offered, each century — given into the crown, given into the ground, and the wild magic flows clean for another hundred years. That is the arithmetic underneath everything. That's why the free cities eat while the borderlands starve. That's why Thornfell thaws and freezes on schedule. Somebody pays."

The words went into me like cold water into a lung.

"The last renewal," I said. My mouth felt wrong around the words. "When was it?"

"Ninety-nine years ago. A woman named Sef. A shepherd. She volunteered. They wrote songs about her for a decade and then conveniently forgot her name, because remembering what peace costs is bad for appetite." He folded his ghostly hands. "The next turn falls within the year. The conjunction of your birth, the date in the prophecy, the failing weather, all of it — the covenant is coming due, and its bill is addressed to you."

"To *me*." I heard my own voice go flat and strange. "The prophecy. The vessel. The shattered crown. Gather the shards, reforge the crown — and die. That's the ending. That's what the book says I'm *for*."

"It says you're the keeper. What the keeper does with the keeping—" 

"Don't." I was on my feet. I don't remember standing. "Don't you dare split that hair with me. You *knew*. You knew since the hour I was born, and instead of telling me, you hid me in smuggler roads and taught me to be small. You taught me to hide my own magic from myself. You let me grow up thinking I was nobody, thinking the worst thing in my future was a tax collector, while all along I was — I was a *pig being fattened for slaughter*, and you were the one holding the trough!"

The candles guttered. My magic surged up with the anger, unasked, and the ash circle flared white, and for one heartbeat every grave on the terrace hummed like struck glass. Maerlin's form flickered hard, edges fraying.

"Yes," he said, when he'd steadied. "Yes. All of it true. Say the rest."

"The rest?" My laugh came out jagged. "There's more?"

"You're owed the rest." And now his composure cracked, and what showed underneath was an old man's grief, worn smooth by four years of carrying it alone in the dark. "I hid the truth because I loved you. I told myself that. I hid you to protect you, and hiding you meant the covenant came due with no keeper ready, no keeper willing, no keeper *informed* — and the land began to sicken, and Aldric began to march, and the witch-finders began to burn villages hunting for a prophesied child who might have lived quiet and safe if I'd been brave enough to lie to you forever." He bowed his head. "My cowardice didn't save you, little bird. It just scheduled your ruin and billed everyone else for the delay. I doomed the realms to spare one girl a hard conversation, and I'd do it again, and that's the most damning thing I know how to say."

The anger in me wanted to burn. It had fuel — years of it, every locked lesson, every *don't ask, little bird*, every time he'd changed the subject when I asked about my mother. I stood there with all that fire in my hands and nowhere to put it.

But I'd spent two weeks watching Serah carry her disgrace like a stone she refused to set down. I'd watched Nyssara look at her mother's empty throne. I'd watched a queen break under a century of captivity. Everyone I met lately was carrying the wreckage of somebody's protection.

"When I was nine," I said, "I fell out of the walnut tree and broke my arm, and you set it yourself, and you cried more than I did."

"What does that have to do with—"

"Nothing. Everything. I'm trying to decide whether I forgive you, and my heart and my head are voting differently, and the walnut tree is the tiebreaker." I scrubbed my face with both hands. "You should have told me. If you'd told me at fifteen, I'd have run. If you'd told me at eighteen, I might have walked to the stone myself out of sheer stubbornness, because I was an idiot at eighteen. There was no version of this where I took it well. But I'd have had *years*. I'd have had a say. Instead I get a ghost and a deadline."

"Yes."

"And the worst part—" My voice broke again. I let it. "The worst part is that it worked. I *was* safe. I had twenty-two years you gave me. Stupid roads, stupid stew, stupid lock-picking lessons. Nobody gets those years. You stole them for me from the whole world's account, and I don't know whether that makes you the best man I ever knew or the worst, and I don't think I get to decide tonight."

"No," he agreed softly. "You don't."

We stood in the circle together, the dead man and the doomed girl, while the candles burned down.

"But hear me, Maerlin, and hear me clearly." I picked up the grimoire and closed it. "I am not a shepherd named Sef. I am not walking to any stone to die because a century-old book says it's my turn to settle the bill. Whatever the prophecy wants, whatever the covenant demands — I will find a third option. Or a fourth. I once talked a toll-keeper out of charging us double by convincing him his scale was haunted. I can talk the universe out of a sacrifice."

Something moved through his ghost-light that I realized, after a moment, was hope — hope and fear braided together, because hope is dangerous for the dead too. "The covenant isn't a contract, little bird. It's a wound that learned to close. Wounds don't negotiate."

"Wounds don't. But the people standing around them do." I crouched and pinched out the nearest candle. "One more question, and then you can rest. The third shard. Two are found. Where's the last one?"

His flicker this time was different. Guilt-shaped. "You already know more than you should. Ask the knight what her king wears on his signet hand. Ask her why Aldric's armies march in perfect health through blighted country." He was fading now, going thin at the edges, the lamp burning down. "Yara. Little bird. When you refuse the prophecy — and you will, you have your mother's jaw — be careful what you refuse it *with*. Crowns can be shared. So can burdens. But the stone will take what it's given, and it doesn't distinguish between a life and a—"

The candle went out. The cold lifted. The vault was just a vault, marble and dust and a bowl of untouched brandy.

"—life and a what?" I said to the empty room. "*A what?*"

The dead keep their own hours, and apparently their own endings.

* * *

Serah was waiting at the gate, and she read my face before I'd crossed half the distance, because of course she did. "Bad?"

"Define bad."

"Yara."

"The covenant runs on sacrifice. One life per century, freely given, due this year, payable by me." I watched her go still — truly still, the stillness of a woman whose body had decided movement was a luxury. "So. Bad."

Her hand found mine without either of us deciding it should. Her grip was careful, the way you hold something you're afraid might break, and something in my chest objected loudly to being handled like glass.

"Then we don't pay it," she said. No hesitation. No weighing. As if I'd told her the ferry fare had gone up.

"That's the plan. I'm working out the details."

"Details." She exhaled, and it shook slightly at the end. "Good. I'm — good at details."

Above us, on the highest terrace, every bell in the necropolis rang once — all of them, unstruck, a sound like the city clearing its throat. Below, in the living streets, dogs started up in a dozen yards at once. Serah's head snapped toward the south wall, where against the stars the watch-fires had begun multiplying.

Fire signals. Three, then five, then too many to count.

"That's the alarm pattern for riders at the gates," Serah said, and her hand tightened on mine, and all the softness in her face hardened into something I'd seen once before, across a river ford. "Not merchants. Not patrols. Witch-finders. They've found us, Yara."

Somewhere behind us, in the Quiet Quarter, faint and patient, a second bell began to ring — one that no living hand had touched.
