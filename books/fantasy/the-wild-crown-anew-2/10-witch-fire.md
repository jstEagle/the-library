## 10. Witch-Fire

I want to say I had a plan. That when the bells of Corvane started screaming — because that's what they sounded like, screaming, a bronze animal with its throat cut — I turned to Serah with some clever scheme already half-built behind my teeth.

What I actually said was: "Oh, that's not good."

Serah was on her feet before the second bell finished dying. She'd been sleeping in her armor for three days now, which told you everything about how well she expected any of this to go. "Witch-finders," she said, at the window, looking down into the lamplight of the Lower Terraces. "That's the Pyre Order's call. Three tolls."

"Three tolls means?"

"Three tolls means they've already found someone." She turned, and the lamplight caught the scar along her jaw, the one she never talked about and I never asked about, mostly because we were both carrying more scars than conversation could hold. "Yara. The council hasn't voted yet. If the Pyre is here before the vote—"

"Then someone in Corvane sold us." I was already stuffing the almanac, the prophecy book, my mentor's handwriting bleeding across every margin, into the oilskin satchel that had become less a bag than an organ of my body. "Which, honestly? Rude. We've only been here four days."

"Four days is plenty of time to sell someone." Serah buckled her sword belt with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had done this so many times the motion had stopped being a decision. "It's practically a civic tradition."

* * *

We made it as far as the stair before we understood it wasn't just a raid.

The Lower Terraces of Corvane are built like a wedding cake dropped down a cliffside — narrow streets stacked on narrow streets, laundry lines strung between them like bunting, everything smelling of fish brine and tallow smoke. From the third landing I could see torchlight moving through them in patterns. Not searching patterns. Herding patterns.

"They're not looking for me," I said, and my voice came out flat and strange, like it belonged to someone else. "They're driving everyone toward the Granary Square."

"That's where they do the testing." Serah's hand found my shoulder, pulled me back from the rail before I could be seen. "Iron touch and a spoken verse. Anyone who flinches from the iron goes on the wagons."

"How many flinch?"

"In a city this size? Dozens. Most of them hedge-witches. Herb-wives. A child who talks to pigeons too fluently." She didn't look at me when she said it, which was its own kind of kindness. "They burn small ones first. It keeps the crowd warm."

There is a particular quality to horror that arrives without surprise — the horror of things you always knew were true finally standing in front of you wearing their boots. My whole life I had hidden my magic the way other people hide a limp, a debt, a shameful relative. Small concealments, daily, until the hiding became load-bearing. And underneath all of it had lived this knowledge, quiet as a stone at the bottom of a well: *this is what happens to people like me when they're found.*

I just hadn't expected the finding to happen while I stood on a balcony watching.

"The council," I said. "The evacuation plan—"

"Died with the vote, if there even was one. Listen." Serah took my face in her hands, which she did rarely enough that it short-circuited something in my chest every time. "There's a smuggler's gate under the tannery quarter. You know these roads better than anyone alive. Get to it. Get out. Nyssara is waiting at the veil-crossing with the shard—"

"You're coming with me."

"I'm going to buy you time." She said it the way you'd say *I'm going to the market.* Like it was nothing. Like it wasn't the sentence I'd been dreading since the ford, since the drowned chapel, since the night she'd broken her orders open like a lock and saved my life underwater. "Don't argue. You're terrible at it. You argue like a scholar — lots of footnotes."

"And you argue like a landslide," I said. "Fine. Fine. But Serah—" I caught her wrist. Beneath the vambrace her pulse was going hard and fast, and I thought, absurdly, *she's afraid, she's been afraid this whole time, she just does the arithmetic anyway.* "Come back. That's not a request or a clever plan. It's just the only term I'm offering."

For half a heartbeat something crossed her face that wasn't knightly at all.

Then the sky over the Granary Square went orange, and neither of us had time for heartbeats anymore.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about your own power: it doesn't feel like yours.

Everything my mentor taught me was small magic by design. A charm to steady a mule. A cantrip to dim a lantern. The trick of reading weather in the way birds sit on wires. Little locks on little doors, each one a lesson wearing a disguise, and I'd spent twenty-three years thinking that was the size of me. A hedge-witch. A smuggler with party tricks.

The first witch-fire hit the tannery district while I was still on the stairs, and I felt it the way you feel a slap meant for someone near you — wrongness, pressure, the covenant itself groaning somewhere beneath the world. Then the screaming started, and the smell, and I stopped being a person with a plan and became a person with a problem.

The problem was this: between me and the smuggler's gate lay six hundred civilians being driven like sheep toward a square where children were tested against cold iron, and every instinct I owned — the smuggler's instinct, the survivor's, the coward's excellent and well-fed instinct — said *go around, go down, go away.*

And underneath that, older than my training, older than my name, something said: *no.*

Later, Serah would ask me what it felt like. This is the honest answer: it felt like taking off a corset I hadn't known I was wearing.

I stepped off the stair onto the landing, faced the burning terraces, and reached for magic with both hands instead of two fingers.

It came like a flood through a door kicked open. Not the trickle I knew — the polite rationed drip of a lifetime's careful hiding — but the whole aquifer. I felt it move up through the stones of Corvane itself, through the old foundations laid when the free cities still traded openly with Thornfell, through ley-threads my mentor had taught me to sense and never once let me pull. And the city *answered*. Lamps guttered and then blazed white. Rain gathered out of a clear night sky over three streets at once. The fire on the tannery roofs bent sideways, like grass under wind, and died.

"Oh," I said, to no one, feeling my hair lift off my neck with static. "Oh, that's — okay. Okay. So that's what's in there."

Exhilaration first. I won't pretend otherwise. There was a moment — I'm ashamed of it and I keep it anyway — when it felt *wonderful*, when the power sang through me like the first swallow of brandy after a frozen crossing, when some voice in my blood that sounded disturbingly like my own said *you could do anything, you could unmake them, look at you.*

Then a witch-finder squad rounded the corner below me, dragging a family — mother, father, a boy of maybe eight with pigeon-feathers in his pockets — and the exhilaration curdled so fast I tasted copper.

The squad captain raised his hand and spoke the ignition verse, and fire bloomed in his palm with that greasy orange color the Pyre Order favored, oil-fed and cruel.

I have thrown exactly two fireballs in my life. Both happened in the next ten seconds.

The first took the captain's flame out of the air and scattered it like a kicked anthill of sparks. The second lifted him off his feet and put him through a fishmonger's awning, which sounds funny and absolutely was not, because he didn't get up, and because I felt the moment his ribs gave like it was happening inside my own chest. The other finders ran. Good. Running was allowed. Running was the entire point.

The boy with the feathers stared up at me from the cobbles, mouth open.

"Gate's under the tannery," I told him, and my voice came out doubled, harmonized with itself, a chord instead of a note, and that frightened me more than anything else so far. "Third arch past the lime pits. Run."

They ran. Half the street ran, following the family, following the feathers-in-the-pockets kid, and I understood with a lurch that I had just become a direction. People flee *toward* things. Fires make people run from them; miracles make people run after. I had lit myself up like a beacon over Corvane and every hidden thing in the city was going to turn toward the light, and some of those hidden things would live, and every single witch-finder in Corvane was going to know precisely where the prophesied vessel was standing.

Well. Thought I, hauling magic up through the stones like hauling nets, arms shaking, teeth buzzing. They were going to find out eventually.

* * *

The next hour is a blur with bright spots burned into it, the way a flash leaves an afterimage.

I remember pulling the rain down harder over the eastern terraces until the Pyre's fires hissed and drowned. I remember walking — walking! — down the middle of Rope-Walk Street with the covenant's old power crackling off me in blue-white threads, and the witch-finders' iron pikes bending toward me like compass needles before their owners dropped them entirely. I remember a hedge-witch, ancient and bent, pressing her granddaughter into my path and saying, "Take her gift too, if you can carry it," and I said "I can't, I'm sorry, I'm barely carrying mine," and the old woman looked at me with such bottomless disappointment that it hurt worse than the burning in my veins did.

I remember Serah.

She found me at the mouth of Granary Square, where the last of the fleeing crowds were pouring past me toward the tannery gates, and where a full company of the Pyre Order had formed up in their white-and-black, forty strong, with a pyre-engine at their center like a siege weapon designed by grief. Their prelate stood on its platform, robed, holding up an iron rod, chanting the long verse — the one that doesn't test, that *binds*, that wraps a mage's power in knots until they can be carried like cargo.

Forty men. An engine of war. A binding-verse already halfway spoken.

And Serah Dain, disgraced, rankless, in borrowed armor, walked out alone into the square between me and all of it, drew her sword, and said in a parade-ground voice that carried to the rooftops:

"Corvane charter law, article nine. No foreign order bears arms within the walls. I am Serah Dain, formerly knight-captain of the Third Marches, and I am invoking my right of challenge. Who's first?"

It was the stupidest, bravest, most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and it bought me eleven seconds.

Eleven seconds is a long time when you've stopped rationing yourself.

I didn't fight them, is the thing. I want that written somewhere. I tore the binding-verse apart mid-syllable — unwove it, unraveled the prelate's chant like picking a knot out of wet rope — and then I took the pyre-engine's own fire and turned it upward, into the empty sky, a column of light that turned midnight over Corvane briefly into noon. Not one finder died. Some would bear burns. Some would dream about that column of light for the rest of their lives, and wake up sweating, and never quite be able to explain to their priests why the dream felt less like terror than like *homesickness.*

Because here is what I saw in their faces, in that noon-made-of-night moment, right before I brought the granary bell tower down across the square's only entrance with a gesture that cost me something I'd never get back — here is what I saw:

They weren't looking at an abomination.

Some of them were looking at a miracle.

That was the horrifying part. Not my power. Their faces. Because Aldric's whole arithmetic depends on witches being monsters, and for one held breath, forty trained witch-finders looked at me and saw something else, and I watched the Pyre Order's story crack down the middle like river ice.

Then the tower fell, the square sealed, the crowd poured through the gap I'd bought them, and I staggered against a wall and discovered what the price tag read.

* * *

Magic, my mentor wrote once, in a margin I'd read a hundred times without understanding: *is a loan, never a gift. Mind the interest.*

I minded it now. Oh, I minded it. It felt like my bones had been used as kindling. My vision had gone silver at the edges, and when I coughed, the cough glowed faintly, which is never a good sign. Somewhere in the last hour I had burned through years — I could feel it, the way you feel a tooth gone from your mouth, a absence shaped like decades I would not get.

Nyssara had warned me. Standing at the veil-crossing with the queen's shard wrapped in silk, she'd said, in that fae way of hers that makes cruelty sound like courtesy: *"Spend it freely, little key. Just know the coin is yourself."*

Worth it, I thought, sliding down the wall to sit in the mud of Rope-Walk Street, watching the last families vanish toward the tannery gates. Ask me tomorrow. Ask me when I'm not glowing.

Boots rang on stone. For one stupid second I hoped it was Serah.

It was worse. It was a crow.

Not a real crow — a construct, a spy-thing, feathers of blackened parchment and eyes like drops of ink. It landed on a broken cart three feet from me and opened its beak, and King Aldric's voice came out of it, smooth and reasonable, the voice of a man who has done his sums and likes the total.

"*Yara Villalobos.* The vessel of the covenant. I confess I expected someone larger." A pause, dry as ledger paper. "*You've cost me a company tonight, and shown me something worth ten companies. I had wondered if the prophecy exaggerated you. It didn't. Which presents a problem, because I need what you are — and I find I cannot trust anyone else to deliver you intact. So I am coming myself. Expect me within the fortnight. Rest. Recover. Spend wisely. I will want whatever remains.*"

The crow folded itself shut like a letter and crumbled to ash.

I sat in the mud of Corvane, glowing faintly at the edges, hollowed out to the wick, and laughed until it hurt — which, given the state of my ribs, was approximately four seconds.

The king himself. Coming for me personally, with the third shard fused to his ring and an army at his back, and me running on fumes and borrowed years toward a veil-crossing where a fae princess and a stolen crown-piece waited, and Serah —

Where was Serah?

I got up. The world tilted politely and let me. I got up again.

Down the smoking length of Rope-Walk Street, limping, swordless, singed, alive — a figure in borrowed armor, waving one arm overhead in a gesture that managed to be both *over here* and *don't you dare pass out before I get there.*

I had bought six hundred people out of a burning city. I had unmade a binding-verse with my bare will. I had announced myself to a king.

None of it felt like victory. All of it felt like the first page of something, and I had read enough prophecies lately to know how authors liked to end their openings.

"Hold on," I told the city, the mountains, whoever kept the ledgers of loans. "I'm not done choosing yet."
