## 11. The Road Neither Map Shows

I have been ambushed at dawn, betrayed at supper, and once challenged to a duel by a man I'd already beaten unconscious, but nothing in twenty-nine years of soldiering prepared me for Yara Villalobos saying the words *trust me* while pointing at a cliff face.

"There's no road there," I said.

"There is," they said. "It just doesn't like being seen."

Nyssara stood a little apart, hood up against the ash-fall that had followed us out of Corvane like a dog that had decided we were its people now. She looked at the blank stone wall with the particular expression of someone who had grown up in a realm where geography was more suggestion than law. "They're telling the truth," she said. "The mountain remembers them."

"The mountain *what*?"

"Walk," Yara said, and walked into the rock.

* * *

Let me back up, because my mother always said I tell stories like a sergeant reads dispatches — everything after the part where it all went wrong.

Three days earlier we had left Corvane burning behind us. Not all of it — the witch-finders were thorough but not yet complete — but the quarter where we'd slept, the granary Nyssara had shown the council as proof of Thornfell's decay, and most memorably, the stable containing Serah Dain's horse, a bay mare named Patience who deserved better than what she got. I mention this because I have decided that if I am to die in these mountains, someone should know I loved that horse more than I have ever loved a person, which until recently included everyone except my late king, and now includes exactly two living beings, one of whom was currently dissolving into solid granite ahead of me.

Yara had burned their lifespan down to embers saving refugees from the witch-fire. You could see it on them: the grey under their eyes, the way their hands shook when they thought no one watched, the hair going white at the temples in patches, like frost claiming a window. They were twenty-two years old and moving like a woman of sixty who had made her peace with stairs.

And still they insisted on walking point.

"Give me your arm," I told them, when the smuggler's path — which did exist, once you committed to the fiction of it, a ledge no wider than a coffin cut into a cliff that the maps called the Gullet — narrowed past all sense.

"I can manage."

"Yara." I kept my voice level, the voice I'd used on green recruits and panicking civilians. "You nearly fell twice since the switchback. If you go over, I cannot follow. The road doesn't remember *me*. It barely tolerates me. So either you take my arm, or you explain to Nyssara why we're burying an empty cairn again."

That landed. They glanced back at the fae woman trailing us, who had developed the disconcerting habit of watching Yara the way scholars watch a candle about to gutter — with love, and arithmetic.

Yara took my arm.

I will record, for the historical record, that this was purely tactical. Their weight through my sleeve was slighter than I expected, and warm, and they muttered *this is undignified* every forty paces or so, which became a kind of birdsong. At some point the muttering stopped, and they simply leaned, and I adjusted my stride without deciding to, and neither of us mentioned it, and both of us knew.

* * *

The covenant's collapse announced itself in small ways before large ones. A stream ran uphill for ten yards and then thought better of it. Our shadows arrived a half-second after we did. Once, I looked back along the trail and saw our own footprints walking away from us in the opposite direction, unhurried, like men leaving a tavern.

"That's normal," Yara said, not looking.

"It is *not*."

"It's normal *here*." They squeezed my arm — tactically. "The veil's thinning. Where it thins, things stop agreeing with each other. Time, distance, cause and effect. My mentor used to say the mountains near the old crossings dream out loud, and you shouldn't eavesdrop."

"And your mentor," I said, "is also the person who bound you at birth to a death-covenant and never told you. Forgive me if I don't adopt his folk wisdom wholesale."

The silence that followed had texture. I felt Yara's weight shift — pulling back a fraction, then, deliberately, settling closer. An apology in the grammar of exhausted people.

"You're right," they said finally. "He lied beautifully, though. That has to count for something."

"He taught you the roads."

"He taught me everything. Including how to lie beautifully." A breath. "I keep waiting to be angrier than I am. Mostly I just miss him. Is that broken?"

I thought of Aldric, whom I had served for nine years before the day I asked one question too many about the witch-findings in the Marchlands. I missed him too. Grief does not check references.

"No," I said. "It's human."

"*That's* debatable lately," they said, and laughed — a real laugh, brief and cracked, and something in my chest performed a maneuver I did not authorize.

* * *

We camped that night in a hollow the smugglers called the Kettle, a bowl of stone where the wind went to sulk. No fire; witch-finders read smoke like priests read scripture. We ate cold smoked trout and hard bread, and Nyssara took first watch, folding herself against the rock with her eyes open in the dark — fae eyes, catching what little light existed and giving it back doubled.

Which left Yara and me lying a polite three feet apart on bedrolls, staring at a sky full of stars that were, in this place, occasionally rearranging themselves into patterns no astronomer had signed off on.

"Tell me something true," Yara said.

"We're being hunted by a king whose armies outnumber us roughly four hundred to one, and I'm lying in a hole in the ground next to a person I've known for a month, and I trust them more than anyone I served beside for nine years. Something is deeply wrong with me."

"Other than that."

I turned my head. They had turned theirs. In the starlight their face was half shadow and half silver, the white streaks in their hair standing out like scars.

"When I questioned Aldric's orders," I said, "it wasn't principle. Everyone thinks it was principle. It wasn't. There was a girl in the Marchlands — eleven years old, caught with a charm her grandmother gave her against fever. The witch-finders wanted to make an example. I asked Aldric to intervene. One word. He wouldn't give it, because mercy is a currency and he was hoarding. And I realized I had spent nine years mistaking loyalty for virtue." I paused. "They burned the village anyway. The grandmother. The girl."

Yara said nothing. They reached across the three feet of cold ground and put their hand over mine, and their hand was trembling — from the burnout, from the cold, from who knew — and they let it be seen, which I understood was the harder thing.

"Something true from me," they said. "I've been running since I was sixteen, and I told myself it was freedom. It wasn't. Freedom would have meant choosing a life. Running was just hiding with better scenery." Their thumb moved once across my knuckles, over the old scar the prophecy book had known about before I did. "Until a month ago I didn't choose anything. The roads chose, the mentor chose, the prophecy chose. You're the first thing I picked."

"The first thing?"

"Don't make it strange, knight."

"Too late. It's extremely strange. I'm having a whole crisis about it quietly."

"Quietly," Nyssara said from the darkness, "is a generous description."

Yara threw a crust of bread at her. She caught it without looking. Somewhere above us, the stars finished rearranging themselves into what I swear was a crude drawing of a crown, then thought better of it and scattered.

* * *

On the fourth day the mountain presented its bill.

I should have known. Nothing in Thornfell is free — not passage, not silence, certainly not roads that exist only for those they remember. We had crossed into a stretch Yara called the Ledger, though they hadn't explained why, and the air had gone thick and slow, like walking through honey that resented you.

Then the path ended at a door.

Not a door in any architectural sense. A doorway-shaped absence in the rock, hung with a curtain of woven birch twigs, and before it sat a figure that had perhaps once been a bear, or perhaps a boulder that had grown ambitions. It was the size of a haywain, moss-grown, with eyes like riverbed stones and a voice that came less through the ears than through the soles of the feet.

**"TOLL,"** it said.

Yara stepped forward, and I saw them do the thing I'd watched them do at Vaelmoor and at Corvane — square their shoulders around a body that was failing them, and pretend otherwise.

"What's owed?" they asked.

The great head tilted. Stone ground on stone. **"THE ROADS KEPT YOU WHEN YOU WERE SMALL AND HIDING. THE ROADS CLOSED BEHIND YOU WHEN THE FINDERS CAME TO THORNFELL. TWICE THE MOUNTAIN LIED FOR YOU. NOW IT ASKS ITS DEBT."**

"Name it."

**"A MEMORY OF WARMTH. ONE ONLY. THE MOUNTAIN IS OLD AND COLD AND IT WOULD LIKE TO REMEMBER WHAT SUMMER FELT LIKE BEFORE THE VEIL SICKENED."**

Beside me, Nyssara made a sound — soft, horrified. "Yara," she said carefully, "memories are not free here. Not for one of your blood. A memory given is—"

"A memory gone," Yara finished. "Yes. I know how ledgers work."

"Then we find another way," I said. "There's always another way. We go around, we fight it, we—"

"Fight a mountain, Serah."

"—we negotiate—"

"It *is* negotiating. This is what negotiation looks like when the other party weighs four tons and predates language." They were already unbuckling their pack, setting it down with the care of someone tidying up in advance. "It's one memory. I have thousands. Most of them are bad."

"Which one will it take?" Nyssara asked, and her voice had gone very quiet, very precise. "Does it choose? Or do you?"

Yara opened their mouth. Closed it. And I watched the truth arrive on their face like weather: they didn't know. None of us knew. The mountain would reach into the pantry and take what pleased it, and it might take the taste of their mentor's terrible cooking, or it might take the night at the Kettle, or the moment at the ford when I'd turned my sword aside instead of using it —

"No," I said aloud.

"Serah—"

"*No.*" I stepped between them and the door. My hand found my sword hilt out of pure habit, which was absurd, and I knew it was absurd, and I gripped it anyway. "Find another toll. Take mine."

**"YOURS IS NOT SOUGHT,"** the mountain said, with what I can only describe as bureaucratic regret. **"KNIGHTS ARE NUMEROUS. KEYS ARE RARE."**

Key. The word from the margins of the dead mentor's book, the word that had sentenced Yara before they drew breath. I hated it with a completeness that surprised me.

"Then hear *my* terms," I said, and heard my own voice drop into the register I'd used across negotiating tables and battle lines. "One memory, freely given, but chosen by the giver. Sworn and sealed. If the mountain takes blind, we walk back down and let Aldric's finders have this whole ridge, and the mountain can explain to the wild magic why it let the last key die of witch-fire on its doorstep."

Silence. Actual geological silence, the kind with strata.

Then the great stone eyes crinkled — the closest a landmass comes to smiling — and it rumbled: **"THE KNIGHT BARGAINS WITH A HILL. GOOD. THE KEY CHOSE WELL."**

"I haven't chosen—" Yara started.

**"ONE MEMORY OF WARMTH, GIVER'S CHOICE. SWORN."**

Yara looked at me. There was something naked in their face, stripped past the wit and the wariness, down to the runaway apprentice who had spent six years being careful. Then they nodded, once, and turned to the curtain of birch twigs, and pressed both palms flat against it.

"I give the memory," they said, "of learning to swim. My mentor, in the millpond below Vaelmoor, the summer I was twelve. He held me up and swore he wouldn't let go and he didn't, not once, even when I flailed hard enough to crack his ribs. The sun was out. The water was freezing. I laughed until I choked. That memory. Warmth. Given."

The twigs shivered. Light moved through them like water through roots, and something lifted out of Yara — I saw it leave, or thought I did, a brightness drawn up through the crown of their head and into the stones — and then the doorway stood open on ordinary darkness, and the figure settled back into being merely a very large rock, at rest, dreaming summer.

Yara swayed. I caught them. Of course I caught them; I would have crossed the world to catch them, which was a thought I filed firmly under *later*.

"What did it feel like?" Nyssara asked softly.

Yara considered, leaning on me, their brow furrowed. "Like a room in the house is empty," they said at last. "I know the house has one fewer room. I can't visit it anymore. But I remember knowing it was there." A pause. "Is that worse or better?"

"Yes," Nyssara said, and led the way through the door.

* * *

Beyond the Ledger the road descended through country that hurt to look at — beautiful the way a fever dream is beautiful. Birch trees grew upside-down from the underside of cliffs. A waterfall fell upward into a lake that hung above us like a ceiling of hammered glass. Twice we passed shrines to gods I didn't recognize, and once a shrine to a god I was fairly sure Nyssara invented on the spot, judging by her expression.

Through all of it Yara walked close enough that our shoulders touched on the narrow stretches, and on the wide stretches too, where touching was no longer required. Neither of us remarked on this. It had gone past remarking, the way a fire goes past the stage where you discuss whether to light it.

We made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, and this time Nyssara took second watch instead of first, and I understood the gift of it, and I think Yara did too, because they didn't pretend to sleep.

"Ask me," they said to the stars.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not-asking for four days. You get a particular line between your eyebrows. It's very disciplined. It's also loud."

I lay there a long moment. The line between my eyebrows, presumably, deepened.

"After the ritual," I said. "After the crown. If we win — if there's an *after* — the prophecy says the price is a life freely given. Yours, presumably. You've read the same margins I have. And every plan you've made, every risk you've taken since Vaelmoor, has had the shape of a person spending down." I turned to look at them. "So ask me to stop you. Or tell me I'm wrong. Because I need to know which war I'm fighting."

The wind moved through the ruined tower, playing the broken stones like a bad harpist.

"I don't have an answer," Yara said finally. "I want to say I'll refuse it. I want to say it so badly it tastes like iron. But I've watched what happens when the covenant isn't paid — the blight, the poisoned wells, Corvane's granaries rotting from the inside. If it's me or the world—" They stopped. Started again. "My mentor spent his whole life making sure I'd never have to answer that question. He failed. Now I spend my nights hoping I'll be brave enough to die well, and my days hoping somebody finds a third option, and I don't know which hope is the coward's."

"Neither," I said. "The coward's hope is that someone else answers it for you."

They were quiet. Then, in the dark, their hand found mine — not tactically this time, not for balance, just found it, and stayed.

"Third option," they murmured. "All right. You find one, knight. I'll keep breathing till you do."

"That's the least romantic proposal I've ever been party to."

"Was that a proposal?"

"Shut up, Villalobos."

But they were laughing, low and tired and alive, and I lay there holding their hand in the ruins of a tower in a country that was coming apart at its seams, and I thought: *find a third option. Refuse the arithmetic. Whatever it costs.*

Above us, the stars had rearranged themselves one final time. I didn't see it until morning, when Nyssara woke us with her hand on her sword — because far off to the east, beyond the mountains' shoulder, the sky over King Aldric's road south was glowing.

Not dawn. Dawn came politely, from the west.

This glow burned gold, and it moved, and it was coming toward us, and riding beneath it, small with distance but unmistakable, flew a banner I knew too well: the crowned fist of House Aldric, and behind it, a prisoner's litter borne high on spears, and in the litter, hair like moonlight and eyes like a struck match, sat Nyssara's mother — the Queen of Thornfell — wearing chains of cold iron and a smile that promised ruin to whoever had made her smile like that.

Nyssara went very still. Then she said, in a voice I had never heard her use, a voice like winter finding a gap under a door:

"She's supposed to be at court. Under guard. Which means either my mother escaped—" she swallowed, "—or Aldric just handed her his own leash, and my mother is about to do something *stupid and magnificent*, and we are directly in its path."

The gold light crested the ridge.
