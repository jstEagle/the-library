## 13. Two Crowns, One Neck

### Serah

I have stood watch a thousand nights in my life, and I can count the quiet ones on one hand. Tonight was shaping up to be another of the loud kind — the kind where you listen for hooves and witch-fire and the particular silence that means someone is about to kill you — and then Yara Villalobos ruined it by humming.

They were sitting on the far side of our little fire, mending a strap on their pack with their teeth and their fingers and, occasionally, a small illegal charm that stitched leather better than any cobbler in Corvane. The humming was tuneless. Deliberately so, I suspected, because everything Yara did with their magic was deliberately something.

"You're staring," they said, without looking up.

"I'm watching for threats."

"I'm the threat."

"You're a nuisance," I corrected. "There's a difference. Threats require competence."

That got me the look — the one where one eyebrow goes up and the whole world gets slightly funnier at my expense. I'd been cataloguing that look since the ford. I could have drawn it from memory by now, which should have been my first warning that I was in trouble. Soldiers don't memorize faces unless the faces matter, and things that matter are things you can lose.

The mountains around us were doing what Thornfell did best: pretending to be ordinary while being anything but. Since we'd crossed back through the veil-road, the pines grew at angles pine trees shouldn't grow, and once an entire hour passed where the fire's smoke fell upward like a grey river running home. Nyssara had called it "the land remembering itself" and then vanished into her tent to brood in the elegant way only exiled royalty can brood.

Tomorrow we went into the fae court. Tomorrow we stole a crown-shard out from under a captive queen's guards, or negotiated it free, depending on which version of the plan survived contact with reality. And somewhere behind all of it, patient as a debt collector, waited the prophecy in its century-old book, with its margins full of my dead mentor's handwriting and its terms full of somebody's death.

"Serah." Yara had stopped mending. "You've gone somewhere grim."

"I live there," I said. "It's roomy."

They laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised out of them — and something in my chest did a thing I refused to name. Naming it would make it a liability. I'd spent twenty years learning to identify liabilities before they killed me. It was the only reason I was still alive after Aldric's court had chewed through everyone who ever trusted anyone.

But Yara set down the strap and looked at me across the fire, and the firelight did something unfair to their face, and I thought: *you're going to say it anyway, aren't you, Dain. You absolute disaster of a knight.*

"Ask me," they said quietly.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not-asking since Greywater. You do this thing where you open your mouth and then reorganize your face into a fortress. It's very impressive. It's also very obvious."

"I am a trained professional," I said.

"You are a woman who has been carrying something for weeks and treating it like contraband. I smuggle for a living, Serah. I know contraband when I see it."

* * *

### Yara

Here is a trade secret: the hardest cargo I ever moved wasn't fae silver or unlicensed charms. It was the truth, carried up a mountain of my own making, in the dark, with no map and no guarantee the person at the other end wouldn't throw it back off the cliff.

I'd been rehearsing this conversation since Greywater. Since the moment Serah Dain put her body between mine and a crossbow bolt without hesitating — without *thinking*, which for her was the scandalous part — I had known two things with total certainty. One: I was falling in love with her. Two: the prophecy book probably required one of us to die, and I had no intention of finding out which of us the ink had chosen.

So naturally I said none of this. Instead I said, "Ask me whatever you've been not-asking," which is the coward's version of bravery, and waited.

She was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. Then she said, "In Vaelmoor, underwater. When you blacked out. I broke my orders to save you, and I've told myself since that it was tactical. That a living key is worth more than a drowned one." She turned her hands over, looked at her palms like they belonged to someone she used to know. "That was a lie. I want you to know it was a lie, because if tomorrow goes wrong, I don't want the last honest thing between us to be a lie."

My heart performed some maneuver that would have gotten it expelled from any respectable body.

"What was the true reason?" I asked, though I knew. Knowing and hearing are different cargoes.

"You know why."

"I want to hear it anyway. I smuggle truths, remember. I like them declared at the border."

She looked at me over the fire, and Serah Dain — who had faced down witch-finders, disgraced herself before a king, and dueled her own second-in-command without flinching — looked, for one bare moment, terrified.

"I stayed because it was you," she said. "Because somewhere between the ford and the chapel, keeping you alive stopped being duty and became the thing duty was standing in front of. Because I think I love you, Yara, and I have no idea what to do with that, because everything I have ever loved has either been taken from me or taken away as punishment, and I am very tired, and tomorrow we walk into the fae court, and the prophecy wants a death, and—" She stopped. Breathed. "There. Declared at the border. Inspect it or shoot me."

* * *

### Serah

The silence afterward lasted approximately one geological era.

Yara stared at me with an expression I couldn't read, which frightened me more than the witch-finders ever had, and I began composing my retreat — *I misjudged the moment, forget it, we should sleep, dawn comes early* — when they stood up, walked around the fire, and sat down beside me close enough that our shoulders touched.

"For the record," they said, "I've been carrying the same contraband since Greywater. I just had a better hiding place for it, because I've had a lifetime of practice hiding things." Their voice did something rough at the edges. "I love you too. I've loved you since you hesitated at the ford — do you know how rare hesitation is? Everyone else in my life acted immediately. My mentor acted immediately. King Aldric acts immediately. You *hesitated*. You let the world be complicated. I decided I wanted to be part of someone's complicated."

"That's a terrible foundation for a relationship," I managed.

"It's the only kind I build. Ask my pack straps."

I laughed. It came out cracked, half a sob wearing armor, and Yara leaned into me, and for a while neither of us said anything at all. The fire settled. Somewhere upslope, the crooked pines creaked in a wind that smelled like rain and honey, which meant the veil-thinning was getting worse, which meant tomorrow was going to be even worse than planned, and none of it mattered quite as much as the weight of another person choosing to stay.

Then, because I am who I am, I ruined it.

"The prophecy," I said.

Yara sighed against my shoulder. "You really are incapable of happiness lasting more than four minutes."

"One of us dies, Yara. We both read the margins. 'Two crowns, one neck' — the binding needs a life freely given. That's the old covenant's arithmetic. A life per century. Your mentor hid it from you, Aldric lost his brother to it, and now there's a prophecy with our names shaped into it and three shards left to gather." I pulled back so I could see their face. "I need to know where you stand. Before tomorrow. If it comes down to it — if the ritual demands what it demanded before — I need to know whether you'll try to pay alone. Because I've watched you do reckless generous things since the day I met you, and every single time, you did them alone, in secret, planning to apologize later. Not this. Not with this."

* * *

### Yara

And there it was. The border inspection I'd invited, catching exactly the cargo I'd hoped to slip past.

Because she was right. Of course she was right; she was always right in the way that made me want to throw things into the fire. I had already drafted the plan. The plan lived in the same locked room as everything else I'd ever hidden: I would go to the covenant-stone, I would give myself, I would leave Serah and Nyssara instructions and a heartfelt letter and absolutely no opportunity to argue, because arguing requires the sacrificer to be present, and I am, professionally speaking, excellent at not being present.

I looked at her face in the firelight — jaw set, eyes steady, waiting — and understood that if I lied to her now, I would be lying to the only person who had ever caught me.

So I told the truth instead. It felt like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was wearing.

"Yes," I said. "I was going to do it alone. I've been going to do everything alone since Maestra Ilse died, because alone is safe, because if no one knows the plan, no one gets hurt stopping it." I swallowed. "And yes, I was going to add you to the list of people I protect by deceiving, right next to every smuggler crew I've ever run with, and I hate that you saw it before I said it out loud."

"Thank you," she said simply.

"Don't thank me yet. Here's the rest of the truth." I took her hand. Her gauntlet was off — I don't know when she'd removed it, but her bare palm was warm and scarred and holding mine back like she meant it. "I don't accept the terms. Not the prophecy's, not the old covenant's. A life per century is not arithmetic, Serah, it's extortion with good handwriting. Ilse bound me into it before I could walk. Nobody asked me. Nobody asked Aldric's brother. Nobody asked the ones before. The whole system runs on people being brave in isolation — one hero, one altar, one generation, everyone else safely ignorant and grateful. And I am done being isolated." I heard my own voice go strange, fierce, the voice I'd had in Corvane when I burned the witch-finders' lines. "So here is my counteroffer. Together or not at all. If the crown demands a death, we refuse the crown. If it demands one of us specifically, we refuse *that too*, and we find a third road, even if we have to carve it through the veil with our teeth. I didn't survive twenty years of hiding to die obediently on schedule."

Serah was quiet. Then: "Defying a prophecy written in blood a century ago."

"Defying it together."

"With a disgraced knight and a disowned fae princess and no army."

"Now you're getting romantic."

Something loosened in her shoulders — something I suspect had been knotted since her knighthood broke. She lifted our joined hands and pressed her lips to my knuckles, right over the scar the book had described a hundred years before I earned it.

"Together or not at all," she said. "Sworn. And Yara — I don't swear lightly anymore. Ask the king."

"I intend to tell everyone who'll listen."

* * *

### Serah

We sat until the fire burned low, and I will record honestly, since honesty appears to be the theme I've been conscripted into: I slept better that night than I had since before Aldric took my rank. Perhaps because the heaviest thing I carry finally had company.

I woke once, deep in the small hours, to movement. Yara had taken second watch without waking me — typical — and sat wrapped in my spare cloak, turning the prophecy book over in their hands. In the dark, their face was unreadable, and for a moment I saw what the witch-finders must see: something old, something marked, something dangerous.

Then they noticed me watching, and the look cracked into that crooked half-smile, and they were just Yara again. Just mine, if I dared use that word.

"Penny for the grim thoughts," I murmured.

"They're not grim." They held up the book. The pages glowed faintly, as they sometimes did now, the closer we drew to the fae court. "The margins changed. Look."

I crawled over. In the space beneath the verse about two crowns and one neck — the verse that had read, every time before, of a single life freely given — new words had surfaced in the old parchment, in handwriting I recognized with a chill.

Maestra Ilse's handwriting. Fresh. As if she were writing it tonight, from wherever the dead keep their letters.

*If they refuse the terms,* it read, *the terms may be rewritten. Three to hold what one was meant to bear. Tell my child: I hid the price because I could not pay it twice. Let them forgive me by paying differently.*

Yara's hand trembled, just slightly. "Nyssara," they called softly, toward the tent. "Wake up. You need to see this."

Beyond the ridge, far to the south, the sky had begun to glow — not dawn. Dawn was hours off, and dawn didn't burn orange in a moving line. Witch-fire, marching north along the mountain roads, toward the court, toward the queen, toward all of us.

"So much for a head start," I said, and reached for my sword.
