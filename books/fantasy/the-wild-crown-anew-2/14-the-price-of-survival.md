## 14. The Price of Survival

The smoke found us before the news did.

We'd been three days in the high country above Greywater, following a smuggler's road that only existed if you already believed in it — which is to say, a road I knew and Serah did not, which is the only kind of road worth having when you're fugitives. Nyssara walked it like she was strolling through her mother's garden, which, given that the covenant's decay had made the Thornfell mountains about as stable as a drunkard's promise, said something either flattering or deeply alarming about fae nerves.

Then the wind turned, and brought us the smell.

"Burnt pitch," Serah said. Her hand went to her sword out of pure habit; there was nothing within forty miles worth cutting except trees, and the trees were doing fine on their own. "That's not wildfire."

"No," I said, and my stomach did something complicated and unpleasant. "Wildfire doesn't smell like a granary."

I've guided contraband through those passes since I was fourteen years old. I know every village that clings to the mountain's flanks like lichen — Hollowmere with its terraced barley, Ashkettle (poorly named even before this week), the weaving hamlets at Dunmarrow where old women still paid their debts in cloth and gossip. Those villages fed the smugglers' roads. The smugglers' roads fed the resistance. And Aldric's arithmetic, which I'd learned to dread from every margin-note my mentor ever wrote, ran simple as a butcher's ledger: starve the roads, and you starve everything that travels them.

"He's not chasing us anymore," Nyssara said quietly. She stood with her face into the wind, silver eyes narrowed against the smoke-haze, and for once she didn't look exiled so much as homesick for a world that was busy burning. "He's harvesting."

* * *

We came down into the valley of Hollowmere at dusk, and I will spare you the worst of what we saw, mostly because I have spent the years since trying to spare myself.

The vanguard had come through at dawn. Fast riders, fire-pots, no quarter asked or offered because none was needed — these were farmers, not soldiers. They'd fired the granaries first, then the byres, then the houses, working outward with the methodical patience of men following an order they could recite in their sleep. *Burn the stores. Drive them up-mountain. Winter does the rest.*

Some of the villagers had fled up the goat-tracks toward the high caves. Some hadn't made it. I counted neither group, because counting felt like agreeing to something, and I wasn't ready to agree.

What was left of Hollowmere's people had gathered in the ruins of the millpond meadow — maybe two hundred souls, maybe fewer, wrapped in blankets and each other, coughing smoke and staring at nothing. An old woman sat in the shallows of the pond itself, holding a scorched loom-shuttle like a baby. A boy of perhaps six kept asking his mother whether the stars were also on fire, and she kept saying no, and he kept asking, because children understand repetition is how you catch adults in a lie.

"There'll be more valleys like this," Serah said behind me. Her voice had gone flat, which I knew by now was how she handled things too large for any other shape. "Dunmarrow tomorrow. Ashkettle after. He's clearing the whole southern face of the range."

"I know the arithmetic," I said.

"Yara." She caught my shoulder. "Whatever you're calculating right now — stop."

But of course I didn't stop. That has never been among my talents.

Here is what I knew, laid out in my head with the cold clarity I usually reserved for mapping a pass at night: somewhere ahead — days away, at most a week — waited the covenant-stone, and the reforging, and the thing the prophecy had demanded of me since before I drew breath. Every scrap of lore Nyssara had pried loose from her mother, every marginal scrawl of my dead mentor's, agreed on one point: the binding would take everything I had. Not metaphorically. The ritual was a vessel poured out to its last drop, and I was the vessel. Whatever strength I carried into that stone circle was the exact measure of what I'd have to hold three shattered crown-shards together while the veil screamed around us.

And here is what else I knew: two hundred people were going to die of exposure and hunger on a mountainside in the next fortnight unless someone did something the mountains themselves no longer could. Because that was the other part of the covenant's collapse — the land was failing. Wells going bitter. Goats birthing dead kids. Barley coming up gray. The old bargain had been fraying for a century, and now it was tearing, and the Thornfell highlands had always lived closer to the veil than anywhere else. The magic that should have been in the soil, in the snowmelt, in the bones of the hills, was leaking out into nowhere.

Or rather — it was sitting, unspent, inside me. The living key. The vessel. Hoarded, per my mentor's design, against a single terrible hour.

"Yara," Serah said again, softer. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you've already decided something awful and you're waiting for us to notice."

Nyssara laughed, short and without humor. "She's known you four months and she reads you like weather."

"Someone has to," Serah said. "You read him like a riddle."

"And he reads himself like a debt," Nyssara said, and that landed harder than she probably intended, because it was true, and all three of us stood there in the smoke-smell while the boy asked his mother about the stars.

I want to be honest, since honesty is apparently the only currency I have left after everything: I tried to find a way around it. I genuinely did. I stood there doing sums like a merchant who suspects he's being cheated but can't prove where. Could I heal the land a little? No — the decay wasn't a wound, it was a *deficit*, and you can't salve a deficit. Could I move the villagers somewhere safer? Two hundred people over the pass in winter, past witch-finder patrols, with no food at the end of the road? I'd be relocating the dying, not preventing it. Could we raid Aldric's supply trains? Serah was already sketching that plan in the dirt, actually, and it was a good plan, and it fed maybe sixty mouths for a month while the rest of the range burned.

There was exactly one source of power on this mountain sufficient to make barren ground bear and bitter wells run sweet, and it was currently wearing my boots and pretending very hard to be a smuggler.

"You could ration it," Serah said at last, quietly, when I finally told her what I was thinking. We'd walked apart from the campfire, out to the edge of the millpond, where the water reflected a sky the color of a bruise. "Spend some now. Keep enough for the stone."

"Spend some now." I looked at my hands — at the scar across the knuckles of my left, the one a book written a century ago had known about before I did. "Serah, I don't know how to do that. Nobody's ever taught me. My mentor hid me my whole life precisely so I'd never have to learn. My entire magical education consists of hedge-tricks, panic, and one truly catastrophic evening in Corvane." I'd pulled down witch-fire over half a city that night. I remembered it as both the best and worst thing I have ever felt, which I suspect is the standard review.

"So we find out what 'some' means," she said. "Carefully. Together."

"Together," I repeated. It's strange what words do when you love someone. That one went through me like warm water through ice.

"It may not matter," Nyssara said, joining us at the pond's edge without sound, because fae. "The ritual needs *a* vessel emptied. If he pours himself out halfway now—"

"Then I'll arrive at the stone half-empty and we'll improvise." I heard my own voice and barely recognized it — steady, decided, already grieving. "Nyssara. Your mother held that court together for a hundred years under worse odds than mine. What did she always say about hoarding?"

Nyssara was quiet a long moment. "That a queen who saves her strength for the war will lose the peace," she said finally. "And that my grandmother said it first, and died proving it wrong, which is why we say it carefully in our family."

"Well," I said. "I'm not planning to prove anyone wrong. I'm planning to spend."

* * *

I won't pretend the working was elegant. Elegance is for mages with teachers.

I started at midnight, kneeling in the ruined barley field above Hollowmere, with Serah standing watch behind me and Nyssara circling the boundary of the rite, laying down what protections she could — thin ones; exile had cost her most of her own gifts, another bill the old covenant had never paid. The land around me was gray and silent in the way of a body that has stopped expecting food. When I pressed my palms flat against the frozen furrows and opened myself, what came back was not silence but *thirst*. A hundred miles of failing earth, all of it aware, all of it reaching.

Oh, I thought. Oh, that's what I am. Not a key. A well in a desert, and the desert knows.

The first thing I gave was memory-shaped, though the memories weren't mine — I understood that much instinctively. The magic wanted to flow as *pattern*, and pattern wants form, and the nearest forms to hand were the shapes of my life: my mentor's kitchen, the smell of ink and tallow; the first night I'd walked the smuggler's road alone and terrified and triumphant; Serah's face lit by witch-fire in Corvane; my own name, spoken by someone who loved me. I fed them into the ground like seed-grain and the ground took them and *turned*.

Barley doesn't grow in a night. But the frost lifted off the field like a held breath released. Beneath the burnt stubble, green stirred — not ripe, not harvest, but *alive*, a promise planted deep enough that spring would keep it. The millpond cleared. Somewhere uphill, a well that had gone bitter two winters ago ran sweet again, and I heard the crying start when the first family drank, and I heard it stop when they realized the crying was joy, and I filed both sounds away in the place where I keep things I'll need later.

It cost. Of course it cost. I'm not going to give you the mystical version where sacrifice feels clean. It felt like being bled. Like walking in winter without a coat, except the coat was years — I could feel them going, whole seasons of myself, decades maybe, pouring out through my hands into dirt that drank and drank. At one point I looked up at Serah and couldn't remember how long I'd known her, and the terror of that blank was worse than any pain, and then the memory swam back — the ford, the hesitation, her sword-arm dropping — and I nearly wept with relief in the middle of a miracle.

"Don't count," Serah told me, crouched at my side, her hand a solid weight between my shoulder blades. She'd figured out what was happening before I said it. Knight-captains are trained to watch for costs. "Don't try to tally it. Just breathe and pour."

"That's terrible financial advice," I managed.

"So arrest me."

Dawn found the field green-edged and the village fed from stores Nyssara had... acquired... from a smuggler cache only I had known about, which tells you how thoroughly my secrets had stopped being mine, and how little I minded. The boy who'd asked about the stars was eating barley porridge and complaining about it, which is the most hopeful sound a child can make.

I slept eleven hours and woke with white in my hair that hadn't been there before. One strand. Just one. I stared at it in the polished blade Serah lent me and thought, with the giddy clarity of the exhausted: *that's the interest payment.* The principal was still owed, and the final creditor was waiting at a covenant-stone somewhere north of here, and I had just spent money I could not earn back on ground that would feed strangers whose names I'd never learn.

I have never in my life made a purchase I was more certain of.

* * *

Ashkettle we reached two days later, and it was worse, because we were late.

Not by much. By hours. The vanguard had ridden through at midday and moved on toward Dunmarrow, leaving the usual signature: granaries first, byres second, homes third. But Ashkettle had fought back — old quarrymen, stubborn as their own slate — and the soldiers had answered in the manner of armies everywhere, which meant that when we crested the ridge at dusk, the village wasn't smoldering. It was *burning*, and people were still inside it.

I didn't deliberate this time. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, in whatever ledger the universe actually keeps: I did not stand on the ridge weighing prophecy against conscience like a moneylender. I ran down the hill already pulling the power up through me like drawing a bow.

What happened next, I experienced mostly from the outside, the way you experience a fever. I remember the fire bending — actually bending, a river of flame turning in its bed to look at me. I remember shouting words I didn't know I knew in a language that predated every language I speak, and meaning them completely. I remember walls of heat parting around running figures like a crowd making way. I remember lifting a collapsed roof-beam that should have required ten quarrymen, with magic that groaned through my skeleton like a ship in a storm. I remember a girl of maybe nine, pinned under rubble, looking up at me floating debris with absolutely no astonishment, and saying, "Are you the one from the story?" and me saying, "Which story?" and her saying, "All of them," and laughing, gods help me, laughing, as I pulled the beam off her.

I remember the cost arriving all at once when I let go.

I woke on my back in the trampled snow outside the village with Serah's face above mine and Nyssara's hand pressed hard over my heart, doing whatever exiled fae princesses do instead of CPR. My whole body felt like a bell that had been struck too hard — ringing, cracked, resonant with damage. When I tried to sit up, the world tilted politely and suggested otherwise.

"How many?" I croaked.

"Everyone we found," Serah said. "Thirty-one alive out of the buildings. Four dead. The rest had already run for the caves." Her voice was doing the flat thing again, but her eyes weren't; her eyes were wet and furious and proud all at once, which is a combination I've come to think of as specifically hers. "You held a burning village open like a door, Yara. For six minutes. I timed it."

"Show-off," I whispered.

"Your hair," Nyssara said, and turned a small hand-mirror toward me.

White. Not a strand this time — a wing of it, over my left ear, stark as gull-feather. I touched it and laughed, and the laugh hurt, and I kept laughing anyway, because the alternative was screaming and I'd budgeted my screams differently.

"Half-empty," I said. "Maybe less than half. Good to know the exchange rate."

Serah took the mirror out of my hand and set it down. "Stop joking about it."

"I'm not joking. I'm *coping*, there's a difference, it's subtler."

"*Yara.*"

"Serah." I caught her wrist. Up close, in the firelight of a village I'd half-killed myself saving, her face was doing something complicated, and I realized she wasn't angry — she was frightened, and frightened people reach for anger the way drowning men reach for driftwood. "Listen to me. I know what I'm spending. I chose it with my eyes open, both times, and I'd choose it again, and the reason I can joke is that I refuse to let this feel like a funeral in advance. If I walk to that stone hollowed out and grim, the prophecy wins twice. So I'm going to make jokes, and you're going to hate them, and that's the covenant *we* signed. Agreed?"

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she bent and kissed me — brief, fierce, tasting of smoke — and said against my mouth, "Agreed. But I get to be furious about it in writing later."

"Put it in the margins," I said. "I come from a tradition."

* * *

We buried the four dead at dawn and walked out of Ashkettle with the survivors streaming uphill toward the high caves, carrying what was left. Word had outrun us, as word does in the mountains — faster than any rider, because it travels mouth to mouth along roads no king can burn. By the time we reached the ridge above Dunmarrow valley, people were already waiting on the path. Not refugees this time. Petitioners. Farmers and weavers and one wandering friar, all of them looking at me the way people used to look at rain during a drought.

"They've heard," Serah murmured. "The green fields. The sweet wells. The white hair." She said the last with an edge, because she knew — we both knew — what crowds do with miracles. They don't thank them. They *need* them, endlessly, and need is a hunger no well fills.

"They're calling you something," Nyssara said. She'd heard it on the wind, which for her was literal. "In the villages. They have a name."

I closed my eyes. Behind them I saw the ledger, plain as day: the covenant-stone waiting, the three shards, the ritual that would take everything remaining. Half-spent already. Maybe less. And below me, a valley full of people about to ask me for water from a well that was running dry.

"What name?" I asked, though some traitorous part of me had already guessed.

Nyssara smiled, sad and sharp as winter sun. "*The Wellspring,*" she said. "They're calling you the Wellspring of Thornfell."

And far below, at the mouth of Dunmarrow valley, I saw the first orange pinpricks of torchlight that was not sunset — Aldric's riders, arriving on the far side, driving the village ahead of them like sheep toward a shepherd they hadn't met yet.

The well was half-dry. The desert was thirsty. And the whole mountain was watching to see what I'd pour next.
