## 15. Council of Ash

The keep they'd chosen for the council had been called Greystone once, before the war took its name along with its roof. Now it was just a shell of blackened granite with a courtyard full of mud and refugees and the particular smell of too many people cooking too little food. I had slept in worse places. That was not the compliment it sounded like.

I arrived a day early to check the sightlines, because old habits die hard and mine had died twice already this year without taking the hint. The leaders of the free cities would come by morning: Corvane's merchant-council in their dyed wool, Harrowgate's marshal with her mercenary escort, the river-towns' delegates smelling of fish and suspicion, and a dozen lesser voices who all wanted the same thing — to be told the world was fine — while dreading anyone who might actually tell them.

Yara came in at dusk, leaning on my arm more than either of us acknowledged.

Their voice had gone two weeks ago at Greywater, burned out screaming wards into being over a column of fleeing villagers while witch-finder fire chased them across three miles of open ground. What remained was a rasp like gravel dragged through smoke. The healers said it might come back. Yara had signed *it might not* at me with the flat expression of someone who had done that math long before the healers got there.

"You're limping," I said.

"You're hovering," they rasped.

"One of us has a job."

They sat down heavily on a bench in what used to be the great hall, beneath a ceiling open to the stars where the beams had burned away. Someone had strung canvas overhead. It flapped in the wind like a patient bird trying to decide whether to give up.

"Tomorrow," Yara croaked, "they're going to look at me like I'm a weapon someone else is holding."

"Yes."

"And you're going to stand behind me looking like a knight anyway."

"That's the other job."

Their mouth twitched — the ghost of the grin that had gotten me into this whole disaster in the first place, back at the ford, when duty had chafed and then torn clean through. They reached up and squeezed my wrist. Their hand was still cold. It had been cold since the refugee camps, since they poured out magic like water from a cracked jug and kept pouring past every sane limit.

"Get some sleep," I said.

"I'll sleep when the covenant stops dying."

"That could be never."

"Then we're both doomed." They closed their eyes. "Wake me if the sky does anything interesting."

I stood watch instead of sleeping, which was also an old habit, and watched the moon cross the broken ceiling, and thought about how strange it was that the fate of two realms now rested on a runaway smuggler's guide with no voice and a disgraced knight who couldn't remember which side she was on anymore. Then I decided strangeness was a luxury, and went to count the guard rotations instead.

* * *

They came with the fog.

Not literally — though I half expected Aldric's spies to manage it — but the morning mist off the mountains rolled through the shattered gates and pooled around the ankles of the delegations as they filed in, so that the great hall looked like a gathering of ghosts arguing about grain tariffs. Which, honestly, was not far wrong.

I counted eleven seats at the trestle table the carpenters had thrown together. Eleven leaders, or near enough; three of the cities had sent seconds because their firsts were dead or besieged. At the head of the table, a chair had been left conspicuously empty, because nobody wanted to sit closest to the prophesied vessel and nobody wanted to be seen sitting farthest, either. Politics is mostly furniture.

Nyssara stood apart, by the east wall, hood down. She had insisted on showing her face. *If they cannot bear to look at me,* she'd said, *they cannot bear to hear me.* Her silver-grey skin caught the grey light, and I watched three separate delegates decide not to stare and fail completely.

Yara stood at the table's end. I stood behind them, one pace back and left, where a bodyguard stands, and tried very hard to feel like anything other than a woman who had fallen in love with a sacrifice.

Marshal Ondrey of Harrowgate opened proceedings, because soldiers always do. "We've lost four hundred souls at Greywater," she said, without preamble. "The crossing holds, but Aldric's main host is a week behind his vanguard, and his vanguard burns everything between here and the river. So before we discuss prophecy—" her eyes flicked to Yara, cool and measuring, "—I'd like to know what we're actually defending."

"The land itself," Yara rasped.

It was worse than a whisper. Half the hall leaned forward to catch it.

"The covenant is failing. You've seen it. Wells going bitter. Orchards fruiting ash. The Thornfell passes rewriting themselves — my own roads, roads I've walked since I was twelve, coming out places they've never gone. That's the veil rotting. When it goes fully, the wild magic underneath comes through, and it doesn't come through gently."

Delegate Vasco of Corvane — silk, rings, a face like a closed ledger — folded his hands. "The old covenant held for three centuries. Under kings who honored it. Perhaps the problem is not the covenant but the current management."

"Aldric intends to seize the shards by force," I said. "He believes power can substitute for bargain. The last man who tried that experiment was named Vaelric the Hollow, and the province he ruled is now a lake with a chapel in it."

"Colorful," said Vasco. "Unverified."

"I was there," Yara rasped. "Under the lake."

That bought a silence. Good. Silence was currency in rooms like this; spend it carefully.

Ondrey leaned forward. "Here's my problem, Villalobos. You're asking us to bet everything — armies, harvests, cities — on breaking a covenant that has, whatever its costs, kept the fae beyond the veil and the fields green for three hundred years. And your alternative is a new covenant, untested, designed by you, sealed by you." She tapped the table once. "Every prophet in history promised the new thing would be better. Give me proof the old one can even be broken without ending the world. Because if it can't, we're better off fighting Aldric alone and letting the fae fend for themselves."

There it was. The question under every question. Yara had known it was coming; we'd rehearsed answers on the road for two days, their handwriting scratchy in the margins of the almanac, mine read aloud when their throat gave out. But rehearsal is rehearsal, and there is a difference between a speech in a barn and a speech in front of eleven people who hold your life in their ledgers.

Yara drew breath — and stopped.

I saw it happen. The rasp caught on something, some scar tissue in the throat, and the sentence died, and for one horrible moment the prophesied vessel of two realms stood silent in front of the council with nothing but a ruined voice and everyone watching.

Nyssara moved first. Of course she did.

"Let me answer instead," she said, stepping from the wall. "Since the question is whether the old covenant can break safely, and I am what happens when it breaks slowly."

Vasco sneered. "A demonstration. From one of *them*."

"No." Nyssara pulled back her sleeve. "From the land. Look at my arm, Delegate. Fae flesh remembers the covenant's health the way your ledger remembers interest. Three years ago this was unmarked." Her forearm, silver-pale, was veined through with lines of dull black, like frost damage in living wood. "This year's growth." She turned her arm slowly, and the murmur that ran the length of the table was ugly and human. "My mother carries the same marks. My mother, who is immortal, is being *aged* by a dying treaty. The covenant isn't failing everywhere at once — it's failing from the edges inward. Which means it can be measured. Which means it can be shown." She looked down the table, and there was iron under the grief in her voice. "You want proof. Come outside."

* * *

We walked them out into the keep's dead garden.

It had been a kitchen garden, once — the refugees had stripped it bare months ago, and nothing had grown since, which everyone had blamed on trampling and autumn. Nyssara knelt at the edge of a raised bed, pressed her black-veined hand flat against the soil, and spoke a word in her mother's tongue that I felt in my teeth rather than heard.

The ground answered.

Not with growth — with *memory*. A shimmer ran through the dead beds, faint as heat-haze, showing what the soil remembered: green rows, the ghost-shapes of cabbages and beans, a gardener's shadow bending. Then the image curdled. The green went grey at the edges, the way fruit goes, and where the veil's rot touched, the memory simply *stopped*, cut off mid-row, as if a page had been torn out of the earth's book.

"The decay follows the ley-lines," Nyssara said quietly, rising. "Which follow the old covenant-stones. Every stone still standing is a knot of health. Everything between them starves." She gestured south, toward the horizon and Aldric's army. "His vanguard is burning villages along the line of the stones. Whether he knows it or not — and his witch-finders know it — he is accelerating the collapse deliberately. A starving land is easier to rule."

"So it dies slower or faster," said Ondrey flatly. "That's not proof it can be *rebuilt*."

"No," said a voice like gravel, and Yara stepped forward.

I wanted to stop them. The healers had been explicit. But they caught my eye — just a flicker, *let me* — and I let them, which may have been the hardest military decision of my career.

Yara held out their hand, palm up. And for the first time since Corvane, since the witch-fire, they let the council see what was left of their magic.

It wasn't much. That was the point, maybe. A thread of light, thin as spider-silk, trembling above their palm — guttering, spent, a candle-end of what had once flattened witch-finder columns. The council stared at it the way people stare at a wounded soldier: pity and fear in equal measure.

"This is what's left of me," Yara rasped, each word costing visibly. "I spent most of what I am keeping your people alive. So understand — I'm not here to sell you a miracle. I'm here because I've read the original terms. All of them. The covenant's price is a life freely given, every century, and it doesn't ask. It takes whoever fits. This time, that's me. I didn't agree to it. I was bound before I could walk." A pause; they steadied against the table's edge. "Your proof that it can be broken safely? There isn't any. There's only proof it *can't* continue. In fifty years the stones fail on their own, unmanaged, unwitnessed, and the wild magic comes through with no one ready. Breaking it on our terms — shared, deliberate, paid in power instead of lives — is the only version of this where anyone gets a choice."

Silence again. The canvas flapped overhead.

It was Vasco who broke it, and to my lasting surprise, not with contempt. "And if we refuse? If we fight Aldric alone?"

"Then you fight him for a kingdom that will not feed your armies within the decade," Nyssara said. "And my mother's court falls, and whatever comes through the tear afterwards will not negotiate tariffs."

More murmuring. Ondrey drummed two fingers on the table, staring at Yara's guttering thread of light with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"Shared," she said finally. "You said paid in power, *shared*. Shared how?"

Yara looked back at me. Just for a heartbeat. Then at Nyssara. Then at the council.

"Three hands," they rasped. "Mine. Serah Dain's. Nyssara's. The crown reforged as a covenant, not a throne — its burden divided so no single soul pays it entire. That's the design. It's never been tried. I won't pretend otherwise."

"Three mortals and a fae princess holding up the sky," Vasco murmured. "My colleagues will call it madness."

"Your colleagues," said Ondrey, "are watching their orchards turn to ash. Madness is relative." She pushed back from the table and stood, and every eye in the ruin followed her. "I've commanded soldiers twenty-two years. I've learned one thing about gambits: never bet on the plan, bet on the planner. I don't know if this child of Thornfell can do what they claim." She looked at Yara — really looked, past the prophecy and the ruined voice. "But Aldric's plan requires them dead, and mine requires them alive, and between those two options I find I prefer the company."

She extended her hand. Yara took it. Somewhere behind me, I realized I'd been holding my breath since dawn.

It did not carry the room — councils are not carried, they are eroded — but it shifted the weight. Vasco hemmed and committed "provisionally, pending Corvane's ratification." The river-towns followed Harrowgate because their barges needed Harrowgate's mercenaries. By the time the fog burned off, we had something no one would call an alliance and everyone would privately admit was the best available: a majority, grudging and frightened and armed.

Afterward, in the emptying hall, Yara sat down on the bench and shook. I put a cloak around their shoulders and crouched in front of them.

"You shouldn't have spoken," I said.

"No," they agreed, rasping almost to nothing. "Worth it, though."

"Worth it," I confirmed, and kissed their cold fingers, and pretended not to see Nyssara pretending not to watch.

It was Nyssara, though, who ended the day badly. She came back through the gates at dusk at a run — a fae running is a bad omen all by itself — with her hood blown back and terror plain on her marked face.

"The northern stone," she gasped. "The last standing knot on the Thornfell line. Aldric's witch-finders reached it an hour ago. They're not burning it, Serah." Her black-veined hand closed on my bracer. "They're *unbinding* it. He's not waiting for the ritual anymore. He's tearing the covenant open right now, tonight, and everything between here and the veil is going to come apart with it."

Yara rose from the bench without a word, without a voice to spend, and reached for their boots.
