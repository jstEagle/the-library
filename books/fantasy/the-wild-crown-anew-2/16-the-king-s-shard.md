## 16. The King's Shard

There are certain advantages to being a usurper that the histories never mention. The chief among them, I have found, is that no one expects you to sleep well.

Kings born to their crowns toss and turn out of guilt, or boredom, or an inherited melancholy they can blame on their fathers. But a man who *took* a throne — who climbed over the bodies of better-credentialed men to reach it — is permitted a certain clarity. I earned this chair the way a mason earns a wall: stone by stone, mortar by mortar, with bleeding knuckles and no illusions about what holds it up. So when I could not sleep, I did not trouble myself with conscience. I sat by the window of the high tower instead, watched the moon do its slow arithmetic over Greywater, and listened to the ring whisper.

It always whispers after midnight. I have come to think of it as a colleague keeping poor hours.

*They are gathering them,* it said tonight, in that voice like water finding cracks in old stone. *The vessel. The exile. The knight who forgot her leash.*

"I know," I told it.

*You know nothing,* said the shard, which is the ring's great flaw as a conversationalist. It has been fused to my signet for three years now — the sorcerers called it a bonding, though from the screaming involved I'd have chosen a different word — and in all that time it has never once flattered me. It only states what it sees, the way a mirror states your face back at you whether you've shaved or not.

I turned it on my finger. In the dark it threw a faint amber light across the maps spread on my desk, catching the borders of Thornfell like embers caught in paper. Three shards, the scholars say, sundered a century ago when the last covenant was sealed. One drowned beneath Vaelmoor. One locked behind the fae queen's ribs, figuratively speaking. And one — the one that mattered, the keystone, the piece that remembers what the crown was *for* — sitting here on the hand of a king nobody crowned.

"You are being dramatic," I told the ring. "They have two shards and no ritual. They have a hedge-witch who can barely sign her name to her own prophecy."

*She signed it a hundred years before she was born.*

"Then she is punctual," I said, "if nothing else."

The ring laughed. It does that sometimes, a sound like ice shifting in a river, and I have never decided whether it mocks me or agrees with me. With the shard, I suspect those are the same thing.

* *

Let me tell you about my brother, because no one else will, and because the ring insists.

His name was Tomas. He would be forty-one this year if covenants kept honest books. When we were boys in our father's minor house — minor enough to matter to no one, which meant we were free in all the ways that didn't feed us — Tomas used to stand at the fence line where our land met the Thornfell foothills and swear he could hear the mountains talking. "They're arguing," he'd say, deadly serious, nine years old and grave as a bishop. "About us. About whether we get to stay."

I told him he was an idiot. I told him mountains don't talk. I was twelve and already learning the first lesson of power, which is that anything you cannot control must be dismissed.

Tomas grew up soft-spoken and strange, the way second sons of poor houses do when they're not needed. While I drilled with the militia and learned ledgers and learned which magistrates could be bought and which merely rented, Tomas wandered. He learned herb-craft from a widow up the valley. He learned the old road-songs. He learned, though neither of us knew it then, enough of the hedge-magic that runs thin and wild through the borderlands to make himself useful to people who dealt across the veil.

When the century turned and the covenant came due — when the fae came down from Thornfell with their cold courtesy and their ledger of names — Tomas put his own name forward.

You should understand what that meant, then. Every hundred years, the covenant between the free cities and Thornfell required a life freely given. Not taken. Never taken; that was the whole point, the elegant horror of the thing. It had to be *offered*, willingly, by someone who understood the price. A tithe of blood to keep two realms from grinding against each other like millstones without grain. Everyone knew it. No one spoke of it. The cities held lotteries in bad decades and found volunteers in worse ones, and the priests dressed it up in language about honor and harvest until even the bereaved could pretend it was something noble.

Tomas volunteered at twenty-three. He kissed our mother's cheek, shook my hand like we were business associates concluding a satisfactory trade, and walked into the veil with two fae wardens and never came back. They gave our family a plaque. A *plaque*. Bronze, the size of a dinner plate, with a wheat sheaf on it and some verse about the mountain remembering.

I had it melted down for sword-fittings. That was the first thing I ever destroyed out of grief rather than ambition, and I remember being surprised at how little it helped.

Here is what I did not understand at the time, and what I have spent twenty years not saying aloud: Tomas did not volunteer out of nobility. I learned this later, from a smuggler I tortured for information about Thornfell's roads — one of the few interrogations I regret, and not for the reasons you'd assume. The smuggler, dying, laughed at me and said: *Your brother? He heard them too. Heard them every night through that fence. They were calling for someone to come argue with them, boy. He wasn't sacrificing himself. He was answering an invitation.*

My brother hadn't given his life to save the world. He'd given it because the mountains asked, and he was the sort of man who answered when the mountains asked, and the covenant simply provided the paperwork.

I have built everything since on the principle that paperwork should never again decide who dies.

* *

"You brood magnificently, sire. Truly. If tyranny fails you, there's a career in theater."

Veyra, my spymaster, stood in the doorway with a lamp and a decanter, which is how she announces that whatever she's about to say will require drinking. She is the only person in the capital who enters my presence uninvited, a privilege I granted her years ago and have twice tried to revoke. She ignores me both times with the serene confidence of a woman who knows exactly how many bodies I'd need buried to replace her.

"The hour is late," I said.

"The hour is precisely why I'm here. Bad news keeps better after midnight. It ferments." She poured two cups without asking, set one within my reach, and settled into the chair across from my desk like a crow claiming a branch. "Greywater went poorly."

"Greywater went *adequately*. We lost a third of the vanguard and gained intelligence worth triple that. Serah Dain has chosen her side at last, openly, which simplifies matters — I'd wondered if she'd waffle indefinitely." I turned the ring slowly. Its amber light pulsed, faintly, like something breathing under cloth. "And the resistance won a battle they cannot afford to follow up on. Victories that exhaust the victor are gifts to the patient."

"Mm," said Veyra, in the tone of a woman filing my analysis under *charming self-deception*. "And the girl?"

"The vessel."

"The girl, sire. She burned out half the witch-finder company at Corvane. She pulled refugees out of a firestorm on the march south. My sources in the camp say she can barely walk, but the soldiers touch her shadow for luck. You can kill a mage. You cannot easily kill a *story*, and she is becoming a story faster than I can counter-narrate." She sipped. "Also, and I raise this delicately: the fae princess travels with her. Nyssara. Whose mother sits in my cells below this very tower, wearing a collar of cold iron and cooperating beautifully, and whose daughter now carries a crown-shard into territory I don't control. Do you see where the arithmetic is failing?"

"I see that you enjoy telling me my arithmetic is failing."

"It's the best part of the job." But her eyes stayed flat and serious over the cup rim, and Veyra's eyes are the truest instruments in my kingdom. "Sire. I have served you eleven years. I found you the ring. I found the sorcerers who bonded it. I have never once told you a plan was impossible, because nothing is impossible, only expensive. So believe me when I price this one." She leaned forward. "The prophecy cannot be controlled. I have read the recovered pages. I have interviewed three archivists and one very frightened oracle. The book doesn't predict, it *insists*. Everything written in it comes true — but never the way the reader intends. The last man who tried to steer it was your predecessor's grandfather, and his dynasty ended in a cellar with everyone else's. You are holding a shard of a broken crown and calling it leverage. It is holding *you*, and calling you a hinge."

For a long moment the only sound was the ring's whispering, which had risen just past the threshold of hearing, the way it always does when someone speaks truths near it. Displeased. Or hungry. With the shard, again, the distinction blurs.

"You think I'm being ruled by it," I said.

"I think," Veyra said, "that grief makes excellent armor and terrible architecture. I watched you build this whole campaign on the bones of a dead boy, sire, and I have never had the cruelty to point at the foundation before. But the ground is shaking now, so." She stood, collected the decanter, and paused at the door. "Offer the parley. Buy the girl's surrender if you can — she may even come willingly, they usually do, the noble ones, it's practically a disease. And when you have all three shards and the ritual in hand, do me one favor."

"Name it."

"Read the fine print. All of it. Even the parts that flatter you. *Especially* the parts that flatter you." She left the way she always does, without waiting for dismissal, trailing lamp-smoke and the sense of having lost an argument I hadn't known we were having.

* *

Alone again, I did what I always do at this hour. I took off the ring.

Not many would understand the risk. The bond is supposed to be permanent; the sorcerers swore the shard would burn the hand that tried to part from it. What they didn't know — what I paid a great deal to learn quietly — is that the binding loosens when the wearer *wants* to let go. The shard feeds on appetite, on the wanting of things. A man who wants nothing is invisible to it. For perhaps ten minutes each night, I sit with the ring on the desk before me and want nothing at all, and in that narrow country the whispers stop.

It is the closest thing I have to prayer.

Tonight the ring sat in its little pool of amber light, sulking, and I looked at it and thought about Tomas's hands. He had our mother's hands, long-fingered, always stained with ink or leaf-bruise. Mine are square and scarred, a workman's hands, fit for holding tools or throats. When the wardens took him through the veil I shook one of those hands, and I have thought often, in the twenty years since, about whether I could have gripped harder. Whether a tighter hold might have changed anything. Whether the entire edifice of my reign — the armies, the witch-finders, the careful brutal arithmetic of feeding two realms on the backs of one — is just a grown man trying to grip a ghost's hand hard enough to keep it.

*Yes,* says the ring, when it speaks of him, which is rare, and gentler than it has any right to be. *That is exactly what this is.*

"Be quiet," I told it, wanting nothing, wanting everything.

Because here is the truth I will say to no living soul, not even Veyra, especially not Veyra: I do not actually care about ruling Thornfell. The fae realm is cold, beautiful, and largely useless, a larder full of things that poison you politely. Let the exiled princess keep it. What I care about is the mechanism. The covenant took my brother because a hundred-year-old contract said so, and no one — not the priests, not the kings, not the mountains themselves — thought to ask anyone's permission except the victim's, and the victim was *twenty-three and heard voices in fences.* A system that runs on the willing sacrifice of boys is not sacred. It is a machine, and machines can be seized.

If I hold all three shards, I hold the machine. No more tithes. No more lotteries. No more mothers receiving plaques. Just order — mine — imposed on both sides of the veil, permanent and accountable to no ancient text. They call it tyranny. I have read the histories of what the realms do without it: a century of quiet, then a tithe, then another, forever, the wheel turning on the same soft-spoken boys with ink-stained hands.

The vessel — Yara Villalobos — thinks she is defying fate by refusing the prophecy. I almost admire it. But she mistakes the shape of the trap. There is no version of this where no one pays. The covenant is failing *now*; the land is souring, the veil is tearing, the wild magic is leaking through like bilge-water. Someone must hold the crown together, and the only question the age actually offers is *whose hands.*

Hers, guided by a dead mentor's margins and a disgraced knight's conscience?

Or mine — a man who has already paid the price once, and knows to the copper what these bargains cost.

I picked the ring back up. The whisper flooded in warm and satisfied, and I let it, because tomorrow I send terms to the resistance: peace, the shard, the ending of the hunt — all of it, in exchange for Yara Villalobos, delivered to the covenant-stone alive and willing. They will rage. The knight will rage hardest. And somewhere in that camp, a tired girl with a scorched voice and a savior's sickness will look at the ruins around her and begin, terribly, to agree.

The ring hummed against my finger like a held note.

*He would have liked her,* it said of Tomas, unbidden, cruel as only memory is. *The boy who answered mountains, and the girl who argues with books. Cut from the same stubborn cloth.*

"Yes," I said to the empty tower, and did not sleep, and did not want to want anything, and failed completely. "That is rather what frightens me."

Far to the south, in the hills above Greywater, a parley-banner was already being sewn. I could feel it the way Tomas once felt the mountains talking — as an argument, gathering, about whether we get to stay.

I intend to win the argument this time.

Whatever it costs.

Whoever it costs.

The ring whispered its approval, and outside my window, the moon finished its arithmetic and left the answer blank.
