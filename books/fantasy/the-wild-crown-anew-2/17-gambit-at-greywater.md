## 17. Gambit at Greywater

The morning of the ambush, I woke before dawn with the specific calm of a woman about to do something unforgivably stupid.

It's a familiar calm. Twenty years soldiering teaches you to recognize it — that strange stillness in the chest where fear should be, like a room swept clean for guests you'd rather not admit. I lay on my bedroll under a hawthorn hedge and listened to the Greywater running high and brown in the dark, and I inventoried my sins in advance so there would be no surprises later.

Sin one: I was about to lead an army of farmers, deserters, and two hundred fae irregulars against the finest host King Aldric had ever fielded. Sin two: I had no rank, no banner, and legally speaking no right to command anything larger than a kitchen. Sin three — the one I kept circling back to, the way a tongue worries a broken tooth — was asleep twenty feet away, breathing slow and even, with their hand curled around a knife even in sleep, because Yara Villalobos trusted nothing anymore, least of all dreams.

"You're doing the list again," Yara said, without opening their eyes.

"I'm reviewing strategy."

"You're doing the list. You get this little line here." They touched the space between their own brows. "Like a cat deciding whether to knock something off a table."

"Go back to sleep."

"Can't. My heart's going like a drummer boy." They sat up, hair a dark ruin, and looked at me across the grey half-light. Something in their face had changed since Corvane, since the witch-fire, since the council of ash. Harder at the edges. Older. "Ask me the question."

I knew which question. We both did. The night before the hardest leg, we had made each other promises by firelight — *together or not at all* — and this morning was the hardest leg. But asking it aloud would make it real in a way I wasn't ready for, so instead I said: "Stay behind the second line. That's not a request."

"See, this is why nobody likes knights."

"Everyone likes knights. It's me specifically nobody likes."

That got the ghost of a smile out of them, which had been the entire purpose, and then there was nothing left to do but stand up, buckle on what armor we had, and go murder a morning.

* * *

Greywater crossing earned its name honestly. The river came down out of the high fells milky with stone-dust, wide and shallow over a gravel bed, spanned by a bridge old enough that the free cities' grandmothers claimed it had been fae-built. Aldric's vanguard — four thousand men, supply train, siege waggons, and the man himself — had to cross there or lose three days skirting the marsh. Three days mattered. Every day the covenant failed worse, the land soured further, and Aldric's soldiers ate a little less. He couldn't afford to skirt anything. He had to come through the water.

So we let him start coming through.

The plan was Vess's, mostly — Vess being the smuggler-captain who'd been running Yara's contraband through Thornfell for years and who had opinions about terrain the way bishops have opinions about sin. Let the vanguard commit to the ford, she said. Let the middle pile up on the bridge, because armies are like bowels and block themselves if you wait long enough. Then drop the weir upstream, flood the gravel shallows, and hit the stuck half from both banks while the river does the arithmetic for you.

"It's not honorable," she'd told the war council, to a room full of people who mostly weren't soldiers and therefore nodded along.

"No," I'd agreed. "It's better than honorable. Honorable is how the last three wars were lost."

Nyssara had said very little. She'd been saying very little for days — watching her mother's shard ride in its wrappings against Yara's chest, watching the map, watching me and Yara pretend we hadn't been seen holding hands. Fae don't blush, exactly. They do something with stillness that amounts to the same thing. She'd volunteered for the east bank with her irregulars, and I remembered thinking: good, keep her away from the thick of it. I remember thinking that. I want it on the record somewhere, in whatever ledger they keep for women like me, that I thought *keep her safe*, as though safety were something any of us could still arrange for anybody.

Dawn came up the colour of dirty pewter. And downriver, right on schedule, the banners came too.

Aldric's host moved well. I'll give them that. Even half-starved, even marching through country that had begun to whisper — hedges blooming black roses out of season, birds flying in shapes no bird should — they came on in column with their shields slung and their silence disciplined. At their head rode the vanguard commander, and my stomach dropped through the gravel when I saw the device on his standard.

Marek Vosse. My second-in-command, once. The best officer I ever trained, and the reason I say *once* is that he stood in the square at Corvane and read out my disgrace while looking me in the eye, because he believed the king's law was worth more than my friendship, and the worst part is that he might even have been right.

"Marek," I said, to nobody, from four hundred yards and a hedgerow away.

Beside me, Vess spat into the river. "Friend of yours?"

"My student."

"That's worse."

"Yes," I said. "It usually is."

* * *

He committed the way I taught him to commit — scouts first, then a screen of crossbows, then the main weight into the ford with the bridge taking the baggage. Textbook. My textbook. There is a particular misery in watching your own handwriting march toward you armed.

We let the first three companies reach the far gravel. We let the fourth and fifth jam onto the bridge behind them, waggons axle to axle. Marek's head turned constantly, checking his flanks, checking the reed-beds — and finding what we'd arranged him to find, which was nothing. Nyssara's fae irregulars can lie against landscape the way mist lies against a lake. Even I couldn't have found them, and I knew where to look.

Then Vess's engineers cut the weir ropes, and the Greywater remembered it used to be a mountain stream.

I've heard men describe floods poetically. Those men have never stood on a bank when one comes. The river rose four feet in a breath and took the shallows back, and the companies in the ford suddenly learned what armour weighed, and the bridge became a scream with a roadway through it. And on both banks, on my signal — a horn, cracked and ugly, blown by a fifteen-year-old deserter with better lungs than sense — the resistance came up out of the ground like a bad conscience.

I won't dress up what happened next. Battles are not duels with extras; they are butchery with geometry, and our geometry was better. Arrows off the west bank into the packed bridge. Pikes at the waterline where the flooded men floundered. The fae irregulars hitting the rear guard out of the reeds with those long thin blades of theirs, silent as bookkeeping. Within the hour, the vanguard was two halves of a broken thing, and neither half could help the other, and the river carried the arithmetic away brown.

And through all of it, one thread ran straight and true down the east road, past the fighting, toward the royal pavilion pitched on the rise with the king's standard hanging dead in the wet air.

Yara went down that thread like a dropped stitch, hood up, magic banked to embers, moving the way they'd once moved contraband through these same mountains — low, quick, apologetic to no one. I watched them go from my position on the west bank and hated every yard of it, because *stay behind the second line* had lasted approximately as long as most of my orders around Yara Villalobos, which is to say not at all, and because the plan required it, and because plans are just the lies we tell ourselves in advance.

Which left me the other thread. The one with Marek Vosse in it.

He'd rallied perhaps two hundred men on the east bank, back against the willows, shield-wall set properly because I'd set it that way for six years. Someone had to crack it before it rolled up Nyssara's flank, and everyone on our side knew who someone was, and I was already swinging onto a horse before I finished admitting it to myself.

"Captain Dain," one of Vess's people said, using the old title like a bandage.

"Not captain," I said. "Just a disgraced woman with a grudge and good footing."

I rode at the wall's left hinge, where a waggon wreck made the shields bunch, and I will confess — because I've decided to spend my remaining honesty where it costs — that part of me sang. Not the killing part. Never that, whatever my enemies say. The part where everything simplifies. Where there is a problem with a shape, and your body knows the shape, and the world narrows to a lane of it. Six years of committees and quartermasters fell off me like a cloak, and I was only a sword with a woman attached, and the woman attached was angry.

The hinge broke. Walls do, at hinges. And through the gap came Marek.

He saw me and stopped — actually stopped, in the middle of a battle, which tells you either how much discipline he had or how much history. His visor was up. He'd always fought with the visor up; I'd told him once it would kill him, and he'd said, *then I'll die seeing what kills me*, and I'd loved him for it the way you love a younger brother who insists on learning things the hard way.

"Serah." My name in his mouth sounded like an accusation filed in triplicate.

"Marek."

"You're on the wrong side of this wall."

"I'm on the side that isn't burning villages."

"The villages sheltered a vessel of prophecy—" He caught himself. Even now, he didn't like the witch-finders' vocabulary. "You know what Aldric does to captured fae. You know what the covenant failing does to the harvests. Order matters, Serah. Order is the only thing between ten thousand people and eating each other."

"And when the order is the thing starving them?"

"Then you fix the order!" For a moment he was nineteen years old again, arguing doctrine with me over cheap wine. Then his face closed, because the moment deserved closing. "You were the best of us. That's what makes this treason and not just failure."

"Careful," I said. "That's nearly a compliment. Draw."

He drew. Of course he drew. He was my student.

* * *

I have fought perhaps forty serious duels and I remember three, and this is now one of them, so forgive me if I give it more space than it strictly earns.

Marek fights — fought? No. Fights; I'll come to that — Marek fights the way I taught him, which means he opens conservative, gives ground on the outside lines, and waits for you to grow impatient with his economy. So I gave him impatience I didn't feel, pressed high-left where his shield sat heavy, felt him take the bait and counter at my ribs — good, textbook, *my* textbook — and stepped inside the counter the way I'd never put in any book, the way you only learn from getting hit by it yourself, which he had done to me twice, years ago, in a training yard with the rain coming down.

My point took him under the arm, above the plate, in the gap. The gap I had shown him, once, laughing, saying *never leave me this opening, Marek, or someone crueler than me will find it.*

He folded. Went down in the willow-mud among the dead of his own wall, and his men broke around us like water around a stone, because that is what walls do when the hinge fails, and I stood over him with my sword shaking and my whole body screaming at me to finish the arithmetic.

His visor was still up. He was still seeing what killed him.

"Do it," he said. Blood at his teeth. "It's clean. One of us should come out of this clean."

"You think dying makes you clean?"

"I think you were right." He laughed, horribly, wetly. "About the order. About — all of it. I read the reports from Thornfell, Serah. What's happening to the land. What the witch-finders do when nobody's watching. I know what I've been holding the door open for." His hand found my wrist, gauntlet to gauntlet, grip going slack. "Finish it. Or get out of my sight. Either way — don't stand there letting me see your face do that."

So I got out of his sight. I called for stretcher-bearers — ours, his, whoever — and left him cursing me with the last of his voice, and walked back up the bank into the noise, and did not look at my hands until much later, and when I did, they were steady, which was somehow worse.

The battle, meanwhile, had stopped being a battle. When a vanguard breaks within sight of the king, the king's guard stops being a guard and becomes an escort, and escorts are judged by one metric only. By mid-morning the Greywater belonged to the resistance, the bridge belonged to the crows, and the pavilion on the rise —

The pavilion on the rise was empty.

* * *

I found Yara standing in the wreckage of Aldric's quarters, turning a wine cup over in their fingers like it might confess.

"He knew," they said, before I'd even cleared the tent flap. Their voice had that scraped-flat quality it got after they used power. Except they hadn't used power. I'd have felt it. "He knew, Serah. Look."

The camp was struck with drill-ground efficiency, but not hurriedly. Tents furled and loaded. The mess fires banked, not scattered. A decoy standard left flying over an empty command post, staff planted in a barrel of sand — and the barrel was old, weathered, positioned days ago. Aldric hadn't fled the battle. He'd never intended to fight it.

"He fed us the vanguard," I said slowly. The shape of it assembled itself in my gut with a horrible click. "Four thousand men, Marek's wall, the whole crossing — it was the price of getting our entire force committed to one place, looking one direction."

"While the main host goes around by the marsh causeway." Yara set the cup down with terrible gentleness. "Three days' detour, he loses. Half a day, with a guide who knows the causeway drains at low water — he doesn't. He was never trying to win Greywater. He was buying time to reach Corvane before we do. Before anyone does."

"To reach—"

The horn sounded then. Two notes, high, urgent — Vess's signal for *rider coming under parley flag*, which in a week of hard campaigning had meant everything from surrender offers to a lost mule. We went out into the trampled mud together, and the crowd parted the way crowds part around bad news, and I saw the rider clearly.

One rider. Aldric's colours, but not Aldric. A herald, young, white-faced, riding a horse that had been ridden too hard, with something draped across his saddlebow that made the crowd's murmur die all at once, the way a candle dies.

Nyssara.

Unconscious or sleeping — fae sleep like the dead when spelled — her silver hair hanging down into the mud, her wrists bound with iron wire, and wrapped around the wire a strip of parchment that I could read from thirty feet because the seal on it was bigger than decency.

Yara made a sound I had never heard them make. Small. Like a door closing in an empty house.

The herald dismounted, held up the parchment, and read in a voice trained for parade grounds:

*"To the rebels assembled at Greywater. Greeting. The bearer of the crown-shards will present herself — himself — itself, whichever applies — at the covenant-stone of Thornfell within nine days, unarmed, unaccompanied, and willing. In exchange, the hostage lives, the prisoner-queen lives, and this army does not burn Corvane, whose walls you know I can burn. Fail, and I will send you the pieces I cannot use. Signed, Aldric, rightful King of the Free Cities and, shortly, of considerably more."*

Silence. The river ran brown and busy over everything it had taken this morning, as though nothing had happened here at all.

Nine days. The covenant-stone. Unarmed, unaccompanied — alone, in other words, which every instinct I owned screamed was the oldest trap in warfare, older than iron, probably older than fire. Beside me Yara stood very still, and I watched them arrive at the same arithmetic I was arriving at, and I watched them begin, visibly, to lie to me about it.

"Well," they said lightly, to the herald, to the mud, to me. "At least he's punctual."

And that lightness — that awful, practiced, smuggler's-road lightness — told me everything, and I grabbed their wrist under the cloak, iron-tight, and said, low enough that only they could hear:

"Whatever you're planning. Whatever version of *alone* you're already building in that head of yours. We had words about this, Yara. Firelight. Remember?"

They wouldn't look at me. They were looking at Nyssara's pale hand hanging in the mud, and at the iron wire, and at some future only they could see.

"Yeah," Yara said finally. "I remember."

And the way they said it scared me more than the whole morning's dead.
