## 18. Terms of Surrender

The parley flag arrived at dawn, tied to an arrow that buried itself in the doorpost of the ruined keep with a thunk that said *I could have been your throat.*

I was eating porridge at the time. It was terrible porridge — burned, under-salted, the kind of porridge you eat not for nourishment but to prove to yourself that the world still has rules. The arrow did not improve my appetite.

"Chivalrous," Serah said from the doorway, arms crossed. She'd slept in her armor again. I'd stopped counting how many nights that made; she counted them herself, I think, like penance beads. "He shoots at the council hall and calls it diplomacy."

"He shot at the doorpost," Nyssara corrected from the window, where she'd been sitting since before first light with the particular stillness of someone who hears things humans can't. "If he wanted to kill anyone, he'd have used fire. This is theater. Aldric likes an audience."

The message itself came down the mountain two hours later under a white banner and an escort of forty riders who stopped exactly far enough away to be insulting. A herald read it in a voice trained for cathedrals:

*To the leaders of the free cities, and to the one called Yara Villalobos: King Aldric, rightful sovereign of the human realms, offers terms of peace. He will withdraw his armies from Thornfell, release all prisoners taken in the witch-hunts, and deliver into the keeping of the fae the second crown-shard now held by his forces. In exchange, he requires only this: the surrender of the prophesied vessel, that the old covenant may be renewed as it was always written, and the land healed by the price it has always demanded.*

*One life,* the herald finished, folding the parchment with ceremony. *In place of thousands. His Majesty considers this arithmetic generous.*

Serah's hand went to her sword. Nyssara's went to her mother's shard, hanging at her throat like a small cold star. And me? I looked out over the assembly of ash-gray councilors and refugees and exhausted soldiers, all of whom had turned — every single one of them, without meaning to, without wanting to — toward me.

Generous arithmetic. I'd heard that math before. My mentor had done it in the margins of a prophecy book when I was a squalling infant: *one life against two realms.* She'd never asked me either.

* * *

They gave me three days to answer. That was in the terms too, which told me something about Aldric — he understood that a sacrifice dragged to the altar screaming is worth less than nothing. Wild magic doesn't honor coercion. Whatever he wanted from my death, he needed me to walk there on my own feet, believing it.

Which was, I'll note for posterity, the single most reasonable thing the man had ever done, and I hated him for it.

The council argued for six hours. I know because I sat through all of them, in a corner, being discussed in the third person as though I were weather. Councilor Veyne wanted to refuse outright and trust the walls of Corvane. Councilor Maren wanted to accept and had the decency not to look at me while she said so. Someone proposed sending a decoy, which died quickly once Nyssara observed that the ritual would simply fail and Aldric would burn everything out of spite.

"The vessel must consent," she said. "Truly consent. The covenant-stone will know a lie."

Everyone looked at me again. I'd gotten very good at not flinching under that look. Years of practice, hiding what I was from smugglers who would have sold me for boot-money. But lately the mask kept slipping, because the people looking at me weren't strangers anymore, and some part of me — the soft, stupid, recently thawed part — kept wanting to tell them the truth.

"I need air," I said, and left before anyone could point out that the air was full of woodsmoke and dying covenant.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about being a prophesied sacrifice: after a while, it starts making sense.

Not in the noble way. Not the way the ballads would have it, if anyone ever wrote ballads about me — though give them time, they will, and they'll get everything wrong. No. What makes sense is smaller and meaner than nobility. It's the arithmetic working on you from inside. Every refugee child coughing ash in the keep's undercroft. Every village on fire beyond the ridge, visible at night like embers in a banked hearth. Every soldier in the evacuation columns who looked at me with hope, because I was the thing in the story, the key, the answer — and hope is heavier than any armor ever forged.

*One life,* whispered the margins of my mentor's book, in her careful, guilty hand. *Against thousands. Against the veil itself.*

She'd hidden me to spare me. She'd also, by hiding me, let the covenant starve for a hundred years until the land started rotting at the seams. Love and ruin, braided together like everything else in my life.

So that night, while Serah stood the wall watch and Nyssara dreamed whatever fae dream in colors no human eye could parse, I sat alone with the shards — two of them, mine and the queen's, humming faintly against each other in a leather roll — and I made the decision that was going to break somebody's heart.

Mine, ideally. That was the plan.

Because here's the other thing: I had read the ritual. All of it, including the parts my mentor had tried to obscure, including the parts carved into the covenant-stone in a script older than the free cities. And a person who has spent their whole life learning magic in secret, off-books, half-illegal, learns one lesson above all others: *the rules are a language, and languages can be lied in.*

The covenant demanded a life freely given. Fine. It did not specify *all* of it. The stone required a soul offered up as the anchor-point for the binding. Also fine — but anchors hold things *down*, they don't consume them. The original design — the elegant, cruel, century-old design — funneled the whole sacrifice into the crown, burning the giver to ash and ceding both crowns to whoever held the reforged circlet. Which, in the original prophecy, meant whichever faction arranged matters best.

Aldric thought he was buying a throne with my death.

I intended to write different terms into the contract while he watched. Redirect the flow. Split it. Bind the crown to *consent renewed*, not blood spent — make the covenant a living thing that drew its power from choice instead of corpses. It would cost me. Oh, it would cost me. The redirection alone, channeled through my own body at the moment of giving, would strip every scrap of magic out of me like wire pulled hot from a forge. Maybe more. Maybe everything.

But it might work. It just might work.

And the beautiful, terrible part — the part I clung to at three in the morning with the shards humming against my ribs like two small hearts — was that if I told no one, no one could stop me. If I walked onto that field alone, consenting freely, smiling even, then the stone would have its sacrifice and I would have my sabotage and everyone I loved would still be alive to hate me for it afterward.

I was very proud of this plan for approximately four hours.

* * *

I wrote the letter first. That was my mistake, or maybe my honesty, depending on which of us you ask later.

I'm a smuggler's guide by trade. You learn to leave notes — *gone ahead, take the high fork, don't wait past moonset.* Short, clear, deniable. So I wrote short, clear, deniable. I told Serah where I was going and why, because leaving without a word felt like a worse betrayal than the truth, and I told myself the truth minus one crucial piece: that I didn't intend to come back the same person, or possibly at all.

*I've gone to Greywater to meet Aldric's escort at moonrise. Don't follow. The terms are fairer than anything the council will ever agree to, and I can do more for us inside the ritual than outside it. Trust me one last time. — Y*

I addressed it to her because she'd find it, because she always found things — contraband in false-bottomed carts, lies in a courier's smile, me, at a river ford, a lifetime ago. Or what felt like a lifetime. It had been perhaps two months. Grief keeps its own calendar.

I tucked it under her whetstone, which was sentimental and stupid, and I slipped out through the postern gate that only I and three dead smugglers knew about, and I made it almost to the tree line before the voice behind me said, "Don't."

Not loud. Just *don't*, the way you say it to a friend standing on a ledge.

Serah Dain stood in the shadow of the wall in her armor, unarmored in every way that mattered, holding my letter. Reading it again, from the set of her mouth. Moonlight caught the gray at her temples — new gray, earned gray — and her eyes had the flat shine I'd seen once before, at Vaelmoor, right before she broke her orders to pull me out of the drowned chapel.

"You wrote 'trust me one last time,'" she said. "Do you want to know what that phrase means to a knight? It means *I am about to do something dishonorable and call it duty.*"

"It's not—" I started.

"Yara." She crossed the distance between us in five steps. "I interrogated prisoners for six years. I know every version of 'it's not what it looks like.' Show me your hands."

"Why?"

"Because when you lie, you rub the scar on your knuckles. The one the prophecy book knew about before I did." Her voice cracked, very slightly, on *before I did*. "Show me."

I showed her. And damn it all — there they were, my traitor thumbs, worrying the old scar like it owed them money.

We stood there in the cold while the whole plan fell apart between us, unspoken, assembled out of the wreckage of my face. She read me the way she read battlefields: fast, total, without mercy.

"When," she said finally. "When do you come back?"

"That depends—"

"*When.*"

"After the ritual. Mostly. Probably." I heard myself, and winced. "Serah—"

"You're going to give yourself to that stone." Flat. Certain. "And you're going to tell me it's fine, because you found a loophole. Because you always find loopholes, it's what makes you insufferable, and you're going to stand on that altar rewriting a thousand-year-old covenant in real time while Aldric's witch-finders watch, and you decided — you *decided* — that the person who pulled you out of a flooded chapel, who burned her last bridge to the crown for you, who—" Her breath went ragged. She mastered it, the way she mastered everything, brutally and immediately. "You decided I'd be safer not knowing."

"You *would* be."

"Don't you dare protect me." And there it was, the crack all the way through, the woman under the armor at last. "Don't you *dare*. Do you know what it is to lose someone to their own heroism? I lost my whole company that way. One noble decision at a time, each one perfectly reasonable, each one *for our own good*, until there was nobody left to be good *to*." She grabbed my collar, gauntlets and all, iron fingers in homespun wool. "You don't get to choose my grief for me. That's not love. That's just sacrifice wearing love's clothes."

The word landed like a dropped stone. *Love.* Neither of us had said it yet — we'd danced around it across half the mountains, in the space between bedrolls, in the way she checked my gear twice and I memorized the sound of her breathing. We'd agreed, on that night neither of us would ever mention casually again, that we'd defy the prophecy together or not at all.

And here I was. Alone. At night. Sneaking out a smuggler's gate with a letter under her whetstone.

"Not at all," I said hoarsely. "That was the deal."

"Yes. It was."

"So arrest me. Knight-captain. Stop the prisoner."

Something flickered across her face — the old duty, the worn groove of it — and then she laughed, awful and wet, and let go of my collar only to press her forehead to mine, armor clinking, breath shaking. "Gods help me, I can't. That's the problem. I could arrest anyone in the world except you."

"Then you see my difficulty," I whispered.

"No." She pulled back, and her eyes had gone hard again — but hard like a drawn bow, not like a shuttered window. "No, I see *yours*, and it ends tomorrow. You're not going alone. You're not going silent. If you're going to rewrite the oldest law in creation on the back of your own soul, then you're going to do it with witnesses, with allies, and with me close enough to catch you when your clever little loophole turns out to have teeth."

"If you're anywhere near the stone, Aldric will use you. Hostage, leverage—"

"He already took Nyssara's mother. He thinks hostages are currency. Let him learn what I am instead." She said it quietly, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Besides — you need someone inside his lines to spring your trap when it goes wrong. And it will go wrong. Everything you plan goes slightly wrong, Villalobos. It's practically a gift."

I wanted to argue. I catalogued the arguments — her safety, the mission, the clean simplicity of a solo sacrifice — and watched them all die on the vine, because every one of them was just my pride wearing armor of its own.

"There's a condition," I said at last.

"One of mine first: you tell Nyssara. Tonight. Before moonset. She'll smell the deception otherwise, and fae take oaths badly broken."

"Agreed." I swallowed. "Mine: if the redirection starts killing me — actually killing me, not just stripping the magic — you pull me off that stone. Even if it breaks the ritual. Even if the veil tears. Swear it."

Serah looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere below, the camp stirred; above, the mountains wore their snow like a judgment.

"I swear," she said. "On my rank. On what's left of it."

"And I swear the rest," I said. "Back by spring. Mostly. Probably."

"Villalobos."

"Working on it," I said. "Give me until the altar."

Behind us, the postern gate creaked, and Nyssara stepped out of the dark with her mother's shard glowing faintly at her throat and an expression that said she had heard every word and had opinions spanning centuries. The moon was going down. Greywater waited, and a king with generous arithmetic, and a stone hungry for a freely given life that had no idea — none at all — what I intended to feed it instead.

"Well," said Nyssara. "If we're committing treason against a prophecy, we should at least be punctual."
