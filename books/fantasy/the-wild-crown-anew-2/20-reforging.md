## 20. Reforging

The world was coming apart, and it had the audacity to do it beautifully.

I want you to understand that, because it's the thing nobody warns you about. Catastrophe isn't ugly. It's a cathedral of light splitting down the middle. The veil over Greywater field tore like wet linen, and through the wound poured everything Thornfell had been holding back for a hundred years — seasons in the wrong order, rivers running uphill, colors that don't have names and were frankly showing off. A stag made of frost walked past me, regarded me with an expression of mild apology, and dissolved into snow that fell *upward*.

Somewhere behind me, Aldric's army was screaming. Somewhere ahead of me, Serah was bleeding. And somewhere in between, the three crown-shards sang.

They'd been singing since I pulled them loose from the covenant-stone's wreckage — one from Vaelmoor's drowned chapel, salt-scoured and patient; one from my mother-queen's broken hands; one torn from Aldric's signet ring with a sound like a bone leaving its socket. They hung in the air before me now, orbiting each other slowly, and their song was not music so much as *insistence*. An argument. The same argument the prophecy had been making my entire life, in my mentor's handwriting, in the dying courier's blood, in every road that had led me here.

*A life freely given,* it said. *One vessel. One death. One crown.*

"No," I told it.

The shards paused in their orbit. If pieces of metal can be offended, these managed it.

Let me back up slightly, because even in the middle of an apocalypse I apparently narrate my own life like a smuggler giving directions: *take the left fork, mind the troll, whatever you do don't look at the sky.* The ritual had gone wrong exactly the way I'd known it would when I'd agreed to Aldric's parley and then planned to betray it. The old covenant hadn't wanted to be subverted. It had wanted to be *paid*. When Aldric forced the rite early — greedy hands on ancient stone, always a winning combination — the contract had shattered instead of transferring, and now all that century-old magic was flooding out of the wound in the world looking for somewhere to live.

Preferably, said the shards, in me. Preferably by killing me first. Efficiency is very important to prophecies.

"Yara!" Serah's voice, raw from smoke. I turned and saw her fighting her way toward me through the wild magic, sword out, armor scorched, hair escaping its braid like she'd lost an argument with a thunderstorm. Behind her, Nyssara moved through the chaos the way only fae nobility can — like the apocalypse was an inconvenience she intended to speak to someone's manager about. Her mother's shard-light clung to her like a second skin.

"The stone's collapsing!" she shouted. "If the binding fails—"

"I know what happens if the binding fails!" I shouted back. "I read the book! I *hate* the book!"

Because I did know. Without a covenant, the veil wouldn't just tear — it would unravel. Two realms bleeding into each other until neither survived the transfusion. The land would rot from the seams outward. I'd seen what a *failing* covenant did to Thornfell's mountains. This would be that, everywhere, forever, with extra screaming.

The prophecy's solution sat in my chest like a swallowed coal: one vessel, freely given, and the realms saved at the cost of everything Yara Villalobos had ever been or might have been. My mentor had bound me to it before I could walk. The free cities had prayed for it without knowing my name. Even Serah's original orders had been, at bottom, *deliver the girl to the altar.*

And I stood there, twenty-some years old, mortal-adjacent, exhausted, singed in places I didn't previously have places, and I thought: *no.*

Not because I wasn't willing. That was the trap, you see. The whole rotten architecture of the old covenant ran on willingness — a life *freely given*, century after century, and everyone involved so proud of how noble it was. Sacrifice as a system. Death as a budget line. I'd spent chapters — years, really — learning exactly how much it cost to be the person other people's safety depended on, and I'll tell you what it cost: everything, paid in installments, with interest.

So I reached into the song of the shards, into that insistent ancient arithmetic, and I did the one thing no prophet, king, or mentor had thought to try.

I renegotiated.

* * *

Here's what nobody understood about the covenant, because understanding it required being raised by a hedge-witch who kept terrible records and excellent secrets: it wasn't law. It wasn't physics. It was a *promise*, made by frightened people a hundred years ago who couldn't imagine anyone doing better, so they wrote the smallest, cruelest promise that would hold and called it destiny.

But promises are made of words, and words are mine. Words were always mine. Take away every scrap of training, every drop of borrowed power, and I could still talk. Ask anyone who's traveled with me. *Ask them at length. Then ask them to stop.*

"You're a throne," I told the shards, walking toward their slow orbit while reality flaked around me like old paint. "That's your problem. You were forged as a throne — one seat, one bearer, one neck under the weight. Every version of this ends with somebody kneeling. Him." I flicked two fingers toward Aldric, who was picking himself up off the ruined covenant-stone with the expression of a man whose retirement plan had just caught fire. "Or me. Or whoever's fool enough next century. But a throne only knows how to take."

The shards flared. The song sharpened into something like laughter. *And what else could you make us?*

"A bridge," I said.

The word landed strange in the tearing air. Even the frost-stag's ghost seemed to pause mid-dissolution.

"Not a crown. Not a throne. A covenant that doesn't sit above anybody — that runs *through* everybody. Shared. Weight split three ways instead of piled on one back. You want a life freely given? Fine. Give me three lives, freely chosen, each carrying a third of the burden, none of them dying, all of them *paying*. Power instead of blood. Presence instead of sacrifice. Somebody has to *stay* and hold the thing together — so let it be people who choose to stay. Together. On purpose. Repeatedly. Forever, if we're unlucky."

*Impossible,* sang the shards, but I heard the waver in it. Prophecies are very confident right up until someone reads the fine print aloud.

"Yeah," I said. "Everyone keeps saying that."

* * *

Serah reached me first. She caught my arm, took in my face — which I'm told does something complicated when I'm about to do something stupid — and said, "No. Whatever it is. No."

"It's a good no," I said.

"Every 'no' you've ever called good has ended with us running."

"This one ends with us standing still, actually. Possibly forever. That's sort of the point." I turned to face her fully, and the wild light of the tearing veil made her eyes look like they were on fire, which felt thematically appropriate. "The covenant needs bearers. Three of them. It needs people who will hold the veil closed from both sides, permanently, with their own power. Their own *lives*. It's not a crown, Serah. It's a job. The worst job in two worlds, and I'm asking you to take it with me."

She stared at me. Around us, a river briefly flowed through the sky, fish and all, because the universe had stopped caring about logistics.

"You'd share it," she said slowly. "You found a way to *share* it."

"I found a way to make it worse and fairer. Which is most of politics, I think. Nyssara!" I shouted, and the fae princess turned from holding back a tide of unmoored magic — literally holding it back, with her hands, because of course she was — and strode toward us through grass that was blooming and dying with every step.

"You have," she said, "approximately one minute before this field ceases to exist."

"Then I'll be quick. Your mother's realm needs an anchor on the far side of the veil. Someone of the blood, freely choosing it. I'm offering you the worst job in two worlds."

Nyssara looked at the tearing sky. At her mother's shard, singing in my orbiting little apocalypse-mobile. At me. Something moved through her face — grief for the court that had exiled her, fury at the queen who'd waited a century to break, and underneath it, so faint I almost missed it, relief. The relief of finally being asked the question she'd been built to answer.

"My mother served a covenant that ate children," she said quietly. "Yes. Obviously yes. Someone has to build something better out of her ruins, and I am *so tired* of watching men break things."

"Serah?" I said.

Serah looked at the shards. At me. At the horizon where Corvane's refugees were still streaming away from the burning villages, where the free cities' council had argued for weeks about whether people like me deserved rights or merely toleration. She had lost her rank for questioning a king. She had broken her orders for a stranger at a ford. Every step of her fall had been toward this, though neither of us had known to call it that.

"If I say yes," she said, "it's not duty. Understand that. Duty got me stripped of command. This is—" She stopped, jaw working, knight-captain vocabulary failing her for once in her life. "This is the opposite of duty. This is *choosing*."

"That's the only currency it accepts," I said. "Freely given. All three of us. Or not at all."

"Then put my hand on the damn rock already."

* * *

Aldric reached us before we could begin. Of course he did. Kings hate endings they didn't author.

He came through the wild magic limping, cloak gone, crown — the plain steel one, the usurper's circlet — knocked askew, and the third shard's absence left his hand blackened to the wrist. He looked older than he had at the parley. He looked, for one unguarded moment, like a man who had once been a grieving boy, before grief calcified into arithmetic.

"Villalobos." His voice was wrecked but level. "The ritual requires a vessel. One. There is no version where you simply *decline* the prophecy—"

"There's no version where I accept it, either. Watch me."

"You'll destroy everything."

"We're going to rebuild everything," I said. "It's worse. It involves meetings."

I extended the offer anyway, because he deserved the chance and because some stubborn, traitorous part of me remembered the brother he'd lost to the last covenant's price. Grief had made him a tyrant, but grief was also the only honest thing left in him.

"Stand with us," I said. "Three bearers becomes four. Share the burden. Rule nothing, hold everything. It's yours if you want it — the real thing, not the throne's counterfeit."

For a heartbeat — one true heartbeat — something in his face *leaned*. I saw it. The boy under the king, reaching.

Then his jaw set, and the arithmetic won, because it always won, because he'd fed it his whole life. "Shared power," he said, "is no power. A covenant held by committee is a covenant waiting to fail. I will not kneel to a *committee*."

"Nobody's kneeling," I said. "That's rather the entire—"

"I'd rather fall," King Aldric said, and meant it, and turned back toward the collapsing stone to seize by force the throne that no longer existed.

What happened next happened fast, and I've replayed it enough times to know there was nothing anyone could have done. He laid his burned hand on the covenant-stone's last intact face and spoke the old words of seizure — *mine, mine, by right of conquest* — and the stone answered him the way a dead language answers a bad accent. The old covenant, already shattered, gave him nothing. But the veil behind him, wounded and wild, gave him *everything*: it opened like a throat.

He didn't scream. I'll give him that. He fell backward into the light with his hand still raised, grasping at a crown that had never been real, and the veil closed over him like water over a dropped coin. The frost came afterward, gently, and covered the place where he'd been standing, and no body was ever found, and I have decided — freely, deliberately, as one of my first acts as a bearer of the new covenant — to believe that somewhere on the other side, there is a boy waiting for his brother, and that the mountain will be kinder to him than he was to us.

Maybe. Probably not. But I get to decide what I believe now. That's the whole point.

* * *

We joined hands over the shards. Serah's grip steady and scarred. Nyssara's cool as riverstone. Mine shaking, because I'm honest with myself about exactly one thing.

"Together," I said, "or not at all."

"Together," they said, "or not at all."

We pressed our palms to the shards, and the shards pressed back, and the prophecy *screamed*.

I felt it fight us. Of course I did — a hundred years of certainty doesn't die quietly. It threw my mentor's handwriting at me, the margins full of *forgive me, forgive me, there was no other way*, and I answered: *there wasn't, then. There is now.* It threw the drowned bells of Vaelmoor and the queen's century of captivity and every village burned to starve us out, and I answered with Serah's hand tightening on mine, with Nyssara stepping closer, with three pulses finding one rhythm.

And then — this is the part I'll be telling badly for the rest of my life — the shards *agreed*.

Light went through us like spring goes through frozen ground. Not gentle. Nothing about it was gentle. It took inventory: Serah's discipline, iron-forged and unbending. Nyssara's lineage, old as roots. My — well. Mine was mostly stubbornness and a smuggler's sense for hidden roads, but apparently that counts. The power didn't pool in any of us. It *threaded*, a loom with three warp-strands, and where the veil had torn, it began to weave.

I watched the sky close. Stitch by stitch, season by season, the wrongness smoothed back into order — not the old order, not the brittle century-old cage, but something suppler. A covenant with give in it. Room to grow. Room, someday, for more hands.

The cost arrived on schedule. Costs always do; I respect that about them. It moved through me like a tide going out, taking things I'd assumed were permanent: the hedge-magic warmth in my fingertips, the whisper of the mountains' moods, the small bright pilot-light of power I'd carried since birth and hidden since childhood. Each gift lifted away like a bird leaving a branch, and I let them go, one by one, saying goodbye to each, until I stood inside myself feeling — quiet. Emptied. Enormously, terrifyingly ordinary.

The veil sealed with a sound like a bell underwater, and the shards sank into our joined hands and were gone — not destroyed, not worn, just *distributed*, become the work itself. Somewhere in Thornfell, I knew without knowing how, the mountains had stopped poisoning their own streams. Somewhere beyond the veil, a captive queen felt her chains become something more like a door.

Serah caught me as my knees quit. Nyssara caught Serah. We went down in a heap in the recovering grass, three people holding up a world, laughing in the unhinged way of the recently not-dead.

"You did it," Serah said into my hair. "You impossible, insubordinate—"

"Don't ruin it with praise."

"What did it cost?" Nyssara asked, sharp-eyed even flat on her back. Fae. "Yara. What did it cost *you*?"

I flexed my fingers. Reached for the magic the way I'd reached for it ten thousand times.

Nothing reached back.

"Everything," I said, and started to laugh, and discovered the laugh was half sob, and decided that was allowed. "Everything, and it was *cheap*. Do you understand? It was the cheapest price anyone's ever paid. I kept my life. I kept my *self*. I just — don't get to be special anymore."

Serah pulled back to look at me, and her expression did the thing — the one that had been undoing me since a ford, chapters ago. "Yara. You were never special. You were *yours*. There's a difference, and you finally get to feel it."

I kissed her, because the world was saved and I was mortal and some opportunities are load-bearing.

* * *

Later — minutes later, hours, time was being repaired along with everything else — we stood at the edge of Greywater field while the first clean sunset in months painted the ruins gold. Refugees were coming back down the roads, cautious as deer. Somewhere, the free cities' leaders were going to need to be told that the apocalypse had been canceled and replaced with a committee, and that the committee included a disgraced knight, an exiled princess, and a former smuggler who could no longer so much as light a candle.

Nyssara touched my shoulder. "The talks," she said. "Between the realms. They'll want envoys. Bearers."

"Tomorrow," I said. "Tonight I'd like to be nobody, somewhere quiet, ideally near food."

But even as I said it, I felt it — faint, at the threshold of hearing, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar. The new covenant, settling into place. Not a voice. Not a demand. Just a *weight*, shared, warm, three-cornered, humming softly in Serah's steady breathing and Nyssara's pale glow and the empty space where my magic used to live.

It was going to be work. Hard work, endless work, the kind that doesn't end with a battle won but begins there.

I found I was looking forward to it.

Then Serah went very still beside me, staring at the road east, toward Corvane, and said quietly:

"Yara. Why is the city wearing mourning colors?"
