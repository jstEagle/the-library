## 21. Mortal Again

The first thing I noticed when I woke was the silence.

Not the silence of a room — I'd slept in enough smuggler's dens to know the creak-and-settle language buildings speak at night, and Corvane's healing hall had plenty to say: beams ticking as they cooled, someone snoring three beds down, a mouse conducting its small business behind the wainscoting. No. The silence I meant was inside me.

All my life there had been a hum under my skin. Hedge-magic doesn't announce itself; it's more like a second heartbeat, a low note you stop hearing until it stops. For twenty-three years that note had been playing. Now it wasn't. And in its absence I could hear everything else — the mouse, the beams, my own breath going in and out like it belonged to an ordinary person who had ordinary business with the world.

I lay still for a long time, waiting for panic.

It didn't come. That surprised me most of all. I'd expected the loss to feel like an amputation. Instead it felt like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was carrying — one of those packs you wear so long your shoulders reshape around it, and when someone finally takes it off you can't stand upright for days because straight isn't your shape anymore.

I sat up. The room tilted politely and settled. My hands shook, but hands shake after anything these days. There was a scar on my knuckles where the shard had burned me during the reforging, thin and pale, already fading. Even my body was being let off easy.

"Well," I said aloud, to no one, in the voice of a person negotiating with the universe. "That's that, then."

The universe did not argue. It never had, really. That had always been other people's department.

* * *

Serah found me on the roof two days later.

I say "found" generously. Corvane's healing hall has a flat section of roof where the herb-dryers go, and I had discovered that if you climbed out the scullery window and walked along the gutter like a sensible criminal, you got sun, quiet, and a view of the city walls that made the whole ruined year look almost worth it. Serah climbed out the same window, walked the same gutter, and dropped onto the shingles beside me with the graceless thump of a woman in armor who has stopped pretending she might ever be graceful.

"You're supposed to be resting," she said.

"I am resting. This is roof-resting. It's very advanced."

"Mm." She lowered herself down, groaned at her knees like a knight twice her age, and looked out over the city with me. Below, Corvane was doing what Corvane does best: commerce. Carts in the streets, banners going up over the council house — new banners, grey-green, stitched with both the free cities' tower and Thornfell's antler. Someone with more optimism than sense had ordered them before any treaty existed. Or maybe that was the point. Sometimes you sew the flag first and negotiate toward it.

"They started yesterday," Serah said. "Talks. Fae delegation came through the veil-gap at Greywater. Nyssara led them."

"I heard the bells."

"You always hear the bells." She glanced sideways at me. "How are you?"

The question deserved better than deflection, mostly because she'd earned better. She'd held my hand while the crown took what it took. She'd felt it happen through our joined fingers — the moment the binding spread itself across three people instead of one, and the surplus price came due from the one who'd been carrying magic since before she could walk. I remembered her face going white across the covenant-stone. I remembered thinking, absurdly, don't let go yet, I want to remember what it's like to be this.

"I keep reaching for it," I admitted. "Like a tongue finding the hole where a tooth was. I'll be doing something — lighting the lamp, warming water — and I'll reach, and there's nothing. Just me. Just hands."

"And?"

"And then I make tea like a normal person, and it takes four times as long, and honestly? The tea's fine. Better, even. Turns out I was oversteeping everything for years because magic doesn't care about patience."

Serah laughed — short, startled out of her, the way she laughed when she forgot she was allowed to. It was still, weeks later, my favorite sound in either realm.

"The healers say the change is permanent," she said. Careful. She always went careful around the edges of things that mattered, like a woman walking ice she'd already fallen through once.

"I know. I asked them to check twice. They checked three times, because they thought I wanted it to be temporary." I turned my palm up, studied the lines like they were a map to somewhere unremarkable. "They were wrong."

"You're sure?"

"Serah." I said her name and let it do the work. "Do you know what I was, before all this? A hidden thing. A key wearing a girl's skin, locked in a box my mentor built and called a life. Every choice I made, some part of me was spent keeping the secret — don't use too much, don't be seen, don't matter, because mattering is how witch-finders find you. I hid so long I forgot hiding was a choice. I thought it was just what I was made of." I closed my fist. "Now the key's melted down into something better and I'm just... skin. Ordinary, mortal, tired skin that gets cold at night and wants breakfast in the morning. It's the first thing I've ever been that nobody else designed."

She was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and laced her fingers through mine — the scarred hand, deliberately, thumb brushing the fading burn.

"Nobody designed that?" she asked.

"No," I said. "That one I picked."

* * *

The talks, as it happened, were going about as well as talks between traumatized humans and traumatized fae could be expected to go, which is to say: badly, slowly, with occasional breakthroughs that everyone pretended weren't emotional because emotions are undignified.

Nyssara gave me the full report that evening, sprawled across the chair in my little rented room like she owned it, which by fae logic she possibly now did, since I'd once promised her something in that room and promises stain property.

"The Corvane council wants the veil sealed," she announced. "Permanently. With wards. They used the word 'quarantine.'"

"Charming."

"My mother's regents want tolls. A gate-tax on every crossing, human or otherwise." Her mouth curled. "The antler-folk are offended by money. They think coin is a human superstition we were better off without."

"They're not wrong."

"Don't agree with them, it encourages them." She plucked an apple from my table — my apple, technically, purchased with actual coin like an actual person — and bit into it. "And Aldric's rump loyalists in the east are telling anyone who'll listen that the queen's daughter sold the veil to witches. Which is ironic, given that their king tried to eat it."

"How is he? Have you heard?"

Her expression flickered — the grief under the polish. Aldric had refused the offered place in the new covenant. Had stepped back into the tearing veil with the third shard still fused to his hand, choosing annihilation over a world he couldn't own alone. Whatever else he'd been — usurper, butcher, a boy who'd lost a brother to the old bargain — he had been consistent.

"Gone," Nyssara said simply. "The veil closed over him. What remains of him isn't ours to bury." She turned the apple in her fingers. "My mother asks about you. She wants to thank you properly, which frightens me. Formal gratitude from the fae court historically precedes either a gift or a curse."

"Tell her tea. Just tea. No ceremony. I'm very out of practice with ceremony and I've grown fond of sitting down."

"She won't sit. Queens don't— " Nyssara stopped. Looked at the apple. Looked at me, mortal and rumpled in my borrowed chair, and something in her face softened into something almost like hope. "Actually. You know what? Maybe she will. Maybe that's the whole point of what you built."

* * *

Grief, I learned, does not schedule itself around your recovery.

It came for me on a grey afternoon a week after I'd left the healing hall, when I went back to the waystation in the low passes — my waystation, the one I'd holed up in with a dead courier's book and no idea I was reading my own obituary. The roof had half-collapsed under winter snow. Someone had squatted in it since; there were old fire-rings and the smell of wet ash. On the shelf where I'd kept my spare flint, someone had left a chipped clay cup, as if the mountain were apologizing for eating my past and offering a token settlement.

I sat on the broken sill and finally cried for Maren.

Not the controlled, dutiful grief of the funeral I'd never gotten to have. The ugly kind. The kind where you say the true things out loud to an empty room: *You bound me before I could say yes. You lied to me every day of my life. You loved me so much you stole my future and called it protection.* And then, ten minutes later, hiccupping against my knees: *You also taught me to read stars in fog and find water in stone, and you died getting that book to me, and I would give almost anything for one more terrible lecture.*

Almost anything. Not anything. I'd paid enough.

The anger didn't cancel the love. I kept waiting for it to resolve into one clean feeling, and it refused, and eventually I understood that refusal was the answer. Grief braided is still grief, but it holds. Unbraided, it frays. So I sat in my ruined waystation and let the strands tangle, and when the light went gold and low I stood up, brushed myself off, and ate a heel of bread like an ordinary mortal woman having an ordinary hard day.

Then I did something I hadn't done in years without magic: I lit the hearth myself. Flint, steel, tinder, patience. It took eleven strikes. The fire caught on the twelfth and burned small and stubborn and entirely mine.

"There," I told Maren's ghost, wherever ghosts go when you've stopped being able to summon them. "No shortcuts. You'd be insufferable about it."

The chimney drew. The smoke went up straight. If the mountains said anything back, they kept it to themselves, which felt respectful.

* * *

Winter deepened. The talks crawled. And I discovered that being powerless is a skill like any other.

You have to learn it. Nobody tells you that. When you can nudge luck and warm your own blood and feel a lie in the air like weather, you stop developing the muscles everyone else uses — asking for help, planning margins of error, accepting that some doors stay shut. I'd spent twenty-three years as the exception. Now I was the rule, and the rule has rules.

I relearned the Thornfell roads the honest way: on foot, in bad boots, getting lost. The covenant's collapse had warped half the passes into pretzels of impossible geography — a valley that looped, a ridge that showed you tomorrow's sunset — but the new covenant was knitting them back, slowly, the way scar tissue closes. Each week the maps were a little less wrong. I redrew them by hand and sent copies to the guild in Corvane, and the guild master — a woman named Osk who'd once blacklisted me for smuggling and now paid me double for cartography, which is the freest city's idea of an apology — pinned them up where the couriers could argue about them.

"You're wasting yourself," Serah said one night, watching me hunched over the drafting table, ink to the elbows. She said it gently, but it had an edge underneath. "You ended a war. You rewrote a prophecy. And you spend your days drawing trails."

"It's not a waste." I blew a hair off the wet ink. "Do you know what a road is, Serah? A road is a promise that says: *you may pass.* That's all the new covenant is, scaled up. Somebody has to draw where the crossings go, or the first frost-feud relitigates the whole war." I tapped the map — the Greywater gap, marked in green, open to both realms. "Besides. I'm good at it. I was good at it before anyone decided I was a vessel. Turns out that was the real me all along, and the prophecy was the side job."

She came around the table and read the map upside down, which drove me mad, which she knew.

"And the dreams?" she asked. Too casual. Knight-captain casual. Ambush casual.

I put down the pen.

Because yes. The dreams. Three nights out of seven, the same one: standing at the covenant-stone again, three hands on three shards, the veil screaming open above us — except in the dream there was a fourth hand. Pale, ringed, reaching for the crown from the wrong side of the light. And every time I woke before I saw whose it was.

"Still the same one," I said. "Stone, shards, a hand I don't recognize. It's probably nothing. Grief does this. The mind replays the fall until it sticks the landing."

"Probably," Serah agreed, in the voice of a woman who did not believe it, and whose sword-hand had been twitching toward the door whenever the wind rattled the shutters lately, though she'd deny that too.

Outside, far off in the direction of Thornfell, thunder rolled under a cloudless sky. We both looked at the window. Neither of us said anything for a long moment.

"Probably," I said again, less surely, and reached for the pen, and my hand — my ordinary, mortal, honest hand — was perfectly steady, which somehow made it worse.
