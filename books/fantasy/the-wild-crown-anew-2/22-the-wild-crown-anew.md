## 22. The Wild Crown Anew

The first frost of autumn came early to Thornfell, the way it always did, sliding down off the high passes overnight and turning every puddle on the smuggler's road into a small, treacherous mirror. I stood at the head of a caravan of six mules, three merchants, and one extremely nervous wool-factor named Odo, and I told them what I'd told every caravan for the past two years:

"Stay on my heels, touch nothing that glows, and if the mountain starts singing, it's not for you."

Odo raised his hand like we were in a schoolroom.

"Yara," he said. "The mountain *sings*?"

"Only when it's in a good mood." I adjusted the strap of my pack — lighter than it used to be, these days, in ways no one could see but me. "You'll want to be here when it's in a bad mood. Trust me."

He laughed, because he thought I was joking. The merchants laughed too. Serah, riding point on her grey mare, glanced back at me with the particular expression she wore when she was amused by something and refusing to admit it, which was most of her expressions.

Two years since the covenant-stone. Two years since I'd stood in the tearing dark with wild magic screaming through my bones like a river in flood, with Aldric falling into the collapsing veil and Nyssara's hand slick with blood in mine, and said — out loud, to a prophecy older than grief — *no.*

Two years mortal. Two years magicless. And I had never once, not even in the worst of them, wanted it back.

That still surprised me when I thought about it, which was less often now. For twenty-odd years my magic had been a thing I hid, then a thing I was hunted for, then a thing that ate me alive from the inside every time I reached for it. People assume losing it must feel like losing an arm. It didn't. It felt like setting down a pack I'd been carrying so long I'd forgotten I could stand up straight.

I could stand up straight now. Mostly. My knees complained about the cold, but they were ordinary complaints, earned honestly by walking, and I found I liked them.

"All right," I called back to the caravan. "Vaelmoor ford is flooded from the rains. We go the long way, over the shepherd's switchback, and we make the waystation by full dark or we sleep in the wet. Questions?"

"The long way," Odo repeated faintly.

"You can wait for the flood to drop, if you like. It won't. Not this season."

Serah wheeled her mare around and brought her alongside me as I started up the trail. Up close, in the flat white light of a frosted morning, the changes in her were easier to see than in a mirror: a scar through her eyebrow she hadn't had before Greywater; grey coming in at her temples that she claimed was distinguished and I claimed was my fault; the old knight-captain's stiffness worn down into something looser, easier, a body that had finally stopped bracing for orders.

"You enjoy terrifying him," she said.

"I enjoy *informing* him. What he does with the information is his business."

"He asked if the mountain sings."

"It does."

"Yara."

"It does!" I spread my hands. "Not words. More like — you know when you put your ear against a rail and feel the far-off train? Like that. Except the train is very old and mostly asleep and occasionally grateful." I shrugged. "It's less strange than it sounds."

"It is exactly as strange as it sounds," Serah said, but she was smiling, the small crooked one, the one that belonged only to me and to mornings like this one, when the road was ours and nobody needed saving.

* * *

We made the switchback by late afternoon, and that was where the mountain sang.

It happened the way it always happened now: a warmth in the air that had no business being there in October, a smell of green things under the frost, and then a low harmonic hum through the rock beneath our boots, felt more than heard, rising and settling like a sleeping animal turning over. The mules' ears went forward. Odo grabbed the nearest merchant's sleeve and pointed at a hillside where — briefly, unmistakably — the dead bracken of autumn flushed green again, a whole slope of it, before fading back to rust-gold like a candle guttering out.

"What was *that*?" he demanded of no one and everyone.

"Covenant," said the oldest merchant, who'd crossed with us before. "Land's healing. Everyone knows that."

Everyone knew that, yes. What everyone didn't know — what only a handful of people in two realms knew — was that the land healed because three women had once stood on a broken stone and refused to let the world be paid for in the usual currency.

I kept that to myself. I kept most of it to myself, these days. Not because I was hiding — that was the difference, the whole difference, the thing I still caught myself marveling at — but because some things belong to the people who paid for them, and I had paid enough for other people's secrets to last several lifetimes.

Still. When the hum faded and the caravan moved on, I let myself fall a few steps behind, laid my bare palm flat against the cold stone of the cut bank, and listened.

*Thank you,* said the mountain, faintly, the way it always said it. Not in words. In warmth. In the green flash on the hillside. In the fact that the pass was passable at all, that the veil between the worlds ran smooth as river glass where once it had torn like rotten cloth.

"Don't mention it," I murmured, and meant it completely.

Serah waited for me at the bend, as she always did, pretending to check her mare's girth.

"It talks to you," she said.

"It says thank you. It's very polite."

"And what do you say?"

I caught up to her. "What I said."

She looked at me for a moment — that look, the long one, the one that still made me feel seen all the way through, which after everything I'd survived was somehow the most dangerous sensation left in the world — and then she nudged her mare onward and said, "Come on, guide. We lose the light, we lose the road."

* * *

The waystation at Shepherd's Hollow had been a smugglers' bolt-hole when I first knew it: one room, blackened beams, a hearth that smoked unless you knew its temper. Now it was a licensed post on a charted route, with a sign, and a toll box, and a ledger where travelers wrote their names. The world had gotten respectable around me and I still wasn't sure how I felt about it.

But the hearth still smoked unless you knew its temper, and I knew, and soon the room was warm and orange-lit and full of merchants arguing cheerfully about wool tariffs while Odo stared into the fire like a man revising his entire understanding of geography.

Serah and I sat by the window with tea, because we were old now — well, older — and because the window faced east, toward the veil-line, where the mountains faded into that silvery shimmer that marked where the fae realm began. On clear nights you could see lights beyond it. Lanterns, sometimes. Once, memorably, what might have been fireworks, though Nyssara insisted the Thorn Court did not celebrate anything so vulgar, and that it had definitely been a meteor.

"She's coming down for midwinter," Serah said. "Nyssara. She wrote."

"She writes every month. She's worse than my mother would have been, if I'd ever had one worth the name."

"She also wrote that the talks are going badly again."

"They're always going badly again. That's what talks are." I blew on my tea. "Corvane wants tariffs on dream-timber. Thorn Court wants the border shrines returned. Both sides are right, both sides are insufferable, and in ten years they'll have argued themselves into a treaty, because that's how peace works — slowly, loudly, and with a great deal of correspondence." I paused. "She's bringing the queen?"

A flicker crossed Serah's face. "Trying. The healers say travel is hard on her now."

Maren of Thornfell, once Queen of the fading realm, had spent a century in captivity learning to be small. Unmaking that had taken longer than unmaking the covenant, and it wasn't finished yet — maybe never entirely would be. But last spring she had walked in the orchards at Corvane without escort, and eaten a plum, and cried over a plum, and Nyssara had written to me afterward: *My mother tasted a fruit grown on the human side of the veil and said it was sweet. A year ago she could not have crossed the border at all. Do you understand what you bought us?*

I hadn't answered that letter for a month, because what do you say to that? Finally I'd written back: *I understand I got to keep my life, and you got to keep your mother, and somewhere a prophecy is furious about both.* Nyssara had sent back a single line: *Prophecies have no feelings. That was their whole problem.*

Wise woman, Nyssara. Going to make a better queen than anyone in either realm deserved.

Across the room, Odo had finally worked up the courage to approach. He hovered at the edge of the firelight with his cup clutched in both hands.

"Beg pardon," he said. "Master Villalobos. Only — the men say you've walked these roads since you were a girl. Since before the war, before the — all of it."

"That's right."

"And you knew them. The ones from the songs." His ears went red. "The Knight of Greywater. The Exile of Thorn. And the — well." He gestured at me, delicately, the way one gestures at a legend sitting in a waystation drinking tea. "The Third Hand."

Serah made a sound into her tea that was suspiciously close to a laugh.

"The Third Hand," I said, tasting the title and finding it, as always, slightly stale. "Yes. I knew them. Still do. They're terrible correspondents, both of them."

Odo blinked. Then, gathering himself like a man stepping off a ledge: "Is it true? About the crown? That there were meant to be — that someone had to—"

"Die," I supplied, kindly. "Yes. That was true. The prophecy required a life, freely given, every century. One person carries the burden so everyone else gets to forget it exists. It was a tidy arrangement, if you happened to be everyone else."

"But it didn't happen."

"No." I looked into my tea. "It didn't. Because when the moment came, three people decided that a covenant bought with a life isn't a covenant, it's a ransom. So they broke the terms and wrote new ones. It cost power instead of lives. Mine, mostly." I smiled at him. "Best trade I ever made."

Odo stared at me. Behind him, the merchants had gone quiet, listening. Outside, the wind moved through the pass, and beneath it — faint, patient, older than any song — the mountain hummed.

"But then it's true," Odo said slowly. "The last verse. The one they sing at midwinter. *Three hands upon the shattered gold.*"

"It's true," I agreed. "Though I'd note the songs leave out the part where the third hand spent most of the story scared, muddy, and lying to everyone she loved. Songs are like prophecies that way. They tell you how it ends and pretend that was the plan all along."

Someone laughed. Then Odo laughed, and then the room did, warm and easy, and Odo raised his cup to me — not to the Third Hand, I think, but to the tired guide by the window — and I raised mine back, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was enough.

Later, when the others slept, Serah and I sat up by the banked fire. She was cleaning her boots, which she did when she was thinking, and I was watching the embers, which I did when I was thinking, and for a while neither of us said anything, because we'd long ago stopped needing to fill silence the way young people do.

"My knee aches," I announced eventually.

"Mm."

"The mortal kind. The honest kind."

"Mm," Serah said again, and set down the boot, and reached over without looking and took my hand, lacing our fingers together the way she'd done ten thousand times now and would, I judged, do ten thousand more, given reasonable luck and my continued refusal to die of anything dramatic.

"Do you regret it?" she asked quietly. This was an old question, an anniversary question, asked each year on the night we'd come down from the covenant-stone. "Any of it. The price."

I considered. Genuinely considered, because she deserved the truth and always had, even when the truth was ugly — *especially* then.

"I regret Vaelmoor," I said. "The village that burned before we got there. I regret every refugee I couldn't carry. I regret that Aldric fell instead of standing in that circle and taking the place we offered him — not because he deserved mercy, but because he was a man who'd lost a brother to the old arithmetic, and he died believing the lie that killed his brother, and that's a waste even in an enemy." I turned our joined hands over, studied her knuckles, the old sword calluses, the ring she wore now that had been her rank and was just a ring. "I don't regret the price. You can't regret a price when you know exactly what you'd have paid otherwise. I regret the cost. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," Serah said softly. "They're not."

"And you? Knight of Greywater. Do you miss the order? The banners? Being somebody's captain?"

She was quiet a long moment. "I miss knowing whose side I was on," she admitted. "It was simpler. Wrong, usually, but simple." Her thumb moved over my knuckles — over the old scar there, the one a century-dead prophecy had known before I did. "Then I met a smuggler who lied beautifully and carried the end of the world in their pockets, and I learned that the only side worth being on is the one you choose, and choosing is harder and colder and infinitely better." She looked at me. "No. I don't miss it. I'm free, Yara. We're both free. That was the whole point, wasn't it? Yours and mine and hers. That's what we built."

Outside, the wind rose, and the mountain hummed its low warm note, and I swear on every road I've ever walked that it sounded, just for a breath, like agreement.

* * *

Midwinter found us in Thornfell Court, which had thawed from a mausoleum into something almost like a home: lanterns in the trees that grew actual fruit now instead of memories of fruit, music in the great hall that didn't make you weep unless it wanted to, courtiers who had remembered, gradually, how to want things again.

Queen Maren presided from a chair built for comfort rather than terror, wrapped in furs, thin as winter sunlight but present, awake, *there*, watching her daughter hold court with the fierce quiet pride of someone who had once believed she would die before seeing this. When the toasts came — and at Thorn Court the toasts came often, now; the fae had discovered celebration the way a starving woman discovers bread — Maren lifted her cup to me across the hall, and mouthed two words.

*Thank you.*

I raised my cup back and mouthed the same reply I gave the mountain: *Don't mention it.*

And later, much later, when the fires had burned low and the courtiers drifted off to their own revels, Nyssara found Serah and me on the terrace overlooking the veil-line, where the border shimmered silver and smooth under the stars, and the lights of the human free cities twinkled beyond it like a second sky.

"The council ratified the border shrines today," she said, dropping onto the bench beside us with the total absence of royal bearing that was, I suspected, my fault and Serah's. "All of them. Corvane caved."

"They didn't cave," Serah said. "They negotiated."

"Corvane has never voluntarily given up anything except arguments. They caved." Nyssara grinned, sharp and bright and utterly her mother's daughter and nothing like her mother's tragedy. "Next session, the tariffs. After that—" she waved a hand at the shimmer, the whole luminous seam between worlds, "—open roads. Both directions. Pilgrims, traders, fools in love. Everything."

"Fools in love," I said. "That's a category of trade goods now?"

"It's the fastest-growing export from your side, according to my customs officers. Mostly they come looking for the places from the songs. The ford at Greywater. The covenant-stone." She glanced sideways at me, gentler now. "The waystation at Shepherd's Hollow. There's a tradition starting, you know. Travelers touching the wall where the Third Hand slept. Leaving tokens."

"What kind of tokens?"

"Buttons. Ribbons. Once, alarmingly, a live chicken."

"A *chicken*."

"It was very reverent about it."

I laughed until my ribs hurt, which is a mortal laugh, an honest laugh, the kind I'd been starved of for twenty years and have hoarded greedily ever since. Serah laughed too, leaning into my shoulder, and Nyssara watched us both with an expression I pretended not to recognize because naming it would have made us all cry, and the night was too good for crying.

Then she stood, and straightened, and became for a moment the queen she was becoming, and said: "The court asks a boon of the Third Hand. Formally. With all the pomp that implies."

"Oh no."

"The spring equinox. The reforging anniversary. We wish to honor the three hands at the covenant-stone itself. All three of us, together, before both courts." She held up a finger before I could object. "Before you say no — my mother has asked. Personally. She has not personally asked for anything in a hundred years, and I am exploiting that shamelessly."

I looked at Serah. Serah looked at me. Between us passed the whole wordless conversation of two years and one shared crown and one long road: the burned villages and the drowned chapel, the necropolis and the witch-fire, the parley and the betrayal and the forgiveness, the stone itself, and the moment three bleeding hands had pulled the shards loose from a dying prophecy and said *no, and further, and ours.*

"We'll be there," Serah said.

"Obviously we'll be there," I said. "Someone has to keep you two from making speeches."

"There will be speeches," Nyssara said serenely. "There will be *several* speeches."

"Gods help us both."

She swept off to rejoin her court, laughing, and the doors closed behind her, and Serah and I stayed on the terrace a while longer, shoulder to shoulder, watching the border-shimmer breathe.

The wind came down off the high passes, carrying the smell of snow and pine and, faintly, impossibly, of green things growing in the dead of winter. Beneath it, so soft you'd miss it if you weren't listening — and I am always listening, now; it's the one sense I never lost — the mountains hummed their low, warm, patient note.

*Thank you,* said Thornfell.

"Don't mention it," I said, and took Serah's hand, and we went in out of the cold, together, into the light and noise and speeches of a world that had, against every prophecy, survived — not because anyone was willing to die for it, but because three people finally refused to let it demand that anyone should.

The roads were open. The crown was wild, and new, and shared.

And for the first time in my life, every step I took next was mine to choose.
