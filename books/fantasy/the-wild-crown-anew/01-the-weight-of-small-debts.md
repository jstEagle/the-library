## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

There are three rules to balancing an impossible ledger, and I learned all of them the hard way before I was old enough to drink legally in the Low Quarter.

One: never write a number you can't defend. Two: never trust a debt that smiles at you. Three — and this is the one that matters — always count twice, because the things we keep have teeth.

I was counting for the third time when the bottled storm started humming again.

"None of that," I told it. The jar sat on the corner of my desk where it had sat for six years, ever since Odile's mother had hauled it up three flights of stairs and deposited it with the words *interest accruing, tell no one*. It was a small storm, as storms go — big enough to ruin a wedding, not big enough to end a town — and every year on the anniversary of its bottling it remembered what it was and sulked against the glass like a cat denied the door.

The hum was normal. What wasn't normal was the ledger beneath my hands.

I run the accounts of the Guild of Impossible Things. That sounds grander than it is. Most days it means tallying borrowed shadows against their return dates, reconciling the interest on a decade of luck sold in thimbles, making sure the woman in Harrow Lane who traded her Tuesday afternoons for a singing kettle hasn't been quietly shorted. Impossible things obey arithmetic like anything else. They just resent it more.

My pen stopped over column nine.

Column nine was mine. My own entries — the ones I'd written, in my own hand, recording my own work: appraisals, audits, the small daily debts of a keeper's life. I know my handwriting the way you know your own face in a mirror, including its flaws. The *y* that drifts. The way I cross my sevens twice because Merrick once teased me about it and I never stopped.

The entry for the ninth of the Frost Month was gone.

Not crossed out. Not blotted. Gone — the way a word goes when you reach for it and find only the shape of where it lived. There was ink-stain memory on the page, a faint ghost of pressure where my pen had pressed, and nothing else.

I turned back eight pages. Another missing. Then another. Eleven entries in my own hand, vanished, and here is the part I want you to understand, the part I've chewed on every night since: the page didn't look damaged. It looked *finished*. Clean margins, balanced sums, as though whatever should have filled that space had been removed by someone who believed the space itself was the mistake.

I did what any competent keeper does. I counted twice. Then a third time, which violates rule three only if you're a coward about it.

Eleven entries. All mine. Nothing belonging to anyone else touched.

I thought, reasonably enough: someone in this guild is editing me.

And then, because I am who I am, I poured myself the last of the black tea, pulled the oldest volume off the shelf behind my desk — the Ledger Prime, chained shut with three locks and a superstition — and did not open it. Not yet. You don't go straight to the deepest water when you've found one footprint on the beach. You check who else has been walking there.

* * *

The Guildhall in autumn smells of tallow, wet stone, and the particular sweetness of bottled weather going slightly stale. I carried my ledger down two floors to the Counting Room, where Master Quill-keeper Anselm was arguing with a shelf.

"You're leaning," I told him.

"I am negotiating," said Anselm. The shelf, which held forty years of deferred wishes, leaned harder. "It wants re-cataloguing. I've told it we'll get to it."

"Its interest compounds weekly. Tell it Friday."

He grunted, which from Anselm is a treaty. I settled at the long table and began the morning's reconciliation, and I'll admit something here that I'd deny under oath: I watched the other keepers. Watched Brenna initial her delivery receipts. Watched young Piotr copy out the terms of a haunted mirror's rental. Nobody hesitated over blank spaces. Nobody stared at a page the way I wanted to stare at mine. Whatever was eating my handwriting, it was eating selectively.

That should have frightened me more than it did. At the time, mostly, it made me feel singled out, and there is a petty little corner of every accountant that finds being audited by the universe almost flattering.

The delivery came at midmorning.

Routine, supposedly. A courier from the Crown Road, mud to the knees, carrying a sealed case for the guild's attention — the usual traffic of a kingdom that runs on arrangements nobody wants written down too clearly. We receive royal business perhaps four times a year. Tolls on miracles. Tariffs on prophecy. The crown taxes everything it cannot explain, which keeps our ledgers fat and our guildmaster rich in ways he does not discuss.

Sable Vane came down himself to receive it.

You should understand Sable before I describe him. He is not a villain out of a ballad — villains have the decency to arrive with weather. Sable is a neat man, a quiet man, the sort of guildmaster who remembers your mentor's name years after she's dead and asks after your mother's cough with genuine warmth. He has run the Guild of Impossible Things for thirty years, and in thirty years I have never once seen him surprised.

He was not surprised now. That was the first wrong thing.

"From the palace," the courier said, and set the case on the table between us.

"Standard assessment," Sable said, to me, without being asked. "Yara will handle it." And he smiled his neat smile, and he left, and the case sat there breathing dust into the light.

Here is the second thing I want you to understand: I have opened four hundred royal assessments. They come in gray folios, stamped with the customs office, itemized in the boring hand of clerks who hate their lives. This one was different before I even touched it. The seal was wrong. Not the customs office. The royal seal — the crowned stag, pressed deep, in wax so dark it was nearly black, the color wax turns when it's been mixed with something that used to be alive.

I broke it anyway. Rule three. Count twice; open once.

Inside was a single page.

Old paper. Older than paper has any right to be in this kingdom — vellum gone the brown of tea leaves, soft at the edges, dense with writing in a script I did not recognize and yet, God help me, half-knew, the way you half-know a song from a dream. Across the top, burned rather than written, stood the royal stag. Burned clean through in places. Whoever had marked this page hadn't stamped it. They'd branded it.

And beneath the burning, one line in trade-script, the plain common hand we use for sums:

*Balance outstanding since the founding. Payment rendered annually. Do not audit.*

Do not audit.

I am the keeper of the Ledger of the Guild of Impossible Things. Telling me not to audit something is like telling rain not to fall. But my hands were steady in a way that felt borrowed, and my eyes kept snagging on the phrase above it — *since the founding* — and on the strange glyphs threaded through the older script, glyphs I had seen exactly once before.

They matched the chain-marks on the spine of the Ledger Prime.

I sat very still. Around me the Counting Room went on: Anselm negotiating with his shelf, Piotr mis-copying a clause, the storm in my desk upstairs humming its small furious hum. Everything ordinary. Everything fine.

Except my own entries were vanishing, and the crown had sent us a page that predates the crown, and the guildmaster had received it without surprise, and somewhere in this building there was a book locked with three locks and a superstition that knew the language on this page the way a mother knows a child's cry.

*Payment rendered annually,* the page said.

Paid in what? By whom? To whom?

I looked down at my hands — a keeper's hands, ink-stained, competent, eleven entries lighter than they should be — and for the first time in six years of balancing impossible accounts, I wondered whether I was the auditor in this guild, or simply the most recent line item.

The storm upstairs hummed. Somewhere below, in the vault where we keep what cannot be priced, something answered it.

I folded the page along its old creases, tucked it inside my coat, next to my heart, where all the worst debts live.

Then I went to find the key to the Ledger Prime — and discovered that Sable Vane already had it.
