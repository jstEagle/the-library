## 2. A Child at the Door

The frost-moon came up the color of a coin left too long in a poor man's pocket, and I was in the counting room, arguing with a bottled storm.

Not arguing, exactly. Reconciling. There is a difference, though the storm would have told you otherwise. It had been lent to us by the Weathermakers of the Salt Coast eleven years past — one thunderstorm, medium violence, two hail events included free of charge — and it had never come back. Storms are like that. They sit on their shelf and sulk and gain interest, and every year the entry in my ledger grew a little heavier, until the debt of weather was worth more than the guildhouse itself.

I dipped my pen. *One storm, aged eleven years. Accrued temper: considerable.*

And then I stopped, because the ink was wrong.

Not the color — the guild buys its black from a widow in Lowmarsh who grinds it with grave-dirt and patience, and it has always been the color of a moonless night. The wrongness was subtler. The words I'd written a moment ago were still there, but the words above them — my own hand, my own dates, an entry I remembered making about a borrowed shadow returned slightly chewed — had faded to a ghost of themselves. Not crossed out. *Erased.* As though the ledger had simply decided I'd never written them.

I pressed my thumb against the page. Paper doesn't lie; that's the first thing they teach you here, before you learn a single glyph. Paper remembers everything it has ever held, if you know how to ask.

The paper said: nothing. Smooth as a river stone. Whatever had been written there hadn't been blotted or scraped away. It had been *unwritten*.

"Yara." Odile's voice through the door, low and quick, the way she spoke only when something was genuinely wrong rather than merely on fire. "You'd better come down."

* * *

The guild of Impossible Things keeps odd hours because impossible things keep worse ones. By the time I reached the entrance hall, half the members had assembled in various states of undress and indignation — Merrick wrapped in his writing-cloak over his nightshirt like a dishtowel pretending to be a king, old Fen hunched by the brazier, and Sable Vane standing at the great door with her hands folded, calm as a woman watching rain she ordered.

Beyond her, on the threshold, stood a boy.

He was perhaps eight years old, though it was hard to say. Mud had claimed him from hair to boots — real mud, riverine, threaded with silt and something that might have been moss or might have been older. His eyes were the unsettling pale gray of water over limestone. And he was talking.

Not crying, not stammering. Talking, steadily and urgently, in a language that made my back teeth ache. It wasn't any tongue I knew, and I know most of them, because knowing languages is most of what a ledger-keeper does. It wasn't trade-cant or court-speech or the hill dialects or anything the Salt Coast traders gargled. The sounds were round and old, vowels worn smooth like stones in a streambed, consonants that seemed to arrive a half-second after he decided on them.

Nobody moved. Then Fen, who is older than several small nations, whispered something under his breath that I will charitably call a prayer.

"What is he saying?" Merrick demanded.

"I don't know," I said. And then, because honesty is a habit even when it's inconvenient: "But I've seen those sounds before."

Sable turned her head just enough to look at me. "Where?"

"In the oldest volume." I hesitated. "In *the* oldest volume. The one we don't lend, don't copy, and don't discuss. The glyphs match. Not approximately — exactly. He's speaking Ledger."

That got their attention. Every keeper learns, eventually, that the pre-kingdom glyphs in our founding volume aren't pictures of words. They're a transcription of speech — a language someone actually spoke, back when promises were new and hadn't yet learned to be careful. We transcribe oaths into it for binding-work, the way you might write a contract in a tongue your counterparty can't read. Nobody speaks it. It's a dead language. Dead things don't knock on doors covered in river mud.

"He says—" the boy was still going, hands moving now, urgent, and I caught fragments the way you catch fish with your bare hands, mostly slip but occasionally something wriggling home. "I think he's asking if this is the house of the keeping. House of the keeping. He wants to know if we—"

"Whether we remember," Sable finished.

The hall went very quiet. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a borrowed shadow rattled its shelf, which shadows do when they're nervous.

"You understand him," I said to Sable. It wasn't a question, and she didn't treat it as one.

"I understand enough." She stepped forward and crouched, unhurried, bringing her face level with the boy's. Up close I could see what the rest couldn't from the door: the mud on him wasn't recent. It had dried and cracked and dried again, layered like tree rings. He'd been walking a long time, or else he'd walked a long way in a single night, which amounts to the same impossibility. "You're safe," she said to him — in trade-speech, slow — and then, softer, in sounds I could barely parse: "*Veth ka lorren. Veth ka lorren.*"

The boy went still. Then he reached out and touched her folded hands, once, gently, the way you touch a door you're afraid might be a dream, and answered in a rush of that old round language, and whatever he said made Sable close her eyes for exactly one second too long.

Then she stood, and she was the guildmaster again, and her voice was all business and no weather.

"Fen, heat broth. Merrick, fetch dry clothes from the donation chest. Yara—" and here she looked at me, and something in the look made my stomach drop before she said another word, "—bring the founding volume to the east room. We'll want it."

"We'll want it for what?"

"For reference." She smiled, and it was her good smile, the one that had talked creditors off ledges and apprentices off ceilings for thirty years. "He'll need somewhere to sleep tonight, Yara. That's all this is."

That's all this is. I have been a member of this guild since I was nine years old, and I can count on one hand the times Sable Vane has said *that's all this is*, and on zero fingers the times it has been true.

* * *

The east room is where the guild keeps what it doesn't display: misfiled miracles, debts in dispute, the founding volume chained to its lectern like a dog that bites. I carried it to the reading table while Sable saw the boy settled — bathed, fed, installed in the little room off the gallery that we keep, according to the inventory, for "arrivals."

Arrivals. Plural. As though this were a category of event the guildhouse had been furnished in advance for.

I opened the founding volume to the first pages and set my lamp beside it, and that was when I noticed the bookmark.

We don't use bookmarks in the founding volume. You don't leave things in a book that old; the book objects, in ways involving headaches. But there it lay — a ribbon of green silk, marking a page roughly two-thirds through, and the ribbon was new. Someone had placed it recently. Recently enough that I could guess who had been in this room, and how long ago they had known to prepare.

The page it marked held a passage in the pre-kingdom glyphs, and beneath it, in a later hand — a translation, old and cramped, the ink browned with centuries:

*And when the crown is hungry and the years run thin, one shall come out of the water and the mud, speaking the first words, being the first promise. Keep him. Feed him. Wait.*

Keep him. Feed him. Wait.

I read it three times. My hands were steady, which surprised me; inside, something was doing arithmetic it didn't want to finish. The boy downstairs. The room prepared. The ribbon placed. Sable saying *that's all this is* in the voice she uses when it isn't.

Feed him.

I copied the passage into my working notebook, letter by letter, glyph by glyph, because that is what I am for. My pen scratched in the silence, and the sound of it suddenly felt loud, guilty, like whispering in a temple. Halfway through, I realized my own entries in the notebook's margin — notes from last month, last week — were gone. Unwritten. Smooth pages where my handwriting should have been.

Something was eating my words. Something had been eating them for a while, and tonight it had sent a child to the door, and the guildmaster had known.

Down the hall, faintly, I heard the boy singing. A small tuneless song, round-vowelled and old, and beneath it — I swear this, and I have sworn stranger things in ledgers that bound — beneath it I heard the guildhouse itself humming along. Floorboards and window-glass, keeping time.

The founding volume's pages stirred, though no window was open, and settled open on a page I hadn't marked:

*On the night of the frost-moon, bring him in. The account comes due.*

I looked at my notebook. At my vanished words. At the green ribbon lying on the table like a shed skin.

Then I blew out the lamp and went downstairs, because the boy was calling my name — my name, in a dead language, pronounced perfectly — and some debts you answer in person.
