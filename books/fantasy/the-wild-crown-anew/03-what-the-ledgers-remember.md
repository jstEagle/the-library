## 3. What the Ledgers Remember

The ledgers did not sleep, which was more than I could say for the rest of the guild.

I'd learned that in my first year, back when I still believed the Guild of Impossible Things was mostly a filing problem. Bottled storms needed turning every third day or they condensed into tantrums. Borrowed shadows had to be aired monthly or they went sour and started casting *other* people's shapes. And somewhere on the third sub-basement level, in a vault kept at a temperature that made your teeth ache, sat the oldest volume we owned: the First Ledger, bound in something I'd always assumed was very well-tanned leather and had recently begun to suspect was not leather at all.

It was also, as of three nights ago, awake.

Not literally. Ledgers don't open their own covers. But when I carried it up to my workroom by lamplight — against about four separate guild regulations, all of which I'd signed, all of which I now regarded as suggestions — the pages turned themselves with a faint papery sigh, like a patient settling into a chair before a long complaint.

"All right," I told it. "I'm listening."

Because here is what nobody tells you about being Keeper of a ledger like ours: the entries aren't records. They're *residues*. Every impossible thing the guild has ever handled left a trace of itself in the ink, and ink remembers what it touched. Which meant that when a mud-caked child stood in our doorway speaking a language dead before the first kingdom was a rumor, and Sable Vane ordered him locked in the east room "for his own safety" in the voice she uses when safety means containment — I knew exactly where to go looking for a translation.

The boy — Tam, because he'd answered to nothing else, though I suspected he'd have answered to anything said gently enough — had been with us two days now. Two days of Cook smuggling him honeyed porridge through the door slot. Two days of Odile pressing her face to the gap under the door making faces until I heard laughter in a language no living throat should know how to make. Two days of Sable asking me, each morning with pleasant unconcern, whether the ledgers had "said anything useful," which is not a question a guildmaster asks unless she already knows the answer.

So I set the First Ledger on my desk, pinned the frost-moon chart beside it for reference, and began the slow work of teaching a book to speak.

* * *

Translation through residue isn't reading so much as eavesdropping. You hold the phrase you're hunting — I'd coaxed fragments from Tam that afternoon, sitting cross-legged outside his door while he traced patterns in the dust, sounds like water deciding to be words — and you wait for the old ink to shiver in recognition.

The first phrase took an hour. It meant *hunger*, but a specific hunger, the kind you swear with rather than suffer. The second came faster: *crown*. The third stopped me cold with my pen hovering over the page:

*I am the promise. I am what was promised.*

I wrote it down because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

Ledger-logic, if you let it, will take you where the guild never intended to go. I followed the entry backward through cross-references older than the kingdom's founding charter, through a margin note in handwriting I recognized with a lurch — my mentor Aldous's, from thirty years before I was born, *see attached debt, do not audit, DO NOT AUDIT* — until the First Ledger lay open across a spread of vellum so old it had gone the color of tea, and the whole shape of the thing assembled itself in front of me the way debts do: slowly, then all at once.

There had been a famine. Before Thornhold, before the royal line had a number after its name, there had been a famine so complete that the chroniclers gave up on words like *scarcity* and simply drew empty bowls, row upon row of them, hundreds of pages. And there had been a young king — not yet crowned, the crown still green wood and wildflowers in those drawings — who walked into a place that is not on any map the guild keeps and made a bargain.

He swore an oath. Not to a god, not to the land, not to any power with a name you could write down. He swore to the crown itself. To the idea of it. That his people would never hunger again if only they would love the crown forever, feed it their loyalty, keep it fed and fat and *alive*.

And the oath, being sworn with everything he had, did what oaths do when they're strong enough.

It stood up and became a person.

I read the passage four times. The ink didn't waver; it was I who couldn't hold still. A child of mud and frost-moonlight, walking out of the dark onto our doorstep two nights past, speaking the tongue the oath was sworn in — not a messenger *about* the oath, not a symbol of it, but the oath itself, given legs and a crooked smile and a habit of eating honeyed porridge like it might be taken away.

Tam was the promise. And every renewal since — every coronation, every feast day, every *long live the king* shouted by ten thousand throats — had been a meal.

I put down my pen. My workroom felt very small and very cold, and somewhere below us, in the east room, a boy was probably asleep, trusting us, and the guild had locked the door.

* * *

Tam dreamed the way water dreams, in pieces, all of them true.

*Mud between toes. The door with the iron slot and the face that makes shapes — Odile-face, funny-face, safe-face. Words come wrong in the mouth here, round words, soft words, not the old words that are sharp like winter.*

*But underneath, older than the mud: standing in a field where nothing grew, watching a young man kneel in the dirt with flowers in his hair instead of gold. The young man is crying and smiling at once, which Tam knows is possible, has always been possible, because Tam is made of the moment someone did both at maximum.*

*"Let them never starve," the young man says. "Take my remembering, take my years, take whatever a crown eats. Only let them eat."*

*And something vast and green and patient leans down out of the sky — no, out of the idea of the sky — and says yes.*

*Tam is the yes.*

*Then fragments. Centuries of fragments. Coronations like banquets. Grief like weather. A king growing older without aging, forgetting his mother's face, forgetting the famine, forgetting why he knelt, keeping only the kneeling. And always, always, hands feeding the crown — guild-hands, ink-stained hands, carrying pages that taste of years.*

*Tam does not understand the ink-stained hands. But one of them belongs to the man who sits outside his door and reads aloud even though Tam can't understand him, voice going gentle on the hard parts, and Tam thinks: this one feeds differently.*

*This one reads like the words are food for him too.*

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. Instead I did what Keepers do worst and most often: I audited something I'd been told not to audit.

Aldous's marginal note — *DO NOT AUDIT*, underlined twice, in the hand that taught me mine — should have been enough. It wasn't. Because next to it, squeezed into the margin like a confession too large for its space, was a second line I'd somehow never seen before, though I must have looked at this page a hundred times:

*If it ever walks in, Noor — it isn't a foundling. It's a receipt.*

My name. His handwriting. A warning written decades ago, waiting.

*A receipt.* As in: proof of payment. As in: something given in exchange for something else. I sat with that word while the candle burned down and the bottled storm on the shelf above me muttered in its glass, restless, like it knew.

The guild had been expecting Tam. Not vaguely, not in the way you expect winter — specifically, deliberately, the way you expect a delivery. And Sable hadn't been surprised at the door. She'd been *punctual*.

I thought about the page from Chapter One of my own dread — the one burned with the royal seal, the routine delivery that wasn't. I thought about how many members had died comfortably, conveniently, on schedule, their pensions paid in full. I thought about Aldous, gone eight years now, healthy as an ox one season and ash the next, and how Sable had called it "the toll," and how everyone had nodded, because the guild has always had tolls.

Tolls to what?

I looked down at the First Ledger, open to the oath, to the drawing of the green crown, and I understood that I was asking the wrong question. Tolls to *whom* was the question. Or to *what*.

The boy down the hall wasn't just the original promise. He was the original *price tag* — the receipt for whatever the guild had been paying, all these centuries, to keep something fed. And if the guild was still paying—

A sound outside my door. Small. Bare feet on stone.

I opened it and Tam stood there, having picked a lock I'd have sworn was beyond any mortal fingers, holding out his cupped hands like an offering. In them: a honeycomb, stolen, presumably, from Cook's stores, broken open and glistening.

He said something in the old tongue. Soft. Then, when I only stared, he pointed at me, then at himself, then pressed his fist to his chest — over the heart — and repeated it, slower, like a man teaching a horse a word it will need to trust.

The First Ledger, still open on my desk behind me, rustled its pages without any draft at all. And when I glanced at it, fresh ink was rising on the margins, translating, in a hand that was almost Aldous's and almost mine and almost nobody's:

*He says: I know you. You are the one who was promised.*

*He says: I have come to be unmade, little keeper. Ask me what I cost.*

Down the corridor, behind me, the east room door stood open, and from the direction of the guildmaster's tower, a light had just come on.
