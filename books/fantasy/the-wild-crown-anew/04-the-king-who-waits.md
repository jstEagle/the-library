## 4. The King Who Waits

I dreamed in the wrong order again.

That is what happens when you are made of a sentence. Sentences do not have to obey time. They begin wherever they like and end wherever they please, and everything between them is negotiable.

So: I was old before I was young. I remembered dying before I remembered being born. And I remembered the crown — oh, I always remembered the crown, even when I could not remember my mother's face, which was the whole point of it, wasn't it, the great clever cruelty of it — before I remembered why anyone had ever wanted such a heavy circle of green iron in the first place.

The guild cellar smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of the ledger they kept me near, as though paper could soothe me. Perhaps it could. Paper is only promises that have learned to sit still. I have always found sitting still difficult.

My name was Tam now. The keeper-boy with the ink-stained fingers had given it to me on the second night, crouched at the bars of the storeroom door with a heel of bread and no fear at all, which was either bravery or foolishness, and having been both myself I did not judge him for it. Tam. A small name. A pocket-sized name. I turned it over in my mouth like a river stone and decided I would keep it, at least until something better came along, or until I came apart, whichever happened first.

But dreams do not care what names you keep.

* * *

In the dream I am nineteen and the wheat is dead.

Not brown — dead is browner than brown. It stands in the fields like the stubble of a shaved head, gray-white, rattling in wind that carries no rain and has carried no rain for three years. I walk through it because a king must walk through his failing fields; the priests say so, and the priests write things down, and written things have a way of becoming true whether or not they were true to begin with. I know this intimately now. I *am* this now.

Behind me walks my chancellor, Aldous, who has brought me here on purpose, though he pretends we are merely inspecting. Aldous has a way of pretending that is indistinguishable from sincerity, which is the most dangerous kind.

"There is an old remedy," he says. "Older than the kingdom."

"I thought nothing was older than the kingdom."

"Nothing *is*, majesty. That is rather the problem. This remedy predates the problem."

He leads me to a hill where a crown grows out of the ground.

I want to be clear about this, because the chroniclers got it wrong later, and the chroniclers were working from documents I myself signed, so the error is at least partly mine. It was not forged by any smith. No hammer touched it. It grew — a ring of living wood and green iron, or iron gone green the way copper does, wound through with vines that had leaves out of season, leaves in the dead heart of the famine-year, glossy and wet and utterly indifferent to the drought. It sat on a stone altar older than any altar I knew how to build, and it was beautiful the way a trap is beautiful, which is to say you only see the beauty, never the trap, until your foot is already in it.

"It asks one thing," Aldous said. "One promise, renewed each year on the frost-moon. In exchange, the rains return."

"What promise?"

Aldous looked at the wheat. He was very good at looking at anything except me. "Loyalty," he said. "Sworn. Renewed. Given freely." He paused. "It insists on the freely."

And I — nineteen, starving kingdom behind me, a million mouths opening and closing like the fields themselves were chewing — I knelt.

Here is what no one tells you about oaths: they are not words. Words are just the shape oaths leave in the air after they pass through, the way a footprint is not the walker. When I spoke the vow, something reached up out of that green iron and took hold of the *meaning* of me, the part underneath my name, and folded it into itself. It did not hurt. That frightened me more than pain would have. It felt like being *agreed with*.

That night, it rained.

* * *

The years go strangely in a dream, so let me count them by their costs instead.

Year one, renewal: I gave it the memory of my coronation. I woke knowing I had been crowned — there were documents, portraits, a very tedious annual feast — but the day itself was gone, like a room in my own house that had been bricked over while I slept. The Wearer wore my loyalty and I wore its crown and the rains came, and everyone agreed this was a fair price, and I agreed loudest of all, because agreeing was becoming easier than thinking.

Year four: my wedding day. My queen was patient with me. She described our vows to me every anniversary with a kindness that broke something in her face a little more each year, and I loved her — I knew I loved her the way you know a fire is warm without remembering striking the match.

Year nine: the faces of the farmers. All of them at once. I had walked those dead fields and then those living ones, and I had known every man and woman who bent them, and the Wearer took the knowing and left me the walking.

Do you see the architecture of it? Each year I renewed the oath, and each renewal cost a memory, and each memory lost made the next renewal easier to swear, because you cannot grieve what you cannot recall. It was not feeding on my loyalty. Loyalty was only the dish. What it ate was the *me* that chose to be loyal. It farmed my consent, harvest after harvest, and left me just enough king to sign the papers.

I understood all of this eventually. That is the worst part. Not ignorance — understanding, arriving too late and staying uselessly lucid in fragments, a man watching himself be spent down like coin by his own hand. The Wearer needed me lucid sometimes. An oath sworn by a husk is worth nothing; it needs a living will to bind, the way a fire needs living wood.

So it let me wake. It let me remember enough to be afraid. Fear makes such rich loyalty.

* * *

Now the dream turns, as dreams do, and I am in the present tense, if a creature made of grammar can be said to have one.

I am in the guild cellar, and I am small — I am wearing a body the size of the promise I once was, which is either poetry or arithmetic, and honestly it might be both — and above me, boots are crossing the guild floor.

I hear them before I see them, because my hearing belongs to the oath, and an oath listens for its own name the way a dog listens for its master. Courtiers' boots. Courtiers' boots have a particular rhythm: unhurried, entitled, the cadence of men who have never once had to run anywhere. Two pairs. Maybe three.

"—the asset," one of them is saying, in the flat court accent that swallows its own consonants like they owe it money. "The Guildmaster understands the situation. The renewal approaches and the asset must be delivered. There are allowances in the arrangement for exactly this."

Allowances. Yes. I knew those allowances. I had watched them flow across the ledgers for centuries, disguised as tithes, as pensions, as "longevity stipends," as line items in a hundred different hands. Years. The guild paid the crown in years — members' years, harvested quietly, siphoned from the long-lived the way a landlord skims a well — and the crown poured those years into the Wearer to keep it fed between renewals, and the Wearer kept the rains coming, and the rains kept everyone from asking why a guild of impossible things had a standing contract with a throne.

Sable Vane's voice answers, smooth as ledger-leather. "The asset is unsettled. New arrivals disturb it."

"The asset is a *promise*, Guildmaster. Promises don't get disturbed. They get *kept*." A pause, the sound of a glove flexing. "Unless the guild intends to renegotiate. We can discuss what renegotiation costs. We've discussed it before."

Silence below. I press my small hands against the cold floor and feel the building around me the way I feel all buildings that hold oaths — as a body feels a held breath. Somewhere upstairs, Yara Nystrom is awake. I don't know how I know that; perhaps promises can smell ink. His heartbeat is quick and stubborn, like a bird deciding whether a window is sky.

He has been reading the oldest volume. I felt the pages turn. Every glyph he traced with his finger tickled somewhere in my chest, the way your own name spoken softly wakes you gentler than shouting.

He is close to understanding. And understanding, in my long experience, is the most dangerous thing a person can carry into a room containing the crown's men.

* * *

Here is the compassion nobody asks me for: I pity the king.

Not the abstract crown. The man under it, the one still breathing in Thornhold with less than half of himself remaining, lucid in scraps, waking some mornings into terror because he cannot remember why he is afraid and the fear is all that's left. He begged, once — I carry the begging, it is stitched into my seams — he begged the Wearer directly, alone in the green dark of the throne room: *take the rest, take it all, but give back her name.* Her name. His queen's. The Wearer considered this with the patience of weather and refused, because a king who remembers what he sold might stop selling.

So he rules on. Half a man, holding up half a bargain, terrified of freedom because freedom means facing the arithmetic of everything he traded away, and terrified of bondage because — well. Because he has read the terms. He wrote them.

Victim and jailer, locked in the same cell, and the cell has a view of excellent gardens.

And I? I am what he swore. I am the promise, walking around in borrowed child-flesh, waiting in a cellar under a guild that sells lifespans by the page. When they come for me — and they will come, tonight or tomorrow, boots and gloves and allowances — they will find either a boy or an unbinding, depending entirely on whether the ink-stained keeper decides what I am worth.

Above me, the outer door opens. Cold air pours down the stairs like water finding low ground.

"Where is it?" says a new voice, and this one is not a courtier's. This one is dry as dead wheat and old as the hill, and every glyph in my chest stands up at attention.

I know that voice. I knelt to it once, in another life I mostly cannot remember.

It has come to collect.
