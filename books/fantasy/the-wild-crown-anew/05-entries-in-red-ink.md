## 5. Entries in Red Ink

There are three kinds of lies in a ledger, my mentor used to say: the lie of omission, the lie of rounding, and the lie that looks you in the eye and dares you to call it one.

Edda Vos taught me bookkeeping the way other people teach knife-fighting — close in, no wasted motion, expect to bleed. She'd been dead four years when I found her name in red ink, which is a sentence I intend never to say aloud again, because saying it makes it true, and I had been doing a brisk business lately in not-true.

It started, as most catastrophes at the Guild of Impossible Things do, with an inventory discrepancy. Small. Almost charming. Three vials of bottled thunderstorm unaccounted for since the spring reconciliation, and half a shadow returned from its loan in noticeably worse condition than it left — frayed at the edges, the way shadows get when borrowers forget to feed them. Ordinary wear on impossible goods. I logged it, sighed at it, and moved on, because that is what a keeper does: he balances the world's strangest accounts and pretends the arithmetic doesn't occasionally whisper back.

But the discrepancy nagged. Not the storm or the shadow — those were noise. What nagged was the *pattern*. Every quarter, going back further than I cared to count, a handful of entries went missing. Not misfiled. Missing. And here is the thing I hadn't told anyone, the thing that kept me up past the frost-moon hours while Tam slept locked in the east room speaking his old tongue to the dark: entries in *my own hand* had begun vanishing too. Little ones. A receipt for a borrowed year of good luck. A note about the guild's interest in a haunted orchard. Gone from the page like water into sand, leaving only the faintest depression in the paper, the way a word does when someone has erased it carefully rather than crossed it out.

Ledgers don't lose things. Ledgers are the one honest institution in any kingdom. So if things were disappearing, it wasn't the ledger's doing.

It was someone's.

* * *

I started after midnight, when the guildhouse goes quiet in that particular way old buildings have — not silent, but listening. The Archive of Unresolved Accounts sits behind the third door on the lower level, behind a lock Edda picked so many times she'd worn the brass smooth. The Guild of Impossible Things keeps two ledgers for everything: the working ledger, which members see, and the resolution ledger, which nobody sees, which holds whatever the working ledger can't bear to admit. Debts we've forgiven. Debts we've abandoned. Debts that were never ours to carry but got carried anyway, because someone with authority signed for them.

The oldest volume lives on a lectern under glass, chained like a dangerous animal. I didn't need to break the chain. I needed only to be the youngest keeper in guild history, trusted with the master key, and to hate myself for exactly as long as it took to turn it.

The hidden pages weren't hidden, precisely. That's the trick of it — they were *bound wrong*, sewn into the spine's gutter where a page would naturally fold out of sight, visible only if you knew to break the binding. Sable Vane, our guildmaster, had ordered the Archive re-bound two years ago. New spine, new thread, very tidy work by a binder from the capital. Tidy enough that no auditor would ever notice unless she was already suspicious and slightly obsessive and had recently watched a mud-caked child speak a language older than kingdoms straight out of the guild's founding volume.

I broke the binding with a bone folder and Edda's letter opener, and I told myself the shaking in my hands was the cold.

The pages were written in red ink. Not seal-red, not the ceremonial crimson the guild uses for debts of honor. This was a duller color, iron-heavy, the shade blood goes when it dries in a ledger's cold paper. And the entries —

Entries are supposed to record transactions. What was recorded here was a transaction the way a butcher's bill records a friendship.

*Received: fourteen years, Halden Ostrick, journeyman. Rendered to the Crown, per renewal schedule.*

*Received: nine years, Maret Fenwick, scribe second class. Rendered per schedule.*

*Received: thirty-one years, Edda Vos, senior keeper. Rendered in full. Note: subject cooperative. Note: subject unaware.*

I read that line eleven times. *Subject unaware.* Edda Vos, who could spot a forged oath at forty paces, who taught me that a ledger's first loyalty is to truth, had handed over thirty-one years without knowing it, and someone had written *cooperative* beside her name like she was a dairy cow.

The pattern resolved itself as I turned pages, cold and clean as arithmetic. Every quarter for sixty years — longer than I'd been alive, longer than most of the members I loved had been alive — small withdrawals. Years skimmed off the top of ordinary guild life. A fever that lingered too long. An accident on the stairs that took a little more than it should have. Members aging faster than their parents had, dying quieter, and the guild mourning them honestly because the guild itself didn't know. Only Sable knew. Only Sable signed. His mark sat at the bottom of every page in red ink, patient as a spider.

And at the head of each page, always the same counterparty notation: *Rendered to the Verdant Wearer, against the Original Debt.*

The Original Debt. Capital letters. Older than any entry in the working ledgers. The phrase reached up out of the page and closed around something Tam had said to me through the ledger's translation three nights ago, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the east room, pointing at glyphs with a grimy finger: *the crown eats what is sworn to it.* I had thought then that he meant oaths. Loyalty. The abstract coin of fealty.

I had not understood that the guild had been paying in meat.

* * *

Sable Vane kept evening office in the map room, where the walls are hung with territories that don't exist anymore and the light comes down amber through windows worth more than most villages. He was mending a quill when I came in, which he did whenever he wanted to look harmless. It was a good performance. It had fooled me for nineteen years.

"Yara." He didn't look up. "You're in the Archive after hours again. I can smell the binding glue on you."

"You re-bound the resolution ledgers two years ago."

"I did."

"You hid pages in the gutters."

The quill stopped moving. When he looked up, his face did the thing I had spent my whole apprenticeship trusting: it went kind, and tired, and honest. "Sit down, Yara."

"I'll stand."

"You'll want to sit." He set the quill down with terrible gentleness. "You've read them. Of course you have; I trained you. Then you know the guild is solvent because the guild pays its bills, and you know what the largest bill is." He folded his hands. "You've met the boy. You've seen the founding volume match him glyph for glyph. So don't stand there looking betrayed — betrayed is for people who didn't do the reading."

"The reading says you sold Edda Vos's life."

"The reading says the Wearer collected what the Original Debt required." His voice stayed level, reasonable, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "Sixty years ago the crown came to us — not to the king, understand, the king hasn't signed anything in centuries except what the Wearer puts in front of him — and it called in the debt our founders took on when they helped bind the first oath. Do you know what happens if the Wearer starves? I've seen the projections. The oaths unravel backward. Every promise sworn to that crown comes due at once, every border, every treaty, every wedding vow made under royal law. Kingdoms don't fall in famines, Yara. They fall in *accounting failures*. I chose to pay in installments. Years. A few at a time, taken from people who'd have lost them anyway, spread thin enough that no one felt the blade." He smiled thinly. "You balance impossible ledgers. Tell me the alternative was kinder."

I wanted to say something magnificent. What came out was: "She trusted you."

"Yes," Sable said simply. "That was the hardest part. She'd have volunteered, you know, if I'd asked. That's why I didn't ask. Consent complicates the magic. The Wearer takes best what isn't given freely — free gifts taste like nothing to it." He rose and came around the desk, and for a moment I saw how old he actually was, how much of himself he must have fed into those pages alongside everyone else. Or maybe guildmasters exempted themselves. Maybe that was the worst line of all, and I hadn't found it yet. "Here is what happens now. You are the finest keeper this guild has produced since Vos. You will take partnership. You will keep the red pages, all of them, and one day you will sign them, because someone must and I am tired. In exchange, the boy stays fed and warm and ignorant, the guild survives, and the kingdom keeps standing. Silence, Yara. It's the cheapest thing on any shelf and the hardest to buy."

"And if I refuse?"

He looked at me with genuine sorrow, which was somehow worse than anger would have been. "Then I'll grieve you honestly at your funeral, and I'll enter the years in the ledger myself, because I have done it before and the guild goes on. That's what no one understands about keeping accounts, Yara. Eventually the ledger stops being a record of what you did." He sat back down and picked up the quill. "It starts telling you what you'll do next."

I walked out with Edda's letter opener still in my sleeve and the red pages' contents burning behind my eyes, and I made it all the way to the east room corridor before my legs remembered how to shake.

Tam was awake. He was always awake now. He looked up at me through the door grate, and he didn't speak his old language, and he didn't need the ledger to translate, because some things are legible in any tongue: he pointed one small finger at my face, then at his own chest, and mouthed a single word.

*Mine,* he mouthed. *Yours.*

They were coming for him. Sable hadn't said it outright, but the arithmetic was plain even to a heart refusing to do sums: the Wearer's appetite was growing, the quarterly installments wouldn't hold forever, and the guild had just taken delivery of the original oath made flesh — the richest account ever opened.

I had until dawn, probably less, to steal the guild's most guarded asset from under its guildmaster's nose.

Somewhere below me, in the resolution ledgers, a fresh page waited in red ink. I intended to make sure it stayed blank.
