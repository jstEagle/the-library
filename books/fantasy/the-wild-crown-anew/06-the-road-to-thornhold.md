## 6. The Road to Thornhold

*Yara*

The Guild of Impossible Things had a saying for moments like this, which was *run*, though we usually dressed it up in nicer language. *Relocate the asset. Expedite the ledger. Pursue alternative premises.* What we were doing that night, scrambling down the drainage stairs behind the guildhouse with a child made of an oath under one arm and three stolen volumes under the other, was called nothing at all, because no one had ever been foolish enough to write it down.

I wrote it down. Of course I did. Somewhere between the second landing and the reeking bottom of the cistern outflow, I scratched into my pocket ledger: *Yara Nystrom, fleeing the guild he loves, carrying the guild's oldest sin. Entry pending.*

The ink faded before I'd finished the sentence.

Tam didn't struggle. That frightened me more than anything else — a boy who had been mud and hunger and wild syllables two nights ago now walked beside me with the terrible calm of someone who has been carried by rivers before. He kept looking back at the guildhouse, not with longing. With recognition. The way you look back at a house you once lived in and burned down.

"Almost there," I told him, which was a lie, because "there" was Thornhold, the capital, four hundred miles away, and I had no plan beyond the word itself. Sable Vane would have riders on the north road by dawn. The only person in the world who might help us was a ferrywoman I hadn't spoken to in six years, who hated me, and who lived exactly one wrong turn from everything.

"Yar-ra," Tam said. My name, or his version of it, worn smooth like a stone he'd been carrying in his mouth. It was the only word of ours he liked. Everything else he said came out in the pre-kingdom tongue, all consonants like breaking branches.

"I know," I said, because whatever he meant, I probably did deserve it.

We reached the river at gray dawn, where the Greywater bends around a drowned orchard, and there she was, exactly as advertised: Odile Vasq, hauling a flat-bottomed ferry out of the shallows with one hand and arguing with a heron with the other.

She saw us and stopped mid-insult. Six years had added scars to her forearms and silver to her braid but subtracted nothing from the scowl.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard—"

"The answer's no, Ledger-boy." She pointed the heron at me, which was somehow more threatening than pointing a knife. "You show up smelling of guild soap and guilt at sunrise, with *that*" — here the heron swung toward Tam, who waved politely — "*that* walking beside you, and you want me to say yes? The last favor I did the guild cost me my ferry license, my mooring rights, and eleven years of good standing. I'm done being impossible for free."

"We can pay."

"With what, entries?" But her eyes had already gone to Tam, and something in her face shifted — the particular softening of a woman who has pulled enough drowning children out of the Greywater to recognize one that hasn't learned to drown yet. "What is he?"

"That," I said, "is going to take the whole crossing."

Odile looked at the river, then at the road behind us, where I swear even the mist seemed to be listening. Then she swore in three languages, threw the heron a fish as severance pay, and shoved off.

* * *

*Tam*

The river speaks the old words. Nobody taught it; nobody had to. Water was old when oaths were young, and it remembers the shape of every promise that ever fell into it.

The woman with iron hands does not speak them either, but she holds the pole the way the first king held his sword — not because she loves the weapon, but because she refuses to let go of it. This is also a kind of oath. The river approves.

The man-who-writes — Yarra, Yarra — sleeps sitting up against the cargo, his book open on his chest like a small dead bird. He smells of ink and fear and, underneath, of something the river knows well: *loyalty, unspent*. A whole purse of it. Things with teeth notice such things from very far away. Tam notices them too. Tam is made of noticing.

In dreams, the crown comes to him again. Green-gold, many-pointed, warm as a held hand. *Little promise,* it says, *little first-word, come home. You were sworn. Sworn things belong.*

*I was broken,* Tam answers, in the dream, in the way of dreams where you are brave. *Sworn things that are broken are owed. You owe me.*

The crown is quiet a long time. When it speaks again it sounds almost kind, which is the worst thing about it.

*Then come and collect.*

He wakes with salt on his face that isn't river spray, and finds the writing-man awake too, watching him with those ledger-keeper eyes that total everything and forgive very little.

"It spoke to you," Yarra says. Not a question.

Tam nods. Then, because the river is flowing through the right words just now, he manages the trade-tongue, heavy and strange in his mouth: "It wants... gathering. Me home. In the green place. Before frost-moon ends."

Yarra goes pale in the interesting way scribes do, all at once, like spilled milk. Behind him, the iron-handed woman curses fluently and poles faster.

* * *

*Yara*

Merrick caught up with us on the third day, which should have been the first warning.

He came limping out of a barley field near the border shrine at Wellspring Cross, dusty, hatless, and full of apologies, and Odile nearly put him in the river on principle. "Disgraced oath-scribe" undersells it; Merrick Fenwick had been expelled from the guild for falsifying a renewal rite, which in our world is roughly the moral equivalent of selling your grandmother twice. But he could read third-conjugation oath-script, which I could not, and Tam needed translating, and need makes strange family.

That's how I've decided to think of it, anyway. Family. Because somewhere between Odile teaching Tam to spit cherry pits off the ferry rail, and Merrick drawing glyph-charts in the dirt while Tam corrected him with the patience of a schoolmaster four thousand years senior, and the night we all slept under the overturned boat in the rain and Odile sang guild drinking songs until Tam laughed — laughed, a sound like a lock opening — somewhere in there, without asking anyone's permission, I acquired a family. Again. For the second time, if you counted the guild, which I was trying very hard not to.

"They'll come through the pass," Merrick said that last evening, sketching our route in the firelight. "Sable won't send many. Three or four. Quiet ones. He'll want the boy intact."

"How do you know how Sable thinks?" Odile asked, not looking up from sharpening her boathook.

"Because I thought the same way for twenty years." Merrick smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "We're all his handwriting, Odile. Some of us just got torn up sooner."

Tam was asleep against my shoulder, breathing the slow breaths of someone who dreamed in geology. I watched the fire and felt the thing I'd been refusing to feel since Wellspring Cross: that someone knew where we were. Always a day behind, never two. Riders sighted at Harrow Ford after we'd left. A toll-keeper asking odd questions an hour before we passed his booth. Coincidence is possible. Coincidence three times running is a ledger entry.

"Merrick," I said lightly. "When they caught you falsifying the rite. What did Sable actually take from you?"

His pen didn't stop moving. "Years," he said, equally lightly. "Standard penalty. Eleven of them. Why?"

"Because standard penalty is five."

The pen stopped.

For a moment the fire cracked and the night leaned in around us, and Merrick Fenwick, disgraced oath-scribe, liar, forger, looked at me across the flames with an expression I recognized because I'd seen it in the mirror for weeks now — the arithmetic of a man deciding what a truth will cost him.

"Get some sleep, Yara," he said gently. "Long road tomorrow."

I slept. I pretended to, anyway, and in the pretense I heard him get up twice in the night, and both times I told myself the second time was only him checking on Tam, because family, because need, because I wanted so badly for the warm thing we'd built on this road to be real that I was willing to audit everyone but him.

* * *

*Tam*

Last day before the shrine. Last night of the small fire and the singing woman and the sad man with the moving pen.

Tam counts his people now. The counting is new. Oaths don't count; oaths are counted. But the writing-man taught him numbers in trade-tongue, holding up fingers, laughing at Tam's accent until Tam pushed him into a ditch, and now Tam counts:

One: Yarra, who smells of unspent loyalty and reads aloud badly.
Two: Iron-hands, whose heart is a mooring rope — frayed at the end, but tied off proper.
Three: Pen-man, whose heart Tam can hear. Hearts are loud, when you are made of promises. Pen-man's heart says *forgive me forgive me* in the rhythm of a man walking to do a thing he will hate.

Tam does not tell. Telling is a spending, and Tam has watched the writing-man spend truths like coins, and seen what the spending buys. Grief. Mostly grief.

Instead Tam lies awake and listens to Pen-man slip away into the dark with a scrap of paper, and watches the stars wheel over the barley, and thinks about the crown waiting in its green city, hungry and patient and *owed*, and about the king inside it whom Tam alone remembers whole — young, desperate, meaning every word.

*We meant every word,* Tam reminds himself. That's the trap of it. That's always been the trap.

Behind him, faint on the wind, comes the sound of hooves on the far side of the ridge. Many. Moving fast.

Ahead of him, at the border shrine where the road narrows, a lantern lights that wasn't lit before.

Tam sits up in the dark, in the space between the two, and begins, quietly, in the oldest tongue, to practice the only sentence that matters:

*This debt is called due.*
