## 7. The Price Already Paid

There are three things every disgraced oath-scribe knows: how to forge a seal, how to spot a lie in ink, and how to recognize the moment his own handwriting has betrayed him.

I had been living with the third knowledge for eleven days. It sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, smooth and cold and impossible to cough up. Every night at camp, while Odile argued with the fire and Yara taught Tam words for *bread* and *safe* and *stay*, I would take out my writing kit and compose a report to Sable Vane. Then I would burn it, because a burned letter is still a letter sent, if you know the guild's older tricks — write it in ash-ink on birch bark, whisper the recipient's name, let the smoke do the walking. The guild called it *correspondence*. I called it what it was: selling out the only people who'd ever let me sit at their fire.

In exchange, Sable was giving me back my years. Forty-one of them, siphoned off over two decades of service to fund the crown's appetite. He kept them in a ledger vault like coins. Like they were his to hold ransom.

I told myself it was only fair. I told myself Yara would understand — he of all people knew what stolen years meant; he'd found his mentor's lifespan itemized in red ink between a bottled storm and a duke's borrowed shadow. I told myself a great many things at that shrine on the border, standing under the carved eye of some forgotten god whose face the moss had eaten, while I pressed my palm to the old oath-stone and fed it our location the way you'd feed a dog you hoped would bite someone else first.

The stone drank the words and glowed faintly green.

That's when Odile said, from behind me, "Merrick. What did you just do?"

I have faced down guild tribunals. I have lied to archivists with faces like closed books. But there is no training for the voice of a ferrywoman who has carried you across three rivers without complaint and now sounds like she's deciding whether to drown you.

* * *

Let me back up, because a confession should be orderly even when everything else isn't.

We'd made good time since the crossing at Wrenford. Odile's ferry, her exile notwithstanding, still answered to her bloodline — the river took one look at her hands on the tiller and decided the warrants were somebody else's problem. We rode her barge up the Graywash until the water went shallow and stony, then walked. Two days through pine country where the frost-moon's afterglow still silvered everything, as though the season couldn't decide whether to let go of the night Tam arrived.

Tam walked beside Yara the whole way. The boy — the oath, the boy, both, neither — had learned perhaps forty words of common speech by then, and he deployed them with an economy that put professional correspondents to shame. *Hungry. Road. Bad men?* And once, looking at Yara asleep against a saddlebag: *Mine.* Which caused Yara to go pink to the ears and caused Odile to laugh hard enough to scare crows out of three counties.

I liked him. That's the thing I can't forgive myself for most easily. Not the guild politics, not the years, not even Sable's soft reasonable voice promising me my life back. I liked them. All of them. And I sold the road beneath their feet anyway, because forty-one years is a long time to be eighty-nine in a body that remembers thirty.

So when we reached the border shrine — a squat stone thing at the treeline, older than the kingdom, the kind of place where oaths were sworn before crowns existed to receive them — I did what I'd been doing every night. I touched the stone. I thought the words. *Yara Nystrom, the child, two exiles, bound for Thornhold via the pilgrim road.*

And Odile, who misses nothing on water or land, watched the glow and understood.

* * *

"You want to say something," I said, "before or after Yara gets here? He's showing Tam the carvings. He'll be back in a minute, and I'd rather be dismembered privately."

"Merrick." She said my name the way she said *rocks ahead.* "Tell me I'm wrong."

"I would love to. You've seen me forge documents; you know my range."

Her hand went to the hatchet at her belt — not drawing it, just resting there, the way a person rests a hand on a friend's shoulder. "How long."

"Since Wrenford. Before, actually. The first report I sent the night we left." I made myself look at her. Cowardice is mostly a failure of eyesight, my first master used to say. Look at what you've done or it will follow you blind. "Sable holds my years in the vault. Forty-one of them. He offered them back. Installments, per report."

Odile was quiet for a moment, which from her was more alarming than shouting. Then: "And what happens to us? To the boy? When your installments add up to a delivered package?"

"I told myself he only wanted Tam back safe. Contained. That nobody would be hurt."

"And yourself? Did you tell yourself that too?"

"I told myself a lot of things," I said. "I'm a scribe. Words are cheap when they're not notarized."

That was when Yara came around the shrine's corner with Tam's hand in his, and whatever he saw in our faces — mine gray, hers flint — made him stop three paces off like a man who's stepped into a web and felt the first strand tighten.

"What happened?" he said.

"Ask your scribe," said Odile.

* * *

I won't perform my own confession twice. It's enough to say I gave it straight: the reports, the ash-ink, the years, the price. I watched Yara's face do several things very quickly and settle on none of them. Tam, sensing weather, moved closer to him and stared at me with those eyes the color of old ink — the first promise ever sworn, looking at the latest betrayal of one. There was poetry in it. I despised the poetry.

"You led them here," Yara said finally. His voice was very level, which I recognized, having audited enough ledgers, as the sound of a man balancing accounts in real time. "This shrine. How far out?"

"Half a day. Maybe less if they rode through the night. They'd have gotten the message last evening."

"Then we run now and argue about your soul later—"

The trees answered him.

Not with sound at first. With silence — birds stopping mid-note, wind forgetting its errand — which in border country means soldiers, or worse. Then came the sound itself: hooves, many, and under them a low grinding hum I knew from guild archives. Oath-engines. Sable hadn't sent men; he'd sent *enforcement*, constructs wound tight with purchased loyalty, which is to say, wound tight with other people's lives.

"Behind the shrine!" Odile roared, and the world became very simple and very fast.

They came through the pines in gray livery with the Verdant sigil stitched where a heart should be. Six riders and two engines — engines being roughly man-shaped, if the man were built of green-lit iron and moved like a debt coming due. Yara shoved Tam behind the shrine's stone bulk. I fumbled for my kit, thinking fast, thinking *forge, distract, buy seconds* — and Odile planted herself between us and the nearest engine with her hatchet and a grin I'd last seen on her facing down a flooded weir.

"Go," she said. "I've broken harder things than this."

"Odile—" Yara started.

"*Move*, bookkeeper."

What follows, I set down plainly, because she deserves plainness. She met the first engine head-on and actually staggered it — a hatchet blow to the knee-joint, sparks like angry fireflies — and while it recalculated, she threw herself at the second, buying Yara and Tam the ten seconds they needed to reach the tree line. I was running, gods help me, I was already running, when the second engine caught her swing on its forearm and simply *closed its hand* around her, and hurled her into the shrine wall hard enough that I heard the stone crack and could not afterward swear the stone was what cracked.

She went down. She did not get up quickly.

Something happened to me then that I can only describe as arithmetic. Forty-one years on one side of the scale. A woman who'd ferried me free of charge, laughed at my jokes, and was now bleeding into moss on the far side. The scale tipped so fast it whistled.

I turned around. I drew every warding glyph my traitor hands knew — and I know *all* of them, that was always the trouble with me — and I wrote them in the air in burning script, the pre-kingdom forms, the ones that predate the guild's permission to use them. The lead engine stopped as if it had walked into glass. Its green light flickered, stuttered, confused by language older than its orders.

"GO," I screamed at Yara. "Take her! I'll hold the line!"

He didn't argue, which told me how bad it was. He hauled Odile over his shoulders — she swore at him, weakly, which told me she was alive — and he ran, Tam's hand locked in his, into the dark of the pines.

The engines recovered faster than I'd hoped and I lasted less time than I'd planned. I remember the first blow taking my writing arm. I remember thinking, absurdly, *left hand's still good, I can still forge.* I remember the green light leaning over me like a curious god. And I remember, distantly, hooves wheeling away — the riders peeling off in pursuit, because I was never the asset. I was the receipt.

* * *

I woke to rain and the smell of poultice, propped against a fallen log with my ruined arm splinted in a ferryman's knot, which is ugly but holds. Yara sat across the little fire, feeding it sticks, watching me with an expression I couldn't read — and I read documents for a living.

Tam was asleep against his side.

"They circled back," Yara said quietly. "After you went down. Odie insisted. She's got two cracked ribs and a shoulder that'll complain for a year, and she carried you half a mile on a litter she rigged from a sailcloth." He added a stick. "You held two engines alone with forbidden glyphs so a family that found out you sold them could get away. I've been trying to fit those two facts in the same ledger all night."

"I can save you the trouble. They don't balance."

"No," he agreed. "They don't. Nothing about this does. Sable steals decades and calls it stewardship. You sell us out and call it survival. And I keep choosing mercy anyway, because the alternative is becoming him." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and steady all at once. "You're done writing to him, Merrick. Whatever he still holds of yours — we get it back the honest way. In Thornhold. From his own vault. Even if it costs more than forty-one years."

"That's a terrible bargain," I said.

"It's the only one on the table."

From the dark edge of the firelight, Odile's voice, rough as river gravel: "If either of you dies on the way there, I'm keeping your boots."

I laughed, and my ribs informed me of their opinion, and above the pines the frost-moon's ghost was fading at last into ordinary dawn. Tam stirred in his sleep and murmured one of his forty words.

It was *Thornhold.*

He'd never been told the name.
