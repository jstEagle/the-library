## 8. The Thing Wearing His Crown

Thornhold did not look like a prison. That was the first lie of it.

From the ferry road, at the hour when the frost made everything honest, the palace was all pale spires and green banners, a place where the light came down gentle as an apology. I had imagined something with teeth. Instead it had gardens — terraced, immaculate, each hedge cut to look like it had grown that way out of sheer good manners. Somewhere behind those hedges, a man was being eaten alive one memory at a time, and the gardeners watered the roses on schedule.

Odile's shoulder still wasn't right. She carried her arm in a sling of Merrick's cloak and refused to call it a sling. "It's a *sash*," she'd said, three times that morning, daring anyone to argue. So we walked through Thornhold's lower gates like pilgrims, which is what we looked like: a limping ferrywoman, a disgraced scribe with ink-stained cuffs and a face like an unpaid debt, a feral boy who kept stopping to taste the frost off the statues, and me, carrying the Ledger under my coat against my heart like the world's most dangerous hot brick.

The plan — if you could dignify it with the word — rested on Merrick. He had spent six years here before his disgrace, copying oaths for the court, and he knew the servants' corridors the way Odile knew river currents. He also knew, because he'd told us by firelight with his eyes fixed firmly on the flames, exactly which door Sable Vane used when he came to make his deliveries. My guildmaster. Selling years of my family's lives to whatever lived in this pretty building, one quiet transaction at a time.

"Left here," Merrick murmured. "Then down the stair that smells of tallow."

"Why does the stair smell of tallow?" Tam asked, in our tongue now. He learned fast. Children do; oaths faster still.

"Because candles burn down there day and night," Merrick said. "Forty years of candles. Nobody remembers who ordered them lit."

I did not like that sentence. I filed it away with the other sentences I hadn't liked since we crossed the border, which was most of them.

* * *

The stair went down farther than any palace has a right to go. Palaces are built upward, toward God and flagpoles; they don't dig. But this one dug, and the deeper we went the older the stone became, until the dressed blocks gave way to something pre-kingdom, cyclopean, carved with glyphs I recognized from the Ledger's oldest pages. The walls were *writing*. We had been descending into a sentence for twenty minutes.

Tam put his palm flat against the wall and read it aloud without hesitation, his voice small and clear in the dark: "*I give my winters so his summer may continue.*"

"That's a renewal clause," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "That's the eleventh renewal. I've seen it in the ledger."

"No," Tam said gently, the way you correct a child. "You've seen the *copy*. This is where they bring him to sign."

We found the candles first. Hundreds of them, tiered on iron stands, burning without smoke around a door of black oak bound in green metal. And beside the door, a chair, a table, a ledger stand — a clerk's post, kept neat as a shrine. On the table lay a fresh page and a pot of red ink.

Red ink. The color of lifespans. The color of every hidden entry I'd spent three sleepless nights auditing back home, finding my mentor's name among them, and Odile's, and Merrick's, and — though I'd needed two tries to make myself read it — mine, entered eleven years ago, before I'd ever held the Ledger. Sable had been spending us like coin since before I understood what we were worth.

"He's due," Merrick whispered, checking the little clock on the table. "Sable comes at moonrise. Every month. There's a standing appointment — I used to book it myself."

"Then we have until moonrise," Odile said, cracking her neck with her good arm. "Door's not going to open itself."

It opened anyway. Not the oak door — a side passage, a servant's gap in the stones, breathing cold air. And from somewhere beyond the candles, faint but unmistakable, came a voice reciting numbers.

"*...four hundred and nine thousand, six hundred and twelve days remaining.*" A pause. "*No. Fewer now. It moves.*"

Tam went very still. Then he walked past all of us, straight to the servant's gap, and stood in it like a boy looking through a fence at a dog he'd once owned.

"He counts what's left of himself," Tam said. "He does it every night. He thinks no one hears."

* * *

The king's chamber was round, and wrong. The ceiling arched up into darkness where stars should have been and weren't. The furnishings were magnificent and untouched — a bed no one slept in, a table set for a meal no one ate — because the room's true occupant didn't use furniture anymore.

He sat in the exact center of the floor, and he was growing into it.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was my trade before it became my curse: the King of the Realm, Aldous the Ninth, Last of the Oathbound Line, was rooted. Green veins ran up from the flagstones into his bare feet, his shins, vanishing beneath robes that had gone stiff with bark along the shoulders. His hair was long and leafed at the ends. His hands, resting in his lap, had gone the color of new wood, and his fingers had fused together below the second knuckle, so that each hand was becoming a single branch, slowly, patiently, the way ivy takes a wall.

And yet his eyes were kind.

That was the horror of it. I had come expecting a monster, or a husk, or a thing wearing a king like a glove. Instead I found a tired man in his fifties who looked at Tam and began, very quietly, to cry.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, *there* you are. You're smaller than I remember. You were never small before." He tried to rise, couldn't, and laughed at himself — a real laugh, cracked and warm, a tavern laugh, nothing royal about it. "Forgive me. I can't get up anymore. The roots take the legs first. They always take the legs first."

"Your Majesty," Merrick started, dropping to one knee, and the king waved his branch-hands in distress.

"Don't kneel, lad, you'll hurt yourself, the floor bites." His eyes found mine, and something in them sharpened — briefly, terribly lucid. "You're the ledger-keeper. Yes. I know the walk. Everyone who carries a true ledger walks like the floor owes them money." He smiled. "You've come to read me my debts. Good. Sit. It's been forty years since anyone audited me."

"We've come to unbind you," I said. "All of you. The oath, the crown, the—"

"The Verdant Wearer." He said the name the way you'd say a spouse's name after a long and complicated marriage. "Careful, keeper. It listens when I say it, and it gets jealous when strangers do." His gaze drifted, softened, lost a decade. "There was a famine, you know. When I was young. My whole reign ahead of me and nothing to feed anyone with. My grandfather's grandfather made a bargain, and each generation renews it, and each renewal buys another decade of full granaries. It only asks—" he lifted a branch-hand, examined it with detached interest, "—little things. A memory here. A name there. A winter. I've given it so many winters I can't recall snow."

Tam knelt in front of him and took one wooden hand in both his small ones. "You swore me," he said. "First of all. Before the granaries, before the renewals. You promised the land itself would never again starve while you wore the crown. I'm that promise. I grew up walking."

The king looked at the boy for a long time. "I know," he whispered. "I know, and I am so sorry. You should have been a harvest. Instead they fed you loyalty until you got teeth."

"And what happens," I asked, "if the promise walks away?"

The lucidity left him like a tide going out. What remained spoke from somewhere deep in the roots, and its voice was the room's voice, the walls' voice, the voice of the glyph-sentence we'd descended through, and it was *glad*.

**THEN THE WEARING CONTINUES WITHOUT THE MAN.**

Every candle flame leaned sideways. The temperature dropped through courtesy and into threat. Odile's hand found her knife even though there was nothing yet to point it at.

**A CROWN NEEDS NO HEAD, KEEPER. ONLY A KINGDOM THAT KEEPS SAYING YES.**

* * *

It offered me things.

Of course it did. That's how these bargains work; I keep the accounts, I know the architecture of temptation better than anyone alive. It didn't threaten. Threatening is for amateurs and Sable Vane. It *negotiated*.

**YOU MOURN ONE WHO WAS SPENT FOR YOU,** it said, and the candlelight bent into shapes, and in the shapes was my mentor — Corvin Ashgrave, dead two years, whose name I'd found in the red entries, sold in monthly installments until there was nothing left to sell. **HE IS NOT GONE. HE IS HELD. EVERY YEAR THIS GUILD EVER SOLD IS STILL IN MY KEEPING, UNCONSUMED. INTEREST ACCRUES. I CAN RETURN HIM WHOLE.**

The chamber filled with the smell of his study — pipe smoke and leather and the cedar box he kept his good pens in. I heard his voice, or the memory of it: *"Balance the books, Yara. All of them. Especially your own."*

**AND YOUR GUILD,** the voice went on, warm as hearthsmoke. **YOUR LITTLE HOUSE OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS, STARVING ON CHARITY AND SENTIMENT. I CAN ENDOW IT FOR A THOUSAND YEARS. STORMS IN BOTTLES. SHADOWS ON LOAN. ALL OF IT, SECURE, FOREVER. YOU NEED ONLY DO WHAT YOUR GUILDMASTER HAS ALWAYS DONE.**

"Which is?"

**LEAVE THE BOY. WALK AWAY. LET THE APPOINTMENT KEEP ITSELF.**

And here is the thing I will not write prettily, because the Ledger deserves honesty: for three full heartbeats, I wanted it.

Three heartbeats. Corvin's laugh. The guild hall at midwinter, everyone drunk and singing, magic humming in the rafters like a contented cat. All of it back, all of it saved, and all it would cost was one muddy child I'd known a season — a child who was, when you got technical about it, not even a person, just an oath that had learned to walk—

An oath that had learned to walk. Learned to laugh at Odile's swearing. Learned to taste frost off statues. Learned to hold a dying woman's hand on a border road and sing to her in a language older than kingdoms because he thought songs helped.

I looked at Tam. He was watching me, and he already knew what the thing was offering, and he was *nodding*. Go on, the nod said. Take it. I'm what I was made for. Spending me is the oldest entry in the book.

"No," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle of it, which somehow made it more convincing than polish ever would. "You've misread my position. I don't negotiate. I *audit*." I pulled the Ledger from my coat and let it fall open in my hands. "And your account, Your Verdance, is badly overdrawn."

The candles went out.

All of them, at once, except one — and by that single flame I saw the king's eyes, human again, terrified and fierce, staring at me over his branch-bound hands.

"You shouldn't have said that," he breathed. "Keeper — *run* — it keeps its appointments, and yours is—"

Above us, far away, a bell began to ring moonrise.

And on the stairs behind us, unhurried, familiar, came the sound of boots I had followed obediently for eleven years.

Sable Vane cleared his throat politely in the dark.

"I see you've met the client," my guildmaster said.
