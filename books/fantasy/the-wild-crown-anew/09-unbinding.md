## 9. Unbinding

### Tam

I remember the beginning of me.

Not the way Yara remembers things, in rooms and doors and names — but the way a word remembers the mouth that made it. Warmth. Breath shaped around want. *Let there never be hunger again.* That was the whole of me once. A promise so heavy it needed somewhere to live, and a young king with mud on his knees who offered me his body as a house.

He did not know what he was building. Neither did I. Oaths do not come with instructions.

Now I stand behind a pillar of green marble in the great hall of Thornhold, and I remember everything at once, which is what I am made of: everything anyone ever promised and then forgot they'd said.

The court is assembled. Hundreds of them, silks and steel, faces like coins worn smooth by handling. Above the throne sits the Verdant Wearer, wearing my king the way ivy wears an oak — patiently, completely, with roots in everything.

And below, small and stubborn as a weed through flagstone, stands Yara Nystrom with a ledger under his arm, about to commit the largest act of accounting fraud in history.

He looks terrified. He also looks like he's already done the sums and found the terror acceptable. I have learned this about humans in my brief life as one: the brave ones are not the ones without fear. They are the ones who carry it anyway, the way you carry a heavy ledger, because someone has to.

*Yara,* I think at him, the way we've learned to speak without words, *when it starts, don't stop. Whatever you see. Don't stop.*

*I won't,* comes back the answer, thin and steady. *Probably.*

That is very Yara. Even his courage hedges its bets.

The herald cries out, and the Wearer turns its crowned head, and every oath of loyalty in the room stirs like wind through wheat — I feel them, all those small daily lies people tell their kings (*long may he reign*, *by my honor*, *so help me*), and they taste the way rot tastes: sweet first, then not.

Yara steps forward into the open floor.

### Yara

For the record: I had planned to open with something dignified. "By authority of the Ledger" or some such. What actually came out was:

"Good morning. This will only take a moment."

Three hundred courtiers stared at me. The Wearer's face — the king's face, what remained of it — twitched at one corner, like a man trying to smile from inside a locked room.

"I'm Yara Nystrom," I went on, because apparently I was committed now, "Keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, youngest ever, which sounds impressive until you learn how few of us survive the position." Somewhere near the doors, Odile snorted. Good. If the audience laughed even once, they were listening, and listening people are harder for the Wearer to feed on than bored ones. "And I am here to settle a debt."

I opened the ledger to the oldest page. Merrick's translation sat beneath it in my own hand, glyph by glyph, the pre-kingdom tongue laid bare like a riverbed after drought.

"*In the year of the starving fields,*" I read aloud, "*the king knelt in the ash-row and swore: my crown for your plenty. My memory for your harvest. My self, given gladly, until the land forgets to need me.*"

The temperature of the room changed. Not colder — *greener*. Something leaned forward through the walls.

"Do not," said the Wearer, and the voice came from every throat at once, including, horribly, mine for half a syllable before I bit down on it. Every sworn soul in Thornhold shuddered. Courtiers gripped their chairs. Guards looked at their own hands as if checking whose they were.

"You've been reading my mail for nine hundred years," I said. "You can listen to me read it back."

I dipped the quill in red ink — Sable's ink, the guild's oldest ink, the ink of debts — and began.

The rewriting was not elegant. It was not poetry. It was bookkeeping, which is its own kind of sacred: crossing out, correcting, initialing the margin. *Until the land forgets to need me* became *until the land remembers it never needed me at all.* *Given gladly* became *given once, and ended.* Word by word, I unmade the grammar of a prison.

The Wearer screamed without sound. Chandeliers bloomed moss. And the king —

The king lifted his head.

### Tam

He is so small, my king. I had forgotten, being large in him so long. He kneels again in the same posture as that first day, ash-row or marble, it makes no difference — knees bent, head bowed, a boy offering himself up because no one told him there were other ways to end a famine.

*It's all right,* I send to him. *I'm the promise. You were only the keeper. Keepers get to put things down.*

He weeps without moving, the way you learn to weep when something lives in your eyes.

Then the doors burst open, and she arrives.

Sable Vane does not run into halls; she *enters* them, and gravity rearranges itself out of respect. Behind her come two dozen guild loyalists, gray-cloaked, ledger-chains at their belts, and my heart — the borrowed, temporary, human heart Yara's presence lent me — drops like a stone in a well.

"Sable," Yara says, not turning around. His quill keeps moving. "You're late. There's a line."

"Boy." Her voice could frost glass. "Put down the pen. You are spending assets you don't understand on a fantasy of mercy. Do you know what happens to the guild without the crown's contract? Without the *interest*?"

"The guild dies," Yara says. "Yes. I've read the accounts. I wrote half of them." Now he turns, and his voice cracks, and he lets it. "You fed it Odile's years. You fed it Master Ellin's. You fed it *me*, Sable — page after page in my handwriting, and I thought I was just tired."

"The alternative was watching everything we built crumble—"

"The alternative," Yara says, "was telling the truth. Once. Nine hundred years ago. Instead you kept feeding it, and feeding it, and calling the hunger *duty*."

Silence. The good kind and the terrible kind, both at once.

And then Keeper Anwen, third chair, sixty years of service, steps out of Sable's line and walks — slowly, joints cracking like old book spines — to stand beside Yara.

Then another. Then Odile, limping, grinning, her bandaged side leaking courage. Then Merrick, pale as vellum, who does not deserve to be there and stands there anyway, which might be the bravest arithmetic of all.

One by one, the guild chooses.

Sable looks at her emptying ranks the way a woman looks at a fire consuming a house she built with her hands. And I see it happen — see her decide, in real time, that if she cannot keep the guild, she will keep the *debt*.

"No," she says softly, and draws the master quill from her own chain — the black one, the original, the one that writes in oaths rather than ink. "No, I don't think so."

### Yara

She moved faster than a woman her age had any right to. Later I'd learn she'd spent forty years of other people's youth buying exactly that speed.

Her quill touched air and air obeyed. Letters formed between us, glowing, binding — *Yara Nystrom shall cease* — and I felt them start to write themselves into me, cold along the bones—

—and Tam stepped into the path of the sentence.

It hit him like a wave hitting a lighthouse. He took the whole of it, *shall cease*, and held it, because he was made of held promises, because holding was the one thing he knew how to do better than anything alive or otherwise.

"Tam!" I was running. The ledger nearly fell. Behind us the Wearer rose off its throne like a tree deciding to be a storm, moss and gold and the king's exhausted eyes rolling in the green of its brow.

"Finish it," Tam gasped. He was smiling. Why was he *smiling*. "Yara. Finish the sentence. It's the only kindness left — finish it—"

My hand shook. Red ink dripped onto marble, each drop a tiny wound in the world.

The last line waited. I wrote it.

"*This oath ends. The debtor is released. The debt is forgiven — not paid, forgiven, freely, by all who were owed.*"

And the guild — my ruined, lying, beloved guild — spoke as one voice, Anwen and Odile and Merrick and every gray cloak in the hall: "**Forgiven.**"

### Tam

Oh.

Oh, that is what forgiveness feels like from the inside.

All my weight, nine centuries of it, lifted at once, and I did not fly — I *unraveled*, gently, the way a knot decides to have been a straight piece of string all along. The Wearer shrieked above us, starving mid-meal, its false loyalty curdling to nothing, ivy sliding off oak, gold tarnishing to dust. The king fell forward into his own body like a man stepping out of a coat too heavy to carry anymore, and I caught him — the last thing I did, the best thing — set him down soft as a leaf.

Yara reached me. Of course he reached me. He was always going to reach me; that was simply a fact about him, like the ledgers, like the terrible jokes.

"Don't," he said. "Don't you dare do the noble fading thing. We'll find another way. There's always another way, I'll check the appendix—"

"There's no appendix for me." My hands were coming apart into letters. It didn't hurt. It felt like being read. "Yara. Listen. You gave me eleven months of being a person. Mud. Bread. Odile swearing at rivers. That's more living than most kings get in fifty years."

"You were supposed to have more. That was the deal. That was *my* deal—"

"I know." I took what remained of his hand in what remained of mine. "Here's my last entry. Are you writing? Write it down."

His eyes were drowning and he was laughing at the same time, my impossible accountant.

"*Yara Nystrom,*" I said, loud enough for the whole court, loud enough for the land, "*kept his word.*"

And then I let go of my name too, because that is what oaths do at the end — they return to silence — and the last thing I carried out of the world was not the famine, not the crown, not the long gray centuries.

It was the look on his face when he realized what I'd just done: given him away, piece by piece, to everyone but me.

I would keep that. Wherever words go when no one speaks them.

I would keep that.

### Yara

The hall was very quiet. Kings knelt in it, freed. Crowns lay unraveled on the marble like shed skins. And I stood in the middle of everything holding a ledger that weighed nothing at all, because the magic had gone out of it, out of all of them, out of the guild and its bottled storms and borrowed shadows — every impossible thing settling, all at once, into merely improbable.

Anwen put her hand on my shoulder. Odile said something rough and kind. Merrick cried, which surprised no one who knew him.

I looked down at the final page. My own handwriting stared back at me — and beside my signature, in letters none of us had written, in the pre-kingdom tongue, small as a child's hand:

*Tam.*

Just the name. The whole of him that was left. Filed, at last, where he belonged.

Outside, spring came early that year — the first wild spring in nine hundred years, no crown to manage it, the land remembering its own name.

And on the guild door, three mornings later, we found one word carved deep into the oak, in a hand none of us recognized, though all of us knew it:

*YARA.*

Every mind in the world had let me go. Every ledger had erased me. But wood keeps what ink cannot, and somewhere, somehow, a promise kept its keeper.

Odile read it aloud, and frowned. "Who's Yara?"

Behind her, Merrick went white. Anwen's eyes filled. And I opened my mouth to answer — and found I didn't know either.
