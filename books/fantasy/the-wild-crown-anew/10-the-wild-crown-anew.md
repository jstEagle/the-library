## 10. The Wild Crown Anew

The trouble with settling every debt in existence, I discovered, is that you wake up the next morning owing nothing to anyone and have absolutely no idea what to do with your hands.

I stood in the guild's great counting room on the first morning of the rest of the world — which sounds grander than it looked. The room was the same room it had always been: dust motes turning slow circles in the light from the high windows, the smell of ink and old paper and the faintly metallic tang that had clung to everything we owned since before anyone could remember. Except now the tang was gone. Now the ledgers were just books.

I ran my thumb along the spine of the Ledger of Impossible Things, the fat old volume that had matched a mud-caked child's language glyph for glyph, and felt nothing come back up my arm. No hum. No tug behind my ribs, like a fishhook set in the meat of me. For thirty years I'd assumed that tug was simply what it meant to be alive. Apparently not. Apparently that was the guild, holding its hook in me, patient as any fisherman.

"You're doing it again," Odile said from the doorway.

"Doing what?"

"Standing there looking at a book like it insulted your mother."

"It did," I said. "Everything in here did. We just didn't know how to read the insults until last week."

She came in carrying two cups of tea and gave me one, which is how Odile says things she can't say out loud. Her arm was still bound where the shrine ambush had nearly taken it off; Merrick's salves were ordinary ones now, herb-and-fat-and-hope, and they worked slower than magic ever had. She seemed almost pleased about it. *Earned healing,* she called it, with the tone of a woman who intended to be insufferable about every scar for the rest of her life.

"King's abdication ceremony is at noon," she said. "Merrick wants us there early. Says there'll be scribes wanting statements."

"There won't be scribes," I said. "There'll be one very tired clerk with a quill he keeps dipping in his own despair. I've been that clerk."

* * *

The judgment of Sable Vane took three days, held in the guild hall because no kingdom court claimed jurisdiction over a debt older than kingdoms. It should have been a spectacle. It wasn't. It was an accounting, which is somehow worse.

Sable sat through all of it with her hands folded on the table, listening as the hidden pages were read aloud — decades of them, red-inked entries where lifespans had been drawn down like withdrawals from a common purse. My mentor Aldous's name appeared eleven times. Eleven years of him, fed to the crown so that the Verdant Wearer would keep wearing it, so that oaths would keep binding, so that the Guild of Impossible Things would remain possible.

When they asked her if she denied it, she said, "No." When they asked her why, she looked directly at me, and for one moment I saw something other than the composed woman who had raised half of us — I saw the arithmetic behind her eyes, the terrible bookkeeping soul who had decided that some sums must never be allowed to close.

"Because someone has to pay for the world," she said. "You paid with a name. I paid with everyone else's. You tell me which of us kept better accounts."

Nobody answered her, because there wasn't an answer, only a wound. They stripped her of her keys and her title and sentenced her to what the freed king called *the oldest penalty*: service without power. She would spend her remaining years — her own remaining years, unextended, unaugmented, dwindling honestly at last — working off the guild's obligations to the families whose members had aged and died early. Debt by debt. Name by name. It was, Merrick observed quietly, exactly the work she was best at.

On the last day, passing me in the corridor, she stopped. I braced for contempt or prophecy. Instead she said, "Your handwriting improved. Under pressure."

Then she walked on, and I understood that this was the closest thing to a blessing Sable Vane would ever give, and that some part of me — the part she trained — would carry it around like a coin I couldn't spend.

* * *

The king abdicated on a grey-bright morning in the square before Thornhold, before a crowd that had come expecting coronation and got something no one had a word for.

He was thinner than the man in Tam's memories, and taller than I'd imagined, and when he lifted the crown from his head his hands shook — not from weakness, I think, but from the strangeness of touching it without it touching back. For nine hundred years the Verdant Wearer had grown into him like roots into soil. Pulling free had left hollows. You could see them when he turned his head too fast, little absences in his expression, gaps where a memory used to live.

"People of the land I was sworn to," he said, and his voice carried without any magic at all, just the raw practice of nine centuries of being listened to. "I was made a promise once. A famine was ending, and I was young, and I swore myself to a thing that promised the harvests would hold. And the harvests held. And I forgot my mother's face, and then my own name, and then why I was standing in this square at all. Every year I renewed the oath, and every year I paid for it with the parts of me that made the oath worth keeping."

He knelt. The whole square gasped like one enormous bellows.

"A king should serve the land," he said. "So let the land be crowned, and let me learn how to kneel properly, because I'm told I've been very bad at it."

And he pressed the crown down into the earth at the center of the square, into the packed clay, and left it there.

They say wildflowers came up through it by midsummer. They say the iron rusted and split and something green split it further, root by root, until the circlet was less an object than a trellis. I went to look, once, in autumn. He was right. The land wears it better. Bramble and bindweed and one stubborn white poppy, growing straight up out of the band where a jewel used to sit. Crowning the land itself wild again — Tam would have loved the phrasing. Tam invented half the good phrases in the pre-kingdom tongue, though none of us knew it at the time.

The harvests held, incidentally. Nobody's oath was holding them anymore. They held because the soil is generous when you stop threatening it, and because thousands of people who had spent their lives paying loyalty-tax to a crown-entity suddenly had those hours back, and spent them, absurdly and magnificently, farming.

* * *

The guild survived the way a tree survives lightning: scarred, upright, and no longer pretending to be anything but wood.

Without the ledger-magic, we became what we perhaps had always been underneath — archivists, mediators, keepers of strange promises that required only patience instead of sorcery. Bottled storms lost their storms and became beautiful glass. Borrowed shadows went home to their owners, who mostly hadn't noticed they'd gone. Merrick stayed on to teach oath-scribing as a craft rather than a covenant: *words are tools,* he tells his students, *not leashes.* He says it firmly, and then he looks at me sometimes across the classroom, still asking forgiveness for the shrine, still receiving it, neither of us ever quite done with that transaction.

Odile runs the ferry again. She lets me ride free, which is her version of the tea.

And I keep the ledgers. Ordinary ones now, with entries that stay where written, in my hand, in ink that stays black. Some mornings I check anyway. Some habits outlive their reasons. But the pages hold. Everything holds now except the things we chose to let go.

* * *

Which brings me to the door.

The guild door is old oak, older than the building, older than the guild itself if Merrick's research is right. On the night after the unbinding — I wasn't there, I was unconscious against a pillar in the throne hall with Merrick shouting my name into my ear, another entry that never made it into any book — Tam did the last thing Tam would ever do.

The child who was the first oath made flesh, the boy who learned our language in six days and taught us mercy in seven, dissolved back into words before the assembled court, willingly, smiling, while I read his original self aloud and rewrote it line by line into freedom. I have described it before. I will not describe it again. Some accounts you close and refuse to reopen even for the sake of a story.

But before the last of him went — before the letters finished unwriting themselves out of the air — he reached down to where I had dropped my penknife during the reading, and he carved something into the guild door with the last strength his small hands had. Then he was gone. Not dead. Oaths don't die; they're only kept or released. He was released, everywhere at once, which is a different thing entirely, and possibly a better one.

Everyone remembers that morning. Everyone remembers the unbinding, the Wearer unraveling like smoke deciding it had somewhere else to be, the courtiers' faces, Sable arriving too late with loyalists who chose, to a person, to stand aside.

Almost no one remembers the carving. Because almost no one can remember *me.*

That was the price, and I paid it knowingly, and I would pay it again, and this is the strange quiet grief I carry: Yara Nystrom exists nowhere in any memory but one. Not in Odile's stories, though she tells stories about "the youngest keeper" and cannot say why her voice goes soft. Not in Merrick's careful histories, though he leaves a blank space in every record where a name should be, and fills it with a small inked leaf, over and over, like a man signing something. Not in Sable's ledger of restitution, though she works off debts to a family called Nystrom that she cannot explain having.

Only in one place.

I go out most evenings and stand in front of the guild door, where the oak grain has healed around the letters the way bark heals around a wound, keeping them forever. Four glyphs in the pre-kingdom tongue. Tam's last word, his final entry, written in the only script that was truly his.

The scholars think it means *keeper.* Merrick thinks it means *friend.* Odile thinks it means *home,* and pretends she doesn't care what it means, and stands beside me anyway, arms crossed, every evening, guarding the silence the way she guarded the shrine road.

I know what it means. I translated it the first night, kneeling on cold stone with tears I couldn't explain to anyone, including myself.

It says **Yara**.

It says my name, in a language no one living speaks, carved by the only creature in the world who remembered me — the first promise ever sworn, keeping faith with the last one kept. The guild door opens and closes beneath it a hundred times a day, and the word holds, and the wildflowers climb the crown in the capital square, and the harvests hold, and my found family loves a man whose name they cannot recall, and it is enough. It is more than enough. Truth cost exactly what it promised to, and it proved worth the price, and every evening I read my own name off a door like a debtor reading his discharge.

Tonight, though — tonight there's something new.

Someone has left a sheet of paper pinned below the carving. Cheap paper, ferry-paper, Odile's stock. On it, in a child's round unpracticed hand, in the pre-kingdom tongue, four fresh glyphs I have never seen before.

Below them, someone has written the translation in Common, in charcoal, in handwriting that trembles:

*"It says thank you. It says it isn't finished. Come to the river."*

The wild crown rustles in the wind off the water, and I reach for my coat.
