## 1. Small Charms

The morning fog came off the Serein in grey ribbons, and by the time it lifted over the market of Vell it had already done its work — softening the edges of the fishmongers' stalls, blurring the soot on the tenement walls, making the whole city look for an hour as if someone had drawn a wet thumb across a charcoal sketch. Jude Fairweather had lived in that hour his whole life. He liked the fog because it made everyone equal: lord and lamplighter walked through it at the same pace, and neither could see more than three stalls ahead, which meant neither could see him clearly either.

He set up his table in the crook between the rope-seller's awning and the wall of the old granary, the same spot he'd held for six years, and laid out his wares with the care of a man arranging an argument he'd already won. Luck-knots first, braided from red thread and river-reed, each one tied with seven knots because seven was a number people trusted even when they couldn't say why. Then the fever-charms, little clay discs pressed with a spiral and baked in his landlady's oven on nights when she asked no questions. Then the dried herbs — not magic, never magic, just chamomile and feverfew and willow bark, sold honestly to mothers with coughing children. The honest goods were the important ones. They were what let him sleep.

"Charms!" he called, and his voice came out rougher than he intended, scraped thin from three years of mornings like this one. "Luck-knots, fever-charms, remedies your grandmother would swear by!"

A woman buying herring two stalls down glanced at him and looked away. A boy chased a dog through the mud and splashed the edge of his table, and Jude moved his knots without flinching, because flinching was a habit you killed early or it killed you.

Three years ago, he told himself, as he did every morning, you were something else. Three years ago you carried a sword instead of thread, and the god of your order spoke in your blood like a second heartbeat, and men crossed roads to avoid your shadow. Now you sell knotted string to farmers. This is not a fall. This is survival wearing the mask of smallness.

He almost believed it before noon.

* * *

The morning's customers came in the usual shapes. A carter wanting a luck-knot for his new team of horses — Jude tied it slow, murmured nothing, charged two coppers, and watched the man walk away already straighter in the shoulders, which was half of what any charm ever did anyway. An old widow bought feverfew for her joints and haggled him down from four coppers to three with the ruthless patience of someone who had outlived every merchant who'd ever tried to cheat her. A young man with ink-stained fingers lingered over the table longer than the goods deserved, and Jude caught the flick of his eyes — cataloguing, weighing — and felt the cold slide of it down his spine before the young man bought nothing and drifted off into the crowd. A clerk, maybe. Or worse. In Vell these days you learned to read the difference between curiosity and surveillance the way sailors read weather, and Jude had gotten good at it, good enough that he'd stopped sleeping well.

Because the truth was this: the law had teeth now. The Edict of the Quiet Throne, eight years old and tightening every season, said there was no such thing as gift, no such thing as god-touched, no such thing as magic — only fraud, superstition, and sedition. Selling charms wasn't illegal if you called them trinkets, and Jude called them trinkets in a voice loud enough for the street. But working them — that was another matter. That was the pyre. The last burning he'd witnessed had been a hedge-healer from the river districts, a grandmother who'd mended broken bones with a touch, and they'd burned her on a cart-bed in Tanner's Square while the criers explained to the crowd that mercy required examples.

Jude had stood in that crowd with his head bowed and the thing beneath his skin lying perfectly still, and he had understood, with a clarity that still visited him some nights like a debt collector, that the silence in his blood might be the only reason he was alive.

So he worked no charms. Not once. Not ever. The knots were knots. The discs were clay. Whatever lived under his ribs — the last faint ember of something vast, the residue of a voice that had spoken to him for twenty years and then, on one terrible night in the mountains, stopped — stayed banked and buried, quieter than his own heartbeat, which was exactly how he intended to keep it until the day he died of old age in a bed with a full purse and an unremarkable grave.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It had survived six years of markets and two changes of magistrate and one very bad winter, and it would have gone on surviving if not for the girl with the burn.

She came just past midday, when the fog had burned away entirely and the sun lay hot on the awnings. She couldn't have been more than six or seven, small enough that her eyes cleared the tabletop exactly, dark-haired, solemn in the way children are solemn when they've been sent on errands too big for them. Her left hand was wrapped in filthy linen, and the linen was spotted where something underneath had wept through it.

"You're the charm-man," she said. Not a question. Children in Vell knew things their parents didn't know they knew.

"I'm a trinket-seller," Jude said automatically, and then winced, because the correction was itself a confession to anyone listening. "What happened to your hand?"

"The kettle." She held it up. The wrapping had gone stiff with old blood and newer pus, and the smell reached him even over the herring and the mud — sweetish, wrong, the smell of an infection that had decided to stay. "Mam says fever-charms are lies but she says I can look."

Jude crouched to bring himself level with her. Up close he could see how pale she was around the mouth, the glassy shine of a low fever already climbing. He'd seen this before. He knew the arithmetic of it. Boiled hand, dirty wrap, three days ignored because medicine cost money and mothers had to choose between food and poultices. Left alone, this became red streaks, then shaking chills, then a small grave behind the tannery with a wooden marker someone would forget to carve.

"Where is your mother?"

"At the wash-house." The girl looked at the table, at the clay discs in their rows. "How much is one?"

Jude opened his mouth to give the answer he always gave — *the feverfew is a copper, take it to your mother, tell her to steep it and wrap the hand clean* — the answer that kept him safe, the answer that had kept him safe for six years while other, braver fools went to the fire.

And then the girl swayed on her feet.

It was small. Just a tilt, the way a candle leans before it gutters. But Jude's hand shot out and caught her elbow without asking permission from his brain, and her sleeve rode up, and he saw the skin of her forearm flushed angry-red in streaks running up from the wrist, and everything in him that was careful, that was cautious, that had spent three years learning to be small —

— that everything went quiet, and something else answered.

* * *

Later, he would try to reconstruct the moment and fail. There was no decision in it. That was the horror he circled for days afterward: there had been no moment where Jude Fairweather chose. His hands simply moved, the way they'd once moved to raise a shield, the way they'd once moved when a god wore them like gloves. He drew the girl behind his table, out of the traffic of feet. He unwrapped the ruined hand with a gentleness that belonged to someone else. The palm was a blistered ruin, the skin split and weeping, and the infection sat in it like a squatting animal.

And Jude — kneeling in the mud of the Vell morning market, in front of God and the fishmongers and everybody — closed his good hand over hers, and reached down into the buried place, and knocked.

He hadn't knocked in three years. He hadn't dared. And what answered was not the voice he remembered, not the great warm thunder of the order's god filling his bones like a struck bell. What answered was smaller. An ember, not a sun. But it was *there*, it turned toward him the way a sleeper turns toward light, and heat ran up his arm and into the ruined hand, and Jude whispered the old word — the one that meant *mend*, the one that meant *be whole*, the one he'd sworn on pain of ash never to speak again — and felt the flesh beneath his palms knit like water finding its level.

The girl gasped. Not in pain.

Jude let go of her as if her skin had caught fire, because in a sense it had — his fire, his crime, three years of discipline spent in four seconds on a market street in broad daylight. He looked at her hand. New skin, pink and tender, unbroken. The red streaks fading even as he watched, the fever-flush draining out of her face. She stared at her own palm, opening and closing the fingers, and then she looked up at him with an expression he recognized from mirrors and nightmares: awe.

"It doesn't hurt," she said. Loudly. Children are loud. Children do not understand what they have.

"Hush," Jude said, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Hush, listen to me —"

"You *fixed* it," she said, louder, delighted, turning in a circle as if to show the whole market, and several faces had already turned toward the commotion — the widow with her feverfew, the carter with his horses, the rope-seller leaning out from under his awning with his mouth open —

Jude grabbed the girl's good shoulder. "Listen. You fell. You caught your hand on the kettle coming home and your mother wrapped it and now it's better because you rested. That's the story. Can you carry a story?"

Her delight flickered, cooled into that unnerving child-solemnity again. "Mam says stories keep us alive."

"Your mam," Jude said, pressing a copper into her unwounded hand and then, after a heartbeat, all three coppers from the widow's haggle besides, "is smarter than most priests. Go. Now. Don't run. Walk fast."

She went. He watched her all the way to the corner, small and quick and whole, vanishing into the crowd with her secret and his.

Then he stood up, and turned around, and understood that the plan was dead.

* * *

The market had not stopped. That was the terrible ordinariness of it — carts rolled, herring changed hands, a beggar sang his two notes by the granary wall. But the looks remained. The rope-seller was suddenly very busy with his coils. The widow gathered her bundles and left without her usual parting blessing. Two men near the well had stopped talking when he glanced over, and resumed a beat too late, in voices pitched for him to hear nothing.

They'd seen something. Maybe not everything — the girl had blocked most of the sightline, his hands had been cupped close, and to a distant eye it might have been only a charm-seller fussing over a sick child, holding her hand, muttering his trinket-mutterings. Plausible. Deniable. Jude clung to that arithmetic the way a drowning man clings to driftwood, sorting it and resorting it while he packed his table with hands that would not quite steady.

But plausibility was a currency, and fear devalued it. One witness who wanted to believe would keep the story. One witness who wanted a reward — and there were rewards now, silver for reports of unregistered gift, the magistrates had posted the notice in autumn and everyone had pretended not to read it while reading every word — one witness was enough.

He should leave tonight. The thought arrived whole, fully formed, wearing its own terrible logic like armor. Leave the room above the chandler's, leave the stock, leave the six years of careful smallness. Take the road north out of the city before the report could travel, because reports traveled faster than carts; a whisper could outrun anything with wheels.

Except.

Jude stood in the emptying crook between the awning and the granary wall, and except filled his chest like cold water. Except Mira was upstairs in the chandler's house right now, fourteen years old and fevered since spring, the wasting sickness that took its time and took everything, and the physician's tinctures cost more than the stall earned and slowed nothing. Except she was the reason he'd come to Vell at all, the sister who'd followed him out of the mountains after the silence, the last piece of his life before the quiet. Except that fleeing meant leaving her, and staying meant the pyre, and there was no road that held both of them and no road that didn't break one.

He looked down at his hands. Ordinary hands, scarred at the knuckles, stained with clay dust and thread-dye. Under the skin of the right one, faint as warmth lingering in a stone after sunset, something pulsed. Once. Twice. Patient. It had been patient for three years, and today it had answered a knock it had waited for, and Jude understood with a sinking certainty that it had liked being used. That it would want to be used again. That the ember, given air, becomes a hunger.

"Charm-man." The rope-seller, at his elbow, voice low, not looking at him. A rough man, a gambling man, a man who owed Jude nothing and whom Jude had quietly forgiven a debt once, years ago, without mentioning it. "There was a crown rider by the well this morning. Asking names. Not yours. Not yet." He dropped a coil of rope onto the abandoned table, payment for a warning that couldn't be spoken aloud. "Market's closing early today, I think. You'd best pack slow and go home the long way."

Jude nodded. He packed slow. He went the long way.

And all the way home through the grey streets of Vell, while the lamps were lit one by one along the Serein and the fog crept back in off the water to soften the city's edges again, he felt the pulse beneath his skin keeping its own count — steady, patient, awake — and he knew that somewhere behind him a story about a charm-man and a little girl was already traveling through the evening streets, moving at the speed of whisper, faster than any cart, faster than any flight, carrying his name toward ears he could not imagine and could not escape.
