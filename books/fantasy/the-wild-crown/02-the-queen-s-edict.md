## 2. The Queen's Edict

The criers came at dawn, and by noon the whole city of Vell had learned to be afraid in the same direction.

Jude heard them first from the window of the flat above his stall, where he was grinding dried feverfew into powder with a mortar worn smooth as a river stone. The sound carried strangely through morning fog — a bell, then a voice, then the bell again — and for a moment he thought it was only the temple calling the faithful to some minor saint's day. Then he saw the crowd gathering in Tanner's Square below, and the red-and-gold livery of the crown among them, and his hands went still around the pestle.

He counted to ten. It was an old habit, one he'd learned in another life, when counting was what you did between the order to charge and the moment arrows left the string. Ten heartbeats. Long enough for fear to become something useful.

Below, a crier climbed the fountain's rim and unrolled a scroll with the ceremony of a man who had done it forty times that week and would do it forty more.

"By edict of Her Radiant Majesty, Queen Isolde, First of Her Name, Protector of the Realm!" His voice cracked across the square like a whip. "Let all persons gifted with the forbidden arts present themselves for registration at the Hall of Records within thirty days. Those who register shall be catalogued, marked, and permitted to live under crown supervision. Those who conceal their gifts—" he paused, and Jude could have sworn the man enjoyed it, "—shall be found. And those who are found shall burn."

The word *burn* rolled through the fog and settled over the rooftops like ash.

Jude set down the mortar. His fingers were steady. That surprised him; he'd expected them to shake. But three years of hiding a god's heartbeat under his skin had taught his body a kind of terrible patience, and all it did now was go quiet, the way a hare goes quiet when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

*Register,* the crier said. *Or burn.*

There was no third option on the scroll. There never was.

* * *

By midmorning the square had changed character entirely, the way a market changes when the fish goes off — same stalls, same faces, but an undertone of rot beneath everything. The criers had moved on to other quarters, but the proclamation had been nailed to the fountain's edge in official script, and people clustered around it like moths around a lamp they suspected would kill them.

Jude opened his stall anyway. He always opened his stall. A charm-seller who stopped selling charms was a charm-seller with something to hide, and besides, Maren needed her medicine, and medicine cost coin, and coin came from luck-knots and fever-charms sold honestly to people who mostly believed they were buying colored thread and dried flowers. Which most days, they were.

Most days.

"Jude." Old Tam the cooper leaned across the stall partition, lowering his voice though there was no need; the square roared around them. "You heard it? The edict?"

"I heard it."

"They say the Hall of Records has already got lists. Names from before, from the last purge under the old king. They say anyone whose name appears twice gets no trial at all." Tam's hands were wringing each other, woodworker's knuckles white. "You keep your head down, eh? You're not— I mean, you sell trinkets. Thread and flowers. Nothing real."

"Nothing real," Jude agreed.

Tam held his gaze a beat too long. Then he nodded, too quickly, and shuffled back toward his own stall, and Jude watched him go and felt the cold arrive in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Because Tam hadn't been reassuring him. Tam had been rehearsing. Practicing the words he'd need later, if soldiers came asking questions, if a neighbor offered a reward, if the arithmetic of survival ever required Tam to remember exactly which of his fellow vendors might have sold something that worked.

That was how it started. Not with bonfires. With neighbors practicing their denials until the denials became true enough to spend.

Jude wrapped a luck-knot for a woman who didn't look at him and took her coin without meeting her eyes either. Across the square, two merchants stood talking near the proclamation, and he saw one of them gesture — briefly, almost delicately — toward the tinker's row, where Widow Ashe kept her stall of mended kettles. He saw the other merchant nod. He saw Widow Ashe, sixty years old with hands like tree bark, notice the gesture herself and go very carefully back to straightening her wares, as if she could make herself small enough to be forgotten.

She couldn't. None of them could. The list already existed, or soon would, and every name on it was a door the crown would kick open one by one.

*Thirty days.* Jude looked up at the fog, gray as old linen, and began, without letting himself admit he was doing it, to count what he owned.

* * *

The flat above the stall was one room and a loft, and it smelled of feverfew and lamp oil and the particular staleness of rooms where someone has been ill a long time. Maren lay in the bed beneath the window, propped on pillows, her dark hair lank against her cheeks. She was twenty-three and had been dying slowly since she was nineteen, her lungs knitting themselves closed with scar tissue no physician in Vell could unmake. The tinctures slowed it. They didn't stop it. Only money — real money, southern money, for the lung-healers of Corvassa — might stop it, and Jude had spent three years failing to earn that much honestly.

"You're making the face," Maren said when he came in.

"What face?"

"The one where you're doing sums behind your eyes and hate the answer." She coughed, wet and long, and waved off the cloth he offered. "Tam told me. The edict. Half the square came past my window whispering about it like it was a funeral."

"It is one," Jude said. "We just don't know yet whose."

Maren studied him. She had always been able to read him better than he liked — younger sister, sharper eyes, the one gift their family had actually passed down. "You're thinking about running."

"I'm thinking about a lot of things."

"Don't lie to me, Jude. Not today." Her voice was thin but level. "Thirty days. If they find out what you are—"

"If they find out, it won't matter where we run. That's the point of the edict. It isn't a hunt, Mare. It's a net." He sat on the edge of her bed, careful of her legs, and stared at his own hands. Beneath the skin, faint as a second pulse, the spark sat where it had sat for three years — silent, patient, a coal buried in ash. He had not reached for it once. Not once in three years. "They register us so they can find us later. Every name in that book is a promise the queen makes to herself. *I know where you sleep.*"

"Then don't give them your name."

"And leave you?"

The silence that followed had teeth. Maren pulled her blanket higher, though the room wasn't cold, and looked out the window at the fog.

"I'm going to die," she said at last, simply, the way she said most hard things. "Maybe this year. Maybe in five. The tinctures buy time, that's all they've ever bought. So don't you dare trade your life for a few more months of mine. Don't you *dare* stand in a square somewhere with rope around your wrists and tell yourself it was for me."

"Maren—"

"No. Listen to me." She gripped his wrist, and her hand, wasted as it was, still had their mother's stubbornness in it. "Whatever you decide, decide it clean. If you run, run. If you stay, stay because staying means something. But don't make me the reason you burn. I couldn't bear being somebody's excuse."

Jude covered her hand with his own and said nothing, because anything he said would be a lie, and she'd asked him not to lie.

* * *

That night, the denunciations began.

Not officially — officially, the edict was four hours old. But Vell was an old city, and fear was older, and fear moved fast through the tenements like water through rotten wood. Jude heard the first of it from the stairwell: the landlady's voice, low and urgent, speaking to someone in the entryway below. He caught fragments. *Third floor. The one who does the mending. She heated a kettle without fire, I saw it myself, years ago, I said nothing then but—*

*I understand, mistress. Your name will be noted. There's a consideration paid for verified reports.*

*A consideration.* Jude stood in the dark of the stairwell and felt something in his chest twist slow and sour. Three years ago, when his god went silent, he had grieved. When he'd fled the order, he'd grieved again. But this was a different feeling, colder and less familiar: watching the machinery of persecution assemble itself out of ordinary human material, hinge by hinge, out of landladies and coopers and merchants with delicate gestures. Nobody in that entryway was evil. They were scared, and scared people will pay almost anything for the illusion of safety, and the crown had priced that illusion at exactly the cost of a neighbor's name.

He climbed back to the flat and did not sleep.

Instead he sat at the table with a stub of candle and a scrap of paper, and made lists, because lists were honest in a way people weren't.

What he owned: forty-one crowns, hidden in the mortar's false bottom. A knife worth little. Clothes worth less. His stock of charms — useless now; selling them after the edict was painting a target. What he owed: the room, half a month. What he needed: passage north out of Vell, and a plan for Maren, and there the list bled ink, because every option ended the same way.

Take her, and she dies on the road. The mountain passes were weeks away even if the roads were clear, and the roads would not be clear; the edict would put checkpoints on every gate within the week. Leave her, and the sisters of Saint Verity's hospice would take her in, would care for her gently, would ask no questions — except that the hospice registered its patients with the city, and the city now fed everything it knew to the Hall of Records, and a sick woman with a brother who vanished the same week the edict posted was a thread any inquisitor could pull until the whole garment unraveled.

Every road led through fire. He wrote the truth of it at the bottom of the page and hated how plain it looked: *There is no way out that doesn't cost someone everything.*

Beneath the window, in the street, a drunk was singing — one of the old hymns, slurred and wandering, the kind the temples hadn't permitted since the Silence. *"Where the gods walked, the wheat grew gold..."* Jude listened to the broken melody and felt, for the first time in three years, the spark stir. Not wake. Stir. A shift of weight in the deep place under his sternum, the way a sleeping man shifts when the weather changes.

It had never done that before.

He put his palm flat against his chest and breathed, and waited, and after a long while the stirring stilled, and the candle guttered, and somewhere across the city a bell tolled the hour of the dead watch.

* * *

On the fourth day after the edict, they burned the tinker.

Not Widow Ashe — she had vanished in the night, kettle-stall abandoned, and the square pretended not to know where. No: a dyer from the river district, a man Jude had never met, whose crime according to the broadsheets was "concealment of forbidden arts." Whether he'd actually been gifted, whether he'd actually concealed anything, whether the informer who named him had seen a working or merely wanted the man's shop — none of that appeared in print. Broad sheets didn't carry doubt. Doubt was bad for morale.

Jude watched from the crowd because not watching was worse. The pyre stood in Tanner's Square, between the fountain and the proclamation nailed to its rim, and the soldiers had built it tall and dry and eager. The dyer was a small man. He did not struggle. At the end, when the smoke rose thick and black against the noon sky, the crowd made the sound crowds make — not cheering, not weeping, something in between, a low communal exhale like a wound being packed — and Jude stood among them with his face arranged into appropriate grimness and understood, with total clarity, that he was looking at his own future.

Thirty days. Twenty-six now. The net was closing stitch by stitch, and the queen's men were patient, and Vell's doors were many but all of them opened inward onto the same trap.

He walked home through streets that had changed overnight. Neighbors who had nodded to him for three years now watched him from windows. A boy spat near his boots and ran. On the shrine-corner where the old god's carved name had been chiseled away generations ago, someone had chalked fresh words in a shaking hand: *WE REMEMBER*. By evening the chalk was gone, scrubbed away by hands unknown, and the corner was bare again, obedient again.

That night he climbed to the flat and found Maren sitting up, dressed, her travel cloak folded on the bed beside her. She had packed a bag. It was small. Everything a dying woman owned fit into a bag the size of a bread loaf, and seeing that undid him more than the pyre had.

"Saint Verity's," she said before he could speak. "I spoke with their mother superior this afternoon. She'll take me. She owes you — don't argue, everyone in this city owes you for one thing or another, that's what happens when you spend years quietly fixing what's broken." Her chin was up. Her hands, he noticed, were trembling in her lap. "And you're going north."

"Mare—"

"Alaric Brannigan runs grain barges up the Vellwater. He owes his son's life to one of your fever-charms — a real one, Jude, don't look at me like that, I've known for years what you really are and I've never once cared. He sails the day after tomorrow, before the river gates get strict. He'll take you as far as Thornmere." She recited it like a manifest, like she'd been planning it as long as he had, longer. "After that you disappear. You change your name. You never send word, never come back, never let anyone know the Fairweather name again."

"I'm not leaving you at a hospice to die alone."

"You're leaving me at a hospice to die *safe*." Her voice cracked on the last word, finally, and she pressed her sleeve to her mouth and breathed until she could speak again. "Brother. My stubborn, stupid, good-hearted brother. They will burn you. Not because you're dangerous — because you're *findable*, and burning one man buys the queen a thousand quiet neighbors. You've seen it. We both saw it today." She took his hand and pressed something into it: a luck-knot, frayed, ancient, tied from their mother's apron-thread when they were children. "So take the road. And live long enough to be sorry about it."

Jude closed his fist around the knot. Under his skin, deeper than blood, the spark turned over again — that slow, heavy shift, like something vast rolling in its sleep — and this time he did not wait it out. This time, alone in the candlelight with his sister's future already decided and his own unwritten, he let himself feel the full shape of it: the heat, the patience, the terrible *waiting* of the thing he carried.

Three years silent. Three years he'd believed himself empty, abandoned, a hollowed-out vessel walking around wearing a dead god's residue. But it wasn't dead. He'd known it since the night the drunk sang in the street, and he knew it now, and the knowing changed everything, because the edict wasn't hunting charm-sellers and hedge-witches.

If the criers knew what slept inside him, they wouldn't have promised a pyre.

They'd have promised an army.

"Day after tomorrow," he said hoarsely. "The grain barge. Before dawn, before the gates fill. I'll take you to Verity's myself tonight, tonight, while the streets still belong to us."

Maren smiled — small, exhausted, victorious — and blew out the candle.

Outside, far off toward the river gate, a horn sounded once, long and low, and did not sound again. But Jude lay awake in the dark listening to his own doubled heartbeat, the quick one and the ancient one, and understood that whatever waited for him beyond Vell's walls, the silence inside his skin was ending —

and the god he carried had begun, at last, to dream.
