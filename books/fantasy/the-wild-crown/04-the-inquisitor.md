## 4. The Inquisitor

Sera Vane learned of the inquisitor's arrival the way she learned most things: from a clerk's trembling hand and a ledger that no longer balanced.

"The requisition," the boy said, standing in the doorway of her office above the tithe-house, "is for forty cells. Cleared and consecrated."

"Forty." She set down her pen. Outside her narrow window, Vell was going about its morning — fishwives crying the tide-catch, carters swearing at the bridge tolls, the smell of tar and bread and river mud that she had long ago stopped noticing. It did not look like a city that needed forty cells.

"Consecrated how?" she asked, though she already knew. There was only one rite the crown still paid for by the cell.

"Salt and ash, mistress clerk. And iron for the doors."

She signed the requisition because refusing it would have been signing something worse: her own name on a list she did not want to read. That was the whole art of her position, if it could be called an art — knowing which papers to touch and which to let pass through her hands as though they were already cold. Sera Vane had been the crown's clerk in Vell for eleven years. She had processed grain levies, harbor fees, the wages of the night watch. She had processed, two winters past, the execution order of a hedge-witch from the Tanner's Quarter whose crime, so far as the record showed, was selling a charm against childbed fever to a woman who later died anyway. The charm hadn't worked. The woman had died of the fever, not the magic. Sera had written *deceased, cause: sorcery (alleged)* in a hand so neat it looked like prayer.

She had told herself then what she told herself now: the machinery is large, and you are small, and a small hand on a large wheel keeps it from turning faster.

The boy was still waiting. "When do they arrive?"

"They arrived last night," he said. "On the *Grey Warden*. The inquisitor came ashore before dawn. They say he walked the length of the Quay without a lamp and every dog on the street went quiet."

*Every dog went quiet.* Sera had heard that particular piece of theater before, attached to other men with other reputations, and had never once believed it. Dogs went quiet for men who fed them poison, more often than not. But she wrote it down in her day-book anyway, because rumor was also paper, and paper was also her business, and a clerk who ignored rumors was a clerk who stopped existing shortly after.

"His name?" she asked.

"Aldric Renn."

The pen paused. Not long — a half-breath, no more. She was careful about pauses; they were the one luxury her trade could not afford.

"I don't know the name," she said.

"No, mistress clerk." The boy shifted. He was sixteen and bad at lying and knew it, which made him honest in the way of animals that know they are prey. "But old Marta in the records room does. She says he was in Vell once before. Years back. Before your time."

"Before my time," Sera repeated, and dismissed him, and sat alone with her ledgers while the light crawled across them like something cautious.

*Before your time.* Eleven years was not before her time. Eleven years was precisely, exactly her time. Which meant the boy's Marta meant longer than eleven years — meant before the Edict of Ash, before the outlawry, back when gifted folk had merely been taxed instead of burned. Back when the city had been a different animal wearing the same skin.

She pulled the older registers, the ones kept in the dry cellar where the ink went brown and the paper went soft as old skin, and found what she expected to find and did not expect to feel anything about: a mercenary company, licensed under the old charter, mustered out of Vell some twenty years gone. The Order of the Quiet Vigil. Nine names on the founding roll. One of them, in the cramped hand of a dead registrar, was *Aldric Renn.*

Another was smudged. She tilted the page toward the window light and could not make it resolve. *Jude Fairweather,* said the ghost of the letters, though she could not have said how she knew, any more than you can know the shape of a thing behind frosted glass. She told herself she had guessed it from context. She was very good at telling herself things. It was, after all, her profession.

* * *

Aldric Renn received the city magistrates in the tithe-house great room at the ninth bell, and Sera attended because someone had to take the minutes, and the minutes of this meeting would matter more than any she had ever taken, though she did not yet know why.

She saw him first across the width of the room and understood, in the way one understands weather, that the dogs-quiet story had been true in spirit if not in fact. He was not a large man. That was the first wrongness. She had expected bulk, the theatrical menace of the capital's witch-finders, all black wool and silver buckles and voices pitched for pulpits. Renn wore grey. Plain grey, travel-worn, a sword at his hip that was not ornamental and a face that was not cruel. He stood near the window with his hands folded behind his back like a man waiting for a late friend, and the room bent around him anyway — the magistrates angled their bodies toward him without seeming to decide to, the servants moved more quietly, and the fire in the hearth, Sera would have sworn afterward, burned straighter, as if ashamed to crackle.

"Lord Magistrate Corvel," Renn said, and his voice was mild, almost kind. "Thank you for coming. I'll be brief, because I know the harvest waits on no one."

Corvel, who had governed Vell for six years and been governed by no one in living memory, actually bowed his head. Sera watched it happen and felt the floor tilt slightly beneath her professional calm.

"The Crown Hunt begins in this district tomorrow," Renn continued. "I have the queen's warrant, which I will show to anyone who asks, and I will ask anyone who hasn't asked to consider why they didn't. The terms are simple. Every person gifted — hedge-charmers, luck-sellers, fever-healers, storm-callers, whatever name the provinces use — will register with my office within thirty days. Registration carries amnesty. Refusal carries burning. I don't make the terms. I carry them. If anyone in this room has a quarrel with the terms, their quarrel is with the throne, and I'd advise them to write it down rather than say it aloud, because writing can be filed."

Someone laughed, uncertainly, and stopped when Renn smiled. The smile changed nothing in his face. That was the second wrongness, Sera thought. A cruel man's smile spends itself on cruelty. This man's smile spent itself on nothing at all.

He worked through the logistics with the efficiency of a surgeon: which quarters would be swept first, how many soldiers, where the cells would stand — her forty cells, salt and ash and iron — how the condemned would be transported, how the ashes would be recorded. *Recorded.* She flinched at the word and covered it by writing faster.

It was near the end, when the magistrates had run out of questions and courage, that Corvel made his one attempt at humanity.

"And the children, Lord Inquisitor? Some of these gifteds are barely more than children themselves. Surely the crown doesn't—"

"The crown burns whoever refuses to register," Renn said. "The age of the body is between the body and its god." Then, after a pause so brief it might have been nothing: "Register early. I'll instruct my clerks to process the young ones first, so their families can see there's no trick in it."

And that, Sera thought, writing it down, was somehow the worst thing she had heard him say. Not the burning — the burning was the machinery, and she had lived beside the machinery all her life. It was the *processing*. The way he spoke of mercy as throughput. The way he had already thought about the order of the deaths, and arranged it for comfort, and called that arrangement kindness, and perhaps even believed it.

The meeting broke up. The magistrates left in ones and twos, avoiding one another's eyes. Sera remained at her table, finishing the minutes, and did not look up when footsteps stopped beside her.

"You're the clerk of record," Renn said. Not a question.

"Sera Vane, my lord."

"Your hand is very clear." He had picked up the top sheet of her minutes without asking, the way men of rank pick things up, and was reading backward through her notes. "You've recorded the pause before 'the crown burns whoever refuses.' Why?"

Her stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. She had marked it — a small dash, a habit, a way of keeping the rhythm of speech in the record — and had not considered that anyone might read rhythm into it.

"To preserve cadence, my lord. Minutes should carry tone as well as content."

"Tone." He set the sheet down. Up close his eyes were a pale grey-brown, the color of river water over stone, and they held her with a patience that felt less like scrutiny than like recognition. "Do you believe in the hunt, Clerk Vane?"

There it was. The trap with no door. She had watched cleverer people than herself walk into versions of it for years — asked whether they supported the edict, whether they loved the queen, whether they believed, and every answer was either a lie or a leash. She gave the answer she had practiced in mirrors she would never admit to owning.

"I believe in accurate records, my lord."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not approval — she would have known approval, she collected approvals the way misers collect coins. It was closer to disappointment, or to grief, or to the particular tiredness of a man who has asked a question hoping to be refused.

"Yes," he said quietly. "So do I." And he turned and left her alone in the great room with the fire and her flawless, faithful, damning pages.

* * *

She began, after that, to watch him. It was not difficult; watching was the native condition of her life. She watched him the second evening, from the gallery of the tithe-house, as he interviewed the first registrants.

They came in a thin, frightened line — a lamplighter whose hands could coax flame without flint, a ferryman who swore the river spoke to him in dreams, an old woman with a knotted cord she claimed could find lost things, though everyone in the Tanner's Quarter knew it found lost children, and had done, for forty years, without fee. Renn took each name himself. He wrote with his own hand rather than delegating, and Sera, watching from above, saw that his writing was slow and exact, and that he asked each registrant the same final question:

"What does your gift cost you?"

Not *what can it do.* What does it *cost.*

The lamplighter said, "My sleep. I dream in flame." The ferryman said, "I haven't swum since I was a boy. The river won't drown me, and it won't let me forget it." The old woman with the cord wept, and said, "Everything. Everything it finds, I lose a little of myself finding it," and Renn bowed his head to her as if she had given testimony in church, and wrote it down word for word.

This was not the conduct of a butcher. Sera had seen butchers; the capital sent them regularly, red-faced men who enjoyed the register almost as much as the pyre. Renn was something else, something she did not have a word for, and the lack of the word troubled her more than cruelty would have. Cruelty fit in ledgers. Cruelty had columns and totals. Whatever moved behind this man's mildness had no column she could think of.

It was on the fourth night that she found the doubt.

She had stayed late over the registration rolls — forty-one names now, the line growing daily as fear outpaced pride — and was crossing the dark courtyard of the tithe-house when she saw him standing alone by the well. Most men, alone, relax. Renn stood the way soldiers stand at attention, except that his sword-hand was pressed flat against the stone lip of the well, hard enough that she could see the tendons stand in his wrist even at that distance. His lips were moving.

Prayer, she assumed. Inquisitors prayed; it was part of the costume. But the crown's rite had words, public words, words about purity and the queen's eternal flame, and whatever he was saying had none of that architecture. It was short. It repeated. And twice — she was certain, though she would swear otherwise under oath — he stopped, waited, and started again, like a man calling into a well and listening for an echo that did not come.

Like a man praying to something that did not answer.

She withdrew before he could turn, and lay awake half the night assembling and disassembling what she had seen. A man who prayed to a silent god. A man who processed the condemned with surgical gentleness. A man whose file said *mercenary, disbanded order* and whose face said *mourning.* None of it cohered, unless—

Unless the hunt was not what the warrants said it was.

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, the way the worst thoughts do, dressed already in evidence. Forty cells for a city that had, in eleven years of her tenure, produced perhaps a dozen burnable offenders. Consecration rites usually reserved for plague pits. A chief inquisitor dispatched personally — *personally*, from the capital, over districts with richer harvests of heretics — to a provincial market town. And underneath it all, in the queen's own proclamation, a single sentence that had passed through Sera's hands three days ago and snagged in her mind like a fishhook in wool: *that the realm may be delivered from the old contagion, root and branch, and the succession secured forever.*

*Root and branch.* You did not send forty cells for branches. Branches were handled locally, cheaply, with rope and pitch. Forty cells and a chief inquisitor were for roots.

She rose before dawn and went down into the dry cellar with a lamp, and stood among the soft old papers, and did not know what she was looking for, only that she was looking. The old rolls of the Quiet Vigil. The disbanded companies. The years around the god's silence, when something had happened — she was suddenly certain something had happened — that the current registers treated as a gap, a blank year, a page missing from the binding.

The page was not missing. Pages were never missing; that was the first lesson of archives, that nothing is ever truly lost, only filed where no one thinks to look. She found the year bound inside a shipping manifest, of all absurd things, misfiled by a clerk fifty years dead, and she carried it upstairs with both hands, carefully, as though it were sleeping.

* * *

The summons came at mid-morning: the inquisitor required her in his office, with the full register of licensed persons for the past twenty years.

She brought the books. She also brought, tucked flat beneath the bottommost ledger where its thin spine vanished against the binding, the misfiled year. She told herself she brought it only because he had asked for everything, and she was thorough, and thoroughness was the only virtue she had ever managed to practice consistently. She told herself a great many things that morning. She was beginning to suspect the habit was failing her.

Renn sat behind a borrowed desk too small for him, reading. He looked up, and for a moment — a half-breath, no more — his composure was not there, the way a wall is not there for an instant when lightning shows you it is only bricks.

"That bottom book," he said. "Where did you find it?"

"It was misfiled, my lord. Under shipping manifests. I corrected the error this morning."

He reached for it slowly, the way one reaches toward a wound, and opened it, and turned pages until he came to the roll of the Quiet Vigil, nine names in a dead man's cramped hand, and Sera watched his finger stop on the smudged entry and rest there, motionless, the way a hand rests on a grave.

"Fairweather," Renn said. Almost silently. To himself, or to the page, or to no one.

"My lord?" said Sera Vane, who had read the name hours ago and understood, in the way one understands weather, that everything — the forty cells, the silent prayers, the root and the branch and the queen's fear — had just come home to roost in this small borrowed office, in this small provincial city, and that the storm it carried had not even begun to rain.

Renn closed the book. When he looked up, his face was the wall again, perfect and grey and load-bearing, and only his eyes were still lightning-lit.

"Clerk Vane," he said, "tell me everything you know about a man named Jude Fairweather."
