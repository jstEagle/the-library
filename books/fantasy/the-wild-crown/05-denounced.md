## 5. Denounced

The knock came at the hour when Vell's tenements went quiet — that thin hour past midnight when even the drunks had found their doorways and the watchmen's boots had worn their circles into the cobbles and gone home to their own beds. Jude knew every sound his building made. The groan of the stair on the third landing, where the beam had warped since before he was born. The drip in the courtyard cistern that counted seconds like a patient clock. The sigh of Mrs. Ollen's window above him, always left cracked because her husband had died suffocating and she could not bear sealed air since.

So when he heard boots on the third landing — six pairs, no attempt at softness, iron-shod and certain — he was already awake, already moving.

He had been dreaming of the wheatfields below his grandfather's farm, which was its own cruelty, because in three days a man with Jude's face would be nailed to every gatepost in the city if he stayed.

The knock on his own door came as he pulled his sister's blanket up over her chin. Mira slept the way she had slept since the fever took her lungs two winters ago: shallowly, badly, her breath whistling through her chest like wind through a broken shutter. The tincture from Tammas Wren cost four silver a vial now, and it bought her nights like this one — sleeping, at least, instead of sitting upright against the wall because lying flat drowned her.

"Jude Fairweather." A voice through the wood, flat as a ledger. "Open in the name of the Crown Hunt."

*In the name of.* Everything in Vell was done in someone's name now. The Queen's name on the edicts, the gods' names forbidden, and in between, the names of neighbors, whispered upward like smoke.

There was only one way out of the room and it was behind the men on the stairs. But there was the window, and beyond the window the roofline of Marrow Lane, and beyond that the whole ragged spine of the tenement district, stitched together by gutters and laundry lines and thirty years of builders who never quite finished what they started.

Jude pressed his lips to Mira's forehead. She smelled of the tincture — bitter herbs and something sweeter underneath, the ghost of the little girl she'd been before.

"Mira," he breathed. Not loud enough to wake her. He couldn't wake her; if she woke she would cry out for him, and they would know she mattered, and whatever mercy the hunt had — and it had none — would spend itself elsewhere.

He set the last three vials on the table beside her bed where anyone could see them, though he knew even as he did it that no one would see them. No one would come. That was the arithmetic of fear, and he had watched it performed all week from behind his market stall: the criers reading the edict, *all gifted persons shall register or be burned,* and the silence after, and then the whispering beginning, the glances sliding sideways down the stalls, everyone measuring everyone else for the price of their own safety.

He had told himself his neighbors were different. He had sold Mrs. Ollen a fever-charm for half price when her granddaughter burned. He had fixed old Piet's shutter without charging. He had carried water up three flights for the widow Brandt when her hands failed her.

He had believed, in the tired corner of himself where belief still lived, that being useful made you safe.

The door shuddered under the first blow of the ram.

Jude went out the window.

* * *

The rooftops of Marrow Lane were a country he had mapped in boyhood, when the streets below belonged to other people's business and the tiles belonged to him. He crossed the gap to the dye-house roof on legs that remembered even while his mind screamed at them to hurry, hurry, the ram was through his door by now and boots would be pouring into his room, and —

And there was nothing to find. He had made sure of that much. The charms he kept were honest charms, luck-knots and fever-strings, wood and thread and prayer-shaped nothing. His real work he had not done in years. Three years. Since the night the god went silent and left him holding nothing but an ache behind his breastbone and a life too small for what had lived inside it.

But they wouldn't be looking for evidence. They'd be looking for him. And when they didn't find him they would ask the building who lived there, and the building would answer, because buildings in Vell had learned to talk.

He flattened himself against the chimney stack of the dye-house and looked back at his own windows.

They had torches. Of course they had torches — the Crown Hunt liked fire, fire was its signature, fire was the punctuation mark at the end of every sentence the crown spoke about magic. Orange light spilled from his room into the street below, throwing shadows of soldiers across the cobbles, long and wrong, like something from a puppet show of damnation. He could hear them turning over his furniture. The crash of his one good chair hitting the floor carried all the way up to the roof.

A soldier's voice, bored: "Bed's warm."

"Two sleepers?"

"One. Girl, sick. Doesn't know anything, or she's playing at it."

Jude's hands closed on the chimney bricks until the rough stone bit his palms. *Doesn't know anything.* Mira, who had once, aged nine, stood between him and a debt collector with a broom and told the man exactly what she thought of men who frightened women. Mira, who knew everything, who had known since the first winter of the fever, when he'd sat up all night with his hand on her sternum doing the thing he swore he never did anymore — not healing, never that, just *easing*, taking the rattle from her lungs into his own chest so she could sleep — who had looked up at him with gray eyes gone ancient and said, *I won't tell, Jude. Not ever. Even if they burn me.*

She was fourteen. She had learned to promise martyrdom the way other children learned to whistle.

He should have taken her north weeks ago. He should have taken her the morning after the criers, packed her onto a grain cart wrapped in blankets and paid any price, swallowed any pride. He had told himself the roads were worse — fevered girls died of roads, died of cold, died of the wrong milk — and every word of it true, and none of it the truth. The truth was smaller and uglier. The truth was that he had a stall and a reputation and a room with a lock, and he had mistaken those things for safety, the same mistake ten thousand people in Vell were making at this very hour, folding their hands and keeping their heads down and believing that the fire would pass over them like a plague with better manners.

Below, a new voice joined the soldiers'. A neighbor's voice.

Jude knew it before he placed it. Everyone in the building knew that voice — Tammas Wren's, the apothecary, the man whose tinctures kept Mira breathing. Tammas had a stoop and ink-stained fingers and had once wept, actually wept, over a dead sparrow Jude found on the stair. Tammas came up twice a week with the medicine and stayed to argue politics in a whisper, careful, coded, the way everyone argued politics now, and Jude had trusted him with the one thing that mattered: his sister's lungs.

"That's Fairweather's room," Tammas was saying. "Third floor, rear. He sells charms at the Saltmarket. Luck-knots. Fever-strings." A pause, and then, in the tone of a man adding a coin to a scale: "I've seen his work take hold. I saw a charm cure a horse's lungfever last spring. It's not trickery. He's gifted."

Silence from the street. Then the leader's voice, satisfied, the voice of a man ticking a box: "Name and statement witnessed. You'll present yourself at the registry office tomorrow for your exemption papers. Cooperators are protected. That's the queen's word."

"I understand," said Tammas Wren.

Jude sat down on the wet tiles because his legs had decided the matter without consulting him.

It wasn't betrayal that undid him — he had expected betrayal, in the abstract, the way you expect weather. It was the *price*. Tammas hadn't sold him for gold. There was no gold. Tammas had sold him for an exemption paper, for a stamp that said *protected*, for the right to keep his shop and his neck in the same postal code. Jude's life, weighed against a piece of stamped paper, and the paper had won, and Tammas had probably been relieved, probably walked home lighter, telling himself he'd had no choice, telling himself the story every denouncer in history has told: *if not me, another. Better me. Better me.*

And the worst of it, the part that would keep Jude company on every road he walked for the rest of this flight, was that Tammas was right. If not him, another. Mrs. Ollen, maybe, kind old Mrs. Ollen with her cracked-open window, if the soldiers knocked on her door and asked her sweetly enough. Piet. The widow Brandt. Fear didn't make people evil. That was the lesson of the week, written in the criers' announcements and the emptying market stalls and the way no one met anyone's eyes anymore: fear just made people *punctual*. It got them to betray on schedule, efficiently, without malice, the way Tammas had, standing in the torchlight in his nightshirt with his ink-stained hands folded, selling his neighbor for a stamp.

Jude realized he was crying only when a tear hit the tile and caught torchlight. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand and made himself look down again, made himself memorize it — the soldiers carrying out his table, his chair, his stock of harmless knots, piling them in the street. Made himself watch as a sergeant held up one of his fever-strings and turned it in the firelight, then dropped it and ground it under his boot.

Made himself listen as they asked Tammas about the girl.

"Sickly," Tammas said. "Not gifted. Just sick." And there — God help them both — there was something in the apothecary's voice that might have been shame, buried deep, a stone dropped down a well that hit bottom fast. He had drawn the line at the child. He had sold the brother but not the sister, and he would live the rest of his life inside that distinction, and Jude found he hated him less for it than he hated himself for understanding it.

"The girl stays," the sergeant said. "Sick children aren't evidence." A pause. "Unless someone says otherwise."

"No one will," Tammas said quickly. Too quickly.

Jude breathed again. Barely.

* * *

He moved north along the rooftops while the raid played out below, because staying to watch was a luxury for men with nothing to lose, and he had one thing left: he needed to see her. One more time. He told himself it was operational — he needed to know she was unharmed, needed to leave word somehow — but he knew the shape of a lie that size when he carried it.

The tenements thinned toward the tannery district, and he dropped into the yard of the fulling mill, where the wool hung in gray rows like a congregation of ghosts, and circled back along the drainage channel to the alley behind his own building. From the shadow of a rain barrel he could see the whole scene lit orange: the pile of his belongings in the street, soldiers lounging, the sergeant writing in a ledger by torchlight. And at the mouth of the alley, wrapped in a blanket against the autumn cold, small and straight-backed, stood Mira.

They had woken her after all.

She wasn't crying. She stood in her nightgown with her blanket around her shoulders like a cloak of state, watching the soldiers carry out her brother's life piece by piece, and her chin was up, and her breath whistled faintly in the cold air, and Jude understood with a pain that felt physical that she was performing. She was performing calm for the soldiers, the way she'd promised — *I won't tell, not ever* — and the performance was costing her everything, and he couldn't go to her, couldn't touch her, couldn't do the one thing brothers were for.

The sergeant approached her. Bent slightly, the condescension of armed men toward children. "You're the Fairweather girl."

"Yes."

"Your brother run often? At night?"

"He works late sometimes." Flat. Perfect. A fourteen-year-old giving a masterclass in the art of knowing nothing, and Jude thought: *where did she learn that,* and answered himself: *from me. From watching me flinch at every knock for three years.* He had taught her caution the way other parents taught prayers, and now it stood between them wearing a blanket.

"If he comes back," the sergeant said, "you'll send for the registry office. There's reward money for families that cooperate. Good money."

Mira looked at the sergeant the way she had once looked at the debt collector. "He wouldn't come back here. You've made sure of that."

Something flickered in the sergeant's face — irritation, maybe, or the faint discomfort of a man hearing a child say a true thing. He turned away. An officer called from the far end of the lane, and the soldiers began to form up, and the torches began to move away down Marrow Lane, taking their orange light with them, leaving the street to the ordinary dark of a city at night, which suddenly seemed like a kindness.

Tammas Wren lingered. He stood beside Mira in the gutter-light and said something low, too low to catch, and Mira turned her head and looked at him, and even across forty yards Jude saw the expression on her face — not forgiveness, not rage, just a terrible young clarity, the look of someone crossing a name off a list — and then she walked inside and closed the door, and Tammas stood alone in the street with his hands hanging at his sides until he too went in.

Jude stayed in the shadow of the rain barrel until his knees ached.

He could not go to her tonight. Tomorrow the description would be posted; tonight the soldiers still half-believed their quarry had simply stepped out for the evening. Every hour he lingered was an hour Mira spent under the eyes of men who would learn, sooner or later, that a hunted man returns to the people he loves — it was practically a law of nature, and the hunt had built its whole machinery on laws of nature. Leaving her was the only protection he had left to give her, and it was worthless, and he gave it anyway.

He spoke to her from across the distance, silently, with the old words, the ones that used to have a god behind them: *Live. Grow old. Forget my face.*

Behind his breastbone, in the place that had been silent for three years, something stirred — faint as a coal remembering fire, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall.

*Carry,* said nothing, in no voice at all. Or perhaps he only wanted it to.

Jude climbed out of the alley and went north, alone, toward the ash-roads and the mountains and whatever waited past them, and behind him the city of Vell settled back into sleep, having sold him cheaply and slept well on the profit.
