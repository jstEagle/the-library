## 6. Road of Ash

The north road out of Vell was a scar of gray mud and old cart-ruts, and Jude walked it with nothing but the boots on his feet and a stolen loaf of black bread in his coat.

Three days he had been walking. Three days of keeping his hood up, of counting soldiers at crossroads, of sleeping in ditches with his hands curled around his own wrists like a man afraid of what his fingers might do unwatched. The city shrank behind him into a smudge of smoke against a low sky, and still he could not stop seeing it: the criers, the lists nailed to the chapel doors, old Tam Wicke dragged from his cooper's shop by men who had bought their beer from him for twenty years. *Register or burn.* The words had followed him out of Vell like dogs.

His sister's face was worse than any of it. He had stood outside her window on the last night, close enough to see the candle, too hunted to knock. He had left her medicine with a neighbor he no longer trusted, paid in luck-knots he was no longer sure were lies, and walked away before dawn because staying meant killing them both.

He did not pray. That habit had died three years ago, quietly, without ceremony — but somewhere past the second milestone he caught himself moving his lips anyway, shaping a name that would not answer, and he spat into the ditch and told himself it was only wind.

*Carry.*

No. He would not think about that either. He was just a man walking north, a charm-seller with empty pockets, one more gray body on a road full of gray bodies fleeing the Crown Hunt.

The road was full of them. That was the thing Vell's criers had not mentioned — or perhaps they had, and Jude had been too busy planning his own escape to hear it. Farmers with carts piled high and children asleep under sacking. A dyer whose blue-stained hands shook as she counted and recounted coins she could not spend anywhere ahead of her. An old man carrying a birdcage with no bird in it, who looked at Jude once and then away, quickly, the way people look at each other now — measuring, weighing, asking silently whether this stranger is the kind who sells names to save his own skin.

Jude kept his eyes down and his pace steady and let them all look away first.

It rained on the third evening. Cold, needling rain that turned the road to porridge and drove the other refugees under hedgerows and into the mouths of culverts. Jude found shelter where he could — the broken lee of a mile-marker shrine, one of the little waystones the old faith had raised and the new crown had defaced, the carved faces hammered smooth so that the stones stood blind and anonymous, mourning something no one could name. He wedged himself beneath its lintel and ate half the bread and watched the rain stitch silver through the dark.

That was when he heard the singing.

Faint, at first. He thought it was the wind until he caught the shape of words — not a hymn, not quite, but something older, a lullaby cadence, rising and falling somewhere off the road to the east. His whole body went rigid. In three years he had not heard that melody; in three years, no one had dared sing it within a day's walk of a garrison. It was the kind of song that got villages registered.

Curiosity was a sin his mother had named him for, and it killed him as surely now as it ever had. He left the shrine and followed the sound through wet bracken, along a drover's track, until the trees opened onto a hollow where a dozen fires burned low behind a ring of wagons — painted wagons, tinker's wagons, mender's wagons, hung with copper pots and bundles of dried herbs that might have been seasoning and might have been anything else. People moved among the fires: perhaps forty souls, all ages, wrapped in travel-stained wool. They looked, at first glance, like any caravan of the roads.

At second glance, Jude saw the wards.

They were subtle — he almost missed them, and hiding things from notice was his trade. But there was a knotwork pattern chalked around the perimeter that was not decoration, and the way the fires burned — steady, unflickering, in a wind that bent the grass flat — was not chance. And over the nearest wagon door hung a small iron charm shaped like an eye with the pupil closed.

A caravan of hidden folk. Gifted folk, on the run from the same hunt he was.

Every sensible bone in his body said turn back. Sensible bones had kept him alive in Vell for three years; sensible bones had taught him that the gifted survived by scattering, by never gathering, because gathering made targets. But the rain was coming harder, and his feet were bleeding inside his stolen boots, and the smell of woodsmoke and stewed barley rose over the hollow like an answer to a question he hadn't asked aloud.

And underneath it — faint as a pulse in a sleeping wrist — the spark turned toward those fires the way a plant turns toward light.

He was still standing at the tree line deciding when a voice behind him said, "Well. Either you're lost, or you're the bravest fool to walk out of Vell this week."

He spun. A woman stood an arm's length away, and he had heard absolutely nothing of her approach. She was sixty if she was a day, broad and weathered, with white hair braided into a crown and eyes the color of river slate — eyes that took his measure in one unhurried sweep and found him wanting in several respects. She carried a staff of blackthorn, and she held it the way a soldier holds a spear, not a grandmother holds a cane.

"Lost," Jude said. "Just lost."

"Mm." She glanced at his boots, his hood, the bread in his coat pocket, the direction he'd come from. "Lost men don't stand in the rain counting the wards on my wagons. Lost men come straight to the fire." She tilted her head. "You counted the eye-charm. Most don't even see it. So either you're a spy for the inquisitors — in which case you're very good, and I'll be sorry — or you've got the gift yourself and you know exactly what you're looking at."

"I'm a tinker," Jude said. "Charms. Luck-knots, fever-bags, that sort of thing. Honest work."

"Honest," she repeated, tasting the word. "I sold honest charms in Vell myself, twenty years ago, before the last edict. Do you know what I learned? That the honest ones are always the most frightened, and the frightened are always the best at lying." She stepped closer. Rain ran off the brim of her hood between them. "I am called Mother Cassia. This is the Road Company, and we will feed you tonight whether you tell me truth or not, because that is what we do. But you will sleep outside the ward-ring until you do."

She turned and walked back toward the fires, and after a moment — because there was nowhere else in the world to go — Jude followed her down into the light.

* * *

They fed him barley stew with leeks and a heel of dark bread, and no one asked him a single question while he ate, which somehow felt more like an interrogation than questions would have. He sat on a wagon step at the edge of the firelight, inside the ring but barely, and watched the Road Company be themselves around him.

They were careful. That was the first thing he understood. No one worked magic openly, but everyone touched it the way he did — sideways, through objects and gestures, disguised as craft. An old man mended a child's torn sleeve and the stitches pulled closed a heartbeat faster than thread alone should move. A young woman with a burn-scarred jaw sorted herbs, and the ones she set aside for medicine seemed to lean toward her hand. A boy of maybe eight traced shapes in the dirt with a stick, and the shapes glowed faintly for a breath before he smeared them out, glancing around, guilty as a thief.

Nobody scolded him. Nobody even looked surprised. And Jude felt something twist in his chest that he refused, firmly, to name.

"You're staring," said the girl with the scarred jaw, sitting down beside him with her own bowl. She was younger than him by ten years, maybe, with quick brown eyes and the particular directness of someone who has already lost everything worth being polite about.

"Am I?"

"You've been watching Brann's stitching and Tally's dirt-pictures and pretending you weren't. It's all right. Everyone here does it the first night. It's like drinking cold water after a drought." She spooned stew. "I'm Merrin. I was a midwife's girl in Corrow Ford. I can ease a birth-pain so the mother sleeps through labor, did you know that? Took me six years to learn it and one morning with the registrars to lose it." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "What do you do?"

"Luck-knots," Jude said. "Fever-charms. Small things. Mostly fraud, honestly. Knots and dried herbs and a good patter."

Merrin snorted. "Mostly fraud. And the rest?"

"The rest is practice," he said, "and knowing what people want to hear."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he realized she was doing what Mother Cassia had done — measuring him, finding the seams. "You say it like a man reciting a confession he's decided not to mean anymore," she said, and got up and carried her bowl away before he could decide how to hate her for that.

Sleep came hard and shallow. He bedded down in the straw of an open wagon near the ring's edge — they had given him that much, at least, though the ward-line still lay between him and the heart of the camp like a sentence not yet served — and he lay listening to forty strangers breathe and telling himself the tightness under his breastbone was only exhaustion.

In the deep hours, the spark stirred.

It had been quiet since Vell — quieter than it had been in months, as though it too were fleeing, as though whatever lived in him had recognized the hunting horns and gone to ground. But now, surrounded by the low murmur of gifted sleepers, by wards chalked in old knotwork and charms hung with closed eyes, it woke the way a cat wakes: slowly, luxuriously, turning over in the cage of his ribs. Warmth spread along his arms. Not fire. Something gentler and far more frightening — a sense of *recognition*, of coming home, and beneath it that single word again, soft as a fingerprint on fogged glass.

*Carry.*

"Stop," Jude breathed into the straw. "Not now. Not here."

The warmth pulsed once, almost petulant, and settled. But it had been enough. Down the row of wagons, the old man with the empty birdcage lifted his head from his blankets and looked directly at Jude across the dark, and did not look away for a long time.

* * *

Morning came gray and busy. The caravan struck camp with the efficiency of people who had done it many times and would do it many more, and Jude discovered that Mother Cassia had decided his status for him overnight.

"You'll take the rear wagon with Doss," she said, appearing at his elbow while he was still rubbing sleep from his eyes. "He's lame and bad-tempered and needs someone to curse at who isn't me. You can mend wheels?"

"I can mend most things."

"Of course you can. Charm-sellers always can." She pressed a bowl of porridge into his hands and studied him with those slate eyes. "You slept inside the ring. Did you notice?"

"I noticed."

"And?"

"And nothing happened." He met her gaze and held it, which cost more than she would ever know. "If I were going to hurt your people, Mother Cassia, I'd have done it while they slept."

"That," she said, "is precisely the reasoning of every spy ever caught. But." She let it hang, then shrugged. "Doss will work it out of you, or he won't. Walk or ride, Fairweather. We make twelve miles before noon, and the inquisitors' riders make thirty."

"How do you know my—"

"You talk in your sleep," she said, and walked away.

So began the strangest week of Jude's life since the week his god died. The Road Company moved north along the drover tracks, avoiding the king's roads, camping in hollows and ruined mills, and Jude fell into its rhythm the way water finds a channel. He learned the wagons: Doss's rear cart with its complaining axle, the herb-wagon where Merrin worked, the children's wagon that smelled of chalk and honey. He learned the rules — no open workings within sight of any road, no songs after dusk, no names given fully even among themselves, in case one of them broke. He learned that they had buried two of their own since the Edict, not dead but fled separately, scattered south, because the company was too large to hide forever and everyone knew it and no one said it.

And he learned, slowly, treacherously, that he liked them.

That was the danger of it. Every night around the fires someone told a story, and every story had a gift in it — small, human, desperate things. A woman who could find water in stone. A man whose touch drew fever out of children and into his own hands, leaving them scarred like burned wood. Merrin easing births. Tally drawing lights in the dust. Ordinary miracles, the size of a life. Jude sat among them with his hood down and his silence up and thought: *these are the people the queen means to burn. These exact people. For exactly these things.*

And underneath the thought, coiled and patient, the spark listened — and grew warmer every time he let himself feel it.

He hid it. He was good at hiding; he had been hiding it since the night three years ago when his god's voice cut off mid-word and everything holy in him went dark except this one coal. He dampened it down beneath layers of practiced nothing, wore his fraud's smile, sold Doss a luck-knot that was genuinely just string, and laughed at the right moments. By the fourth day, Doss had decided Jude was a coward with clever hands, which was close enough to the truth to be comfortable. By the fifth day, the children had decided he was theirs, which was considerably less comfortable.

"Tell the story again," Tally demanded on the sixth night, hauling at his sleeve. "The one about the fox and the moon."

"It's not much of a story."

"Everything's a story if you do the voice."

So he did the voice, and the children laughed, and around the fire Merrin smiled at him over her mending, and Mother Cassia watched him from across the flames with an expression he could not read and did not try to. And Jude thought, with something like grief: *this is what it would have been like. If the god hadn't gone silent. If the crown hadn't come. If I were just a man.*

Then the old man with the empty birdcage sat down beside him in the dark beyond the firelight, and the evening changed.

"They call me Sorrel," the old man said. "I don't give the rest of the name. You understand why."

"I understand why."

"I kept birds," Sorrel said. "Forty years. Canaries, finches — songbirds, all of them. When the registrars came to Corrow Ford, they took the birds first. Said song was a working." He turned his cup in his hands. "I carry the cage so I remember what we're running from. Empty things, kept for what they used to hold." He looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were pale and terribly clear. "You carry something too, charm-seller. I felt it the first night. Like standing next to a banked forge. Like the air before lightning."

Jude kept his face still. Ten years of selling lies to frightened people had built him for exactly this moment, and he spent all of it at once. "You felt wrong, old man. We're all frightened. Fear feels like weather."

"Fear feels like fear," Sorrel said mildly. "This felt like a cathedral." He drank, unhurried, and rose to go, and paused. "Mother Cassia was a priestess, you know. Before the purging. High altar of Saint Verren's, if you remember the old calendar. She knows every sign the old faith ever wrote, and she has been watching you since the night you came out of the rain." He tapped the side of his nose. "Whatever you're hiding, son — and it's bigger than a canary — ask yourself what happens when a priestess finally decides to look."

He shuffled off toward his wagon with his empty cage swinging from his hand, and Jude sat alone in the dark with the fire's heat dying on his face and the spark burning quietly, steadily, traitorously under his heart.

*Carry,* it murmured, content as a sleeping child.

"Yes," Jude whispered to it, to the dark, to no one. "I know. Just not yet. Please. Not yet."

Northward, beyond the black line of the hills, the road climbed toward the mountains — and somewhere on those heights, rumor said, the inquisitor Aldric Renn had left the king's highway and turned onto the drover tracks.

The company didn't know it yet. But the birds had stopped singing that morning, all along the valley, and Mother Cassia had marked it, and said nothing, and begun to pray.
