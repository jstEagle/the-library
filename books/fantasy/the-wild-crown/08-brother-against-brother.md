## 8. Brother Against Brother

The rain had followed them out of Vell for three days, thin and cold as a needle drawn through cloth, and Aldric Renn had stopped noticing it somewhere on the second morning. That was the trouble with the road — it wore a man down until he mistook endurance for peace.

He stood at the edge of the wheat country with his hood back and let the water run down his face while his trackers read the mud below him. Six riders and eleven walkers, by the sign of it. A cart with a cracked axle that had been repaired twice with green wood. Children's footprints beside an adult's, small and quick, the stride of someone being carried in turns. A caravan of the hidden kind, moving north by night, sleeping in ditches by day, leaving behind them the particular disorder of people who had never been taught to travel but were learning fast because they had no other choice.

"They'll make for the old temple road," said Brannic, crouched over the prints. He was the youngest of Renn's retinue and the hungriest; hunger was useful, up to a point. "There's nothing else out here worth hiding in."

"There's everything out here worth hiding in," Renn said. "That's rather the point of it." He turned his collar against the rain. "They're not running toward anything. They're running away from Vell, which means they haven't decided where to die yet. We have time."

Brannic looked up at him with something between admiration and unease. It was the look Renn had grown used to over six years of service — men watching him work and finding the work too clean, too quiet, and wondering what it cost him. The answer was: nothing anymore. Or nothing they would recognize as cost. There had been a time when every warrant signed meant a sleepless night, when he'd sat awake listening for the voice of his god and hearing only his own heartbeat, and somewhere in those years the sleeplessness had simply stopped, the way a wound stops bleeding when there is no blood left.

He mounted his grey mare and rode north along the hedgerows, and the wheat closed around him, tall and wet and golden-grey under the low sky, and he thought about Jude Fairweather, which he did most days now, though he had learned not to say so.

* * *

The last conversation had happened four years ago, in the chapter house above the Salt Gate, on the night the silence came.

Renn remembered it in pieces, the way a man remembers a fire — not as a sequence but as a series of unbearable images. The bell ringing wrong, three strokes instead of the two that called the Order to evening prayer. Brothers coming into the hall with their faces white, each of them having discovered alone and then all together that the god was gone. Not dead — that word came later, argued over for years — but *silent*, the way a room goes silent when someone you love has walked out of it and taken the warmth with them.

Jude had been the first to speak. Of course he had. Jude had never once in twelve years of brotherhood managed to hold his tongue past the moment honesty required breaking.

"He's not testing us," Jude had said, standing in the middle of the hall with rain still dripping off him from the watch he'd cut short. He'd run the whole way from the wall, Renn realized later. Run, because he'd felt it first and couldn't bear to be alone with it. "Stop saying he's testing us. I've heard the tests of the gods in scripture and this isn't one. A test has a shape. A test ends. This is—" His voice had cracked, and he'd pressed on through the crack as if it weren't there, which was the most Jude thing about him. "This is a door closing. And I want to know who locked it."

"Blasphemy," said Master Corven, their prior, from the high seat. "Grief makes men say wild things, brother. Kneel. Pray. He will return."

"And if he doesn't?" Jude hadn't knelt. He never knelt well; even in prayer he knelt like a man about to stand up again. "What do we do tomorrow? What do we do with the oaths? Do we keep guarding a door no one is behind?"

"Jude," Renn had said quietly, from the bench near the door, meaning *stop*, meaning *not here*, meaning *I can't watch you do this*.

Jude had turned to him, and Renn would carry the look on his face for the rest of his life — not anger, worse than anger, betrayal arriving ahead of schedule. "You felt it too, Aldric. Don't tell me you didn't. You've been gray around the mouth since the bell rang. So don't sit there and police my grief because yours frightens you."

The hall had gone very quiet. Thirty brothers pretending not to listen, which is to say thirty brothers listening.

"My grief is mine," Renn had said, and heard how cold his own voice came out, how much it sounded like Corven's, like the crown's, like every voice he'd ever sworn never to sound like. "And my oath is not conditional on being answered. If the god is silent, then we hold faith in the dark or we don't hold it at all."

"That's beautiful," Jude said. "Write it down. Put it on the door. And when they come — and they will come, Aldric, within the year, you mark me — when the crown comes asking why we still exist, what will your faith in the dark be worth? Will it feed anyone? Will it stop a single pyre?"

"You think there will be pyres."

"I think the queen has been waiting for exactly this since before her coronation," Jude said, "and I think every man in this room knows it, and I think the only question left is which side of the door we're standing on when she opens it."

It was Corven who ended it, calling the hall to prayer, ordering Jude confined to his cell for words unbecoming. But the break had already happened, clean as a snapped bowstring. Renn stayed. Jude left before dawn, taking nothing but his sword and his kit and the small wooden charm he'd carried since novitiate, and Renn had lain awake listening to his boots on the stair and told himself that grief made men fools, that Jude would come back when the wildness burned out of him, that the Order held and the oath held and the dark was only dark.

Six months later the crown dissolved the Order. Two years after that, the first witch-fires went up in Vell. Four years after the bell rang wrong, Aldric Renn wore the crown's grey and silver and hunted the gifted through the wet wheat fields of the north, telling himself with each warrant that he kept faith in the dark, that the dark was only dark, that the door was only a door.

He was beginning, lately, to hear how much the litany sounded like a man whistling past a grave.

* * *

They took the stragglers at dusk on the fourth day, in a hollow of alders where a stream crossed the road.

Two of them — a young man with a smith's forearms and a girl of perhaps fifteen with a burn scar covering half her face, walking a hundred paces behind the main party, which was the mistake that stragglers always made. Renn had given the order for no horses in the last mile, and his men came out of the trees soft-footed, and it was over almost before it began. The smith swung a blacksmith's hammer he must have carried three hundred miles for exactly this moment, and Brannic took the blow on his shield and put him down with the flat of a sword, professionally, without cruelty. The girl didn't fight at all. She just stood there with her scarred face lifted to the rain and her hands open at her sides, and something in her stillness made Renn's chest hurt in a way he had no use for.

"Bind the man," he said. "Leave her hands free. She's a child."

"She's gifted," Brannic said. "The edict doesn't distinguish."

"The edict," Renn said, "is a piece of paper, Brannic, and I am a human being, and when the two conflict I will let you know." He heard the edge in his own voice and was surprised by it. Brannic blinked and obeyed.

They made camp in the hollow, fires small and screened, and Renn sat the prisoners by the fire and gave them stew, because starving prisoners told lies and fed prisoners mostly told the truth. He questioned them gently, which unsettled them more than roughness would have. He had learned long ago that fear closes mouths and kindness, oddly, opens them — people want to be understood, even by the man holding the leash.

The smith's name was Tam. The girl was called Wren. They were cousins, they said, from the weaving villages south of Vell; Wren could warm water and ease fevers with her hands, and Tam had never shown a gift at all but had refused to register and refused to abandon her, which in the crown's arithmetic made him as guilty as any witch.

"And the caravan?" Renn asked. "Who leads it?"

"A priestess," Tam said. Then, correcting himself with the reflexive flinch of a man who has learned what the word costs: "She was a priestess. Mother Cassia. Of the old order."

Renn kept his face still. Inside, something turned over slowly, like a stone shifting in a riverbed. Cassia of the Grey Cloister. He had her name in three files in his saddlebag. She'd been slipping convoys north for two years, and every time he closed on her she dissolved like smoke, and he had begun privately to suspect she had help inside the hunt itself, a suspicion he filed away and did not examine too closely, the way he did not examine many things.

"How many children?"

"Seven," Wren said. Her voice was flat and steady, and she watched him the whole time she spoke, unblinking, as if memorizing his face for a reckoning. "Nine if you count the babies, and everyone counts the babies."

"Where are you going?"

Tam said nothing. Wren said nothing. They looked at each other, and Renn saw the whole calculus of fear pass between them — what they might buy with information, what it might cost the others, whether the man in grey and silver was a man at all or just the edict wearing boots.

"It matters," Renn said, and was surprised to find he meant it in a way the sentence didn't explain. "Whether there are children changes what happens next. I give you my word on that."

"Your word," Tam said. Not bitter. Just tired, and wondering, and done believing.

"Yes," Renn said. "My word."

Wren tilted her head, studying him, and then she said, in the tone of someone laying a card face-up on a table, "You knew our shield-brother. Before."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark an owl asked its one question of the night. Renn found that his hands, folded loosely in his lap, had gone very still, the way hands go still when a man is afraid of what they'll do if allowed to move.

"What makes you say that?"

"You have the look," she said. "Mother Cassia described it. Men who served the old order — you sit like them. You listen like them. When Tam dropped the hammer you struck to stun, not to kill, and you didn't even think about it, and you were ashamed right after, and you didn't know why." She shrugged, a small terrible shrug for fifteen years old. "And there's a man traveling with us now who talks about his shield-brother sometimes. In his sleep, mostly. He says his name like it's a wound he keeps checking."

The hollow seemed to narrow. The firelight seemed to lean closer. Renn heard himself ask, in a voice he barely recognized as his own, "What name?"

Wren looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her decide something. "Fairweather," she said. "Jude Fairweather."

* * *

Later, when the camp slept and the sentries walked their slow rounds, Renn sat apart with his warrant case open on his knees and did not look at the papers inside it.

Jude. Here, in the north, in Cassia's caravan, four years and three hundred miles from the door he'd walked out of, and Renn had been riding parallel to him for days without knowing it, reading his trail in broken axles and children's footprints, closing on him hand over hand like a man hauling in a rope whose other end was tied around his own heart.

He tried to summon the righteous clarity that the work required. He tried to see Jude as the warrants described him: an unregistered carrier, a node of contagion, a threat to the succession and therefore to the peace that the succession secured. He had written such descriptions himself. He knew their grammar better than anyone alive.

But what rose instead was Jude at nineteen, laughing on the wall-walk with rain in his hair, insisting that a man could serve the god best by serving the people the god was supposedly for. Jude kneeling in the mud of the plague quarter with his sleeves rolled up, doing the work the priests wouldn't sully themselves with. Jude in the doorway on the last night, pausing on the stair, looking back — Renn had pretended to be asleep, and Jude had stood there a long moment, and then the boots had gone on down, and Renn had lain in the dark with his eyes shut and let his brother walk out of his life rather than be the one to move first.

*I want to know who locked the door.*

Four years later the queen's edict had answered the question in the ugliest way possible: whoever locked it, the crown had thrown away the key and built a pyre beside it.

Brannic came and stood at the edge of the firelight. "Orders, sir? The main party will reach the temple ruins by tomorrow dusk if they keep pace. We can take them on the open ground."

Renn looked at the coals. In them he could see, if he let himself, the shape of a pyre — and in the pyre, if he let himself, a figure with rain-dark hair and a stubborn chin, and around the pyre seven children, nine if you counted the babies, and everyone counted the babies.

"Tomorrow dusk," he said. "We take them at the temple road. Full company." He closed the warrant case. "And Brannic — the Fairweather file stays in my saddlebag. No one else reads it."

Brannic hesitated. "Sir? Is there something—"

"No," Renn said, and heard the lie land between them, and picked up his cloak and lay down facing away from the fire. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we hunt my brother."

He said it to the dark, where no one could hear, and the dark — as it had been for four years — said nothing back.
