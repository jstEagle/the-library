## 9. Fire in the Wheatfield

Jude woke before dawn to a silence that was wrong.

The caravan had made camp in the elbow of a wheatfield three days north of the river country — a sea of grain gone gold and heavy-headed, waiting for harvest no one would come to bring. Mother Cassia liked such places: open ground where riders could be seen coming for miles, where no hedgerow could hide an ambush. But this morning the birds were not singing, and the sentry's cough from the eastern rise was absent, and the dogs the caravan kept — two mangy things named Brindle and Sorrow — were not barking at all. They were pressed flat against their rope, ears back, making a sound low in their throats like water about to boil.

He lay still in the wagon bed and listened with more than his ears.

For three years he had lived beneath his own skin like a man living in a cellar under a busy street — aware of footsteps overhead, unable to see them. The spark sat in him the way an ember sits in banked ash: patient, dim, giving off nothing but a faint warmth he had learned to ignore. He had gotten good at ignoring it. Good at the small denials, the constant dampening, the practiced dullness of his own blood.

But now something moved through the field's silence like a fish through still water, and the ember turned over in its sleep, and Jude felt it — felt *himself* — tilt toward the disturbance the way a compass needle tilts toward iron.

*Soldiers,* said the thing inside him, in no words at all. It did not use words. It used certainty.

He was out of the wagon with his boots in his hand before his mind finished agreeing.

The camp was a scatter of wagons and lean-tos among the wheat's edge, forty souls if you counted the children, and you always counted the children first because they were the reason the caravan moved at night and slept in daylight hollows and never lit fires larger than a cupped hand. Cassia rose from her bedroll as he passed, already awake, her gray braid loose over her shoulder, her eyes finding his face and reading it the way she read weather.

"You feel it too," she said quietly.

"Riders. East and south both." His mouth was dry. "They're not trying to be quiet."

"No." She looked east, where the sky had begun to bruise gray at the horizon. "They don't need to be quiet when there are forty of them and nine of us who can do anything at all."

"Cassia—"

"I know what you're going to say." She turned to him then, and in the half-dark her face was all planes and shadow, older than her years, older maybe than any years. "You're going to say you should go on alone. You've been saying it in your posture for two weeks, Jude Fairweather. I let you believe I hadn't noticed because the noticing was doing you good. But not today. Today you stay behind the wagons with the little ones, and you keep your spark in your chest, and you let us handle soldiers the way we've handled them before — by being poor, tired, uninteresting people worth no bounty."

"They're not here for uninteresting people."

"How would you know?" It was not cruel. It was a test, and they both knew it, and he failed it by saying nothing. Cassia exhaled through her nose. "Keep it hidden," she said. "Whatever happens. Promise me that much, at least."

He promised, because she asked, and because lying to her felt less like lying than like praying — a small dishonesty offered up in hopes of mercy.

Then the sun came up over the eastern wheat, and the riders came out of it.

* * *

There were thirty of them, perhaps more, moving in a wide crescent through the grain with the unhurried confidence of men who knew the arithmetic of the encounter. They wore the crown's colors — black and white, the ermine-and-ash of Queen Isolde's hunt — and their lances carried the new banners, the ones with the flame stitched below the crown, the flame that meant *burning ordered here*. At their center rode a figure in gray steel without ornament, and even at four hundred paces Jude knew the set of those shoulders, the way the rider held his head slightly forward, as if perpetually leaning into a wind only he could feel.

Aldric Renn.

Three years since the god went silent. Three years since the night the order broke apart in the mountain passes — since Aldric had knelt in the snow and sworn himself to the crown that hunted everything they had once protected, while Jude had walked south alone with a dead god's weight in his chest. Three years, and his body still reacted to that silhouette before his mind could intervene: the loosening in the gut, the half-formed word *brother* rising toward his teeth.

He killed the word. He had practice.

"Inside the ring of wagons," Cassia was saying, calm as a woman directing traffic at market. "Tam, Wrenna — wards on the east side, low ones, just enough to slow a horse. Everyone else, hands visible, weapons down. We are farmers. We are pilgrims. We are nobody." Her voice never rose, and yet it reached every ear in camp, the way it always did — the last small gift her own god had left her, a voice that organized the frightened.

Jude took his place among the children behind the third wagon. There were six of them, ranging from a toddler asleep against a barrel to a girl of ten named Pip who had been born knowing how to make bread rise without yeast and had nearly been burned for it in Vell. Pip looked up at him now with enormous eyes.

"Is it the fire-men?" she whispered.

"It's soldiers," Jude said. "Soldiers are just men with orders. Orders can be survived."

"What if the orders are bad?"

"Then we survive them anyway," he said, "and outlive the men who gave them." It was the kind of thing Cassia said. He heard it leave his mouth and marveled briefly at how a person could become someone else simply by standing among the frightened long enough.

The riders drew rein at the edge of the wheat. A lieutenant spurred forward, a young man with a voice like a herald trained too well. "In the name of Queen Isolde, First of Her Name, Protector of the Realm and Scourge of Unlawful Craft! This caravan is detained under the Edict of Registration. All persons will present themselves for inspection. The gifted will be marked and conveyed. Resistance is—" he consulted nothing, reciting from memory, savoring it, "—treason against the crown, punishable by fire."

Cassia walked out to meet him, alone, hands open at her sides. From the wagons Jude watched her small straight back and thought: *she has done this before.* How many times? How many roads, how many young lieutenants, how many careful lies about sick relatives and pilgrimage vows?

"We're simple folk," Cassia called. "Grain merchants out of the river country, bound for the mountain markets. You'll find no craft here but baking and mule-tending."

"Mother," the lieutenant said, with the flat courtesy of a man reading a script that did not include improvisation, "we have a description. A man traveling under false names. Tall. Dark-haired. Scarred left forearm. Traveling with a caravan out of Vell." He unfolded a paper, though he did not need it; he had clearly memorized it. "The crown pays fifty crowns for information leading to his capture. One hundred for the man himself, alive."

The camp went very still. Jude felt forty pairs of eyes arrive at the wagon behind which he crouched, and just as quickly look away — the peculiar choreography of the hunted, everyone suddenly fascinated by their own hands, their own mules, the sky.

Fifty crowns. He thought of the fever-charm that had worked in the Vell market, of the neighbor whose name he'd never learned pressing coin into a soldier's palm. Fear had a price list now, printed and posted. It was almost tidy.

And then the lieutenant said the other thing.

"Search the wagons," he said. "Take the children first."

* * *

Later — in the years after, when people asked him about the wheatfield, and they always eventually asked — Jude would try to explain the moment, and never quite manage it. Because the truth was not that he decided. The truth was that the decision had already been made somewhere deeper than deciding, in the cellar beneath the street, in the ash around the ember, and all his conscious self did was step aside.

Two soldiers dismounted and came toward the wagons. One of them reached for the canvas flap. Inside, the toddler stirred and made a small waking sound, and Pip put her arms around the smaller ones, and Jude saw — with a clarity that felt like falling — exactly how the next hour went if he did nothing. The children sorted. The marked ones taken. Pip, who could make bread rise with a wish, conveyed to a burning cart or a burning square. And himself, hidden, safe, alive, keeping his promise to Cassia, keeping his secret, keeping everything except the only things that mattered.

The soldier pulled back the flap.

Jude stood up.

"Here," he said, and his voice came out strange, doubled, as though another throat spoke underneath his own. "You want me. I'm the one your paper wants. Leave them."

Every head turned. The lieutenant smiled the smile of a man whose morning had gone according to script. "Jude Fairweather," he said. "Or whatever you're calling yourself these days. On your knees, hands where—"

The world tilted.

It was not dizziness. It was perspective. Jude had spent three years looking at his power from above, from outside, from the weary distance of a man regarding a debt he could not pay — and in one breath the angle inverted, and he was looking *out* from inside it. The ember was not an ember. It had never been an ember. It was a coal the size of his soul wrapped in three years of ash, and the ash was peeling away like bark in a fire, and underneath—

Underneath was morning.

He felt it move up through him the way dawn moves across a field: not fast, but unstoppable, touching everything. Heat bloomed in his sternum, ran down his arms, pooled in his palms. The scar on his forearm — the old shield-brother's mark, cut in better years — lit from within like a brand seen through flesh. And the god, his silent god, three years silent, spoke its second word in as many weeks:

*Now.*

The nearest soldier lunged for him, and Jude raised his hand — hardly a gesture, barely intent, the way a man might brush a fly — and the air between them *ignited*. Not flame, not at first. Light. A wall of white-gold radiance that struck the soldier's breastplate and hurled him backward twenty feet into the wheat, armor ringing like a dropped bell, and where Jude's hand had passed, the air itself stayed bright, shimmering, humming with a note too low to hear and too deep to ignore.

The horses screamed and bolted. Half the crescent of riders lost formation at once.

And the wheat caught.

It caught the way dry gold catches, which is to say all at once, everywhere, a running fire that leapt from stalk to stalk faster than a man could shout. Flame rolled eastward across the field in a broad bright tide, and the heat of it washed over the camp, and the sparks rose in spirals, and the smoke began to build its black column against the sunrise, and Jude stood in the middle of it with his hands burning and his heart singing.

That was the terrible part. That was the part he would never say aloud to anyone who hadn't felt it.

It sang.

Power like that does not feel like duty or desperation. It feels like a key turning in a lock you'd forgotten was a lock. Every year of hiding fell off him like burnt husks, and what remained was vast and bright and *right*, and some ancient animal part of him spread its arms in the firelight and wanted more — wanted to chase the riders down, wanted to see how far the light would reach, wanted to find out at last what a god's whole strength felt like instead of a god's leavings. The hymns they'd sung in the old order came back to him entire, words he hadn't spoken since the silence, and he understood suddenly why the first carriers had wept during battle. Not from grief. From gratitude. It was like being given back a limb. Like breathing after drowning.

Pip was tugging his sleeve. "Mister Jude," she said, "the field's on fire."

"Yes," he breathed.

"Aren't you scared?"

And the question went through the singing like a pin through silk.

He looked at his hands. The light was still there, coiled obediently, waiting to be aimed. He looked at the wheatfield, a quarter mile of grain already consumed, the fire racing toward the treeline and the dry grass beyond, and beyond that — he turned, following the line of the burn — the south road, and the village of Harrow's Bend, three miles distant, its thatched roofs and hay barns and its hundred innocent chimneys all downwind of a fire he had started with a flick of his wrist.

He looked at the soldiers, regrouping now at the field's far edge, and saw that they had stopped looking at him with the contempt reserved for fugitives. They were looking at him the way men look at a storm. And one of them — a sergeant, quick-minded — was already wheeling his horse, already driving hard for the south road, already carrying the message that would travel ahead of any rumor: *found him, the carrier is real, he burns like the old stories, send everything.*

"There," Jude said, and his voice shook. "I'm scared of there."

Because exhilaration, he understood now, standing in the roar of his own unveiling, was just horror wearing festival clothes. Everything the light touched, it signed. Every stalk of burning wheat was a signature. The column of smoke wrote his name across the sky in letters visible for thirty miles: *HERE. THE LAST ONE. HERE.* And everyone standing near him — Cassia, Tam and Wrenna, the mule-tenders, the sleeping toddler, Pip with her bread-rising hands — everyone within reach of his shadow was now, forever, written on the same page in the same hand.

He had kept them safe by being hidden. In eight seconds he had made them the most famous forty people in the kingdom.

"The fire," Cassia said beside him — he hadn't seen her cross the space between wagons; perhaps he had been elsewhere entirely — "will reach the treeline in a quarter hour. The wind is wrong. Harrow's Bend is downwind." She looked at him, and her face held neither condemnation nor comfort, only the terrible clarity of a woman doing sums. "Can you turn it?"

"I don't know."

"Then learn," she said, "quickly."

* * *

He learned.

It cost him — he would carry the ache in his bones for a week afterward, a deep-tissue weariness like the flu of the soul — but he learned. He found, feeling along the edges of the power the way a blind man feels along a wall, that the fire answered him the way the horses answered a whisper: reluctantly, partially, with a wildness that never fully consented. He could not extinguish it. But he could *herd* it. He walked to the field's southern edge with the flames bending around him like reeds in a current, arms outstretched, sweat boiling off his skin, and he pushed — shoved the whole burning mile of it westward, away from the treeline, away from the road, grinding it into a plowed fallow strip where it died hissing in the bare earth.

The wheatfield was gone. All of it. A black scar in the shape of a burned letter, smoking under the risen sun. The smell would be in his hair for days. Somewhere in the chaff and ash, he knew, the farmer who owned it was watching his year go up, and there was no charm in Jude's pack that could pay for that, and the knowledge sat in his stomach like a stone alongside all the brighter stones.

The soldiers did not press the attack. They had formed up again at a respectful distance — half a mile, no closer — and they sat their horses in a long thin line against the smoke, watching. Waiting. No longer hunting. *Observing.* One rider detached from the line and galloped south, and no one tried to stop him, and everyone understood.

At the field's northern edge, apart from his men, Aldric Renn had not moved. Through the heat-shimmer Jude watched him watch the burned field, and watched him raise one gauntleted hand — not a threat, not a salute, something between — before turning his horse and riding slowly away, leaving the lieutenant and the line and the fifty-crown arithmetic behind him.

"He knows," Jude said hoarsely.

"He knows," Cassia agreed. "Which means the queen knows by week's end. Which means everything after this is different." She surveyed the camp — the wagons hitched in desperate haste, the children loaded, the gifted folk with ash on their faces looking at Jude with expressions he could not bear to catalog just then: awe in some, fear in others, and in Pip's eyes the uncomplicated shining worship that frightened him worst of all. "They'll sing songs about the wheatfield, you know. By winter, every gifted child in three kingdoms will know the story of the man who burned a mile of grain to save six children. And every witch-hunter, every inquisitor, every frightened farmer with a torch will know it too."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

"I know." She put her hand briefly on his scorched sleeve. "Rest while you can, carrier. Tonight we run — not from them." She nodded south, toward the thin dust of the messenger's wake. "From the *everything*."

Jude looked back once as the caravan rolled out — at the black field, at the column of smoke still climbing into a sky that had seen gods die and would soon enough hear about one waking — and felt, beneath the exhaustion and the stone in his stomach, the coal settle warm in his chest, contented as a fed animal.

It had waited three years to be seen.

Now everyone in the world would be looking.
