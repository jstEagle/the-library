## 10. The Queen's Bargain

The raven arrived at dawn, as ravens did — mud-footed and insolent on the balcony rail of the Amber Tower, its message case scratched with the seal of the Vell garrison. Queen Isolde read the dispatch standing at the open glass, her dressing gown unfastened at the throat because the chambermaids had not yet come and she had never learned to be ashamed of being seen unfinished. The city spread below her in the grey light: slate roofs furred with frost smoke, the river running brown and slow between the granary quays, the bell towers tolling the hour as though the world were a well-ordered thing.

*A carrier has been identified,* the report said. *North road. Traveling with the Cassia caravan. Pursuit engaged under Inquisitor Renn.*

She read it twice. Then she folded it along its creases, very precisely, and set it alight from the candle she kept burning on the balustrade for exactly this purpose, and watched the words curl to black ash in the porcelain dish.

A carrier.

Not a hedge-witch with a talent for finding lost rings. Not a fever-healer skimming sickness off the poor for coppers. A carrier — one of those rare vessels in whom the old divinity had pooled like rainwater in the cupped hands of a statue. Her grandfather's private papers called them *reliquaries*. Her father's called them *plagues waiting for flesh*. The oldest document she had ever touched, in the archive beneath the Hall of Records that no living servant knew existed, had called them something else entirely.

It had called them *kings*.

"Your Majesty." Maerin, her lady of the bedchamber, stood in the doorway with the morning's wardrobe draped over one arm. "The Council convenes at the ninth bell. Lord Chancellor Vess says—"

"I know what Vess says. Vess says whatever is safest to say." Isolde turned from the balcony. "Lay out the grey wool. And the iron collar-brooch."

Maerin blinked. Grey wool was mourning dress, or penance dress, or the dress a queen wore when she wished the court to remember that she governed by labor rather than by ornament. "Grey, Majesty?"

"The kingdom is about to be given a gift," Isolde said. "I want them to see how seriously I take it."

* * *

The Council chamber smelled of tallow and wet stone, as it always did in autumn, when damp crept through the foundations of the palace and no amount of braziers could argue with it. Isolde took her seat beneath the great banner of House Aldemar — the crowned stag, silver on black, stitched by nuns three generations dead — and watched her councilors arrange themselves around the long table like men taking seats on a physician's bench.

Lord Chancellor Vess spoke first, because Vess always spoke first; he had learned in thirty years of service that whoever framed a question owned its answer. "Majesty, the reports from the provinces continue to improve. Registration in the southern marches is near complete. Burnings are down—" he caught himself, smoothed the word away with a small gesture, as though the fire could be unsaid, "—*executions* are down forty percent since the amnesty-for-registration proclamation. The people are learning that cooperation is survival."

"The people are learning that their neighbors' names can be sold," said Lady Corvane dryly. She was the only councilor older than Isolde, and the only one who had held her seat through two reigns, which made her either indispensable or dangerous depending on the week. "We have built an economy of denunciation, Chancellor. I do not call that improvement. I call it harvest."

"It keeps the fields quiet," Vess said.

Isolde let them go on for a while. This was part of governing that no chronicler ever recorded: the hours spent listening to men restate their positions in slightly different words, waiting for the moment when the real business could be conducted without anyone noticing it had begun. She turned her signet ring slowly on her finger. The crowned stag bit into her thumb-pad. Below the table, where no one could see, her knee bounced once, twice — a nervousness she had trained out of her face but never out of her body — and went still.

Then she laid the garrison dispatch on the table.

"A carrier has been found," she said.

The silence that followed had texture. She felt it settle over the councilors like a net over fish — the flinch in Vess's shoulders, the way young Lord Amery stopped chewing his thumbnail, the sudden stillness of Lady Corvane's fan.

"That cannot be," Vess said at last, carefully. "The lineages were ended. Your grandsire saw to it. There has been no confirmed vessel in—"

"In ninety years," Isolde finished. "Yes. And yet here is the word *carrier* written in the hand of Captain Ossler of Vell, who is many things but not imaginative." She slid the paper down the table. "Inquisitor Renn is in pursuit."

Vess read. His lips moved. When he looked up, his face wore the expression of a man doing arithmetic he does not like. "Majesty. If this is true. If there is a living vessel of the old power walking free in the north country—"

"Then every claim this throne rests upon is a house built over a grave," Isolde said, "and the ground is shifting. Yes, Chancellor. I am aware."

She let the words sit. Around the table, the councilors exchanged glances — some fearful, some calculating, one or two merely confused, men who had spent so long inside the official history that they had forgotten it was a wall built over a hole. That was the trouble with founding myths, Isolde thought. They required maintenance. Every generation, someone had to go down into the dark with fresh mortar.

Her grandfather had gone down with fire instead. He had hunted the vessels through nine years of civil war, had burned the temples and salted the groves and put the last of the god-touched to the sword at a place whose name had been struck from every map, and then he had sat down with his clerks and written the peace: *the crown rules by right of victory over the tyrant-gods, who demanded worship and gave famine.* Clean story. Tidy. Taught to children in hornbooks illustrated with little devils wearing antlers.

What the hornbooks did not illustrate was what her grandfather's own letters confessed in the margins, in ink gone brown as old blood: that the gods had been silent for a century before the war. That the temples had been empty shells tended by frightened priests. That the "tyrants" he destroyed had been starving villages of healers and charm-menders and women who could whisper fevers out of infants — and that he had burned them not because they were strong, but because a crown founded on divine sanction could not afford even one living reminder that the divine had chosen differently.

And now one had surfaced anyway. After ninety years. Out of the mud and the market-stalls, like a coin from a drowned purse.

"Majesty?" Vess again. "Your instructions?"

Isolde rose. She walked to the window — the council chamber faced east, over the Hall of Justice and the great square where proclamations were read and sentences carried out, and beyond it the spire of the Cathedral of the Ascension, built on the bones of the old temple, its new stones deliberately laid at an angle no architect would choose, so that the skyline itself declared conquest. She looked at all of it for a long moment.

"You will draft a proclamation," she said. "To be read in every market square from here to the northern passes. Hear me fully before any of you speak." She turned. "Full and irrevocable amnesty for every registered gifted person in the realm. Their crimes forgiven. Their brands struck from the rolls. Freedom to practice their small arts openly, licensed by the local magistrate, taxed gently, protected by law."

Amery made a strangled sound. "Majesty, the guilds will riot. And half the registered will think it a trap—"

"It *is* a trap," Isolde said pleasantly.

The silence came back, deeper this time.

"One condition," she continued, walking back along the table, her boots ringing on the flagstones. "The carrier must be delivered to the crown. Alive. By whichever hand delivers him — a neighbor, a fellow traveler, a priestess, a brother — that person's whole community receives the amnesty. Not just themselves. Everyone they name." She reached the head of the table and rested both palms flat upon the map of the kingdom that was let into the wood there, her fingers spanning the northern roads. "Every gifted man and woman in this kingdom will know that their freedom costs one stranger. One. A single soul none of them owes anything to, standing between them and the fire forever."

Lady Corvane's voice was very soft. "You mean to make them hunt him for you."

"I mean to make them *choose*," Isolde said. "Armies burn caravans and create martyrs. Edicts create informers, and informers create nothing at all — just quiet streets and quiet roads and a fugitive with nowhere left to sleep. Renn can chase this man across every province in the north and catch nothing but cold. But if the persecuted themselves learn that salvation lies in his capture..." She straightened. "He becomes the loneliest creature in my kingdom. And loneliness, my lords, has brought down stronger things than gods."

"And if he is everything the old texts claim?" Corvane asked. "If he truly carries what your grandfather burned? Alive, Majesty, you said. What if delivering him alive proves... difficult? What if he burns a county on his way to the block?"

"Then we burn him," Isolde said, "and the amnesty stands regardless, purchased with his corpse, and the story becomes *the crown's mercy against the last monster.* Either ending serves." She met the old woman's eyes and did not blink. "That is why one writes the terms before the battle, Lady Corvane. So that every road leads somewhere useful."

Vess was already drawing fresh paper toward himself, quill moving, hungry for the work. Amery had recovered enough to begin listing the guilds that would need placating. The machine of the state was turning over, catching, running — Isolde could feel it in the room the way she felt weather in her hip, the familiar grind of governance absorbing catastrophe and producing paperwork.

Only Corvane lingered as the others gathered their things.

"When the amnesty is proclaimed," the old woman said quietly, at the door, "the registered will come forward in their thousands. And among them will be children born since the hunts began. Children who were hidden. You understand that the trap cuts toward us as well? Every amnesty is also a census. We are offering to lift the veil ourselves."

"I know what I am offering," Isolde said.

"Do you?" Corvane studied her with eyes gone pale and fine as tissue paper. "Because I served your father, child, and your father's father, and I will tell you what none of these men dare: the fear in this room is not of the carrier. It is of what happens if the people believe, even for a season, that the old blood might be holy again. Fear is a load-bearing wall in this palace. Mind you don't mistake it for your own strength."

She bowed — shallowly, as she alone was permitted — and was gone.

* * *

That night Isolde did not sleep. She dismissed Maerin early, sent the guards from her door on the pretext of a headache, and took the candle down the servants' stair behind the wardrobe wall — thirty-one steps, worn hollow in the middle by generations of feet that had carried more than linens — to the room that appeared on no plan of the palace.

It had been her grandfather's counting room once. Now it held a single desk, an iron strongbox, and the archive: eleven documents, each in its oilskin sleeve, each copied in secret from the sealed vault beneath the Hall of Records during the first year of her reign, when she had still believed that knowing the truth was the same as being armed by it.

She had read them all a hundred times. She read them again anyway, by candlelight, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth.

The treaty of the first war, with its clauses about *persons of vessel-blood*, written in the same legal hand used for grain tariffs. The inquisitor-general's ledger, columns of names, ages, and methods — the methods column mercifully abbreviated to single letters. And the letter, the one she always saved for last, from her grandfather to her grandmother, dated the winter after the war: *I have made the realm safe for our line by making it unsafe for every other kind of soul. Some nights I dream of the ones at Marrow Ford and wake glad. Tell me that means I am a king and not a butcher. Do not write back.*

Do not write back. As though even the paper might testify.

Isolde closed the sleeve and sat in the dark a while, the candle guttering, her reflection faint in the strongbox's iron lid — a woman with a tired mouth and her mother's cheekbones, wearing the crown's shadow whether or not the crown itself was on her head.

Here was the thing she had never said aloud in council, never admitted even to herself past the edge of sleep: she did not hate the gifted. She did not especially fear them. What she feared was arithmetic. Nine hundred years of Aldemar rule rested on the sentence *the gods chose us.* If the realm ever learned that the gods had chosen no one — or worse, that they had chosen everyone, cheaply, generously, the way rain fell on roofs — then the crown was not sacred. It was just the family that had killed best. And thrones built on that foundation did not fall in glory. They fell the way rotted beams fell: suddenly, in the night, on top of the sleeping.

The carrier was not a threat because of what he could do. He was a threat because of what he *was*: proof, breathing and walking and selling charms in a market like any common man, that the divine had never needed a crown at all.

So the bargain. Amnesty for his body. Mercy priced in betrayal. Let the persecuted sell him, and their hands would be too full of their own salvation to reach for old hymns. Let them refuse, and they would die for him, and dying men made excellent cautionary tales.

Either way, the lie held. Either way, the stag stayed crowned.

She snuffed the candle and climbed back up the thirty-one steps. In her bedchamber, dawn was greying the glass. Somewhere north, on a road she would never see, a man who did not know her name was running for his life with a god in his skin, and she had just made every soul around him a price on his head.

Isolde lay down and did not close her eyes.

*The trap is set*, she thought. *Now we learn what people are worth when they are afraid.*

The answer, when it came — carried south by fast rider, scrawled in a garrison captain's blunt hand — arrived sooner than any of her councilors expected, and it was not the answer she had drafted her proclamation for.
