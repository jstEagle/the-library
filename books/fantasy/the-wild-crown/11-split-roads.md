## 11. Split Roads

The fire had burned low before anyone said his name.

Cassia heard it first as a murmur at the edge of the camp — a woman's voice, pitched low, then a man's answering grunt — and she knew, with the certainty of a priestess who had spent forty years reading the silences between words, that the caravan had begun to speak of Jude Fairweather while he was not there to hear it. That was how these things always began. Not with knives. With murmurs.

She sat apart from the main fire, wrapped in a wool cloak gone grey with road dust, and listened the way she had once listened to congregations: not to what was said, but to what was being decided beneath it.

The caravan lay in a hollow of the north road where the pines grew thick enough to hide their cookfires from the sky. Twenty-three souls now — they had lost old Tammas to fever two nights past, and gained nothing in exchange but a shallow grave and a silence no one wanted to fill. The wagons stood in a rough circle like a hand closing into a fist. Beyond them the wheat country stretched away south, gold under the last of autumn's light, and beyond that, somewhere in the dark, the crown's riders were looking for them. For *him*.

Cassia turned her hands over in her lap. Once they had been hands that held a censer; now they were hands that mended harness and buried children. The gods had left her long before they left the world, or so she had believed until three days ago, when she had taken Jude Fairweather's wrist in the ruined temple and felt the thing inside him turn toward her touch like a sleeper turning toward a lamp.

The last spark of godhood. Carried unknowing by a charm-seller with tired eyes and a mercenary's careful hands.

And tonight, her flock was deciding whether to sell him.

* * *

They gathered an hour after full dark, all twenty-two of the living arranged around the central fire, and they did it badly, shuffling and avoiding one another's eyes, because none of them had ever done this before and every one of them knew exactly what they were doing. Cassia took her place among them rather than above them. She had earned no throne since the temples fell. She had only a voice, and she meant to spend it.

"Say it plainly," she said, when the silence had gone on too long. "Whatever it is. Say it plainly and let us be done with pretending."

It was Maren who spoke first — Maren, the tanner's widow, whose gift was small and useless and had gotten her husband killed anyway, because the witch-finders did not care how small a gift was, only that it existed. She was a broad, plain woman with burn scars up one arm, and she did not look at Cassia when she spoke. She looked at the fire.

"There's a crier in every town from here to the capital," she said. "Registered folk get amnesty. All they have to do is give up the carrier." Her jaw worked. "I have a daughter. She's registered. Her name is on a list in Vell, and every day that list isn't honored is another day some soldier can decide it doesn't matter."

"You think the queen will honor it," Cassia said. It was not quite a question.

"I think," Maren said, "it's the only paper in this whole cursed kingdom that says my daughter gets to live."

"And you believe a queen who burns churches will keep faith with a proclamation."

Maren finally looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and hard. "I believe I'm tired, Mother Cassia. I believe we've walked four hundred miles eating bark soup and burying people, and I believe there's a man sitting right over there—" she jerked her chin toward the edge of the firelight, where Jude's wagon stood dark and quiet, "—who could end all of it just by existing in the wrong place. He's already marked. Whatever they'll do to him, they'll do it whether we point the way or not. All our pointing buys is us."

The murmur that went around the fire was low, but it was agreement. Cassia felt it pass through the circle like a cold wind through wheat.

"Tammas is dead," said Hobb the wheelwright, quietly. "The fever took him because we've got no medicines and no safe roads to buy them on. My boy coughs all night. Every night." He spread his great calloused hands. "I don't want blood on me. I want my son breathing. If there's a difference between those things now, I can't see it anymore."

"No," Cassia said. "You see it fine. You just can't afford it."

The words came out sharper than she intended, and she saw them land — saw Hobb flinch, saw Maren's chin come up in defiance, saw young Pellin, the hedge-witch's grandson, turn his face away as if he'd been struck. Good, she thought. Let it hurt. Pain was the last honest thing left in this conversation.

"Mother Cassia." This was Aldous Grey, the ex-soldier who drove the lead wagon, and his voice was gentle in the way of men who had killed enough to stop enjoying anger. "No one's proposing we put a knife in him. We send word to the nearest garrison. A name, a direction. That's all. He runs, maybe he outruns them, maybe not. But the hunt ends for everyone else. The amnesty holds. We live."

"He won't run," Cassia said. "That's the tragedy of the man. He'll hear we sold him and he'll go willingly, because he'd rather bleed than make twenty-two frightened people choose between their consciences and their children. And then the queen will have what she wants, and the amnesty will die with him, because a promise made by Isolde Vael is worth exactly as long as it takes to become inconvenient." She rose, slowly, knees cracking, an old woman's body that had once knelt on marble floors. "I know this crown. I crowned its grandmothers. I watched them sign charters with one hand and hold the torch with the other. There is no bargain down that road. There is only the moment you stop being useful."

"Then what?" Maren demanded, standing too now, and the circle tightened like a fist. "What's your answer? Faith? Prayer? You want us to trust in the gods?" Her laugh cracked in the middle. "The gods are dead, Mother. Everyone knows it. Everyone's known it for three years. All that's left is us, and the cold, and the queen's riders, and one man carrying something even he doesn't understand. You want truth? Here's truth: I love you. You pulled me out of Vell with a rope burn around my wrists when no one else would stop the cart. And I would still trade him. Tonight. Without losing sleep." Her voice broke on the last word, which was the only part of it that was a lie. "So vote. Vote, and let those of us who have to live with it stop pretending we wouldn't."

The silence afterward was the worst kind — the kind that has already made its decision and is only waiting for the words.

Cassia looked around the fire. At Hobb staring at his boots. At Pellin crying without sound, the way children learn to cry when sound is dangerous. At Aldous Grey meeting her eyes steadily, sorrowfully, a good man preparing to do a thing he knew was wrong because the arithmetic of survival had stopped leaving room for anything else.

"The vote stands," Aldous said softly. "Those for sending word to the garrison?"

Hands rose. One, then three, then nine, then most of them, some raised high and defiant, some lifted halfway and held there like things drowning. Maren's arm was straight as a lance.

Cassia counted. It was her duty. She counted every hand, and she let each one cut her, because a shepherd who stops feeling the crook does not deserve the flock.

"Carried," Aldous said.

"No," Cassia said.

Every face turned to her.

"Not carried," she said. "Because the vote is moot. Look at the wagon."

They looked. Jude Fairweather stood beside his wagon at the edge of the firelight. How long he had been there, no one could say — long enough, certainly. His saddlebags hung from his shoulder. His sister's medicine bundle was strapped across his chest, the way he always wore it, close over the heart. He looked very tired, and very calm, and that calm was the most terrible thing about him.

He had heard everything. They all knew he had heard everything, and he knew they knew, and the shame of it went around the fire like a plague, touching every face.

* * *

Jude came forward slowly, and the crowd parted for him the way water parts around a stone — unwillingly, inevitably.

"Don't," Cassia said, before he could speak. "Don't forgive us. I couldn't bear it tonight."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face and died. "I wasn't going to forgive you," he said. "I was going to thank you. There's a difference." He looked around the circle, at the raised hands, at the lowered eyes. "I've been a soldier. I know what a war council smells like, even when it calls itself something kinder. You did what hungry, scared people do. I'd have voted with you, maybe. I've sold out better men than myself for less reason." His hand moved briefly to the medicine bundle over his heart. "I'm not angry. Angry is a luxury. But I'm leaving, and I'm leaving tonight, and here's why: not because you voted. Because you *could*."

"That's not fair," Maren whispered.

"No," Jude agreed. "It isn't. Nothing since the edict has been fair. But understand what you bought tonight. The queen is offering amnesty for the carrier. The moment I'm delivered, the offer dies — not because she breaks it, but because she won't need it anymore. You'll have traded the only lever any gifted person in this kingdom has for a piece of paper signed by a woman who burns churches." He shouldered his bags. "I'm not saying don't send your word. Send it. Maybe it saves your daughter, Maren. I hope to every silent god it does. But don't tell yourselves it was mercy. Call it what it is. Survival. There's no shame in survival. Only in the story we tell after."

He turned to Cassia last. Up close she could see the faint gold threading under the skin of his throat, the pulse of the spark she alone had touched, and it seemed to beat in time with her own heart, which was an unbearable intimacy and she would carry it to her grave.

"The temple," she said quickly, low. "North, past the split oak, the covenant road. There are others — the remnants of the old order, in the mountains. If the oaths hold anything—"

"They held nothing here," he said gently. "But thank you. For the rope burn, Mother. For pulling me out of Vell when no one else would stop the cart." He smiled, and it was Maren's phrase turned back on them, and it hurt worse than any curse would have.

Then he was walking, past the fire, past the wagons, past twenty-two people who could not meet his eyes, out into the dark of the pine wood, and the night closed behind him like a door.

* * *

Afterward, the camp did not celebrate. Cassia had expected — feared — relief, the loosening that comes after a hard thing is done. Instead the fire burned and no one fed it, and Maren sat with her scarred arm wrapped around herself, rocking slightly, and Hobb went to check on his coughing son and stayed a long time.

Pellin came to Cassia last, when the others had drifted to their bedrolls. The boy was sixteen and had his grandmother's gift of finding lost things, which had never found anything but trouble. He crouched beside her chair.

"He heard the vote," Pellin said. "And he still thanked us."

"Yes."

"How do we live with that?"

Cassia looked into the coals for a long time before she answered. "Badly," she said. "We live with it badly. That's the price. Anyone who tells you different is selling a charm." She reached out and gripped the boy's thin wrist. "But hear me, Pellin, and carry it: the vote was wrong. Not mistaken — *wrong*. And the fact that fear made it understandable does not make it right. Remember tonight. However it ends. Because the day will come when someone asks you to trade again, and you'll need to remember what it cost, and decide whether you're willing to pay it twice."

The boy nodded and slipped away.

Alone by the dying fire, Cassia drew out the little tin star she had worn beneath her clothes for thirty years — the eight-pointed covenant star, banned, melted-down, hidden against her breast through every search and every year of silence. She held it in her palm and felt, faintly, absurdly, a warmth in it that might have been memory or might have been the road's heat or might have been something more.

*Carry,* the spark had said through Jude's sleeping lips, three nights ago in the ruined temple. She had told no one. She would tell no one. But she understood now what the god had meant, and it had never meant Jude alone.

The vote had been cast, and the word would go to the garrison by morning — Aldous would see to it, and she would not stop him, because stopping him would only break the caravan and save nothing. But Cassia was an old woman, and old women knew there was more than one way to send word.

Before dawn, she woke Pellin, and gave him bread, and a message, and pointed him north past the split oak, onto the covenant road, toward the mountains where the last oaths of the old order still slept — if they slept at all.

"Run fast," she told him. "And pray the mountains remember what we've forgotten."

Behind them, far to the south, the wheat country waited gold and silent under the stars — and somewhere along its edges, riders were already moving toward the smell of smoke.
