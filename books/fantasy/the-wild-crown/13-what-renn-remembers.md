## 13. What Renn Remembers

The cells beneath the Vell garrison had been granaries once, in the years before the Edict, when the crown stored grain instead of people. Aldric Renn knew this because he had helped draw the conversion orders himself — ink and parchment, lines and seals, the clean bureaucracy of fear. He remembered standing at a drafting table with a cup of cold tea, ruling straight margins around the word *holding*, and thinking nothing of it at all.

He thought of it now, descending the stairs with a lantern in one hand and Cassia's testimony folded against his ribs like a splinter he could not dig out.

The two stragglers had been taken three nights ago on the north road — a boy of perhaps sixteen who limped when he thought no one watched, and a woman named Maren Ostler whose gift, according to the intake ledger, was the mending of small broken things. Spoons. Buckles. A bird's wing, once, though that was unverified. Renn had read her file twice. There was something obscene about how little it contained. A life reduced to a column of mended objects, and beneath it, in Sera Vane's neat administrative hand: *No threat noted.*

And yet here she was, three days in a stone room with a straw tick and a bucket, because the queen's edict did not distinguish between a mended spoon and a burning field.

Renn paused at the bottom of the stairs. Through the grate of the first cell he could see the boy asleep, curled toward the wall, hands tucked under his chin like a child pretending the world was smaller than it was. In the second cell, the woman sat upright, awake, watching the door as though she had been watching it for hours. Perhaps she had. The gifted learned quickly what was coming for them. They had always learned quickly. It was the one inheritance the old faith had left them: vigilance.

"Inquisitor." Maren Ostler's voice was dry but steady. "Come to finish the questioning, or to sit awhile?"

"Sit," Renn said, surprising himself.

He set the lantern on the floor between them, lowered himself onto the stool the guard had left, and unfolded the papers. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere above, boots crossed the yard; somewhere below the earth, water moved through old channels the city had forgotten it owned.

"You've read my testimony four times," she said. "You hold it differently each time. The third reading you held it upside down."

"I did not."

"You did." Almost gently. "Men don't notice their own hands when their minds are elsewhere."

He looked at her properly then. She was perhaps fifty, grey-streaked hair cut short for travel, a burn scar along one forearm that the healers' reports attributed to a kitchen accident eleven years ago. Her eyes were the brown of river water over stones, and they were not afraid of him. That was the strangest thing. Three days in a cell, an edict over her head, and she looked at him the way a schoolmistress looks at a boy who has come to her with the wrong answer delivered too confidently.

"Cassia," Renn said. "Your caravan mother. She told you what the carrier carries. Tell me again. All of it. Exactly as she said it."

"Why?"

Because the words had been circling him for three days like birds over a battlefield, and he needed to hear whether they sounded different spoken by someone else. Because in his dreams last night he had stood again on the mountain at Ashfall Keep, twelve years old inside his memory, and Jude Fairweather had said *what if we're not orphans, Aldric, what if we're heirs* — and Renn had laughed at him, and the laughter still sat in his chest like swallowed glass.

"Because," he said carefully, "an inquisitor must know what he burns."

Maren studied him for a long time. Then she nodded slowly, as if he had finally asked the correct question, and began.

"She said the gods did not die in the first war. Everyone says died, but Cassia was a priestess before the purges, and she says the word is wrong. She said they *emptied*. That when the crown came for the vessels — the men and women who carried the divine spark in those days — the gods saw what was coming. Not prophecy. Just arithmetic. The crown was killing the vessels faster than the gods could speak through them. So the gods did what any drowning man does." Her voice stayed level, almost tender. "They gave up their bodies. They poured themselves into whoever would receive them. Thousands of carriers, scattered, hidden, each holding a fragment so small it seemed like nothing. So small that three generations later, most carriers don't even know what their gift is. They think it's luck. Knack. Fever-charms that work once in a hundred tries."

Renn's hands were very still in his lap. "And the largest fragments?"

"Went quiet," she said. "Went into hiding inside the ones strong enough to hold them. Sleeping, Cassia calls it. And now—" she tilted her head at him, watchful, "—now one has begun to wake. You know this already, Inquisitor. You've seen the wheatfield."

*Fire in the Wheatfield.* Even the report's title had a poetry to it that made his skin crawl. He had written it himself, flat and clinical, and still the words burned off the page. Forty soldiers routed by one man. A harvest gone to ash. Children carried out through smoke that bent around them like it had been instructed to. And Jude Fairweather standing in the black stubble with light running off him like water off a roof in rain.

Renn had not seen it. He had read it. Eleven reports, cross-examinations, a survivor's sketch of the figure in the fire — and in every account, without exception, the same impossible detail: the flames had not touched the children. Not scorched, not singed. Fire does not discriminate. Fire is the crown's oldest argument, and it had refused, for one dawn hour in a northern wheatfield, to make it.

"And the crown," Renn said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "In Cassia's telling. What did the crown do in the first war?"

Maren Ostler smiled, and there was no comfort in it.

"You've requested the archives yet?"

* * *

Sera Vane brought the seal herself.

She came to his quarters past midnight, which was unlike her — Sera kept clerk's hours with monastic discipline — and she laid the writ on his desk without ceremony, though he noticed her hand lingered on it a half-second longer than efficiency required.

"The underchancellor resisted," she said. "The queen's private archive isn't subject to routine inquiry. I told him routine wasn't the word I'd have chosen either." A pause. "He asked if I understood what I was countersigning. I said yes."

"Do you?"

"No." She met his eyes, and Renn saw something in her face he had never seen there in six years of working beside her — not fear exactly, but its cousin, the look of a woman who has found a loose thread and cannot stop pulling it. "But you haven't slept since the stragglers came in, and you've reread the same page forty times, and yesterday you stood in the granary cells for three hours without asking a single question. Inquisitor Renn does not sit in silence. He asks questions until the walls confess." She straightened. "So whatever you heard in there, it's in the archive too, or it isn't anywhere. Go find out which."

The queen's private archive lay beneath the Citadel's west wing, behind three doors and a stair that descended farther than any building had a right to be deep. Two wardens escorted them down with torches, unlocked the final iron gate, and withdrew without a word, as though even they preferred not to know what breathed down there.

It was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Rows of shelving vanished into darkness on either side, stacked with ledgers bound in leather gone stiff and dark with age, scrolls in lacquered cases, maps whose vellum had yellowed to the color of old teeth. No dust. Someone swept these halls. Someone tended this place, generation after generation, keeping the records clean and unread.

Renn lifted the first ledger from the shelf marked with the dates he wanted — *The Unification Wars, Final Years* — and felt its weight settle into his arms like something living.

"What are we looking for?" Sera asked softly.

"The truth," he said. "I'm told it's kept down here."

* * *

The first war had another name in the official histories. Every schoolchild in the realm learned it: *the War of Cleansing*, the righteous struggle in which the first Queen Isolde's grandfather, King Aldous the Liberator, cast down the tyrant-priests and freed mankind from the caprice of cruel gods. Renn had memorized the catechism at eight years old, recited it at his commissioning, sworn his oath upon it. *The gods demanded blood and called it devotion. The crown demanded only loyalty.* Clean lines. A good story.

The ledgers told a different story in a clerk's cramped hand, and the clerk had not been writing propaganda. He had been writing inventory.

*Third month of the campaign. Vessels taken at Harrowmere: eleven. Of these, seven yielded sparks upon the pyre. Four yielded nothing. The god-power within them was not present at immolation. Recommendation: interrogate surviving faithful as to the rite of "pouring," believed to transfer divine essence to voluntary hosts. Note: the common folk shelter these hosts willingly. Whole villages lie to us. This is no longer a war against priests. It is a war against everyone.*

Renn turned the pages slowly. Torchlight trembled across the columns.

*Fifth month. The pouring confirmed. The enemy does not fight; they disperse. We burn a vessel and capture ash. His Radiance orders the widening of the registries. Every soul in the realm to be questioned regarding gifts of luck, healing, sight. The small carriers are the treasury now. Each one holds a coin of the old divinity, and coins may be gathered.*

*Seventh month. Casualty figures among the gathering companies: severe. The small sparks, pressed together, grow restless. Two carriers ignited spontaneously during transport, killing nine men. His Radiance remains resolved. He has commissioned the Iron Cistern at Vell for storage of the gathered essence. He intends, I am given to understand, to pour all the fragments back into one vessel of his own choosing. A god of his own making. An heir to power that cannot be contested.*

There was a diagram on the next page. Renn stared at it for a long time before understanding what he was looking at — a vessel, drawn in careful cross-section, human-shaped, with channels carved into the stone around it to direct the flow. And beneath the drawing, in the clerk's smallest script, a single line:

*The vessel chosen is a child. May whatever is left of heaven forgive us.*

Sera made a sound beside him, very small. He realized she had been reading over his shoulder, and that her hand was pressed flat against her mouth.

"Isolde," she whispered. "The first Isolde. The Liberat—"

"Read further," Renn said. His own voice sounded far away to him, as if someone else were speaking through his throat.

They read further.

The final ledger of the war recorded how it ended, and it did not end the way the catechism taught. King Aldous had not won. He had *lost* — lost his gathered hoard when the carriers, in one coordinated night that the chronicles called the Night of Small Fires, broke the Iron Cistern and scattered the sparks beyond all hope of reassembly. Lost them into thousands of ordinary people: farmers, midwives, soldiers, children. The gods had not been destroyed by the crown. They had been *distributed*. Hidden in plain blood, passed quietly down the generations, sleeping in luck-knots and fever-charms and mended spoons, waiting — for what, no record said. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps only for someone to ask.

And the crown's great crime, the original sin beneath three centuries of throne-right, was not that it had slain the gods. It was that the gods had never fought at all. The war had been a massacre of surrendering people, and the royal claim to divine sanction rested entirely on making certain no one ever learned the difference.

The last entry was in a different hand — firmer, imperial, the first Isolde herself. Renn recognized the seal pressed into the wax at the page's foot: the crowned sun, unchanged in three hundred years, the same sun that hung on every banner he had ever marched beneath.

*Let the record show: the powers are dispersed among the populace and cannot be recalled. Let it further show that any carrier of significant fragment, should one ever wake, would hold in his body the proof of our founding and the means of our unmaking. Therefore let the hunts continue in perpetuity, not for justice — justice was abandoned at Harrowmere — but for silence. We do not burn the gifted because they are dangerous. We burn them because they remember.*

*We burn them because they remember.*

Renn closed the ledger. The sound of it was very loud in the dark.

* * *

They climbed the long stair in silence. At the second door Sera stopped, torchlight carving her face into planes of shadow, and Renn saw that she was crying without any change in her expression at all — tears simply falling from steady eyes, as if grief had bypassed her face entirely and gone looking for an exit.

"I countersigned your writ," she said. "That puts my name in the record of this access. When the underchancellor reviews it — and he will —"

"Then don't report it."

"That's not how reporting works, Aldric." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, angrily. "I serve the machinery. I've served it for nineteen years. I keep the lists, I file the registers, I tell myself the lists protect people better than chaos would." Her breath caught. "I told myself the burning was regrettable but necessary. And it was a lie built on a lie built on a ledger written by a man who couldn't sleep either — I could see it in his handwriting, the way it deteriorates after Harrowmere, he *knew* —"

"Sera."

"They're going to burn Jude Fairweather," she said. "Publicly. The queen's already drafting the rite; I've seen the requisitions. Oak and pitch, a scaffold in Vell square, amnesty proclamations posted in every market to flush out anyone who might shelter him. And now I know what the oak and pitch are actually for. Not destruction." Her voice dropped to nearly nothing. "*Erasure*. He's the proof, Aldric. He's the last page they couldn't tear out. And we're the ones holding the torch."

Renn stood in the doorway a moment, saying nothing, because everything in him was saying it at once and none of it could get out whole.

He thought of Jude at fourteen, laughing on the training ground, dirt on his face. Jude at twenty, swearing the order's oath with his hand over Renn's shoulder — *shield-brothers, one breath, one grave*. Jude three years ago going silent as a struck bell, and Renn calling him faithless, calling him hollow, walking away rather than sit beside a friend's grief because grief looked too much like weakness. Twelve years of brotherhood, and he had let a silence end it, because believing Jude empty had been easier than believing the world was.

Jude had never been empty. He had been *entrusted*. Carrying, all this time, the very truth Renn had sworn his life to suppress — carrying it humbly, quietly, selling fever-charms in a market stall, hiding a god's heart under a peddler's coat while Renn climbed the ranks of the machine built to destroy it.

Duty said: report what you have found, request reinforcements, proceed to Vell, take the carrier alive, deliver him to the rite. Duty was a road he had walked his whole life, paved and lit and lined with honors.

Truth said: the road ends at a pyre, and you are the woodcutter.

"I need paper," Renn said at last.

Sera blinked. "For a report?"

"For a letter of resignation." He was already moving down the corridor, fast, his stride lengthening into something that had not been a walk in years — something closer to a run, closer to the man he'd been at twenty with a shield on his arm and a brother at his side. "No. Two letters. One for the underchancellor. One for the queen herself."

"Aldric — they'll hang you."

"They'll try." He reached the top of the stair where the torchlight of the living Citadel fell warm and gold across the floor, and for one strange instant it looked to him like firelight, like a wheatfield at dawn, like the glow he had spent his career hunting. He stepped into it. "But first, Sera — first, before they seal the gates — send a rider north. Tell them the inquisitor is coming to Vell."

"Coming to *do* what?"

He was silent a heartbeat. Then, quietly, with the calm of a man setting down a weight he had carried since childhood:

"To warn my brother."
