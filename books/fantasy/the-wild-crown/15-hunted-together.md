## 15. Hunted Together

The mountain hold had a name once — Karst Vey, the Order of the Gray Shield had called it, carved into the lintel above a gate wide enough for six men abreast. Now the name was half-eaten by lichen and the gate stood open to the wind, which came down off the peaks smelling of snow and old stone and nothing else.

Jude stood in the courtyard that had been his whole world for eleven years of his life, and it was smaller than memory had made it. That was the way of things. You carried places inside you like charms in a pouch, and when you finally took them out to look at them, they'd shrunk.

"You're standing on my grave," said Hesk.

Jude turned. The old man sat on the mounting block near the stable arch, wrapped in a cloak that might have been gray once but was now the color of wet ash. He looked older than Jude remembered, and Jude had remembered him old. His hands were knotted around a stick he wasn't using as a walking stick so much as holding hostage.

"Hesk."

"They told me you were dead." Hesk's eyes were milky at the edges but they found Jude's face with terrible precision. "Three years ago. When the god went quiet, they told me all of you died. I believed it because I wanted to. It was easier than believing you'd walked away."

"I didn't walk away," Jude said. "I fell down. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Hesk spat, off to the side, with the deliberation of a man who had been doing it in this courtyard for forty years. "Where are the others?"

"That's what I came to ask."

The silence that followed was the kind of silence Jude had learned to hate over three years of living inside it. Not peaceful. Expectant. Somewhere above them, in the broken bell tower, wind moved through the old bronze and made a sound like a voice trying to remember a word.

* * *

There were three survivors. He found them by lamplight that evening, in the refectory where they still kept the long tables out of habit, though there was no one left to feed.

Hesk, of course. And Brenna Ode, who had been a shield-sister to both Jude and Aldric, her arm now ending in a leather cap just below the elbow — she'd lost the hand at Redford Bridge, in the last battle anyone could agree to call a battle rather than a massacre. And young Tam, who hadn't been old enough to swear the full oaths when the silence came, and who had stayed anyway, tending the empty hall the way a son tends a grave of a father whose name he barely knows.

They fed him barley stew and hard bread, and asked him questions in the careful voices people use around a wound they can see but don't want to touch.

"The crown hunts us," Brenna said. She said it flatly, the way she said everything since Redford Bridge. "Not just gifted folk. Us specifically. Renn's writs name the Gray Shield by its old sigil. Someone remembers we existed, and someone wants us not to."

"Renn." Jude set down his spoon. Even saying the name felt like pressing on a bruise he'd been avoiding for years.

"He's thorough," Tam offered, then seemed embarrassed by the praise and stared into his bowl.

"He was always thorough," Jude said. "That was the point of him. I was the one who cut corners. He was the one who went back and checked every corner I'd cut." He heard his own voice go soft and hated it. "He'll come here eventually. This is the first place anyone would look."

"Then why did you come?" Brenna asked.

Because I have nowhere else, he didn't say. Because every road out of Vell led somewhere worse, and the mountains at least held the bones of the only family he'd ever sworn anything to. Because Cassia had looked at him across a ruined temple and told him he was carrying the end of the world under his ribs, and he needed, desperately, to stand in a place where the world had already ended once and see that people could still make soup afterward.

"Because I'm tired of running alone," he said instead.

Hesk snorted. But later, when the others had gone up to their bunks, the old man caught Jude's sleeve in the doorway and held it with surprising strength.

"There's something wrong with you," Hesk said quietly. "There has been since you walked through the gate. I've been near dying men and near divine men both, boy, and you smell of neither. You smell of *between*." His grip tightened. "What did the god leave in you?"

And because he was tired, and because Hesk had earned better than lies, Jude said: "Everything."

* * *

He told them most of it that night, by the banked fire in the refectory. Most of it. He spoke of the silence three years ago, of the night the god's presence — warm and vast and constant as a hearth-fire behind his sternum — simply went dark, leaving only an ember he couldn't find and couldn't feel. He told them about Vell, the charms, the sister, the hunt. He did not tell them what Cassia had named the spark, because saying *the last fragment of the godhood itself* aloud in a room with these three would change the air between them forever, and he was a coward about exactly one thing: being looked at like a relic.

Brenna listened with her single hand flat on the table. When he finished, she was silent a long moment.

"Aldric will come," she said. "Not because of the writs. Because of you."

"He doesn't know I'm here."

"He'll know. Aldric always knew where you were. Half our fights started because he knew where you were and disapproved."

Tam laughed before he could stop himself, and the laugh cracked something open in the room — grief, maybe, or fondness, the two being close relations in that hall. Jude found himself laughing too, briefly, helplessly, at the image of Aldric at nineteen, arms crossed, radiating disappointment at some shortcut Jude had taken through a hedge or a moral principle.

Then the laugh died, because the fire popped, and in the pop he heard again — faint, so faint — the word that had been following him since the ruined temple.

*Carry.*

"You should sleep," Hesk said, watching him. "You look like a man listening to something."

"I am," Jude admitted. "It hasn't finished the sentence yet."

* * *

They came at dawn.

Jude woke to the wrongness of it — the particular silence of a courtyard where sentries should be moving and weren't. Three years of hiding had built him an instrument out of instinct, and the instrument was screaming.

He got Brenna up, got Tam's bow into Tam's hands, and was reaching for Hesk's door when the first crossbow bolt buried itself in the frame beside his head.

"Down!" He shoved Hesk back into the room, threw himself sideways down the corridor as more bolts sang past, and hit the floor rolling toward the stair. Through the arrow-slit windows, gray light and gray figures below — soldiers in the queen's black and silver, moving through the outer yard with the unhurried competence of men who had done this many times and been paid well for it.

How? His mind clawed at it even as his body ran. The pass had one road, watched. No fires lit after dark, no—

The tinker. Two days back, on the road, the tinker with the repaired cart wheel who'd asked too kindly after their numbers and their destination. Jude had noticed, had filed it away, had said nothing — because noticing had become a habit and acting on it had become a luxury.

Betrayal, he thought, climbing through the loft window into the freezing gray, is never expensive. It costs a few coins and a clear conscience, and everyone has those to spare.

Below him, the courtyard erupted. He saw Brenna in the stable door, sword in her remaining hand, holding three soldiers at bay with the economy of twenty years' practice. He saw Tam on the wall, loosing arrows with shaking hands that steadied by the third shot. He saw Hesk standing in the middle of it all, absurdly, unkillably, shouting curses at the soldiers like a man shooing crows from a field.

And he saw, coming through the gate at a walk, unhurried, in a traveling cloak over the queen's colors, a tall figure Jude would have known by silhouette at any distance, in any crowd, at any hour of any year of his life.

Aldric Renn.

The spark moved.

It had been an ember, a whisper, a word unfinished — and now it surged against his ribs like a bird against a window, hot and huge and *awake*, and Jude gasped aloud at the sheer size of it. For one vertiginous moment he understood what Cassia had been afraid of. This was not a candle. This was the memory of a sun, folded small.

No. He clamped down on it, the way he'd taught himself, the way he'd survived three years. Not here. Not with Brenna and Tam and Hesk below, with soldiers everywhere, with the whole gray morning waiting to be turned to ash by one slip of his control.

He went over the roof tiles on his belly, toward the bell tower, because the tower had height and one stair and a door he could bar, and because a part of him — the oldest part, the part that had been a soldier before he'd been a seller of luck-knots — was already choosing ground.

Behind him, he heard Brenna's voice break on a shout. Heard steel ring on stone. Heard Hesk roar, *"Go, boy! GO!"*

He went.

* * *

The bell tower's top chamber held nothing but the great bronze bell, green with age, hanging motionless in the dawn cold. The door was oak, iron-banded; he dropped the bar across it and heard it seat home with a sound like a verdict.

Then he stood in the middle of the floor and tried to breathe.

Through the arrow slits, the fight below was already ending. It had never been a fight, really — it was a rounding-up. He saw Brenna forced to her knees, disarmed, her stump raised in furious defiance. Tam dragged down from the wall, his bow snapped under a soldier's boot with theatrical cruelty. Hesk — Hesk was on the ground, and Jude's heart stopped until the old man moved, swearing, swatting at the hands that hauled him upright.

Alive. All three alive. Rounding-up, not butchering. Renn's work. Renn took prisoners; Renn had always taken prisoners, even when taking prisoners was tactically stupid, because Aldric Renn believed with his whole iron soul that every death was a debt someone would eventually have to answer for.

Footsteps on the stair. Unhurried. Familiar. Jude knew the cadence of them — left foot slightly heavier, from an old injury at the Fords — and knowing it hurt more than any blow could have.

The bar jumped in its brackets. Once. Twice. Then a voice through the oak, level and cold and underneath it, so far underneath that anyone but Jude would have missed it entirely, wrecked:

"Open the door, Jude."

"No."

"I have a warrant for your execution signed in the queen's own hand. I have thirty men downstairs and your friends in irons. Open the door, or I take it down and take you, and the difference between those two outcomes matters to me in ways you cannot imagine, so please — *please* — do not make me find out how much."

Jude closed his eyes. "You always did talk too much when you were frightened."

Silence. Then, terribly quiet: "You think I'm frightened of you?"

"I think you're frightened of what you'll have to do. Same thing, with you."

Another silence, longer. When the voice came again, the coldness had cracked along one seam. "They told me you died. Three years ago. Do you know what I did with that? I built a life on top of it, the way you build on ruins. And now I climb these stairs and you're *alive*, and the order is three old ghosts, and the god we served is *silent*, and the queen wants you burned, and I am supposed to be the instrument that does it." A breath. "Open the door, Jude. I'm asking."

"And if I do?"

"Then you surrender, and I bring you south alive, and I spend whatever influence I have left making sure the pyre takes a very long time in being built." The words were measured, each one placed like a stone in a wall. "It is not mercy. It is the best I can purchase with what I am."

"It's not nothing," Jude said quietly. "But it's not enough."

"It's what exists!"

"No," Jude said. "It isn't."

And he lifted the bar.

Later he could never fully explain why. Perhaps because he'd spent three years behind doors, behind silences, behind a hidden pulse under his skin, and he was done with doors. Perhaps because it was Aldric, and some bonds don't break, they only rust. Perhaps because the spark was singing against his ribs and he was afraid that if he didn't face his oldest brother while he still controlled it, the next time they met, neither of them would survive the meeting.

The door swung open. Morning light poured up the stairwell.

Aldric Renn filled it — taller than Jude remembered, grayer at the temples, the queen's black and silver worn like a penance rather than a uniform. His sword was drawn but low. Behind him on the stairs, two soldiers started to advance, and he stopped them with one raised hand without ever taking his eyes off Jude.

"Everyone downstairs," he said. "If anyone climbs these stairs, I'll have you flogged." They went. Of course they went. He was Aldric Renn.

Then they were alone, in the round stone chamber with the dead bell, and the door was shut, and two men who had once shared a shield wall and a god stood four feet apart with a drawn blade between them.

"So," Jude said. "You found me."

"Tinker on the pass road." Aldric's jaw worked. "I know. I know what it cost you to trust him, and I know what it cost him to sell you, and I know which of those two prices was higher." He raised the sword. Slowly. Deliberately. Until the point rested a hand's breadth from Jude's chest — right over the sternum, right over the place where Cassia said the spark slept.

"Do you know what this is?" Aldric asked. "This is me doing my duty. Watch closely, because I have imagined this moment for three years — finding you, facing you, finishing what the silence started between us. Every night since Vell, I have rehearsed it. So watch, Jude. Watch me do it."

The point trembled.

It was a small thing. A hair's tremor, the kind any man's hand makes when tired. But Jude had stood in shield walls with this man; he knew every language his body spoke, and this was not fatigue.

"You can't," Jude said softly.

"I can." The point trembled again. "I swore—"

"To the crown. I know. I read your writs; they name us, Aldric. They name the order. Our brothers. Our sisters. You're carrying their death warrants in your coat, and you climbed these stairs anyway, alone, and dismissed your soldiers, and put your sword over my heart." Jude took one step closer, and the point touched cloth, touched skin, dimpling it — and he didn't stop. "Why did you send them away?"

"Because—" Aldric's breath went ragged. "Because I wanted to look at you first. Before. I wanted—" His voice failed, and what came out beneath it was younger, rawer, nineteen years old and furious in a hedgerow: "I wanted my brother to tell me it isn't true. Tell me you're not what they say. Tell me the spark is a story, tell me the silence was just the silence, tell me there's something left of the man I swore for."

The spark was awake against Jude's ribs, pressing outward, answering the proximity of the one person alive who had knelt beside him at the same altar. And Jude understood, suddenly, with the clarity that comes in the space between blows, that there was only one thing left to give.

"Look," Jude said. "Not at me. *With* me. Like the old days — the mind-link, the shield-pair bond. One breath. That's all I ask. One breath of what I carry, and then you can strike, and I won't stop you."

"That's a trick."

"Yes," Jude agreed. "It probably is. Are you brave enough to find out?"

For a long moment, the only sound was the wind in the dead bell above them, humming its almost-voice.

Then Aldric Renn — chief inquisitor of the Crown Hunt, breaker of covens, burner of writs — sheathed his sword, and stepped forward, and pressed his brow against his brother's brow the way they had done a hundred mornings before a hundred battles, and opened the old door in his mind that he had sealed shut three years ago with grief and mortar.

Jude gave him the memory.

Not the power — never the power, that would burn a mortal mind like straw — but the *memory*. The last night. The god's presence vast and warm behind his sternum, and around him the whole sleeping order, and the sense — sudden, tidal, terrible — of the god gathering itself, folding itself down, pouring itself *into* him like wine into a single cup, and the last words spoken not in sound but in meaning, direct into the marrow of his soul:

*Carry. Not for me. For them. Carry until they can carry it themselves.*

And beneath it, fading with the god's final heartbeat, the reason — a glimpse of the first war, of crowns and slaughter, of gods dying not of age but of *murder*, and the desperate, loving, catastrophic choice to hide divinity inside the least of their servants so that no king could ever find it and kill it again.

The memory lasted one breath.

When Aldric pulled back, his eyes were wet, and his face had gone the color of the ash outside, and he looked at Jude as if seeing him for the first time — or perhaps for the first time in three years, truly, since the night they'd screamed at each other in the rain and called each other traitor and coward and worse.

"You're not a carrier," Aldric whispered. "You're a grave."

"I'm a *promise*," Jude said. "There's a difference. I'm still learning what it is."

Aldric staggered back a step. Put a hand against the wall. The great man, the iron man, undone by a single breath of truth. "The queen," he said hoarsely. "She believes destroying the spark ends the threat. She believes its destruction secures her line. If she burns it out of you—"

"Then she kills the last god and proves her grandfather's crime was justified, forever." Jude's voice was steady. Inside, the spark hummed against his ribs, warm and terrible and patient. "Unless someone stops her. Unless someone shows her the archives, the truth, the—"

He stopped.

Aldric had gone very still. And Jude, who knew this man's silences as intimately as his speech, felt the bottom drop out of the morning.

"Aldric. What do you know about the archives?"

The inquisitor's eyes came up, and in them Jude saw something that had no business being there: guilt. Old, buried, recently exhumed guilt.

"Before I left the capital," Aldric said slowly, "I requested access to certain sealed records. Records concerning the first war. Concerning... what the crown found, centuries ago, when it began to understand where the gods had gone." He passed a hand over his face. "Someone helped me. Someone inside the chancery. And someone else — someone who watches everything — may have seen the request."

The wind moaned through the bronze above them.

"If she knows," Jude said, "then she knows the truth is loose. And if she knows the truth is loose—"

"Then the bargain changes," Aldric finished grimly. "Amnesty becomes bait. The hunt becomes a net. And every gifted soul in Vell becomes leverage against the one man who carries what she fears." He straightened, and something shifted in his bearing — some weight re-settling onto different shoulders. "My soldiers are downstairs. Your friends are in irons. In ten minutes I either march you south in chains, or I commit treason on this mountain and burn my life to the ground behind me."

Jude held his brother's gaze. Outside, the dawn was breaking gold over the peaks, indifferent and enormous.

"How long do you need?" Jude asked.

Aldric Renn smiled — and it was the old smile, the shield-wall smile, fierce and doomed and glad. "None," he said, and drew his sword — and drove it point-first into the queen's warrant, pinned to the table, nailing it there like a declaration of war.
