## 17. City of Chains

Jude had thought he knew Vell. He had thought he knew its moods the way a husband knows a wife's breathing in the dark — the market's morning roar, the river fog that came off the Vellwater at dusk and turned every lantern into a halo, the particular quality of silence that fell over Tanner's Row when the watch made its rounds. Three years he had sold his small charms beneath the eaves of the Saltmarket, and he had believed there was no corner of the city that could surprise him.

The city that rose before him now, seen from the ridge above the north road at gray dawn, was a stranger wearing his wife's face.

Walls of palisade timber had gone up where the road met the outskirts — raw-cut pine still bleeding sap, lashed together in three days by soldiers who did not care how it looked, only what it kept out. Watchtowers stood at intervals along it, and from each tower hung something that caught the early light and swung slowly, patiently, like weeds in a current.

He did not need to be closer to know what they were.

"They're not even pretending anymore," Aldric said beside him. His voice was flat, the voice he used when feeling would only slow him down. He had shaved off the inquisitor's beard on the road; without it, and without the gray warrant-cloak, he looked almost like the man Jude remembered from the mountains — younger, harder around the eyes, but recognizably the shield-brother who had once slept back-to-back with him through two winters of border war. "Under the old edicts, at least, there was a fiction. Registration as mercy. The hunt as justice. Now—" He nodded at the towers, at the turning things. "Now it's just fear with a seal on it."

Sera crouched lower in the bracken, her satchel of stolen archive-pages pressed against her chest like a child. She had barely slept since the breach was reported; every night on the road she woke convinced she heard hoofbeats, and twice she had been right, though the riders had passed them by in the dark. "Martial law," she said. "They declared it eight days ago. We heard the criers in Harrow's Ford." She swallowed. "There will be lists at the gates. Every face checked against the rolls."

"Then we don't go through the gates," Jude said.

* * *

They went under them instead — or rather through the throat of the city's oldest secret, the culvert where the Millrace Ditch passed beneath the wall, built two hundred years ago when Vell was a free town and its burghers had cared more about moving water than moving armies. Cassia had known of it. Cassia knew a hundred such passages, and her network — scattered now, hunted, half of it burned or fled — had left its marks for those who knew how to read them. A chalk spiral inside the culvert mouth, fresh enough to smear. A knot of red thread tied to an iron stave, the old covenant signal: *a friend holds this door.*

The water came to their thighs and the dark came to their lungs. Jude moved through it with his hand on Aldric's shoulder and thought, absurdly, of the fever-charms he used to sell, the little braided cords he swore would draw sickness out through the skin. Lies, mostly. Kind ones. He wondered if any of his customers were still alive to remember being cheated gently.

Then the culvert bent, and light leaked down through a grate overhead, and he smelled woodsmoke and wet wool and frying onions and something else beneath all of it — ash, faint but everywhere, the way it had soaked into the whole city, and he understood that he was home.

It felt nothing like home. It felt like walking into a house after burglars have finished with it, where every object sits in its proper place and every object is wrong.

* * *

Vell under martial law was a city learning a new language.

Jude saw it within the first hour, from the shadowed doorway of a cooper's shop while a patrol clanked past: the way people walked. Not the loose market-day stroll he remembered but a narrow, careful gait, eyes fixed ahead, shoulders drawn in, everyone making themselves smaller — a whole population practicing apology. Shopkeepers had painted numbers on their shutters. He did not understand the numbers until he saw a soldier stop before a baker's stall, consult a slate, and call out *"Four!"*, and the baker came out with four loaves already wrapped, no word exchanged, as though the bread had baked itself out of terror.

And everywhere the chalk marks. On doorposts, on wells, on the church of the Ascendant Sun whose bells had been recast into signal-gongs. Most were official — the crown's registration sigil, a closed eye, stamped beside doors where gifted folk lived, so that neighbors might watch and soldiers might count. But some were not official. Small marks, quick ones, hidden at knee height or under lintels: a spiral, a knotted line, a fish. Cassia's network, still breathing, still passing messages in plain sight.

"There," Sera breathed, nodding toward a tinker's cart by the fountain square. An old woman sat behind it mending pots, and on the cart's near wheel, where the fender curved, someone had chalked a spiral with a single line struck through it. *Danger here. Route closed.* And below it, almost erased, another sign Jude recognized from the caravan days: the mark meaning *the mother is safe.*

"She's here," Sera whispered. "Cassia came back into the city?"

"Cassia would come back for one straggler left behind," Jude said. His chest hurt suddenly, thinking of Mother Cassia slipping back under the walls for the sake of a lame pot-mender or a frightened boy, when every sensible soul had run north. "We find her. She'll know where Maren is."

His sister's name sat in his mouth like a stone. Three months since he had climbed over the rooftops of his own tenement with soldiers breaking down his door below, leaving Maren's medicine on the table because there had been no time, no time, no time. He had told himself she would manage, that the neighbor woman Odile would see to her, that guilt was a luxury for men who survived. The telling had never quite taken.

* * *

Aldric saw the city differently, and hated himself for how well he understood it.

He had helped build machines like this one. Not in Vell — in Corveil, in Dunmark, in six towns whose names blurred together — but the anatomy was always the same, and he read it now the way a surgeon reads a body on the table. The palisades: containment. The numbered rations: dependency, so hunger could be dialed up or down street by street. The closed-eye sigils on doorposts: not punishment yet, but inventory — the crown marking its livestock before the culling. And the informers. They were the easiest to spot, once you knew. The man who leaned too long against the same post. The girl selling water who watched faces instead of coins. Fear made people fluent in betrayal; you could hear it in how quickly their eyes moved.

"You're doing it again," Sera murmured. They stood in the lee of the tannery, waiting for a patrol to pass, close enough that her shoulder touched his arm.

"Doing what?"

"Reading the city like a warrant." Her voice held no accusation, only weariness. "You can't help it. I keep catching myself doing it too. Counting patrols. Estimating response times. As if the archive never happened, as if I'm still theirs."

"Old habits are just oaths with the words worn off," Aldric said.

She looked at him then, really looked, and something passed between them that had been building since the night he burned his warrant — recognition, two servants of a rotten house standing in its ruins together. "Do you think we can ever be anything else?" she asked. "You were theirs fifteen years. I was theirs nine. When does the ink wash out?"

"I don't know," he said honestly. "Ask me after we've saved someone. If saving anyone is still possible."

It was the wrong moment for it, perhaps, but he meant it, and she seemed to take it as enough.

The patrol passed. They moved.

* * *

Odile's door had the closed eye on it.

Jude stood across the lane from his own former tenement for a long time, hidden in the recessed entryway of a shuttered dyehouse, and stared at that small white circle with its slash of black lid. So they had registered her. Or they had registered the household — his household, the fugitive charm-seller's sister, marked whether she was gifted or not, guilty or not, simply by association with his name. The chalk would not wash out; he could see the ghost of older marks beneath it, scrubbed and re-stamped, scrubbed and re-stamped. Someone had tried. Someone had fought it, quietly, with a rag and water, and lost.

The window of Maren's room — the ground-floor room they'd given her because her legs could no longer manage stairs — was dark. Midmorning, and dark. Curtains drawn.

Every instinct he owned screamed at him to cross the lane, knock, say *it's me, it's Jude, I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry.* Instead he made himself stand in the dye-house shadow and count. Patrols: one every quarter hour on the main street, two men and a corporal. The tenement's other windows: the widow Hessa's curtains open, the cooper's boy visible at his sill — watching. The boy was watching the street, and the boy's eyes had the quick flicker Aldric had taught him to recognize, and Jude felt the knowledge settle into him coldly, like river water into boot leather:

Anyone might sell them. Everyone might. That was the design. That was the machine working exactly as built.

"We go tonight," Aldric said quietly at his shoulder. "Not now. Now we find Cassia's people and learn the shape of the trap before we walk into the middle of it."

"I abandoned her once already," Jude said. The words came out with edges on them.

"You fled," Aldric said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that you're standing here now, in chains-city, to finish it." He paused. "I know about leaving people. Believe me. I know what it costs to be the one who walks away — and I know the only coin that buys any of it back is coming back."

* * *

They found the network by letting it find them.

That was Cassia's method, Sera realized, as the afternoon wore on and they followed chalk-mark to chalk-mark through the tangle of Weavers' Lane — each sign pointing to the next, each next one slightly fresher, a trail laid for the desperate and the pursued that required patience and faith in equal measure. The network trusted no one who arrived quickly. You proved yourself by enduring the long way round, by sitting hungry in doorways while patrols passed, by choosing, over and over, to keep following a fish scratched into brickwork rather than bolt for the gates.

The trail ended at a candlemaker's shop behind the Saltmarket, its front shuttered and dusty, its back room reachable through a coffin-maker's yard — because, as the boy who led them there said with a gap-toothed grin, *who searches a place that smells of death?* Inside, the air was warm with tallow-smoke and crowded with people who should not have existed: a lamplighter with burn-scarred hands and light that pooled in his palms when he forgot himself; twin girls maybe ten years old whose shadows moved a half-second out of step with their bodies; a one-eyed ferryman; a scholar of the old rites with ink-stained fingers; eleven souls altogether, plus children sleeping on pallets behind a curtain.

Mother Cassia sat among them like a stone in a streambed, and when her eyes found Jude across the room, she did not smile. She rose, crossed to him, took his face in her two broad hands, and studied him the way she had once studied the spark — with awe and terror sharing the same expression.

"You came back into the fire," she said. "Foolish man. Blessed man. Both at once."

"The caravan—"

"is scattered but alive. Nine reached the northern sanctuaries. I came back for the rest." Her thumbs pressed hard against his cheekbones, as if checking that he was real. "And your sister lives, Jude. Odile keeps her. But hear me." Her grip tightened. "The queen's men came asking after Maren Fairweather a week ago. Not to arrest her. To bait you. They've left her alive and unmarked and they've let the whole street know they're watching her door. You are expected, carrier. This whole city is an altar set for you, and every kindness in it is a candle."

Behind him, Jude heard Aldric exhale slowly, and Sera say nothing at all, and somewhere in the room a child turned in her sleep.

"And if I go anyway?" Jude asked.

Cassia released his face. For a moment the old priestess looked out of her eyes — the woman who had buried a god's church and kept singing anyway.

"Then we do what the faithful have always done," she said. "We sing louder than the fire."

Outside, far off toward the citadel, a signal-gong began to toll — three strokes, a pause, three strokes. The boy with the gap teeth paled. The lamplighter's cupped hands went dark.

"That's the muster-call," Aldric said quietly, and Jude heard the old inquisitor's knowledge come back into his voice like a blade sliding home. "They've found something. Or someone's sold something." He looked at Jude, and in his eyes was the last conversation they needed to have and no time to have it.

"Someone sold us," Sera whispered.

The gong tolled on, and under it, rising through the floorboards from the streets of Vell, came the sound of boots — many boots, in step, closing from every direction at once.
