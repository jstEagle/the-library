## 18. The Queen's Altar

The rite chamber beneath the palace had no windows, which Isolde had always taken for a kindness. A queen should not have to see the sky when she did what needed doing.

She descended alone, as she did every year on this night, her slippers silent on stairs worn smooth by four generations of royal feet. Torches flickered in iron brackets as she passed, and the shadows moved the way they always moved down here — reluctantly, as though even darkness was uncertain whether it belonged to her.

At the bottom lay the altar.

It was older than the palace above it, older than the dynasty that ruled from it, hewn from a single block of black granite that no mason in living memory could name or claim. Its surface bore channels cut long ago for purposes Isolde had never been taught. She had been taught everything else. The genealogies back eleven generations. The borders won and lost and won again. The names of every carrier burned upon this stone since her great-great-grandmother first declared the gods dead and the crown eternal.

But not what the channels were for.

*Because,* her father had said, laying his heavy hand on her fourteen-year-old head, *a ruler who asks questions about the altar will one day hesitate at it.*

Isolde Veyra, Queen of Vell and the Nine Provinces, Sovereign Protector of the Faith Reformed, had never hesitated at anything in thirty-one years of rule. She had signed death warrants with a steady hand. She had watched her own uncle kneel on this very floor and burn, because he had sheltered gifted folk in his northern estates and blood was blood, even royal blood, especially royal blood. She had buried a husband and crowned herself regent in the same week and wept exactly once, privately, into a pillow that was changed by morning.

She was not a cruel woman. That was the lie she told herself most often, and she told it well, because cruelty required appetite and she had none. What she had was arithmetic. The spark that lived somewhere inside Jude Fairweather — if the inquisitor's reports were true, if the carrier truly existed — was not a person's problem. It was an equation. A living fragment of godhood, unclaimed, ungoverned, capable of waking powers that had once cracked continents. Destroyed, the dynasty stood forever. Preserved, it stood at the pleasure of whatever the spark chose to become.

Isolde had never cared for pleasures that were not her own.

She lit the candles herself, seven of them, white tallow set in sockets of bone. The old texts — the few that survived her grandmother's bonfires — spoke of nine flames, but nine had felt excessive, theatrical, and Isolde distrusted theater in a rite the way she distrusted it in government: as evidence of weakness dressed in costume. Seven would do. Seven had done for three years now, since the reports of the silent gods first reached her and she began preparing for the day a carrier surfaced.

Tonight the reports said he was in the mountains. Tonight the reports said her chief inquisitor had gone quiet, two letters unanswered, his last dispatch more question than confirmation. Tonight the reports said the amnesty had worked exactly as designed — registered folk denouncing the unregistered, the hunted hunting each other, the whole apparatus of persecution running now on its own fuel without her having to push it.

And tonight, for the first time in years, the arithmetic refused to sit still in her head.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold altar and let herself feel it: nothing. No hum, no warmth, no residue of all the burning done here. The stone kept its secrets the way good servants kept secrets — perfectly, and without love.

"Tell me," she said to it, quietly, because there was no one left alive she could ask. "What were you for?"

The stone answered nothing, and Isolde straightened, smoothed her robes, and climbed back up toward the light, where her heir was waiting, and where the honest answer to that question had been murdered so many generations ago that even its ghost had stopped haunting anyone but queens.

* * *

Prince Corin was ten years old, and he had his father's habit of sitting cross-legged on the floor instead of the chairs provided, which drove the tutors mad and made Isolde's chest ache in ways she had learned to file under *later*.

He looked up when she entered the solar, a book open across his knees. He had her dark hair and her father's brow, and when he frowned in concentration — as now — the resemblance to her dead husband was so precise it was almost indecent.

"You're late," he said. "Master Ellery says queens are never late."

"Master Ellery has never been queen." She sat in the chair across from him, arranging her skirts with the care she gave to all public surfaces. "What are you reading?"

"The Founding." He held it up: the illustrated primer every royal child memorized before their tenth year, the one with gilded edges and woodcuts of Saint Aldous driving the false gods out of the world. "The part where the gods went mad and the first queen had to stop them."

"Ah." Isolde kept her voice pleasant. "That part."

"Aldric says—" He caught himself, corrected the title automatically, the way children absorbed doctrine before they understood it. "Saint Aldous didn't really drive them out, though, did she? Master Pell says the Primer is simplified. For children." His chin lifted slightly, the small defiance he was only just learning to spend. "I'm nearly ten. So what actually happened?"

There it was. The question. Every heir asked it eventually; Isolde had asked it herself, at this same age, in this same room, and her mother had given her the same answer Isolde was now expected to give — the polished one, the load-bearing lie, the story that held up the throne the way the vaults held up the floors.

*The gods grew proud and dangerous. The crown ended them to save the world. Every burning since has been housekeeping.*

She opened her mouth to say it.

Corin waited, book in his lap, and behind him the evening light came through the tall windows gold and forgiving, and Isolde looked at her son — at his father's frown, at his uncalloused hands, at the absolute trust with which he waited to be told what was true — and found that she could not.

Not tonight. Not after the altar. Not with the smell of tallow smoke still in her hair.

"What actually happened," she said slowly, "is complicated."

"Mother." Exasperated, wounded, ten years old. "You always say that."

"Because it is always true." She rose and crossed to the window, buying herself the mercy of not being watched. Below, the capital spread out in lamplight — her city, her arithmetic, forty thousand roofs over forty thousand people who slept soundly because she did not. "When your ancestor Aldous took the throne, the world was different. There were... people who carried things. Power. Old power, from before crowns. And the crown decided that such power could not be permitted to exist outside the crown's control. So it was gathered. All of it. Where it could not be gathered, it was destroyed."

"In the Primer they're monsters," Corin said. "The carriers. They have black eyes in the pictures."

"People draw their enemies however it helps them sleep."

The silence behind her had a different texture now. She turned. Her son was looking at her with an expression she recognized with horror as her own — the narrow, weighing look she gave ministers when their numbers failed to add up.

"Did we do something bad?" he asked. "A long time ago?"

*Yes.* The word sat on her tongue like a stone. *We did the worst thing. We took the world away from everyone who shared it and called the taking salvation, and then we built altars to keep the secret, and I am the fourth queen to feed people to that altar rather than let them answer your question.*

"We did something necessary," she said. "Those are not always the same as good. When you are queen, you will understand the difference better than you want to."

"That's not an answer either."

"No," Isolde agreed. "It isn't." She knelt — knelt, in her formal robes, on the floor beside him, because some concessions cost less than others — and closed the primer gently over his hands. "Listen to me. One day you will read other books. Older ones. Some of them are in the archive beneath the east wing, and some of those are lies too, but they are older lies, and older lies are closer to the truth the way a river near its source is cleaner. When you find them, you will learn things about our family that will make you ashamed. When that happens, I want you to remember something."

"What?"

"That shame you can carry is worth more than peace you have to guard with fire." She touched his cheek. "Your great-grandmother never learned that. Your grandfather never learned it. I am—" the word came out smaller than she intended— "still learning it."

Corin studied her for a long moment with those weighing eyes. Then, with the terrible simplicity of children, he said:

"Is that why you're going to burn the charm-seller?"

The solar went very quiet. Somewhere below, a door closed; the palace breathed around them.

"Who told you about the carrier?"

"Everyone's talking about it. The guards think I don't listen." He shrugged, apologetic and unrepentant. "They say he can wake the old gods. They say that's why the edicts happened, why Aunt Maren's seamstress was taken last month. Because of him." His voice dropped. "If you burn him, does it stop? All of it?"

*Yes,* the arithmetic said. *Destroy the variable and the equation resolves. It has always resolved. It will resolve again.*

But Corin was watching her, and Isolde heard herself say, "I don't know."

Her son blinked. In ten years, she had never said those words to him. She was not certain she had ever said them to anyone.

"If the stories are true," she went on, and the strange thing was how easily the truth came once the first crack opened, how it poured like water finding a broken seal, "then destroying the spark ends the danger. But it also ends the possibility. Whatever the old power might have become — whatever good might have grown from it — dies with it. Forever. And forever is a very long time to be wrong."

"But the Primer says—"

"I know what the Primer says. I wrote the preface to your edition." She laughed, short and bitter, surprising them both. "Come here."

She held him, her heir, her arithmetic's final sum, and felt his heartbeat against her ribs, quick and ordinary and utterly uninterested in dynasties. Over his head, through the window, the first star was out above the eastern mountains — where, if the reports were right, a man who had once sold luck-knots in the Vell market was running from everything she had built.

Three years ago the gods had gone silent, and Isolde had celebrated, because silence meant security. Now she wondered — with the cold, slow dread of a woman checking an account that has been balanced too neatly for too long — whether silence had ever been security at all, or only the held breath before an answer.

* * *

The messenger came at midnight.

Isolde was awake — she was always awake now, sleep having become another negotiation she kept losing — and she took the dispatch standing at the map table, breaking the wax with her thumbnail.

From the garrison at Coldmere. Sealed with Renn's cipher, which was itself remarkable, since Renn had been silent for six days.

*The carrier travels north with remnants of the Fairweather company. Two stragglers captured. Testimony obtained. Requesting access to the sealed archive, first tier, under my personal warrant. Matters concerning the founding. I will explain nothing by letter.*

Isolde read it twice.

The first reading, she noted the operational content: the carrier located, the net drawing closed, the endgame finally in reach. The second reading, she noticed the thing that made the candleflames seem suddenly thin.

*Matters concerning the founding.*

No inquisitor requested the sealed archive. In thirty-one years, no inquisitor had so much as glanced at the locked doors beneath the east wing, because every inquisitor understood — was selected for understanding — that some stones were better left on some chests. Renn knew this. Renn had been her most reliable instrument for nine years precisely because he never asked what the channels in the altar were for.

And now he wanted the first tier.

She thought of her son's face in the gold evening light. *Did we do something bad?* She thought of the black granite keeping its secrets without love. She thought of the seamstress, Aunt Maren's seamstress, whose name Isolde had never learned and whose face she nevertheless saw sometimes, between sleeping and waking, mouth open around a scream the arithmetic did not record.

"Matters concerning the founding," she repeated aloud to the empty room.

Then she did the thing her father had warned her never to do. She hesitated.

It lasted perhaps four seconds — four seconds in which the Queen of Vell stood over a map of her own kingdom and felt, clearly and completely, the shape of the trap she had inherited. If she granted the request, Renn would find the founding records, and Renn would learn what she had spent her life not knowing, and the machinery of the hunt — her machinery, built on certainty — would begin to eat itself. If she refused, she confirmed to her sharpest instrument that the archive mattered, which was its own confession. Either way, the secret was already bleeding.

There was a third option. There was always a third option; her grandmother had taught her that much and called it wisdom. Burn the archive. Burn the request. Burn the inquisitor who asked.

Her hand hovered over the dispatch.

Outside, far to the northeast, thunder rolled over mountains that had no storm coming — a low, long sound, almost like a voice clearing itself after years of disuse — and Isolde Veyra, who had never hesitated at anything, stood in her empty war room with her son's question caught in her throat like a fishhook, and wrote her answer.

She granted the archive access. Full tier. Renn's warrant countersigned in her own hand.

Let him read, she told herself. Let him learn what I never let myself learn. Perhaps a believer who breaks is more useful than a doubter who pretends.

And beneath that reasoning, in the cellar of herself where queens keep the truths that cannot bear daylight, another motive stirred, quiet and unforgivable:

She wanted someone else to read it first. She wanted, just once, not to be alone with what the altar was for.

She sealed the letter, rang for the courier, and stood at the window watching his horse diminish down the midnight road — carrying her permission north toward the mountains, toward the carrier, toward the answer to her son's innocent question.

Behind her, in the rite chamber far below, seven candles guttered out one by one, though no draft had entered the room.

By morning, the thunder had reached the foothills. By evening, the reports would come: the inquisitor's banners seen moving fast along the north road, and traveling with them — though the scout could scarcely believe his own eyes — the unmistakable gray livery of the crown, worn openly by a man who had, witnesses swore, fed his warrant to a cookfire and ridden on without looking back.

The queen read that report at dusk, and understood at last that the arithmetic had failed, and that what was coming down from the mountains was not a fugitive at all.

It was an answer. And answers, once spoken, could not be burned.
