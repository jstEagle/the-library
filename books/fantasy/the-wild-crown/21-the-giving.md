## 21. The Giving

The square had been built for burning.

Jude knew that now. He could feel it in the stones beneath his knees — the way old fire remembered itself in granite, the way the mortar between them had been mixed with ash from the first pyres, three centuries of smoke soaked into the bones of Vell's great market square. The same square where he had sold luck-knots for six coppers and fever-charms for nine. The same flagstones where Mara Tull had once haggled him down to seven because her boy was sick and she was proud and would not beg.

They had chained him to a post at the center of it all, because even now — even after everything — the crown wanted theater.

The pyre around his feet was a careful architecture: birch logs stacked in a lattice, pitch-soaked faggots tucked into the gaps like eggs in a nest, a ring of oil-darkened sand to keep the fire honest, to keep it *contained*. Someone had studied this. Someone had spent their life learning how to burn a person well, and Jude found he could not stop cataloguing the craftsmanship, because if he stopped thinking about the workmanship he would have to think about the fire itself, and there were still things left undone.

*There are always things left undone.*

The crowd filled the square to its edges and spilled up the steps of the guildhall and hung from the balconies of the dye-houses along the east row. He had never seen Vell so full. It had never been full *for* anything good.

He looked for faces.

That was the mistake. He should have kept his eyes on the middle distance, on the royal dais where Queen Isolde sat beneath a canopy of white and gold with her heir beside her — the boy, Prince Edmun, ten years old and grave as a little magistrate, watching his mother's justice with the careful blankness children learn when they watch something they do not understand. Jude should have watched *her*, the woman who had built an altar out of other people's ashes and called it a dynasty.

But he looked at the crowd instead, because three years of silence had taught him that gods hide in crowds, and because somewhere in that sea of strangers were people he had charmed and cheated and saved.

Mara Tull stood near the fountain with her son on her hip — her son, whole and brown-faced and loud, shrieking at the pigeons as though the world had not ended. She was wearing one of his fever-charms on a cord at her throat. He recognized the knot. Sloppy work, that one; the third loop crossed wrong. It had never done anything at all except be bought by a frightened mother on a cold morning, and she wore it anyway.

Near her, a cluster of men and women in undyed wool — the branded ones. The registered gifted. The amnesty had come down from the capital like a bargain-basement mercy: *kneel, take the iron, keep your lives.* Each of them wore the mark on their cheek or their wrist or their brow, a small seared sigil the shape of a closed eye. Cassia had called it what it was. *"A brand is for cattle or for covenant,"* she'd said, spitting into the road dust. *"The queen thinks she's chosen cattle."*

They were standing very straight, those branded ones. Not kneeling. Not cowering. Just — standing. In rows, loosely, the way soldiers stand when no one has given the order but everyone knows it already. And as the sun climbed toward noon and the priests of the Crown Rite began their intonation, low and droning, one of them began to hum.

Jude's breath caught.

He knew the tune. Every child of the old parishes knew it whether they'd been taught it or not — it lived in lullabies and work songs and the rhythm of oars and looms, worn down smooth by centuries of use until hardly anyone remembered it had been a hymn. *The Lay of the Ninefold Root,* Cassia had called it, teaching it to the caravan children around a fire in the hills while the wheatfield's smoke still stained the northern sky. *We are the root, we are the branch, we are the fruit and the falling leaf—*

The humming spread the way warmth spreads through a cold room.

One voice became five. Five became fifty. The branded ones first, then the plain-clothed faithful who had never registered because they had nothing to register, then the market folk, then — impossibly, terrifyingly — the soldiers themselves, rank upon rank of them, some mouthing the words without seeming to know they did, some staring straight ahead with wet eyes and locked jaws.

The priest's drone faltered. Stopped.

Queen Isolde rose from her throne.

"Silence," she said, and her voice carried the way voices carry when they've been trained in war camps and council chambers, sharp enough to cut the hymn into rags. "This is crown law. This man carries forbidden fire. The sentence is just. The sentence is *final*. Let the rite proceed."

And it might have. Jude believed, in that moment, looking at the ranks of pikes and the archers on the rooftops and the cold arithmetic of Isolde's face — it might have proceeded, and the singing would have broken, and the crowd would have become a crowd again, which is to say afraid, which is to say nothing at all.

Then the god spoke.

Not through him. That was the thing he would try to explain afterward, to Renn, to Cassia, to anyone who asked, and fail every time, because language was built for one mouth and this had none. For three years the spark inside him had been a coal banked under ash — a pulse, a whisper, a single word spoken once in the dark of a ruined shrine: *carry.* He had carried it alone. That was the whole of his priesthood, unwitnessed, unshared: he had carried it the way you carry a dying ember through a night forest, cupped and close and terrified of the wind.

But now the wind was a thousand throats singing, and the spark heard it, and rose.

Heat bloomed in Jude's chest — not pain, not yet, but pressure, like a heart growing too large for its house. The chains on his wrists began to glow a dull orange. The pitch faggots at his feet smoked without catching. Somewhere in the lattice of birchwood a knot popped, and the sound went through the square like a shot, and the hymn wavered—

"Don't stop," Jude said aloud, and his voice came out doubled, harmonized with itself, with something older underneath it, and every person in the square heard it as though he stood at their shoulder. "Whatever else happens today — don't stop."

They didn't stop.

The fire took. Of course it took; that was the design, the craft, the whole long study of how to burn a carrier properly — and flame roared up around him in a golden column, and the crowd screamed, and Jude closed his eyes inside the fire and felt nothing.

That was the miracle no one had planned for. The flames broke against him like water against a pier. He stood in the heart of the pyre and the heat bent around him in slow rivers, and the spark rose higher, and higher, and he understood suddenly — with the terrible clarity that comes only at the point of no return — why the gods had gone silent, why they had poured themselves into carriers and hidden, why they had whispered *carry* and nothing more.

Because this was what it felt like from the inside. This hunger. This vastness pressing against the walls of one small mortal life, wanting *out*, wanting *everything*, wanting to reach through the crowd and the city and the world and remake it in fire and glory and righteous vengeance — three hundred years of burned carriers crying out through him for repayment, and every debt denominated in blood.

The spark wanted the queen's line ended. The spark wanted the archive burned. The spark wanted the first war fought again, properly this time, to its conclusion, crown against covenant until one word remained standing in the ashes.

And it was asking him. Politely. The way a flood asks a door.

"No," Jude said.

Inside the fire, alone, unheard by any living soul, he wrestled it the way you wrestle a brother — with love and fury and your whole weight. *Not vengeance. Not inheritance. You gave yourself to me to survive, and I carried you, and here is the thing I learned carrying you: nothing survives by being owned. Not crowns. Not gods. Not even you.*

The spark flared, furious, grieving, ancient. And then — slowly, like dawn deciding — it listened.

* * *

He opened his eyes inside the fire.

The flames had gone strange — no longer hungry gold but a deep hearth-red, steady, domestic, the color of fires that warm rather than consume. They rose around him in a column and then, at the top, they *broke*, scattering upward into motes of light like sparks off a grindstone, drifting over the square on a wind that had not existed a moment before.

And everywhere a mote touched, something woke.

The branded woman nearest the fountain — Sera had told him her name later, Wren, a hedge-healer with a burned eye-mark on her wrist — gasped and pressed her palm flat over her heart. The soldier two ranks back dropped his pike because his hands had begun, very faintly, to glow. Mara Tull's son laughed aloud at nothing, at everything, at the pigeons bursting upward trailing threads of light. The motes drifted through the crowd like pollen, like snow falling upward, settling into chests and brows and open mouths, and each person they touched shivered and looked around wildly, because they could feel it — *all of them could feel it* — a warmth behind the ribs that had never been there before.

"What is happening?" Queen Isolde's voice cracked across the square. For the first time since Jude had seen her, the arithmetic in her face failed. She gripped the arm of her throne. "What is he *doing*?"

He wasn't doing it. That was the truth no one would ever quite believe, least of all her. He was only opening his hands.

The chains crumbled. Not melted — *unmade*, the iron forgetting itself, rusting to red dust that sifted down through the hearth-light onto the unburnt logs of the pyre. Jude stepped down off the platform, barefoot onto stone still cool beneath the fire's impossible courtesy, and raised both arms, and spoke to the crowd in a voice that was half his own.

"The old gods poured themselves into vessels to survive the first war," he said. "One vessel for each god. One keeper for each flame. And the crown hunted the vessels, generation after generation, because a fire in one hearth can be smothered. Because one carrier can be burned." He turned, slowly, so the whole square saw his face through the thinning column of light. "So we will not be one hearth anymore."

He spread his arms wide.

"Take it."

For a heartbeat, nothing. Ten thousand people holding one breath.

Then the spark *left* him.

It did not hurt. That was the cruelest mercy of it — he had braced for agony, for tearing, for the ripping-out of the thing that had lived beneath his skin for three years and longer, the pulse that had been his secret and his curse and his last thread to the divine. But it went the way breath goes when you finally exhale after holding too long: a rush, a release, a lightness so profound it was almost vertigo. The motes thickened over the square, thousands of them, tens of thousands, streaming off his skin like embers off a struck flint, and they dove into the crowd with something like joy.

The god spoke one last time.

Not through him. Through *everyone.* Through Wren the hedge-healer and the weeping soldier and the baker at the square's edge and the little prince on the dais, whose eyes went wide as saucers and whose lips moved silently around words too old for him. Ten thousand voices said it at once, in unison, in a language older than Vell and newer than this morning, and every person heard it in their own tongue, in their own dead mother's voice, in the voice they used for prayers they'd forgotten making:

*Root and branch. Fruit and leaf. Grow.*

Then — quiet. Real quiet, the kind the world makes after snowfall. The motes settled. The hearth-fire guttered out on the pyre, leaving the birch logs pale and untouched as bone.

Jude stood alone in the center of the square, hollowed out, ordinary, weeping without any decision to do so, and the crowd did not cheer. They did something worse and better. They put their hands over their hearts — all of them, at once, the way you check for a wound — and felt the small warm pulse there, theirs now, unownable, and the sound that rose from ten thousand throats was not a roar at all.

It was a sigh. Long and low and relieved, like a held note finally resolved, like a city exhaling three hundred years.

On the dais, Queen Isolde stared at her own hands, which glowed faintly, traitorously, like everyone else's. Beside her, young Prince Edmun looked up at his mother and asked — loudly enough for the front ranks to hear, and the front ranks passed it back and back until the whole square knew — the question she would spend the rest of her reign trying to answer:

"Mama, why does my chest feel like someone lit a candle?"

Jude's legs gave out. He knelt on the cool stone among the drifts of chain-rust, empty at last, and found — to his astonishment — that emptiness felt like the morning after a fever breaks. Aldric Renn reached him first, moving through the parted crowd in his scorched warrant-coat, and pulled him upright, and held him the way shield-brothers hold each other after a battle: hard, wordless, both of them shaking.

"You gave it away," Renn said into his shoulder. His voice was wrecked. "You had a god in you, and you gave it away."

"Not a god," Jude managed. He laughed, and it came out cracked and wet and real. "A seed. And you can't burn a seed that's planted in ten thousand gardens."

Behind them, across the square, the crowd was already changing — neighbors turning to neighbors, strangers gripping strangers' hands, comparing the new warmth in their chests like folk comparing the weather. Mara Tull pushed through to him with her son on her hip, and pressed the sloppy fever-charm, the one that had never worked, into his palm, and closed his fingers around it.

"It works now," she said simply.

And far away, in the capital, in a hall of white and gold where a queen sat sleepless before a mirror, studying the faint new light behind her own ribs — the light she could neither command nor destroy nor deny — the age of inherited fire quietly ended, and the age of shared breath began.

But that reckoning, and the abdication that would follow, and the charter written by candlelight by hands that still glowed — all of that was tomorrow's work.

Tonight, Jude Fairweather walked home through his own city for the first time in a year, unchained and unhunted and utterly, gloriously ordinary — and above him, in every window, in every doorway, in ten thousand sleeping chests, the old gods breathed.
