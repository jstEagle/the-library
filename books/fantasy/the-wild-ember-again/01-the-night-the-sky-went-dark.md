## 1. The Night the Sky Went Dark

The trouble with being exiled to a valley that suppressed all magic, Emrys Quintana had decided over nine long years, was not the absence of power. It was the abundance of festivals.

"Emrys! You're holding up the lantern line!" Brin Oakeshaw bellowed from the far end of the village green, where forty-odd residents of Lower Hollow were attempting to string paper lights between the well and the old alder tree in a pattern that had made sense on paper and looked, in practice, like a spider's nervous breakdown.

"I'm not *holding up* anything," Emrys called back. "I'm admiring it. There's a difference."

"There's a difference when it's done. This is a disaster."

She laughed and crossed the grass to where he stood on his ladder, a coil of lantern-wire around one shoulder and an expression of profound betrayal aimed at the knot in his hands. Brin was sixty if he was a day, built like a rain barrel, and had appointed himself master of festivities every Founding Festival for as long as Emrys had lived here — which is to say, since the day she'd arrived with nothing but a travel warrant, a false surname, and the particular silence of a woman who did not talk about where she'd come from.

"The pattern's wrong," Brin said.

"It's beautiful."

"It looks like a drunk drew a constellation."

"That," said Emrys, "is arguably more honest than most constellations."

He snorted, and together they wrestled the wire into something resembling order while the rest of the valley trickled onto the green. The Hollow Valley did everything small and did it thoroughly: its harvests, its grudges, its festivals. Tomorrow night there would be pies judged with religious fervor, a fiddle contest that always ended in the same three-way feud, and the lighting of the Founding Fire, which the elders insisted dated back to the first settlers who'd warded the passes against the wild magic beyond. The wards themselves nobody thought about much. They hummed along under the soil like roots, invisible and eternal, and Emrys tried very hard never to think about them either.

Nine years ago they had been her salvation. The valley's suppression ran deep enough to smother even what she was — what she had been. On her first night here she'd lain awake in a borrowed bed waiting for something to rise in her chest, the way it always had, the pressure behind the sternum, the taste of ozone at the back of the throat. Nothing came. Just the ordinary dark, and rain on the roof that fell because clouds wanted it to fall, and no part of it hers. She had cried until morning, quietly, into a pillow that smelled of someone else's lavender.

Then she'd gotten up, found work at the mill, and begun the slow business of becoming a person who mended fences and argued about pie.

"Emrys." Tamlin's daughter Pip skidded to a stop at her knee, six years old and sticky with honey-cake. "Is it true you're doing the fire speech this year?"

"I am absolutely not doing the fire speech."

"Mama says you are."

"Your mama," said Emrys gravely, crouching to her level, "is a liar and a menace, and if she tells you otherwise, you run."

Pip shrieked with laughter and bolted, and Emrys straightened, and that was the moment the sky went out.

It didn't fade. It didn't cloud over. The stars simply stopped — all of them, at once, as though a hand had passed over the world and pinched out every flame. One heartbeat the sky was salted silver; the next it was a smooth black lid, so total that the darkness seemed to press down on the green like water.

Emrys felt it before anyone else did.

Not in her eyes. In her teeth. In the roots of her hair. A sound too low to be a sound — a note beneath hearing, the way an earthquake announces itself to animals before it reaches the stones. She stood very still on the grass while around her people began to murmur, to point upward, to laugh uncertainly and then stop laughing.

And underneath the black sky, beneath the soil, beneath nine years of blessed quiet —

Something moved.

The wards.

She knew it instantly, the way you know a house has gone cold: by absence. All these years she had lived inside the suppression without feeling it, the way fish don't feel water. Now the water drained away, and Emrys Quintana felt the shape of her own magic rush back into her like blood into a numb limb — violent, prickling, *alive* — and with it came a hunger so sudden and so vast that her knees nearly buckled.

"Oh," she said softly, to no one. "Oh, no."

The wards hadn't merely weakened. They had died. She could feel it now that the fog of suppression lifted: the great rootwork of the valley's founding, the patient lattice the first settlers had laid through stone and riverbed, gone silent. Not sleeping. Silent. Dead wards didn't hum. And dead wards meant that whatever had been kept out was no longer being kept out anything — and whatever had been kept *down* —

She pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

The last time the wards around her had failed, a storm had come off the mountains that flattened a forest, drowned a river valley, and killed eleven people whose names she had spent nine years refusing to learn by heart and knowing anyway.

"Emrys?" Brin had climbed down from his ladder and was squinting at her. "You've gone white as flour. It's just clouds, isn't it? Some weather thing?"

"Just clouds," Emrys said.

Her voice came out steady. That surprised her. Somewhere in the last nine years she'd apparently learned to do this — to build a calm the way other people built walls, board by board, and stand behind it while the past clawed at the seams. She looked up at the starless sky with the rest of them, and let them see a woman puzzled by weather.

But inside, behind the boards, everything was screaming. Because the hunger wasn't abating. Her magic had come back after nearly a decade of drought, and it was not grateful, and it was not gentle. It pooled in her chest like floodwater looking for a breach, and it *wanted*. She could feel it wanting — the sky, the wind, the static crawling on every lantern-wire on the green. It wanted to be used. It had been locked in the dark for nine years, and it had opinions about that.

*You don't get to want things,* she told it silently. *You're not a person. You're a scar.*

The hunger pressed against her ribs and said nothing, which was worse.

* * *

By full dark, half the valley had decided the vanished stars were an omen and the other half had decided they were a trick of the light, and both halves had concluded that pie would help. The festival committee, in the grand tradition of committees everywhere, voted unanimously to proceed with tomorrow's celebrations as planned, because canceling would mean admitting something was wrong, and admitting something was wrong was above their pay grade.

Emrys helped hang the last lanterns because her hands needed occupation. Then she walked home alone along the mill road, and the moment the village lights fell behind her, she let herself shake.

The road ran beside the millstream, black water talking to itself over the stones. Above the eastern ridge, where the pass wound down toward the lowlands, the old ward-stones stood in their ancient line — she could see the nearest one from here, a leaning grey finger that generations of children had dared each other to touch. For nine years she'd felt nothing from it, which had been the entire point of living here.

Now she could feel it like a toothache. Or rather, feel the hole where it should have been. The lattice was broken somewhere deep in the hills, snapped clean through, and magic was already seeping back into the valley floor like water into a cellar. She could taste it in the air — faint, but real. Wild-things magic. Weather magic. *Her* kind of magic, threading home.

And threaded through it, faint as a smell of smoke on a far horizon, something else. Something that did not belong to the valley at all. Old. Cold. Patient in a way that made her hindbrain whisper *run*.

"No," she said aloud, to the road, to herself. "Whatever you are, no. I'm a millhand. I mend fences. I make a very respectable apple butter."

The dark did not answer, which she chose to find reassuring.

She reached her cottage — two rooms, a crooked chimney, a garden she'd coaxed out of stubborn ground — and lit her lamp with hands that were almost steady. Almost. She sat at her table and looked at the little room that contained the whole of her life: mended chair, mended table, mended woman. Nine years of careful, deliberate smallness. She had built it grain by grain, and she had told herself the story so many times she almost believed it: that exile had been punishment, yes, but also mercy; that the valley had saved her from herself; that a life without power was a smaller cage but a kinder one.

Almost believed it. Right up until tonight, when the cage door had swung open on its own, and instead of relief she had felt the thing inside her stretch and *smile*.

That was the part she couldn't say out loud, even to herself, except in the strictest privacy of her own skull: the hunger felt familiar. Not new. Not strange. Like meeting an old friend's face in a crowd — the specific, greedy delight of her own power, the thing that had lived in her at fourteen, at sixteen, at nineteen, whispering *more, more, more*, right up until the night it got everything it asked for.

She put her head down on the table, on her folded arms, and breathed.

A knock at the door made her head snap up.

For one irrational instant she was certain it was the dark thing from the ridge, arrived to collect her. Then came Brin's voice, unmistakable, pitched in the special register he reserved for emergencies involving livestock or baked goods.

"Emrys! Emrys, are you awake? Elder Maren's called the council — well, she's called *me* to call everyone, which is how councils work here —"

She opened the door. Brin stood on her step holding a lantern, and behind him the whole village glowed with festival light against a sky that had nothing in it. His face was doing something complicated.

"They want everyone at the meetinghouse," he said. "Even you."

"*Even* me?"

"You know what I mean." He shifted. "Maren said — she said, and I'm quoting, 'fetch Quintana, she'll have opinions.' Which, fair."

"I have one opinion," Emrys said, reaching for her shawl, "and it's that we should all be very calm."

"That's two words and a threat of composure. That doesn't count." He peered up at the empty sky again, and his voice dropped, lost its bluster. "My grandmother used to say the wards sang on festival eve. Quiet, like. Under the ground. I never heard it myself." He looked at her sideways. "Did you ever hear it?"

Emrys looked back at him, at this good man who had taught her fence-mending and never once asked why she flinched at thunder, and gave him the truth in the smallest coin she had.

"Everyone hears it, Brin. They just don't know that's what it is. It's the feeling that everything's all right. Tonight it's not there."

He absorbed that. Nodded slowly. "So we're not all right."

"We might be," she said. "We might not be. Let's go find out."

* * *

The meetinghouse smelled of tallow and wet wool and forty years of arguments. Every elder of consequence was present, plus several inconsequential ones who had promoted themselves for the occasion. Elder Maren presided from her chair with the air of a woman who had been interrupted during the best decade of her retirement.

Emrys took a seat at the back, by the wall, where she always sat. Where exiles and troublemakers sat. She had earned the seat honestly.

The discussion was exactly what she expected. Cloud cover, said one faction. An eclipse, said another, though of what, precisely, was left diplomatically unexamined. Ash from distant fires, offered a third, and this one landed in Emrys's stomach like a stone, because she had tasted the air on the ridge and ash was not what she'd tasted.

It went on for an hour. It was going to go on for four more. She could feel it happening, the great slow machinery of a community deciding to believe nothing, because believing nothing required no action, and action cost hay, and men, and certainty.

Then the doors banged open.

Dax Ferrow stood in them, the youngest shepherd on the high pasture, soaked to the thighs and gray-faced, his dog trembling against his leg.

"The pass," he said. "There's people coming down the pass. Dozens of them. Maybe hundreds." He swallowed. "They're coming out of the lowlands. They're saying—" He stopped, started again. "They're saying the stars fell. All of them. Everywhere. And there's soldiers following the refugees down out of the hills, and the soldiers—"

His voice cracked. The dog whined.

"The soldiers are drinking the light," Dax said. "Off the lanterns. Off the fires. They walk up to a flame and it goes into them, and they don't stop walking."

Silence took the room, complete and awful.

At the back, by the wall, Emrys Quintana sat very still, and behind her carefully constructed calm, nine years of buried past pressed up against the seams like floodwater against a door — because she knew, suddenly and completely, what drank light and marched under a starless sky, and she knew that the wards hadn't simply died of age or accident.

Something had killed them. Something was coming through the gap.

And the hunger in her chest turned toward the pass like a compass needle, and pulled.
