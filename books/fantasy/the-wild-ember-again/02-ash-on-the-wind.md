## 2. Ash on the Wind

The morning after the sky went dark, Emrys Quintana did what she had done every morning for nine years: she lit the bakery oven.

It was a small defiance, or perhaps just a habit wearing the costume of one. The valley was in an uproar—elders shouting in the meetinghouse, children asking why the night had come down like a lid, old Marta Tull insisting it was an omen and that she had *said so* at last year's Founding Festival and been laughed at—but bread did not care about omens. Bread wanted heat and patience, and Emrys had learned to give both to things whether they deserved them or not.

She was scoring the second batch of loaves when Tam's cousin's boy came pelting down the lane, barefoot, breathless, and entirely without manners.

"Emrys! Emrys, the pass—the refugees are coming through the pass!"

"Good morning to you too, Pim," she said. "Have you considered shoes?"

"There's *hundreds* of them!" He grabbed her apron with floury hands, which she would be forgiving but not forgetting. "And they're saying—" He lowered his voice as though the words might bite him. "They're saying the soldiers drink light. Out of the air. Out of *lamps*. They say a man's shadow got pulled right off him."

Emrys set down her blade. The loaves sat there, scored and waiting, and she found she could not look at them, because looking at them meant thinking about the night before, and thinking about the night before meant thinking about the wards snapping with a sound like bone breaking, and thinking about *that* meant thinking about what had seeped back into her chest in the dark of her little room above the bakehouse—a warmth, a hunger, a thing turning over like a sleeper waking—and she was not ready to think about any of that before breakfast.

"Pim," she said, "run ahead to the meetinghouse and tell them I'm coming. And put on shoes."

He ran off shoeless, which told her exactly how much authority she commanded in this valley, even after nine years of excellent sourdough.

* * *

The eastern meadow had become a camp by midmorning.

They came in straggling lines down from the high pass—farmers with their arms full of whatever they'd had time to grab, shepherds leading flocks too thin to be worth stealing, a carter whose wagon held three families and a goat that looked, frankly, better rested than any of them. The valley folk turned out along the fence lines with bread and water and that particular helpless kindness of people who know their charity is a cup bailing a sinking ship.

Emrys moved among them with baskets, because hands needed doing something, and because listening was easier when your hands were busy.

"—took the whole sky over Vellin," a woman was saying, rocking a child who was not crying, which was somehow worse than crying. "Not clouds. Took it. Like drinking through a straw."

"They had lanterns," said an old shepherd to no one in particular. "Corvane's men. Pale ones. They'd hold the lantern up and the flame would bend toward it, all wrong, like it was being *poured*, and then the light was gone and so was—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "So was my brother's eyesight. Just for a while. They said it grew back. Mostly."

"The mages first," someone else murmured. "Always the mages first. They can smell it on you."

Emrys kept walking. Keep your head down, she told herself, in the voice Ilsa had used on her for nine years, the voice that lived in her skull now rent-free. You are a baker. Bakers are boring. Be bread.

But then she reached the far edge of the meadow, where the newest arrivals were being settled on the driest ground, and she heard the voice.

"*Storm-born.*"

It came from under a tarp strung between two carts—a shelter for the worst-off, the ones who wouldn't be moved again. A woman knelt beside a man laid out flat, his robes gray with road dust and something darker. Warden's robes, Emrys realized, with a jolt. The ash-gray wool with the seven-pointed star stitched at the hem. Nobody wore those anymore. Nobody had worn them openly since before her exile; the order had scattered like sparks after the fall of the old regime, its survivors hiding in plain sight as pilgrims, herb-sellers, madmen.

"I'm sorry?" Emrys said, because the woman was staring at her, and staring was rude, and rudeness deserved acknowledgment.

"He keeps saying it." The woman's face was hollowed out. "Since we crossed the pass. Storm-born, storm-born. I thought he was raving." Her eyes flicked to Emrys, apologetic, confused. "Is it—is he yours? Do you know him?"

"No," Emrys said, which was true, and felt like a lie, and she hated how quickly she'd learned the difference.

She should have walked away. She knew that even then. There is a version of this story where Emrys Quintana sets down her basket, wishes the poor man a comfortable death, and goes home to her loaves, and the world ends slightly differently and no one writes a song about it.

Instead she knelt.

Up close he was younger than the robes suggested—thirty, maybe thirty-five, with a scholar's soft hands cracked raw from hard travel. His lips were gray. When he breathed it sounded like wind through a broken shutter.

"Water," Emrys said, and the woman brought the skin, and Emrys held it to his mouth while he drank with the desperate economy of a man rationing his own final hours.

His eyes opened. They were pale, almost colorless, and they fixed on her face with an intensity that made the morning feel colder.

"You're real," he rasped. "I dreamed the wards were dead. I dreamed it and told myself it couldn't be, because if the wards were dead then the storm-born was loose in the world, and the world isn't ready—" A cough took him, wet and long. "I walked nine days. Nine days telling myself I was mad."

"I think you've got me confused with someone," Emrys said gently. "There's no storms here. There never are. That's rather the point of the place."

"Not here." His hand came up—shaking, filthy, impossibly strong—and closed around her wrist. "Under. Under everything. I can feel it. We were taught to feel it. The sleeping weather in a person." His colorless eyes went wide, wider. "Oh. Oh, it's *big*. What did they do to you? What did they *bury* in you?"

Emrys's heart was suddenly very loud. "Let go of me, please."

"They told us you died." He wasn't listening. Ravers rarely do. "The ones who remembered. They said the child burned out on the mountain, that the storm ate her too, that it was a mercy. I believed it. I *wanted* to believe it, because the alternative was that she was alive somewhere, and if she was alive, then the ember had to find her, and finding her meant crossing everything Corvane owns." He laughed, a terrible small sound. "Everything Corvane owns turns out to be most of everything."

"Ember," Emrys repeated. The word sat strangely in her mouth, warm.

And then his other hand came out from beneath his robe, and pressed something into hers.

It was small, fist-sized, and warm—not sun-warm, not body-warm, but *hearth*-warm, coal-warm, the kind of warmth that has an opinion about things. It looked like a chunk of black glass shot through with veins of dull red, and the red pulsed. Slowly. Patiently. Like breathing.

Like a heart.

"What is this," Emrys whispered.

"The last one," the man said. "The last unspoiled heart of the Ember Wardens. The rest fell out of the sky two nights ago—you saw it, everyone saw it—he's harvesting them, he's been harvesting them for years, feeding them into his machines, his *soldiers*—" Another cough, worse. The woman made a sound like a swallowed sob. "They caught us at the shrine. I ran. I have run my whole life and I am so tired of running, and it wants you, do you understand? It has wanted you since the mountain. It remembers you. It's the only thing left that does."

"I don't understand any of this," Emrys said, and her voice cracked on *any*, and the man smiled—actually smiled, gentle as a teacher at a struggling pupil.

"You will. It shows things. That's what it's *for*. Memory is the Warden's craft—we don't hold power, we hold what power *costs*, so no one forgets, so no one does it twice." His grip tightened on her wrist. "Take it south. To Caldreth. Find the vault under the salt-cathedral. And Emrys—"

Every hair on her arms stood up.

"—when the stars come back," he whispered, "and they will come back, one way or another, tell them Ysolde Vane is sorry. Tell her the storm wasn't her fault. Tell her she was *aimed*."

"That's not my name," Emrys said.

"It's the only name that matters," he said. "Ysolde. Ysolde of the Hollow Valley, Ysolde who called the storm on Warden's Mount, Ysolde who killed the council and doesn't remember it. I forgive you. We all forgave you years ago. I only wish someone had thought to tell *you*."

Then his eyes rolled back, and his grip loosened, and the woman was screaming for help, and Emrys sat in the trampled grass of the eastern meadow holding a warm stone that beat against her palm like a second pulse, and did not move for a very long time.

* * *

They buried him at dusk, up on the rise behind the meetinghouse, where the valley buried everyone who arrived already half-gone. No priest would say the rites for a stranger of no known faith, so Ilsa did it herself—short, plain, unsentimental, the way Ilsa did everything except grow herbs, which she did with the tenderness of a woman apologizing to each plant individually.

"Ash to ash," Ilsa finished. "Dust to dust. Go quietly."

"Amen," said the eleven people present, and began drifting away into the blue evening.

Emrys stayed. So did Ilsa, which was not a coincidence; Ilsa had a genius for non-coincidences. She had been Emrys's mentor since before the exile—had argued for it, in fact, had stood in front of the council and called it mercy when others called it sentence—and in nine years she had never once asked Emrys what she remembered of that night. Not once. It was the single greatest kindness anyone had ever done her, and Emrys had only understood that recently, the way you understand load-bearing walls.

"He talked to you," Ilsa said now, to the grave. "Before the end. The widow told Marta, and Marta told everyone, because Marta is Marta."

"He was raving," Emrys said.

"Mm." Ilsa's silence had texture. It had layers. "He called you by a name."

"The fever talks."

"Ysolde." Ilsa said it the way you set down something heavy—carefully, both hands. "That was the name on the warrant. Before the council sealed it. Before we gave you a new one and a new life and asked you never to look back."

Emrys's hand had gone, without her permission, to her pocket. To the shape within it. Warm. Patient. Beating.

"Ilsa," she said, and stopped, because she did not know how to finish. *Ilsa, there is a dead man's heart in my pocket and it beats. Ilsa, he said I killed the council. Ilsa, I have spent nine years being a baker and last night the magic came back into me and it was hungry, it was hungry, Ilsa, and I lay awake all night listening to it want.*

"Yes," Ilsa said, as though Emrys had said all of it. Perhaps her face had. Nine years of practice reading a student who'd learned early that her feelings could crack mountains had made Ilsa fluent. "Tomorrow the council votes on whether to seal the passes and keep the valley shut. You'll be there."

"I'll be there anyway. I'm on the bread rotation."

"And you'll speak," Ilsa said. "You'll tell them what the refugees told you. About the light-drinkers. About the mages taken first. And they will call you a troublemaker, because you are the most convenient troublemaker this valley has ever produced, and frightened people love a convenient troublemaker." She finally turned from the grave. In the last light her face was old and steady and sorry. "And tonight, girl, you'll come to my cottage, and you'll show me what's in your pocket, and you'll tell me everything that man said. All of it. Even the parts that frighten you."

"Especially the parts that frighten me?" Emrys managed. Humor was a reflex, worn smooth by use.

"Those are the only parts I'll believe," Ilsa said.

They walked down together through the darkening fields. Above them the sky hung empty and enormous, starless, like a page torn out of a book everyone had memorized. In Emrys's pocket, the ember pulsed against her hip—warm, patient, wanting—and she could have sworn that with every step toward the valley's heart, toward home, it beat a little faster.

As if it knew the way.

As if, somewhere under nine years of flour and quiet, so did she.
