## 3. What the Valley Remembers

The Hollow Valley had a way of deciding things that Emrys had learned to dread in her first year of exile and had never quite unlearned. Nothing was ever decided quickly. Nothing was ever decided loudly. The valley elders gathered in the meetinghouse beneath the great carved beam—the one that read *We Remember What the Mountain Forgot*—and they talked, and they poured tea, and they talked some more, until whatever truth had arrived at their door was worn smooth as a river stone and small enough to fit in a pocket and ignore.

She sat through four hours of it.

The meetinghouse smelled of beeswax and old cedar and the particular staleness of a room where too many people had been nervous for too long. Outside the shuttered windows, the Founding Festival continued in a state of stunned, half-hearted defiance—a fiddler playing to an audience of eleven, children still trying to bob for apples in a trough that nobody was watching. Someone had relit the lanterns along the green, as if light could be substituted for stars. It could not. Everyone knew it could not, and so the lanterns burned with a desperate, overachieving brightness that hurt to look at.

"The passes," Elder Maren was saying, for what Emrys judged was the third time, "will be sealed. Stonefall and the Widow's Gap both. We have the timber, we have the hands, and by week's end no one comes in or out without this council's say."

"And what will this council's say be?" Emrys asked.

Maren looked at her the way one looks at weather—warily, from a distance, hoping it will pass. Maren had been old when Emrys arrived nine years ago, and she seemed determined to die at precisely the age she was now, having conceded nothing to time but her hair color.

"That is not yet determined."

"Then let me help determine it." Emrys stood. She hadn't meant to stand. Her legs had simply decided, the way they sometimes did, that sitting was a form of complicity. "Word has to go south. Tonight. To Merrowgate, to Caldreth, to every free city on the map. Riders, pigeons, a man on a fast horse with a bell around his neck—I don't care how. But the cities need to know that the wards are dead, that the sky went dark, and that soldiers are coming who can drink light out of the air. Because that is what the refugees said. That is what *I* saw at the pass before you turned them away."

A murmur moved through the benches like wind through wheat. Elder Corick, whose family had grown barley in the valley since before anyone could remember why that was impressive, raised a placid hand.

"Emrys." He said her name gently, which was worse than saying it harshly. "No one doubts your... experience. In matters of the wider world."

*In matters of storms,* he did not say. *In matters of the thing you did.*

"But consider what we actually know," Corick continued. "The stars vanished. Unsettling, yes. The wards failed—which Tam Vey's people tell us happens when the anchoring stones crack, and there was an earthquake felt as far as Thornbury, so perhaps nothing more sinister than geology. And a handful of refugees told frightening stories. Frightened people do that."

"They weren't frightened people," Emrys said. "They were *burning*. One of them had light pouring out of her mouth when she tried to speak. You want to call that geology?"

"I want to call it *unconfirmed*," said Maren. "And I want to call our duty what it has always been: the protection of this valley. Two thousand souls, Emrys. Not two thousand heroes. If there is a war coming—and I grant it may be—we survive it the way this valley survived every war since the Founding. Quietly. Behind stone. Pretending, if necessary, to be exactly as dull as we look."

There it was. The valley's oldest prayer: *let us be beneath notice.* It had worked for three hundred years. It had even worked for her, mostly, though she suspected she had never once been beneath notice so much as beneath observation.

"Sealing the passes doesn't make us safe," Emrys said. "It makes us a jar with the lid on. Whatever's coming doesn't knock on jars. It pours in."

"Poetry," sniffed Elder Hask, who farmed bees and mistrusted all speech longer than a sentence. "We vote."

They voted. Of course they voted. Eleven hands for sealing the passes and sending no word; the twelfth was old Ilsa's, and it hovered—for one long moment Emrys thought the old woman would raise it beside hers—before it settled, at last, flat on the table.

Twelve to one. Or rather, twelve to none, and one abstention that felt like a door closing very softly.

"Motion carries," said Maren. "The passes seal at dawn. Emrys—" and here the old woman's voice softened into something almost kind, which Emrys recognized as the tone one uses with livestock being led somewhere, "—no one blames you for being alarmed. You've been through things. But the valley remembers why you came here, dear. Best not to give folks reason to remember harder."

*The valley remembers.* They said it constantly, the elders, as though memory were grain in a storehouse they personally guarded. Emrys walked out into the lantern-bright night knowing exactly how much the valley remembered, because she had spent nine years making sure it remembered nothing true about her at all.

* * *

She found Ilsa waiting by her cottage, sitting on the low wall with a shawl against the cold, feeding strips of dried apple to a mule who had clearly been bribed into silence. Ilsa had taught her letters her first winter—had taught half the valley's strays their letters—and she had never once, in nine years, asked Emrys a single question about the storm. This restraint had always struck Emrys as either profound wisdom or profound cowardice, and she had long since concluded it was both, in proportions that shifted daily.

"You abstained," Emrys said.

"I'm eighty-one years old. Voting is strenuous." Ilsa held out a strip of apple to her instead of the mule. Emrys took it and ate it without tasting anything. "You argued well. Better than well. You made Corick look reasonable, which is nearly impossible—he works very hard at it."

"It didn't matter."

"No. Being right rarely matters in a room. It matters afterward, on the road, where rightness gets to do its real work." Ilsa reached into the folds of her shawl and produced a folded square of oilcloth, worn soft at the creases. She pressed it into Emrys's hands with the brisk discretion of a woman passing along bread rather than treason. "Mind the corners. They're sharp."

Emrys unfolded it just enough to understand what it was: a map. Hand-drawn, decades old, annotated in Ilsa's cramped brown ink. The valley. The two passes. And beyond them—the whole southern reach, threaded with fine lines marking paths Emrys had never heard of. A shepherd's track over the Shoulder that avoided Stonefall entirely. A dry creek bed behind the Widow's Gap. Small crosses where someone, long ago, had noted water. Small circles where someone had noted something else, and left it unexplained.

"Ilsa—"

"You didn't get this from me. You got it from a trader named Osk, who died three years ago and can't defend his reputation." The old woman's eyes were bright and utterly unrepentant. "And here is your lie, since you'll need one, and you were always terrible at lying—your face does a thing, like a window slamming shut. Practice it. When the guards at the timber line ask why you're out past curfew, you tell them you're checking the ward-stones for cracks. Tam Vey's folk put everyone to work on the wards today; it's plausible. Nobody questions a woman walking toward trouble. Only a woman walking away from it."

"Why are you doing this?"

Ilsa considered the question honestly, which was more than most people did with any question. She looked up—not at Emrys, but at the blank black ceiling of the sky, where nine years of familiar constellations should have been.

"Because forty years ago I stood in that meetinghouse and voted to keep my head down while the old regime burned the Warden shrines in the high country. I told myself the same things Maren told you tonight. Beneath notice. Behind stone. And it worked—it worked beautifully—and then one day I was old, and the world outside was worse, and I understood that my whole life had been spent paying interest on a cowardice I'd taken out as a loan when I was young." She patted Emrys's hand once, briefly, hard as a bird landing. "I don't recommend it. Terrible interest rates."

"The guards will stop me anyway."

"Some might. Most are farm boys scared of the dark, and the dark is much worse tonight than usual, which works in your favor. Fear makes people look down." Ilsa stood, joints crackling like green firewood, and adjusted her shawl. "One more thing. The coal—that warm stone the dying woman gave you at the pass. Don't show anyone. And don't sleep holding it."

Emrys went very still. "How did you know about—"

"I was a girl when the Wardens still flew," Ilsa said simply. "I remember what their hearts looked like when they fell. Goodnight, child. Mind the corners."

She shuffled off down the lane toward her own cottage, small and upright against the lantern-light, leaving Emrys alone with a map full of other people's courage and a lie she'd have to practice in the dark.

* * *

She practiced the lie twice on the walk to the eastern timber line, whispering it under her breath like a spell, which in a way it was. *Checking the ward-stones. Checking the ward-stones.* Her face wanted to slam shut like a window every time; she made herself keep it open, keep it bored, keep it the face of a woman thinking about nothing more dramatic than firewood.

The valley at night, without stars, was a stranger wearing her home's clothes. Paths she could have walked blindfolded turned traitor underfoot. The mountain that sheltered the valley's northern edge had swallowed its own outline. And everywhere, faintly, she felt it again—the seep of magic rising through the dead wards like water through a floorboard, thin and cold and hungry. Nine years of drought, and now the rain was coming back, and some shameful part of her turned her face up toward it like a plant.

At the timber line, two guards stood over a barricade of freshly felled pine. She knew them both: Doss, the miller's son, and a round-faced boy she only thought of as Young Pell. Doss had a spear he was clearly holding for the first time in earnest. Young Pell had a torch, and was holding it slightly too close to his own sleeve.

"Halt," said Doss, without conviction. "Curfew's called. Who goes there?"

"A neighbor," Emrys said. "Emrys. From up the hollow. Council sent me to check the ward-stones for cracks—Tam Vey's crews have been at them all day, and Elder Maren wants the eastern line confirmed before dawn."

It came out steady. The window stayed open. Somewhere in her chest, a small astonished voice observed that she had, apparently, learned to lie after all these years, and that Ilsa would be insufferable about it.

Doss squinted at her. Beyond him, the broken ward-line hummed its bone-snapped silence, and Emrys felt the hunger in the air lean toward her like a dog scenting meat. She kept her face bored. She thought about firewood.

"Ward-stones," Doss repeated.

"Cracks," Emrys agreed. "Earthquake felt as far as Thornbury, apparently."

That did it. Nothing disarmed a farm boy like the suggestion of paperwork already approved by someone older. Doss stepped aside, and Young Pell lifted the torch to light her way, and Emrys walked between them through the gap in the barricade feeling each step like a coin spent she couldn't earn back.

At the top of the rise she stopped and looked back once. The Hollow Valley lay below her in its bowl of darkness, lanterns strung along the green like a child's drawing of a sky, two thousand souls voting themselves safe behind stone and timber and the oldest prayer there is. She thought of Ilsa's face tilted up toward the missing stars. She thought of the dying woman at the pass, light running from her mouth like a secret escaping.

She thought of the ember, wrapped in wool at the bottom of her pack, warm as a sleeping bird, patient as a debt.

"Checking the ward-stones," she told the dark, and turned south, toward the free cities and the road Ilsa had drawn for her, carrying the valley's memory in oilcloth and its forgetting at her back—and behind her, far off in the north, she could have sworn she saw the mountains flicker, just once, like a lamp guttering before it goes out.
