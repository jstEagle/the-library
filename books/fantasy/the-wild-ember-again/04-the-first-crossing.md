## 4. The First Crossing

Ilsa's map was drawn on the back of a funeral invitation, which told Emrys something about how the old woman had spent the last nine years.

*The north goat-path,* the map read, in a hand that shook only slightly. *Past the shrine nobody tends. The wards are dead there anyway, so don't tell me you needed luck.*

Emrys had folded it into her coat at dusk and spent the evening doing the most dangerous thing of all: nothing. She sat through the last supper of the Founding Festival, ate stew she couldn't taste, laughed twice at jokes she didn't hear, and let the whole valley believe she was simply a bitter exile drinking too much cider. The elders had voted to seal the passes. The elders had voted to light the festival fires anyway, because tradition was tradition and the sky being empty was no reason to disappoint the children.

The sky being empty. As if the stars were guests who'd stepped out for air.

She watched the bonfire from the edge of the square, ember-stone warm against her ribs where it rode in a sling of linen against her skin. It had not cooled since the cultist pressed it into her hand. It pulsed, slow and patient, like something asleep that was choosing to stay asleep out of courtesy.

"You're leaving tonight."

Ilsa settled beside her on the well's low wall, not looking at her, which was the old woman's way of granting privacy while refusing to be ignored.

"I'm taking a walk," Emrys said.

"A long one. In the wrong direction."

"The passes are sealed."

"The *gates* are sealed." Ilsa snorted. "Goats have never respected our votes and neither, apparently, will you." She pressed something small into Emrys's palm—a twist of paper. Inside: dried plums and a single silver coin. "For the road. Bribe someone or buy something sweet. Whichever saves your life first."

"Ilsa—"

"Don't." The old woman's voice went quiet and fierce. "You saw clearly when none of us wanted to. That's a lonely gift, girl. Go spend it somewhere it'll matter." She stood, patted Emrys's shoulder once, hard, as though knocking something loose, and walked back toward the fire without looking back.

That was the whole farewell. Emrys held the plums until her hand stopped shaking, then went to get her pack.

* * *

The goat-path was exactly as advertised: steep, unloved, and past a shrine nobody tended. The shrine was a waist-high cairn of river stones with a rusted bell hanging from a crook of wood. Wardens' work, maybe, or just some farmer's hope. Emrys touched the bell as she passed. It didn't ring. Nothing rang anymore; even the wind seemed to be holding its breath.

The broken wards hung around the valley like the memory of a fence. She could feel them—or feel their absence—as a thinning in the air, a place where the world had been stitched shut for nine years and the stitches had rotted through. On the other side of that thinning was everything she'd been exiled from. Magic. Her magic. The storm she barely remembered summoning, still living somewhere in her blood like a scar that ached before rain.

She stood at the boundary with one foot in the valley and thought, absurdly, of learning to swim.

Because that was what it would be. The wards hadn't just kept magic out of the Hollow Valley—they'd kept it out of *her*, gently, every hour of every day, like a dam holding back a river she'd forgotten she was made of. Nine years of quiet. Nine years of being ordinary, of being merely a woman with bad dreams and good bread-making hands. And now she was about to step off the dam's edge.

"Right," she told the empty hillside. "On three. One—"

Her foot decided for her.

The moment she crossed, the world *hit* her.

There was no gentler word for it. Magic came back into her the way floodwater comes back into a dry channel—not flowing but *crashing*, filling every hollow space the wards had left behind, and she had forgotten how much of her was hollow. She went down onto her knees on the goat-path with her hands pressed flat against the earth because the earth was the only thing keeping her from blowing apart.

Lightning lived under her skin. Not metaphorically—she could see it, faint blue threads crawling up her own forearms, jumping between her fingers, tasting the air for somewhere to go. Her hair lifted. The stones of the shrine rattled in their cairn. Somewhere below her, a flock of night birds burst screaming from the trees.

"*Stop,*" she gasped, to herself, to it, to whatever was left of the person who had done this once before and been exiled for it. "Stop, stop, stop—"

It did not stop. It had waited nine years and it intended to say hello thoroughly.

And beneath the terror—the part of her that hated herself for noticing—was joy. Raw, shameful, electric joy. Because this was hers. This was what they'd cut away, and it fit her like her own name, and it felt like being handed back a limb she'd mourned. She was laughing, actually laughing, kneeling in the dirt with lightning crawling up her arms, and she couldn't have said whether she was crying.

Then the storm tried to come *out*.

The pressure built behind her sternum, in her teeth, in the backs of her eyes. The air above the ridge began to churn. A cloud formed where no cloud should be—one small, furious, personal cloud—and the first thunder rolled across the hills like a warning shot.

"No," Emrys said, and meant it with everything she had. "Not again. Whatever you are, whatever I am—we are NOT doing this again."

She did the only thing she could think of. She grabbed for the ember.

* * *

The coal-stone flared against her skin, and the world folded sideways into memory.

---

*Rain on a battlefield that has no name yet. She is very small—six? seven?—and someone enormous is carrying her, running, and the enormous someone is singing badly. There are others ahead, figures in robes the color of banked coals, standing in a ring around something that glows.*

*"This is how we keep it," the enormous one says, breathless. "We don't hold power, little storm. We hold the remembering of it. Power burns out. Remembering keeps."*

*A ring of hands takes her from the enormous one's arms. Warm hands. Many hands. She is passed along the circle like bread at a table, and each pair of hands gives her something—a sound, a smell, a name spoken softly—and she feels herself becoming fuller and safer with every step around the ring.*

*"Say it back," an old woman whispers, pressing Ember-light into her palms. "Say the storm back to itself, and it will know you're home."*

---

Emrys came back to herself lying flat on the goat-path, soaked in sweat, the cloud above her gone as if it had never dared exist.

She lay there breathing for a long time. Then she sat up, took the ember out of its sling, and looked at it properly for the first time—not as a relic, not as a dying man's burden, but as what it evidently was.

"You're teaching me," she said. "Aren't you?"

The stone pulsed. Once. Patiently. Like a tutor tapping a pointer against a slate.

"That memory—that wasn't mine. I was—" She frowned, groping for it. "I might have been there. I don't remember being there. But it felt like..." She struggled. "Like a kitchen you grew up in. You know where the bowls are kept even if you can't picture the room."

Pulse.

"And when I reached for you, my storm—it stopped. You gave it somewhere else to go." She turned the ember over in her fingers. Warm, not hot. It had never burned her, not once, even when it should have. "You're teaching me the thing the Wardens knew. How to carry it without drowning."

The ember pulsed again, and this time something came with the pulse—not a full memory, just an impression, the way a smell carries a whole season in it: the sensation of *holding*. Of a storm the size of the sky resting in a chest the size of a fist, obedient, because it was loved and it knew it.

"Oh," Emrys said quietly. "Oh, that's how you do it."

She sat cross-legged on the path and began, clumsily, to try.

* * *

Three hours later, she could make the lightning sit in her cupped hands like a startled bird instead of tearing up her arms. It was the worst bird-training in history and involved several small fires, one of which she had to beat out with her boot while hissing at it to be quiet, because the last thing a fugitive needs is a signal fire announcing her location to the entire countryside.

But she was learning. The ember fed her fragments in the spaces between attempts—never whole memories, just edges of them. A lesson in breathing that some dead Warden had given a frightened child. A trick for grounding lightning through the heels instead of the hands. A lullaby, of all things, whose rhythm matched the pulse of contained power, and which she caught herself humming under her breath as she picked her way down through the treeline.

"It's not a lullaby," she informed the ember. "I'm humming it ironically."

Pulse. Which she chose to interpret as skepticism.

Dawn found her at the edge of the foothills, where the goat-path spilled down into open country—the wheat plains south of the valley, gold-green and endless and completely, terrifyingly exposed. No more friendly mountains. No more borders. From here to the free cities lay a hundred leagues of country that belonged, increasingly, to Vael Corvane.

She ate two of Ilsa's plums and looked at the horizon. Smoke stood up from it in three places, thin and orderly, the smoke of deliberate burning rather than accident. Armies cooked breakfast too.

Somewhere out there were soldiers who drank light from the air. Somewhere out there was the answer to why the stars were gone, and why the ember knew her true name, and what she had done—what she had *been made to do*, whispered a voice she didn't trust—in the years the wards had walled off.

Her magic stirred under her skin, testing its new boundaries, restless as a dog waking in a strange house. It felt different out here than it had in the valley's shadow. Wilder. Half-feral, like something raised in captivity set loose in real weather. She could work with that. She could probably survive it. She was less sure the countryside could.

"One day at a time," she told it sternly. "And no storms unless I say so. We are going to be subtle. We are going to be *invisible*. Do you understand me?"

The magic crackled faintly along her wrist, in a pattern suspiciously close to a shrug.

They came down off the hills together, the storm-caller and the storm, and by midmorning Emrys Quintana stood at the edge of a wheatfield taller than her head, hungry enough to eat Ilsa's remaining plums plus the paper they came in, and listening to voices.

Human voices. Two of them. Ahead, in the wheat, coming closer, accompanied by the soft crunch of someone walking where they shouldn't and eating something at the same time.

"—telling you, the patrols moved east, I've got it on excellent authority, which is why we're cutting through here and not the road like sensible people—"

"There are no sensible people left, Tam, that's rather the point of the war—"

Emrys sank down into the wheat and realized, with dawning horror, that she had crossed the entire border of the valley, survived the return of her own suppressed magic, and begun relearning the lost craft of the ancient Wardens—

—and the first living souls she met were looting a wheatfield.

The voices stopped. The wheat went silent.

Then, cheerfully, from ten feet away: "Well. Good morning. Lovely weather for trespassing, isn't it?"
