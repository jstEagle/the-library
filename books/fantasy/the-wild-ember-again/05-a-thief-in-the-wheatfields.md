## 5. A Thief in the Wheatfields

Tam Vey had been robbed by professionals his whole life — tax collectors, guildmasters, two former wives, and a parrot with a talent for purse strings — so he liked to think he knew quality when he saw it.

The thief currently ruining his wheatfield was not it.

She crouched in the stubble like a heron that had read about crouching in a book, all wrong angles and visible elbows, and she'd made the classic amateur mistake of going for the farmhouse *last*, after the granary, after the smokehouse, after the well bucket where he kept the good cheese wrapped in oilcloth. A professional hit the house first, while everyone slept through their one chance to notice anything. This woman was working backward through his entire inventory with the methodical patience of someone doing penance rather than larceny.

Tam lay flat in the ditch at the field's edge, chin on his forearms, and watched her fill a sack with what he calculated, at current road prices and accounting for the war premium every smuggler south of the pass had tacked on since the stars went out, was roughly forty percent of his quarterly profit.

The moon was a thin nail-paring overhead, which suited him fine. No stars meant no starlight for Corvane's patrols to drink, and no starlight meant they traveled blind and mean and mostly by day. The night belonged to people like Tam now. It was the one gift the dark sky had given the honest criminal class.

He should just let her go. That was the sensible play. She was clearly half-starved, clearly running from something, and clearly — he squinted — talking to herself.

He sat up a little straighter. Yes. Talking to herself. Her lips were moving, steady and low, like she was having a conversation with the sack of his stolen turnips. Or possibly praying. He'd seen refugees pray over food. He'd seen them do worse over less.

"Right," Tam murmured to himself. "That's either madness or magic, and both fetch a price."

He didn't go for the patrol. That deserved saying plainly, even if only to himself, because Tam Vey tried to be honest with at least one person in any given room, and lately that person was usually him. Corvane's men paid well for storm-caller sightings — paid in light, actual drinkable light siphoned into flasks, which was the most unsettling currency Tam had ever pocketed. But he'd watched what happened to villages that traded in people. The soldiers drank something out of the mages they took, some bright thread of them, and left the rest standing there like houses after a fire: sound walls, nothing home. Tam's mother used to say you could measure a man by what he wouldn't sell. His fence, Odo, said you could measure him better by what he would, and Odo had bought Tam's grandmother's wedding ring off him at a fair price, so between the two philosophies Tam tried to land somewhere in the middle, near the exits.

He circled wide through the barley, quiet as a man whose entire livelihood depended on quiet, which it did, and came up behind the thief just as she straightened with his sack over her shoulder.

"Don't scream," he said pleasantly. "It wakes the dog, and the dog is a liar. He'll tell you he bites."

She screamed anyway. Short, strangled, immediately regretted — she clapped a hand over her own mouth like she was shutting a door on the sound — and then she spun, dropped low, and did something with her stance that made Tam revise several opinions at once.

That was a fighter's crouch. That was a *soldier's* crouch, or close kin to one, weight forward, sack abandoned without a thought for it. Her eyes went past him twice — checking his flanks, checking the dark — before settling on his throat. Not his purse. Not his knife. His throat. People who looked at throats had killed before, or been very close to it.

"Easy," Tam said, hands up, palms out, his most disarming smile deployed like a shield. "I'm the owner. You're robbing me. This is a simple arrangement with a simple ending: you put down my dinner, I don't yell for anyone, we both pretend this never happened, and I'll even throw in directions to the next farm over, because Bertram Hollis deserves it and has for years."

The woman didn't relax. Up close she was younger than her posture, mid-twenties maybe, with wind-cracked lips and the kind of exhaustion that sits behind the eyes like a tenant who's stopped paying rent. There was ash on her sleeves. Not hearth ash — road ash, the fine grey grit that had been falling out of the sky for weeks now, ever since the stars fell or were taken or whatever the priests had settled on calling it this month. Ash from burned things a long way off.

"You're not a soldier," she said. Her voice was hoarse, days of disuse in it.

"No. Soldiers have better boots." Tam wiggled his own, which were excellent boots, actually, but the point stood rhetorically. "I'm a trader. Independent. Unaligned. Deeply neutral. If Corvane wins I sold him grain; if the free cities win I sold them grain; history will record me as a humanitarian."

"You're a smuggler."

"I'm hurt. A smuggler is just a trader with a better sense of humor about tariffs." He nodded at the sack. "Take the bread. Take the cheese, I insist, it's Bertram's cheese anyway, I won it at cards. But the dried plums are medicine, not food, and I need them for a client with delicate—"

Her knees buckled.

It wasn't dramatic. No swoon, no cry. She just folded, mid-negotiation, like a tent with a snapped pole, and went down into the wheat stubble hard enough that Tam winced in sympathy with her kneecaps. By the time he'd crossed the distance she was propped on one arm, white-faced, furious at herself for it, waving him off with the other hand.

"Don't," she managed. "I'm fine. I haven't eaten. That's all."

"That's never all," Tam said, "but fine." He fished in his coat, produced the plum he'd been carrying for exactly this species of moment — smugglers learned fast that the world was full of fainting women and men who needed bribing and only one of those groups responded to fruit — and held it out. She stared at it like it might be a trap. Everything about her stared like it might be a trap.

"It's a plum," he said. "I checked. Thoroughly. I'm very thorough about plums."

She took it and ate it in four bites, chewing slowly, deliberately, like someone relearning how, and Tam crouched at a polite distance and studied the thing he'd been trying not to study since she turned around.

On her inner wrist, half-hidden by her sleeve, there was a mark. Old scar tissue, deliberate, a spiral struck through with three lines — and Tam's stomach performed a slow, expensive roll, because he knew that mark. Everyone from the old country knew it, or had, before the wars burned the knowing out of the maps. His grandmother had worn it on her forearm too. She'd covered it with a bracelet for the last thirty years of her life and gone pale as milk whenever thunder rolled.

Storm-caller. Sworn line, marked at birth or at binding. The kind of person who hadn't existed openly in nine years, because the last time they'd existed openly, an entire council of Wardens had died in one night of weather, and the regimes that followed had made very sure the weather stayed personal and small forever after.

And then the wards of Hollow Valley had died, and the stars had gone out, and here was a starving woman with a dead line's mark on her wrist stealing turnips in his field and talking to herself in the dark.

"So," Tam said lightly, because lightness was load-bearing in his life, "funny question. Hypothetically. If a person had, say, a mark like the old storm lines — purely hypothetical — would that person also happen to be carrying anything else of interest? Anything small? Warm? Coal-colored?"

She went still in a way that answered everything. Not frozen-fear stillness. *Guilty* stillness. The stillness of a card player with a winning hand and no idea how much her face was worth.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Course you don't." He sat back on his heels. "Let me try again. My name's Tam Vey. I run goods south through the bell roads when the bells are sleeping and east along the coast when they're not. I know every toll, every patrol rotation between here and Caldreth, and every back door into Merrowgate including two that Odo doesn't know about yet, which is a personal record I intend to exploit before winter." He paused. "You need to get somewhere. You're not getting there on foot with a sack of turnips and no food, because the roads are crawling with men who drink light and the nights are full of things that used to be stars. And I need cargo worth the trip, because fuel's tripled since the sky went dark and sentiment, tragically, does not burn."

"I'm not giving you anything," she said. Flat. Tired. But her hand had drifted, he noticed, to her side, where under her coat something hung against her ribs. Something she'd been keeping warm, maybe. Something that had kept her talking to it in the dark.

"I haven't asked you for anything," Tam said. "Yet. I'm building up to it. It's a process. There's a whole speech."

"Skip the speech."

"Straight to terms, then. Fine. Terms: passage. Real passage — cart, cover story, papers that will survive a bored inquisitor's second look. South, wherever you're going, within reason. In exchange—" he held up a finger before she could cut in "—not the thing itself. I'm not an idiot, whatever Odo tells people. I don't want to carry it, guard it, or be responsible for it when the drinking-men come sniffing. I want *first sale*. When you decide what happens to your little coal, whatever you decide, I get to broker it. Ten percent. Standard rate."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, mostly, and under it a wary recalculation, like she'd had a whole argument prepared and he'd declined to have it.

"Why?" she asked. "You could sell me tonight. They pay in light."

"They do." Tam looked up at the empty sky for a moment. It still caught him sometimes, the black of it, like a hole in the world where the ceiling used to be. "My family came out of the old wars," he said, and surprised himself by saying it, because he told almost no one that, because it was the one piece of inventory he'd never once put on a scale. "Not the famous part. The messy part, after. The part where people like your grandmother—" he nodded at her wrist, and watched her pull her sleeve down, quick and ashamed, and hated himself a little for the flinch it cost her "—learned to wear bracelets. I've seen what those patrols leave behind when they're finished taking. It's not a person anymore. It's a coat somebody's still wearing." He shrugged. "So no. I don't sell people. Bad for the conscience, worse for the reputation, and my reputation is the only asset I own outright."

The woman studied him for a long moment. Wind moved through the wheat, a dry whisper, and far off — too far off to be anything tonight, but worth noting — thunder muttered over the mountains like an old man grumbling in his sleep.

"Emrys," she said at last.

"What?"

"My name. Since we're trading." She got her feet under her, slowly, testing them. "Emrys Quintana. And I'm not selling the ember. Whatever you think you're brokering."

"The ember," Tam repeated, tasting the word, filing it away in the drawer where he kept things that were going to complicate his life. "Never heard of it. Sounds valuable."

"It isn't for sale."

"Everything's for sale. Some things are just priced in grief, and those take longer to negotiate." He offered her his hand up. She ignored it and rose on her own, which told him something else useful about her, and retrieved his sack of provisions, which told him something more useful still — she hadn't run while his back was metaphorically turned. She was staying. Starving, hunted, marked, mad-enough-to-talk-to-a-rock Emrys Quintana was standing in his field choosing to stay, and Tam Vey, who trusted nobody and believed in less, felt the treacherous first stirring of something he refused to call hope.

"One condition of mine," she said.

"Name it."

"If I tell you to run — you run. Don't ask why. Don't come back for me." Her eyes were steady and bleak and older than the rest of her. "There's a storm in me that got out once. Nine years ago. I don't remember it, and I'd like to keep it that way, and if it gets loose again, everyone nearby is a debt I can't pay."

Tam considered several responses — a joke, mostly, jokes being his armor of choice, and he had a good one loaded about weather warnings and farmers — and set them all aside, because she wasn't joking and deserved one honest sentence in return.

"My grandmother used to say the same thing about herself," he said. "Different words. Same shape." He picked up his sack, slung it over his shoulder, and jerked his chin toward the dark bulk of the barn. "Cart's inside. We move at false dawn, before the bells wake. Sleep in the hay loft — and fair warning, the dog will lie to you about whether he bites, but he's a coward, kick him gently and he'll love you forever."

She hesitated at the edge of the dark, one hand pressed flat against her side, over the thing that hung warm against her ribs. For a moment Tam thought she was going to say something else — something true, maybe, the dangerous kind — but she only nodded once and walked toward the barn like a woman walking into exile again and finding it familiar.

Tam stood alone in the ruined wheat with forty percent of his quarterly profit trampled into the dirt, ten percent of something priceless promised, and a storm-caller asleep above his cart by morning.

"Well," he told the empty sky, because the empty sky was excellent at keeping secrets these days, "Odo is going to laugh himself sick."

Far to the south, over the hills where the alarm-bells hung silent and waiting, thunder rolled again — closer this time, and answering.

* * *

They rolled out at false dawn with the mist still lying in the hollows like spilled milk, Tam driving, Emrys riding in the back among the grain sacks with a blanket over her head and the ember — she'd finally shown it to him, briefly, at gunpoint of her own grim insistence that he understand what he was ferrying — tucked against her chest. It had glowed. Softly, steadily, like a coal remembering fire. Like something alive.

"Merrowgate first," she'd said, settling into the straw. "Then we'll see."

"We'll see," Tam agreed, snapping the reins, "is my favorite phrase in trade. Right after *no refunds*."

Behind them, the broken wards of Hollow Valley stood open to the road like a door off its hinges, and ahead the bells hung in their long rows, waiting for any spark of magic dumb enough to sing.

Tam drove south and told himself the warmth spreading through the cart bed was just morning sun.
