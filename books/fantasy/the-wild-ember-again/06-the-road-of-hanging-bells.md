## 6. The Road of Hanging Bells

The first bell they found hanging from a gallows-tree outside the valley road was small enough to fit in Tam's palm, and it screamed anyway.

Not rang—*screamed*. A sound like a kettle made of grief, thin and rising and wrong, and Emrys had thrown herself flat in the wheat stubble before her mind caught up with her body. Tam stood there chewing on the last heel of bread from Hollow Valley, watching her with the particular patience of a man who has decided that being entertained is cheaper than being afraid.

"It only does that," he said around the bread, "if you touch it. Or breathe on it. Or think at it too loudly, if half the stories are true."

"I didn't touch it."

"You looked at it like you wanted to marry it."

Emrys got up slowly, brushing chaff from her coat, and studied the bell from a careful six paces away. It was bronze, or something shaped like bronze, and it hung from a black iron bracket nailed into the tree as casually as a lantern. Inside its mouth, where a clapper should have been, there was nothing. Just a small darkness that seemed to drink the afternoon light and hold it.

Corvane's work. She'd heard about the bells even in exile, in the garbled way news reached places that had voted to stop having news: alarm-bells strung along every road south, tuned not to sound but to *magic*. Walk past one carrying so much as a hedge-witch's charm and it would sing your name to the nearest garrison.

"Right," she said. "We go around."

"There's no around." Tam pointed with the bread. The road ran through the wheat country like a scar, and along it, at intervals of perhaps two hundred yards, more brackets waited on fence posts, dead trees, the eaves of an abandoned shrine. She could see eight of them from where she stood. She did not want to count how many she couldn't. "The whole road's strung like a festival, except instead of ribbons it's screaming metal. Welcome to the new Corvane, same appetite as the old regime but with better infrastructure."

"You're very calm for a man whose head is worth money."

"My head isn't worth anything. It's what's under it they pay for." He swallowed the last of the bread and dusted his hands. "Which brings us to the small problem of you, storm-caller, walking down a road lined with devices specifically designed to notice people like you."

"I can control it."

He looked at her. It was, she was beginning to learn, his most devastating weapon—that look, mild and unhurried, saying *I have met liars before and you are merely competent at it.*

"Can you," he said.

She wanted to say yes. What came out instead was the truth, which had been happening to her more often lately and which she resented deeply. "I don't know. Before the valley, I never had to be quiet. And for nine years I had nothing to be quiet *with*. Now—" She flexed her right hand. Under the skin, faint as warmth from a hearthstone through a wall, the magic sat. It had been sitting there since the wards died, patient and enormous, like a dog that had spent nearly a decade asleep and now wanted very much to run. "Now I have to carry all of it past every one of those things without letting it so much as hum."

"And the ember?"

She touched the coal at her breastbone through her shirt, where she'd sewn it into a sling of cloth against her skin. Warm. Always warm. Sometimes, at the edge of sleep, it murmured—but that wasn't the ember's problem. That was hers. "The ember doesn't ring bells. It's memory, not power. Mostly."

"*Mostly,*" Tam repeated, savoring it. "Wonderful word. I'm going to get it stitched on a hat."

They went around anyway, or tried to. It took them four days to cross what the road would have done in one, cutting through wheat fields gone gold and unharvested because the farmers had fled or been taken, wading ditches, doubling back twice when Tam spotted riders on the far ridgeline. Emrys learned to walk in a way she had never walked before: holding her breath without holding her breath. Every step, she kept the storm inside her pressed flat, folded small, smothered under attention. It was like carrying water in her cupped hands for miles. It was like smiling through a funeral. It was the hardest thing she had ever done, and she had once survived nine years of being nobody.

By the third day her nose bled twice. By the fourth, her hands shook so badly she couldn't work the fire drill, and Tam lit it with flint and steel while pretending not to notice, which was its own small kindness.

"You know," he said that night, over a fire no bigger than a cupped palm, "when I said passage in exchange for the ember, I pictured this taking less time."

"The road is strung with screaming bells."

"The road is strung with bells for *normal* travelers too. Normal travelers walk on it. They wave at patrols. They complain about the weather. You've made us into two criminals crawling through a ditch, and one of us"—he pointed at himself with a stick—"didn't even do anything."

"You're smuggling me toward Merrowgate."

"I'm escorting merchandise. There's a difference, and the difference is that merchandise doesn't make me crawl through ditches." He fed the stick to the fire. But after a while he said, quieter, "Your nose is still bleeding."

She wiped it with her sleeve. "It does that."

"When I was a boy, we had a neighbor in the Tarnish who'd been pressed-ganged into the old regime's war engines. Memory-soldier. They'd burned everything out of him except the fighting. He used to sit on his step and hold perfectly still for hours, and my mother said it was because moving cost him something. Stillness was the cheapest thing he owned." Tam shrugged, elaborately casual. "You sit like that sometimes. Like quiet is expensive."

Emrys looked at the fire instead of him. "Quiet is the only thing keeping us alive."

"That wasn't a criticism." He lay back against his pack. "It was an observation. There's a difference, and the difference is that observations are free."

She almost smiled. It hurt her face, which meant she'd needed to do it.

* * *

On the fifth day, they smelled the smoke.

It came on the wind from the west, sweet and foul at once—thatch and grain and something underneath it that was neither. By noon they could see it: three columns of black rising off a village tucked into the river bend below them, maybe four miles distant. Too far to help. Close enough that Emrys could feel, through the flattened gray blanket of her held-down magic, the moment the light-drinkers started their work.

It came to her as pressure. Like ears popping before a storm, except inverted—the air *draining*, light pulled out of the afternoon in long slow swigs. The smoke columns flickered. The colors of the wheat dimmed, going the color of old bone, then recovering, then dimming again in a rhythm that made her think of feeding. Of swallowing.

Tam stood beside her on the ridge, and for once he wasn't performing anything. His face was just a face, doing what faces do when they watch a village burn.

"They're culling," he said. "That's what they call it. My fence in Merrowgate used the word last spring like he was discussing a wool surplus. Culling the mages." He spat, with feeling. "They find anyone who can so much as charm a wart off a pig, and they drink them. Not kill. Drink. Take the light out and leave the rest standing, staring at walls."

"I know." She knew it in her bones, worse than he did. She could feel the drinking itself, a tug at the base of her skull, and beneath her suppression the storm stirred and rolled over and *noticed*, the way a big animal notices another predator on its territory. Her teeth ached. Her fingertips buzzed. Everything in her wanted to answer—to reach across four miles and put lightning down the throats of whatever was feeding at that village—and she had to fold it back down, and down, and down, until her vision spotted at the edges and blood ran warm over her lip again.

"Emrys."

"I'm fine."

"You're bleeding on your boots and vibrating like a struck fence wire."

"I said I'm *fine*."

He was quiet a moment. Then, gently, in the voice he probably used on spooked horses and suspicious customs officers: "If you let it out here, those bells will scream from here to the coast, and every soldier between us and Merrowgate will come running, and the people in that village will still be dead or drunk, just with company."

She knew. That was the obscenity of it—she *knew*, and knowing changed nothing about what her body wanted. Restraint, she was learning, was not the absence of action. It was action, continuous, exhausting, invisible. Nobody would ever write songs about the storm she didn't call.

"Walk," she said thickly. "Please. Get me away from the edge of this hill before I do something generous."

They walked. Tam set a pace just short of a run and talked the whole way—not because he had anything to say, she realized eventually, but because talking gave her something to hold onto besides the pull of the drinking-light. He told her about the worst deal of his career (a crate of "antique" candlesticks that turned out to be church property, consecrated, and mildly cursed), about his mother's opinion of his profession ("she called it 'lying with logistics'"), about a dog he'd owned at fourteen named Baron who had once eaten an entire wheel of contraband cheese and been too proud to show pain. Emrys listened with blood on her lip and her fists clenched, and by the time the columns of smoke sank behind the hills, she had laughed twice, horribly, wetly, at the story of Baron and the cheese, and both times it had felt like surfacing from deep water.

That night she slept badly, and dreamed in someone else's memory.

* * *

The ember showed her a road.

Not the main road—a different one, older. In the dream she stood on a hillside in high summer a hundred years gone, and a woman was walking ahead of her up a track of white stones. The woman was tall and brown as a doorbeam, with silver threaded through her braids, and she moved with the easy authority of someone who has never once had to apologize for existing. A Warden. Emrys knew it the way you know a thing in dreams, without evidence.

*Watch,* the woman said, though her lips didn't move, or maybe they did; dream-logic is generous. *A Warden leaves two roads behind her. One for the world, and one for the world's worst day.*

She stepped off the track at a leaning stone—just a boulder to any eye, gray and lichened—and laid her palm against it, and sang three notes so low Emrys felt them in her sternum rather than heard them. The hillside opened. Not dramatically; the shadow of the boulder simply became a doorway, and beyond it a path ran down through the earth, lit by stones that glowed like banked coals, going on farther than the light could follow.

*Mark the stone,* the Warden said. *And mark the song. We hid these paths when the first tyrants came, and we swore our successors would remember. If you are seeing this, successor, then we failed, and it falls to you.*

Then the dream tilted, as dreams do, and Emrys saw the hillside from above, decades later—the same boulder, the same slope, but with landmarks she recognized. A split oak. A stream bending around a fallen tower. The broken arch of some grand building drowned in ivy.

A place on the map Ilsa had given her. Twenty miles south. Off the bell-road entirely.

She woke before dawn with the melody already fading, and grabbed it the way you grab a name slipping out of reach, humming it under her breath until it stuck. Three notes. Low. She practiced them silently against her own ribs while Tam snored with the total commitment of a man with no conscience and excellent digestion.

When he woke, she had the map spread between them and her finger on the ivy-drowned arch.

"There's another way," she said. "South of the bell-line. An old Warden path. Hidden doors, warded ways—no bells, because the bells were built by men who never knew these roads existed."

Tam leaned over the map, hair flattened on one side, radiating skepticism. "Let me summarize my concerns. One: you dreamed a secret road. Two: you trust the dream. Three: you want to take me, a professional skeptic, down a hole in a hillside based on point one."

"Yes."

"Huh." He scratched his jaw. "In my defense, my objections are usually better funded than this. Fine. But if the hole eats us, I want it noted for posterity that I raised the issue."

"Noted."

* * *

The boulder was exactly where the dream had left it, which frightened Emrys more than if it hadn't been. She stood before it in the gray of late afternoon with Tam fidgeting behind her, and she put her palm against cold lichen-stone, and she sang the three notes.

Her voice cracked on the second one. She was out of practice at singing anything but silence. But the low note caught somewhere in the rock and *held*, resonating, gathering, and then the shadow under the boulder deepened and opened onto steps going down into a soft amber glow.

Tam made a noise like a teakettle discovering religion.

"Warden roads," Emrys said. Her voice shook, and she let it. "A hundred years old, give or take. They built them for the world's worst day."

"Is this the world's worst day?"

"It's certainly in the running."

They went down. The path was dry and clean, lined with coalswarm stones that brightened politely as they passed and dimmed behind them, and the air smelled of dust and time and, faintly, impossibly, of summer. At the bottom of the first flight, Emrys stopped, because the walls had words carved into them in the old script—she couldn't read most of them, but the ember could, and it translated against her breastbone like a hand smoothing a wrinkled page:

*YOU WHO WALK HERE ARE THE FAILURE WE FEARED AND THE HOPE WE PLANTED. GO CAREFULLY. GO SOUTH. DO NOT LET THEM TAKE THE LIGHT.*

Below the inscription, smaller, in a different hand, newer, scratched rather than carved: *three of us made it this far — R — keep going*

Nobody had scratched anything after that.

Tam read the wall twice. For once he didn't joke, and the silence between them had weight and shape, and Emrys found she didn't mind it.

"She left a message," Tam said finally. "R. Whoever she was. Hundred years ago, dying world, and she stopped in the dark to leave a note for strangers." He shook his head. "People are unbelievable. In the good direction, occasionally."

They camped that night in a dry chamber off the path, with no fire needed because the walls themselves gave a gentle warmth, and Emrys slept without suppressing anything for the first time in five days. The storm inside her stretched like a cat in sunlight, and the ember hummed, and if either of them noticed her crying quietly in the amber dark, kindly, neither mentioned it.

* * *

Two days later, the path ended where the dream hadn't shown her: at a stone door swollen shut with roots, in a hillside overlooking the river plain—and beyond the river, hazy in the morning light, the towers of Merrowgate rose against the sky.

Or rather, against where the sky should have been.

Emrys stopped on the threshold with the root-wrapped door at her back and stared. Above Merrowgate, where other cities had clouds, this city had a *ceiling*. A pale, pearly, faintly luminous dome, like the inside of a shell, stretching from horizon to horizon. Light fell from it evenly, sourcelessly, the way light falls in a temple.

"Tam," she said. "What am I looking at?"

He came up beside her, and she watched his face do several things quickly and land nowhere good.

"That," he said slowly, "is new. That was not there when I left in spring."

The ember pulsed against her skin, hard, once, like a heart missing a beat. And through it—not her memory, not any Warden's memory, something older and colder—came a single clear impression, a word in no language she knew, which her bones translated anyway:

*Harvested.*

Somewhere under that beautiful false sky, the light of stolen stars was being stored. And somewhere in that city, Tam's fence was waiting with a fortune in hand, and Lady Serevan's inquisitors were waiting with softer questions, and every road forward led down into the light of a sky that had been sold.

"Well," said Tam, in the bright, doomed voice of a man committing to a terrible plan, "I did say Merrowgate pays best prices in the free cities. Come on. Try to look like merchandise."
