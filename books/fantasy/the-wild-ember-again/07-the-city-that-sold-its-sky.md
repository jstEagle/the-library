## 7. The City That Sold Its Sky

Tam Vey had a rule about cities on the edge of surrender: never be inside the walls when the haggling ends. Sellers got sentimental at the end of a bargain. Buyers got violent. And bystanders—well, bystanders got trampled, pressed into militia service, or made into examples, depending entirely on which side's boots found them first.

So naturally, here he was, walking through Merrowgate's Salt Gate at midmorning, leading a storm-caller in a borrowed hat.

"The hat's not working," Emrys said.

"The hat is working beautifully."

"Everyone keeps looking at me."

"They're looking at your hat because it's a magnificent hat. Peacock feathers. Very fashionable. Nobody looks twice at a woman in a magnificent hat." He adjusted his own cap and gave her his best reassuring grin. "They're certainly not thinking, *there goes the most dangerous person in the valley, disguised as a festival-goer.*

"You said that out loud."

"I said it quietly."

She pulled the brim down. Under it, her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. Nine days on the road together had taught Tam the full taxonomy of her not-smiles: there was the *I'm tolerating you* one, the *you're an idiot but a useful idiot* one, and the rarest, the one where she forgot to arrange her face at all and just looked tired and young and like someone who'd once laughed at things. He was careful never to mention that last one. Mentioning it made it go away.

Merrowgate smelled the way it always had—brine, tar, frying fish, the sweet rot of the seaward docks—but underneath it now ran a new scent, thin and wrong, like the air after lightning only without any lightning. Like a struck match that refused to finish striking.

"That smell," Emrys said quietly.

"I know."

"It's been following us since the pass."

"I know," Tam said again, and touched her elbow, steering them off the main boulevard before she could stare at anything too long. He knew these streets the way he knew the inside of his own coat, every pocket and hidden seam. "Rule of surviving a dying city: don't gawk. Gawkers get noticed. Noticed people get questions. Questions get answers, and in Merrowgate right now, answers are currency, and the mint is run by men I wouldn't trust with my grandmother's spoons."

"You don't have a grandmother."

"I have a fence who acts like one and bites like one. Same thing. Come on."

* * *

The house of Marigold Fenn sat wedged between a chandler's and a shuttered playhouse, three stories of leaning brick with a green door that hadn't been green in twenty years. Tam knocked the pattern—two, pause, three, pause, one—and the door opened on a chain, and one gray eye took him in.

"Well," said Marigold Fenn. "The prodigal embarrassment returns."

"Marigold. You look terrible. Is that a new shawl?"

"It's the same shawl I've worn for eleven years and if you say one more word I'll leave you on the step." The eye slid past him. "And this is?"

"A friend. Traveling companion. Business associate of no particular—"

"Let them in, you old fraud," said a voice from within, "before someone sees you doing sentiment in public."

The door opened. Marigold Fenn stood all of five feet nothing, wrapped in the shawl in question, with hands like knotted rope and the unblinking appraisal of a woman who had once fenced stolen cathedral relics and gotten away with it by being too polite to arrest. She looked at Emrys for a long moment.

"Hat's ridiculous," she said. "Inside. Both of you. Quickly."

* * *

Her parlor was dim and crowded and smelled of tea and old paper. Marigold poured for three without asking, which was its own kind of welcome, and settled into her chair like a cat reclaiming territory.

"Now," she said. "Tam. You've brought me something."

"I've brought myself. And a friend. There's a difference."

"There's always a difference. It's usually the profit margin." She sipped her tea, eyes on Emrys over the rim. "You came down the north road. Through the pass. Past the burn villages. In the same week the sky went empty." She set the cup down. "Child, take off that ridiculous hat. You've got the look of a woman carrying something hot."

Tam felt his stomach drop. "Mari—"

"Quiet. Adults are talking." But her voice had gentled, the way it did when she was about to do something dangerous and kind. "Every smuggler, every refugee, every rat leaving the northern ships has whispered the same word for nine days. *Ember.* Something fell from the sky and somebody caught it. Corvane's patrols turned three towns inside out looking for whoever caught it." She folded her hands. "So either you're the luckiest fools alive or the unluckiest, and either way, I can help. For a price."

Emrys set down her tea. Slowly, deliberately, she reached up and removed the hat.

For a moment she didn't show Marigold anything at all. Then she opened her hand, just enough, and the light of the ember breathed between her fingers—a coal-red glow that pulsed, very faintly, like something sleeping. Or dreaming.

Marigold went still. Tam had seen her face down a customs captain with forged seals and a knife at her ribs; he'd never seen her go still like this.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor girl. You're carrying a heart."

"How—" Emrys started.

"I'm old, dear, and I was old when the Wardens were young. Sit still." Marigold rose, crossed to her writing desk, and began pulling drawers. Her voice kept moving while her hands searched. "Here's what happens now, and listen carefully, because I'll only be generous once. There are buyers in this city. Serious ones. Free-city councilmen with deep purses who think a Warden's heart is a shield against Corvane. Merchants who think it's a trade good. One or two fools who think it's jewelry." A drawer slammed. "And Corvane's people, who aren't buying."

"So we sell fast and leave," Tam said. "That was rather my thinking. Mari, you've got contacts—"

"I have one contact who could pay for it properly, and he arrived yesterday with a purse so heavy he walks tilted." She turned, holding a small velvet case, and looked at Emrys with an expression Tam couldn't read—pity wearing business's clothes. "Girl. Whatever you think that thing is going to do for you, it won't. Hearts are for keeping, not carrying, and you're neither Warden enough nor dead enough to hold one safe. Sell it to me. Ten thousand crowns. More than you could spend in ten lifetimes, even spending badly, which from the look of you, you would. You walk away rich, anonymous, and free. South on the coastal packet, gone before the week's out. That's not a price. That's a rescue."

Ten thousand crowns. Tam's mouth went dry. He'd planned jobs for years that netted less than a tenth of that. With ten thousand crowns he could buy back the boat, buy back the name, buy a house with a door that locked from the inside—

He realized both women were looking at him.

"No," Emrys said.

Marigold blinked. "No?"

"It's not mine to sell."

"Everything's yours to sell, child. That's what ownership means."

"It's not *owned*. It's—" Emrys closed her fist around the ember's light. "It's a person. Or what's left of one. It remembers things. It's trying to tell me things. You don't sell a person to a man who walks tilted."

"Everyone sells everyone," Marigold said, not unkindly. "The trick is choosing the buyer."

"Not this. Not—" Emrys stopped herself. When she spoke again her voice was lower, rougher. "You didn't see what I saw at the vault-road shrine. You didn't hear them. They were *people*, Marigold. They burned for six hundred years keeping the sky lit, and Corvane picked their bones clean, and I am not handing the last one to a purse no matter how heavy it walks."

Silence. Marigold sat back down slowly. Tam stared at his tea.

"And you?" Emrys said to him. Quietly. Which was worse than loudly.

"Mare," he said carefully, "ten thousand crowns is—"

"Don't." Just one word, but it landed like a dropped plate.

"—is a lot of money," he finished, because he was a coward and also honest, "and I'd be lying if I said I hadn't thought about it. I think about money professionally. But." He rubbed the back of his neck. "But I also watched you sleep sitting up against a wall for three nights because you said the ember dreams loud and you didn't want to dream alone. So. No. We don't sell it. We get out of this city, though. Today. Because Mari's right about one thing—we've got a lit lantern in a room full of moths."

Marigold snorted. "Romance. In my parlor." She stood, brisk again, all softness packed away. "Fine. Keep your heart, girl, and may it keep you better than most hearts manage. Tam—upstairs, third room, there's a packet for you under the floorboard. Papers, coin, a route south that avoids the bells. Don't thank me, it's billed to your account."

"Mari—"

"Your account," she repeated, "which is *catastrophic*, and this doesn't touch the principal, so don't flatter yourself." At the door she paused, and without turning around: "Corvane's inquisitor lodged at the Pearlspire two days ago. Lady Serevan. If you're still in this city tomorrow, you won't need papers. You'll need a miracle, and those are out of season."

* * *

They made it four streets.

Tam felt it before he understood it—that wrongness in the air sharpening, the struck-match smell thickening until it coated the tongue. Ahead, the boulevard opened onto Lantern Square, and a crowd had gathered there in the loose, shuffling way crowds gather when they want to watch something and don't want to admit it.

"Market," Emrys said.

"That's not a market."

He should have turned them around. He knew that even as his feet carried him forward, because Tam Vey had one true flaw among his many decorative ones: he always wanted to see the end of the story. He threaded them along the crowd's edge, Emrys's hood up now, the peacock hat abandoned in Marigold's parlor, and looked.

In the center of the square stood six soldiers in Corvane's gray-and-white, and among them, unhurried, moved three figures that Tam's eyes kept sliding off of. They wore no armor. Their skin had a faint luster, like candle-wax held near a flame, and where they walked, the shadows of things leaned toward them instead of away.

Light-drinkers. He'd heard the refugees' word for them outside the pass and thought it a figure of speech.

In front of the soldiers, kneeling in a line, were seven people. Ordinary people. A fishwife. Two boys in apprentices' smocks. An old man with ink-stained fingers. A woman holding another woman's hand so tightly that Tam could see the white of the grip from thirty feet away.

Mages. Or close enough. People the city had registered, licensed, taxed—the free cities had always done that much—and who now knelt on their own cobblestones while clerks with ledgers checked names off a list.

"They're consenting," Emrys whispered. "Look. Nobody's—they signed something. There's a *document*."

There was. Tam could see it: a clerk at a folding table, an official seal, a queue of citizens waiting their turn to sign. The surrender terms had been posted three days ago; he'd read fragments nailed to the gatehouse. *Registration of magical persons for the common defense.* Compensation provided. Families exempted. It was all terribly civilized.

One of the light-drinkers stopped before the fishwife. Tilted his head, almost courteously. Then he leaned down and breathed in, and the color went out of her—not her blood, her *light*, some brightness Tam hadn't known was there until it left. She sagged. The soldiers caught her, laid her gently aside, and the clerk made a mark.

The next in line shuffled forward. The old man with ink-stained fingers was crying, but he signed anyway. His signature was neat. Practiced. A man used to signing things.

"They queued," Emrys said. Her voice had gone somewhere flat and far away. "Tam, they're queuing."

"Because the alternative is what happened at Brast," Tam said. He heard his own voice doing its merchant's thing, smooth and reasonable, and hated it. "Because they were told their families keep their homes. Because the mayor negotiated. Because it's orderly." He swallowed. "Because it's easier to sell yourself than be taken, and Merrowgate has always been good at selling."

"That's not selling. Selling implies you get something."

"They're getting their children's lives. Best price available."

Emrys turned and looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious. "You sound like you believe it."

"I *have* to sound like I believe it," Tam said, and the honesty escaped before he could catch it, "because the other option is standing in this square screaming, and screaming doesn't feed anyone or save anyone, it just gets you added to the ledger." He took her arm. "Come away. Please. There's nothing we can do here except become a mark in someone's column, and I refuse. I absolutely refuse."

She let him pull her back, step by step, out of the square. Behind them the clerk's pen scratched on. Somewhere in the queue a mother was explaining to a small child, in a bright, terrible, reasonable voice, that everything was fine, that this was just a check-up, that the nice men would be quick.

* * *

They walked fast and silent toward the harbor, and Tam talked, because silence was a hole and he fell into holes. About the packet boat leaving on the evening tide. About the route south, the cove at Grayhaven, the cousin-of-a-cousin who owed him. Emrys answered maybe one word in ten, and each one was a stone.

At the harbor stairs she finally stopped.

"She looked at us," Emrys said.

"Who?"

"The fishwife. When they laid her down. Before her eyes—" Emrys's jaw tightened. "She looked straight at where we were standing. In the crowd. And she *apologized.* With her face. Like she was sorry to be seen like that. Like *she'd* failed *us*." Her breath came out shaky. "Nine days I've been telling myself we move fast, we stay quiet, we get the ember somewhere safe, and every step of it I've been walking past burning villages with my hood up telling myself the same thing you told me in that square. That there's nothing we can do. That survival is the job."

"It *is* the job," Tam said. "Staying alive is the whole job. Dead heroes liberate nobody, they just make nice ballads for the people who killed them."

"Then why does it feel like complicity?"

"Because it is," he said simply. "All survival is, a little bit. You eat, someone else doesn't. You hide, someone else gets found. The trick—the only trick I know—is to stay alive long enough to owe it back." He gestured vaguely southward, toward the bells and the armies and the darkening sky. "You're carrying six hundred years of owing-it-back on your neck, Emrys. Don't add ours on top tonight. Tonight we get on a boat."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he braced for the not-smile, the cold one, the one that put walls up.

Instead she said, "Owe it back. That's the most decent thing you've ever said."

"Don't spread it around. I have a reputation."

And then the harbor bells began to ring—not the tide bell, not the fog bell, but the quick triple-stroke Tam had heard exactly once before, in a port that was burning behind him. Alarm. And beneath it, rolling up from the direction of the Pearlspire, a sound like a great many boots finding a rhythm.

A runner pounded past them down the quay, shouting to anyone who'd listen: "Curfew! City's closed! The inquisitor's sealed the gates—nobody in, nobody out—"

Tam looked at Emrys. Emrys looked at Tam.

"So," she said. "About that boat."

"The boat," said Tam Vey, "has just become a very long-term investment."
