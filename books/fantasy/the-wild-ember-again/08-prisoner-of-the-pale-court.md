## 8. Prisoner of the Pale Court

The trap was not a net. Emrys would have respected a net.

It was a bowl of soup.

They had been three days out of Merrowgate, following the river road north toward the high passes and the Warden vault Ilsa's map promised, when Tam announced that what they both needed more than anything in the world was hot food that had not been cooked over their own small fire. He knew an inn, he said. A quiet one. The kind of place where nobody asked questions because nobody cared enough to ask them.

"You're a smuggler," Emrys had said. "Aren't safe inns the opposite of your entire profession?"

"Safe inns are exactly my profession," Tam said. "Unsafe ones are just where amateurs eat."

So she followed him into the Drowned Lantern, into warmth and woodsmoke and the smell of onions, and she was halfway through the best barley soup of her nine years of exile when her hands stopped working. They simply set down the spoon, all on their own, as if someone else had borrowed them.

"Emrys," Tam said quietly. His face had gone carefully blank, which on Tam was the loudest alarm there was. "Don't turn around."

She turned around anyway. She'd never been good at instructions, which was, she reflected with terrible clarity, probably how all this had started, nine years ago, whatever *all this* was.

The woman standing in the doorway wore grey. Not the grey of soldiers or the grey of travelers—this was grey the way frost is grey, precise and total, a coat cut so cleanly it seemed less sewn than decided. Her hair was silver-white and bound tight, her face was calm as still water, and at her belt, where another officer might have hung a sword, hung a small brass lantern with no candle in it.

Every lamp in the inn dimmed as she crossed the threshold. Every fire sank an inch, like dogs flattening their ears.

"Lady Serevan," Tam breathed. He said it the way other people said *landslide.*

"Inquisitor of the Pale Court," the woman agreed, in a voice like paper folding. Her eyes found Emrys across the room with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once searched for a thing and failed to find it. "You have something of mine, storm-caller."

"I really don't," Emrys said. It came out steadier than she felt. Her hand had drifted, without permission, to the satchel where the ember sat warm against her hip, and she saw Serevan see it, and understood she might as well have drawn a map.

"No," Serevan agreed mildly. "But you will do nicely for now."

* * *

They did not bind her hands. That frightened her more than ropes would have.

The Pale Court's column moved through the hills like weather—a hundred soldiers in ash-grey, wagons draped in canvas, and above it all the light-drinkers riding their pale horses with lanterns swinging empty at every saddle. Wherever the column passed, the air went thin and colorless, the way the world looked after snowmelt. Emrys rode in an open wagon under guard, and the ember against her ribs pulsed like a second heart, faster than her own.

Tam rode beside the wagon, hands conspicuously visible, talking his way deeper into the guards' good graces with each mile. By the second evening he had learned four names, lost eleven coins at dice, and gotten two soldiers laughing hard enough to choke. Emrys watched him work and felt something complicated move in her chest. He could have slipped away at any ford. Instead he stayed, riding a captive's escort line, telling jokes to men who served the man burning the free cities.

"Why?" she asked him once, low, when the wagon slowed on a switchback.

"Because you owe me passage money," Tam said. Then, quieter, without looking at her: "Because I've seen what Corvane does to prisoners from the Pale Court, and I'd rather not find out what's left afterward." He flicked the dice in his fingers. "Also you snore, and I want witnesses."

"I don't snore."

"You absolutely snore. Like a landslide with opinions."

She almost laughed. It was the last easy moment for a long time.

* * *

The Pale Court itself was not a building. It was a pavilion of white silk that traveled with the army, and it was inside that silk, on a chair too elegant to be called a throne, that Lady Serevan conducted her interviews.

Emrys was brought in at dusk. The silk walls glowed faintly from within, lit by nothing, and the air tasted of cold iron and old snow. Serevan sat behind a low table laid with tea things—actual tea things, porcelain painted with wrens—and gestured graciously to the cushion across from her.

"Sit. You'll be stiff from the wagon. My soldiers forget that prisoners are still bodies."

Emrys sat. The tea, when Serevan poured it, smelled genuinely wonderful, and that offended her deeply.

"You're not going to ask me about the ember," Emrys said.

Serevan's eyebrows rose, a fraction. Genuine interest, or a very fine imitation. "No."

"It's the most dangerous thing in the world. Your master wants it. Everyone says so."

"My master wants many things." Serevan lifted her cup, blew across the surface with the small domestic care of any woman at the end of a long day. "I am not here for the ember. If I wanted the ember, I would take it. It sits against your left side in a oilcloth wrap, and you check it eleven times a day—I counted. No, Emrys Quintana. I am here to talk about a night nine years ago. The night of your exile. The night of the storm."

The tea went cold in Emrys's mouth.

"I don't remember that night," she said. It was the truth, worn smooth by nine years of repetition. "That's rather the point of my whole tragic story. The storm took it out of me along with everything else."

"Mmm." Serevan drank. Set the cup down with a click of porcelain. "Do you know what memory is, physically? Where it lives?"

"In the head. Presumably."

"In the blood, in the bone, in the pattern of your magic," Serevan said. "When a storm-caller calls a storm large enough to unmake a mountain council, the storm takes its payment. But it doesn't erase. Nothing erases. It *files*. Somewhere in you is a perfect record of that night, sealed behind scar tissue thicker than any ward in the Hollow Valley." She leaned forward, and for the first time her stillness looked like effort. "I want you to open it."

"Why?" Emrys laughed, and hated the sound. "So you can hold it over me? So Corvane can use it? Whatever I did that night, I've paid for it nine times over. I was a child. I've been told that a thousand ways by a thousand elders who wouldn't look at me while they said it."

"I know you were a child," Serevan said.

Something in the flatness of it made Emrys stop.

"I know precisely how old you were. Thirteen years, four months. I know the name the old regime gave you before Quintana. I know the shape of the working that was done to aim you." Serevan folded her hands. "You think you are the only person whose past has been taken and kept? I am the Inquisitor of the Pale Court, storm-caller. My entire office is memory. Who do you imagine edits the memories of everyone who matters?"

The silk walls seemed closer than they had a moment ago.

"Corvane was there that night," Serevan said. "At the Warden convocation at Starfall Bridge. He was young then—young, brilliant, and already indispensable to the old regime. He stood at the edge of the valley and watched the sky go black, and he watched your storm come down off the peaks like the fist of an angry god, and he watched it kill the last free council of the Ember Wardens to a man. Seven Wardens, Emrys. The seven who could have stopped what came after. Your lightning wrote his future for him. There is no Vael Corvane, Usurper, without the storm you cannot remember calling."

"That's not—" Emrys's voice cracked. She pressed her palms flat against her knees. "That's not possible. The Wardens were already gone by then. That's why the valley built the wards, why they exiled me—the last of them died years before I was born, everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what the victors wrote down." Serevan's voice did not rise at all; that was the horror of it. It stayed level as a frozen lake while the ground fell away beneath Emrys. "Seven Wardens met in secret at Starfall Bridge because they were the last seven, hunted for decades, hiding in the one place magic ran too wild for the regime's hunters. And the regime could not reach them with soldiers. So they reached them with you."

"No."

"They aimed you like an arrow, and they were very proud of the craftsmanship, and afterwards they were very embarrassed by the mess. A child who could do that was a child who might someday do it again—for anyone. So the same men who used you declared you abomination and drove you into exile, and the Hollow Valley built its little suppressing wards, and everyone agreed to call it mercy." Serevan poured herself more tea, unhurried. "It was mercy, of a kind. It was also a cover, of a kind. Both things were true. Most true things are two things."

Emrys discovered she was shaking. Not dramatically—not the shaking of grief or fear—but a fine continuous tremor, as if her body had finally admitted something her mind had spent nine years refusing. The ember pulsed against her hip, warm, urgent, like a dog scratching at a door.

"You're lying," she whispered.

"I could show you," Serevan said. "That is what I'm offering. Open the seal. Let me read the night with you. You want the truth—I can see it eating you alive, it has been for nine years, you carry guilt like a stone you refuse to set down because you don't know what's engraved on it." Her grey eyes caught the pale light. "I need that record too. There are things in that night that my lord has taken from me, and I have been his faithful instrument for twenty years on the promise that someday he would give them back. We want the same door opened, you and I. For different reasons. Perhaps that makes us honest with each other in a way neither of us can afford with anyone else."

For one mad moment Emrys considered it. Considered reaching into herself, into the scar-tissue dark, and pulling.

Then she thought of Ilsa's face. Of the elders' vote. Of Tam losing eleven coins to make guards laugh so he could stay near a prisoner he owed nothing. Of the refugees from the pass, babbling about soldiers who drink light. Of the villages burning on the horizon while she walked mute past Corvane's bells.

Whatever was sealed inside her had made all of that possible. She wasn't sure she could survive knowing it. She was certain the world couldn't survive her handing it to the Pale Court.

"No," Emrys said.

Serevan regarded her for a long moment. Then she sighed, and it was almost gentle.

"I thought not. You're stubborn. Good—it means the record will keep." She rose, smoothing her grey coat. "Keep your ember close, storm-caller. Guard it well. Sleep with it against your heart." At the tent flap she paused, backlit by the colorless glow of the camp, and said, without turning: "And when the memories finally break loose on their own—as they will, that ember has been whispering to the seal since the day it touched your hand—remember that I offered to hold your hand while you read them. Remember that I warned you."

She swept out. The lamps in the guard posts brightened again behind her, like a held breath released.

Emrys sat alone among the wren-painted porcelain with her hands trembling and the ember hammering against her ribs, faster now, insistent, and beneath the wool of her shirt she could feel it—faint but unmistakable, like a voice through a wall.

It was saying her name. Not Quintana. The other one. The one tied to the storm.

And it was not asking anymore.
