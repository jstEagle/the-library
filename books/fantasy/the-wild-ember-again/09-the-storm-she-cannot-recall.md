## 9. The Storm She Cannot Recall

The ember did not let her sleep so much as it took her.

Emrys learned this the hard way, in the third hour after she finally lay down beneath a leaning oak at the edge of a farmer's fallow field, with Tam snoring ten feet away and pretending—badly, even unconscious—not to be guarding her. The moment her eyes closed, the coal in her satchel warmed against her hip like a cat settling in, and the world folded inward.

She was standing on a mountaintop under a sky full of stars.

Not the empty sky of the last weeks. Not the bruised, starless black that had followed the death of the wards. This was *before*—she knew it the way you know things in dreams—and the stars were so thick they seemed less like lights than like a congregation, thousands upon thousands of them, all facing one direction.

Facing her.

"You're doing it again," said a voice beside her, warm and dry as old paper.

She turned. A woman stood there in Warden grey, silver-shot hair cropped short, hands folded behind her back in the manner of someone who had spent a lifetime resisting the urge to point at things disapprovingly. Her face was lined and kind and utterly unfamiliar, and yet some deep cellar of Emrys's mind rang like a struck bell at the sight of her.

"Do what?" Emrys heard herself say—and flinched, because the voice was not hers. It was younger. Lighter. A child's voice.

"Counting them," the woman said. "You do it when you're frightened. One for every star. As if naming a thing could keep it from falling."

"I'm not frightened."

"No," the woman agreed gravely. "You're terrified. There's a difference, and it matters. Frightened children hide. Terrified ones count stars and pretend they aren't about to be used."

*Used.* The word landed in the dream like a stone into still water, and the ripples went out and out, and the mountaintop began to change around them—the stars dimming one by one, not vanishing as they had above the valley but *draining*, their light pulled sideways in long pale threads toward something below the horizon—

Emrys woke gasping, hand already at the satchel.

The ember pulsed against her palm. Patient. Almost gentle. *More*, it seemed to say. *You have more to see.*

* * *

By the second night, she stopped fighting it.

This was not courage. She wanted that recorded somewhere, in case Ilsa ever asked, or in case some future historian wrote about the woman who burned the ember: *she faced the memories bravely*. No. She stopped fighting because fighting hurt worse than surrendering. The ember had been leaking into her since the dying cultist pressed it into her hand—it had been leaking since before that, probably, since the moment the wards snapped and nine years of suppression cracked open like an egg—and each night she resisted, the memories came anyway, in fragments, in shards, arriving like weather.

Better, she decided, to walk into the storm than to wait for it to find her. That, at least, sounded like wisdom. It was mostly exhaustion.

So she sat up by the dead fire with the ember cupped in both hands, and she said aloud, feeling foolish, "Show me."

And the ember, which had perhaps been waiting exactly for permission—or perhaps simply for the correct quantity of despair—opened like a door.

* * *

She was small again. Six years old, maybe seven. She knew herself by her hands first: small hands, scraped knuckles, a wrist bearing the faint spiral mark that Tam had recognized in the wheatfields with a low whistle and the words *storm-caller, and a marked one at that*. In the memory, the spiral was new. Pink. Fresh as a brand.

She stood in a white room. Not clean-white; the white of a place scrubbed of anything that might hold warmth. A table. Two adults across it, and though their faces swam whenever she tried to look directly at them—memory defending itself, or the ember mercifully blurring them—one detail held sharp as a nail: the sigil embroidered on the man's sleeve. A crown over crossed lightning bolts.

She would see that sigil again, years later, stitched on the banners of Corvane's army.

"She responds well to isolation," the man was saying, in the bored tone of a man discussing crop yields. "Grief is the best primer. Grief and praise, alternated. You give her nothing for a week, then you tell her she's the only thing standing between the Wardens and everyone she loves. After that, she'll call down the sky if you ask nicely."

"And when she learns what we've aimed her at?" The second voice. A woman's. Cooler.

"She won't. Children don't ask what the target looks like. They ask if they've been good." He leaned forward, and his face almost resolved—a heavy jaw, eyes the color of pond ice—"That's the whole art of it, Serevan. You don't break them. You build them around the breaking, so they thank you for the shape."

*Serevan.*

Emrys surfaced from the memory gasping, the name caught in her throat like a fishhook. Lady Serevan, who had sat across from her in the Pale Court with her terrible gentle patience, asking not about the ember but about *the night itself*—asking, Emrys realized now, not as an interrogator gathering intelligence but as a collector confirming provenance.

Checking her work.

Or checking Corvane's work. Checking the work of whatever machine had made a six-year-old into a weapon and then, when the weapon misfired, buried the misfire under an exile and a mercy and a story everyone could live with.

The ember pulsed. *There is more,* it said, in its wordless way. *You asked for truth. Truth does not come in pieces you can put back down.*

"No," Emrys whispered to the dark. "I suppose it doesn't." And she closed her eyes, and walked back in.

* * *

The mountain came next. The real version of the dream.

She was nine. The white room had become a barracks, then a wagon, then a cold trail up through pine country, and the man with the pond-ice eyes had become a set of orders delivered through intermediaries who never met her gaze: *the Wardens are meeting on the high peak. The Wardens are traitors. The Wardens want to take the sky away from ordinary people. You are the only one who can stop them, little storm. Only you.*

And she had believed it. That was the part that made her sick, awake and shaking under the oak while Tam muttered in his sleep. Not that they'd lied to her. Lies were cheap and everywhere. It was that she had believed them *gratefully*. They had taken a child who had already lost—something, someone; the memory kept that door locked even now, though she could feel its shape—and given her a purpose with edges sharp enough to cut, and she had hugged it to her chest like the only warm thing in the world.

The summit. The council hall of the Ember Wardens, open to the sky, ringed with stones that hummed faintly, that sang. Grey-robed figures seated in a circle, and among them the dry-voiced woman with the cropped silver hair, who looked at the small girl being escorted up the path and did not look afraid.

She looked *sad*. And then, terribly, she looked *familiar*—and recognition moved through Emrys like a blade finding an old scar. The woman from the first dream. The one who teased her about counting stars. Someone had loved her once, before the white rooms. Or been paid to stand near her until love grew anyway, which amounted to the same thing and broke the same.

They let her approach. That was the unbearable arithmetic of it. Nine years later, turning it over and over, she understood: they had seen a child coming up the mountain and they had *let her approach*. Because she was a child. Because Wardens did not strike first at children. Because their entire order was built on the mad, beautiful premise that power must kneel to conscience, and so they knelt, and the storm fell on them kneeling.

She remembered the moment of calling only in flashes. The taste of copper. The spiral on her wrist burning white. The man's voice in her ear—no, not in her ear, he'd never come that close, but the words were his, worn smooth from repetition, *only you can stop them*—and the sky opening like a wound, and the light of a thousand stars bending down and *inward*, drinking the Wardens' own guardianship into the storm's heart, turning their protection into fuel—

No. No, that wasn't right. She hadn't drunk the stars. She couldn't have.

But the ember showed her, patiently, the way you show a child their own reflection: the threads of starlight streaming down into the storm, and the storm passing them along, down the mountain, into vessels waiting below. Men in the livery of the crown-and-lightning. Men collecting what fell, with buckets and iron cages and instruments she had no names for, harvesting the murdered Wardens' stolen light while the child on the summit screamed herself hoarse inside a thunderhead she could no longer control.

Corvane's men. Corvane's harvest. Corvane, whose armies now marched on the free cities fueled by exactly this—by the light her hands had torn loose nine years ago. The first vintage. The founding stock. Everything Vael Corvane poured into his conquest had begun that night, in that storm, called by her.

The last free Warden council had died believing, to the last of them, that they must not harm the child at its center.

* * *

She woke at dawn with frost on her eyelashes and no memory of having cried, though her face was stiff with it.

Tam was already up, crouched over a fire he'd built small and smokeless, wearing the expression he wore when he was trying very hard to be thinking about breakfast. He glanced up, took one look at her, and reached into his pack without a word, producing a twist of dried apricots and holding it out like a man offering a rope to someone dangling over a cliff.

"Eat," he said. "Then tell me how bad."

"That bad."

"Hm." He considered this. "Worse than the interrogation?"

"The interrogation told me I'd killed people. This told me *how*." Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. Somewhere in the night she had passed through weeping and out the other side into a strange clear country. "I was nine, Tam. Maybe younger when they started. They kept me in a white room and taught me that grief was ammunition and praise was a leash, and then they pointed me at the last free Wardens in the world and told me they were monsters." She turned her wrist over. The spiral sat there, pink-tinged in dawn light, looking suddenly like what it had always been: a leash-ring. "My exile wasn't punishment. It was cleanup. Somebody in the old regime decided the weapon had served its purpose and shouldn't be found near the scene. The Hollow Valley wasn't a prison. It was a *drawer*."

Tam fed a twig to the fire. He was quiet long enough that she almost apologized—for the weight of it, for dumping it on him—but then he said, "My family died in the magic wars too. You know that."

"You said. Fire, you said."

"Fire." He nodded slowly. "Everyone says fire. It's tidier. But it wasn't fire, Emrys. It was a battle between two lords' pet storm-callers, and my village was between them, and afterward both lords sent riders to apologize and offer compensation, and my mother's ghost would have laughed herself sick at the idea that an apology was worth coin." He looked up, and for once there was no charm in his face at all, just the raw plank underneath. "I've spent eleven years telling myself the world runs on bargains because bargains can be counted. What those people did to you—that's not a bargain. That's not even cruelty. Cruelty at least *sees* you."

"They saw me fine," Emrys said. "That's what I keep snagging on. The man in the white room—he knew exactly what he was doing. He talked about me like a crop rotation. *Grief is the best primer.* He was proud of it. And Serevan—" the name came out of her like a splinter working loose, "—she sat across from me in that cell asking about my memories, and she wasn't hunting the ember. She was auditing. Making sure the cover-up held. Making sure *I* still didn't remember."

"So what do you do with that?" Tam asked. It wasn't unkind. It was the question of a man who had spent eleven years converting grief into logistics, and it was, she realized, the most respectful thing anyone had said to her in days.

What did she do with it?

For nine years, her guilt had been a shapeless thing, an ocean without a floor—*I did something terrible, I am terrible, the storm lives in me*—and she had carried it the way you carry any ocean: badly, constantly, drowning by inches. But oceans had floors. She had one now. She could stand on it.

A child had called the storm. Men had aimed the child. Men had harvested what fell. Men had drawn up the exile papers and called it mercy and slept soundly after, and one of those men had gone on to wear a crown of stolen starlight and march on cities full of people who had done nothing at all.

The rage arrived quietly. That was what startled her. Not the screaming storm-rage she remembered from the edge of her suppressed power, all heat and static and loss of control. This was cold and level and precise, and it felt, for the first time in nine years, like it belonged entirely to *her*—not to the spiral on her wrist, not to the ember, not to the white room. To her.

"I stop apologizing for existing," she said. "And I start aiming."

Tam winced. "Aiming's how you got here."

"Aiming *wrong* got me here." She looked east, toward the road, toward Caldreth and the free cities and, beyond everything, the bridge where her exile began. The ember sat in the grass between them, pulsing softly, and for the first time it didn't feel like a burden or a bomb. It felt like a witness. Forty-one hearts, Ilsa's notes had said, or some number close to it—forty-one Wardens' worth of memory, waiting nine years for someone to finally hear them correctly. "They gave me a target once, Tam. It was a lie. I'd like to return the favor."

He studied her for a long moment. Then he sighed the sigh of a smuggler watching profit die in real time.

"You know," he said, poking the fire, "when I took you on, my plan was to sell the glowing rock and retire. I want it noted, for posterity, that I had a plan, and it was a *good* plan, and it involved a villa."

"Noted."

"And instead I'm going to end up helping you aim yourself at the most dangerous man on the continent, because—" he gestured vaguely at her, at the ember, at the morning, at the whole ruined state of affairs, "—because apparently I'm like this now."

"Apparently."

"Don't make it worse by being grateful. Gratitude is where it starts." He stood, shouldering his pack, and offered her a hand up, and his grin came back crooked and half-real, which from Tam was practically a vow. "Come on, storm-caller. If we're going to get ourselves killed, I want breakfast first, and I know a fence in Merrowgate who owes me money."

Emrys took his hand and rose, and the ember rode warm against her chest like a small stubborn heart, and she thought: *Merrowgate. Good. Merrowgate is where they sold their sky.*

She had a few questions for the buyers.
