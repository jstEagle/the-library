## 10. Tam's Bargain

The thing about betraying a fence, Tam Vey had learned over the years, was that you ought to do it before breakfast. Betrayals committed on an empty stomach always went sideways. A man needed bread in him to make good decisions, and Tam was about to make the worst decision of his life on nothing but cold tea and spite.

He crouched in the alley behind the Pale Court's records annex, watching two guards argue about dice. Merrowgate's surrender negotiations had made everyone sloppy. Why post a proper watch when the city had already agreed to hand over its mages like a merchant settling a debt? The guards' hearts weren't in it. Their dice were.

"Left pocket," Tam muttered to himself. "It's always the left pocket."

He'd spent three days and most of his remaining coin learning the annex's rhythms. Three days of listening to dockhands gossip about the storm-caller woman Serevan's people had pulled out of a wheatfield riot — *Emrys Quintana*, though nobody said the name above a whisper, because names like hers had a way of summoning things. Three days of telling himself he was only gathering intelligence so he could price it properly later. Sell the location of Corvane's prize prisoner back to her allies, if she had any. Sell it to Serevan's rivals, if she didn't. There was always someone willing to pay for a secret.

That was the story he'd been telling himself, anyway. It had developed holes in it around day two.

* * *

The trouble, as far as Tam could reconstruct it, had started with the fence.

Odd Ottavio ran the nicest den of stolen goods in Merrowgate, tucked behind a chandler's shop that smelled permanently of tallow and lies. Tam had brought him the ember's description secondhand — never the thing itself, Emrys had been firm about that, gripping the little coal-stone like it might bolt — and Odd had gone pale in a way Tam had never seen in thirty years of commerce.

"Do you know what that is?" Odd had whispered, wiping his hands on an apron that was already cleaner than the conversation deserved.

"A very expensive rock."

"That is a Warden heart, you beautiful idiot. That is the last uncorrupted piece of starlight in the world, and you're haggling." Odd had named a figure then. Tam had made him repeat it twice, because the first time he'd assumed he'd misheard. Enough coin to buy a ship, a crew, a new name, and a coastline far from any war. Enough to stop being what he was — a smuggler, a fixer, a man who knew every back door in three cities because the front doors had been burned down behind him long ago.

And Tam had said yes. He'd shaken on it. He'd walked out into the gray Merrowgate morning feeling lighter than he had in years, and he'd gotten exactly four streets before he'd stopped in the middle of Fishmonger's Row and stood there like a man who'd forgotten where he lived.

Because he'd thought about how she'd held it. Not like treasure. Like something alive that trusted her.

"You're getting soft," he told himself, out loud, earning a look from a fishwife. "You've known the woman a week. She stole your provisions. She called your horse stupid — and it *was* stupid, but that's not the point."

The point, he admitted, standing in the fish smell, was that he'd seen her face when she talked about the valley that exiled her. It was the face of someone carrying a weight so old they'd forgotten their shoulders were supposed to be straight. And he'd seen her face when the light-drinkers came through Merrowgate's mage quarter, and she'd grabbed his arm hard enough to bruise, and whispered, *"We have to remember them. Someone has to remember them,"* while the soldiers drank the magic out of screaming strangers like wine from a jug.

Tam Vey remembered people for a living. It was practically his trade. You couldn't move contraband through a city without knowing which widow took bribes in candles and which constable could be bought with a compliment. He remembered everyone.

He just hadn't let himself remember his own family in eleven years.

* * *

So here he was, behind the records annex, at dawn, with a stolen guard's cloak, a flask of drugged wine, and no plan beyond the first door.

"Evening, gentlemen," he said, strolling up to the dice-playing guards with the easy gait of a man delivering something official. "Relief watch. You're late for your shift change."

"We don't shift till—" one started.

"New orders since the surrender. Everything's new since the surrender. Frankly I'm surprised anyone remembers anything." He held up the flask. "Brought a toast, too. Annex commander's celebrating. Word is the inquisitor got the storm-caller talking."

Both guards straightened. Good — curiosity was a hook, and Tam had baited it well. "Talking about what?"

"Wouldn't know. Wouldn't want to." He lowered his voice conspiratorially. "But between us? They say she called the storm that killed the Wardens. Nine years ago. The one the old regime covered up." He'd pieced that together from dock gossip and Emrys's own half-sleep mutterings, and the delicious horror on the guards' faces told him it landed. "Anyway. Toast?"

They toasted. Within ten minutes both were snoring against the wall, and Tam felt briefly proud of himself, which was the last prideful feeling he would enjoy for some time.

The annex cells were beneath the building, reached by a stair that smelled of wet stone and old fear. Tam had bribed a laundry maid for the layout — the Pale Court kept its prisoners close to the records, because Lady Serevan believed a person's history was the best lock there was. You didn't need chains when you had a memory. You just needed to edit it until the prisoner couldn't imagine anywhere else to be.

Tam found her cell by sound: a low, rhythmic tapping, patient as rain. When he peered through the grate, Emrys Quintana was sitting cross-legged in the dark, tapping her finger on the floor in patterns, and her eyes had a strange ember-light in them even though — he checked — she wasn't holding the stone.

"They took it?" he whispered.

Her head snapped up. For a heartbeat she looked at him like a stranger, and Tam's stomach dropped — and then she blinked, and it was her. "Tam? How did—"

"Questions later. Escape now. Can you walk?"

"I can walk." She unfolded herself, wincing. Her wrists were ringed with pale bands, like burns. "They didn't take the ember. They tried. It wouldn't leave my cell. It sat against the door like a cat refusing a bath, and eventually Serevan decided a thing that stubborn was worth more studied than seized." Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile. "She asked me questions about the night of my exile. About people I don't remember. She knew things about that night, Tam. She knew *Corvane* was there."

"We'll discuss the nightmare details on the road. Door's locked, I assume?"

"It's locked, warded, and Serevan personally keyed it to my fear. It opens when I'm genuinely terrified and closes otherwise. It's very clever. I hate it."

"Ah," said Tam. "Well. Fortunately, terrifying you is my area of expertise." He pulled a folded paper from inside his cloak and pressed it to the grate. "I sold the ember to Odd Ottavio. Shook hands on it yesterday. Price of passage, I told myself. Strictly business."

The paper was the contract, or rather the contract's ashes — he'd burned it that morning and kept the scorched corner as a keepsake of his own stupidity. Through the grate, Emrys read the burnt scrap, then looked at him.

"You're showing me this because...?"

"Because I'm an honest criminal, and because I need you to be properly horrified, because apparently this door runs on terror and I am running short of options." He grinned, and hated that the grin shook. "You're looking at the man who just set fire to his entire future, Quintana. Ship, crew, coastline, all of it. Gone. I shook on a fortune and then unshook it, which, incidentally, is not a thing a man can survive doing twice."

The lock clicked.

They both stared at the open door.

"Huh," said Emrys.

"Don't say 'huh.' Say 'thank you, Tam, you magnificent fool.'"

"Thank you, Tam, you magnificent fool." But her voice caught on the words, and she stepped past him into the stairwell, and her hand brushed his sleeve — briefly, like she wanted it to stay longer and wouldn't allow it. "Why?"

Ah. There it was. The question he'd been dodging for three days, now standing in a dungeon stairwell with nowhere to hide.

"Up first," he said. "Answers cost extra."

* * *

They went out through the laundry — the same laundry whose maid he'd bribed, because the best exits are the ones you've already paid for — and through the chandler's lane, and along the canal where the morning fog still hung thick enough to swallow two fugitives whole. Emrys moved well for a woman who'd spent days in a cell, and she kept the ember cupped in her palm like a candle flame she was sheltering from wind. It glowed faintly now, Tam noticed. It hadn't glowed like that before the Pale Court got hold of her.

Whatever Serevan had done down there, it hadn't been only interrogation.

By the time they reached the derelict boathouse Tam kept on the canal's dead-end reach — technically owned by a fictional oyster merchant named Master Pell, who was Tam's finest creation — the fog was lifting, and there was no more road to postpone the question down.

He lit a stub of candle, dug out the last of his decent tea, and put the kettle on, because some conversations required props.

"Eleven years ago," he said, watching the water instead of her, "there was a town called Brindlefen. Up in the hill country, before Corvane, back when the old regime still ruled and the Wardens still watched the sky. My family kept a ferry there. Ma, Da, my sister Wren, my grandmother who cheated at every card game ever invented." He smiled at the kettle. "Grandmother once cheated *solitaire*. We never figured out how."

Emrys said nothing. She was good at that, he was learning. She listened like listening was a form of holding onto someone.

"The old regime was fighting the last of its magic wars. Border skirmishes, mostly. Storm-caller units, Warden detachments, the usual horrors that respectable histories later call 'the Unrest.'" His voice went flat and careful, the way he spoke when he was handling something fragile. "One night a battle came through the valley above Brindlefen. Nobody in town picked a side. We were ferrymen. Our politics began and ended with whether the water was high or low. But the old regime had these weapons, see — people, really. Children, some of them. Trained up to call weather like soldiers call artillery. And one of those weapons was aimed at the hills that night, and the storm came down the valley instead of staying up where it belonged, and it hit the river, and the river rose, and..."

He turned the kettle off without pouring. The tea had seemed important a minute ago. Now it seemed like a thing other people did.

"My grandmother drowned cheating at cards," he said. "Wren was fourteen. My parents went with the ferry itself, trying to hold it against the flood, because that was the family trade and they died loyal to it. I was in Merrowgate buying rope. That was my job — I'd run ahead to buy rope. Rope, Tam Vey, that was going to make us rich." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I've been buying rope ever since. Every deal, every cargo, every clever exit — I keep thinking this shipment will be the one that buys back what the water took. Which is insane, obviously. Dead's dead. Coin doesn't reach that far down."

"But the ember does," Emrys said quietly.

"The ember might." He finally looked at her. Her face was lit unevenly by the coal-glow in her palm, and there was something in her expression he couldn't read — grief, recognition, maybe both wearing each other's coats. "You said it holds memories. Living ones. Whole nights, whole people. You said you *saw* things in it — storms, faces, voices." He swallowed. "If a stone can carry the night my family died — if somewhere in all that starlight there's a record of Brindlefen, of the ferry, of a girl named Wren who deserved better than a line in an archive — then it's not a rock, Emrys. It's the last door back to them. In a manner of speaking. The only manner there is."

"And that's why you broke me out." She said it gently, without judgment, which somehow made it harder. "Not for me. For the door."

"For the door," Tam agreed. "Strictly business. You're a means to an end. I'd thank you not to get sentimental about it."

"You burned your contract with Odd."

"The ink smudged."

"You're grinning like a man walking to his own funeral and enjoying the procession."

"I'm a professional." He sat down heavily on a crate that smelled of tar and old summers. "Look. Here's the truth, since you'll drag it out of me anyway. I planned to sell you out. Day one, that was the plan. Then I watched those light-drinkers work the mage quarter, and I watched you — not fighting, not weeping, just *remembering*, saying someone had to — and I thought, there's a person who knows what it costs when the world decides some lives are inventory. And I thought, I have spent eleven years making sure nothing matters enough to lose again, and it has kept me safe and it has kept me small, and I am so tired, Emrys. I am tired all the way down."

The boathouse was quiet except for the canal lapping at the pilings and, faintly, the ember humming — an actual hum, low and warm, like a hearth two rooms away.

"So that's the bargain," Tam said. "I get you south. Past the patrols, past the bells, wherever your map says the Wardens kept their secrets. In exchange, when we find whatever vault or shrine or hole-in-the-earth holds their memories — you let me look for Brindlefen. One look. That's all. I'm not asking to bring anyone back. I know better. I'm asking to *see* them one more time. To know they were real, and that somebody besides me remembers the shape of them." His voice cracked on the last word and he pretended it hadn't. "And after that — do what you like with the thing. Burn it, crown a king, throw it in the sea. I'll help. That part was always true, I think. I just needed to dress it up as greed so I could say it out loud."

Emrys crossed the boathouse and sat beside him on the crate. For a while neither spoke, and the fog outside burned away in the strengthening sun, and the ember hummed its low hearth-song between them.

"Brindlefen," she said at last. "I dreamed about a river town. Down in the ember-memories, the night they interrogated me. A flood. A ferry. I thought it was just old war, background noise." She turned the stone in her fingers. "Maybe it wasn't noise, Tam. Maybe it was waiting for you to ask."

Tam discovered, somewhat to his alarm, that his eyes were burning, and blamed the candle smoke, and blew the candle out so no one could check.

"Well," he managed. "Then we'd best go find this vault before Serevan finds us. Because she will, you understand. I burned that contract, but Odd doesn't burn things — he files them. By tomorrow noon, half of Merrowgate's underworld will know a storm-caller slipped the Pale Court, and the other half will know who helped her."

"Where do we go?"

Tam pulled Ilsa's map from his coat — borrowed, copied, and annotated with three smuggling routes the original's author had surely never intended — and spread it on his knee. His finger traced south along the coast road, past the burn scars on the parchment where villages had been struck through, down toward a blank stretch of shoreline marked with a single faded symbol: a circle of seven dots, like stars arranged around an absence.

"There," he said. "An old friend used to land contraband on that beach. Said the cliffs there hummed at night. Thought he was mad." He looked up at Emrys, and despite everything — the burned future, the dead waiting in the stone, the inquisitor who would be hunting them by dusk — he found himself smiling. "Course, that was before I learned the sky can fall and the dead can sing. South, Quintana. We're going south, and we're going to rob a grave of its memories, and if we're very lucky, nobody will write a ballad about it."

The ember pulsed once in Emrys's palm — bright, brief, almost eager — and outside the boathouse, on the far side of the canal, a bell began to ring.

Not one of Corvane's alarm-bells. Worse. The harbor bell. The one that rang only when the Pale Court's riders mustered.

Tam sighed and stood, cracking his knuckles. "Well. No time like the present to ruin my life completely. Tell me you can swim, because Master Pell's boat leaves in five minutes, and I warn you now — she handles like a rumor."
