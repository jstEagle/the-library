## 11. Salt and Cinders

The sea smelled like the end of the world.

That was unfair, Emrys supposed. The sea smelled like salt and rot and cold iron, the way it probably always had. It was everything else—the smoke plume behind them where Merrowgate had been, the absence of stars overhead, the wound in Tam's shoulder that kept needing new bandages—that made it feel apocalyptic.

"Left," Tam said from his seat on a driftwood log, pointing at her stance with his good arm. "You're leaning left again."

"I'm not leaning left."

"You're leaning left like a ship taking water. Whatever you're doing with that rock in your hand, you're doing it sideways."

Emrys looked down at herself. She was, in fact, leaning left. She straightened, scowling at the ember cupped in her palms, as if it were personally responsible. In fairness, it might have been. The ember sat there, warm and patient and faintly smug, the way it always did when she tried to make it do something and it declined.

"It's not a rock," she said. "It's a heart. A dead woman's heart. Show some respect."

"I'm showing plenty of respect. I'm showing respect from six feet away, which is the correct distance for a dead woman's heart." Tam shifted, winced, and resettled against a boulder with the theatrical care of a man who intended to milk that shoulder for sympathy until the end of his natural life. "Also, you've been talking to it for three days. Dead women don't answer, Emrys. That's the whole trouble with them."

She didn't tell him that sometimes they did.

* * *

The coast road south of Merrowgate was all shale and gull grass, winding along cliffs where the wind came off the water hard enough to lean against. They walked it by day and hid by night, because Serevan's light-drinkers patrolled the high road inland, and because Emrys's storm over the burning city had announced her to every listening ear between the coast and Corvane's throne. She'd called lightning out of an empty sky. Somewhere behind them, an inquisitor with stolen starlight in her veins was following the echo of it like a hound on blood.

So: training. That was Ilsa's word, dredged up from memory. *You don't fight what you are, girl. You drill it. You put it through its paces every morning until it stops being a wild thing and starts being a working animal.* Ilsa had said that about her temper, once, but it applied just as well to a storm-caller carrying the last uncorrupted Warden heart across a country full of people who wanted to crack it open and drink it.

The problem was that nobody alive knew how Wardens actually worked. The order was nine years dead—killed, she now understood with a grief that arrived fresh every morning like a bill collector, by her own nine-year-old hands—and their craft had died with them. All she had was the ember itself, and the ember's memories, which came to her the way weather came: without permission, without warning, and usually at inconvenient hours.

Three nights ago she'd been dreaming of nothing in particular when the ember had shown her a harvest festival in a city whose name she didn't know, seen through the eyes of a Warden named Sefa who had died four hundred years before Emrys was born. Sefa had been old and tired and had carried forty years of other people's memories in her chest, and the dream-memory had included—not explained, never explained; the ember showed rather than told—a practice. A shape of attention. When the memories pressed too close, Sefa would... widen. Not push them away. Widen around them, the way the sky widens around clouds, holding them without gripping, letting them pass through.

Emrys had woken with tears on her face that belonged to a woman dead four centuries, and had thought, with the clarity of three in the morning: *I can learn this.*

Which was how she'd come to be standing on a cliff at dawn, holding the ember at arm's length, attempting to widen around it while a smuggler with a hole in his shoulder offered unsolicited commentary.

"Again," she muttered, and reached.

The ember opened its memories the way a door opens onto wind. This time it was rain on a battlefield, and a boy soldier's terror, and the Warden Vessal walking the field afterward gathering the dead men's last thoughts like a gleaner after harvest, and—

Emrys gripped. She felt herself grip, felt the widening collapse into a fist, and the battlefield memory seized her whole: the mud, the screaming, the copper taste, the terrible intimacy of a stranger's dying. Her knees hit the shale. The world went narrow and hot.

"*Breathe*—" Tam's voice, distant. "Emrys. Breathe or I swear I'll—"

She breathed. She widened. Badly, clumsily, the way a drowning person widens around water—but she widened, and the memory loosened its teeth, and after a long gray moment she was kneeling on the coast road with salt wind in her hair and no mud anywhere except in her chest, where some of it would stay forever.

"That," Tam said, hauling her upright with his one good arm and considerably more strength than he advertised, "is the last time we do that near a cliff edge."

"It's working." Her voice came out scraped. "It's slow, but it's working. Before, the memories took me for hours. That one only had me for a minute."

"A minute is plenty of time to walk off a cliff."

"Noted." She sat down hard beside him and looked at the ember, which pulsed once, gently, almost apologetic. Or maybe reproachful. Reading a dead woman's heart was like reading any letter—you brought your own tone to the words. "Sefa did this for forty years. Held hundreds of lives inside hers and stayed herself. If she could learn it, I can."

"Sefa," Tam said, "was presumably not learning it while hunted by star-drinking inquisitors with a smuggler for a nursemaid."

"No," Emrys admitted. "But she probably also didn't have anyone telling her she was listing to port the entire time. So it evens out."

Tam grinned despite himself—the quick, crooked grin that he deployed like a coin, and that lately he'd stopped bothering to charge for—and settled back against the boulder. After a while he said, quieter, "It had you. Just now. Where'd you go?"

"A battlefield. Four hundred years ago. A man named Vessal collected the dying soldiers' last memories so their families could have them back." She turned the ember in her fingers. Its warmth soaked into her palm, steady as a sleeping animal. "He was tired. He was so tired. But he wasn't bitter. He said—" she searched for it, the phrase surfacing like a fish—"he said the memories weren't a burden you carry. They were passengers. You're the boat, not the hold."

Tam considered this. "Boats sink."

"Yes," Emrys said. "Some of them do."

He was quiet a moment longer, and then he pulled Ilsa's map out of his coat, because that was Tam's way with heavy things: set them down gently, then change the subject before they could roll back on top of you. "All right, boat. Show me where we're going."

* * *

Ilsa's map was a masterwork of deception, which made sense, because Ilsa had drawn it for a girl who might be searched. To a guard's eye it was a shepherd's chart of the Hollow Valley hills, all springs and sheep tracks. But Ilsa had taught Emrys to read maps the way she'd taught her everything else—sideways, patiently, with a stick in the dirt—and folded into the coastline were lines that only appeared if you held the paper against low sun: a road that followed no river, ending at a cove marked with a symbol Emrys hadn't recognized until the ember had shown her Sefa's hands drawing it, four hundred years ago, in a ledger of vaults.

A circle with a vertical line. A star, extinguished. *Here lies one who keeps quiet.*

"The vault's real," Emrys said, for maybe the tenth time, tracing the cove on the map. South of them, past the fishing village of Brackwater, past the salt flats, a place called the Gullet—a slot of a bay between black cliffs where the tide ran wrong. "Sefa recorded it. The Wardens kept repositories. Records, relics, hearts."

"Hearts," Tam repeated flatly.

"Memory-hearts. Like the ember. There might be others." She hesitated. "There might be knowledge. About what the ember is. What Corvane's harvesting actually does. What it *can't* do." Because that was the question underneath all her questions, the one she practiced widening around instead of gripping: *could it crown a tyrant? Could someone use it? Could I?*

"You want to know if your cargo is a weapon," Tam said, watching her with those too-quick eyes of his.

"I want to know what I'm carrying."

"You're carrying the last honest thing in a dishonest kingdom, and everyone from here to the capital wants to take it away from you and do something with it. That's what you're carrying." He tapped the map. "And now you want to walk us both into a hole in a cliff to find out if it's also a bomb. Fine. I've made worse decisions for less noble reasons. Usually involving dice."

"You don't have to come."

"I'm aware." He said it lightly, but something under it wasn't light at all, and Emrys had learned enough about Tam Vey to know that the lightness *was* the weight, worn as armor. He'd told her about his family on the second night out of Merrowgate, briefly, in the dark, facing away from her—the regime's magic wars, the village that had been a name and then wasn't, the ember as the last thing that remembered them. He hadn't mentioned it since, and she hadn't made him, and between them had grown the careful silence that two damaged people build when they've decided the other one's damage matters.

"Brackwater by tomorrow dusk," she said instead. "The Gullet the day after. We move fast and we don't touch our magic."

"Your magic," Tam corrected. "I don't have any. I have a knife, a winning smile, and a shoulder that's going to fester if we don't find a healer soon, which is why I'm voting for Brackwater whether it's wise or not."

* * *

Brackwater was twenty houses and a chapel and a smell of fish that Tam claimed was an acquired taste and Emrys suspected was simply punishment. But it had a healer—an ancient woman named Mother Quill who rebandaged Tam's shoulder with the tenderness of a farrier and charged them two silver and a story.

"A story?" Tam said.

"News travels slow here. Tell me something true about the war."

Tam glanced at Emrys. Something passed between them—the ongoing negotiation about how much truth was safe—and then, to her surprise, Tam leaned forward and told Mother Quill about Merrowgate. Not the burning. The surrender negotiation first, the councilmen in their good coats shaking hands with Corvane's envoys while mages were already being culled in the harbor district; the way the city had sold its sky a piece at a time and called each piece prudence. He told it well, without embellishment, which was somehow worse than embellishment.

Mother Quill listened with her hands folded. When he finished she said, "My sister lived in Merrowgate," and that was all, and she gave them bread for the road and wouldn't hear of more silver.

They slept that night in the chapel loft, and Emrys woke at moonset with the ember humming against her sternum and Sefa's voice already rising in her like tide.

This time she was ready.

She sat up, careful of the floorboards, and widened. It was easier in the dark, alone, with no audience and no cliff. The memory unfolded: Sefa as a young Warden, standing before the extinguished-star vault in the Gullet, receiving instruction from a teacher whose face the ember had worn smooth with time. *The heart remembers what the mind forgives,* the teacher was saying. *When the memories come, do not sort them. Do not rank them. You are not their judge. You are their keeper. A keeper opens the door every day and lets the air through, or the house goes stale and the ghosts grow bold.*

Young Sefa had asked—her voice bright with youth—*And if I fail? If one day I open the door and can't close it?*

The teacher had laughed. *Then you will be in excellent company. Every Warden who ever lived failed exactly that way, at least once. We are not strong instead of breakable, child. We are strong because we know precisely how breakable we are, and plan accordingly.*

Emrys opened her eyes in the dark loft. Below her, Tam slept with his bandaged shoulder toward the wall and his knife under his hand even in sleep, and the ember glowed faintly against her skin, and she thought: *plan accordingly.*

She'd spent nine years planning around her power—suppressing it, denying it, treating it as the crime itself rather than the instrument of one. And she'd spent three weeks since the wards fell treating the ember the same way, as a burden to endure rather than a discipline to learn. But guilt wasn't a plan. Guilt was just gripping, wearing a different coat.

Tomorrow they'd reach the Gullet. Tomorrow there might be answers—about the ember, about Corvane's harvest, about what a storm-child aimed at the Wardens could possibly owe the order she'd destroyed. But tonight, she could keep the door open. Tonight she could sit with Vessal's battlefield and Sefa's festival and all the other passenger-memories, and let the air through, and not drown.

She widened around the ember's warmth, and it hummed, and for the first time since the stars went out, the hum sounded less like hunger and more like company.

* * *

They reached the Gullet at midmorning on the third day, and Emrys stopped dead on the cliff path above it.

The bay was a blade-cut between walls of black stone, the water inside running green and slow against the tide's logic, churning where it shouldn't churn and lying flat where it should boil. And carved into the seaward cliff, forty feet above the waterline and visible only from the exact angle where they stood, was the sign: a circle with a vertical line, a star extinguished. Weathered nearly smooth, older than anything else in sight.

"There's no path down," Tam said, peering over the edge with the enthusiasm of a cat considering a bath.

"There doesn't need to be." Emrys lifted her hand, and the ember answered, warming, pulsing, and below them—deep in the cliff face, behind stone that had kept quiet for four hundred years—something answered back. A light kindled behind the rock like a lantern behind a shutter. Then another. Then a dozen, a constellation waking in the stone, tracing a stair that spiraled down through the cliff itself, each step glowing softly, waiting.

The wind off the sea pushed at her back, impatient. Behind them, somewhere far up the coast road, she imagined she could almost hear the ring of Serevan's bells.

"They kept the lights burning," Emrys said quietly. "All these years. Waiting for a Warden to come home."

"Or," Tam said, "for anyone at all, which is how we end up in stories, usually badly." He sighed, hitched his pack, and started down toward the glowing stair. "Well. Come on, boat. Let's go meet your ghosts."

The steps held their warmth under Emrys's boots all the way down, and the sea closed overhead like a held breath, and the vault of quiet stars waited below with its doors still lit—as if, all this time, it had known someone was coming.

As if it had known *who*.
