## 12. The Vault of Quiet Stars

The door in the cliff face had been waiting for them for three hundred years, and it did not seem particularly pleased to see them.

It was a slab of grey stone no different from the rock around it, except that when Emrys pressed her palm against it—following the last instruction on Ilsa's map, *where the mountain keeps its heartbeat*—she felt a pulse answer. Slow. Patient. Like something sleeping with one eye open.

"Charming," Tam said behind her. "A door with a pulse. Nothing ominous about that at all."

"It's Warden work," she said. "The whole mountain's probably alive in some small way. Lichen, moss, things like that."

"Things like that," he repeated. "You say that the way other people say *it's only a spider*."

She smiled despite herself. Nine days on the coast road, and they had developed a language of small jokes the way castaways develop rafts—out of necessity, and not entirely seaworthy. Tam Vey could make humor out of anything: bad weather, worse bread, the light-drinkers' patrols they'd doubled back to avoid twice. It was armor, she knew. She'd seen what was under it once, briefly, on the night he'd burned his own safe future to pull her out of Merrowgate. She hadn't mentioned it since. Neither had he.

The stone ground aside without a sound, which somehow made it worse. A door that heavy should have complained.

Cold air breathed out of the dark, carrying a smell like snow and old paper and, faintly, woodsmoke. The ember against Emrys's sternum—she carried it now in a sling of woven wool against her skin, because it slept easier there, and because when it dreamed, she preferred to know about it—warmed suddenly, like a dog hearing a familiar footstep in the hall.

"Oh," Emrys said softly. "Oh, we're expected."

* * *

The vault was not what she'd imagined, and she'd imagined quite a lot during the climb: treasure, certainly, or weapons, or rows of crystallized starlight glowing blue-white in the dark. What it actually was, was a library.

A vast one. A cathedral of one. Shelves rose into darkness beyond the reach of Tam's lantern, carved directly from the living rock, and every shelf was full—not with books, exactly, but with hearts.

Emrys understood this immediately and completely, the way you understand a blow before the pain arrives. Each object on those shelves was a stone the size of a fist, dull grey, unremarkable—except that each one held, suspended inside it like a fly in amber, a spark. Some sparks were bright. Most were dim. A few shelves held stones that were simply empty, hollowed-out husks, and looking at those made her throat close.

"They're not all dead," she whispered. "Tam. They're not all—"

"I see them." His voice had lost its levity entirely. He stood very still, the way he did when he was frightened and didn't want anyone pricing that information into their dealings with him. "Are they... aware?"

The ember pulsed against her chest. And through it—not words, nothing so kind as words—came a sensation like a great hall full of sleepers turning over. Listening.

"Some of them," Emrys said. "I think some of them are dreaming. The rest are..." She touched one of the dim stones, gently, the way you touch a stranger's hand at a funeral. Cold. But underneath the cold, a rhythm. "Resting. Waiting to be woken or buried. I don't know which is kinder."

"Well." Tam set down his pack and rubbed his hands together with forced briskness. "When one breaks into a tomb, one likes to know if the occupants will be joining the conversation. Lead on, storm-caller. There's bound to be an index somewhere. Libraries always have indexes. It's their one redeeming feature."

There was, in fact, an index. In the center of the vault stood a reading table of black stone, and above it floated a single point of light—the only true starlight in the room, Emrys realized, a captive little sun no bigger than a pearl, patient as everything else in this place. Beneath it lay a ledger bound in grey leather, and when Emrys opened it, the writing shifted under her eyes until she could read it.

*Ilsa Quintana's granddaughter* —no. She blinked and looked again. *Emrys. Daughter of the storm-line. If you have come this far, the sky has already failed.*

She read the first page aloud, quietly, while Tam pretended not to hover and hovered.

*We are the Ember Wardens. We do not rule; we remember. Our hearts hold the memory of the First Burning, so that no power in this world—crown, creed, or catastrophe—may rewrite what was done, or claim it never happened. Memory is the only wall tyrants cannot tunnel under, though many have tried, and several are trying still.*

"Cheerful lot," Tam murmured. "Go on. I want to hear about the tyrants."

She read on. Page after page, and the gentle warmth of the place began to curdle.

The Wardens had recorded everything. The old regime's magic wars. The child-weapons—*vessels*, the ledger called them, and Emrys's stomach turned at how tidy the word was. The harvest of the first stars, centuries ago, and why it had been forbidden ever since: because starlight taken by force did not stay obedient. Because it *hungered*. Because a man who drank enough of it stopped being entirely a man and started being a hunger wearing a man's ambitions as a coat.

And then, near the end, in a hand shakier than the rest:

*If the last heart remains uncorrupted—if it has been carried by one who chose memory over power—it holds two endings. Crowned upon a willing brow, it can wake the sleeping hearts and raise a new council of Wardens. A king or queen of quiet stars, sworn to remember. Or, fed to its own fire, it will unmake every stolen spark in the world—all the harvested starlight, torn free and returned—and take the bearer's gift with it.*

"The bearer's gift," Tam repeated slowly. "Meaning..."

"Meaning whoever does it loses whatever magic they had." Emrys heard her own voice from very far away. "All of it. Permanently."

He was quiet for a moment. Then, carefully casual, in the tone of a man discussing cargo manifests: "Well, that's convenient for me. I haven't got any magic. Terrible at it. Failed the aptitude whatever-it-is at age nine. So obviously I should carry it."

"You're not carrying it."

"I'm just saying, if the job description is *must be magically useless*—"

"Tam."

"—then I am uniquely qualified, is all I'm saying—"

"*Tam.*"

He stopped. Looked at her. And for once he didn't dress the look up in anything.

"You've already decided," he said. "Haven't you. You've known since you read the second ending."

Emrys looked down at the ledger. "I decided eleven days ago," she said, "in a wheatfield, when I saw what Corvane's people do to mages who won't drink. I just hadn't found the paperwork for it yet." She tried a smile. It came out crooked. "I'm a great one for finding the paperwork late."

"That's a terrible joke."

"I know."

"Don't make it again."

"No promises."

But his hand found hers across the table anyway, brief and warm, and neither of them said anything else about it, because some bargains are signed in silence.

* * *

They made camp in the vault's antechamber, because the alternative was the open slope where Serevan's patrols ranged, and because—though Emrys didn't say this aloud—the sleeping hearts seemed to rest easier with her nearby. She could feel it when she drifted toward sleep herself: the great slow attention of the place settling around her like a blanket pulled up by someone else's hands.

In the morning, she read.

She read for hours while Tam inventoryed the antechamber's stores—ancient, sealed jars of waybread that were somehow still good, because Warden craft kept what it promised—and what she read remade her understanding of everything she carried, piece by piece, like a house being taken apart to see whether it would stand.

The ember was the last uncorrupted heart. Not merely the last heart—the last *uncorrupted* one. The ledger described what Corvane had done, or rather what his masters had begun and he had inherited: a harvesting that didn't just steal starlight but *edited* it. Hearts taken by force could be rewritten. Memories could be cut and spliced like tapestry, threads removed, new patterns inserted, until even the heart itself believed the false version. That was how he controlled the harvested power. That was how he controlled his light-drinkers. That was how he controlled—

Emrys set the ledger down.

That was how he controlled everyone.

"Tam," she said. Her voice came out strange. "The night of my exile. The storm. I've been assuming my memory failed because I was a child, or because I broke my own mind to survive what I'd done. But what if it didn't fail?" She turned the ledger toward him, to a passage she'd read four times now. "*An edited vessel may be given any past its handlers wish it to believe.* What if my memory wasn't broken, Tam? What if it was *arranged*?"

He came and sat beside her and read it, lips moving slightly, which he'd deny forever. When he finished, he was silent long enough that the little captive star above the table hummed audibly in the quiet.

"Then the thing you've spent nine years hating yourself for," he said at last, "might not even be the real crime. Just the receipt for it." He exhaled through his teeth. "That's either the cruelest thing I've ever heard or the kindest, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Both," Emrys said. "I think it's both."

The ember stirred against her chest—not painfully, but insistently, the way a sleeper tugs a blanket. And through the bond that had grown between them over these weeks, half-teacher and half-companion, came something new: not a memory, but a *warning*. An image, sharp and simple. Grey uniforms. Lanterns moving along the cliff path below. The particular wrongness of light-drinkers walking under a starless sky, drinking the darkness itself for warmth.

Emrys was on her feet before she'd fully understood what she'd seen.

"TAM."

He was up almost as fast, knife already in his hand, which she'd learned was less a weapon and more a comfort object. "Patrols?"

"Worse. Coming up the switchbacks. A lot of them." She pressed her hand flat over the ember. "And they're not searching for us. They're searching for *this*. They've known about the vault all along—they just couldn't open it."

Below them, faint through the mountain's skin, came a sound Emrys knew from her nightmares: the thin, sweet shriek of alarm-bells being rung by hands that had never needed to ring them honestly. And beneath the bells, a voice amplified by stolen starlight, smooth as poured cream, echoing up the cliff face.

"Emrys Quintana." Lady Serevan's voice. Patient. Almost kind. "You're in a library. How fitting. I've come about a book too—one with your name in it. Come out, and we'll discuss the ending. Refuse, and I'm afraid we'll write it ourselves."

Tam glanced at the vault door, then at the ledger, then at Emrys, and produced the ghost of a grin. "So," he said. "Do we tell her the book's overdue, or shall I improvise?"

Emrys put her hand against the living stone and felt the whole mountain gather itself around her like a held breath—a crown in one pocket, a funeral pyre in the other, and an army at the door.
