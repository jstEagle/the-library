## 13. What Serevan Wants

Lady Serevan kept her memories the way other people kept jewels: catalogued, polished, and never worn where they might be stolen.

She rose before dawn in the inquisitor's pavilion, as she always did, and performed the rite of recollection. It was a small thing—a candle, a bowl of water, three minutes of absolute silence in which she named what she was certain of. *I am Serevan Vail. I was born in the ash-country east of the Corvane Marches. When I was nine years old, my village burned, and Vael Corvane carried me out of the fire.*

Every morning, the same certainty. Every morning, it settled over her like a coat that fit.

She had been doing this for eleven years, though she did not think of it as a ritual. She thought of it as discipline. Memory was the first casualty of magic—everyone knew that—and an inquisitor who could not trust her own past was worse than useless. So she had built hers like a fortress: load-bearing certainties, walls of detail, a moat of repetition. The fire. The arm around her shoulders, smelling of smoke and leather. The voice saying, *I have you. No one will ever do to you what was done tonight.*

No one ever had.

She dressed, pinned the pale silver badge of the Pale Court at her collar, and went to see about the storm-caller.

* * *

The prisoner had not slept. Good. Sleep let people reorganize their lies, and Emrys Quintana's lies were already load-bearing enough for two.

Serevan entered the tent with two cups of tea and no chains, which was its own kind of message. She set one cup within the woman's reach and took the camp stool across from her, arranging her grey traveling cloak with the care of someone who believed that orderliness was a moral position.

"You didn't touch the tea," Serevan observed.

"I've read about you," Emrys said. Her voice was hoarse but level. "You drink light. You take it from mages. I'd rather not give you anything else for free."

"Ah." Serevan sipped her own tea. "The rumors. May I tell you what we actually do? It's less dramatic and considerably more efficient."

Emrys said nothing, which Serevan had learned to interpret as *proceed*.

"The Wardens were custodians," she said. "They held starlight the way a granary holds grain—for the common good, rationed against famine. When they fell, all that stored power fell with them, scattered, leaking, burning fields and boiling rivers wherever it pooled. My lord Corvane gathered it. He is not drinking the sky, whatever your smuggler friends say. He is *warehousing* it. And warehousing requires collectors who can survive proximity to the stores." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "That is all a light-drinker is. A clerk with a high tolerance."

"That's a very tidy way to describe what happened at Merrowgate," Emrys said quietly. "I walked through the culling grounds. The mages you 'collected' weren't clerks afterward. They were empty."

Serevan felt the familiar flicker of discomfort—the one she always filed under *the cost of necessary work* and moved past quickly, before it could develop into something with furniture.

"Grief makes everything look like theft," she said. "You of all people should know that. You burned a Warden council to the ground when you were barely more than a child."

Something crossed Emrys's face—too fast for Serevan to catalogue it, and she prided herself on cataloguing everything. Fear? No. Recognition. As if Serevan had said a word in a language the woman hadn't heard since childhood.

"You keep saying that," Emrys said slowly. "The council. Everyone keeps saying it like I should remember."

"Do you?"

"No."

Serevan leaned forward despite herself. In eleven years of interrogations, this was the answer she had wanted from no one so badly.

"Then we are alike," she said, before her discipline could stop her.

* * *

Afterward she told herself the admission had been tactical. Rapport loosened tongues; every inquisitor knew that. But walking back through the camp in the grey hour before full light, past soldiers striking tents and oxen complaining about the mud, she found her hand pressed flat against her sternum as if holding a door shut.

Because there it was again. The seam.

It lived just behind her memory of the fire. She had found it years ago during her training, when the Pale Court's mind-healers had taught her to walk the corridors of her own past looking for tampering. They had all done it—every officer of the Court—because Corvane's enemies used memory-work, and a compromised inquisitor was a loaded weapon pointed the wrong way.

She had walked her corridors and found them clean. Almost.

There was a door behind the fire. Not locked—she could open it any time she wished. But when she opened it, there was nothing on the other side except more fire, perfectly preserved, down to the sound of the roof beams and the smell of her mother's stew still cooking in a house that was already dying. It was too perfect. Real memory frayed at the edges. This one gleamed.

And beneath it, if she lay very still at night and listened, there was a sound like a room where someone had once cried for a very long time.

She had reported the anomaly once, early on, to her teacher in the Court. Master Ondrell had patted her hand and smiled his gentle smile and said, *"My dear, everyone's childhood burns. The question is whether you'll let it make you useful or merely sad."*

She had chosen useful. She chose it every morning at the water bowl.

But lately—since the stars went out, since the harvest had begun running hot and strange through the Court's veins—useful had started to feel like a word other people had put in her mouth.

* * *

Corvane summoned her at midday, which meant he wanted something.

He received her in a captured farmhouse whose owner had, presumably, been given generous terms elsewhere. He stood at the window with his back to her, watching his army assemble on the plain below, and Serevan waited, because waiting was also a discipline, and because in eleven years she had learned that Vael Corvane's silences were instruments he expected others to play.

"They found the vault," he said finally. "Or nearly. Serevan, my right hand. Tell me what you know of the Ember Wardens' hearts."

"Hearts?" She had prepared briefings on Warden architecture, Warden supply lines, Warden sympathizers among the free cities. Hearts had not appeared in any ledger.

"The records speak of them obliquely. The last uncorrupted heart of the old order." He turned. He was not a handsome man anymore—the harvested light aged him strangely, burning him smooth, like sea glass—but his eyes still did the thing they had always done, which was to make you feel that being seen by him was a promotion. "If it falls into the hands of the free cities, they will crown a king with it. If it comes to me, I can stabilize the harvest. End the leakages. Make the power safe." He smiled. "You believe in safe things, don't you, Serevan?"

"I believe in ordered things," she said. "Safe is a rumor order tells itself."

He laughed, genuinely, and she felt the old warmth move through her like sun through a window. Eleven years of that warmth. Eleven years of being the one officer he spoke to plainly.

"Find the Quintana woman," he said. "Bring me the ember. And Serevan—" he paused, and his voice did something soft and terrible, "—when it's done, I will show you the truth of the night of your fire. All of it. You deserve to know exactly how you were saved."

Her heart did a slow, traitorous lurch.

"You've offered me that before," she said carefully.

"And I've never delivered, because the moment wasn't right. The memory is delicate. It requires—" he gestured vaguely at the sky, at the absent stars, at everything he was eating, "—resources. The ember is the resource. Bring it to me, and I will open that door behind your fire myself. Whatever is in the dark, you'll finally see it by daylight. Isn't that what you want?"

It was. It was the only want she permitted herself, the small crooked want under all the orderly ones: *to see the whole room.* She had spent eleven years polishing a memory with a hole behind it, telling herself the hole was nothing, and here was the one man alive offering her the missing wall.

"Yes," she said.

"Then go," said Vael Corvane, her rescuer, her savior, the man who had carried her out of a fire she could not stop remembering too well. "And bring me the heart of the Wardens. Bring me the ember."

* * *

That night she sat with her candle and her bowl of water and did not perform the rite.

Instead she opened the door behind the fire, and stood in the perfect memory, and made herself notice, for the first time in eleven years, what was wrong with it.

She was nine years old in the memory. Nine-year-olds are heavy, awkward cargo; they scream, they claw, they cannot run. But in the memory she was weightless against Corvane's shoulder, calm as a doll. And the arm around her—it was strong, certainly, but the sleeve was wrong. She remembered the smell of smoke and leather, yes. But underneath the smoke, if she let herself truly listen, the memory smelled of *ozone*. Of storms. Of a power she had no business associating with the night her village died.

And there was one more thing, the thing that made her set down the candle with a steady hand while her pulse hammered like a trapped bird:

In the memory, the fire was behind her. Always behind her, whichever way she looked. She had checked the geometry a hundred times in a hundred recitations, and the fire never moved, because it was painted on the inside of the door.

Which meant the door did not lead to the night of the fire.

It led away from it.

Serevan sat in the dark of her tent, the candle guttering, and allowed herself—just once, briefly, like tasting a poison to identify it—the thought she had forbidden for eleven years:

*What if he didn't carry me out of the fire? What if he carried me into it? What if I am not the rescued child of Vael Corvane, but the first thing he ever took?*

The thought passed through her, and left a silence where eleven years of certainty had been.

She thought of the storm-caller in her tent, hoarse and level, saying *everyone keeps saying it like I should remember.* Two women, then, carrying edited pasts through the same war. One of them had an ember that was said to hold true memory—uncorrupted, undiluted, the last honest record of the old world.

Serevan had told herself she wanted the ember for the harvest. For order. For the stabilization of stolen starlight and the safety of the land. Those reasons were real. They would hold her weight.

But underneath, in the crying-room behind the door, there was another reason, small and crooked and human:

She wanted to stand in front of that living memory the way you stand before a mirror after years of trusting portraits—and finally learn her own face.

At dawn she gave the order to march on the Ashwood, where a storm-caller and a thief were moving south with the last heart of the Wardens.

"Take Quintana alive," she told her light-drinkers. "The woman remembers a night I've forgotten. Between us, we may yet piece together the truth."

And privately, in the place she did not report to anyone, she added the rest: *whatever it costs him.*
