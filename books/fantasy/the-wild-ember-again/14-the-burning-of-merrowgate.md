## 14. The Burning of Merrowgate

They saw the smoke from three miles out on the coast road, and Tam said nothing for a full minute, which was how Emrys knew it was bad.

Tam Vey could talk through anything. He'd narrated their escape from Merrowgate's lower quarter with a running commentary on the quality of the gutters. He'd made jokes while Serevan's light-drinkers searched the harbor district door to door, jokes so bad that Emrys had nearly surrendered herself just to make him stop. But now he stood in the road with his hands loose at his sides and his face turned toward the horizon, where a brown-gray column rose off the city like a second, uglier sky.

"Wind's wrong," he said finally.

"It is?"

"For sailing. For everything." He squinted. "It's blowing the smoke inland. Someone wants them to breathe it."

Emrys felt the ember shift against her sternum, where she wore it sewn into her coat now, warm as a sleeping bird. It had been warmer all day. She'd told herself it was the exercise, the climbing, the long miles since dawn. She was getting very good at telling herself things.

"Corvane promised clemency if they surrendered," she said.

"He promised *terms*." Tam spat, which he only did when frightened. "There's a difference, and it's measured in smoke."

They came over the last headland at dusk, and Merrowgate lay below them like a meal already half-eaten.

* * *

The city Emrys had left six days ago had been gaudy and greedy and alive—lanterns strung across every canal, fishwives screaming prices that were frankly criminal, Tam haggling with a fence who wore three rings per finger and a fourth in his ear. The city below her now had no lights at all except fire. The outer quarters burned in slow, deliberate rows, the way fields are burned before planting, and along the harbor wall she could see the shapes of Corvane's soldiers moving with terrible unhurried patience. They weren't looting. That was the thing that made her stomach turn over. Looting would have meant they wanted something from Merrowgate. This was arithmetic. The free cities had refused his terms at Caldreth, and this was the sum being shown.

"They're making an example," Tam said quietly beside her. He'd gone pale under his tan. "This is what he does. Burn one city properly and the next nine open their gates without a fight."

"The council knew," Emrys said. "Caldreth knew when they voted. They voted anyway."

"Councils vote. Cities burn." Tam rubbed his jaw. "I keep waiting for you to tell me there's something we can do, and I keep remembering that your entire plan was *don't get seen*, which we have now spectacularly achieved by standing on a hill watching a city die."

She didn't answer. Because the ember was not just warm anymore. It was pulling.

It had done this before—in the Ashwood, in the vault, in the small hours when she slept badly and it fed her dreams of green lightning and voices like distant bells. The Wardens' memories lived in it, layered like sediment: the old war, the harvest, the hearts of stars carried in human chests. And lately, more and more, it pulled toward certain things. Toward magic being used nearby. Toward magic being *taken*.

It was pulling toward the harbor now, hard, the way a compass pulls north.

"Tam," she said slowly. "Why is he burning it in rows? Why so careful? Fire isn't careful."

He frowned at her. Then at the city. Then his face did something complicated.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, no."

"You see it too."

"He's not burning the mages." Tam's voice had gone flat, the smuggler's voice, the voice that priced things. "He's *sorting* them. You don't destroy a warehouse of goods. You inventory it first."

Below them, along the quay, Emrys saw it clearly then: figures being marched out of the burning rows into the clear ones. Men, women, a few children old enough to show the flicker. The light-drinkers moved among them with their pale lanterns, and where the lanterns passed, the prisoners' shadows bent *wrong*, leaning toward the light like plants toward sun.

The ember beat against her ribs like a second heart. *Those are ours,* it seemed to say, in the wordless way it said things. *Those are the ones left.*

"There'll be hundreds," she said.

"There'll be whatever there'll be, and none of it will be our problem if we're smart." Tam grabbed her sleeve. "Emrys. Look at me. I know that look. I've seen that look on people about to do extremely stupid heroic things, and it never ends with everyone alive. We go around. We go to Caldreth, we tell the council, we let the council—"

"The council will deliberate." She watched a row of houses collapse and the light-drinkers step through the ash, unhurried, sorting. "By the time they finish deliberating, every mage in Merrowgate will be in a wagon heading north, and their light will be in Corvane's veins, and the council can write a very strongly worded resolution about it."

"So what's the alternative? You can't fight an army."

"No," she agreed. "But I can rain on one."

* * *

She made him swear first, which he thought was ridiculous, and she insisted anyway, because she had learned in the Ashwood that intention mattered—that the ember answered differently to a person doing a thing than to a person becoming a thing. She knelt in the scrub grass above the city with Tam standing awkward guard, and she made him hold her hands and witness.

"If I start going under," she said, "if my eyes go white or I stop answering, you say my name. Not my title. Not the name the cultist gave me. Just Emrys."

"That's your name."

"I know. That's why I chose it." She squeezed his fingers. "Nine years ago someone aimed me at a target and I never even learned I'd been aimed. I'm not aiming myself anywhere tonight. I'm calling weather. Rain. Wind. Cloud. The most boring, ordinary, farmer-approved weather there is. Say it back to me."

"Rain," Tam said. His hands were shaking slightly; hers were not, which frightened her more than the reverse would have. "Wind. Cloud. Boring. Farmer-approved. And if your eyes go white I yell 'Emrys' like a dockworker."

"Good."

"And Emrys—" He stopped. Started again. "You swore never to call storms again. You told me that in the wheatfields, first day. You said it was the one promise you'd kept through everything else."

She looked down at the burning city, at the tiny figures being sorted into wagons, at the pale lanterns drinking.

"Yes," she said. "And tonight I'm going to break it, and I'm going to be seen doing it, and by morning every spy on this coast will know a storm-caller lives again—and they'll come for me, and that will be the point. Every light-drinker looking for me is one not sorting children into wagons." She let go of his hands. "Visibility is a weapon too. Corvane taught me that. He should be flattered I'm learning from him."

"That's the worst plan I've ever heard, and I once smuggled a live wyvern calf in a flour barrel."

"Wyverns are smaller than people think."

"*That's your takeaway.*"

She closed her eyes and reached for the sky.

* * *

Calling the storm was nothing like she remembered, because what she remembered was a lie—or rather, a child's memory, worn smooth by nine years of wards and silence. She remembered power as a floodgate, as something that burst. What the ember showed her instead was a conversation. The air above the coast was heavy with sea-moisture, pregnant with pressure, wanting only permission, and the ember opened in her chest like a hand unclenching, and she asked.

Not commanded. Asked.

The cloud deck offshore heard her and came.

It came the way grief comes—all at once and then in waves, rolling over the headland, swallowing the burning glow, and the first drops hit the scorched streets of Merrowgate like applause, like jeering, like mercy. Thunder followed, and Emrys rode the sound down into her own body and found it did not feel like the old hunger. It felt like work. Like holding up something heavy with good posture. The Wardens' discipline, threaded through the ember's memories, held her spine straight inside the storm: *carry the memory, don't become it. Carry the storm, don't become it.*

She became, briefly, enormous. She felt every raindrop as a fingertip. She felt the fires dying under her attention, street by street, the careful rows of destruction drowned mid-sentence. She felt the light-drinkers too—they shrieked when the rain touched them, actual shrieking, their stolen starlight guttering in the wet like candles in wind—and she allowed herself one moment of savage joy about that before reeling it back in. Discipline. Posture. Rain, wind, cloud. Boring. Farmer-approved.

The wagons on the north road were another matter.

She felt them loading—the flickering captives packed in like cargo—and something older and angrier than the ember stirred in her, something that might have been her own nine suppressed years, and the lightning wanted to find those wagons, wanted it desperately, and she understood in that instant exactly how a person becomes a weapon: not by being forced, but by being *righteously furious* and given power.

*Tam,* she thought, wildly. *Say my name.*

"—EMRYS!" His voice, thin and human somewhere far above the clouds, cutting through like a gull through fog. "EMRYS! Rain! Boring! FARMER-APPROVED!"

She laughed inside the storm, a cracked, gasping laugh, and turned the lightning aside—not at the wagons, not at the soldiers, but up, up into the heart of the thunderhead, spending her fury on the empty sky where it hurt no one but lit the whole coast white as judgment. And in that white flash she did the other thing, the harder thing: she reached down into the city with senses no storm should have and *found* them. The hidden ones. The mages who'd gone to ground in cellars and attics and the hollows beneath canal bridges, forty, fifty, sixty sparks of frightened light that the light-drinkers hadn't finished cataloguing.

*Run,* she said into the rain, not in words but in pressure, in the language the ember knew. *Run now, while the sky is mine.*

And they ran. She felt them scatter like sparks off a struck flint, out the water-gates, into the marshes, along roads the rain would wash clean of footprints. A handful. Dozens. Not enough, never enough—but alive, alive, alive.

Then the counterattack came, and it was worse than she'd imagined.

* * *

She felt it as a tug-of-war. Somewhere below, the senior light-drinkers had planted themselves and were pulling—pulling at her storm itself, drinking at its edges the way they drank at mages, trying to sip her lightning out of the air. The storm thinned where they stood. Rain faltered to mist in circles around them, and through the gaps she saw faces tilted up toward her clouds, mouths open, eyes shining with harvested starlight, hungry.

They couldn't take the storm from her. But they were patient, and they were many, and she was holding up the whole sky.

Her nose was bleeding. She noticed this with distant interest, the way one notices things near the end of things. The ember was blazing now, pouring Warden-craft into her faster than she could shape it—*ground yourself, root yourself, borrow the earth's weight*—and she did, driving her awareness down through the headland rock, anchoring, buying time, but time against an army is a currency you spend and never earn back.

"EMRYS!" Tam again, closer now, shouting from just behind her. "The ridge! Riders on the ridge—scouts, they've seen the lightning, they're coming UP THE HILL—"

Let them come, some old cold part of her whispered. Let me show them what—

*No.* She caught the thought like catching a falling knife. *That's how it starts. That's exactly how it started, nine years ago, someone righteous and terrified with a sky full of power.*

She let the storm go.

Not all at once—she wasn't reckless enough for that, and besides, the ember gentled the release, easing the pressure out over the sea in long gray sheets, spending the thunder harmlessly on open water. But within minutes the rain over Merrowgate was only rain, natural and dumb and blessedly unmagical, and the fires hissed and steamed, and the light-drinkers stood in the wet streets blinking like men waking from a shared dream.

Emrys opened her eyes. The world was small and dark and her whole body ached as if she'd carried a house up a mountain, and Tam was crouched in front of her gripping her shoulders, saying her name in a completely normal volume, which somehow meant he'd been yelling it for a while.

"I'm here," she croaked. "I'm here."

"You're bleeding."

"Noticed."

"You called a *storm*. In front of an army. On purpose. After—" He ran a hand through his hair. "After everything you said about never—"

"I know what I said." She wiped her nose, looked at the red on her fingers, and found she couldn't muster any regret. Below, in the drowned dark, boats were slipping out of Merrowgate's water-gates, low in the water, crowded. Some of them carried her sparks. "How many spies do you think saw that lightning?"

"All of them," Tam said glumly. "Every spy, every scout, every fisherman on the coast. By tomorrow Corvane will know a storm-caller broke his party. By the day after, Serevan will know it was *you*." He paused. "Actually, knowing her, she'll know by breakfast. Terrifying woman. Excellent penmanship."

Emrys got her feet under her, swayed, and let him haul her arm across his shoulders. Together they limped away from the overlook, away from the smoking city, toward the marsh paths and the long road east to Caldreth.

"They'll hunt us now," she said.

"Oh, absolutely."

"Through the marshes, the hills, all the way to Caldreth."

"With great enthusiasm and probably hounds."

Emrys looked back once. Under the clearing clouds, one star had fought through—a real star, high and hard and indifferent—and the ember pulsed toward it like a dog hearing its name.

"Good," she said, and was surprised to find she meant it. "Let them come. I'm done hiding in valleys. Whatever's coming, I want it looking *here*"—she touched her chest, where the last heart of the Ember Wardens beat warm against her sternum—"because every hour they spend hunting me is an hour they're not harvesting, and I intend to be *extremely* findable."

Tam groaned. "I miss the version of you that stole bread quietly."

"She's still here. She just has better priorities now." She grinned at him, bloodied and exhausted and more certain than she'd been since the night the stars went out. "Come on, Vey. Caldreth by week's end. The council needs to hear what I saw—and this time, they're going to listen, because I'm done asking politely."

Behind them, Merrowgate smoldered in the rain. Ahead, the marshes waited, and somewhere beyond them, an army, an inquisitor, and a war that would not wait.
