## 15. The Free Cities Convene

**Emrys**

Caldreth smelled of salt and burnt cedar, and beneath both, faintly, of fear.

Emrys had expected a city under siege to be loud—bells, shouting, the clatter of evacuation. Instead Caldreth had gone quiet in the way a house goes quiet when someone dies inside it. The harbor was half-empty. The fish markets ran at half-voice. Children still played in the lanes, but their games had changed: she watched two boys behind a cooper's yard playing light-drinkers and Wardens, and neither of them would agree to be the drinker, so they argued until they forgot what they were playing altogether.

Tam walked beside her with his hood up and his hands in his pockets, which she had learned meant he felt guilty about something. It was his third-most-comfortable posture.

"Before we go in," he said, "I should tell you that the council is going to be terrible."

"You've said."

"And I don't just mean ordinary council-terrible. I mean these are nine cities' worth of merchants and admirals and one very aggressive beekeeper, all terrified, all convinced they're the only adult in the room. There will be speeches."

"Tam."

"There will be *so many* speeches."

She stopped in the mouth of an alley where gulls wheeled overhead like scraps of torn paper. "You sold me passage out of Merrowgate. You broke into Serevan's own supply train for those horses. You have lied to border guards in four accents I can identify and two more I can't. And you're nervous about *a committee*?"

"Committees are the only thing I've ever feared," Tam said solemnly. "Blades are honest. A committee will smile at you while it decides what you're worth." He glanced at her wrist, where the sleeve covered the storm-mark, then lower, to the satchel against her ribs where the ember sat warm as a sleeping bird. "They know we're coming?"

"Ilsa's letter reached them before we did."

"Then they know what you're carrying." He said it lightly, but not lightly enough. "Emrys. When they ask—and they will ask—you could lie. Just this once. Say it's a rock. Say it's your grandmother's soup stone. People believe anything if you say it with enough sorrow."

The ember shifted against her side, the way it did when it dreamed—a slow pulse of warmth, a flicker of someone else's summer. She had stopped flinching from that. Mostly.

"They'll ask," she said. "And I'll tell them the truth."

Tam sighed the sigh of a man watching a friend walk into weather he'd already checked. "Of course you will. That's the whole problem with you. You keep doing the brave thing as though it were simply the next thing."

They went in through the Council Gate, past guards who looked at her too long and too carefully, and Emrys understood, with a cold settling feeling, that everyone here already knew exactly what she carried.

* * *

**Tam**

The Hall of Nine Lanterns had been built by people who believed that architecture could overrule human nature. Nine great lanterns hung from the vaulted ceiling—one for each free city—and beneath them sat the delegates on a horseshoe of benches, arguing before Emrys and Tam had even crossed the threshold.

"—and I say again," a woman with an admiral's braid was declaring, "that whatever the Quintana woman carries belongs to the alliance, because everything belongs to the alliance now, that is rather the *point* of an alliance—"

"The alliance," said a fat delegate from Vell with the air of a man who had made this point many times and enjoyed making it, "is a ledger, Admiral, and ledgers require *assets*, not—"

"—sentiment—"

"—mysticism—"

"—one girl with a satchel!"

Tam, who had spent his life reading rooms, read this one quickly and did not like the totals. The delegates weren't afraid of Corvane, or not only of Corvane. They were afraid of each other. Every one of them was calculating how to come out of this war owning more than they'd entered it with, and every one of them knew the others were doing the same math. It was the arithmetic that had let Merrowgate negotiate its surrender in whispers. It was the arithmetic that would let Corvane eat the coast city by city, politely, one ledger at a time.

At the head of the horseshoe sat the Presider of Caldreth, an old man named Ondrell with a face like a folded map and eyes that missed nothing, including, Tam noted, the exact spot where Emrys's satchel rested against her ribs.

He leaned toward her. "For the record, my suggestion stands. Soup stone."

"Noted for the record," she murmured back.

A herald with a voice like a cracked bell announced them—"Emrys Quintana, of the Hollow Valley, bearer of the Ember; and Tam Vey, of... various places"—and the hall's noise dropped into a listening silence that was somehow worse than the arguing.

Ondrell gestured them forward. "You've traveled far, Storm-caller."

"Former storm-caller," Emrys said. Her voice carried without effort; Tam had noticed that about her, the way quiet seemed to make room for her rather than the other way around. "And I'd rather we began honestly than comfortably."

"Honestly," repeated the admiral, whose nameplate read KESTRA VOSS, and smiled like a knife being introduced at a dinner party. "What a novel opening. Then tell us plainly, girl: is it true? Is that a Warden heart in your bag?"

Every lantern seemed to lean in.

Emrys opened the satchel.

Tam had seen the ember a hundred times by now—had held it once, briefly, and put it down with shaking hands—but he had never seen it in front of strangers. In the dimness of the hall it glowed like a coal remembered rather than seen, deep orange shot through with threads of gold, and the light moved. Not flickered. *Moved*, the way light moves when there is something alive underneath it, breathing.

The silence lasted three full seconds. Tam counted. It was the longest silence he had ever heard in any room containing politicians.

"It is a living heart," Emrys said. "The last uncorrupted Warden heart in the world. Everything else the Wardens were, Corvane has harvested. This is what's left."

Voss recovered first, because admirals always do. "Then the matter is simple. We take it, we study it, and we turn Corvane's stolen sky against him. One heart, properly wielded—"

"No."

It was such a small word for the size of the hole it left in the hall.

* * *

**Emrys**

The ember pulsed against her palm, warm, attentive. She closed her fingers around it—not hiding it, holding it, the way you hold the hand of someone the room is talking about.

"Delegate Voss," she said. "May I ask you something? Your fleet at Saltmere—when the stars went dark, how many of your navigators could still find true north?"

Voss's jaw tightened. "That is not relevant."

"It's entirely relevant. You want to wield a Warden heart the way Corvane wields his harvested starlight—as fuel, aimed by whoever holds the bellows. But the Wardens weren't weapons. They were *keepers*. Their power came from carrying memory honestly. That's why Corvane can burn cities with his stolen light and still lose ground every time the wind changes—he's burning something alive and calling it oil." She lifted the ember slightly. "This isn't ammunition. If you treat it as ammunition, it will break you the way it broke the old regime, the same way it's breaking him. Slower, maybe. Prettier, certainly. But broken all the same."

A delegate from Vell—the fat one, whose nameplate read MERCANTOR HALDANE—leaned forward with the patience of a man about to explain economics to a child. "Poetry, young woman, does not stop armies. Corvane's columns are eleven days from the Salt Road. Poetry will not stop them. A weapon will. Or do you propose we fight him with *memory*?"

"Yes," said Emrys.

Haldane blinked. Somewhere in the benches, someone laughed, short and disbelieving.

"I've seen what happens when men like you get hold of something like this," she went on, and heard her own voice go quieter and harder. "I was there for it, in a manner of speaking. The old regime took a child and turned her into a weapon because it was efficient. Corvane learned everything he knows from them. He is not an aberration. He is a *conclusion*—what you arrive at when you decide that living things are assets and the end justifies the arithmetic." She looked around the horseshoe, at each face in turn. "Hand me the ember, you say. Very well. Which of you holds it? Which city? Because the moment one of you takes it, the other eight become enemies of the ninth, and Corvane won't even need his armies. You'll do his work for him, politely, in this room, with minutes taken down."

"That," said Haldane, "is an insult to this council."

"It's an insult to *people*," Emrys said. "Councils are just where they keep them."

Tam made a small sound beside her that might have been a cough and might have been pride trying to escape.

Ondrell raised one hand, and the hall settled—or mostly settled; Voss and Haldane kept muttering like weather. "You speak bravely, Quintana, for a woman with no army, no city, and no name the law remembers kindly. So tell us what you *do* propose. You refuse us the heart. Refusing is easy. Governing is work."

"I propose you fight the war you can win," Emrys said. "Not the war he wants you to fight. He wants your mages cowering and your councils squabbling over miracles. Instead: evacuate the Salt Road towns before his columns arrive. Break your granaries open and feed the refugees before hunger makes them his. Get your remaining mages out of the cities he's watching and into the hills where they can't be culled. None of it needs magic. All of it needs you to trust each other more than you trust your ledgers." She paused. "And leave the ember out of it."

"And if that fails?" Voss asked softly. "If honesty and granaries lose to fire?"

Emrys's hand tightened on the ember, and she felt it answer—not with heat but with something older, a patience like bedrock. *We were keepers*, it seemed to say. *We watched empires mistake themselves for weather.*

"Then I will deal with it," she said. "Me. Not the council. Not a weapon passed hand to hand until it corrupts them all. Me, and no one else carries that weight, because I'm the one who helped make this mess and I'm the one still standing who knows how to clean it up."

The hall erupted.

* * *

**Tam**

Later, Tam would maintain—for the rest of his life, probably, to anyone unlucky enough to buy him drinks—that the shouting match lasted four hours. Emrys insisted it was ninety minutes. Tam stuck with four hours, because he had spent most of them watching the delegates rather than listening, and time moved differently when you were watching a room decide whether to arrest somebody.

Because that was the shape of it, under all the rhetoric. Half the hall wanted the ember seized. Two delegates wanted Emrys detained "for the security of the alliance," which was the sort of phrase men used when they wanted to do a thing and have history believe otherwise. The admiral, to Tam's surprise, had gone quiet halfway through and stayed that way, studying Emrys with the expression of a sailor recalculating a chart.

In the end it was Ondrell who cut through, rapping the Presider's staff until the lanterns shivered.

"We will not seize the heart," he said, "because the lady is right, and every person in this room knows she is right, which is why everyone is so angry." A rumble. He talked over it. "But neither will we send her off unwatched. The bearer will remain in Caldreth under the alliance's protection—"

"Protection," Tam muttered. "That's a load-bearing word."

"—until the council determines—"

And Emrys stood up, small and straight in the middle of the horseshoe, and said, in the tone Tam had heard her use exactly twice, both times right before the world got complicated:

"With respect, Presider—I didn't come here to ask permission, and I'm not staying to accept custody. I came because you deserved to know what's coming and what I intend to do about it. Now you know. The rest is mine to carry."

She bowed—the Hollow Valley style, hand to heart, old-fashioned as bread—and walked out of the Hall of Nine Lanterns with her chin level and the ember glowing softly against her ribs like a secret that had finally decided to be proud.

Tam followed, because following her had become, at some point he couldn't quite pinpoint, simply what he did.

The corridor outside was cool and smelled of lamp oil. Emrys made it forty paces before her shoulders sagged and she leaned against the wall, laughing shakily, the laugh of someone who has been holding a note far too long.

"Four hours," she said.

"Ninety minutes."

"It felt like four hours."

"You were extraordinary," Tam told her. "Truly. 'Councils are just where they keep them'—I'm having that engraved on a cup. Possibly several cups. I know a man."

"They were going to arrest me, weren't they. Near the end."

"Two votes short, I'd guess. Ondrell saved you, though whether out of wisdom or because he'd rather use you than cage you, I genuinely cannot say." He hesitated, then gave her the truth, because she'd earned it and because lying to her had started to feel like kicking a dog. "You've made enemies today. Real ones. Haldane doesn't forget slights, and Voss—Voss struck me as the patient kind of dangerous. And word of what you carry is already loose in this city. Half the harbor knows. By tomorrow, the whole coast will."

"I know."

"And you're still walking out the front gate."

Emrys pushed off the wall and looked at him, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen since the valley—something tired and certain and almost happy. "Tam, I spent nine years in a place that sealed its passes and pretended the sky wasn't falling. I know exactly what safety costs. I paid for it once already." She touched the satchel, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "Besides. We have somewhere to be."

"Oh?"

"Ilsa's map. The next mark on it—it's not a vault this time. It's a waypoint, north of here, in the Ashwood. She marked it *shelter*. Someone left a trail for us, Tam, years ago, long before any of this happened. Someone saw it coming." She started toward the gate, then paused. "Also, if we leave tonight, we skip the banquet Ondrell is definitely going to insist on, and I have eaten enough political fish to last a lifetime."

"A woman of principle," Tam said gravely, and fell into step beside her.

They were three streets from the gate, in the blue hush between lamplighting and full dark, when Tam felt it first—a wrongness in the air, a pressure behind his ears like a held breath. The gulls went up off the rooftops all at once, thousands of them, silent.

Down at the harbor, where the lamps burned along the quay, the lights began to go out. Not all together. One by one, in a line, moving toward the city. Moving the way soldiers move.

Emrys's hand found the satchel, and the ember woke against her ribs with a flare Tam could see through the canvas, gold bleeding through the weave.

"Light-drinkers," she breathed. "Inside the walls. Inside the *alliance's* walls—"

From the direction of the Hall of Nine Lanterns came a sound none of them had expected: a scream, high and human, cut short.

Tam grabbed her arm. "Gate," he said. "Now. Run."
