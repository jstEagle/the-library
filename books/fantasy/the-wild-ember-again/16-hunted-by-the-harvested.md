## 16. Hunted by the Harvested

The Ashwood earned its name honestly. Every trunk was grey as old bone, every leaf the color of a fire's last thought, and the light that came down through the canopy was thin and apologetic, like a guest who knew he'd overstayed.

Emrys walked at the front because the ember wanted her to. It had opinions now—she'd stopped pretending otherwise three days ago, somewhere past the salt marshes, when it had grown warm against her sternum whenever she chose the wrong fork in the road and gone quiet as a held breath when she chose right. Tam said she was anthropomorphizing a rock. Tam also talked to his horse in full sentences and expected answers, so Emrys felt entitled to discount his opinion accordingly.

"It's not a rock," she said now, for perhaps the hundredth time. "It's a heart."

"A heart is an organ that pumps blood. That is a stone that gets sulky when you walk through spiderwebs."

"It doesn't get sulky."

"Last night you woke me up because, and I quote, *it feels crowded here*. A stone cannot feel crowded, Emrys. Crowding requires an opinion about other objects, and opinions require—" he gestured broadly with one hand while the other kept his horse's reins slack, "—a self. Which rocks famously lack."

"Then explain why it's been warm since dawn and colder the deeper we go."

Tam opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked up at the grey canopy as if the trees might back him up. They declined.

"That," he admitted, "is unsettling, I'll grant you."

They were nine days out of Caldreth, six days ahead of any pursuit they could confirm and two days behind one they could feel. Emrys felt it in the ember first—a faint pull, southward and westward, like a fish on a line—and then in her own skin, which had begun to prickle with static in the late afternoons, the way it had before storms when she was young and stupid and powerful. The harvested ones were out there. Serevan's light-drinkers, moving through the world with dead stars where their hearts should be, drinking the brightness out of anything that burned.

Ilsa's map said the Warden vault lay two days north, past a streambed that hadn't existed in Ilsa's youth and did now, thanks to some geological argument nobody had won politely. Two days. Emrys measured everything in days now. It was how you stayed sane when the thing hunting you might already be behind the next hill.

"Tell me again," Tam said, "why we're going toward the vault instead of away from the woman who wants what's in your shirt."

"Because Serevan will find us either way, and if she finds us I'd rather know what we're carrying than just carry it."

"You realize that's the same logic people use to read their own medical charts."

"Yes, and those people live longer than the ones who die of curiosity suppressed."

He snorted. Ahead of him, his horse—Bramble, a mare with the temperament of a tax collector—flicked an ear at nothing.

Emrys stopped walking.

The ember wasn't just cold anymore. It was *loud*, though no sound reached her ears: a pressure behind her ribs, a ringing she heard with her bones rather than her blood. She put a hand flat against her chest without quite meaning to.

"Tamsin." His full name, and she watched the humor drain out of his face like water from a cracked bowl. Nobody used Tam Vey's whole name except customs officers and people delivering bad news. "How long have the birds been quiet?"

He listened. Bramble listened. The wood listened back, and gave them nothing—not a wren, not a crow, not the idiotic chattering of squirrels they'd been complaining about since the marshes.

"Oh," Tam said softly. "Oh, that's not good."

* * *

They didn't run. Running made noise, and noise made you findable, and besides—Emrys had learned this much from the ember's memories, from the Wardens who had once patrolled these very woods—the harvested didn't hunt with eyes. They hunted with light. Anything that burned, they saw: lanterns, cookfires, the small warm furnace of a living body. The trick was to be nothing. To bank yourself down to embers, to use the word she hated and meant anyway.

So they went still. They backed Bramble into a hollow beneath a fallen trunk and muffled her tack with wool, and they stood among the ash-grey trees and did not breathe louder than the wind.

It came at dusk, which was either tactically elegant or simply rude.

They came between the trees the way frost comes across a window—not walking so much as *arriving*, one pale figure after another, seven of them, wrapped in cloaks the color of the wood itself. And where their faces should have been there was light. Not glowing, exactly. Drained. Like looking at a lamp after the oil's gone but the glass remembers being warm.

Emrys had seen one before, from a rooftop in Merrowgate, at distance, at night, doing its work. Up close was different. Up close you could see that the light moved. It swam behind their faces like fish under ice, and every few moments one of them would tilt its head, listening to something inside itself, and the light would shift.

"They can't see us," Tam breathed, so close to her ear it was barely sound. "Right? Tell me they can't see us."

"They see warmth," she breathed back. "Be cold."

"Being cold is my single greatest talent and I have never once been praised for it."

"*Tamsin.*"

He shut up. Bless him forever, he shut up.

The seven figures spread through the trees with terrible patience. One passed within ten feet of them, close enough that Emrys could hear it—not breathing, because it didn't need to breathe, but a faint crystalline hum, like a wineglass sung at the edge of shattering. Its face-light swept the hollow where Bramble stood. The mare, to her eternal credit, did not move.

But the ember—

The ember *burned*.

Not the gentle warmth of guidance. This was a shout. Heat bloomed against Emrys's sternum so fast and so fierce she gasped, and the gasp was a mistake, because sound was warmth too, in a way, and the nearest light-drinker turned its drained face toward her like a compass needle finding north.

"There," it said. Its voice was beautiful, which was somehow worse than if it had been monstrous. Voices like that belonged in cathedrals. "There is memory here. There is *heart*."

"Run," Emrys said, and shoved Tam, and the woods came apart around them.

* * *

What followed was not a chase in any honest sense of the word. Chases have hope in them. This was arithmetic. The light-drinkers didn't run; they *glided*, robes whispering over leaf-mould, and they cut angles through the Ashwood as though the trees had agreed in advance to make way. Emrys pulled Tam off the path and into the deep tangle, over deadfall, through stands of saplings thin as knitting needles, and it bought them minutes, only minutes, because the ember was blazing now and blazing things are visible to things that drink light.

She tried to smother it. She pressed her palm hard against her own chest, whispered at it, begged it in the old formal cadences the memories had taught her—*quiet, quiet, little heart, be dark, be dark*—and it dimmed, briefly, obediently, the way a frightened child goes quiet when you cover its mouth.

That comparison arrived in her mind uninvited and she flinched from it.

"They're herding us," Tam panted. He'd torn his sleeve somewhere and hadn't noticed. "You see that, yes? Left's open. Left has always been open. I hate left."

"I see it." She did. The gaps in the pursuit weren't accidents. They were being funneled southeast, downslope, toward the streambed and the open ground beyond it, where seven pale shapes could circle without trees in the way. Serevan hadn't sent hunters. She'd sent sheepdogs.

And then, because the day had decided it hadn't yet done enough to her, Emrys heard the voice.

Not the beautiful crystalline voices. A human one, dry and precise and carrying effortlessly through the wood, the voice of a woman who had never once needed to raise it.

"You needn't run, Quintana." Lady Serevan, somewhere upslope, unseen. "I've read your file. You tire quickly. Nine years of damp valley air—it's a wonder you've come this far. Give me the ember and walk away. I give you my word you'll be unmolested. I keep my word; ask anyone whose opinion survived asking."

"She lies beautifully," Tam muttered. "I'd have believed her, and I *like* you."

Emrys didn't answer, because the ember answered for her. It flared—hot, insistent, and this time it wasn't warning. It was *pointing*. South. Away from the funnel, toward a fold in the land she couldn't see, and the heat of it carried something new: not just direction but recognition. The ember knew what was south. The ember had friends there, or graves.

"This way," she said, and broke left after all, the direction she'd sworn two minutes ago was a trap, gambling everything on a rock's opinion.

The trap snapped shut behind them anyway.

* * *

They burst out of the trees onto the streambed—Ilsa's streambed, the new one, all white stones and thin water—and found the sixth light-drinker waiting for them, patient as winter, standing mid-stream with its drained face tilted.

Behind: four more gliding out of the wood. Above: Serevan's voice, closer now, saying something Emrys didn't bother to parse because parsing was for people with options.

The light-drinker in the stream raised one hand, palm out, and the light behind its face gathered, brightened, became a point of stolen starlight aimed at her heart.

Emrys had a half-second to think a great many things, and the one that surfaced, absurdly, was Tam's complaint about spiderwebs.

The second bolt came from the treeline, and it wasn't aimed at her.

Later she would reconstruct it: Tam, who had drifted a step to her left without her noticing, who had spent nine days insisting the ember was a rock and she was a madwoman, stepping into the space between Emrys and the light-drinker in the treeline because that was apparently the sort of man he'd been all along, under the jokes, the way ore is gold under dirt.

The starlight took him through the shoulder and he spun and went down into the shallow water, and the sound he made was small and shocked, the sound of a man interrupted mid-sentence.

"*TAM!*"

The world went narrow and red. She was on her knees in the stream before she remembered moving, hands pressing the wound, hot blood welling between her fingers, and Tam looked up at her with an expression of genuine, offended bewilderment, as if the universe had broken a promise to him personally.

"Well," he said. "That's. Hm."

"Shut up. Shut up, stay down, you absolute *idiot*—"

"Technically you should say thank you, I believe that's customary—"

The remaining light-drinkers were closing, unhurried, faces swimming with dead stars, and Serevan's voice came down the slope again, gentle as snowfall: "Now you understand the arithmetic, Quintana. Your life, or the stone's. I'll take either. I'd prefer both, but I'm told I'm sentimental."

Emrys knelt in the water with her friend bleeding under her hands and the ember roaring against her ribs like a caged animal, and she understood—with a clarity that felt less like thought than like falling—that she had exactly one move left and it was the one she'd sworn off in Merrowgate, in the burning, in every quiet moment since: pouring herself into the ember until there wasn't enough of her left over to be a person.

So she did it. But differently this time.

Because the ember had changed. Somewhere between the marshes and the morning and the moment Tam stepped sideways, the thing in her shirt had stopped being a burden she hauled and started being a companion she consulted, and when she opened herself to it now, she didn't push memory in the way you force a door.

She knocked.

*Here,* she offered. *This is what I have. A smuggler bleeding in a stream. Seven dead stars walking. I don't know how to do this. Show me.*

And the ember, warm as a hand taking hers, *answered*.

* * *

The memories came up through her like water through a well, but they were not hers alone anymore. They were the Wardens'—all of them, every heart that had ever burned in the long watch, and they rose together, a chorus, a congregation, and Emrys stopped kneeling in a stream and stood, in some other place, in a circle of light that was also a circle of people.

They were old. They were young. They were every age there had ever been watchers of skies. And the eldest of them, a woman with frost in her braids and a smile like a knife wrapped in wool, looked at Emrys with an expression of vast, tired recognition.

"Little storm," she said. "You brought us a thief."

"He's a good thief," Emrys said, and her voice shook. "He's dying. Please. I don't know your craft. Teach me the carrying. Teach me how to hold you without breaking."

"We were always going to teach you," the frost-braided woman said. "Why do you think we chose a child who could hold a storm? Not for the throwing, little one. For the holding." She extended her hand, and behind her, rank upon silent rank, the dead Wardens did the same, hundreds of hands, thousands, an army of the patient. "Memory is not a weapon. It was never a weapon. It is a *lamp*. Now—hold up your end of the light."

* * *

In the streambed, the light-drinkers paused. All seven at once, heads tilting, the fish-swimming light behind their faces going suddenly, terribly still.

They heard it. Emrys saw them hear it—the moment the ember's voice went out into the Ashwood, not as heat or light but as *memory*, as the accumulated watch of ten thousand nights, and the sound it made was not a sound at all but a presence, the way a cathedral is present, the way grief is present.

The light-drinkers had been people once. Everyone forgot that. Emrys forgot it constantly, in the practical way of someone at war. But standing in the stream with Tam's blood cooling on her hands, she watched those drained faces turn toward the ember's call, and what moved behind them was not hunger.

It was homesickness.

One of them—the one in the stream, the one who had aimed at her—made a sound. It was small and cracked and human, and it dropped to its knees in the white water like a puppet with cut strings, and the starlight behind its face guttered, flickered, and for one heartbeat showed something underneath: a face. Young. Frightened. A boy, maybe, from a village whose name Corvane's harvesters had never bothered learning.

Then the discipline of the stolen power clamped down and the light smoothed over, but the pause had lasted long enough. Long enough for Emrys to gather Tam up. Long enough to stumble upstream along the stones, into the fold of land the ember had pointed to, where the ground dipped into a hollow roofed by roots older than the valley wards.

Long enough to live.

* * *

She got a fire going, which was insane, which was painting a target on them, but Tam was shaking with cold and shock and the wound needed cleaning, and the ember, when she asked it—asked it, the way you ask a friend to stand watch—wrapped its warmth around them like a blanket and dimmed itself to almost nothing, a coal's glow, invisible past arm's reach.

The bolt had gone through the meat below his collarbone, front to back, missing everything that mattered by distances Tam later described as "insulting." She cleaned it with stream water and the good spirits from his pack—the ones he guarded like a dragon—and stitched it while he narrated, because he always narrated, because silence scared him worse than blades.

"For the record," he said, grey-faced, teeth chattering around the grin he refused to drop, "I want it noted that I was hit saving a storm-caller, and that when the ballads are written, I want a verse. Just one. A short one. Something about my hair."

"Your hair is currently full of stream gravel."

"A *dignified* verse about my hair."

"You'll get a footnote."

"Emrys Quintana, I took a star-bolt for you—"

"And I'm stitching you shut with fishing gut, which makes us even." Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. She finished the last knot and sat back and looked at him—pale, ridiculous, alive—and the fear she'd been carrying since the moment he fell came out of her all at once, badly, as a laugh that was mostly sob. "Don't ever do that again."

"No promises. You're extremely hard to resist rescuing." He flexed the shoulder, winced, and settled. Then, quieter, watching the coal-glow of the ember at her throat: "It talked to you. Back there. I saw your face."

"Yeah."

"What did it say?"

Emrys considered lying. It was their custom, nearly, the polite fictions they traded to keep the road light. But the ember was warm against her skin, and somewhere in the dark above them the dead Wardens kept their watch, and lies felt heavier than usual tonight.

"It introduced itself," she said. "Properly. Turns out it's not a rock."

"Hm." Tam closed his eyes. "I want it noted that I called it a heart weeks ago and deserve credit."

"You called it a sulky rock."

"History is written by the wounded, Emrys. Sleep. I'll take first watch, such as it is, from horizontal."

She slept, and dreamed of frost-braided women teaching her the shape of holding, and woke before dawn to the ember's warmth sharpening into a point, urgent and bright, pointing north-northwest through the root-hollow's ceiling—toward the vault, two days' travel, maybe less.

But there was something else in the warmth this time. A new thread in the chorus. When she listened closely, she could pick it out: a voice she recognized from the streambed, thin and cracked and unmistakably young, the boy-face from behind the drained light.

A voice that was whispering, over and over, in a language the ember translated directly into her bones:

*Come and find me. Come and find me. I remember my name.*
