## 17. The Usurper's Offer

They came for her an hour past dawn, when the mist off the Ashwood still clung to the fields like a thing reluctant to let go.

Emrys was washing her face in a stream when Tam hissed her name from the treeline, and she looked up to see them riding out of the fog: a dozen riders in grey and silver, and above them a banner she had not seen since the day of her exile—white ground, black bridge, the sigil of parley. The old sign. The true one. Whatever else Vael Corvane had stolen, he had kept the heraldry of honorable meetings, which told her something about him that no report from the free cities ever had. A man who wanted to be seen as legitimate. A man who cared what people thought.

"He's mad," Tam said flatly, crouched beside her with his hand nowhere near a weapon because there was nothing a weapon would do here. "He's actually mad. That's Corvane. That is *Corvane*, under a parley banner, like this is a border dispute about sheep."

"Go," Emrys said.

"Not a chance."

"Tam."

"I've been kidnapped, robbed, shot at, and emotionally manipulated by you for two months, Quintana. You don't get to dismiss me now." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but his jaw was set, and she noticed he had placed himself between her and the riders without seeming to notice he'd done it. "Besides, someone has to count their archers."

There were eight archers. She counted too. Old habit from a life she barely remembered, surfacing through nine quiet years like a stone showing through shallow water.

The rider in front dismounted. He was younger than she expected, with a courier's tired face, and he bowed—actually bowed—to her, mud-spattered and hollow-eyed as she was.

"Emrys Quintana," he said. "The Lord Protector requests your presence. Unarmed. Unbound. His word on it."

"His word," Tam repeated, sotto voce. "Lovely currency. What's the exchange rate?"

"The Lord Protector swears by the old forms," the courier said, with the flat patience of a man reciting something he'd been made to memorize. "Parley given is parley kept. He asks only to speak. An hour. Then you are free to go wherever you wish."

*Wherever I wish,* Emrys thought. As if there were anywhere left. Behind her, south, lay Caldreth and its squabbling council. Ahead, east, the road to Starfall Bridge ran straight and open through country that had stopped pretending to be safe. And everywhere, everywhere, the sky sat empty and black at night, like a mouth with all its teeth pulled.

She should have refused. Every sensible part of her—the part that had survived nine years of exile by keeping her head down and her hands in the soil—screamed to refuse, to run, to put another valley between herself and this man.

But the ember against her sternum, wrapped and bound beneath her shirt, had gone warm. Not warning-warm. *Listening*-warm. And in the weeks since the Ashwood, since the dead Wardens' voices had poured through her like light through cracked glass, she had learned to tell the difference.

It wanted her to hear him.

"All right," she said, and heard Tam make a small strangled sound beside her. "One hour."

* * *

They met in a field.

That was the first strange thing. She had expected a tent, a table, guards, theater. Instead the courier led her over two hedgerows and a stile to an open pasture where the mist was burning off, and there was a single man standing alone in the wet grass, hands clasped behind his back, looking up at the pale morning sky as though checking it for flaws.

Vael Corvane was not what the pamphlets promised. The free cities' woodcuts showed him gaunt and terrible, crowned in shadow, drinking light from a chalice. In person he was broad and ordinary-faced, fiftyish, with grey coming in at his temples and the posture of a man who had spent decades sitting through long meetings. He wore plain dark wool. No crown. No visible weapons.

He turned when her footsteps reached him across the grass, and smiled, and the smile was genuinely warm, and that was the most frightening thing yet.

"Emrys Quintana," he said. "You have your mother's way of walking into a room."

"My mother died when I was four."

"I know. I attended the funeral." He said it without cruelty, the way one might mention having enjoyed the same book. "I knew both your parents. I knew the woman who trained you before the Hollow Valley took you in. I knew the Warden-council you never met, though they voted on your life twice before you were old enough to walk. You have spent nine years thinking yourself forgotten, child. You were never forgotten. You were *filed*."

The ember pulsed once against her chest, slow and deep, like a heart turning over in its sleep.

"You wanted an hour," Emrys said. "Talk."

"Direct. Good. The exiles always come back either direct or broken, and broken makes for tedious conversation." He gestured at the field around them, the hedgerows, the distant smoke of some farm's morning fire. "Do you know what I did three nights ago?"

"Merrowgate," she said, and could not keep the flint out of her voice. "You burned Merrowgate."

"I took Merrowgate's *mages*," Corvane corrected gently. "The burning was an accident of arithmetic. When you harvest starlight from a living mage, there is a—a spillage. Like bleeding a deer. You can be clean about it or you can be quick about it, and I chose quick, because my alternative was letting the harvested power destabilize and take half the coast with it." He sighed, and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and for one absurd moment he looked less like a usurper than like a tired clerk. "You think I enjoy this. I want you to understand that I do not enjoy this."

"Comforting," Emrys said. "I'll tell the ashes."

"Yes." He nodded slowly. "Yes, you will, and you'll mean it as a wound, and I'll accept it as one. But hear me first. You owe me an hour, and you're brave enough to give me the whole of it, because you've already guessed that some part of what I'm going to say is true, and you need to know how much."

She hated him a little for that, because he was right.

* * *

He walked as he talked, slowly, along the hedgerow, and she found herself walking with him, matching her steps to his, hating that too.

"Do you know why the stars fell?" he asked.

"The Wardens died."

"The Wardens were *killed*. Nine years ago. All of them, in a single night, by a storm that came out of a clear sky and struck the Warden-council at Starfall Bridge with a precision no natural weather has ever had." He glanced sideways at her, and his eyes were not unkind. "You were there, Emrys. You were eleven years old. You were aimed at them like a drawn bow."

"I don't remember."

"No," he said softly. "You don't. Because remembering would have destroyed you, and the people who used you were not monsters, exactly—they were desperate men and women at the end of a war, and they decided that a shattered child was cheaper than a shattered kingdom. They wiped you and gave you to the Hollow Valley with a story about a storm you summoned carelessly. Exile as mercy. Exile as burial." He stopped walking. "And I have spent nine years living inside the consequences of what they did that night."

The ember was burning now, steadily, and Emrys pressed her hand flat against her ribs as if she could hold it still. "Say what you mean."

"What I mean is this." He turned to face her fully. "When the Wardens died, their hearts didn't die with them. A Warden's heart is a vessel—it holds memory, and memory, in this world, *is* power. Their hearts scattered. Fell like the stars they tended. And I have spent nine years gathering them, because someone had to. Do you know what happens to unanchored starlight, Emrys? It doesn't rest. It *seethes*. It gets into ley-lines and rivers and the roots of mountains, and it warps them. Left alone, in another decade, this land would have torn itself open. I am not harvesting power for conquest. I am harvesting power for *containment*. The conquest—" he spread his hands, apologetic, enormous, "—the conquest is what containment costs. Free cities don't surrender their mages willingly. Someone has to hold the lid down while I work."

"And the lid requires an army."

"And the lid requires an army," he agreed. "Yes. I've never pretended otherwise. I'm not a good man, Emrys. I'm a necessary one, which is worse for everyone involved, myself included."

She laughed—she couldn't help it—and it came out sharp and ugly in the quiet field. "*Necessary.* Serevan uses that word too. Your light-drinkers used it in Merrowgate while they emptied people like wineskins."

"Serevan believes what she needs to believe. So do my soldiers. So does every army since the first one. That isn't a flaw in my argument; it's a fact about all arguments of state." He held up a finger. "Here is the part you must actually weigh. My containment is failing. Not morally failing—*physically* failing. The harvested starlight is too much for any mortal vessel, even mine. It wants a Warden's heart to settle into, a proper anchor, and there is exactly one left in the world. One uncorrupted ember. And you, Emrys, are carrying it against your chest like a keepsake."

The field went very quiet. Somewhere beyond the hedge, a lark was singing, absurdly, beautifully, indifferent to empires.

"If I fall," Corvane said quietly, "if I am killed, or if I simply lose control—as I nearly did over Merrowgate, as I *will* within the year—then everything I have gathered tears loose at once. Every stolen star, every drained mage, every drop of the Wardens' old light, released raw into a world with no Wardens to shepherd it. It will not burn the land, child. It will *unmake* it. Weather without pattern. Memory without anchor. Rivers running backward. People waking up as other people." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to something almost intimate. "Give me the ember. Let me anchor what I've gathered. In exchange: your exile ends today. Full exoneration—the truth of that night published, your name cleaned, the record set straight. And your magic returned to you unsuppressed, untamed by anyone, yours. You could call storms again. Honest ones. Yours."

For a moment—one long, traitorous moment—Emrys stood in the field and *wanted*.

It rose in her like water finding a crack. To be innocent. To have the last nine years rewritten not as crime but as tragedy, her not as weapon but as victim, the storm not hers but *done to her*. To stand under an open sky and feel the pressure of weather again, the wild sweet electricity she had been severed from at nineteen and had dreamed about every night since, dreaming the way the legless dream of running. He was offering her back her own soul, laundered and folded and returned.

And the worst part—the part that made her feel sick—was that she believed him. Not all of him. But the shape of it was true. She had felt the harvested starlight herself, in the Ashwood, pouring out of Serevan's light-drinkers: vast, wounded, seething, *unanchored*. The ember had flinched from it. Something that vast, cut loose, would drown the world in its dying.

His argument was almost true. That was what made it a masterpiece. Every lie worth telling is built on a load-bearing beam of truth.

* * *

"And if I say no?" she asked.

"Then I take it from your corpse, and the land suffers the same fate either way, except that you'll have died for nothing and I'll have lost the only conversation I've enjoyed in years." He said it mildly, almost sadly. "But you won't say no. You're not built for it. You're built for exactly one thing, Emrys Quintana, and it isn't refusal."

The ember flared hot against her sternum—not pain, not quite, but insistence, like a hand gripping her wrist.

And through the flare, faintly, she heard it. Not words. Never words, not anymore, but something older than words: a chorus-echo, the dead Wardens' voices braided together, and threaded through them a single note of recognition. *We know this man,* the note said. *We remember this man.*

And underneath the recognition, cold and clear as deep water:

*He is lying about one thing.*

Only one. But one was enough, because she could feel which one—the lie had a texture, a wrongness, the way a forged coin is slightly too smooth. He had spoken truly of the night of her exile. Truly of the harvest, the seething starlight, the danger. Truly, even, of his fear.

But when he had said *containment*—when he had described himself holding the lid down, gathering the fallen light to protect the land—

The ember remembered the Wardens' law, the first law, carved into the vault walls in the Ashwood: *A heart of memory must be given freely or it corrupts the bearer.* And Vael Corvane was not asking for the ember freely given. He was asking her to surrender it, and he had built a palace of true things around that single false foundation, and she understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, that he did not want to contain the starlight.

He wanted to *become* it.

A new Warden. The only Warden. A king crowned in the hearts of the dead, holding every star in the sky inside his single mortal body, forever—and whatever Corvane believed about necessity this morning, standing in a wet field smelling honestly of honest grass, she had seen what happened to men who held too much light. She had watched it drain the faces of Merrowgate's mages. She had felt it in Serevan's touch, hungry and hollowed.

The Wardens had never been one. That was the whole secret of them. That was why the ember sang in chords.

"Your hour's nearly done," Emrys said.

"It is."

"Then here's my answer. No."

Corvane studied her for a long moment, and then—he surprised her—he smiled again, rueful and real, like a chess player watching a good move he hadn't predicted. "I thought perhaps. It was worth the hour to try. You should know that everything I told you was true except one thing, and you should spend tonight wondering which one." He stepped back, and the courier and the riders materialized out of the mist-line as if they'd never been more than ten feet away. "Keep the ember a while longer, Emrys. Carry it to whoever you trust. And when you finally understand what I understand—that there is no one left safe to hold it but me—you know where to find me."

He paused, one foot in the stirrup, and looked back at her.

"Oh—and a courtesy, between people who share a history. Serevan knows where you sleep tonight. I delayed her as long as the parley allowed." He mounted, gathered the reins. "I'd start running now."

The banner dipped, the riders wheeled, and the mist folded closed behind them like water over a stone.

Tam came over the stile at a dead sprint, white-faced, sword drawn at absolutely nothing. "Emrys—what—did he—"

"We ride," she said. Her hand was pressed flat over the ember, which was beating now, fast and urgent, like a heart that had just remembered what fear was. "Now. And Tam—when we stop, there's something I have to tell you. About the bridge. About the night it all started."

"Why?"

"Because Corvane wasn't lying about everything." She swung up into the saddle, and the eastern horizon was already bright with morning, and somewhere behind the brightness, hunting them, came the sound—not yet audible, not yet real, but coming—of bells. "And I think the ember wants to show me which part wasn't."
