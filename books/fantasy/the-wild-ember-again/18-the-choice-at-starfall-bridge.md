## 18. The Choice at Starfall Bridge

The bridge was smaller than Emrys remembered.

That was the trouble with nine years of exile: you carried a place inside you like a stone in your shoe, and when you finally came back to it, the stone was always bigger than the thing itself. Starfall Bridge was a span of grey granite over a gorge that dropped two hundred feet to a river gone thin and silver with autumn. A few wind-bent pines clung to the banks. Someone's sheep had gotten loose and stood regarding the approaching armies with the mild disapproval of its kind.

It was where her exile had begun. It was where everything, apparently, intended to end.

"They're early," Tam said beside her. He was leaning against the bridge's western post with the studied casualness of a man who had never once been casual in his life. "Corvane's whole line. I told Serevan's scouts noon parley, and they've come at dawn like a bunch of overeager wedding guests."

"Maybe they brought a gift."

"Knowing Corvane, it's a siege engine."

Below them, on the southern bank, the usurper's army was assembling out of the morning mist in disciplined blocks—black lacquer and pale steel, banners bearing the harvested-star sigil that still made Emrys's teeth ache to look at. On the northern bank, the free cities' forces were a scrappier thing: Caldreth's spearmen, Merrowgate survivors who had walked out of their own burning, a company of Ashwood rangers, and perhaps three hundred volunteers whose only qualification was fury. Between the two hosts, the bridge itself stood empty, waiting.

And above them all, the sky remained empty too. No stars by night, no comfort by day—just a flat, bruised ceiling where the heavens used to be.

Emrys touched the ember through her shirt. It sat against her sternum like a second heart, beating slightly out of rhythm with her own, warm as a sleeping bird.

"Last chance to run," she said.

Tam snorted. "Run where? The man has armies on both ends of every road I've ever loved." He paused, and his humor thinned to something more honest. "You know I'd argue for running anyway if there were anywhere."

"I know."

"That's the worst part about you, Quintana. You're very hard to be cynical around."

* * *

They came to the middle of the bridge at midmorning, under a parley banner so white it looked ashamed of itself.

Vael Corvane rode first. Emrys had expected a monster out of the ember's memories—had braced for the boy-soldier turned tyrant she had glimpsed in flashes of the old war, all ambition and cold arithmetic. What arrived was a broad, grey-templed man in unremarkable armor, with the tired face of a provincial magistrate. He looked like someone's uncle. That frightened her more than any monster would have.

Lady Serevan rode at his right hand. Her pale eyes found Emrys across forty feet of granite and did not waver. There was something wrong in them this morning—a hairline crack in the porcelain, as though she had slept badly, or dreamed something she couldn't put down.

"Emrys Quintana," Corvane said, and his voice was mild, almost kind. "Storm-caller. Child of the Hollow Valley. Do you know, you're the last living person who was there at the beginning?"

"The beginning of what?" Emrys asked. "Your theft? Because yes. I've seen it now. All of it."

Something flickered behind his calm. "I see the ember has been talking to you." He swung down from his horse and walked to the bridge rail, looking north toward the valley, toward the free cities beyond it, with an expression that was—gods help her—genuine. "Then let me tell you what it hasn't told you. The starlight I harvest is dying, Quintana. Not dimming—dying. The Wardens' hearts weren't made to burn outside living bodies. They're going out one by one inside my engines, and when the last goes, it won't gutter politely. It will tear. All of it at once. Every stolen star detonating back into the world."

"You could stop harvesting," Emrys said. "Release what you've taken. Let the Wardens' hearts go home."

"And give up the only power standing between these cities and the old regime's remnants? You saw what the regime did. *You*—" he gestured at her, gently, horribly, "—were what the regime did. A child aimed like an arrow. Your storm killed the last free Warden council, girl. Everything since—the wars, my rise, all of it—that storm started. I am not the disease. I'm the tourniquet."

He said it with such weariness, such plausibility, that for one obscene moment Emrys almost believed him. That was the seduction of the clean lie: it didn't ask you to be wicked. It asked you to be reasonable.

"But," Corvane continued, "the ember changes things. One uncorrupted Warden heart, fully alive, carrying nine hundred years of memory. Set into my engine array, it would stabilize the rest. Balance them, the way a keystone balances an arch. No detonation. No catastrophe. And in exchange—full pardon. Restoration of your name. And your magic returned clean, unburdened, yours again. No exile. No hunger. Just the storm, obedient at last."

There it was. The thing she had wanted for nine years without letting herself want it. Her name, washed. Her magic, harmless. Her crime, forgiven—or better, reclassified as someone else's.

Beside her, Tam had gone very still.

* * *

"He's lying," Tam said that evening, in the free cities' war camp, in the command tent where the council had argued itself hoarse and finally, wearily, handed the decision to her. "He's always lying. He lies the way other men breathe."

"Not entirely," Emrys said quietly. "That's what frightens me. The starlight is failing—I felt it in the vault, Tam, the records say the same. If his engines tear, it could crack the sky open worse than it already is. He might even believe he's the tourniquet."

"So take his offer. Stabilize him. Live."

"No." She pulled the ember from her shirt and held it up between them, and it pulsed in the lamplight, patient and warm and unbearably alive. "I've spent weeks learning what this is. Not a weapon. Not a key. A person—or what's left of nine hundred persons, folded down into one heart. And here's the thing none of them understand, not Corvane, not the council, nobody." Her voice shook, and she let it. "It can't be wielded safely by anyone. Not him. Not me. Not the holiest soul in Caldreth. Because the moment you set it into an engine—an arch, a crown, anything—you're asking a living memory to hold still forever. And nothing that remembers can hold still. It will fight. It will tear. Maybe slower than his stolen stars, but just as surely. There's no safe hand for this, Tam. There's only the pyre."

Tam stared at her. And Emrys watched the man who joked his way through burning villages arrive, at last, at the argument he couldn't joke past.

"So burn it," he said roughly. "Burn it and lose everything. Your magic, your name, whatever's left of the storm you were. Burn it and become—what? Ordinary? Is that your grand victory?"

"It's everyone else's survival."

"And what about—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, and this was the worst part, because quiet Tam was true Tam. "What about the dead? My family died in the regime's magic wars, Emrys. My mother, my sisters, burned in a border town because some general wanted to test a new weapon. Nine years I told myself if I ever got my hands on something like this—" he nodded at the ember, "—something with that much power, I could buy them back somehow. Not literally. I'm not mad. But make it mean something. Make their deaths the price of something worth having. And now you're telling me the price gets paid and nothing—nothing—is built on the other side of it. Just ash and an ordinary woman."

"An ordinary woman," Emrys said, "and rain."

"What?"

"When the Wardens' hearts go home, the sky heals. Rain comes back to the dry places. Crops grow. Children grow. That's not nothing, Tam. That's everything, actually—it's just everything that doesn't get a statue."

He looked away from her, jaw working. When he spoke again his voice had gone low and fierce. "Use it yourself, then. Don't give it to Corvane, don't burn it—*use* it. You've been learning the Wardens' craft for weeks. You could carry it. Become the last Warden. Keep the memories alive the honest way, in a living body. Take his offer off the table and beat him with it."

"I would become him," she said. "Sooner or later. That's what the vault taught me. That's how the first Wardens fell—not to enemies. To the weight." She closed her hand around the ember, and it beat against her palm like something small trying to be brave. "It can't be held, Tam. It can only be let go."

"And you're so certain you're strong enough to let go?"

"No," Emrys said honestly. "But I'm the only one here willing to find out."

* * *

Dawn came grey and starless, and with it came the battle.

She never learned who broke the parley. Later, in the ember's memories, she would see it a dozen ways—Corvane's outriders moving first, a Caldreth archer loosing early, a misread signal flag—but that morning there was simply a horn, and then the world became noise.

The armies met at the bridge's mouth, and Starfall Bridge lived up to its name in the ugliest possible way. Corvane's light-drinkers went to work along his lines, and Emrys watched men and women drink brightness out of the air itself—watched the morning go dim around them like a candle being cupped—and then hurl that brightness north in lances that turned shield walls into shadow and screaming.

"Hold the bridge!" someone shouted. "Hold the bridge!" And the free cities' line bent and bent and did not break, mostly because breaking meant the river gorge, and the river gorge meant falling.

Emrys fought at the center with the ember blazing against her chest, and she did not call the storm. That was the discipline of it, the agony of it: magic flooding back into her now that the valley wards were long behind her, wild and eager, rising in her throat like a shout, while she kept her teeth shut. Every time she reached for it, the alarm-bells Corvane had strung across the countryside would scream her position to every spy on the coast. Worse—every time she reached, she felt how much of her power was woven of the same stuff as his stolen light. Storm-calling and star-harvesting, cousins. Twins. She could feel his engines pulling at the same well she drank from.

If she fought him with her storm, she fed him.

So she fought with a spear she barely knew how to use, beside Tam, who fought with two knives and a great deal of profanity, and gradually the northern line gave ground, and ground, and gave—

Until the light-drinkers broke through to the bridge itself, and Lady Serevan came across it walking, unhurried, pale eyes fixed on Emrys like a woman reading a letter she'd written to herself years ago.

"Give me the ember," Serevan said. Around them the battle roared, but between the two of them there was a strange pocket of quiet, the way there always is around people deciding things. "You know I'll take it otherwise. I've killed gentler people than you for less."

"I know," Emrys said. "Serevan. In the vault, the records—we found your file. Did you ever wonder why you remember your childhood so clearly? Too clearly? Every detail polished, like it was given to you rather than lived?"

The inquisitor went white.

"Because it was given," Emrys said softly, and the ember flared against her chest, and she let it show Serevan—just for a moment, just enough—the truth. A girl in a regime laboratory. A young officer named Vael Corvane ordered to edit a witness. The knife of borrowed magic going into a child's memory, cutting out her mother's face, her own name, and replacing them with a story: *you were saved, you were chosen, you owe him everything.*

"You lie," Serevan whispered. But her hand had drifted from her sword hilt to her own temple, and her breath had gone ragged, and Emrys recognized the look because she had worn it for nine years. It was the look of someone standing on ground that has just admitted it was never ground at all.

"He edited you," Emrys said. "You were his first victim. Before the armies, before the stars. Before any of it. He made you loyal the way a carpenter makes a chair."

For a moment—one endless heartbeat—Serevan stood at the exact center of her own unraveling, and Emrys thought: *she'll kill me now. People do. It's easier than grieving.*

Then Lady Serevan, first victim of Vael Corvane, turned around, drew her sword, and cut down the light-drinker reaching for Emrys's back.

"Bridge!" she bellowed, in a voice that had commanded interrogators for a decade and now commanded something very different. "The bridge is lost! Fall back to the south bank—now!"

* * *

It should have ended there. With Serevan's defection shattering Corvane's line, with his own first creation turning her blade against him, the battle should have collapsed into rout and surrender.

Instead, the sky began to eat itself.

Emrys felt it before she understood it—a horrible suction, a draining, as though the whole world were a lung exhaling and unable to stop. Along Corvane's rear lines, the great engine-wagons were flaring white, one after another, their stolen starlight burning too fast, too hot, exactly as the vault's records had warned. Whether Corvane had triggered them deliberately or whether the dying hearts had simply torn loose, she didn't know. It didn't matter. Above the battlefield, the bruised ceiling of the sky began to *crack*, thin veins of light spidering across it like frost on a window—and through the cracks came nothing. Worse than nothing. A hunger.

"There it is," Tam said, at her shoulder, bloody and gray-faced. "The tourniquet coming off."

"He'll feed the cracks," Emrys breathed. "He'll pour everything he has left into stabilizing them—the engines, the harvested stars, maybe the sky itself—and it will hold for a year, maybe ten, and then—"

"And then?" 

"And then it takes the whole sky with it when it goes." She looked down at the ember in her hands, pulsing now, urgent, its nine hundred voices rising within her like a tide. *Now,* they seemed to say. *Here. This is why we waited.*

At the bridge's far end, Corvane stood beneath his cracking heaven with his arms spread wide, drinking the failing light of his own empire, holding up a dying sky with stolen hands.

And Emrys Quintana finally understood what she had ridden south to do. Not to win a war. Not to carry a crown. To walk into the heart of the tearing light with the last living Warden heart held against her chest, and let go of everything—her magic, her name, the storm-child she had been and the exile she had become—all at once, into the fire.

"Emrys." Tam caught her arm. His eyes were wet and furious and pleading. "*Don't.* Use it. Become the Warden. Carry it. I'll stand guard over you myself, I swear it, I'll—there has to be another way, there's always another way, I've spent my whole life finding the other way—"

"Tam." She took his face in both hands. Behind him the sky cracked wider, and the light-drinkers screamed, and the battle forgot itself in terror. "Find the other way after. Build the rain. Grow the crops. That's the buy-back—for your family, for mine, for everyone. Not power. *Weather.*"

His laugh came out broken. "You are the single most infuriating person I have ever smuggled."

"I know." She kissed his cheek, quick and fierce. "Keep them off me. As long as you can."

Then Emrys Quintana stepped onto Starfall Bridge, where her exile had begun, and walked into the storm-heart of the cracking sky, and the ember rose in her hands like a small sun finally allowed to finish its sunrise.

Behind her, Tam Vey drew both knives, turned to face the harvest, and swore—in three languages, fluently—that nothing else would reach her.

Ahead of her, the sky opened.

And Emrys opened with it.
