## 19. Reignition

The bridge had no business being beautiful.

Starfall Bridge spanned the gorge in a single arch of pale stone, older than any kingdom, older than any of the wars that had washed over it and receded. Nine years ago I had crossed it going north, alone, with nothing but a guard's grudging escort and the taste of ash in my mouth. The valley elders had stood on the far side and watched me go, and not one of them—not even Ilsa—had said my name aloud.

Now I stood on the near side again, and the bridge was full of soldiers.

Corvane's line stretched across it like a black seam stitched through the morning light: pikes, banners, and behind them the light-drinkers in their bone-pale armor, each one carrying a stolen star somewhere under the skin. You could see it if you looked—the faint glow at their throats, like swallows of lightning they hadn't yet digested. There were hundreds of them. Against us there were perhaps two hundred free-city volunteers, Tam's smuggler contacts who fought with boat-hooks and kitchen knives, and one inquisitor whose loyalty was currently a coin still spinning in the air.

And me. With a coal warm against my sternum and every dead Warden's voice whispering underneath my ribs.

"They've stopped advancing," Tam said beside me. He had a cut along his jaw from the Ashwood and had refused, with great theatrical offense, to let anyone bandage it properly. "That's either good or very bad."

"It's Corvane," I said. "It's theater. He wants an audience."

"Of course he does. Men like him always want an audience." Tam rolled his shoulders. "I once knew a fence who negotiated like this. Made you wait while he arranged his rings just so. I robbed him instead. Best decision of my life."

"Your life is full of best decisions."

"I'm still alive, aren't I? That's practically a winning streak." But his hand found mine for half a second—brief, calloused, real—and then he stepped back into his place among the volunteers, because we both understood how this worked now. Love arrived sideways in our lives, disguised as logistics.

The parley banner rose from the center of the bridge.

* * *

Corvane rode out himself, which told me everything about his confidence. A man who feared losing did not make himself the centerpiece.

He was not what the songs made him. No crown, no black cloak snapping dramatically in the wind—he wore plain officer's gray, and his face was tired in a way that looked almost honest. That was his genius, I was learning. Vael Corvane looked like a man doing an unpleasant job as well as it could be done. The kind of face that apologized to you while it signed the order.

"Emrys Quintana." He said my true name too—the storm-name, the one the dying cultist had whispered—and hearing it in his mouth was like finding someone else's fingerprints inside your own house. "You've come a long way to die on a bridge."

"You've come a long way to build an empire on stolen light," I said. "We're both sentimental."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Almost approval. "I offered you restoration once. I offer it again, and this time without witnesses to complicate your pride. Walk away. Keep the ember buried somewhere no one will ever find it. In ten years, when my harvest is stable and the land has healed, no one will remember there was ever a war."

"The land hasn't healed," I said. "I've walked it. The rivers run wrong. The wheat comes up silver and doesn't feed anyone. Your stars are eating everything, and you know it—that's why you need the ember. It's the only heart you haven't drunk dry, and your harvested power is coming apart at the seams."

For a moment—a long, dangerous moment—his mask slipped, and underneath it was not a monster but a frightened man holding a dam together with his hands. "Yes," he said quietly. "It is. Which is why you should give it to me. Because I am the only person alive with the machinery to contain what happens if it isn't contained. You'd burn half the coast trying to do alone what my Wardens—" He caught himself. His Wardens. His *prisoners*. "Give it to me, storm-caller. Or watch me take it, and take the sky down with us."

Behind him, Lady Serevan sat her horse like a drawn blade, and she was watching him.

Not watching me. Watching *him*.

In the vault, days ago, she had read the same records I had. She had learned what Corvane's memory-craft could do, because she had felt the seams in her own past pull open like old stitches. She had ridden to this bridge anyway—but she had been watching him ever since, the way you watch a fire you've only just realized has been burning your house down around you for years.

"Last chance," Corvane said.

"No," I said.

He sighed, and it was almost gentle. "So be it. Take her."

* * *

The battle began the way all his battles began: with light.

The light-drinkers opened their throats and poured out starfire in sheets, white and silent and wrong, the color of a sky at noon when it should be midnight. Our front line folded like paper. I heard Tam screaming orders in smuggler's cant—words that meant *left, left, get low, the hooks, use the hooks*—and then I stopped hearing anything at all, because the ember woke up.

It didn't speak. It never spoke in words. But nine years of suppressed magic came flooding up through me like a river through a broken levee, and the ember rode the flood, and suddenly I remembered—I *remembered*, fully, bodily—what it was to be the storm.

I raised my hands and called the rain.

Not the wild thing that had torn the Warden council apart when I was a child weapon too small to know what I held. This was the craft Ilsa's map had led me toward, the discipline the ember had taught me in the vault's quiet dark: carry the memory without being consumed. Aim the grief. The clouds answered from three horizons at once, and the first thunder cracked over Starfall Bridge like the world clearing its throat.

Rain hit the starfire and hissed into steam. The free-city line rallied behind the cover of it. For one bright, stupid moment, I thought we might actually hold.

Then Corvane stopped being patient.

He walked to the center of the bridge, unrolled his harvested sky like a merchant displaying cloth, and pulled.

I felt it happen before I understood it. Every light-drinker on the field staggered as he drew the stolen stars out through their skin—hundreds of swallowed suns streaming upward into his hands, gathering into a sphere above the bridge that burned without warmth and lit without shadow. The soldiers nearest him simply dropped, emptied, husks in gray armor. He didn't look at them. He had never looked at them; they had been currency since the day he took them.

And the sphere began to eat.

Not the battlefield. The *sky*. I watched the actual stars—the few that had crept back since the night they fell—wink out one by one above the western horizon, devoured, dragged toward his gathered light like moths toward a lamp that ate moths. The dark overhead deepened to something absolute, a hole where the heavens used to be.

"He's collapsing the harvest," Serevan said. She had come to stand beside me without my noticing, her armor streaked with soot and someone else's blood. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman standing on the last intact plank of a burning floor. "If he can't stabilize it with the ember, he'll consume it all. Every star. Everything. He'd rather own a dead sky than share a living one."

"That's madness."

"Yes." She said it simply, without drama. "I know. I served it for eleven years." She turned her head, and in the terrible white light I saw her face clearly for the first time—saw the woman underneath the inquisitor, young and edited and grieving for a self she couldn't retrieve. "He built my memories, Quintana. My devotion. My whole self, stitched from his convenience. And I felt it tear loose in the vault, and do you know what was underneath?" She almost smiled. "Nothing I recognized. And nothing worth defending him with."

"So what are you going to do?"

She drew her sword and started walking toward the bridge.

"Lady Serevan," I said, "that's suicide."

"Probably." Over her shoulder: "But I'm told I have a great deal of stolen time to spend."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way catastrophes do—all the slow dread compressed into ninety seconds of noise.

Serevan reached Corvane's guard and went through them like weather. She had spent a decade hunting rogue mages for the Pale Court, and every trick she'd ever used was written in her body; the light-drinkers around her hesitated to strike their own commander, and hesitation was death. Tam's volunteers surged forward behind her, boat-hooks and fury and sheer outnumbered stubbornness. The black line wavered. Cracked. I saw Corvane turn, see his own inquisitor cutting toward him, and for the first time all day his tired, reasonable face showed something human: betrayal landing exactly where he'd planted it.

"Serevan!" he shouted. "You are *mine*—"

"I was," she said, and struck.

Her blade caught his shoulder and the gathered sphere screamed. Not the man—the light. All those murdered stars shrieked at once, a sound like glass remembering it used to be sand, and Corvane lost his grip on the harvest.

That was the mistake that ended him and nearly ended everything else. The sphere tore loose from his hands and hung there, masterless, ravenous, and began pulling harder—not just at the sky now but at the ground, the river, the air itself, drinking the world to fill the shape its thief had carved into it. The bridge stones groaned. The gorge winds reversed. Somewhere behind me a volunteer began to scream and couldn't stop.

Tam grabbed my arm. "Emrys. Tell me you have an idea. Please tell me you have an idea, because I've staked my entire personality on you having an idea."

"I have an idea."

"Is it a survivable idea?"

I touched the ember through my shirt. Warm. Patient. Full of every Warden who had ever given their heart to hold a sky in place, waiting, all of them, all this time, for someone to finish the work.

"No," I said honestly.

His grip tightened. "Emrys—"

"It can't be wielded, Tam. Not by him, not by the council, not by me. I finally understand what the vault records meant. It's not a weapon and it's not a crown. It's a *key*—and keys don't fight locks, they open them. If I take it into the storm-heart, into the middle of that thing, I can release the Wardens' hearts into the harvest. All of them at once. The stolen light won't be stolen anymore—it'll be *returned*. Freed. It'll tear out of him like water out of a broken cup."

"And you?"

I looked at him. He already knew. He'd probably known since the Ashwood, since the bells, since the night he caught a storm-caller stealing bread and decided, against every instinct that had kept him alive, to help her instead of selling her.

"That's the price of opening a lock from the inside," I said.

"There has to be another way. There's always another way, I've spent my whole life finding the other way—"

"Not this time." I pulled his hand off my arm gently, and kissed his knuckles, because if love arrived sideways in our lives it deserved at least to leave facing forward. "When it's done, the rain will fall. Make sure someone counts the drops for me."

"Emrys—"

I stepped off the bluff into the wind.

* * *

The storm caught me like an old friend who'd been waiting at a station for nine years.

I rose on it—no wings, nothing so graceful, just the updraft and my own nature finally, *finally* aimed at the right enemy—and the ember blazed against my chest, and the dead Wardens rose with me. Not bodies. Voices. Light. Every heart Corvane's machine had crushed, every guardian the old regime had spent like coin, singing up through my bones in harmonies older than exile. They weren't angry. That was the unbearable part. They were *glad*. Glad someone had carried them home.

Below, the sphere drank the last visible star, and the sky over the world went absolutely black.

Above, I opened my hands, and the ember rose to meet it, and I let go of everything else.

The storm-heart opened like a door I had been born on the wrong side of.

* * *

Light took the bridge, the gorge, the drowned fields, the faces of everyone I loved looking up—

and somewhere inside the blaze, small and stubborn and ordinary, a girl who had once been a weapon thought: *at least this time, it's my choice.*

Then the door closed, and the world began to end, or begin—I couldn't yet tell which.
