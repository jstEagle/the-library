## 20. The Unmaking

The storm-heart was not a place. That was the first thing Emrys understood, once she stopped trying to understand it as a place at all.

It was a *remembering*.

She stood—or she had the sensation of standing—on a bridge made of nothing but the idea of a bridge, the same one where her exile had begun nine years ago, except now it stretched across a chasm of light so bright it had no color. Around her, the harvested stars wheeled like startled birds. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one a heart that had once beaten in the chest of a Warden, each one ripped out and caged in Corvane's machinery of conquest, and all of them were singing.

Not screaming. She had expected screaming.

They were singing the way her mother used to sing when rain came to the Hollow Valley after a long dry summer—low, wordless, relieved. As if they had been waiting so long for someone to open the door that they had made their peace with waiting, and now here she was, hand on the latch, and they could hardly believe it.

"You're late," said a voice near her shoulder, dry as old paper.

Emrys turned. The Warden stood there—the first one, the one whose memories had been folded into the ember since before Emrys was born—a woman shaped from dusk and stubbornness, with eyes like the last coals of a banked fire. In the ember's memories she had always been distant, half-glimpsed, a teacher seen through fog. Here she was close enough to have wrinkles. Close enough to look tired in a way that made Emrys's chest ache.

"I know," Emrys said. "I'm sorry. I took the scenic route."

"Nine years of exile, three months of running, one smuggler with terrible taste in hats." The Warden's mouth twitched. "Yes. Scenic."

"How do you know about Tam's hat?"

"The ember remembers what you remember, child. You looked at that hat four hundred times. You thought about setting it on fire eleven times."

"...Twelve," Emrys admitted.

The Warden laughed—a real laugh, warm and cracked—and for one impossible moment the storm around them went quiet, as if the whole sky were leaning in to listen. Then the light pressed close again, hungry and grieving at once, and Emrys felt the weight of it settle on her shoulders like wet wool.

Below the bridge—if below meant anything here—Corvane's stolen power writhed. She could see it now for what it truly was: not a hoard but a wound. Starlight crammed into vessels too small to hold it, forced into shapes it hated, burning through soldiers and siege-engines and the pale, hollow-eyed light-drinkers who carried it in their veins like poison they'd learned to call wine. It was tearing at the seams of the world. Left alone, it would eat the sky itself, and then everything Corvane had built would come down on everyone who had ever lived under it.

"All right," Emrys said. Her voice sounded small against all that light. "Tell me how this works. Tell me exactly how this works, because I want to be very clear about what I'm agreeing to before I agree to it."

"You already agreed," the Warden said gently. "On the bridge. When you walked into the storm instead of away from it."

"I'd still like the details."

"The details are simple." The Warden took her hands. Her grip was cool and solid and human, or had been human once, which amounted to the same thing. "You open your memory. All of it. Every storm you ever called, every night you can't recall, every piece of yourself you've spent nine years pretending wasn't yours. You give it back to them." She nodded upward, at the wheeling hearts. "Memory calls to memory. Your storms will show them the shape of freedom—they'll remember what it is to move, to choose, to fall as rain instead of being *held*. And then they'll go."

"And me?"

The Warden was quiet for a moment. In that quiet, Emrys heard everything the woman wasn't saying: the arithmetic of it, the price written in small careful numbers.

"You were made from the same craft," the Warden said finally. "Storm-calling isn't a gift you were given, Emrys. It's a door that was opened in you by people who should never have touched a child. When the stars go free, they will take the door with them. Not your life. Not your heart. Not your memories—those stay yours, every one, even the terrible ones. Especially those." Her thumb brushed the mark on Emrys's wrist, the storm-caller's sigil, faintly warm. "But the calling itself. The wind answering when you speak. That goes home with them."

Emrys thought about it.

That was the strange part, looking back on it forever after—that in the heart of the end of everything, she gave herself a moment simply to think. About the first storm she could remember, age six, lifting her off her feet in the orchard while her mother screamed her name from the doorway. About the valley's wards pressing down on her magic like a hand over her mouth for nine years, and how even suppressed, even punished, even exiled—some small animal part of her had always known the storm was there. Waiting. Hers. The one thing in all the world that had never once lied to her about what it was.

And about Tam, down on the bridge behind her, shouting her name into a hurricane with a bleeding side and an expression she suspected she would be hearing about for the rest of her life.

"It'll hurt," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"Good," Emrys said. "It ought to."

She opened the door.

* * *

It did not hurt the way she expected. It hurt the way grief hurts—not sharp but vast, everywhere at once, a tide going out and taking the shoreline with it.

Her memories poured out of her like light through a broken window, and the stars caught them.

She gave them the orchard storm, and somewhere in the wheeling dark a heart remembered flight. She gave them the night of her exile—the true night, the whole of it now, unhidden, the council chamber and the child she had been and the Wardens dying under a sky she had torn open at the command of men whose names she'd never known—and ten thousand voices went silent with the weight of it, and then, unbelievably, began to sing again, softer, a lullaby-shaped thing. Forgiveness was not a word anyone spoke. But it was in the music. She heard it anyway.

She gave them Ilsa's kitchen, the smell of bread, the map folded into her palm. She gave them Tam's terrible jokes, the wheatfields, the road of hanging bells, Serevan's face in the moment she learned what had been done to her. She gave them Merrowgate burning and the handful of mages pulled alive from the smoke because a girl who had sworn off storms broke her vow one more time for strangers.

And the stars drank it all in, and grew brighter, and brighter, and brighter—

—and Corvane's power *shattered*.

She felt it happen from the inside. Far away, in the world where bodies and bridges and armies lived, the usurper's harvest tore loose from its moorings like ivy ripped from a wall. Every light-drinker staggered as the stolen fire left their veins. Every siege-engine went dark. Every soldier who had marched under a sunless sky suddenly stood beneath something vast and returning, and Emrys felt, distantly, the sound a hundred thousand people make when the stars come back—not a cheer, not yet, just one long involuntary breath, the sound of a held breath released after years.

Corvane himself she sensed only as a great hollowing, a man discovering in real time that the mountain he had built himself into was made of other people's hearts, and that the hearts were leaving.

Good, she thought, with a ferocity that surprised her. Let him feel it. Let him feel exactly what it's like to be emptied by something larger than yourself.

Then the tide reached her own shore.

The storm rose up out of her—not violently; tenderly, if a storm can be tender, and this one was, this one had been hers since she was six years old and it lifted her like a parent lifting a child over a fence. It knew her. It had been the only constant of her life, the companion of her exile, the hunger under her skin, and now it gathered itself up out of her bones the way you gather your things before leaving a house you loved, slowly, touching each room.

*Thank you,* it seemed to say. Which was absurd. Storms didn't speak. But the memory of it would stay with her longer than almost anything else from that night: the sense of being thanked. Of having been, all along, a partnership rather than a possession.

"No," Emrys whispered, into the singing dark. "Thank *you*. I'm sorry it ends like this. I'm sorry I ever—" She couldn't finish. The storm finished it for her, folding around her once, briefly, the way a blanket settles over sleeping shoulders.

Then it was gone.

* * *

Silence.

Not the silence of suppression, which had been a silence like a gag. This was ordinary silence. The silence of a night with no wind in it. Emrys lay on her back on scorched stone—at the base of Starfall Bridge, she realized dimly, though the bridge above her was less a structure now than a suggestion—and stared straight up.

The stars were coming back.

They did not snap on all at once. They returned the way dawn returns, a few at first in the east, then dozens, then rivers of them, pouring back into their ancient places until the whole sky was so thick with light it looked like the sea seen from underneath. The Milky Way unfurled overhead like a banner someone had kept folded for years and finally shaken out. There were constellations she had only read about in Ilsa's forbidden books, patterns the valley's elders called myths because no living person had seen them. They hung there, patient, unmistakable, as if they had never left—as if *she* were the one who had been gone, and the sky had waited all this time for her to look up.

Emrys laughed. It came out ragged, half sob, and she let it, because there was no one to perform composure for, and because it was either laugh or lie here forever.

She tried, experimentally, to call the wind.

Nothing.

She tried again, reaching for the old door in herself, the place where the pressure used to build before a storm, the hum in her teeth, the itch behind her eyes—

There was a place where the door had been. That was the worst of it. She could feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth, the tongue going again and again to the gap. Where the storm had lived there was now simply... weather. Real weather, indifferent weather, weather that would do what it pleased whether she begged it or not. Rain clouds gathering far to the west, moving at their own pace, owing her nothing.

Ordinary. She was ordinary.

She lay there a long time, feeling the shape of that word, testing it for cracks. Ordinary Emrys Quintana, who could walk through the Hollow Valley without wards. Who could stand in a crowd without elders flinching. Who could raise a daughter someday, if she ever wanted one, without lying awake wondering what regime might find a use for her.

Ordinary, and alive, and emptied out like a house after the movers have gone.

Footsteps. Unsteady ones. Then Tam's face swung into view above hers, upside down from her angle, bloodied at the temple, filthy past description, wearing—the gods preserve her—the hat. Half of it had burned away. He looked, she thought fondly, like a rooster that had survived a house fire.

"Emrys." His voice cracked on the second syllable. He dropped to his knees beside her, hands hovering over her without landing, patting the air as if checking she was solid. "You're—you're breathing, okay, good, excellent, don't stop doing that. Can you hear me? Blink twice if—"

"I'm fine, Tam."

"You're *fine*. You walked into a godsdamned star-storm, alone, after telling me—I quote—'wait here,' which is the single most ominous phrase in any language—"

"TAM."

He stopped. He was shaking. She saw it now, up close: not fear for himself, fear that had nowhere to go, rattling around inside him looking for an exit.

"I'm fine," she said again, softer. She reached up—her arm ached, everything ached, beautifully, mundanely, the aches of a body and not a battlefield—and pushed the ruined hat back from his forehead. "Better than fine. Come here and see."

He frowned, but he lay down beside her in the ash, gingerly, favoring his bandaged side, and followed her gaze up.

Tam Vey, smuggler, cynic, man who had once told her the stars were just holes where the sky leaked and who charged extra for night travel because "nothing good happens under them"—went completely silent.

A tear tracked down through the grime on his cheek. He didn't wipe it away. He didn't say anything clever. For a full minute he just breathed, staring up at a sky he had genuinely believed he would never see again, the sky his parents had died under the first time it went dark.

"There's so many," he whispered finally.

"I know."

"That one there—" He pointed. "Is that—my mother used to point out that one. The Fisherman. I thought I'd invented it. I thought I'd *made it up*, because I was a kid, and—"

"You didn't make it up."

"Yeah." His voice was wrecked. He laughed wetly. "Yeah. Okay." Then, because he was Tam, and silence and sincerity could only carry him so far: "So. Hypothetically. If a person had staked a considerable sum on the ember—emotionally speaking, not financially, mostly—and that ember had just, say, detonated into the entire night sky—"

"Considerable emotional losses, yes."

"I'm ruined," he said solemnly. "Financially and spiritually. You'll have to support me now. I'm thinking a small cottage. Near the sea. You do the cooking, obviously, I've seen your cooking."

"My cooking is fine."

"Your cooking is a war crime, Emrys, and I say that as a man who has *seen* war crimes this month." But he said it while reaching over and finding her hand in the ash, and neither of them let go, and above them the Fisherman wheeled slow and golden across a sky that went on forever.

Down the slope, she could hear voices—Serevan's, hoarse, giving orders that were for once not monstrous; the free cities' scouts shouting in disbelief; someone weeping openly and without shame. Somewhere in the wreckage of the bridge, she knew, soldiers were pulling Vael Corvane from the ruins of his own ambition, alive, diminished, mortal. That reckoning belonged to tomorrow, and to the cities, and to the long slow work of justice without shortcuts.

Tonight there was only the ash, and the cold coming in honest off the mountains, and the two of them lying side by side under ten thousand returned stars.

"Emrys?" Tam said, after a while. Sleep was dragging at his voice.

"Mm?"

"What's it like? Being—" He gestured vaguely at all of her. "After."

She considered it. The gap where the door had been. The missing tooth. The terrifying, miraculous lightness of a self with nothing hidden in its basement.

"Quiet," she said at last. "Really, really quiet."

And far to the west, over the Hollow Valley, over the wards being rebuilt and the gates soon to be cut open in them, the first true rain in nine years was gathering—all on its own, asking no one's permission—and beginning, very gently, to fall.
