## 21. After the Stars Return

They took Corvane alive on the third day after the bridge, which surprised everyone except Tam.

"He's a man who spent ten years drinking starlight," Tam said, picking at a bowl of gray porridge in the Caldreth mess hall. "Men like that don't die. They get *deposed*. There's always some clerk with a ledger full of his crimes and a strong opinion about due process."

He was right. The free cities' soldiers found him in the wreckage below Starfall Bridge, half-buried in ash, his harvested power gone so thoroughly from him that he looked—Emrys heard later, from someone who had seen it—not like a fallen king but like an old man who'd been caught in the rain. He did not fight them. He asked whether anyone had survived the bridge, and when they said yes, most of them, he nodded once and let himself be chained.

That was the part Emrys couldn't stop turning over. Not the chains. The question about survivors.

"You're frowning at your breakfast," Tam observed. "The porridge is doing that already. You don't need to add to it."

"I keep waiting for it to feel finished," she said.

"It won't." He shrugged. "Finished is for books. This is just *after*. After goes on for ages. It's the longest part of anything."

* * *

Caldreth had been the last city to hold out against Corvane, and now it was the first to have to figure out what came next, which was rather like being handed the bill for a feast you'd slept through. The council met in the old salt exchange—a vaulted hall built by merchant princes who had believed, sincerely and incorrectly, that their grandchildren would want to look at marble while arguing about tariffs.

Emrys attended the first session because refusing outright seemed rude. It was also, she admitted privately, because she wanted to see what justice looked like when nobody could cheat with magic anymore.

It looked slow. That was the honest answer. A woman from the harbormasters' guild read aloud for forty minutes the list of Corvane's seized assets, and a man from the weavers objected to the valuation of three warehouses, and somewhere in the middle of it all a delegate fell asleep, audibly, and had to be nudged awake by his neighbor.

"This is what you bled for," Tam whispered. "A meeting about warehouses."

"Yes," Emrys said, and found, to her own surprise, that her eyes were stinging. "Isn't it wonderful?"

Because that was the thing. Before, a man like Corvane would simply have taken the warehouses, and the stars, and the mages, and the future, and no committee of tired merchants in bad wool could have stopped him. Now there was only the slow machinery of ordinary people insisting on counting things correctly. It was tedious as gravel. It was also the first government in nine years that couldn't be unmade by one person's ambition and another person's storm.

On the fourth day, the council's speaker—an angular grandmother named Odile Ferrant who ran the city's grain reserves with the tenderness other people reserved for grandchildren—asked Emrys to stand.

"The free cities owe you a debt that cannot be paid," Odile said. "We can only offer you a place among us. A seat on this council. A voice in what we build."

The hall turned toward her. Emrys stood, feeling every eye, feeling the absence where the storm used to live inside her—the quiet that still startled her some mornings, reaching for thunder and finding only her own heartbeat.

"Thank you," she said. "No."

A rustle went through the benches. Tam, seated behind her, made a small sound of approval that he tried to disguise as a cough.

"You saved us," Odile said. Not accusingly. Genuinely puzzled.

"I burned something down," Emrys said. "Mostly myself, but not entirely. And I'm not the right person to sit in judgment over what comes next, because I know exactly what I'd be tempted to do with a seat like that." She paused. "I spent years being told I was dangerous. Then a few months being told I was necessary. Both of those felt true from the inside. A council needs people who are suspicious of themselves, and I've had quite enough of my own company on that front. Find someone who's never been a weapon. Let them learn caution the boring way."

Odile studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a dry, creased thing.

"My late husband wanted a statue of himself in the harbor square," she said. "I talked him out of it. Best argument we ever had." She inclined her head. "The offer stands if you change your mind. And the debt stands regardless. Debts are patient."

"That," Emrys murmured as she sat back down, "is the most frightening thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

"Wait until they send the invoice," Tam said.

* * *

Serevan came to her on the sixth day, under escort, wearing plain gray clothes that hung wrong on her—clothes chosen by someone else, for someone else's idea of a prisoner.

She looked smaller without the pale court's regalia. Smaller, and older, and somehow more honestly made, the way a house looks after you strip away the painted facade and find good bones underneath anyway.

"They're giving me a trial," Serevan said, without preamble. She stood across the table in the guest room they'd given Emrys—given her, not locked her in, though Emrys noticed the door didn't lock from the inside either. Trust was being extended carefully, like a hand across ice. "Or a hearing. They can't decide what to call it. Half of them want to hang me and half of them think hanging me would be, quote, 'wasting an asset.'"

"What do you want?"

Serevan sat down. Her hands were steady, but she looked at them as if checking.

"I remembered," she said. "All of it. On the bridge. What he took from me. What he—" Her jaw tightened. "Do you know what it is, to find out your whole self was furnished by someone else? That your love, your loyalty, your *purpose* were rooms he built in your head?" She exhaled slowly. "You do know. Of course you do. Yours was a different kind of theft, but you've lived with a hole where your history should be since before I ever met you."

"I lived with it badly," Emrys said.

"So have I. For about six days." A ghost of the old dry humor surfaced and submerged again. "The trial will ask me what I did. I intend to answer fully. Every interrogation, every mage culled, every family broken for the harvest. I remember all of it now—that's the joke of it, isn't it? He gave me perfect recall along with everything else. I'll be the best witness they've ever had."

"And then?"

"Then exile." Serevan said it plainly, the way you'd state a destination. "Not theirs. Mine. I'll ask for it. There's a settlement north of here, past the Salt Reach—people who were broken by the old wars, who went out past the maps to build something without any of it. I'm told they take anyone willing to work." She almost smiled. "I'm very good at work. I'm told I'm terrible at everything else."

Emrys was quiet for a moment. Nine years ago she had walked out of the Hollow Valley with nothing but shame and a name she couldn't use. She knew the shape of that road.

"They'll hate you out there too, at first," she said.

"Yes."

"And then they'll forget why, and then they'll forget to hate you, and one morning someone will hand you bread without thinking about it, and that will be the hardest day of all."

Serevan looked at her sharply. "Is that how it was?"

"That's how it was."

Something moved through the other woman's face—grief, maybe, or relief, or the strange cousin of both. She stood, smoothed her borrowed clothes, and at the door she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, "on the bridge. When I turned. You didn't convince me. He convinced me—he couldn't help editing one memory too many, and it unraveled. But you gave me the space to see it. You could have killed me and didn't." She lifted her chin slightly. "When they write this all down, make sure they write that you're a fool."

"I'll dictate it personally," Emrys promised, and Serevan huffed something that in a better world might have been a laugh, and left.

Tam emerged from behind the changing screen, where he had absolutely been eavesdropping the entire time.

"'Make sure they write that you're a fool,'" he repeated. "Poetic. She's wasted on exile. She should write ballads."

"She nearly flayed the skin off you in the Ashwood."

"All the great artists are difficult." He dropped into the vacated chair and stole a heel of bread from the plate. "So. The council offered you a throne-let and you said no. Serevan's exiling herself into a life of turnips. Corvane's awaiting trial in a cell with a view of the harbor. Everyone's accounted for." He chewed, watching her. "Everyone except us. What happens to us?"

The *us* sat in the room between them, plain as furniture. Tam Vey did not say things like *us* casually. Tam Vey once described a near-death experience as "a scheduling inconvenience." Emrys set down her cup.

"I'm going home," she said. "To the Hollow Valley."

His chewing slowed. "The valley that exiled you."

"The same. The wards are being rebuilt—Ilsa wrote, they've got gates now. Actual gates, that open, with hinges and everything. Very modern." She smiled faintly. "And I want to be there when the first caravan comes through. I want to watch people arrive and be welcomed instead of warded off. I want—" She stopped, started again. "I spent nine years learning how to leave places. I'd like to learn the other thing."

Tam was silent long enough that Emrys began composing graceful retreats in her head. Then he said, "What's the road north like this time of year?"

"Muddy. Cold. Infested with toll collectors."

"Sounds terrible." He brushed crumbs from his vest with tremendous dignity. "Fortunately for you, I know a smuggler's route that avoids all three. Well. Avoids two of the three. The mud is non-negotiable."

"Toll collectors included in the route?"

"Included, befriended, and mildly exploited. I have arrangements." He stood, offering her his arm with the flourish of a man presenting a royal decree. "Madam storm-caller. Madam former storm-caller. Shall we go be ordinary somewhere with worse weather?"

Emrys laughed—really laughed, the first time since the bridge—and took his arm.

Outside the window, over the rooftops of Caldreth, the stars were coming out one by one, punctual and indifferent and unbearably beautiful. Somewhere among them, perhaps, the freed Wardens were finding their way back into the sky's old offices, remembering how to burn.

But that night, walking the harbor road with Tam arguing cheerfully about the optimal bribery of northern tollmen, Emrys Quintana thought the brightest thing in the dark was the lantern ahead of them, and the road it lit going home.
