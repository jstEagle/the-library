## 22. The Wild Ember Again

The road to the Hollow Valley had not gotten any shorter in the year since Emrys had left it, but it had gotten better marked. Someone—probably Ilsa, probably out of spite—had planted waystones every mile, white stones carved with a small open gate and the words THE VALLEY IS FOUND in letters deep enough to read from horseback.

"Found," Tam said, squinting at one as they passed. "That's optimistic grammar for people who've lived in the same hole for nine generations."

"They mean found again," Emrys said.

"I know what they mean. I'm being funny."

"You're being a smuggler with nothing left to smuggle. It's an adjustment."

He made a wounded noise that would have carried more weight if he weren't leading his mule with the tenderness of a man carrying a sleeping child. The mule was named Duchess, which Tam insisted was ironic, and which the mule seemed to take as literal instruction. She had bitten two toll collectors and one minor official of Caldreth's new council, and Emrys had come to love her deeply.

They walked. The land around them was doing something it hadn't done in years: growing without supervision. Fields ran wild along the roadside, wheat and barley and things Emrys couldn't name pushing up through old ash, because the Ashwood's ash, it turned out, had been good for the soil all along. The free cities called it the Reckoning Season. The farmers called it a very late spring and argued about whether to thank the Wardens or the weather.

Emrys kept her opinions on the subject to herself. She was, by every measurement anyone had yet devised, an ordinary woman now. Her wrists were bare where the storm-marks had been; the skin there was smooth and pale and unremarkable, and she still caught herself looking at it sometimes the way you look at a room after the furniture is gone.

The rain started an hour before sundown. Not a storm—she would have felt the difference even without magic, the way a former sailor feels the sea—a plain, honest rain, steady as a metronome, falling straight down through the pines with no wind behind it at all.

Tam pulled up his hood. "You're smiling at it," he accused.

"I'm allowed."

"You smile at it like it's a letter from someone. It's water, Em. It falls out of the sky because of condensation, which is a thing I learned recently and intend to use often."

"It fell the night I arrived home last time too," she said. "The first time. When I was eleven and my mother brought me over the pass." She tilted her face into it. "I'd forgotten until just now."

"That's convenient."

"It's memory. It comes when it likes. You taught me that."

Tam grumbled something about teachers and their students and walked on, but he let the silence sit comfortably between them, which was how Emrys knew he was happy. Tam talked when he was afraid or selling something. He went quiet when he was content, and lately he had gone quiet more and more often, and Emrys had decided not to remark on it, in the same way one decides not to poke a fire that has finally caught.

* * *

The border of the Hollow Valley announced itself differently now. Once it had been wards—invisible, absolute, a wall of suppression so complete that Emrys had spent nine years inside it feeling like a bell with a cloth stuffed in its mouth. Now there were posts. Wooden ones, chest-high, carved with the same open-gate sigil as the waystones, strung together not with ward-thread but with actual bells—small copper bells that rang softly in the rain, announcing visitors rather than repelling them.

Emrys stopped at the first post and stood there longer than she meant to.

"You could go around," Tam offered. "There's a gap about half a mile east. I looked."

"Why did you look?"

"In case we needed to leave quickly. Old habits." He shrugged. "Also in case you needed to stand at the border looking like a woman deciding whether to knock on a door she once set on fire. I looked for that too."

She laughed, and the sound surprised her—light, unforced. "I didn't set anything on fire."

"The elders might disagree."

"The elders voted unanimously to welcome me home. I have it in writing." She tapped the satchel where the letter sat, folded twice, its wax seal unbroken because she hadn't been able to bring herself to break it yet. Opening it would make it real, and she had learned to be careful about what she made real.

"Then what's the problem?"

"The problem," Emrys said, "is that the vote was six days ago, and in six days a person can change their mind forty times, and I would deserve every one of those changes."

Tam considered this. Then he reached over and took the letter out of her satchel, broke the seal with his thumbnail, and read it aloud in a terrible imitation of an elder's cracked solemnity: *"Come home, Emrys Quintana. The valley keeps your memory honestly now, which is to say it keeps all of it, including the parts we preferred to forget. The gate opens at dusk. Bring the smuggler if you must. — Ilsa."*

"She wrote 'if you must,'" Tam repeated, delighted. "Your mentor and I are going to be great friends."

"She already knows everything about you. I wrote to her."

"I know. That's why I'm nervous."

* * *

Dusk came violet and slow, and with it came the valley itself, unfolding below them like something from the ember's oldest memories—except the ember was gone now, burned to nothing at Starfall Bridge, and these memories were only her own, ordinary and human and hers.

Lamplight in the windows. Woodsmoke. The Founding Festival banners going up along the main street, early this year, the poles freshly cut—the valley celebrating not the founding this time but the reopening, though the elders had argued for three hours about what to call it and settled, in classic Hollow Valley fashion, on calling it nothing at all and simply holding it.

And at the border posts, waiting in the rain under oilcloth cloaks, stood seven figures.

Emrys counted them twice. Seven elders. Nine years ago there had been twelve, and every one of the remaining five had died or stepped down or, in Elder Brannic's case, moved to the coast "for his health," which everyone understood to mean away from the memory of what they'd done to a frightened girl with a storm in her hands.

"They came," she said stupidly.

"You have it in writing," Tam reminded her.

Ilsa stood at their center, older than Emrys remembered, thinner, her hair gone fully white and cropped short for practicality. But her eyes were the same: sharp, unsentimental, capable of reducing a grown mage to a stammering apprentice with a single raised eyebrow. That eyebrow was currently deployed.

"You're standing in the rain," Ilsa said.

"You're standing in the rain."

"I live here. What's your excuse?"

Emrys crossed the distance between them and let her mentor hold her, which Ilsa did stiffly, briefly, and completely. Around them the other elders shifted, and Emrys braced for speeches—for apology, which she had rehearsed declining, or gratitude, which would have been worse.

Instead, Elder Maren, who had cast the loudest vote for sealing the passes a lifetime ago, simply held out her hand and said, "You look tired. There's stew."

"There's—" Emrys blinked rain out of her eyes. "Stew."

"And bread. And a bed at the Wayfarer's Rest, unless you'd rather stay with me, but I keep chickens now and they judge."

"Then stew," Emrys managed.

"Good. Decisive. We heard you were decisive at the bridge." Maren's voice did something small and careful around the word *bridge*, and Emrys understood that the valley knew—not the legend version, not the ballads that Tam swore were already circulating in three cities ("there are *verses*, Em, one of them rhymes 'unmaking' with 'heartbreaking,' I couldn't stop it")—but the true version. The version where a young woman had been aimed like a weapon at innocent people, and later chose to unmake herself rather than become one again, and the choice had cost everything except her life, which was somehow the most expensive part.

"We'll want to hear it all," Elder Corrin said quietly. "When you're ready. All of it—including what we did. The council keeps minutes now. Honest ones. It's harder than it sounds."

"I know," Emrys said. "I've seen honest minutes. They ruined my whole life."

Corrin laughed—a startled, rusty sound—and some knot in the evening loosened.

* * *

The stew was excellent. The chicken-judging bed at the Wayfarer's Rest was clean and warm, and Tam took the room next to hers "strictly for security purposes," a claim undermined by the fact that within an hour he was downstairs losing gracefully at cards to three festival-goers and winning, somehow, a hat.

Emrys slept without dreams for the first time in a year.

In the morning she walked the valley.

It was the walking that undid her, more than the stew or the welcome. Because the valley was the same—every lane, every stone bridge, the millpond with its patient ducks—but she was different, and the difference showed her everything. For nine years she had lived here as through gauze: present, tolerated, watching the mountains with a hunger she'd mistaken for homesickness. Now she saw the place plainly. The scorch-mark on the north field from her storm, long ago—someone had plowed around it for years, and this spring had finally plowed *through* it, planting barley right across the scar. A fence repaired. A roof re-thatched. Small acts of people deciding to continue.

At the old ward-stone by the millrace—the anchor-point of the suppression wards, dead now, mossy, used by children as a dare-table to stand on—she found fresh carvings. Dozens of them. Names, mostly. Some dates. One entry, in a child's wobbly hand, read: *HERE THE MAGIC SLEPT AND NOW IT DOES NOT AND EVERYTHING IS FINE.*

Emrys sat down beside it and cried a little, quietly, the way you cry at weddings.

"Everything is fine," Tam said, arriving with two cups of cider and no comment on her face. "According to a reliable source."

"Go away."

"Nope." He handed her a cup. "I've decided I like it here. Don't tell anyone. My reputation can't survive it."

"What reputation? You gave your last stolen ledger to the Caldreth archives. Voluntarily. The archivists wept."

"That was strategy."

"You alphabetized it."

"*Strategy,*" Tam said with dignity, and drank his cider.

* * *

Ilsa found her at dusk on the third day, up on the ridge where the old schoolhouse had stood before the regime burned it. Only foundations remained, and a young apple tree somebody had planted in the middle of them, already bearing.

"You'll be asked," Ilsa said, sitting down beside her without preamble. "The council will ask. They're drafting a charter for the new Warden order—or whatever replaces it—and they want your name at the top of it."

"I know. Tam told me. He's already designed the seal. It's a duck."

"A duck."

"He says power should be humble. He says it while wearing a hat he won at cards, so I believe him."

Ilsa smiled, then let the smile go, because she was Ilsa. "Will you do it?"

Emrys turned the question over. A year ago—even a month ago—she would have said yes out of guilt, or no out of fear, and both answers would have been wrong in the same way. But she had spent the winter learning the craft of carrying memory without being consumed, the Wardens' discipline, practiced now without any ember at all, just practice: naming what happened, setting it down honestly, picking it up when it served and putting it down when it didn't.

"No," she said. "But I'll teach. If they build their order on consent instead of harvest, on gates instead of walls—someone should teach the first students what it costs to be aimed. And what it costs to aim." She glanced at Ilsa. "Someone who remembers both sides of it."

Ilsa was quiet for a long moment. Rain had threatened all day but not fallen; the clouds hung low and gray and undecided.

"You know what I keep thinking," her mentor said at last. "The night the stars went dark. Everyone in the valley thought it was an ending. I thought it was an ending—I voted to seal the passes because I thought the world outside had ended and we should bury ourselves before it buried us." She shook her head. "And it wasn't an ending. It was a door. We just had to build the hinges ourselves."

"That's almost poetic, Ilsa."

"Don't tell anyone. My reputation can't survive it."

From down in the valley, faintly, came the sound of the copper border-bells ringing—someone arriving, or leaving, the gates doing exactly what they were made to do, opening and closing, open and closing. Emrys listened to it the way other people listened to music.

* * *

The rain began that night, just past midnight, soft against the shutters of the Wayfarer's Rest.

Emrys woke and lay listening to it, and felt—nothing. No pull, no hunger, no storm gathering in her blood. Just the sound of water on wood, the smell of wet earth through the window crack, the ordinary miracle of a sky doing what skies do.

She got up anyway, wrapped a blanket around her shoulders, and stood in the doorway of the inn, watching it fall on the valley that had exiled her and then, slowly, painfully, learned her name again—all of it, the guilty parts included—and asked her back.

Down the street, lamplit despite the hour, the festival hall glowed. Someone was singing. Badly. Tam's voice, unmistakably, working through verses of the ballad he claimed not to endorse, and a crowd laughing, and above it all the rain coming down steady and kind on rooftops and fields and the old scar in the north field where the barley grew.

Emrys Quintana, storm-caller, exile, weapon, Warden-in-all-but-name, stood in the doorway of an inn in her home valley and was none of those things anymore. She was a woman with a blanket and bare feet and a full heart, and tomorrow there would be stew and council meetings and students, someday, and years—years, she hoped, whole ordinary years of them.

The rain fell. Everything was fine, according to a reliable source.

She smiled at it like a letter from someone, and went back inside, and closed the gate gently behind her.
