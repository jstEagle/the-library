## 2. Tonight, By Name

The rain came to Copperveil the way debts did: slowly at first, then all at once, and always on the day you'd hoped it wouldn't.

Isandro Okafor watched it through the guildhouse's tall front windows while she waited for the day's first client, her tea going cold in its chipped clay cup. Rain was bad for business in most trades. In hers it was excellent. Storms made people remember what they'd forgotten to fear, and remembering made them spend.

She had been balancing the morning ledgers when the knock came—three soft raps, unhurried, familiar as an old ache.

"Door's open, Master Ellery," she called, without turning around. "You're early. You're always early. One of these days I'm going to stop being surprised, and then where will we be?"

The man who shuffled in out of the wet was seventy if he was a day, wrapped in a coat that had been fashionable during someone else's grandmother's youth, clutching a parcel against his chest the way a miser clutches coin. Old Ellery had been trading at the Ledger Guild for longer than Isandro had been alive. He dealt in small things—a lock of hair from a woman he'd loved at nineteen, the last hour of a summer afternoon bottled by some dead artificer, a song nobody else remembered the words to. He never sold anything he actually wanted to keep. He only ever pawned things when winter got too close, and every spring he bought them back, penny by penny, like a man paying ransom for his own heart.

"You look terrible, Master Ellery," Isandro said, pulling out the client's chair for him. "Sit. Tea?"

"I look honest," Ellery said, settling down with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions about stairs. "Which is more than can be said for half the people who walk through that door. And no tea. I can't stay long." He set the parcel on the counter between them and left his hands on it a moment longer than necessary. "I need to trade something."

Isandro put down her pen. That was rule one of the counter, written into her bones if not the guild charter: *when a client hesitates, you wait.* The ledger could keep.

"What is it?" she asked gently.

"Not what." His hands trembled slightly as he turned the parcel over. "Who. Well." A wet, rueful laugh. "Neither, really. It's a book."

"A book," Isandro repeated. Books were not impossible things. The Ledger Guild traded in impossible things—bottled storms, borrowed memories, second chances decanted into glass. A book was just paper and stubbornness.

"A particular book." Ellery peeled back the oilcloth with the care of a surgeon. Beneath it lay a volume bound in cracked green leather, swollen with damp, its corners furred with mold. Water had gotten into it somewhere along the years—one entire edge of the pages rippled like the skin of pudding—and the gilt lettering on the spine had flaked away to ghosts. But even ruined, there was something about it. The kind of something that made the fine hairs on Isandro's arms stand up and take notice.

"It was my mother's," Ellery said. "And her mother's before her, and I don't know how far past that. Family says it's older than the city walls. I used to think that was just the sort of thing families say." He pushed it an inch across the counter, then pulled it back, then pushed it again, caught between two hungers. "The cellar flooded last month. Everything I own was under three feet of river. Everything except this, because I'd taken it upstairs because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because lately I've been having dreams about it. Bad ones. And my granddaughter found me standing in the hall at three in the morning holding it, and I didn't know how I got there."

Isandro kept her voice level. "What do the dreams say?"

"That's the trouble." He looked up at her, and his eyes were frightened in a very specific way—the fear of a man who has spent his whole life being sensible and has run out of sense. "They don't say anything. They *count*. Numbers going backward. Down to a date." His throat worked. "Tonight's date."

The rain filled the pause, drumming patiently on the windows.

"And you want to pawn it."

"I want it *gone*." The word cracked out of him. Then, softer, ashamed: "I know what we have. I've sat in that chair forty years, girl. I know the difference between pawning and selling. Pawning means I mean to come back." His hand flattened on the green leather. "I don't mean to come back for this one. Sell it. Whatever your rules say you must do with a thing like this. Just don't let it come home to me."

Isandro looked at the book. The book, in its sodden, patient way, seemed to look back.

*Rule eleven*, said the counter litany in her head. *A client's fear is information. Weigh it before you weigh their coin.*

"Master Ellery," she said, "you've been a client here since before I could hold a pen. So I'll tell you what I tell no one: take it to Veyra. Take it upstairs, first floor, ask for the guildmaster herself. Tell her it's a matter of provenance."

His eyebrows climbed. "You don't want the commission?"

"I want you to sleep tonight," Isandro said. "Besides, I'm a balancer, not an assessor. Ruined books aren't my line." This was true, and also a lie so smooth she barely noticed the seam, because the truth underneath it was that she wanted the book nowhere near her, and the wanting itself frightened her more than the book.

Ellery studied her for a long moment. Forty years of sitting in that chair had taught him things too.

"You're afraid of it," he said.

"I'm careful of it. There's a difference."

"There is," he agreed. "But it's a thin one, and I've fallen through thinner." He rose, groaning, rewrapped the book with hands that had steadied themselves by force of habit, and paused at the door. "Your grandfather trained you well, girl. Whoever he was. You have his same way of looking at a thing like you're reading its fine print."

He went out into the rain. Isandro stood at the window and watched him go, and told herself the cold feeling under her ribs was just the weather coming through old glass.

It was almost true. Her grandfather had taught her everything—how to hold a ledger line straight, how to hear a lie in the space between a client's sentences, how to be indispensable and invisible at the same time. What he had never taught her was why he'd insisted, on his deathbed, gripping her wrist hard enough to bruise, on making her swear one final oath beyond all the others:

*Nobody knows your name. Keep it that way. Not the guild. Not anyone. If anyone ever speaks it aloud—run.*

He hadn't told her what the name was, either. He'd simply leaned close and breathed it into her ear, once, and she had carried it ever since folded up small behind her breastbone like a coin sewn into a lining—never spoken, never written, almost never thought. Almost.

Isandro Okafor was the name the guild knew. The name on her contracts, her wages, her neat signature at the bottom of ten thousand balanced lines. The other name was not hers to use. It belonged to whoever she might have been, in whatever life her grandfather had burned down to build her this quiet one.

She went back to her ledgers. The ink behaved. The columns summed. For four hours, the world was arithmetic, and arithmetic asked nothing of her but accuracy.

Then the bell over the door rang again, and the afternoon stopped behaving entirely.

* * *

The courier was a guild runner named Pell, sixteen years old, all elbows and enthusiasm, who delivered the internal post with the gravity of a man carrying state secrets and the discretion of a town crier.

"Balance room, third floor, priority seal!" he announced, slapping a folio onto her desk. "Veyra's own office! She wants the quarterly reconciliations done by close of business, she says, and she says—" Pell dropped his voice into a fair imitation of the guildmaster's dry rasp, "*'Tell Okafor I trust her numbers more than my own eyes.'*"

"Tell her thank you," Isandro said. "And tell her flattery is a form of currency and should appear on the books."

Pell laughed all the way down the stairs.

Isandro untied the folio with fingers that were perfectly steady, thank you, and began to work. Quarterly reconciliation meant matching the vault inventory against the sales ledger against the acquisition records, page by page, item by item, hunting the small discrepancies where things leaked. Bottled storm, lot 114, one unit sold to the Margrave of Hessing for the salving of his drought—check. Memory of a first snowfall, pawned, unredeemed, ninety-one days and counting toward forfeiture—check. Second chance, gently used, returned unsatisfied by client citing nausea—

Her pen stopped.

Lot 7. Acquisition date: one hundred and six years ago. Provenance: sealed. Contents: sealed. Status: sealed. And beneath the status line, in fresh red ink that had not been there at the last audit, a single annotation in a hand she recognized as well as her own heartbeat, because it was the guildmaster's:

*Pending review. Do not touch.*

Isandro stared at it. Lot 7 was famous, in the way of things everyone knew and no one discussed—the oldest item in the deep vault, acquired the same year the guild's founding covenant was renewed, whatever that meant; even the renewal ceremony had been sealed. Apprentices dared each other to press their ears against the vault wall above it. Some claimed they heard tapping.

Like rain against the inside of a wall.

Isandro shook her head, wrote *noted* beside the annotation in her small precise hand, and moved on. But her eyes kept snagging on it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth, and by the time the light through the high windows had gone from gold to grey, she had checked the rest of the folio twice and found it clean.

She was stacking the finished pages when the sound started.

At first she took it for the rain. But the rain was against the *outside* of the building, diffuse and directionless, and this was inside—below, muffled, rhythmic. Three taps. A pause. Three taps. Patient as a creditor.

From the direction of the deep vault.

Isandro sat very still. Around her the balance room ticked through its evening noises—settling beams, cooling pipes, the wind worrying the shutters. Ordinary sounds. A building talking to itself, the way old buildings did. Everyone knew that.

Three taps. Pause. Three taps.

She gathered her folios, blew out her lamp, and left the balance room at a walk, because running would have meant admitting there was something to run from, and she had built twenty-nine years on the foundation of never admitting such things.

* * *

Her rooms were on the fourth floor, at the end of the corridor the staff called the Quiet Wing—not because it was silent but because the people housed there preferred to be. Isandro had chosen it deliberately: corner position, two exits, a window overlooking the courtyard wall, and neighbors who valued privacy the way she did. She unlocked her door, stepped in, lit one lamp, and locked it again behind her, all without breaking stride. Habit wore a groove, and the groove held.

The room was small and ruthlessly tidy. A bed made with hospital corners. A shelf of reference works arranged by subject and then by height. A single chair, a single table, a single cup. People who visited—if anyone ever visited—said it looked like a cell, or a showroom. Isandro thought of it as a held breath.

She ate standing up, bread and cheese and an apple that had seen better weeks, and she was reaching for her nightclothes when her eye caught the table and her stomach dropped through four floors of guildhouse into the cellar below.

The green book lay on her table.

Water-swollen. Mold-furred at the corners. Wrapped in oilcloth she had last seen leaving the building under old Ellery's arm.

Isandro did not move for the space of ten heartbeats. Ten slow ones, counted the way her grandfather had taught her to count when fear arrived: *one for the door, two for the knife, three for the fire, four for flight.* The lamp flame bent in a draft from the window, righted itself.

She approached the book the way one approaches a sleeping animal, which is to say slowly and with a great deal of dignity and no plan whatsoever. Up close it smelled of river water and age and, faintly, inexplicably, of woodsmoke—as though it had survived a fire somewhere in its long damp life.

*I should burn it,* she thought. *Right now. Before it opens.*

But it was already open. It had been lying closed on the counter downstairs—she was nearly sure, she was *almost* sure—and now it lay open on her table to a page near the end, and the lamplight fell across it, and Isandro read.

The script was old, hand-inked, the letters leaning like drunks supporting each other. Much of the page had been eaten by water, words drowned mid-sentence, whole lines dissolved into grey blooms. But the prophecy's spine survived the damage, the way spines do:

*...and when the last flame gutters low,*
*the covenant comes due, and the debt is called.*
*On the night the rains return twice in one week,*
*a swordspeaker shall be named—*

There the water had chewed a hole through the heart of the verse. But the next line, the line beneath, had been written larger than the rest, pressed hard into the paper by a pen driven by a hand that wanted certainty, and it had survived intact:

*—and her name shall be Serevai Okafor-Ash.*

Isandro's knees gave. She caught the table's edge. The apple rolled off and thumped against the floor and neither of them noticed.

*Serevai Okafor-Ash.*

Not the name on her contracts. Not the name on her wages. The name her grandfather had breathed into her ear once, at the end, with his grip bruising her wrist: *if anyone ever speaks it aloud—run.*

No living person knew it. She had believed that the way she believed in load-bearing walls. It was the architecture of her entire life. And now it lay in faded ink on a stranger's page, dated—to judge by Ellery's dreams, by the doubled rains of this very week—tonight.

"No," Isandro said aloud, to the book, to the room, to the universe in general. "No, absolutely not. You are a trick of damp. Ink runs. Paper lies."

Paper did lie. That was practically the guild's founding principle. But the ink hadn't run. Every damaged word on the page had bloomed and blurred except those seven, which sat crisp and black as the moment they were written, as if the water had gone carefully around them, respectful, the way one steps around a sleeper.

Serevai Okafor-Ash.

*Ash.* Her grandfather had never explained the surname. He had only, once, very drunk on festival wine, looked at the family hearth fire for a long time and said, "We used to tend something bigger," and then refused to elaborate for the remaining eleven years of his life.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain thickened, hammering the shutters, and beneath the drumming—faint, far below, four floors down through stone and iron and a hundred years of guild secrets—came the sound again.

Three taps. Pause. Three taps.

Faster now. Or perhaps that was her pulse.

Isandro did the only reasonable thing, the thing her grandfather's ghost would have applauded: she closed the book, wrapped it in the oilcloth, and hid it. Not in the obvious places—under the mattress, behind the loose brick were the first places any searcher looked, and she had helped search enough rooms to know. Instead she knelt, worked up the third floorboard from the end, the one whose nail she'd loosened years ago for exactly no reason she'd ever admitted to herself, and set the book in the shallow cavity between joists, wrapped around her grandfather's old whetstone for disguise. Floorboard down. Rug over it. Lamp trimmed low.

Then she stood in the middle of her small held-breath room and realized her hands were shaking, and made them stop, and failed, and let them.

*Run*, her grandfather had said. She looked at the window, the courtyard wall, the easy dark beyond. She had money saved. She had skills that traveled. She could be over that wall in thirty seconds and three districts gone by dawn, and by tomorrow noon she could be a woman named anything at all, starting over the way she'd been built to start over—

The floorboard stayed down. Her boots stayed on.

Because—she examined the feeling honestly, the way she examined a ledger line—because running was what the name demanded, and she had spent twenty-nine years obeying that name without ever once choosing it. And because the book had said *swordspeaker*, and the word had landed somewhere beneath her breastbone, next to the buried name, and hummed.

And because—this was the part she liked least—old Ellery had carried that book forty years and slept fine until this week. Something had woken it. Something had woken the vault. Tonight of all nights. Coincidence was a currency too, and the guild had taught her that it was the rarest one of all, and never to accept it at face value.

A floorboard creaked in the corridor.

Isandro went still. The Quiet Wing's residents retired early; after nine the corridor belonged to mice and memory. But the creak came again—slow, deliberate, the measured tread of someone walking a length they knew well and taking their time about it. It passed her door.

Stopped.

Moved on. Stopped again, further down, by the stairwell. Silence stretched, taut as wire.

She knew that tread. Twenty-nine years in this building; you learned the walks the way you learned voices. Heavy heel, light toe, the slight hitch of an old injury on the left side that Veyra never spoke of and everyone pretended not to notice. The guildmaster walked her corridors at night sometimes—everyone knew that too, and called it diligence, and were comforted.

But Veyra's rounds ended at the second floor. They had ended at the second floor for fifteen years.

Isandro crossed to her door without a sound and put her eye to the gap beside the hinges, where the lamplight of the corridor showed as a thin bright thread. A shadow lay across the thread. Shoulders. The angle of a head tilted slightly, listening—or considering—or both.

It lingered.

One minute. Two. Long enough for Isandro to count her own heartbeats and lose track at sixty. Long enough for dread, which had been a seed in her chest since the counter that morning, to send down roots, unfurl, bloom black and quiet as nightshade.

Then the shadow shifted. The hitch-step moved away, down the corridor, unhurried, and the stairwell door opened and closed with a click soft as a kiss.

Isandro stood in the dark of her room with her hand pressed flat against her sternum, over the buried name, and made herself breathe.

In the morning, she knew—she knew the way she knew a sum was wrong before she found the error—the guildmaster would summon her. There would be tea in that dry rasping voice, and warmth, and some small question shaped like kindness.

And for the first time in her career, Isandro Okafor would have to decide whether to answer it with the truth.

Below her, four floors down, in the sealed dark of the deep vault, something tapped three times—and paused, as if waiting for her reply.
