## 3. The Guildmaster's Kindness

Isandro had been summoned to the top of the guildhouse exactly twice before in her eleven years of service.

The first time, she was nineteen and had mispriced a bottled thunderclap by three silver marks, which meant the guild had sold a storm for less than it cost to catch one. She'd climbed the spiral stair with her stomach in her throat, rehearsing apologies in four dialects, only to find Guildmaster Veyra laughing—actually laughing—over the ledger entry.

"Three marks," Veyra had said, tapping the page. "You've given some farmer's son a thunderstorm for the price of a good dinner. Do you know what he'll do with it?"

"Resell it at a profit?"

"He'll open it," Veyra said, "on a hilltop, on his wedding day, so everyone will remember that the sky itself came to celebrate. That, Okafor, is worth more than three silver marks. It might be the cheapest miracle we've ever sold." Then she'd bought Isandro lunch at the Lamplighter, and Isandro had walked home floating, certain she'd found the one place in the world where her particular strangeness would be understood.

The second time had been a summons to witness an audit. Formal, tedious, forgettable.

This—the note slipped under her door at dawn, written in Veyra's own hand, requesting her presence *at her convenience, dear girl*—was neither of those things.

*Dear girl.* Veyra called half the junior clerks *dear girl*. But she did not write notes by hand. Veyra's notes were dictated to a scrivener, stamped with the triple-seal, and delivered with the impersonal efficiency of a tax notice. This note smelled faintly of cedar oil and had been folded once, carelessly, like something written late at night at a desk still cluttered with other business.

Isandro read it four times while her tea went cold.

*Okafor — Come up when the morning bells finish. I have something to discuss that shouldn't wait for quarter-day. Bring nothing. — V.*

*Bring nothing.*

She looked at the shelf above her desk, where the water-damaged prophecy book sat wrapped in oilcloth behind a row of inventory manuals, disguised as boring as she could make it look. She had not opened it since the night the client traded it away. She had told herself she hadn't opened it because it wasn't catalogued yet, because procedure mattered, because a book entered the ledger properly or not at all.

That was true. It was also not the whole truth, and Isandro had spent her whole life being very careful about the difference between things that were true and things that were merely convenient.

The morning bells began.

She left the book where it was. She climbed the stairs.

* * *

The guildmaster's office occupied the entire crown of the tower, and it was the only room in the building Isandro had never been able to read. Everywhere else in the guildhouse, the ledgers told you everything: what had come in, what had gone out, who owed whom, what the interest ran. The office had no ledger. It had windows—four of them, one facing each direction—and a desk old enough that its grain had gone soft as cloth, and Veyra herself, standing at the east window with her back turned, watching the city wake below her.

Veyra Ashkettle was sixty-one years old and built like a doorstop: short, wide, immovable. Her hair was iron-grey and cropped brutally short, and her hands, when she finally turned around, were the hands of someone who had spent thirty years handling objects that did not want to be handled. There was a burn scar across her left palm shaped like a moth. Nobody asked about it. Everybody wondered.

"Okafor." She said the name warmly, and gestured to the chair across from her desk. "Sit. Have you eaten? Don't lie, I can always tell."

"I had tea," Isandro said, sitting.

"That's not eating." Veyra pulled a small tin from her desk drawer and set it down between them. Inside were honey-cakes from the bakery on Coriander Street, the expensive ones, dusted with real sugar instead of beet-syrup. "Eat. That's not a request, it's a professional observation. You've lost weight again. You do this thing where you forget meals exist whenever a new shipment comes in. I've watched you do it for eleven years."

"You watch me?"

"I watch everyone," Veyra said mildly, taking the chair opposite. "It's the job. Eat the cake, Isandro."

So Isandro ate the cake, which was warm somehow, as though it had come out of the oven five minutes ago despite the bakery being twenty minutes' walk downhill, and which tasted like her grandmother's kitchen, which was impossible, because her grandmother had died when Isandro was six and Veyra could not possibly know—

No. Of course she couldn't. It was a trick of memory and hunger and sugar. Everything had an explanation. That was the whole point of the Ledger Guild: nothing was magic if you wrote down where it came from.

"I'll get to the point," Veyra said, "because you're the sort who prefers the point, and because flattery makes you suspicious, which is one of the ten thousand things I like about you." She laced her fingers over the moth scar. "Quarter-day is in three weeks. At quarter-day I intend to name a new Undermaster of Acquisitions."

Isandro stopped chewing.

Undermaster of Acquisitions. Second-highest post in the guild. The last Undermaster had retired to a vineyard in the south two years ago, and Veyra had run the position herself since, which everyone agreed was unsustainable and nobody expected to change, because everyone knew Veyra trusted no one enough to hand them the keys to the incoming trade. Every name whispered for the post had been quietly passed over. Sarelle, too ambitious. Odo, too careless. Master Fenwick, too fond of drink and of telling people so.

"There are twelve senior clerks ahead of me," Isandro said carefully.

"There are twelve senior clerks ahead of you, and not one of them can do what you do." Veyra counted off on her scarred fingers. "You've never lost a penny in reconciliation—not one, in eleven years, which is a record going back to the founding. You speak four dead languages well enough to price texts in them. Last autumn you caught the counterfeit grief-vial before it hit the floor, and you didn't make a spectacle of it, you just quietly moved it to the review shelf and let the appraiser find his own mistake, which was kinder than he deserved and smarter than most people manage. And—" she paused, and her eyes, pale grey as winter harbor-water, settled on Isandro with a weight that felt almost physical, "—you want almost nothing. Ambition burns people out, Okafor. Greed bends them. But you—you just love the work. Clean hands, clean heart. That's what the position needs. Someone who loves the ledger more than what's in it."

The honey-cake had turned to clay in Isandro's mouth.

*Clean hands, clean heart.* She thought of the oilcloth bundle on her shelf. She thought of the entry she had not written, the first unwritten line in eleven spotless years. She thought of the date in the prophecy book, tonight's date, and the name beside it—a name no living person knew, a name that had been buried with a village and a fire and everything else, except apparently inside her own skull.

"I'm honored," she said, which was true, and which was also a stone thrown into deep water to see how far the ripples went.

"You're suspicious," Veyra corrected, with a smile that took any sting out of it. "Good. Trust the gift but count the fingers. Now." She leaned back. "There's one formality. A small one. The council requires a fidelity interview before elevation—it's tradition, nothing more, a box to tick. I'm allowed to conduct it myself for internal candidates." She reached into the drawer again and produced a slim folder, which she laid on the desk between them without opening. "Standard questions. You've seen the list; it hasn't changed in forty years. Have you ever falsified an entry? No. Have you ever accepted payment outside the guild? No. Et cetera, et cetera." Her hand came to rest flat on the closed folder. "And then one question that isn't standard. One I added myself."

The tower was very quiet. Below them, through the floor, Isandro could feel the morning trade beginning—the murmur of the counting hall, the clink of weights, the soft rustle of a thousand pages turning. The heartbeat of the only family she had ever had.

"A client came to you two nights ago," Veyra said. "Late trade window. Older gentleman, river accent, wet sleeves. He pawned a book."

The room tilted, gently, the way a ship tilts when the swell comes under the keel.

"It wasn't logged," Veyra continued. "Which is unlike you. Which is why I noticed. The night clerk marked your window closed at the second bell, but the door-ward swears you saw someone out after. So." She unfolded her hands, all patience now, all warmth, the kindness of a woman who had fed her honey-cakes and remembered her losses and promoted her over twelve better-credentialed rivals. "Tell me about the book, Isandro."

* * *

Later—much later, years later, in another life entirely—Isandro would try to reconstruct those few seconds and find they wouldn't hold still. What she remembered was strange and small: the dust motes turning in the light from the east window; the moth-shaped scar; the absurd detail that Veyra's inkwell had a chip in its lip, and she had used this inkwell every day for eleven years and never noticed the chip until now.

What she knew, with the clarity of a woman falling, was this: she could tell the truth, and lose the book, and lose the promotion, and lose whatever fragile thread the prophecy had put into her hands—or she could lie.

To Veyra. Who had bought her lunch at nineteen. Who kept honey-cakes in her desk because she worried her clerks forgot to eat. Who was, in every way that mattered and several ways that didn't, the closest thing to a mother the world had ever offered her.

The oath lived in Isandro's blood. Every guildsworn carried it—not metaphorically; actually. Sworn words had weight in the old ways, and the Ledger Guild's covenant was older than the city. When you swore to keep accurate books, the vow settled somewhere beneath the ribs, a small warm coal of rightness, and every honest entry fed it, and you never thought about it, the way you never think about your heart beating.

Isandro opened her mouth to tell the truth.

"The client was a book-mender," she said. "Retired. He wanted an appraisal on a damaged text—family piece, water-damaged, mostly ruined. I looked at it. It was worthless. Mold in the binding, ink bled through past saving. I sent him to the paper-restorers on Tanner's Row, because selling him anything would have been theft, and I closed my window after because there was nothing to log. No transaction. No entry required."

The words came out smooth and level and complete, each one fitted to the next like stones in a wall, and they were almost entirely false.

The coal beneath her ribs shifted.

It was not pain, exactly. It was movement—the sensation of a sleeping animal turning over, or of a key rotating halfway in a lock and stopping. Something that had been warm went briefly cool. Something that had pointed one direction swung a degree off true, like a compass near iron, and settled there, and stayed.

Nobody in the history of the guild, Isandro thought wildly, has ever described what this feels like. Because nobody in the history of the guild has ever lied while sworn.

Veyra watched her for a long moment. Long enough for Isandro to count her own heartbeats—seven, eight, nine—and to understand, in a distant and academic way, that she was either about to be arrested or promoted, and that both possibilities now felt equally unreal.

Then Veyra smiled, and it reached her eyes, and the whole terrible tension broke like ice going out of a river in spring.

"Mold in the binding," she repeated, chuckling. "Sent him to Tanner's Row. Of course you did." She stood, crossed to the window, and looked out at the city with her hands clasped behind her back. "Eleven years, and you still won't take a bad trade even when no one's checking. You know what the founders would have said? They'd have said you lack killer instinct. I say it's the only instinct worth keeping."

"So—" Isandro's voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "The fidelity interview. Passed?"

"Passed," Veyra said. "Pending council ratification at quarter-day. Until then, tell no one—not out of secrecy, but because Sarelle will make your life miserable for three weeks and you deserve better than three weeks of Sarelle." She turned back from the window, and for a moment her face did something complicated—something that looked, oddly, like grief wearing happiness as a disguise. "There's one more thing. Since you'll be Undermaster soon, there are parts of the vaults you'll need access to. The deep archive. Sub-level four." She held out a ring of black iron keys, old ones, their teeth worn nearly smooth. "Come down tomorrow evening, after close. I'll walk you through it personally. Some of what we keep down there is... delicate. I'd rather you hear it from me than from rumor."

Isandro took the keys. They were heavier than they looked. All of them were heavier than they looked.

"Sub-level four," she repeated.

"Older than the rest of the building," Veyra said lightly. "Older than the street it stands on, if you believe the surveyors, and the surveyors don't like believing it." She smiled again—that same complicated smile—and walked Isandro to the door herself, and pressed one more honey-cake into her hand for the stairs, the way you feed a child, the way you feed family.

"Go on," Veyra said. "And Isandro—whatever you find down there tomorrow, remember that everything I have ever done in this guild, I did to protect the people in it. That includes you. Especially you."

* * *

Isandro made it down four flights of the spiral stair on legs that worked perfectly and felt borrowed. She made it into her office. She closed the door. She stood with her back against it, holding a honey-cake she could not eat, and let the shaking start.

She had lied. To Veyra. First time in her career, first time in her adult life, maybe first time ever to anyone who mattered, and the oath had *moved*, and it had not punished her, and somehow that was worse—if it had burned her hand off she could at least have known the rules.

And underneath the vertigo, underneath the sick swimming guilt, another thought surfaced, quiet and cold as the harbor at dawn:

Veyra had noticed a single unlogged visitor. Veyra, who ran the entire guild and watched twelve hundred clerks, had traced one wet-sleeved book-mender to one closed trade window within forty-eight hours.

She hadn't asked about the book because she cared about procedure.

She'd asked because she already knew what the book was.

Isandro looked at the shelf. The oilcloth bundle sat behind the inventory manuals, patient as a held breath. Tonight, the prophecy had said. Tonight, by name.

Tomorrow evening, sub-level four. Black iron keys older than the street.

She had less than a day to decide whether she was walking into her inheritance—or her cage.
