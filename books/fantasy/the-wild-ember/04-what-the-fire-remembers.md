## 4. What the Fire Remembers

The city of Vharen smelled wrong to Sefu Oduya.

He had been in the city three days, and every hour the smell grew worse—not filth, not smoke, though there was plenty of both. It was something underneath those smells, something old and aching, like a wound that had learned to pretend it was scenery. He caught it in the fish market, in the temple incense, in the breath of children running past him on the harbor stairs. The people here didn't notice it. You couldn't notice a smell you'd been born inside.

Sefu noticed it because he had spent eleven years learning what dragons smelled like when they were happy, and eleven years since learning what they smelled like otherwise.

*Grief,* he thought, hauling his false-bottomed cart of "salted eels" up another flight of rusted stairs into the underlevels. *The whole city is marinating in grief, and they call it prosperity.*

"Oi," said a voice below him. "Eel man."

A boy of perhaps ten stood at the bottom of the stairs, holding out a coin that was, by its color, mostly wishful thinking.

"Eels are salted," Sefu called down. "You want unsalted."

"I want one eel."

"One salted eel is a punishment. Ask your mother."

"She sent me." The boy jingled the coin. "She says you're new, and she says your prices are honest, and she says if you cheat her she'll know where you sleep."

Sefu considered this. It was, he reflected, the most thorough threat assessment anyone in Vharen had conducted on him so far, and the boy delivered it without malice, almost fondly, the way you might recite a favorite prayer.

"Tell your mother," Sefu said, tossing down an eel wrapped in paper, "that I respect her very much."

"You have to take the coin."

"The coin is decorative. Keep it. Buy something sweet and don't tell her."

The boy grinned and vanished into the warren of stairways and hanging laundry that made up the underlevels of Vharen—a city stacked on top of itself like bad handwriting, where the rich lived in the light and everyone else lived in the beautiful, complicated dark. Sefu shouldered his cart and went deeper.

Eleven years ago, he had been a warden of the Emberhold, one of forty sworn keepers of the dragon-flame. Now he was an eel merchant. Life was generous that way, if you squinted at it sideways and refused to look directly.

* * *

He found the first thread of it that night, in a tavern called the Drowned Rat, which was neither drowned nor notably infested—Vhareni naming conventions were a mystery Sefu had decided not to pursue.

He hadn't meant to go drinking. Drinking was expensive and dangerous for a fugitive; it loosened tongues, including his own. But the tavern sat above one of the guild's lesser counting-houses, and as he passed the door, the smell hit him so hard he actually stopped walking, mid-stride, like a man who has walked into a wall made of memory.

Dragon-fire. Bottled. *Nearby.*

He went in. He ordered the cheapest thing on the menu, which turned out to be a bowl of something gray that claimed to be soup, and he listened.

Merchants, mostly. Tired men and women complaining about tariffs and tides. But at the corner table sat two guild factors in their slate-gray coats, drinking with the loose-shouldered ease of men whose employer pays well and asks little.

"—third shipment this month," one was saying. "Storms, memories, luck, whatever the client wants. The vault-master says demand's never been higher."

"It's the season," the other said. "Weddings. Everybody wants a bottled wonder at their wedding. My cousin bought a captive sunset for his daughter's betrothal. Paid four hundred crowns."

"Four hundred! For what?"

"For a sunset, fool. In a bottle. You uncork it at the feast and the whole hall glows like evening in the summer isles." The factor laughed. "Worth every crown."

Sefu looked down at his gray soup and breathed very slowly through his nose.

Because he knew where the sunsets came from. He knew where all of it came from—the storms, the memories, the luck, the light. There was only one fire in the world old enough and generous enough to give off wonders as a side effect of simply existing, and she was dying under a mountain while drunk men priced her dreams at four hundred crowns a bottle.

He finished the soup. It tasted exactly as sad as it looked.

* * *

The warehouse took him two more days to find, and it was worse than he'd feared, because he had been hoping to be wrong.

It sat in the deepest tier of the underlevels, behind a fish-rendering plant, unmarked except for the guild's small brass seal—a ledger book crossed with a sword. Sefu had walked past it twice, mapping the guards (two, bored), the locks (good but old), and the drainage tunnel (unguarded, because no one guards the way sewage travels).

On the third night, wearing borrowed clothes that smelled authentically of rendered fish, he went in through the drain.

The tunnel was exactly as pleasant as expected. He emerged in a sub-cellar, wrung out his sleeves, and climbed a maintenance ladder with the patience of a man who had once spent nine days motionless in a mountain crevice waiting for a dragon to finish sulking. Dragons sulked long. You learned patience or you learned nothing.

The ladder ended at a hatch. Beyond the hatch: the smell.

Not a thread now. A river. Sefu put his hand on the hatch and found it trembling slightly—no. Not the hatch. His hand. Eleven years of discipline, and his own fingers shook against cold iron.

*Steady. Look first. Feel later.*

He lifted the hatch an inch.

Below him stretched a warehouse floor the size of a temple nave, lit by shielded lamps, lined floor to ceiling with shelving—and on the shelves, in rows upon rows upon rows, thousands upon thousands of bottles.

Small ones, wine-bottle sized. Great ones, taller than a man, cradled in iron frames. Some glowed soft gold. Some churned with miniature weather. One shelf held nothing but light—pure, patient, honey-colored light, packed in flasks like preserves.

And beneath all of it, under everything, woven through every glass wall like a heartbeat heard through a hospital door, was the smell of Ashveth.

Sefu lowered himself through the hatch and stood among the shelves, and did not move for a long time.

* * *

Here is what the ledgers would say about these goods, if ledgers could be honest:

*Bottled storm, captured from the high passes. Bottled memory, drawn from a willing seller. Bottled luck, harvested fair.*

Here is the truth:

There was no such thing as a bottled storm. Storms could not be caught; they were too vast, too quick, too much themselves. But a dragon's exhalation carried weather in it—Ashveth's breath had seeded rains across three kingdoms in the old days, had fed harvests, had turned drought years green. What the guild sold as *captive storms* were breath-motes, flakes of the last dragon's weathering, skimmed off her sleep by machines the founders had built a century ago and never once asked permission to build.

There was no such thing as bottled memory, either. Memory lived in flame the way rings live in trees. Every year of Ashveth's six centuries was stored in her fire, every face she'd loved, every mountain she'd flown over—and the guild's extraction engines could draw a single year out, bottle it, and sell a grieving widow her husband's smile back for a price. They told the sellers the memories came from volunteers.

They came from a prisoner in a shell of salt and rust who was too weak to stop them.

Sefu walked the aisles slowly. He read labels. *Summer light, 401st year, premium grade. Courage, distilled, wedding strength. First snow, child-safe dilution.* Each label neat. Each label a sentence in a confession nobody had ever written down.

He reached the great flasks at the back—the reserve stock, the things too large or strange for retail. And there he stopped, because in the largest flask of all, big as a coffin and twice as dignified, floated something that made his knees simply decide the matter without consulting him.

A song.

He couldn't hear it through the glass. But he could see it—or see the shape of it, the way you can see the shape of wind in a wheat field—a slow golden spiral turning in the liquid light, and he knew it. Gods help him, he knew it. It was the Lullaby of the Emberhold, the song the wardens sang at dusk, the one old Warden Mekonnen had composed with Ashveth herself, trading verses back and forth across a decade until neither could say which lines were hers and which were his.

They had bottled the lullaby. They were selling the sound of a friendship.

Sefu sat down on the cellar floor between the shelves, put his head in his hands, and wept.

* * *

He wept the way he had learned to weep in the mountains after the fall of the Emberhold—quietly, thoroughly, without any expectation that it would fix anything. Grief, the wardens used to say, is just love with nowhere to go. Sefu had a great deal of love and absolutely nowhere to put it, and so it pooled in him year after year, rising like water behind a dam.

But dams exist to make power.

He didn't know how long he sat there. Long enough that the shielded lamps seemed to dim and brighten with his breathing. Long enough that when he finally raised his head, his eyes had gone dry and hard and clear, and the grief had done what grief does when you refuse to let it be the end of the story.

It had become a list.

He stood. He wiped his face on a sleeve that regretted it. And then, methodical as the clerk who must have catalogued all this plunder, he began to inventory.

Breath-storms: three hundred flasks, near aisle one. Memories: the entire east wall, organized by year—her years, Ashveth's years, numbered like vintages. Light. Luck. Songs. Dreams. Six centuries of a living soul, decanted and priced and stacked to the ceiling in a room that smelled of fish.

And at the far end, behind a cage of black iron, the extraction equipment itself—pumps and coils and condensers, humming faintly even now, even idle, like insects dreaming.

Sefu pressed his palm flat against the nearest shelf and felt the flask under it answer. Faint warmth. Faint recognition. The way a dog knows its own name even spoken by a stranger.

*I'm sorry,* he told them—all of them, the whole glowing inventory. *I'm sorry we let this happen. We were forty wardens and we were not enough, and I ran, and you paid for my running. I'm going to take you home. All of you. Even if it takes the rest of my life, which, given my current employment prospects, may be mercifully short.*

One flask, third shelf, flickered—just once, just slightly, like a candle acknowledging a draft. Or like something answering.

Sefu went still.

Wardens' lore said the fire remembered. That it wasn't fuel, wasn't commodity, wasn't magic in the way merchants mean magic—something inert to be spent. It was *attention*. Old attention. And old attention, given long enough, starts to notice things.

He leaned close to the flask. Inside, the golden spiral of some stolen dream turned its slow turn, and as he watched, it bent—imperceptibly, unmistakably—toward him. Toward the warmth of a living hand on the glass.

"Oh," Sefu whispered. "Oh, you're still in there. You're all still in there."

Somewhere above, a floorboard creaked. Then another. Footsteps, unhurried, descending the warehouse stair—the night factor making rounds, most likely, lantern in hand, keys at his belt.

Sefu had perhaps ninety seconds to be anywhere else.

Most men would have fled straight back down the drain. Sefu stayed exactly four seconds longer than safe, long enough to pull out the stub of charcoal he kept for marking trails, and write on the underside of the shelf—one word, small, in the wardens' cipher that no guildman living could read:

*Counted.*

Then he was gone, down the ladder, through the hatch, along the drain, out into the night air of the underlevels, which had never smelled so sweet.

* * *

He rented a room above a chandler's shop that night—the kind of room where the bed was theoretical and the window was aspirational—and sat on the floor with his back to the wall, doing sums by candlelight.

Not money sums. He'd stopped caring about money somewhere around the time he started eating eels professionally. Other sums.

Three thousand flasks, conservatively. One dying dragon beneath the mountains to the north, her flame guttering toward dark. One covenant broken a century ago, its oath-stones scattered and sold like everything else. One fugitive warden with no allies, no resources, a criminal record in four provinces, and a cart full of salted eels.

Against that: the fire remembered. The fire was still awake in there, still turning toward any warm hand. Which meant it could still be reached. Which meant the theft was not yet total, and total was the only outcome he had sworn—eleven years ago, in the ashes of the Emberhold, alone—that he would never accept.

He needed help. That was the honest sum. He could not carry three thousand souls out of a city in a fish cart.

He also needed information, and this was the sum that troubled him, because there was exactly one source of it: the Ledger Guild itself. Its records, its routes, its vault-masters. And the guild was a fortress of procedure, and procedure was a language, and Sefu spoke maybe six words of it.

What he needed—what the whole doomed enterprise needed—was someone inside. Someone who knew the ledgers. Someone close enough to the guild's heart to read its secrets and, ideally, far enough from its heart to be persuadable.

He blew out the candle, lay down on the theoretical bed, and stared at the ceiling.

And that was when the other thing surfaced—the thing he'd been carrying unopened since the Drowned Rat, the way you carry news you haven't decided how to hold.

The factors had mentioned a name. Casually, in the way of men discussing office gossip. Something about the balancing-house, about a swordspeaker—*the* swordspeaker, the last one on their rolls—who kept perfect books and spoke to no one and had, according to the younger factor, "eyes like she's counting your sins and finding them few but interesting."

Isandro Okafor. Last sworn swordspeaker of the Ledger Guild. Keeper of balances. The one member of that thieving house who, rumor insisted, had never taken so much as a miscounted penny—and who therefore was either the guild's most honest servant or its most useful liar, and Sefu had met enough people in eleven years of hiding to know that the second was always more likely, and the first always more dangerous to bet against.

Either way, she balanced the books.

Which meant she knew where everything was.

Sefu closed his eyes and let the plan assemble itself the way plans do in the dark—unwisely, completely, all at once.

Tomorrow, he would find the swordspeaker. Tomorrow he would do something rash and probably stupid, because grief had sharpened into purpose and purpose, left unsupervised, tends toward burglary.

He fell asleep smelling of fish and dragon-fire, and dreamed, for the first time in eleven years, of the Emberhold at dusk—and in the dream, the lullaby was being sung by someone else, someone with a ledger under one arm and eyes that counted sins and found them forgivable.

He woke before dawn with the certainty already set in him like a healed bone.

The city above was stirring. Somewhere up there, in the rain-slicked morning, a woman with perfect books was about to have her day ruined.

Sefu almost felt sorry for her.
