## 5. An Unlikely Bargain

The trouble with hiding a book in a guildhouse full of people who catalogued impossibilities for a living was that someone, eventually, would come looking.

Isandro had known this when she tucked the prophecy beneath the false bottom of her ledger chest. She'd known it when she locked the chest, and when she slept badly on top of the key, and again at dawn when she woke with her hand curled around it like a talon. Knowledge, however, was a poor substitute for a better plan, and by the time the evening shift settled over the guildhouse—lamplighters moving down the corridor, the smell of ink and lamp-oil and someone's ill-advised attempt to fry onions in the counting room—she still hadn't found one.

So she did what she always did. She worked.

The night's entries were ordinary enough to be soothing. A storm in a bottle, sold to a theater manager who wanted authentic weather for a production about sailors; Isandro had personally checked the seal twice, because an unsealed storm was the sort of mistake you only made once, and usually not twice. A memory pawned—a woman trading away the recollection of her worst winter, three years' interest accruing, redemption possible but unlikely. Isandro wrote the receipt in her careful hand and watched the woman leave lighter and smiling, which was the way of it, mostly. People thought they were selling burdens. Sometimes they were right.

She was balancing the column of weights and measures—the guild insisted every impossible thing be weighed against something mundane, so that the books would balance in a language auditors could read—when she heard the door.

Not the street door. Her door. The little one off the records room, the one that was supposed to be locked.

Isandro did not look up. This was a discipline she'd spent eleven years perfecting: the appearance of a woman so absorbed in her work that she registered nothing, noticed nothing, was nothing. Invisibility through tedium. It had kept her safe through two guildmaster transitions, three audits, and one memorable incident involving a smuggled chimera egg.

But her hand, without her permission, slid along the underside of the desk and closed around the hilt of the short sword she kept there. Not a guild-issue blade. Older. Heavier than it looked. The one thing she owned that she could not account for in any ledger.

"You're sitting on my entry," said a voice.

It was not Veyra's voice. That was the first strange thing. The second strange thing was the smell—woodsmoke and high cold air, the smell of somewhere very far above or very far below, brought into her tidy records room like weather.

Isandro looked up.

He stood just inside the door as though he'd grown there: a tall man in a patched traveler's coat gone gray with road dust, dark-skinned, silver threading his close-cropped hair, and eyes that held the particular exhausted ferocity of someone who had walked a very long way toward something he dreaded reaching. His boots were ruined. His hands were scarred in the old way—burn-scarred, rope-scarred, the hands of someone who worked with living fire.

And he was holding her ledger. Tuesday's ledger. The one she'd left—she ran the day backward frantically—on the counter of the receiving room while she checked the storm's seal. Fourteen minutes, maybe fifteen. Unattended.

"Entry forty-one," he said, tapping the page with one scarred finger. "'One vial, dragon-fire, common grade, purchased from source undisclosed, weight balanced against four hundredweight of river gravel.'" He looked up at her. "You wrote *undisclosed*."

"It's a standard formulation," Isandro said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "When the provenance is irregular, we write *undisclosed*. It keeps the auditors from asking questions we'd have to answer."

"Undisclosed," he repeated, softly, tasting the word like something spoiled. "I've been walking for six years. Through three ranges and two wars and a salt desert where even the ghosts were thirsty. And every trail of stolen flame ends in a ledger where some careful soul has written *undisclosed* in a beautiful hand." He set the ledger down on her desk, gently, the way you'd set down something dead. "Do you know what's in your bottles?"

"Storms," Isandro said. "Memories. Luck, occasionally. Second chances, if the client can afford them." She recited it like the guild catechism, because it was the guild catechism, and because saying it aloud steadied her the way rules always had. "Impossible things. That's the trade."

"That's the *inventory*," the man said. "Here's the trade." He leaned forward over her desk, and up close she could see the burn scars went up under his jaw, old ones, deliberate-looking. "Somewhere in this building is a warehouse. Shelves and shelves of little glass vials, each one glowing like a coal. You call it dragon-fire, common grade, and you sell it to theaters and alchemists and rich men who want their lamps to never go out. And every drop of it was taken from a living creature who did not agree to give it. Do you understand me? It wasn't shed. It wasn't left behind. It was *taken*, while they lived, and it doesn't stop hurting just because it's in glass."

Isandro's heart was doing something complicated and unwelcome. "Who are you?" she asked. "How did you get in here? There are wards—"

"I've been sleeping in your walls since Tuesday," he said. "Your wards keep out thieves. I'm not a thief. I'm a warden." He straightened, and something in the way he said it made it land like a title, heavy as any guild rank. "Sefu. Last of the wardens of the Ashreach. And you—" his eyes narrowed, and Isandro felt the look travel over her like a customs inspection, "—you didn't argue with me."

"What?"

"The fire. Taken from a living creature. Most people in this building would have started arguing by now. About legality, about contracts, about how the dragons were compensated under the founding covenant." His voice dropped. "You just sat there looking like someone had shown you a receipt for something you'd suspected all along."

Isandro became very aware of the ledger chest behind her chair. Of the false bottom. Of the book inside it with tonight's date written beside a name no living person should know.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

And there it was. The second lie of her career, arriving only hours after the first, coming easier this time, and the oath in her blood shifted again—that faint wrongness, like a floorboard giving slightly underfoot. Sefu's head tilted. Whatever a warden's senses were, she had a horrible suspicion they included lying.

"Hm," he said.

"Hm yourself," said Isandro, and then, because terror has always made her rude: "If you're going to rob us, rob us. If you're going to report me, report me. But don't stand in my records room making speeches. Some of us have columns to balance."

For a long moment he simply stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a short, cracked, genuinely amused sound, as if something in him had rusted shut years ago and had just creaked open despite itself.

"Columns," he said. "Six years, and the person I finally corner turns out to be defending her bookkeeping." He dragged a stool over and sat down heavily, uninvited, the posture of a man prepared to be thrown out and too tired to care. "All right. Let's do this properly. I'll tell you what I want, and you tell me why I shouldn't take it, and we'll both lie a little, and we'll see where we end up."

"That's not how bargains work at the Ledger Guild."

"No," Sefu agreed. "That's how they work everywhere else."

* * *

They negotiated the way two suspicious cats negotiate shared territory: slowly, with frequent retreats and at least one incident involving bared teeth (metaphorical, in her case; Sefu's hand had drifted toward something in his coat before Isandro reminded him, sweetly, that guild enforcers slept directly upstairs).

What he wanted, it emerged, was the prophecy book.

Which meant he knew about it. Which meant—

"How?" Isandro said flatly.

"The wardens have our own records," he said. "Older than yours. Ours say a swordspeaker would come—one who carried the oath in her blood and her true name in a book of ash-paper. We've been watching the guildhouses for thirty years, waiting for someone to match the description." He shrugged. "Then three nights ago, every vial in your warehouse flickered at once. All of them. Like candles in a draft. Old fire knows things, Isandro Okafor. It remembers being part of something whole."

Her blood went cold. "I never told you my name."

"You didn't have to." He said it almost apologetically. "The fire told me. It's been calling your name since I got within ten miles of this city. Quietly. Like it was afraid of being overheard." He leaned in. "Now. Here's my offer, and hear all of it before you reach for that sword under your desk, which yes, I can see, and no, I'm not flattered.

"You read me the prophecy—all of it, every line—and in exchange, tomorrow night, I show you what's really in your warehouse. Not what the ledgers say. What the fire *is*. You'll see it the way a warden sees it. And after that, if you still want to be a Ledger Guild clerk, I'll walk out of your city and trouble you never again."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take the book tonight, while you sleep, and I read it myself, and you spend the rest of your life wondering what it said." He spread his scarred hands. "I'd rather not. Stealing from you feels wrong in a way I can't explain, and I've learned to trust feelings I can't explain. But I will. Ashveth doesn't have time for polite."

"Ashveth," Isandro repeated. The name arrived somewhere beneath her ribs and stayed there, warm and enormous, like a swallowed coal.

"The last dragon," Sefu said simply. "Whose fire is in those vials. Whose flame is guttering. Whose death will end more than you can imagine—not just the wonders, the *covenant*, the old binding between what your kind calls the human realms and the elder world. When she goes out, everything the guild trades stops working. Every bottled storm goes flat. Every pawned memory rots. Every second chance curdles into its opposite." He met her eyes. "And that's the *good* outcome."

The records room was very quiet. Somewhere above, an enforcer laughed at something; the lamplighter's footsteps passed and faded; the onion-frying experiment continued to assault the building.

Isandro thought about the woman who'd pawned her worst winter. About the theater manager and his bottled storm. About eleven years of believing she was keeping accounts for something good, or at least something neutral, and about entry forty-one, *purchased from source undisclosed*, in her beautiful careful hand.

Two liars, she thought. Sitting across a desk from each other. Both of them certain, both of them frightened, both of them hiding something behind their eyes—he behind six years of road dust, she behind a locked chest with a false bottom.

"All right," she said. "A bargain. But mine has terms."

"Of course it does."

"One: you don't touch the book. I read it aloud; you listen; that's all. Two: whatever you show me tomorrow, you show me honestly—no warden tricks, no half-truths shaped like whole ones. Three—" and here her voice betrayed her, just slightly, going quieter, "—three, you tell me why you cried about it. In the warehouse. Don't bother denying it; I read the dust on the floor, and grief leaves a mark like anything else."

Sefu went still. For a moment the exhausted ferocity dropped away entirely, and underneath it he just looked tired, and old, and alone in a way that Isandro recognized from the inside.

"They were dying when I left them," he said finally. "My dragons. My charges. One by one, year by year, and I couldn't stop it, because the thing killing them was *bookkeeping*—neat, legal, well-organized theft, signed off by reasonable people balancing their columns." He looked at her, and there was no accusation in it, which was somehow worse. "So yes. I cried. On your warehouse floor. Amongst your inventory. I hope that doesn't inconvenience your filing."

"It won't," Isandro said. "I'll note it as water damage."

The laugh escaped him again, that rusty, reluctant sound, and something in the room changed shape—some tension easing by a single degree, the way ice shifts before it becomes water. Two liars, recognizing each other across the desk. Not friends. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. But allies of a specific and durable kind: the kind made of shared secrets and mutual exhaustion and the dawning suspicion that the other person might be the only honest one available.

She unlocked the ledger chest. Lifted the false bottom. Took out the book of ash-gray pages, water-stained, older than the guildhouse, older perhaps than the city.

Sefu drew breath when he saw it, a sharp involuntary sound, and his scarred hands trembled once before he stilled them.

"Read it," he said hoarsely. "Please. Start anywhere. Start at the beginning."

Isandro opened the book to the marked page—to tonight's date, to her own impossible name written in a hand like falling embers—and began:

"*When the fourth stone sleeps in a thief's strongbox and the fifth walks free in a captain's coat,*" she read, "*then shall the swordspeaker come, whose name is Isandro, called by fire before her birth, and the ledger of stolen things shall be laid open—*"

Outside, the lamplighters finished their rounds. Above, the guildhouse settled into its nightly quiet, enforcers dreaming, clerks snoring, the warehouse full of little glass suns flickering in the dark.

And far below the harbor, in a shell of salt and rust, something vast and ancient turned toward the surface, listening—and, for the first time in a hundred years, hoped.
