## 6. The Broken Covenant

The oath-hall had been a temple once, then a courthouse, then a granary, and now it was nothing at all—a great ribcage of stone standing open to the sky where the roof had fallen in a century ago. Rain came through the wound in soft gray sheets and pooled on the mosaic floor, which still showed, if you knew how to look, five figures standing in a circle with their palms turned outward.

Sefu knew how to look. He had been looking at mosaics like this one since before he could write.

"Mind the third tile," he said over his shoulder, as Isandro picked her way across the flooded floor behind him. "It's loose. The whole eastern half of this building is held together by stubbornness and moss."

"You brought me to a ruin," Isandro said. Her voice was flat, but he was learning to read the flatness—she used it the way other people used screaming. "In the rain. To show me a floor."

"I brought you to a ruin," Sefu said, "to show you what your guild is standing on."

He heard her stop walking.

He let her stand there a moment in the dripping silence while he crossed to the center of the circle, where the mosaic figures met. There had been something there once—a keystone of colored glass, maybe, or an altar. Now there was only a shallow depression in the stone, worn smooth by ten decades of rainwater pooling and freezing and thawing, and at its bottom, a single word cut deep enough that the moss hadn't claimed it:

**WITNESS.**

"Sit down," Sefu said. "This takes a while, and I'd rather tell it sitting. My mother always said history goes down easier with your feet up."

"She sounds like she was very wise," Isandro said, and lowered herself onto a fallen pillar with the careful dignity of someone who intended to be unimpressed.

"She was," Sefu said. "She also died angry, so take that as you will."

* * *

He sat on the wet stone because wet stone was honest company, and began where all warden children began: with the fire.

"Before the guild, there were dragons. That part your ledgers won't even bother to lie about—they'll just leave it out. Not five dragons. Not fifty. Thousands. The northern ranges glittered with them at dusk like the sky had spilled. They kept the weather honest, they kept the old magics fed, and they did not trouble themselves much with us, because why would they? What are we, from up there? A brief weather event with opinions."

Isandro's mouth twitched. He noted it and pressed on.

"But dragons live long, and long-lived things make bargains. And about three hundred years ago, the human realms were at each other's throats so badly that the rivers ran red twice in a generation. The dragons looked down at all this—and here's the part nobody wants to hear, Isandro—they took pity on us. Pity. The way you'd pity two cats fighting under your house. Annoying, but they're *your* cats."

"So they came down," he said, "and they offered a covenant. Their fire—the true fire, the kind that doesn't just burn but *binds*, the kind that can hold an oath so deep into the world that breaking it would be like breaking a mountain—in exchange for peace between our realms, and a promise: that we would tend their dying when their age came, as they tended ours. Five humans were chosen to hold it. One from each realm. Swordspeakers, because a sword can be an instrument or a weapon, and the choice is made every time you pick it up."

"The first swordspeakers." Isandro said it quietly, testing the weight of it.

"Five of them," Sefu said. "Look at the floor."

She looked. He watched her see it properly for the first time—not five decorative figures, but five people, rendered with the loving specificity of an artist who had stood in front of living models. The tall woman with her sword point-down, hands resting on the pommel. The broad man with his blade laid flat across both open palms, an offering. The old one seated, sword across her knees like a sleeping animal. The young one mid-turn, blade raised not in attack but in salute. And the fifth, at the circle's heart, holding no sword at all—just an open hand extended to the others.

"That's Ashveth's place," Sefu said softly. "The dragon's seat in the covenant. She didn't hold a sword. She held the others' oaths. That's what a warden does—we're descended from her attendants, the ones who kept the fire while the swordspeakers kept the word."

"And the stones?"

"Each swordspeaker carried an oath-stone. Dragon-fire cooled into crystal, with their vow sealed inside it. As long as all five stones remained together, the covenant held. It wasn't magic the way your guild sells magic—bottled, priced, shipped. It was magic the way a marriage is a marriage. It only worked if everyone stayed."

The rain thickened against the broken roof-beams. Somewhere in the walls, water found a new channel and began to trickle.

"For two hundred years," Sefu said, "it worked. Two hundred years of no war between the realms. Do you understand what I'm telling you? We had two hundred years of the impossible thing, and we got bored of it."

He made himself say the rest plainly, because his mother had taught him that plainly-said grief was a gift and prettily-said grief was a performance.

"The founders of the Ledger Guild were merchants. Realms-treaty clerks, originally—honest work, once. They managed the trade routes the covenant protected. And somewhere in the second century, one of them asked the question that ruins everything: *if the covenant makes the roads safe, who profits more, the swordspeakers or the toll-keepers?*"

"They couldn't break the covenant," Isandro said slowly. He could see her working it through, ledger-brain running ahead of him. "You said it holds like a mountain."

"It does. If all five stones stay together. So the founders never broke it." Sefu's hands had closed into fists without his permission; he opened them, finger by finger, the way you teach yourself to do when anger is a tool and not a master. "They broke the *speakers*. Four murders in four years, dressed up as accidents, fevers, duels gone wrong. Each time, the dead speaker's stone was 'recovered' by guild agents—for safekeeping, naturally. Safekeeping that lasted a hundred years and counting. The fifth speaker, the last one alive, watched her family die one by one and finally fled into hiding rather than be the last lock on a door the thieves already owned. Without her, without any of them, the covenant went quiet. And the stones went into vaults."

"And the fire?"

"The fire they stole openly. When a dragon dies, the flame doesn't go out—it *loosens*. Comes free of the body like a soul out of a shirt. Wardens are supposed to guide that fire back into the world, into new eggs, into the deep veins of the earth where weather and wonder come from. Your guild's founders learned to catch it instead. Bottle it. Cut it. Sell it." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, hated the crack, and let it happen anyway, because his mother had also said that a warden who performs his grief is lying and a warden who hides it is stealing—from the fire itself. "Every storm in a jar in your guildhouse. Every memory pawned, every second chance sold at markup. That's dragon-death, Isandro. Your guild has spent a hundred years selling pieces of the dead and calling it wonder."

* * *

For a long moment she said nothing. The rain filled the silence, patient as water.

Then: "Show me."

"Show you what?"

"Show me it's true." She was on her feet, and there was something terrible and clean in her face—not doubt exactly, or not only doubt. The look of an accountant who has found a discrepancy of one copper piece and will tear apart the whole archive until it explains itself. "You're a fugitive telling a story that damns my family. Every guild child learns about the Founders' Charter, about the *gifted* fire, the fire freely given by the last dragons out of gratitude. You're asking me to believe that's all—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "My whole life is built on those ledgers balancing. Show me."

Sefu breathed out. Of all the responses he'd braced for—fear, fury, a knife—this was the one that undid him a little. She wanted evidence. She wanted the truth even if the truth gutted her. That was rarer than courage.

"Come here," he said. "Stand in the middle. On the word."

She hesitated, then stepped into the shallow depression, rain running off her hood, her boots dark with water. She stood where a dragon had once stood, though neither of them said so.

"Now give me your hand. Palm up."

"If you try anything—"

"I'm going to sing," Sefu said. "Badly. That's the entire ambush."

Her mouth twitched again. She gave him her hand.

Her palm was cool and calloused in the way of someone who actually used her sword, which pleased him obscurely. He closed his eyes, found the low note in his chest where wardens keep their grief and their joy in the same room, and sang the first line of the Laying-Down Song—the one sung over dying dragons, the one that says *you may rest, we are here, the world will remember.*

Nothing happened for three heartbeats.

Then the mosaic woke.

It started under their feet—a warmth rising through the wet stone, and light, faint as dawn through fog, tracing the channels of grout between the tiles. The five figures lit from within. Color flooded back into them: the tall woman's cloak went river-blue, the broad man's blade went gold along its edge, and the fifth figure's open hand glowed white-hot, and the light ran outward from that hand in five lines—one to each speaker, and one line running off the edge of the mosaic, out through the ruined wall, toward the harbor, toward the deep places, toward—

Toward something vast and dim and guttering that Sefu felt in his own chest like a second heartbeat, fainter than it had been ten years ago, fainter than it had been last winter, and he thought, unbidden: *not much longer. Whatever else happens tonight, not much longer.*

Beside him, Isandro made a small sound.

He opened his eyes. She was staring down at the glowing figure at the circle's heart—at the open hand—as if she recognized it. And then the light reached the place where her sword hung at her hip, the plain guild-issue steel she carried the way she carried everything, quietly, and the blade answered. Just a thread of brightness along the fuller, just for a breath, like a dog hearing its name after years.

She snatched her hand back. The hall went dark. The rain resumed its ordinary gray.

"What was that," she whispered. "What was—that. The sword. Why did my *sword*—"

"Because the covenant isn't gone," Sefu said gently. "It's starving. And things that are starving reach for whatever comes close." He nodded at her hip. "And because you're a swordspeaker, Isandro Okafor. Blood-chosen, fire-marked. The last one. The prophecy book isn't wrong, and neither am I, and I think—" he paused, choosing honesty over kindness, because she seemed like someone who'd rather be wounded than handled, "—I think some part of you has known since the night you read your own name."

She sat back down on the pillar. Hard. Rain dripped off her chin.

"My parents were guild clerks," she said at last, to the mosaic. "Boring people. Kind people. They balanced accounts and paid their taxes and died within a season of each other, and I joined the guild because it was the only home that would have me, and I have been *good* at it, Sefu. Twenty years. I've never taken a dishonest coin. I've talked desperate people out of pawning things they'd regret. I once chased a thief six rooftops to return a bottled lullaby because the buyer's daughter needed it more than the thief needed the money." Her laugh came out cracked. "And you're telling me the whole time, the whole *time*, I've been the proud head clerk of a graveyard."

"Yes," Sefu said.

"You could lie to me. Make it gentler."

"A warden who lies about the fire ends up feeding on it," he said. "Ask anyone. Well. Ask me. Ask the four people who should be standing where you're sitting."

That landed. He watched it land—saw her shoulders drop a fraction, saw the fight go out of her posture and something else arrive in its place, heavier but steadier.

"The stones," she said. "The four stolen ones. Where are they?"

"Scattered. Sold, traded, gifted, lost. The founders were clever—they dispersed them so no one holder could ever reassemble the covenant. Some are in private collections. At least one is in a guild vault—I'd bet my remaining tooth on it. And the fifth—" he spread his hands, "the fifth speaker's stone went with her when she vanished. Never recovered. Either buried with her or waiting."

"Waiting," Isandro repeated. "You say that like stones wait."

"Everything in this story waits," Sefu said. "That's the horror of it. Dragons wait. Stones wait. Covenants wait. It's only people who run out of patience—and look what we do when we do."

* * *

She was quiet for a long time after that, long enough that he started to worry, and then she said, in the tone of a woman reading a ledger line aloud: "If what you're saying is true, then the guild didn't just steal from dragons. They murdered five people. They murdered the peace. Every border war since, every famine that ran unchecked because the realms wouldn't share grain—that's theirs. That's on the founding charter, written in fire we were never told was blood."

"Yes."

"And Veyra knows?" Her voice caught on the name, and Sefu marked that—filed it away gently. The guildmaster mattered to her. That was a lever, and also a wound, and he'd need to be careful with both. "Veyra's read the founding archives. She must have."

"Veyra knows a version," Sefu said carefully. "They all do. The version where the fire was a gift and the speakers were heroes who faded away natural and the guild inherited the stewardship fair and square. Lies survive best when the liars believe them. But the archives don't lie, Isandro. The ledgers don't lie. Your own books prove everything I've told you—if you know how to read them. And you do. Better than anyone alive, I'd guess."

She looked at him then, really looked, rain-wet and hollow-eyed and ferocious underneath. "Why me? You could have gone to the wardens' remnants, the old families, anyone. Why drag a guild clerk into a ruin to ruin her life?"

"Because of the sword," Sefu said simply. "Because the fire answered you and not me. Because the prophecy names you. And because—" he smiled, tired and true, "—because when I put a knife to your throat in your own counting room, you offered me tea and a deal instead of screaming. I've been alone with this truth for eleven years, Isandro. I wanted someone who balances books. Someone who can't stand an entry that doesn't add up. Because it doesn't add up. It hasn't added up for a hundred years, and you're the only person I've ever met who might be constitutionally incapable of letting that stand."

Isandro huffed something that was almost a laugh, and rubbed her face with both hands, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—the flatness burned away, something rawer and more dangerous underneath.

"The first stone. Where do we start?"

"*We*," Sefu repeated, savoring it.

"Don't make it strange."

"Wouldn't dream of it." He stood, joints cracking, and offered her his hand up. "We start tomorrow, before dawn, before the guild wakes. Because I have to tell you something else, and you're going to hate it."

"Of course I am."

"The night I broke into your guildhouse, I wasn't following the fire's scent alone." He watched her eyes narrow. "I was being followed myself. Wardens aren't the only ones who hunt by smell, and your guildmaster keeps something in her tower that knows my face. By tomorrow evening, Veyra will know a fugitive warden reached you. Which means by tomorrow evening, Isandro Okafor, the most honest woman in the Ledger Guild is going to be a suspect."

Rain drummed on the broken hall. Below their feet, faint as a banked coal, the mosaic's warmth lingered on.

"Good," Isandro said, and pulled her hood up, and for the first time since he'd known her—which was, admittedly, only days—she sounded like someone whose books had finally, terribly, begun to balance. "Then we'd better move fast."
