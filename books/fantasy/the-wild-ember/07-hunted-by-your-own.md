## 7. Hunted by Your Own

The bell above the guildhouse door had a voice like a cat stepping on a harp. Isandro had heard it ten thousand times and never once thought of it as an alarm until tonight, when it rang at half past the ninth hour—hours after the guildhouse should have been dark—and she understood, with the cold clarity of a woman reading her own death warrant in a ledger's margins, that the bell was ringing for *her*.

She was on the roof.

Not because she'd planned to be on the roof. Because three minutes earlier she had slipped out the scullery window with a satchel containing one water-damaged prophecy book, two changes of clothes, a whetstone, and—for reasons she would examine later, possibly under professional care—a jar of pickled quail eggs she'd bought from a street vendor who swore they were lucky.

The rain came down in the particular way it did over Vellmarrow in autumn: not falling so much as *settling*, a gray wool blanket of drizzle that soaked you through in the time it took to have a regret. Isandro crouched behind the chimney stack of the tallow-chandler's shop across the lane and watched the light bloom gold in the guildhouse windows below.

Six lanterns lit. Six. The night staff used two. The rest were for company.

And there, framed in the great window like a portrait hung slightly crooked, stood Guildmaster Veyra Ashkettle, holding up a piece of paper so the whole room could see it.

Isandro couldn't hear the words through the rain. She didn't need to. She knew the paper. She'd balanced its twin entries herself a hundred times: *WANTED — THIEF OF THE GUILD — REWARD AND FORGIVENESS TO WHOMEVER DELIVERS HER.*

Forgiveness. That was the part that made her laugh, actually laugh, a small unhinged sound swallowed instantly by the drizzle. Forgiveness. As if she were the stray dog and Veyra the kind farmer nailing up her picture. As if the crime were taking something, rather than *learning* something.

Below, the guildhouse doors opened and four enforcers came out into the rain, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been wet against their will.

Isandro did the arithmetic she was best at—the kind that kept people alive. Four enforcers on the street. Two more would take the canal gate, because Veyra always cut off the water first; she'd taught Isandro that herself, back when Isandro was eleven and still believed everything the guildmaster said was a kind of weather, natural and good. Which meant the east stairs to the ferry landing were already closing, which meant—

Which meant she was going to have to go through the Tanners' Reach, alone, at night, in the rain, carrying pickled eggs.

Her mother would have had opinions.

*Move,* said the part of her that had kept her alive through nineteen years of invisible competence. And Isandro moved.

* * *

The Tanners' Reach earned its name honestly and unfortunately. Even in the rain the smell of lime pits and cured hide rose up like a complaint the neighborhood had been filing for two hundred years without result. The walkways were slick with a substance Isandro chose not to identify, and the eaves dripped in rhythms that almost sounded like words if you were frightened enough.

She was frightened enough.

She went fast and low, hood down, one hand on the strap of her satchel, counting her footsteps the way she counted entries—because numbers were a floor you could stand on when everything else tilted. Forty steps to the covered arcade. Sixty to the rope bridge over the dye-channel. Eighty to—

"Ledger-sworn."

The voice came from her left, out of the shadow between two drying sheds, and it was calm in the way that made her stomach drop. Enforcer Dagny Holt. Of course. Veyra always sent Dagny, because Dagny never got angry and never got tired and once tracked a memory-thief through three days of carnival crowds purely by noticing he'd stopped buying candied ginger.

"Dagny," Isandro said, and hated how normal her voice sounded. Nineteen years of practice, and here it finally paid off: she could sound fine while dying inside. "You're out late."

"You're out *wrong*." Dagny stepped into the open. Rain ran off the brim of her oilskin hood. Behind her, two more shapes resolved out of the murk—young ones, Pell and Ossa, still wearing the eager faces of people who hadn't yet learned that catching thieves mostly meant paperwork. "Guildmaster wants you home, Isandro."

"Home." Isandro tasted the word. It sat strangely. "That's what we're calling it now?"

"That's what it's always been." Dagny's hand rested easy on her baton—not threatening, just present, the way a wall is present. "Whatever you found in that book, whatever Sefu told you—it can be sorted. You know how the guildmaster feels about you. Come in, give your account, let the ledgers do what ledgers do."

And that was the trap, wasn't it. Not the batons, not the six lanterns. The *account*. Give your account. Stand before the great book and speak plainly and be weighed. Nineteen years of Isandro's life had been built on exactly that ritual: tell the truth, balance the column, sleep well.

Except the last entry refused to be written. Except the truth was that the guild itself was the debt—that every bottled storm and borrowed memory in their vaults was dragon-fire wearing a receipt, and that the founding covenant had been broken by the very hands that wrote the rules, and that the woman who loved her like a daughter had maybe known all along.

"I gave my account for nineteen years," Isandro said. "Every trade, every pawn, every impossible thing. I never once fudged a number. Did you know that? I'm the only clerk in guild history with a perfect audit record."

"I know," Dagny said, and there was something almost gentle in it. "It's why this hurts everyone."

"It shouldn't hurt anyone. That's the whole point of what the book says." Isandro backed up a step, feeling the rain find the gap between her collar and her neck. "Dagny. The fire in those bottles. Do you know where it comes from? Actually comes from?"

Something flickered across Dagny's face—the smallest hesitation, the pause of a woman who had also, perhaps, wondered about things at three in the morning and then firmly gone back to sleep.

"Don't," Dagny said.

"A hundred years ago there were five swordspeakers—"

"*Don't.*" And now Dagny moved, and the two young ones moved with her, and the gentle moment closed like a ledger snapping shut. "Pell, the far arch. Ossa, the channel side. Isandro—I'm asking once."

Isandro looked at the arch. Looked at the channel. Did the arithmetic again, faster, and didn't like any of the sums.

Then she did the thing she had sworn, quietly and completely, never to do.

* * *

Here is what nobody in the guildhouse knew about Isandro Okafor, because she had spent nineteen years making sure of it: she was not merely a clerk who could handle a blade. Swordspeech was not swordsmanship. Swordsmanship was muscle and geometry and could be taught to anybody with patience and a high pain tolerance. Swordspeech was older than that—it was the art the five original swordspeakers had carried, the tongue in which a drawn blade became a sentence, a parry an argument, a killing stroke the final word of a contract no one else could read.

It was also, per the guild's own charter, the property of the Ledger Guild. Sworn swordspeakers spoke for the covenant. There were supposed to be five.

There was one, and she'd hidden it like a shameful appetite, and now, in the rain, in the stink of the tanneries, with three friends coming to drag her home to be forgiven—she opened her mouth and spoke it.

"Pell," she said—not his name, but the word that meant *stand*, spoken in the old tongue, pitched through the flat of the short sword she'd drawn without quite deciding to. The sound wasn't loud. That was the unsettling thing about swordspeech; it bypassed the ear and went somewhere nearer the spine. Pell stopped mid-stride as though he'd walked into a pane of glass. His face went slack with astonishment, the face of a man discovering the floor has opinions.

Ossa got two steps further, because Ossa was quick, and then Isandro turned, blade whispering a word that meant *between*, and suddenly there was very much a *between*—the sense of a line drawn in the world itself, running from her sword-tip to the wet cobblestones, and Ossa skidded to a halt on the wrong side of it with the expression of someone who has just realized the puddle might be deeper than advertised.

Dagny didn't stop. Dagny had twenty years and no imagination about stopping, which was her whole terrible excellence. She came in low and fast and honest, baton sweeping for Isandro's wrist, and Isandro met her—not with a word, but with plain steel, parry, turn, give ground, give ground. Because some sentences you don't say to family even when family is trying to arrest you.

"You've been *hiding* this?" Dagny breathed, pressing forward. Rain flew off their colliding weapons. "Nineteen years? Veyra raised you—"

"I know." Isandro's arm sang with the impact. Her boots slid on the slick stones; she caught herself on a drying rack and a hundred stiff hides clattered like applause. "That's what makes me sure she's wrong, Dagny. If she were right, lying to her wouldn't feel like drowning."

"She'll send everyone after you now."

"I know that too." Isandro gave one last full-strength parry that rang them both backward, and in the space it opened, she spoke a third word—the word for *afterward*, or possibly *farewell*, the grammar was famously ambiguous—and stepped backward off the edge of the dye-channel.

She had planned for the water. She had not planned for the water to be quite so committed to its work. The current took her like a debt collector, spun her under a bridge, scraped her politely along a mooring post, and delivered her—gasping, satchel clutched, dignity entirely dissolved—into the reeds downstream near the old cloth market, roughly a mile from anywhere she was supposed to be.

She lay in the mud among the reeds and laughed until her ribs hurt, because the alternative was crying, and she had a rule about crying in front of witnesses, even reedy ones.

Above her, faint through the rain, she heard Dagny shouting orders—not pursuit, not yet. Regrouping. Reporting. By morning there would be a dozen enforcers and a description and probably, knowing Veyra, a reward notice written in Isandro's own preferred style of calligraphy, which would sting worse than anything.

By morning she needed to be gone. Out of the city, or deep enough beneath it that morning couldn't find her. She had a stolen prophecy book, a name that belonged to a dead order, a sword that had finally remembered its vocabulary, and a jar of pickled quail eggs that had, she noted with relief, survived the river.

Lucky indeed.

She wrung out her hair, settled the satchel across her back, and looked east along the waterfront, where the lamps of the lower docks shivered on black water. Somewhere down there, in the warrens behind the fish-cries and the tar-sheds, lived the kind of people who didn't ask questions and didn't file reports—fences, runners, canal-rats, the small economy of the overlooked. She'd processed their trades for years. They owed her nothing. But a few of them owed the guild *less* than they feared it, and fear, properly spent, was currency too.

One stone. That was where the book said to start—one oath-stone, humming in the dark, waiting. She didn't know where. But she knew who might.

There was a pickpocket called Nour who'd been fencing guild goods without ever once setting foot in the guild, which meant somebody was feeding her, which meant somebody on the inside had a leak, which meant—

Isandro stood, dripping, in the reeds, and felt the strangest thing settle over her where the terror had been. Not safety. Nothing like safety. Something more like a column finally balancing after years of quiet error: the terrible, clarifying click of a ledger that has stopped pretending.

They had made her invisible for nineteen years. Fine. Invisible was useful.

She pulled her hood up and walked into the rain, toward the docks, toward the dark, toward the first stone—leaving behind her, on the wet stones of the Tanners' Reach, a single line of water that was not rain, running from the place where a sword had spoken the word for *farewell*, and glowing, faintly, like an ember refusing the dark.
