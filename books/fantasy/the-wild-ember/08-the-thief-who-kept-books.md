## 8. The Thief Who Kept Books

The flophouse sat where the Lantern Canal bent like a man pretending he hadn't been drinking—half in shadow, half in the greasy amber light that leaked from the tanneries downstream. Isandro had passed it a hundred times without seeing it, which was precisely why she saw it now. Buildings that went unnoticed were either worthless or very well paid to be forgettable, and the Ledger Guild had taught her that the second kind always had vacancies.

She paid for three nights with coins she'd bitten and re-bitten until her jaw ached. The clerk behind the desk—a woman whose eyes had the flat patience of someone who had seen every variety of midnight arrival—asked no name. Isandro gave her one anyway: "Maren." It was the name stitched into the collar of the coat she'd stolen from a drying line two districts back, and she had decided it was rude not to honor the donation.

Her room was four steps by five, smelled of river mold and someone else's pipe smoke, and contained a bed that creaked when she only looked at it. She wedged the prophecy book under the loose floorboard, sat on the mattress with her back to the wall, and did what she had done every night of her adult life.

She kept books.

Not guild books—she would never balance a column again without hearing Veyra's voice saying *an entry is a promise, Isandro, and promises are the only currency worth anything*—but her own. A small ledger she'd carried since apprenticeship, its spine cracked soft as old bread. In it she recorded debts she could still pay: the baker on Tallow Street who'd once fed her free when she was eleven and starving; the ferryman whose oar she'd mended; the nameless client whose bottled storm she'd sold knowing it was already dying in the glass.

Item by item, debt by debt, she wrote herself into order. This was the trick of surviving, she had learned young. Not strength, not cleverness. Order. A person who knew exactly what she owed and to whom could not be unmade by anything as simple as fear.

Except that tonight the columns refused to close.

*Debt owed: Sefu.* She stared at the line. The dragon-warden had let her walk out of the underlevels alive after she'd pointed a sword at him, which was one entry. He had shown her a warehouse full of bottled light that wept when opened, which was another. But the true debt was stranger and heavier: he had looked at her ledger-trained face and said, *You didn't know. You honestly didn't know what they've been selling,* and something in his voice had been worse than anger—it had been pity, and pity was a debt with no fixed term.

And beneath that, unwritten but present as a bruise:

*Debt owed: the dragons.*

She closed the book. Outside, rain began again, drumming the canal into static. Somewhere across the city, Veyra's enforcers would be working through the night, checking doors, checking faces. Somewhere, a description of her was being read aloud in a voice trained to sound reasonable. *Last swordspeaker. Dangerous if spoken to. Report immediately.*

She lay down without undressing and discovered that the bed creaked even when nobody moved at all, which felt less like a flaw and more like a philosophy.

* * *

She woke to the sound of an argument being conducted at professional volume through her floorboards.

"—all I'm saying is that a candle is a *shared resource*, Nour."

"And all I'm saying is I found the candle, Tam, so by the law of finders—"

"You don't have a law of finders. You have a law of whatever-I-say-is-the-law-because-you're-bigger."

"That's not—" A pause. "Okay, yes, that's exactly my law. But it's a good law. It's served me beautifully."

Isandro lay still, listening. There was a rhythm to this argument that told her it was old, worn smooth from repetition, the way staircases wore hollow in the middle. Nobody argued about candles with this much affection unless they'd been arguing about them for years.

She should have left. Every instinct the guild had ground into her said: *complication, exposure, leave now, travel light.* But her boots were across the room, the floorboard with her book in it groaned like a guilty thing when stepped on, and—she admitted this to herself with some irritation—she wanted to hear how the candle dispute resolved.

It resolved with a knock at her door. Three raps, quick and confident.

"Maren," came the voice through the wood—the bigger one, Nour's. "You've got the corner room, which means you're closest to the draft, which means technically the candle belongs half to you, which means I need you to settle something."

Isandro considered her options. Then she considered that whoever was behind that door had just demonstrated, in under thirty seconds, that they knew exactly who was staying in which room, which meant the flophouse clerk talked, which meant these people were either dangerous or simply poor, and in her experience the poor were far more dangerous because they had nothing to lose and excellent hearing.

She opened the door.

Two people stood in the corridor, lit by the disputed candle itself, which Nour held aloft like a magistrate's gavel. Nour was short, wiry, somewhere south of thirty, with a scarf wrapped high enough to suggest a hairstyle with a legal history. Her eyes were quick and brown and currently performing innocence with the subtlety of a street juggler. Beside her stood a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the haunted posture of someone who had spent years being yelled at by people holding clipboards. His vest was good wool gone threadbare at the cuffs. Isandro recognized the cut instantly: guild-issue, clerk's grade, three seasons out of fashion.

Tam saw her noticing. His hand drifted toward his cuff with the doomed instinct of a man trying to hide evidence by touching it.

"Half," Isandro said.

Nour blinked. "Half what?"

"Half the candle. You said I get a vote. Half yours, half mine, and the ink-stained gentleman can have the wick-end, where all the decisions actually happen."

There was a silence of exactly the length required for two people to decide whether to be alarmed. Then Nour grinned, wide and unguarded, and Isandro understood she was looking at a person who smiled the way other people drew knives—fast, and to establish terms.

"I like her," Nour announced to the corridor generally. "Tam. We're keeping her."

"We are not *keeping* people," Tam said. "That's how you ended up responsible for the goat."

"The goat was a gift."

"The goat was a *consequence*."

* * *

They ended up in Tam and Nour's room, which was larger than hers because Tam had negotiated for it using skills Isandro recognized with a jolt of homesickness: the clerk's art of making a smaller share sound generous. One candle, indeed. One chair, one trunk, two pallets, and a window that looked out on the canal with the resigned expression of a window that had seen things.

"So," Nour said, settling cross-legged on the trunk and passing Isandro a chipped cup of tea that tasted mostly of hot water's ambitions. "Maren, is it?"

"It's on the coat," Isandro agreed.

"Uh-huh." Nour sipped her own tea. "And the coat's not yours."

"Does it matter?"

"Not to me. To him, maybe." She jerked her chin at Tam. "He gets twitchy about names. Occupational hazard."

Tam had gone very still in his chair. He was watching Isandro with the particular dread of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish. "Your hands," he said finally.

"What about them?"

"Ink," he said. "Ledger ink. Iron-gall, the cheap batch—we used to complain about it every quarter-day, the whole records division, there's a petition in the archive, I *filed* it—" He stopped himself, breathed. "You write columns. Right-handed. Fast. And you flinched, earlier, when I said 'the archive.' Nobody flinches at 'archive' unless they've loved one or fled one."

The candle guttered. Rain worked at the window. Isandro weighed her lies and found, to her surprise, that she was tired of carrying them. She had lied to Veyra two nights ago—her first lie in nineteen years of service—and the oath in her blood had shifted like a bone setting wrong. Every lie since had sat on that fracture. Perhaps that was why she heard herself say:

"Isandro Okafor. Senior reconciler, Third Circle. Or I was, before last night, when the guild declared me a thief."

Tam made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling.

"*Okafor,*" he repeated. "The Okafor. You balanced the Meridian account. The impossible one, the storm-bottling contract with the floating decimals—I read your reconciliation, it was beautiful, it was *elegant*, I showed it to—" He caught himself again, visibly wrestled something down. "I showed it to my supervisor. Who reported me for unsolicited archival enthusiasm."

"You got disciplined for complimenting a ledger?" Nour asked.

"I got disciplined for a great many things," Tam said with dignity. "The compliment was merely the first charge."

Isandro laughed before she could stop herself—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a long winter. She couldn't remember the last time she'd laughed in front of anyone. The realization sobered her faster than any interrogation could have.

"Why are you here?" she asked. "Both of you. Truly."

Nour and Tam exchanged a look—the married look, or whatever their version of it was, the look of people who had divided up the telling long ago.

"She goes first," Tam said. "Hers is shorter."

"Hers is *better*, is what he means." Nour set down her cup. "I'm a pickpocket. Good one. Best on the Lantern bend, ask anyone, don't ask the watch. Two years ago I started finding easy marks—guild couriers, warehouse men, clerks running late-night deliveries. Little things. A lacquered box here, a sealed tube there. Never heavy, never guarded proper. I figured the guild was careless." Her grin faded around the edges. "Then I started fencing the goods, and the buyers started acting strange. Whispering. Paying too much. One old man opened a little glass bottle I'd lifted off a courier, and he cried. Just stood there in his shop and wept, and wouldn't say why, and gave me triple and told me never to bring him another one. So I stopped selling those. Kept them. I've got six in a sock under my floorboard and I'll be honest, Maren-who-isn't-Maren, they hum at night."

"They would," Isandro said quietly. She thought of Sefu on his knees in the warehouse, and the flame in the bottles leaning toward him like children toward a father. "They're not what the ledgers say they are."

"No," Tam said. "They aren't." He leaned forward, and the candlelight caught the ink stains, the tremor in his hands. "I was Fourth Circle records, acquisitions annex. My job was copying entries. That's all. Copy, seal, file. For nine years I copied entries and never once asked what an entry *was*. A storm, bottled. A memory, decanted. A second chance—do you know what the ledgers called second chances, Okafor? Line items. *Recovery of asset, minor, perishable.* Someone's whole life, and it had a shelf-life notation." His voice cracked; he steadied it with visible effort. "Eight months ago I pulled the founding covenant for a routine audit. The original. Sealed copy, pre-schism. And the numbers—" He shook his head. "The numbers didn't reconcile. They *couldn't*. The guild's founding surplus, the entire treasury of wonders they built everything on—it has no lawful origin. Every entry traces back to a source that doesn't exist in any register. Except I found the source. In the oldest marginalia. A word the archivists redacted so many times it left a groove in the page."

"Dragons," Isandro said.

"Dragons," Tam agreed miserably. "I made the mistake of writing a memo. One memo, flagged for internal review, marked *urgent*. By morning my clearance was revoked, my quarters were reassigned, and I was informed that my services were concluded with gratitude. Which is guild language for *run*." He managed a thin smile. "So I ran. With, I confess, certain documents."

"He took a whole satchel," Nour said fondly. "My hero."

"They were going to burn them!"

"They were going to burn *you*, love, the satchel was incidental."

Isandro sat with it. Three people around one candle: a thief who kept stolen wonders in a sock because they made an old man cry, a clerk who had committed career suicide over a reconciliation error, and herself—the last swordspeaker, hiding under a dead woman's name with a prophecy book under her floorboard and a dragon-warden's debt written in her own hand.

"You understand," she said slowly, "that if the guild finds this room, none of us walks out of it."

"Oh, absolutely," Nour said cheerfully. "Tam says that every morning. It's practically our prayer. *May the guild find this room never, and may breakfast include eggs.*"

"That is *not* our prayer."

"It's a lovely prayer."

Despite everything—the hunt, the oath-fracture, the bottled grief humming across the city—Isandro felt something loosen in her chest. It was alarming, how easily it loosened. Nineteen years of competent solitude, of keeping herself invisible even inside the guildhouse, of eating alone and balancing alone and sleeping alone, and apparently all the fortification required to undo it was one bad candle and two criminals who bickered like weather.

"I have information," she heard herself say. "About the covenant. About what the guild broke, and what's coming due. Most of it I can't explain yet. Some of it I don't understand yet myself. But there are objects—oath-stones—scattered through this city, and I need to find them, and I cannot do it alone, and I am asking two people I met an hour ago to help me commit what the guild will definitely call treason."

Silence. The candle flame stood very straight, as if listening.

"Question," Nour said, raising a finger. "Do the stones hum? Like the bottles?"

Isandro blinked. "I—possibly. Why?"

"Because Tam's satchel has been humming since Tuesday and he keeps insisting it's the plumbing."

"It *could* be the plumbing."

"TAM."

He sighed the sigh of a man whose secrets had a shorter shelf life than his memos, rose, and unlatched the satchel. From beneath a brick-thick layer of misfiled documents he drew a cloth bundle, unwrapped it on the table between them, and there it lay: a stone the size of a fist, gray-green, ordinary-looking, except that it glowed. Faintly. Rhythmically. Like something breathing in its sleep.

The hair on Isandro's arms rose. The oath in her blood—the old oath, the broken one, the one she'd sworn at seventeen and never questioned—shifted again, and this time it didn't feel like a fracture.

It felt like recognition.

"That's an oath-stone," she whispered. "That's the first one. How—"

"Fenced goods," Tam said apologetically. "Lot 44, disposition sale, unclaimed inventory. I bought it for six coppers because it made my teeth itch and I thought it might be evidence." He rubbed the back of his neck. "In my defense, it *is* evidence."

Nour leaned back against the wall, arms crossed, grinning at Isandro through the candlelight. "So. Treason, then."

"Treason," Isandro confirmed. "Tomorrow. Tonight we sleep, and we plan, and—" she paused, struck by the absurdity and rightness of it, "—and someone should probably buy more candles. Shared resource."

Nour's grin widened into something warmer, something that had nothing to do with establishing terms. "Welcome home, Maren-who-isn't-Maren," she said. "Fair warning: we keep people."

Outside, the rain eased, and beneath the canal—far beneath, past silt and rust and salt—something vast turned over in its sleep, and dreamed of debts.
