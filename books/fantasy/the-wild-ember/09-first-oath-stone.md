## 9. First Oath-Stone

Tam had spent eleven years as a guild clerk, and in that time he had learned exactly one thing worth learning: everything is a ledger. Every debt, every kindness, every sin—it all gets written down somewhere, by somebody, eventually. He had thought he'd escaped the ledgers when they threw him out. Now he was walking toward a pawnshop with three fugitives at his back and a confession lodged in his throat like a fishbone, and he understood that the largest ledger of all was the one he kept behind his eyes.

The shop was on Candlewick Row, in the crook of the city where the canal bent like a question mark. Tam had not been there in four years. His feet remembered the way anyway, which felt like a betrayal of its own.

"You're quiet," Nour said, falling into step beside him. She walked the way she picked pockets—light, sideways, always appearing where you didn't expect her. "Quieter than usual, I mean. You've got the look of a man doing arithmetic."

"I'm always doing arithmetic."

"No," she said. "You've got the look of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't *like*."

Ahead of them, Isandro moved through the morning crowd like a woman made of smoke—hood up, shoulders loose, eyes cataloguing exits. Sefu brought up the rear, tall and careful, drawing fewer glances than Tam expected. People saw what they expected to see: a big man in a laborer's coat, nothing more. The dragon-warden's tattoos were wrapped in linen bandages, which he claimed were for a boil. Nobody believed him. Everybody politely pretended to.

"It's her, isn't it," Nour said quietly. "The lover."

Tam's stomach performed a small, practiced maneuver. "Ex-lover. Former beloved. A closed account."

"Closed accounts don't make your voice do that."

"Do what?"

"*That*." She gestured at all of him. "That creaky-door thing."

He didn't answer, because the answer was yes, and because ahead of them Isandro had stopped, and because the shop sign had come into view through the drizzle: **MERIT & DAUGHTERS — FAIR RATES, FAIRER WORDS**, painted in gold that had once been bright and was now the color of old honey.

Merit. Odile Merit. Four years, and the name still landed in his chest like a dropped coin.

* * *

The bell over the door had been broken since before Tam knew her; Odile said a pawnshop should announce no one, because everyone who entered was carrying something they'd rather keep hidden. So they simply pushed inside, into warmth and dust and the particular smell of a place where other people's lives sat on shelves waiting to be redeemed.

Odile Merit stood behind the counter with a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, examining a ring. She was shorter than Tam remembered, or perhaps he had grown taller around the absence of her. Her hair was iron-grey now at the temples. She did not look up.

"Be with you in a moment," she said. "Unless you're here to rob me, in which case be quick about it, I have lunch at noon."

"Hello, Odile."

The loupe came down. For a long moment the only sound in the shop was the tick of a clock missing its pendulum weight, which meant it counted seconds unevenly, which Odile had always said was more honest than a clock that pretended time was regular.

"Well," Odile said at last. "They let clerks out of the guildhouse these days without an escort. The world has gone soft." Her eyes moved past him, counting. Isandro. Sefu. Nour. Back to Tam. "Or you're not a clerk anymore."

"Not for some years."

"Fired?"

"Worse. Disgraced."

"Mm." She set the ring down on green felt with terrible gentleness. "And yet here you are, bringing trouble to my door, which suggests your disgrace has *teeth*. Sit down, all of you. Not you—" this to Sefu, whose hand had drifted toward a shelf of bottled voices, "—touch nothing. You have the hands of a man who breaks things by admiring them."

"He does," said Nour, delighted, and sat.

* * *

It took twenty minutes to get there, because Odile Merit could not be hurried any more than a stone could be hurried, and because Tam kept finding side roads away from the confession. He told her about the flood that had ruined the eastern annexes' records. She told him about her daughter, married now, living two canals away. He asked about the shop. She answered. And underneath it all ran the current, the thing unsaid, until finally Isandro—because Isandro had the tact of a drawn blade—set a small object on the counter between them.

She had found it on a shelf near the window: a brass letter-knife, engraved with the Ledger Guild's seal. Tam recognized it instantly. He had given it to Odile six years ago, the last gift before the last fight.

"That's mine," Odile said. "Priced at eight coppers, if anyone asks. No one ever asks." She looked at Tam then, fully, and her eyes were the same grey-brown as rain over slate. "Say what you came to say, Ishikawa. You were never good at circling. It's why we fought."

Tam looked at his hands. Clerk's hands. Ink-stained, soft-palmed, guilty.

"The guild keeps things," he began. "Things people pawn and never redeem. That's normal—that's the trade. But there's a vault below the counting-house. The Deep Shelf, we called it. Things go in and the ledgers say *destroyed*, or *lost*, or *never received*, but they're not destroyed. They're kept. Kept by order of the guildmaster." He swallowed. "I was the clerk for the Deep Shelf. I logged every item. I knew what was in there."

"And?" Odile said, though her voice had changed, gone careful.

"And four years ago, someone came to redeem an item from the Deep Shelf. An old man. He'd pawned something decades before—a stone. Grey, oval, no bigger than a fist. He wanted it back. He'd scraped together the redemption fee, all of it, coins wrapped in cloth, and I—" Tam's throat closed. "I told him the item had been destroyed. I showed him the ledger entry. My own handwriting. *Item lost in Annex fire.* There was no fire. The stone was sitting thirty feet beneath us, and I knew it, and I lied to his face because Veyra had ordered the Deep Shelf sealed and sealed meant sealed, and because I was afraid of losing my position, my rooms, my—"

"Yourself," Odile said quietly. "You were afraid of losing yourself."

"I told myself it was just a stone. Some old man's sentimental rock. What harm could it do to keep it?" He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Then yesterday I learned what the stones are. What they *were*. And I learned that the old man—the one I turned away—he died that winter. Pauper's grave in Saltfield. And his stone is still in that vault, and it might be the only chance the world has, and I sent him home empty-handed with his coins and his hope and my neat little lie."

The clock ticked its crooked tick. Nour had gone very still. Even Sefu's breathing seemed to have stopped.

"So," Tam finished, because a ledger must balance, "that's the entry. Debit: one betrayal, compounded four years' interest. I don't know how to pay it. I never did."

* * *

He expected anger. He deserved anger. He had rehearsed the scene on the walk over—Odile's scorn, the door, the walk back through the rain having failed everyone. Instead Odile Merit reached beneath the counter and came up with a bottle and five small cups, and poured, and pushed a cup across to each of them, and drank her own in one motion.

"My mother founded this shop on a rule," she said. "Fair rates, fairer words. Do you know what fair words means, Tam? It means you tell the truth *when it costs you*. Anyone can tell the truth when it's cheap." She refilled her cup. "You just told the truth at ruinous expense, in front of strangers, with your whole face falling off while you did it. That's the first fair sentence you've spoken to me in six years."

"That's forgiveness?"

"That's *inventory*," she said. "Assessment of goods received. Whether it's forgiveness depends on what you ask next."

Isandro leaned forward. "We need the stone, Odile. The one the old man pawned. We believe it's an oath-stone—one of five, scattered when the covenant between humans and dragons was broken. If Ashveth dies, the last dragon, then everything the guild trades—every impossible thing—dies with her. Or worse than dies. We think the thefts are feeding on her flame."

"And you want mine."

"If it's here."

"Oh, it's here." Odile stood, brushed off her apron, and beckoned. "Come and see what your guild taught me, Ishikawa. Come and see what a woman learns keeping other people's secrets on a shelf."

* * *

The back room smelled of cedar and lamp oil. Shelves rose floor to ceiling, and on them sat objects that made Nour inhale audibly: a birdcage with no bird, humming faintly; a mirror that reflected the room a half-second late; a jar of darkness labeled, in faded ink, *NIGHT, ONE GOOD ONE*.

"Every pawnbroker in this city holds unredeemed goods," Odile said, running her finger along a shelf edge. "Most sell them after the term lapses. I never sold certain items. I couldn't say why, at first. Then I understood: some things aren't *goods*. They're *custody*. Someone walks in and pawns their dead wife's wedding ring, and you know—they know—they'll come back for it someday, even if someday never comes. You don't sell custody. You keep faith with it." She stopped at a shelf at eye height, reached up, and took down a wooden box, plain and unremarkable, tied with string. "The old man told me what it was, when he pawned it. I thought he was mad. He said, *This is a promise made flesh, and one day someone will come who can hear it sing.*"

She set the box on the table and untied the string with the same terrible gentleness she'd used on the ring.

Inside, on a bed of wool gone grey with age, lay a stone. Oval, grey, unremarkable—except that as Isandro stepped closer, it began to hum.

Not a sound, exactly. A vibration in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the wet chambers of the ear. Tam watched Isandro's face change—watched her go from tired fugitive to something older and more frightened and more certain, all at once.

"It knows me," Isandro whispered. "It's been waiting."

"It's been *humming*," said Nour, backing up a step. "Why is it humming? Stones shouldn't—Sefu, tell me stones do that."

"They don't," Sefu said softly. He had removed his bandages somewhere in the last minute, and his forearms were bare, and the warden's tattoos—spirals like whirlpools, like coiled serpents, like flames seen through water—were moving. Faintly. In time with the stone. "But they used to. Before my order fell, the wardens carried tokens that sang when an oath-keeper drew near. I thought every one of those tokens had gone silent forever." He looked at Isandro with an expression Tam couldn't read—hope, maybe, worn thin by decades of disappointment. "Take it. Please. Take it before it stops again."

* * *

Isandro reached out—and stopped.

"What is it?" Tam asked.

"She hasn't paid," Odile said. There was no mockery in it. "I can see it from here. She's thinking about the redemption fee." Odile folded her arms. "Well? It's a pawnshop. Everything has a price. That's not cruelty, girl, that's honesty. The old man paid forty years of savings to reclaim what was his, and I turned him away too—not with a lie, but with a locked door and a guild writ I had no power to argue. We are both of us standing in the shadow of that day." Her voice roughened. "So name your price, swordspeaker. What will you give?"

Isandro was silent a long moment. Then she said, "A promise. Witnessed aloud."

"That's not currency."

"It's the only currency I have left. When this is done—when the covenant stands again or doesn't—I'll come back, and I'll sit in your shop, and I'll tell you everything. All of it, the parts that flatter nobody. And whatever you decide I owe after hearing it, I'll pay. That's the trade."

Odile studied her the way she'd studied the ring, loupe-less, thorough. Then she snorted—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after long disuse.

"Clerk," she said to Tam, without looking away from Isandro, "did you teach her that, or did she teach you?"

"She scared it into me," Tam admitted.

"Hm. Good." Odile pushed the box across the table. "Witnessed aloud, then. Everyone heard it. Nour, you're a pickpocket; your testimony is legally worthless but morally binding. Take it—and take this too." From her apron pocket she produced a small purse and pressed it into Isandro's hand. "Travel money. Don't argue. Consider it the interest on forty years of a promise kept safe. The old man's name was Edmun Vael. When your stone sings again—if it sings again—you say his name to it. Let him hear."

Isandro closed her fingers around the purse. "Edmun Vael," she repeated. "I swear it."

* * *

They were nearly at the door when Odile spoke again.

"Tam."

He turned. She had followed them as far as the counter and stood with her hands flat on the wood, the way she used to stand when the argument wasn't finished but the night was.

"Four years ago, you chose the guild over an old man's grief. Today you chose an old man's grief over your own skin. That's not the same man." She paused. "It's better bookkeeping."

"Odile—"

"Don't." But her voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough. "I spent four years being angry at you, and it turns out I was angry at the right person for the wrong reasons. I thought you left because I was difficult. Turns out you stayed too long at a job that was eating you alive. Both things can be true. Both of us can be fools." She straightened her apron. "When this mad business is done—whatever it is—come back. Buy the letter-knife. Eight coppers. We'll call the account open."

Tam's vision blurred embarrassingly. Behind him, he heard Nour whisper, "*Are they going to kiss,*" and Sefu rumble, "*Not in front of the stone,*" which made no sense whatsoever and was somehow exactly right.

"I'll come back," Tam managed.

"See that you do." Odile returned to her loupe and her ring, and the interview was over, the way all her interviews ended: mid-sentence, on her terms, leaving you standing in the doorway with your heart rearranged.

* * *

Outside, the drizzle had thickened into honest rain. Isandro walked with the box held against her chest like something newborn, and the stone inside hummed against her heartbeat, and Tam found himself matching his steps to hers without meaning to.

"So," Nour said, skipping a puddle. "One down. How many to go?"

"Four," Isandro said. "No—three more. This makes one."

"Three more stones," Tam said, "and every one of them harder than the last. The second sits in Veyra's private vault. I've seen the manifest. I logged it, back when I still believed the entries meant anything." He hesitated. "Which means I know the locks. And the guard rotations. And the blind spot in the east stairwell that the architects swore didn't exist."

Isandro stopped walking. Rain ran off her hood. She looked at him with an expression he was beginning to dread and treasure in equal measure—the look of a woman deciding how much to trust a confessed liar.

"You're volunteering to break us into your old guildmaster's house," she said.

"I'm volunteering," Tam said, "to correct an error in the record. It's what clerks do." He pulled his collar up against the rain, and beneath the fear, beneath the shame still settling in him like sediment, something unfamiliar stirred—something that felt almost like purpose. "Besides. You heard her. Fair words. I've got four years of silence to make up for, and I intend to itemize."

Behind them, faint through the rain and the noise of the street, the stone in its box hummed—and for half a heartbeat, Tam would have sworn it hummed in harmony with a second note, distant and low, rising from somewhere beneath the harbor. Like something vast, turning over in its sleep.

Like something listening.

Isandro heard it too. He saw her go rigid, saw her press the box tighter, saw her turn her face toward the water with an expression that had nothing to do with hope and everything to do with dread.

"We need to move," she said quietly. "She knows we've started. And I don't think—" She stopped, swallowed. "I don't think we have as much time as the prophecy says. Something's changing. Faster than it should."

Nobody argued. Four figures melted into the rain-soaked streets, heading for the river, carrying one small grey stone and all the promises they hadn't yet learned how to keep.
