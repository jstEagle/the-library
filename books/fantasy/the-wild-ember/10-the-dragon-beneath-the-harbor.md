## 10. The Dragon Beneath the Harbor

Sefu had never brought anyone to a dragon before.

For eleven years he had been the last warden, and wardens did not share their dragons. That was not pride—it was arithmetic. Every person who knew where Ashveth slept was one more throat for the guild to squeeze, one more mouth that could be bought with the very fire she was dying of losing. He had kept her secret the way you keep a wound covered: not because it is shameful, but because the air gets in.

But tonight he stood on the rotting pier at the harbor's dead end with four unlikely companions—two of them still arguing about who had eaten whose dried plums—and understood that secrecy had become just another way of being alone.

"She's under *there*?" Nour peered over the edge of the pilings into water the color of old tea. "Under the harbor? Under the *water*?"

"Under the silt, under the wreck of the ferry *Kindly Fortune*, inside a shell of salt and rust that she built herself." Sefu crouched and touched the black water. It was cold even through his calluses. "She's been there longer than the city has had its name."

"And she's—" Tam adjusted his spectacles, which fogged immediately in the harbor damp, "—large?"

Sefu considered how to answer honestly. Tam was a clerk; honesty would land differently on him than it would on anyone else.

"The last survey the wardens made, before I was born, measured her at four hundred feet from snout to tail-tip." He watched Nour's eyebrows climb. "That survey was taken when she was young. And hungry. She has grown since."

"*Grown*," Nour repeated faintly. She looked at Isandro. "You knew about this?"

"I read a prophecy book," Isandro said. "It didn't include measurements."

"Everything worth knowing comes without measurements," said Tam, with the weary authority of a man who had once tried to file an unmeasurable thing and been laughed out of three departments.

Sefu almost smiled. Then the smile caught on something in his chest—the same thing that always caught when he stood here, above her, doing nothing while she guttered. Eleven years of coming down to sit on this pier like a man visiting a grave that occasionally breathed.

"Rules," he said, standing. His voice came out rougher than intended, so he started again. "There are rules. First: she will speak to whoever she chooses, or no one. Don't take it personally if she ignores you. She's ignored kings. Second: don't offer her anything. Not gifts, not apologies, not promises. Dragons have heard every kind of promise humans make, and most of them were lies with good posture."

"What's third?" Nour asked.

"There is no third," Sefu admitted. "I only feel better when things come in threes."

"That's a clerk instinct," Tam said, "and I'd thank you not to insult me with it."

They went down.

* * *

The way beneath the harbor was not a passage so much as an apology—a gap in the world that the sea had agreed to overlook. Sefu led them along a maintenance tunnel from the days when the city had maintained things, then through a flooded customs house where tax records floated past in gray sheets, and finally out into open water at a depth where the lamplight died and the dark became a substance you moved through rather than within.

He carried the only light: a single coal of true dragon-fire, kept in a lantern of warden-glass. It burned without heat, without smoke, a small stubborn gold point. It had been lit from Ashveth herself, nine years ago, and in all that time had never dimmed—which was either a miracle or a reproach, depending on the day.

Isandro swam beside him. In the green-black water her face was pale as paper, but her eyes tracked everything: the tunnel mouths, the rusted chains, the shapes that might have been fish and might have been older residents. She had put up a fight before coming—quiet, reasonable arguments, the kind that were actually requests to be talked out of fear—but she had come. Sefu was learning that about her. She argued like someone buying time to be brave.

The others followed in a loose line behind. Nour swam like she'd been born in floodwater, which she probably had. Tam swam like a filing cabinet being thrown downstairs, with dignity and considerable splashing.

Then the floor of the harbor fell away.

They hung above a trench, and at the bottom of the trench, something vast shifted its breathing.

Even after eleven years, Sefu's body forgot, every time, what was coming. The first sign was warmth—a current rising past them, blood-warm in the cold water. Then the smell: salt and iron and something underneath both, something like a hearth in a house you'd forgotten you loved. And then the light.

She was guttering. That was the truth of it, the terrible domestic truth. Where once the wardens' songs described a dragon burning like a second dawn, Ashveth now glowed the way a lamp glows at the end of its oil: patches of ember-orange along her flank, long stretches of dark scale between, and one slow pulse of light beneath her ribs, in and out, in and out, like a sleeper deciding each breath whether to keep taking them.

Her shell rose around her—a cathedral of fused salt and ship-iron, crusted with barnacles, hung with drowned nets like faded bunting. She had built it herself, scale by scale, breath by breath, the way a wounded animal builds a den or a lonely person builds a life.

Sefu felt the others stop moving. Felt their awe arrive like weather.

Nour's hand found his sleeve. "Is she asleep?"

"No," Sefu said softly. "She's listening. She's been listening for a hundred years. That's most of what's left of her—a hundred years of listening to a city that doesn't know it owes her anything."

He struck the warden-knock against the shell: three blows, pause, two blows. The rhythm of a friend asking leave to enter.

The pulse beneath her ribs stuttered.

And the whole harbor grew quiet—not silent, because the sea is never silent, but attentive. Even the currents seemed to hold their positions.

Then, without any sound at all, in the space behind their eyes where dreams live:

***Come in, little keeper. And bring your strays.***

* * *

They did not swim down so much as get gathered. The warm current took them like cupped hands and set them gently in a dry pocket of air within the shell—a chamber the size of a chapel, ribbed with iron, floored with crushed salt that glittered faintly, though the only light was the slow ember-pulse of the dragon beyond an archway wide enough to admit a cathedral.

Ashveth's head rested in the chamber itself, folded low, one eye half-open. The eye was the size of a cart wheel and the color of cooling bronze, and it regarded them with the patience of a mountain regarding weather.

Tam sat down abruptly in the salt. "I'm fine," he announced, from the floor. "This is a normal amount of fainting."

"You haven't fainted," Nour pointed out.

"I'm getting ahead of it."

Isandro had gone very still. Sefu watched her watch the dragon, and saw something pass over her face—not fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman reading a ledger entry that refuses to be written.

Ashveth's attention settled on him first, warm and heavy as a blanket.

***Eleven years,*** she said, in the voice-that-was-not-a-voice, which nonetheless managed to convey both fondness and mild scolding. ***Eleven years of visits, and you finally bring guests. Were you afraid I would embarrass you?***

"I was afraid they'd eat your snacks," Sefu said.

***I have no snacks.***

"He's joking," Nour stage-whispered. "He does that when he's terrified. I've catalogued it."

The great eye turned to Nour, and something ancient flickered across it—amusement, Sefu thought, actual amusement. ***A thief. Good. Thieves understand value better than priests do.***

Then the eye moved to Tam, who had achieved a seated position and was clutching his soggy ledger bag like a talisman. ***A record-keeper. One of the ones who wrote us down as inventory.***

"They—they misspelled some entries," Tam said miserably. "In our defense, nobody proofread."

***And the swordspeaker.***

The eye came to rest on Isandro, and the whole chamber changed temperature. Not colder. Deeper. As though the room itself leaned in.

Isandro did not flinch. Sefu admired that and worried about it in equal measure.

"You know my name," Isandro said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the salt-chapel, too human.

***I have known your name since before your grandmother learned to walk,*** Ashveth said. ***Your predecessors spoke it into the covenant. Ishikawa, daughter of no guild, last of five lines. They wrote you into the oath the way a builder writes a keystone into a plan—for a building not yet raised. We waited a long time for the keystone to notice the arch.***

"I'm not—I've never sworn anything."

***No. You were born already sworn. There's a difference, and it matters, and we will come back to it.*** The eye blinked, slow as a season turning. ***First, the debt.***

* * *

Later—much later, when Sefu tried to describe that night to himself, lying awake in a canal-side flophouse smelling of mildew and other people's cooking—he could not reconstruct the dream as a sequence of events. It had arrived the way weather arrives, all at once and everywhere.

He only knew that Ashveth had spoken to Isandro alone, and that the rest of them had been given the edges of it: images that drifted through the shared dark like debris through floodwater.

A hillside of eggs, each one glowing like a coal, and hands—human hands—carrying them away in ledger-stamped crates. A sky full of wings thinning year by year until it held only one. A hall of white stone where five speakers swore five oaths, and four of the stones going dark in the same night, sold to collectors, sunk in temples, locked in vaults. A word repeated like a heartbeat through all of it: ***unpaid, unpaid, unpaid.***

Beside him in the dark, Isandro wept. She did it silently, furiously, the way people cry who learned young that crying costs something. Sefu reached out and found her hand and held it, and she let him, which was perhaps the bravest thing either of them did that night.

When the vision released them, the salt-chapel was gold-lit and ordinary again—or as ordinary as anything can be with a dying dragon watching.

***Now you know the shape of it,*** Ashveth said. Her voice had grown quieter, the embers along her jaw dimmer. The conversation was costing her; Sefu could see the price ticking up in the fading light. ***The guild calls it trade. Four hundred years of trade. But you cannot trade what was never yours, child. My fire was given, once, freely—to warm your winters, to light your forges, to seal a peace between realms. Given, with the understanding that the giving would be witnessed and honored. Instead your founders wrote receipts for things they had stolen and called the thefts an economy.***

"We can give it back," Nour said fiercely. She was gripping her own elbows. "Everything. I've fenced half the guild's stock without knowing what it was—we can find it, we can return it—"

***Some of it returns easily. Bottled storms go home to the sky. Borrowed memories go home to their heads.*** The eye dimmed further. ***But fire is not a thing, thief-girl. Fire is a promise that keeps burning. What they stole from me cannot be poured back. It has to be re-sworn. Reforged. The covenant was broken at its source, and only the source can mend it—and the source is me, and I am nearly done.***

"How nearly?" Sefu asked. His voice cracked on it.

Ashveth looked at him for a long moment, and in that look he felt eleven years of pier-sitting acknowledged—all those nights he'd come down with songs and complaints and news of the city, talking to her the way you talk to a sleeping parent, hoping some of it lands.

***Soon enough that you should hurry, little keeper. Slow enough that you have time to do this right. Those are not in conflict.*** The eye swung back to Isandro. ***The prophecy your client pawned—it speaks truly, though it leaves out the parts that matter most. Prophecies are written by people afraid of being blamed for specifics. There are four stones. There is a forge that remembers. And there is a price, which I will not say aloud tonight, because you would try to pay it alone, and a covenant paid alone is just another kind of theft—you'd be stealing the ending from the people who love you.***

"I wouldn't," Isandro said.

***You already are,*** Ashveth said, gently, terribly. ***I can smell a hidden ledger on you from here.***

The chamber went very quiet. Isandro's face did something complicated—shame and defiance wrestling in a narrow alley—and lost, mostly, to shame.

Sefu's chest ached. He knew that look. He'd worn it for eleven years: the face of a person carrying something heavy who has convinced themselves that putting it down would be selfish.

***Rest,*** Ashveth said, and the warmth of her breath rolled through the chamber like a tide. ***All of you. The current will carry you up. Swordspeaker—when you wake, you will remember everything I showed you, whether you wish to or not. Debts unpaid do not fade with sleep. They compound.***

Her eye began to close, the bronze lid descending like a harbor gate.

***And keeper—***

"Yes?" Sefu said.

***Thank you for the jokes. They were terrible. They were also the only laughter I have heard in nine years.***

* * *

The warm current bore them up through the dark, and none of them spoke until their heads broke the surface of the flooded customs house and they stood gasping in the shallows among the floating tax records.

Nour wrung out her hair. "So. We're stealing back everything the guild ever stole, from the most dangerous institution in the city, to save a dragon the size of a district, using rocks."

"Oath-stones," Tam corrected automatically, then winced. "Sorry. Habit."

"And the dragon knows Isandro's hiding something," Nour went on, merciless, "and basically told her to knock it off, and Isandro said 'I wouldn't,' which everyone present understood to mean 'I absolutely would.'"

"Nour." Isandro's voice was level. Too level.

"I'm just saying, for the record." Nour shrugged. "We're family now, apparently. Families keep records too. Mine's just kept out loud."

Something crossed Isandro's face—Sefu couldn't name it, but he saw the exact moment she decided not to answer. She turned instead to him, water streaming off her, eyes still red-rimmed from the dream.

"The Emberhold," she said. "The forge that remembers. How far?"

"Nine days north by river," Sefu said. "Faster if we can buy passage."

"With what money?" Tam asked gloomily, patting his soaked bag. "I have four coppers and a receipt for a horse that died in the previous century."

Sefu opened his mouth to say they'd manage, they always managed—

And stopped.

Because far above them, muffled by forty feet of harbor stone and brick, a bell had begun to ring. Not the hour bell. The alarm bell—the one the guild rang when it wanted every enforcer in the city to know that something had been found.

Or taken.

Or named.

Isandro went rigid. "That's the guildhouse pattern," she whispered. "Three-and-one. That's a declaration. That's—" She swallowed. "That's what they ring when they name a thief."

The bell rolled on and on above the water, and in the dark beneath the harbor, Sefu could have sworn he felt—faint, distant, unmistakable—one slow ember-pulse quicken, like a heart answering its own.
