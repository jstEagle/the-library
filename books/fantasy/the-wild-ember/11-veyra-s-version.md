## 11. Veyra's Version

Guildmaster Veyra Oss had three rules she never broke: she drank her tea before it cooled, she read every ledger entry twice, and she never, ever lied to herself.

The first two rules made her a good merchant. The third made her, in her own estimation, a good person—which was why it stung so badly that morning when she caught herself lying to Isandro's empty chair.

She'd rehearsed the conversation the night before, standing at her window with the city spread out below her like an open book: *Isandro, child, sit down. I know about the prophecy book. Tell me what you saw.* She'd rehearsed it in a voice that was firm but kind, the voice of a woman who had raised a stray and meant to keep her. And then this morning, over cold tea—breaking rule one, which should have been her first warning—she'd stood in Isandro's doorway, opened her mouth, and said instead:

"The storm-bottling inventory is off by two vials. See to it."

And Isandro had said, "Yes, Guildmaster," in that flat, careful way of hers, the way she said everything, and walked past Veyra smelling faintly of canal water and old paper, which was wrong, because Isandro hadn't been assigned to the archives in weeks.

Veyra watched her go and thought: *I have known that girl since she was eleven years old picking her teeth with a letter-opener, and she just lied to my face.*

It shouldn't have hurt. Lies were currency; the guild ran on them the way mills ran on river-water. But Isandro's lies were different. Isandro was the one person in the guild who had never learned to lie, and Veyra had built half her quiet hopes on that fact like a mason builds on bedrock.

Now the bedrock was shifting, and Veyra did what she always did with things that frightened her: she sat down, poured fresh tea, and started reading.

* * *

The prophecy book itself sat in her private vault, wrapped in oilcloth, looking exactly as unremarkable as it had looked when her enforcers lifted it from the water-damaged client three days ago. A cheap binding. Water-warped pages. The kind of book that pawnshops used to prop up wobbly tables.

Except for page forty-one. Page forty-one named tonight's date, and beside it, in a hand that predated the guild by a century, it named a name.

*Ishandra Okafor-Vey.*

Veyra had not spoken that name aloud in thirty years. She had given it to a screaming infant pulled from a burning caravan on the Salt Road—the daughter of two swordspeakers who had chosen the road over the guild, fools both, brave fools, dead fools—and then she had buried the name along with the parents, because names are hooks, and the world was full of people fishing for swordspeaker blood.

She'd called the child Isandro instead. A small lie, a protective one. The first lie between them, though neither had known it.

Veyra unwrapped the book now and read page forty-one again, for perhaps the hundredth time, and felt the same thing she'd felt the first time: not fear. Anger.

Because she knew this book. Or rather, she knew its author. Brother Cassian, the last archivist of the old order—the order whose founding covenant Veyra had spent her entire career quietly, methodically dismantling. He'd written his prophecies in the margins of trade manifests like other men wrote grocery lists, and every one of his predictions had come true, which Veyra considered less a miracle than a design flaw.

*Covenant reforged by the last speaker's hand,* the margin read, in Cassian's crabbed script. *Fire returned to its thief. Ledgers burned. The guild ends as it began: in flame.*

Well. Veyra set down the book and looked at her hands—small hands, neat hands, hands that had signed away more wonders than most people would see in ten lifetimes—and thought, with the particular clarity that came from never lying to oneself:

*Not if I burn it first.*

* * *

"You're brooding," said Captain Doss from her doorway.

"I'm thinking. Brooding is for poets and teenagers."

"Hard to tell the difference in this city." Doss came in, helped himself to tea without asking—a liberty he'd taken for twenty years and would take until one of them died—and settled into the chair across from her desk. "The girl?"

"What about the girl."

"She lied to Marrow about where she was Tuesday night. She's been to the underlevels. And she's carrying a book that isn't in any ledger."

Veyra sipped her tea. "You've had her followed."

"I've had everyone followed since the book surfaced. That's the job."

"That's paranoia wearing the job's coat." But there was no heat in it. Doss was right, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third person at the table. "What do you want, Captain?"

Doss turned his cup slowly on its saucer. He was a big man gone soft around the middle, with a face like a dropped loaf of bread, and he was the only person alive who could ask her the next question. "I want to know if we're bringing her in or letting her run."

"She hasn't done anything."

"She's got a prophecy with her name in it and she's sneaking through the underlevels with a stolen grimoire. In my experience that's how 'hasn't done anything' starts."

"And in your experience," Veyra said, "what do we do with people who've done nothing? We've hanged enough of them."

Doss didn't flinch. He never flinched; it was infuriating. "I'm asking because you love her," he said simply. "And love makes your orders stupid. It has before."

Veyra looked at him for a long moment. Outside her window, the rain had started again, silvering the rooftops, washing the city toward evening.

"Yes," she said. "I love her. Say it plainly, then, since we're being honest: yes. I found her in the wreck of her parents' caravan with her mother's sword-pin clutched in her fist, and she bit me, and I decided on the spot that she was mine. Not the guild's. Mine. And every plan I've made for fifteen years has had her at the center of it, and now some dead monk's scribbling says she's going to end everything I've built, and you want to know if I'm going to bring her in."

"Yes."

"No." Veyra set down the cup with a click that sounded, even to her, like a lock turning. "I'm going to talk to her. Tonight. Properly, this time, no inventories. And if she tells me the truth—if she gives me the book and tells me what she knows—then none of the rest matters. Prophecies are just gossip with good posture, Doss. They only come true for people who help them along."

"And if she doesn't tell you the truth?"

Veyra looked out at the rain. Somewhere down in the underlevels, her girl was walking into something with a century-old name in her pocket and a lie in her mouth, and Veyra's chest hurt in a way she refused to examine, because examining it would mean admitting that the ache wasn't betrayal.

It was recognition. Isandro had learned to lie from someone. Veyra just couldn't remember teaching her.

"Then I'll be very disappointed," she said, "and then I'll do what must be done. Same as always."

* * *

That evening she went down to the vaults alone, which she almost never did, because vaults were for clerks and clerks were for trusting. But there was a drawer she wanted to open, and she didn't want witnesses for what lived in it.

Fourth shelf, behind the deed-boxes: a packet of letters tied with faded ribbon. Her own handwriting, young and furious, from thirty years ago. Letters to a man named Aldric Vey—her father, founder of the modern Ledger Guild, architect of the system she now ran—and every letter said some variation of the same thing.

*You cannot build peace on theft. The fire belongs to them. Give it back while there is still something left to give it back to.*

And his replies, in a hand like iron filings: *The covenant was tyranny dressed as mercy. Five swordspeakers holding five realms hostage over a dying lizard's hearth-fire—that is not peace, daughter, that is extortion with better manners. We liberated the flame. Every wonder we sell, every storm we bottle, every memory we pawn—we took power out of the hands of monsters and put it in the marketplace, where it belongs.*

She had believed him. That was the terrible part, the part she never told anyone: she had believed him completely, for years, right up until the night she stood in Ashveth's old mountain pass and heard what was left of the dragonsong, thin as a dying man's breath, and understood that her father had not liberated anything. He had robbed a house where the family was already starving, and then written himself a ballad about it.

But by then the guild was hers to run, and running it badly would help no dragon anywhere, and so she had made her peace the way practical women do: she kept the ledgers immaculate. She never sold a bottled wonder below cost. She gave a tenth of all profits to the Warden's Rest charity, which fed the few remaining dragon-wardens, whom her father had ruined, whom her father's system had orphaned—Sefu's people, though she didn't know yet that the name would matter.

Fair prices for stolen goods. It was, she told herself, the best available version of justice. She had never once lied to herself about it.

(Except, whispered a small voice, about whether "best available" was the same as "good." She told that voice to fetch her tea.)

The letters went back in the drawer. Veyra stood a moment longer in the cold vault air, listening to the building settle around her—the tick of cooling pipes, the far-off shuffle of the night clerks, the whole vast organism of the guild breathing its paper breath—and let herself feel, briefly and honestly, what she actually felt.

She was not a villain. She needed Isandro to understand that, whoever else misunderstood it. She had spent thirty years keeping the machine humane from inside the machine, and if the covenant died, if the old order rose again with its swordspeakers and its hostage-flames and its holy tyranny, then everything her father had broken and everything she had salvaged would drown together, and the drowning would be called justice, and it would still be drowning.

*The girl will see,* Veyra told herself, climbing back up into the lamplit warmth of her office. *When I lay it all out—all of it, the letters, the history, the truth she doesn't have—she'll see. She has her mother's brains and her own stubborn heart, and hearts can be reasoned with if you speak to them properly.*

She checked the clock. Nearly midnight. Isandro would be closing the trade floor about now, balancing the day's impossible things, and Veyra decided she would wait up, and pour two cups, and say everything she had failed to say that morning.

Then the night clerk knocked, pale as milk, with a report that rearranged the furniture of Veyra's certainty.

An intruder in Warehouse Six. Underlevels. Someone had gotten past three locks and two wards and had stood among the fire-vials long enough to leave tears on the floor—actual tears, the clerk reported, bewildered, salt-tracks in the dust—and had then walked out through the front, bold as noon, after stopping at the trade desk to ask one question of the night clerk:

*Which of your people keeps the ledgers? Which one signs the entries for the fire-vials?*

The clerk had answered honestly, because the intruder had asked politely and seemed genuinely sad.

He'd asked for Isandro by description. By the sword-pin she wore.

Veyra stood very still in her office with two cups of tea going cold on the desk, and the third rule held her upright the way it always had: she did not lie to herself.

*A warden. In my city. Asking after my girl.* And beneath the fear, treacherous and warm, another thought entirely—one she would deny under torture: *Good. Perhaps someone outside this madhouse will finally tell her the truth, since apparently I no longer can.*

By dawn, the reports had worsened. Isandro hadn't come home. Her rooms were untouched, her sword-pin gone from its hook. And down at the canal district, word moved through the flophouses like grease through a drain: a guild clerk gone rogue, a pickpocket seen buying river passage, and somewhere in the wet dark, a girl with a famous face and a stolen book, running.

Veyra poured the cold tea out the window. It fell into the rain and vanished.

"Doss," she said, when the captain arrived at her summons. His face already knew. "Find her before the wardens do. And Captain—" she picked up the prophecy book, wrapped it in oilcloth, and held it against her ribs like a wound, "—if anyone asks, she stole it. Let them hunt a thief. Thieves get brought back alive."

"And if she won't come back alive?"

Veyra looked at the rain, and the rain looked back, gray and endless, full of everything she'd spent thirty years refusing to say out loud.

"Then bring me whoever's driving her," she said. "I'll negotiate with the devil himself before I let a prophecy finish a sentence about my daughter."
