## 12. The Price on the Door

The ferryman's price was written on a slate hung from his door, and Nour read it three times before she believed it.

*Passage to Greyvault Landing: forty crowns per soul. No haggling. No exceptions. Yes, even for you.*

That last line had been added recently, judging by the freshness of the chalk, and it felt personally insulting in a way Nour decided to take as a compliment.

"Forty crowns," she said, as if repeating it might change its value. "Each."

"That's what the door says." The ferryman—broad as a barrel, beard braided with fishhooks, sitting on his stoop shelling peas like a man who had never once been hurried by anything, including drowning—did not look up.

"There are five of us."

"Then that'll be two hundred crowns, and my condolences."

Nour turned around. Behind her, in the narrow alley that smelled of canal water and frying onions, her family-in-progress was conducting an inventory that would have made a guild clerk weep. Tam sat on an overturned crate, feverish but stubbornly upright, counting coins out of his boot with the careful precision of a man who distrusted his own fingers. Sefu stood very still, which Nour had learned was how he prayed, or grieved, or both. Isandro was doing arithmetic on her palm with a stub of charcoal, and getting an answer she clearly hated.

"We have sixty-one crowns," Isandro announced, "and a button I believe is magical but might just be a button."

"It hums," Nour offered.

"So does the harbor at night. That doesn't make it currency."

Nour crouched down to the ferryman's eye level, deploying the smile that had opened more locked drawers than any crowbar. "Old man. Friend. Treasure of the waterfront. Surely there's room for negotiation."

"No."

"You haven't even heard my offer."

"I've heard every offer ever made on this stoop, girl. I've been offered my own boat back, twice. I've been offered a haunted rooster. I've been offered—" he finally looked up, and his eyes were pale and flat as a dead fish's, but not unkind, "—a memory. That one I almost took."

Nour kept the smile on her face through sheer professional discipline. "A memory," she said lightly. "Who'd want those?"

"Memory-broker up Copperlane way. Pays better than the crown mint, because what he sells can't be counterfeited. Buys them too." He went back to his peas. "You've got the look of someone carrying inventory."

"The look?"

"Full pockets behind the eyes." He shrugged. "Runs in certain trades."

Nour laughed, bright and easy, the laugh she used when someone got too close to something true. It worked on everyone except, apparently, men who shelled peas like they were defusing them.

Behind her, Isandro said quietly, "There's another broker. Cheaper. Rougher part of town."

"How do you know that?" Tam asked.

"Because I used to work for one," Isandro said, "back when I was pretending to be an ordinary person with an ordinary trade. Bottled storms pay poorly until you learn where the real money is." She rubbed the bridge of her nose. "I hate that I still know this."

* * *

The memory-house in Saltrow didn't look like much, which was the fashion among places that dealt in things worth stealing. A crooked shopfront wedged between a dye-works and a shuttered chandlery, its window displaying nothing but a single glass jar of fog that occasionally formed, briefly, the shape of a hand pressing outward.

The broker inside was small, silver-haired, and precise as a scalpel. His name, according to the little brass plate, was Master Ellery Quill, which Nour suspected was fake in the way that only genuinely wealthy people bothered with.

"Passage money," Isandro explained, while Tam hovered by the door looking like a man who wanted to object on principle but couldn't remember what principle applied. "We need two hundred crowns by tomorrow's tide."

"Mm." Quill steepled his fingers. "And what are you offering? Coin is scarce among fugitives. I assume you've brought goods."

Sefu shifted, and Nour felt him tense beside her—the big warden had been uneasy since they'd walked in, his eyes tracking the jars along the walls the way other men tracked exits. Each shelf held dozens of small vessels: vials, locket-glass, wax-sealed orbs. Some held weather. Some held light. Some, she realized slowly, held nothing visible at all, and those were the ones that made Sefu's jaw go tight.

"Memories," Quill prompted, gentle as a knife going in. "It's what most people bring. Everyone carries something they'd be better off without. A grief. A shame. A face they're tired of seeing." He smiled. "I'm a philanthropist, really. I take away burdens."

"And keep them," Sefu said. His voice was low, gravel over deep water. "In jars. On shelves. Where they wait."

"Where they're *safe*, warden. Nothing decays in good glass." Quill's gaze flicked to Sefu with new interest. "You know, you have the look of—"

"We're not selling his memories," Nour cut in smoothly, stepping half in front of Sefu. Old habit, standing between a mark and their senses. Except Sefu wasn't a mark, and neither was she, anymore, supposedly. "We're selling mine."

"Nour." Isandro's voice, quiet. Warning.

"We need two hundred crowns. I've got the deepest pockets." Nour spread her hands, all showmanship. "Ask anyone. Best pickpocket in six districts. My head is full of other people's valuables. Half the memories in here probably came through my fingers first."

Quill leaned back, appraising her with the frank greed of a connoisseur. "Stolen memories. Second-hand. They fetch less—they fray at the edges, lose provenance. Unless..." He tilted his head. "Unless you have something *original*. Something that's never changed hands. First-owner stock commands a premium."

The shop went very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the fog in the window jar pressed against the glass again, patient as a held breath.

Nour thought about her inventory. She had a hundred small treasures: the taste of a festival wine she'd lifted off a merchant's tongue while he slept; the sound of a specific bell in a city she'd robbed at fifteen; the exact weight of the first purse she'd ever cut, and the dizzy pride of it. Good stock. Sellable stock.

And underneath all of it, at the very bottom, wrapped in cloth so old she'd forgotten it was wrapping—

She had the face.

* * *

Here was the thing about Nour, and she'd told this story a hundred times, in a hundred taverns, always laughing: she didn't know who her mother was. Found on a church step, raised by the streets, blah blah, pass the ale. It was a good story. It had a rhythm to it. People liked the version where she shrugged.

What she did not tell people was that somewhere around her fourth winter, before the church step, before everything, there had been a kitchen. A low ceiling. A smell of cardamom burning slightly because someone was singing instead of watching the pot. Hands lifting her up onto a counter. And a face above hers, laughing, saying her name—

Not *Nour*. Something else. A smaller name, a home-name, the kind mothers use when nobody else is listening.

She had carried that face since before she could carry anything else. She'd never sold it. Never traded it. In the lean years—the years when selling it would have bought a month of food, a winter of coal, once an actual bed with an actual door—she'd kept it. Because it was hers. Because it was the only thing she owned that nobody could say she'd stolen.

Because it was proof that somebody, somewhere, once looked at her like that.

"First-owner stock," Quill repeated, watching her face do something she couldn't control. "You do have something. Don't you."

"I have a question first." Nour's voice came out steadier than she deserved. "When you sell it on—to whoever buys it. What do they get? Exactly."

"The memory entire. Every sense. Sight, sound, scent, the emotional coloring laid down at encoding." Quill ticked it off like a grocer listing figs. "They'll live it as theirs. Most buyers of that grade are collectors. Wealthy, sentimental types who were never loved enough as children and can afford to rent the feeling." He smiled again, that scalpel smile. "Your mother's face will be treasured, I assure you. Possibly by several someones, over the years. Memories keep, and they sell."

*Several someones.* Nour turned that over. Somewhere down the line, some rich stranger would close their eyes and see a smoky kitchen and hear a woman sing off-key and feel—feel what Nour felt, which was—

Which was the whole point. Which was being four years old and utterly, catastrophically loved.

"They won't know it isn't theirs," she said.

"That's rather the nature of the product."

"Yeah." Nour blew out a breath. "Okay. How much for the best thing I've got."

Isandro caught her arm. "Nour. Wait. We'll find another way. Barges walk cargo upriver, there's a lock system past the tanneries, Tam knows the toll schedules—"

"Tam took a crossbow bolt two weeks ago and still goes gray climbing stairs," Nour said. "Ashveth has maybe a season left—I've seen the way Sefu counts days when he thinks no one's watching. And you—" she pointed at Isandro, "—you're the last swordspeaker alive and the prophecy book has your name in it, which means the world is on a schedule whether we like it or not." She gentled. "It's a face, Isandro. It's a nice face. But it doesn't open vaults, and it doesn't save dragons, and it's been sitting in my pocket behind my eyes for twenty years waiting for exactly one useful thing to do."

"That's not what it's *for*—"

"It wasn't for anything. That's what I'm telling you. It was just mine." She squeezed Isandro's hand, once, and let go. "Now it gets to matter."

Quill had produced, silently, a ledger of his own—a slim black book, beautifully bound—and Nour felt a flash of pure spiteful joy at the thought of Isandro's expression. Even the memory-brokers kept ledgers. The whole cursed world ran on them.

"Name the figure," Nour said.

"For first-owner stock of that evident quality?" Quill's pen hovered. "Two hundred and fifty crowns. Plus ten for the appraisal, waived, because I like you."

"Three hundred, and you throw in whatever's in the fog jar."

"The fog is decorative."

"The fog is trying to escape. I saw it. Three hundred."

Quill regarded her for a long moment, and then—she'd swear this on a stack of stolen bibles—he *grinned*, quick and genuine, a boy's grin under the silver hair. "Done. You haggle like a Saltrow native."

"I am a Saltrow native."

"You said six districts."

"I contain multitudes."

* * *

The extraction chair was upholstered leather gone soft with use, and it reclined like a barber's, which somehow made it worse. Quill worked with clean, quick hands: a circlet of thin silver set with a single drop of glass at the brow, a vial that steamed faintly though the room was cold.

"It doesn't hurt," he said, in the tone of a man who said this forty times a day and had learned to mean it as comfort. "Most clients describe it as a door closing gently. You'll keep the knowledge that the memory existed—you'll know you had a mother's face, that it was precious—but the specifics go into the vial. The kitchen. The song. Her." He paused. "The name she called you, if there was one."

"Right." Nour lay back and stared at the ceiling, where someone had stuck glow-moths in a pattern meant to be soothing and mostly succeeded.

"Nour." Isandro stood over her, arms crossed, wearing the particular scowl of a woman losing an argument she'd already lost. "Last chance."

"You know what's funny?" Nour said. "I spent my whole life being the one who takes things. Quick fingers, quicker exit. And everyone always assumed I'd feel guilty about it someday, and I never did, because everything I ever took belonged to people who had too much." She reached up and found Isandro's hand. "This is the first time I've ever given anything back on purpose. Feels different. Worse, obviously. But different."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

Tam limped forward from the doorway, fever-flushed, and pressed something into her free hand: a copper coin, worn smooth. "Ferryman gave it back to me when I overpaid him by mistake yesterday. Honest old codger." His voice cracked in the middle. "For luck. Come out the other side of this still knowing us, eh?"

"I'll know you," Nour said. "I'll just be meeting you fresh. Try to make a better first impression this time. Maybe lead with the poetry instead of the tax fraud."

"That was *one* poem—"

"Silver circlet," Quill said softly. "On three. One. Two—"

"Nour." Sefu's voice, from the back of the room, deep enough to feel in the floorboards. She looked over. The big warden had taken off his hat, which she'd learned was what he did for the dying and the brave. "Whatever she gave you—whatever that woman put into you—it doesn't leave the world. It goes into the glass, and glass keeps, and I have sworn an oath on the last dragon's flame that every stolen thing in this city will find its way home." His eyes were wet and steady. "Including this. I will carry your mother's face to you myself, someday, if I have to buy it back jar by jar. That is my promise, witnessed aloud. You remember promises. Those you can keep."

"Three," said Quill, gently, and touched the glass drop to her brow.

He was right. It didn't hurt.

It was a door closing. She felt it swing—felt the kitchen go, the smoke-smell, the burned cardamom, the off-key singing rising and fading like a tide going out. Felt the face tilt above hers, laughing, mouth shaping a small warm name, and Nour grabbed for it, greedy thief to the last, snatched at it with everything she had—

And kept one thing. Just one. Not the face, not the voice, not the name.

Just the feeling. The exact shape of being lifted up and utterly loved. It slipped through the closing door like light under it, too small and too fast to catch, and settled somewhere deep where the glass drop couldn't reach—or maybe Quill simply missed it, or maybe some things refuse to be inventory.

The door shut.

Nour lay blinking at the glow-moths, breathing hard, holding Tam's coin in one hand and Isandro's in the other. She knew, distantly and cleanly, that she had been a foundling, that she had once possessed something priceless, that she had sold it well and for a good cause. The shape where the memory had lived was smooth and empty, like a riverbed after drought.

But at the bottom of her chest, something glowed. Small. Warm. Unaccounted for, on no ledger anywhere.

"Well?" Isandro's face swam over her, scared and fierce. "How do you feel?"

Nour considered the question honestly, which was novel.

"Like I paid for something," she said at last, and sat up, and laughed, because if she didn't she'd do the other thing. "Two hundred and fifty crowns' worth. Someone count it. And someone check that old pea-shelling extortionist's boat, because if we're paying forty crowns a head, I want to see the hull before I set foot on it."

Quill counted out the crowns in neat towers, then paused, vial of pearly light in hand, and studied it with a craftsman's frown.

"Strange," he murmured, mostly to himself.

"What?" Isandro asked sharply.

"The volume's short." He shook the vial; the light within moved slow and heavy, like honey. "By a good measure. As if something refused to pour out." He glanced up at Nour with narrowed, interested eyes. "Memories don't do that, girl. Whatever you kept hold of in there... you kept hold of it *well*."

Nour slid off the chair, pocketed her towers of crowns, and grinned at him with all her old brilliance.

"Professional habit," she said. "Never give a mark everything you've got."

Outside, the tide was turning, and a boat waited at a door with a price on it—one they could finally afford—and beyond the harbor, grey and far, the mountains stood over the vaults where the second oath-stone slept in a guildmaster's private dark.

Isandro looked at them a long moment, then said quietly, "Tomorrow we go get it. And Nour—" her voice caught, and steadied, "—thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Nour said, jingling the crowns. "Wait till you see me haggle with a mountain."
