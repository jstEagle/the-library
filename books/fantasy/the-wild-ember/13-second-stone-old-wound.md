## 13. Second Stone, Old Wound

The house on Lantern Row had not changed.

Isandro stood across the street in the shadow of a chandler's awning, rain dripping off her hood, and hated how well she still knew it. The blue door with the brass knocker shaped like a closed fist—Veyra had chosen it herself, years ago, saying a guildmaster's door should remind visitors who was in charge. The window boxes full of rosemary because Veyra claimed she cooked with it, though Isandro had lived there four years and never once smelled lamb on the air. The third-floor light burning behind amber glass, because Veyra worked late the way other people breathed.

She had climbed through that window forty times as a girl, sneaking back from midnight trades. She had never once imagined climbing through it like *this*—as a thief in her own mother's—*no*, she corrected herself firmly, *not mother, mentor, mentor, keep the word where you can carry it*—her old mentor's house.

"You're stalling," Tam murmured beside her. He was pressed into the doorway of the chandler's shop, disguised in a fishmonger's apron that Nour had stolen for him and that he wore with the dignity of a man wearing his own funeral shroud. "Which is understandable. I stall constantly. I once stalled three days before telling my landlady I'd broken her washbasin. But we are, technically, on a schedule."

"The enforcers change watch at the fourth bell," Isandro said. "We have time."

"We have time," Tam agreed. "We also have Nour on the roof, Sefu pretending to be a beggar two streets over—and he is *terrible* at it, by the way, he keeps giving people actual money—and a plan with approximately one step."

"It's a good step."

"Going in through the window."

"A classic step."

Tam sighed and adjusted his apron. "When this goes wrong, I want it noted that I said something."

"Noted," Isandro said, and crossed the street.

* * *

The garden gate was locked. It had always been locked; Veyra believed locks were less about keeping people out than about making them announce themselves. Isandro picked it in six seconds with a hairpin she'd stolen from Nour's collection, and felt a small, disloyal thrill at how easy her old life's tricks came back to her hands.

The kitchen was exactly as she remembered. Copper pots hanging in order of size. The kettle on its hook. A loaf under cloth, going stale, because Veyra bought bread out of habit and forgot to eat it out of distraction. Isandro had spent her first year at Lantern Row secretly toasting those forgotten loaves so they wouldn't go to waste, and Veyra had pretended not to notice, and that pretense had been, in its way, the first kindness anyone had ever shown her that asked nothing back.

Her chest did something complicated. She put it away in the same place she kept everything tonight and moved toward the stairs.

The vault was behind the study, behind the false bookcase, behind a door whose lock Veyra had rebuilt twice since Isandro had last picked it—which meant Veyra had been thinking about Isandro picking it. Which meant, some part of Isandro noted with cold clarity, that Veyra had known, on some level, that a day might come when Isandro would come home as an intruder.

The lock took eleven minutes. Her hands shook only during the last three.

Inside, the vault was smaller than she remembered, or perhaps she had simply grown. Shelves of ledger-books, their spines stamped in gold: *Impossible Things, Acquired*. Bottles in padded cases—a storm here, a summer afternoon there, a lullaby someone had sold in desperation and regretted ever after. And on a velvet stand in the center, alone, lit by a single enchanted lamp that never burned out because it had been purchased rather than made:

A stone. Gray-green, fist-sized, humming so faintly Isandro felt it in her teeth before she heard it with her ears.

It hummed *at* her. The first stone, tucked against her ribs in a sling under her coat, hummed back, and the two notes twined together like voices finding harmony.

"Hello," Isandro whispered foolishly, and lifted it from its stand.

The moment her fingers closed around it, the lamp guttered. Every bottled wonder on the shelves shivered in its case—the storm-cloud pressing itself against the glass like a dog hearing footsteps, the summer-afternoon bottle glowing briefly gold. They knew her. Or they knew what she carried. Either way, the whole vault woke up around her like a room full of sleeping animals scenting blood.

And then, from the shelf above the ledgers, a bundle of letters slid loose—one too many stones removed from a careful architecture, the whole arrangement sighing into collapse. Letters spilled across the floor. Dozens of them. Tied in bundles with the guild's green ribbon, addressed in a hand Isandro knew better than her own.

Veyra's handwriting. To no recipient she could see—these were drafts, or copies, or letters never sent.

Isandro should have taken the stone and gone. That was the plan. One step, a good step.

She untied the nearest bundle instead.

* * *

The first letter was dated nineteen years ago. Isandro did the arithmetic without wanting to. Nineteen years ago, Veyra had already been guildmaster for a decade. Nineteen years ago, Isandro had been six years old and unclaimed, living off what she could lift in the Saltmarket.

*To whoever finds these,* the letter began, *or to no one, which is more likely.*

*I have read the Aldermoor copy of the prophecy entire. I had hoped the rumors were scribe-fancy, but no. It names the date. It names the child. Okafor. I have sent men to the Saltmarket to find a girl of that name and they report there are dozens, none marked, none speaking. Perhaps the prophecy lies. Prophecies do that. But if it does not lie, then somewhere in this city is the last swordspeaker, and every day she goes unfound is a day the guild breathes easy.*

*I find I do not know which outcome I am praying for.*

Isandro sat down on the floor of the vault, the stone warm against her ribs, the letter trembling in her hands.

Nineteen years ago. Before the orphanage. Before the apprenticeship. Before Veyra had found her—no.

Before Veyra had *found* her. Had Veyra? She remembered that day with a clarity that had always felt like grace: the guildhouse steps, the rain, the woman in gray kneeling down to her eye level, saying *you have honest hands, girl, would you like to learn what they're worth?* She had thought, all her life, that she'd been rescued by chance.

She read the second letter.

*I have narrowed it to three girls. I will take all three into the guild under different pretexts and watch. This is not cruelty. Whatever the prophecy wants of this child, the guild can give her a better life than the Saltmarket will. If she is never needed, she will be a clerk, a trader, a person. If she is needed—*

If she is needed. Isandro laughed, a small ugly sound, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

*I have chosen Ishikawa. The quiet one. She balances ledgers like she's praying over them, and she keeps herself small, which is either wisdom or damage, possibly both. I have moved her into the house on Lantern Row. She thinks it is a reward. In a sense it is. In another sense it is the most secure room in the city.*

*I caught myself today teaching her sword-forms she was never meant to learn. The old forms. The speaking forms. I told myself it was to keep the knowledge alive, but that is a lie, and I have never lied to these pages. The truth is that if the prophecy comes for her, I would rather she be armed. Whatever else I am, whatever the founders were, I will not send an unarmed child to meet a prophecy.*

*Unless I decide the prophecy must be stopped. There are days I think that too.*

The bundles went on. Years of them. Isandro read faster now, hungry and sick, watching Veyra's certainty war with her love across two decades of unsent pages:

*The girl suspects nothing. Tonight she brought me tea without being asked and I nearly burned these letters out of simple shame.*

*The date approaches. The prophecy named it. Three days hence. I have doubled the enforcers and told no one why. If the book surfaces—if she reads it—she will run. I know her. She runs toward rules, but a rule that names her true name will feel like a cage, and she has spent her whole life learning to slip cages.*

*I could tell her. I have drafted the conversation forty times. Every version ends with her looking at me the way she looks at falsified ledgers.*

*There is a version of me that loves her enough to let her choose. I am trying to become her. I am not there yet.*

The last letter was recent. Weeks old. The ink barely faded.

*She has the first stone. Of course she does; the pawnbroker's stone was always going to sing for her. Tam has gone over—I grieve him more than I expected, which tells me something about myself I don't care to examine. Sefu is in the city; our watchers lost him in the underlevels, which means the wardens' training is as good as reported.*

*I could stop her. I could stop her tonight, with a word, with thirty men.*

*Instead I have left the vault lock hard but not impossible, and the stone where she will look, and I am sitting upstairs listening to the rain and waiting to see whether the daughter I chose will come home as a thief.*

*Whatever she takes, I hope she knows I left the light on for her.*

Isandro looked up. Through the vault door, past the false bookcase, through the study's open transom, she could see it: the third-floor window with its amber light, burning.

Burning *now*. At an hour when Veyra was usually asleep.

The floor creaked above her. Once. Deliberately. The old board outside the study, the one Veyra stepped on for years so that Isandro would always know when she was coming and never be startled—an honesty of footsteps, she used to call it.

Isandro stood very still, the stone in her arms, the letters scattered around her like fallen leaves, her heart hammering out a message in a code only the two of them had ever shared: *I'm here. I know you know. Neither of us is ready to say it.*

Above her, the footsteps retreated. A chair scraped. Silence, except for the rain.

She gathered the letters—all of them, every bundle, because she could not leave them and she could not explain why even to herself—and tucked them inside her coat next to the first stone. She took the second oath-stone last, and it settled against the other one with a sound like a held breath finally released.

At the kitchen door she paused. The stale loaf sat under its cloth. On impulse, hopeless and stupid and aching, she cut a slice, toasted it over the banked coals the way she had at fourteen, ate it standing up, and left the rest on the table where Veyra would find it in the morning.

A message in the only code that mattered anymore: *I was here. I'm not gone. I don't know what I am yet, but I'm not gone.*

* * *

Nour dropped from the wall beside her two streets away, moving like water finding its level. "Well?"

"I have it." Isandro patted her coat. "Stone and... other things."

"And you're crying."

"I'm not—" She touched her cheek. Rain. Definitely rain. "It's raining."

"Mm," said Nour, who had grown up on the streets and knew every species of rain there was, including the kind that came from inside a person. She didn't push. That was the thing about Nour; she could steal anything except your privacy. "Sefu says move. He also gave away four coppers while pretending to be a beggar, so honestly, we're lucky the whole guild isn't here."

They reached the corner where Tam waited by the canal stairs, wringing out his apron, and Isandro handed him the letters without a word. He read the top one by the light of a shuttered lantern. His face went through several expressions in quick succession, ending somewhere near grief.

"She knew," Tam said quietly. "About the prophecy. About *you*. All along."

"She chose me out of a lineup of orphans and then taught me sword-speech in secret and then wrote letters about it for twenty years and never sent any of them." Isandro heard her own voice go flat and strange. "I don't know what to do with that, Tam. I've been angry at her for days, and anger was *working* for me, and now—"

"Now she's a person," Tam said. "That's the worst thing a person can be. When Veyra dismissed me, I wanted her to be a monster. Monsters are easier to leave."

Down the canal, a barge lantern swung. Sefu materialized out of the dark with the particular urgency of a man carrying news he hates. "Enforcers," he said. "Two squads, moving toward the Salt Gate. Toward *us*. Someone tipped them—not Veyra's signature, someone lower. Captain Dario's seal."

"Dario," Nour repeated. "The big sad one with the daughter?"

"He's not sad," Isandro started, and then stopped, because actually, yes, he was, she had just never had a category for it before this week.

"They'll close the gate before dawn," Sefu said. "If we're not through it tonight, we're not through it."

Isandro looked back once, up the hill, at the amber window burning on Lantern Row. *I left the light on for her.* She turned away before it could mean anything she wasn't strong enough to resist.

"To the gate," she said. "All of us. Quickly."

Behind them, far above, the light stayed burning until they were out of sight—as if someone upstairs was determined to keep watch over a daughter who had just robbed her, and forgiven her, and was already, in twenty different unsent sentences, trying to figure out how to say so.
