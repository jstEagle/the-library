## 14. Ambush at the Salt Gate

Tam had counted doors for eleven years as a guild clerk. He knew the arithmetic of buildings the way other men knew psalms—how many steps to a vault, how many locks between a clerk and a lie, how many paces from the record hall to the door where Veyra took her morning tea and he had once, stupidly, hopefully, delivered it himself.

So when the Salt Gate came into view at the end of Ropewalk Lane, his first thought was not *freedom*. His first thought was a number.

*Four.*

Four enforcers loitering by the customs shed, wearing the gray-and-ember livery of the Ledger Guild, pretending with the terrible commitment of professionals to be bored. One was reading a shipping manifest upside down. That was the detail that made Tam's stomach drop through the cobbles and into the harbor silt below. Nobody read a manifest upside down unless they wanted to look like furniture.

"Tam." Isandro's voice was quiet beside him. She'd stopped walking so smoothly that anyone watching would think they were three travelers pausing to admire the gate's old carvings—which depicted, ironically, the five swordspeakers of the founding covenant, their faces chiseled off by some pious mason two generations back. "How many?"

"You saw them too."

"I asked how many."

"Four visible." He wet his lips. "Which means at least six. Guild doctrine says never post fewer than one-and-a-half times what you want seen. Veyra wrote the amendment herself. She said half-measures were how cities burned."

Isandro made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter had been wrung through a debt-collector's press first. "Of course she did."

Behind them, Nour was juggling a stolen apple, because Nour dealt with tension the way rivers dealt with boulders: by flowing around it loudly until it gave up. "We could just walk through," she said. "I've walked past worse. There was a bailiff in Corvane who owed me money and still shook my hand every market day."

"These aren't bailiffs," Tam said. "These are people I trained. Well—people I filed paperwork for. Half of them learned crossbow maintenance from my inventory lists."

"That's very romantic," Nour said. "The man who armed them, leading us into their arms."

Sefu stood a little apart, hood up, and Tam noticed—as he had been noticing for three days now—that the warden always positioned himself downwind. Old habit, Isandro had explained. Dragons tracked by scent; wardens learned to leave no trail of themselves. It made Sefu hard to find even in a small room, a man perpetually halfway out the window of any conversation.

"There's another way," Sefu said. "Through the tannery district, down to the barge quays. Slower. Uglier. Alive."

"The barges don't run till the tide turns," Tam said. "Two hours."

"Then we have two hours to become very boring people buying very boring things near a tannery," Isandro said. "Everyone agree? Good. Nour, give me the apple. You can't haggle while juggling."

"I absolutely can—"

"*Nour.*"

She handed it over, but she gave Tam a look on the way, the kind of sideways glance that had been happening more often lately, warm and quick as a struck match, gone before he could decide whether he'd imagined it.

He hadn't imagined the first one. He was nearly sure about the second. The third had happened over breakfast and he'd knocked his tea into his own lap thinking about it, which Isandro had cleaned up without comment and which Sefu had watched with the expression of a man observing weather patterns he found personally inconvenient.

They turned toward the tanneries, and that was when the bell began.

* * *

It wasn't a church bell. Church bells argued with each other across the city, each parish convinced its hour was the true one. This was the guildhouse bell—a flat, ugly bronze voice that Tam had heard exactly twice in eleven years. Once for the death of a guildmaster. Once for the discovery of an internal theft exceeding ten thousand crowns.

Now it rang a third pattern, one they'd only ever drilled on paper: the long-short-long that meant *asset flight in progress*, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of boots. Many boots. Moving fast.

"Tannery district is cut off," Isandro said, already turning, already calculating. Tam could see her doing it—the swordspeaker's mind working like a ledger balancing itself, entries sliding into columns. "They'll have the quay road. They'll have the bridge."

"So we swim," Nour said.

"The harbor is full of guild divers looking for a dragon."

"So we *swim badly*—"

"Nour." Tam heard his own voice come out strange, clipped, the voice he'd used in the record hall when correcting a misfiled deed. Clerk's voice. Authority borrowed from a life that no longer existed. "The Salt Gate has a postern. South side, behind the salt merchants' row. It's keyed to a clerk's ring. I still have mine."

Three faces turned to him.

"You kept your ring?" Isandro said slowly. "You fled the guild, testified against its founders' memory, joined fugitives—and you kept the *ring*?"

"It was sentimental!" Which was true, and also completely false, and Tam felt both truths collide somewhere behind his sternum. He'd told himself it was sentiment. A brass band worn smooth by eleven years of turning locks, engraved with his start date and the little motto all clerks got: *EVERY ENTRY ANSWERED.* He'd told himself a lot of things, lately, about why he'd really taken it. About the part of him that had known, even as he ran, that someday someone would need a door opened and he would rather die than say *I couldn't.*

"Sentimental," Sefu repeated, in the tone of a man filing something away forever.

"It's four hundred yards of open ground to the gate," Tam went on, talking faster now, because if he stopped talking the fear would catch up. "Open, flat, cobbled. If they're watching it—and they will be watching it—we cross in the open or not at all. So. Nour and I go first, casual, a couple arguing about a fare. Isandro and Sefu follow thirty seconds behind. We open the postern, you two come through, we drop into the culvert on the far side, and it lets out under the granary stairs where the river barges moor at low water. Tide turns in ninety minutes. We'll be early, wet, and free."

"And if the ring doesn't work?" Isandro asked gently. "If they've revoked clerk access since you left?"

"Then I'll knock," Tam said, "very politely, and apologize for the interruption."

Nobody laughed. Then Nour laughed—sharp, bright, defiant, laughing *at* the fear the way she picked pockets, quick and inside its guard—and after a breath Isandro smiled, and even Sefu's mouth did something that in a less austere face might have qualified.

"All right," Isandro said. "But new plan for the crossing. Nour stays with me and Sefu. Tam goes first alone."

"What?" Two voices at once—his and hers.

"They know your walk, your face, your file. You're the least recognizable of us—clerks are furniture, remember?" She held up a hand before he could object. "And if the ring fails, I want the two fastest of us close enough to do something about it. That's the whole reason. Nothing else."

But she looked at Nour when she said *nothing else*, and Nour looked at the ground, and Tam stood there holding his brass ring and feeling like a ledger entry someone was trying to quietly strike out.

"No," Nour said.

"Nour—"

"He goes alone, I go with him. That's the trade. Take it or I'll follow anyway and be louder about it."

For a moment Isandro just looked at her—that long assessing look Tam had seen her give impossible trades, bottled storms, the true value of a thing versus its price. Then she exhaled. "Fine. Fine! Thirty seconds. Go."

* * *

The open ground was worse than four hundred yards. Open ground always is.

Tam walked it the way he'd once walked the vault corridors: measured, unremarkable, a man with nowhere to be and no particular talent for being anywhere. Nour hung on his arm complaining about his pace in a carrying, aggrieved tone that was, he realized with a start, entirely performance—she was scanning reflections in the salt merchants' windows, cataloguing every shadow along the wall, her fingers doing idle-seeming tricks with a coin that were actually counting heads.

*Seven,* she murmured, barely a breath. *No, eight. Two on the wall walk.*

"Eight," Tam breathed back.

"Mm, and ANOTHER thing," Nour announced loudly, jabbing his ribs, "you promised my mother we'd be married by spring, you absolute turnip—"

"We agreed on *summer*—"

The postern door was where memory said it would be, squat and salt-stained behind a stack of empty brine barrels. Tam's hands were steady until they weren't. The ring slid in. Turned. Met resistance—his heart performed a full somersault—and then, with a grinding complaint of rust, gave.

"Eleven years," he whispered, "and they never changed the lock. I *audited* the locksmith's contract. They renewed it. Unbelievable."

"Complain later. In." He went; she followed; he hauled the door shut behind them and dropped the interior bar just as the first shout went up outside—not alarm yet, just a guard's bored challenge becoming, syllable by syllable, something else.

The culvert was everything unpleasant its name promised. Ankle-deep water, ceiling low enough that Tam traveled bent double, darkness relieved only by the gray coin of light at the far end and Nour's hand finding his in the black with unerring, unnerving accuracy.

"You've done this before," he said.

"Once. Different city. Escaping a man whose wife I may have accidentally—" She paused. "It's a long story involving a parrot."

"I look forward to the full accounting."

"You won't get it. Some stories improve with scarcity." Her grip tightened. "Tam."

"Yes?"

"If this goes wrong at the other end—if they're waiting at the granary—you run. Not walk, not negotiate, not explain. Run. You're the only one who knows the archive codes for the oath-stone locations. Isandro needs your head more than your—"

"My what?"

"Don't fish. It's undignified."

Light ahead. The culvert grate, and beyond it the granary stairs, and beyond *that*—he pressed his eye to the iron—river, mudflats, three barges riding the turning tide, and no gray-and-ember livery anywhere in sight.

"Clear," he said, and had the grate half lifted when the world went wrong.

* * *

Later, Tam would reconstruct it in fragments, the way he reconstructed any disaster for a report: cause, sequence, cost.

Cause: the culvert exit was clear, but the granary loft above it was not. Sequence: a crossbow's ratcheting click above them, a voice shouting *Guild! Down!*—and Nour, stepping out of the dark hole into open light, framed against the sky like a portrait in an expensive frame.

Cost:

He didn't remember deciding. That was the part he kept returning to afterward, feverish, turning it over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. There was no decision. There was simply the fact of Nour in the open, and the fact of the bolt, and then there was the fact of his own body arriving between those facts faster than any thought could travel.

The impact took him in the left shoulder, high, near the collarbone. Sound arrived late—a crack, Nour screaming his name, the clatter of the crossbow being reloaded and then, blessedly, the sound of Sefu moving through the world the way wardens moved, which was to say the guards probably experienced it as several unrelated accidents occurring in rapid succession. Isandro's swordspeech rolled across the yard like weather—Tam heard the crates answering her, the chains, the winch-hook swinging wide and singing, the guild's own tools defecting to her side—and then hands were on him, many hands, and the sky was replaced by the roof of a barge cabin and Nour's face above his, furious and terrified and crying, which was the worst part, because he had never seen Nour cry and had privately believed she was structurally incapable of it.

"You *idiot*," she was saying. "You absolute, self-important, *furniture*—"

"You called me a turnip earlier," Tam managed. "I preferred that."

"Shut up. Shut up, I'm looking at the wound, shut *up*—"

"It missed anything important. I'm mostly padding."

Her hands were pressing cloth against his shoulder, competent and shaking at once. "Bolt's through the meat, not the bone. I've pulled worse out of worse." A breath. Quieter: "Don't you ever—I saw it, Tam. I saw it coming and I was too slow and you weren't slow at all. You didn't even *think*—"

"No," he agreed. The pain was arriving now in earnest, deep and rhythmic, a tide of its own. "Turns out I'm bad at thinking. Eleven years of ledgers and it never took."

Something cracked in her face then, all the performance stripped away, and Nour—who stole memories for bread and gave away names like they cost nothing—pressed her forehead to his unhurt shoulder and said, in a small voice with no audience in mind:

"I keep your face. Did you know that? Of everything I've ever lifted. First thing every morning. Last thing every night."

Tam lay very still, the barge rocking, the wound burning, and understood that he had just been given something more valuable than anything ever entered in any ledger he had kept.

"Every entry answered," he whispered.

"What?"

"Nothing. Old motto. It finally means something." His eyes were heavy. The fever was already gathering at the edges of the pain, patient as a creditor. "Nour. If I sleep—"

"You'll wake up. Because if you don't, I will find whatever afterlife is running this operation and rob it blind until they give you back. Now sleep. I'll be here."

He believed her. That was the last coherent thing he thought for a long time.

* * *

Fever made arithmetic of everything.

He drifted through calculations that made no sense—balancing columns where the debits were dragons and the credits were candle flames, checking entries written in a script that was almost Isandro's handwriting and almost Ashveth's dream-voice, hearing Veyra's flat, kind voice reciting *every entry answered, Tam, that was always the covenant's whole grammar, you simply read it wrong*—

And through all of it, warmth. A hand that left and returned, left and returned, keeping its appointments with the regularity of tide tables. Cool cloths on his brow. Bitter teas he swallowed on faith. Once, surfacing near dusk, he heard Isandro and Sefu talking low on the deck above, and Sefu saying: "The bolt was guild-issue, but the fletching is Veyra's personal guard. She sent her best. She wants Isandro *back*, not dead—but her best shot at a fleeing thief is still a killing shot. That's the arithmetic of it."

And Isandro: "Then we outrun her arithmetic."

Fever again. Dreams of the record hall, but the shelves had become cliffs and the files had become birds, thousands of them, every entry in the guild's history taking wing at once, and somewhere beneath it all a vast slow heartbeat under the harbor, guttering, guttering—

He woke properly on the second night, to lamplight and the smell of tar and river, and found Nour asleep sitting up beside him, her hand still wrapped around his good hand, her chin dropped to her chest, a pickpocket's knife loose and forgotten in her lap. On her other side sat a plate with a honey cake, slightly stale, saved.

Tam watched her breathe and made one final entry in the private ledger he'd begun keeping since the night everything broke open—an account no guild would ever audit, in a currency that had never once appeared in the guild's books.

Debit: one bolt. Credit: everything.

Above deck, muffled through planking, Isandro's voice carried on the night air, tight and urgent, calling Sefu to come look at the horizon—where, past the river bends, toward the mountains they were fleeing toward, the sky glowed a deep ember-red that no sunrise had any business making at midnight.

Ashveth's direction. Ashveth's color.

"Hold on," Tam whispered—to Nour, to the dragon beneath the harbor, to all of them. "We're coming. Every entry answered."

The red glow pulsed once, like a heartbeat skipping, and the barge drove on into the dark.
