## 15. Third Stone, Drowned Choir

The temple had been drowning for six hundred years and had made its peace with it.

Sefu felt that peace the moment they waded into the shallows—a stillness in the water that wasn't emptiness but attention. The drowned did not sleep here. They waited.

"You're certain this is the place?" Tam asked from the barge, keeping his boots dry with the fussy precision of a man who had once been important and missed it desperately. His fever had broken two days ago, but he still moved like a man made of borrowed parts, each joint reattached slightly wrong. "Because the map says the Temple of the Ninefold Chorus, and this looks like a very large rock with delusions."

"The map is right," Sefu said. "Look at the columns."

They looked. At first there was nothing—just black stone shoulders breaking the surface of the lagoon, furred with salt crust and barnacle. Then Nour tilted her head, squinting against the late light, and said, "Those are singing holes."

"Singing holes," Isandro repeated.

"Carved pipes." Nour pointed. "See? The whole column's hollow inside, like a flute. Someone built a temple that plays itself when the tide comes through."

Sefu nodded, something loosening in his chest. "The wardens' records called it the Drowned Choir. It was a listening place. Before the dragons went quiet, humans came here to hear them sing across the water, and the temple would catch the sound and hold it." He paused. "That was before my order failed them."

He said it plainly, because he had decided somewhere on the river between the Salt Gate and here that he would stop flinching from it. The others had noticed. He'd caught Isandro watching him with that careful look of hers—the one she probably thought was neutral, the one that was actually a whole ledger of unspoken entries—and Tam had stopped making jokes about wardens in his hearing, which was its own kind of kindness.

"Well," Nour said, swinging her legs off the barge's edge, "if we're going to rob a haunted church, best do it before dark."

"We're not robbing it," Sefu said. "We're asking."

"Right." She grinned at him, quick and crooked. "Asking. That's what I told the man whose purse I lifted last week."

* * *

The water took them by degrees. First ankle-deep over paving stones worn smooth as river glass, then knee-deep in the great flooded nave, where the nine columns rose out of the lagoon like the ribs of some patient leviathan. The tide was coming in, and as it came, the temple began—very faintly—to sing.

It wasn't music, exactly. It was breath. Long, low exhalations moving through the hollow columns, each one pitched differently, weaving together into a chord so old and so sad that Sefu felt his throat close around it. Beside him, Nour had gone uncharacteristically silent. Even Tam, treading water at the rear and complaining about it under his breath, had stopped complaining.

Isandro stood hip-deep in the nave with her hand resting on her sword hilt—not gripping it, just touching it, the way one touches a friend's shoulder—and her eyes were wide.

"They're here," she whispered. "I can feel them. Like the oath-stone, but—" She shook her head. "But sadder. Like a room full of people who've been waiting for someone to come home."

"They've been waiting a long time," Sefu said.

The water around them shivered.

Not from wind. Not from current. From below—from the shapes that were rising now through the green-dark, unfolding like banners. They had been human once; you could see it in what remained, the suggestion of shoulders, of hands, of bowed heads. But the sea had been working on them for centuries, smoothing them into something simpler: figures of pale driftwood and pearl, their faces featureless as eggs, their long robes trailing into weed. There were dozens of them. Perhaps hundreds. They stood throughout the flooded nave without displacing the water, which should have been impossible, and they all faced inward, toward the altar that broke the surface at the far end like a drowned king's throne.

On the altar lay a stone.

Even half-drowned in twilight, even at forty feet, Sefu could feel it—the third oath-stone, humming that low note he'd learned to know, the note that answered the blood of swordspeakers. Isandro made a small sound beside him, involuntary, and took one step forward.

Every drowned head turned toward her at once.

"Don't," Sefu breathed.

She froze. The spirits did not move otherwise. But the chord of the columns changed—shifted down, deepened, until the whole nave thrummed with a note like a struck bell heard from inside the bell. And into that note, without mouths or voices, words arrived. They didn't come through the ears. They came up through the water, through the bones, settling behind the sternum like swallowed stones.

*Who enters the choir?*

Sefu waded forward before he could think better of it, putting himself between Isandro and the nearest spirit. "Travelers," he said aloud, and his voice sounded absurdly small, a coin dropped into a well. "And one who owes your kind a debt."

The heads turned to him.

*A warden.* The word moved through the water like a cold current. *A keeper of flame. A watcher who watched.*

"Yes."

*You watched,* the chorus said, and there was no accusation in it, which was somehow worse—only a vast, tired statement of fact, the way one might say *the tide comes in.* *You watched while they were caged. You watched while the fire was drawn off in bottles. You watched, and your order sang hymns about vigilance.*

"I did." His voice cracked on the second word. He steadied it. "I'm not here to argue with you. I'm not here to say we didn't know, or couldn't have known. We knew. We knew what the ledgers meant, and we chose our vows to men over our vows to dragons, because the men were near and the dragons were far, and it is easier to obey the hand that feeds you than to answer for the fire you cannot see going out."

The chord held. The spirits held. Somewhere behind him he heard Tam whisper, "Is he preaching? He's preaching to ghosts," and Nour hiss something back that was probably *shut up.*

*Words,* the chorus said. *Wardens were always good with words. Your order wrote ten thousand pages on the sacred duty of protection, and not one page on how to apologize.*

One of the spirits drifted closer. Up close, Sefu could see that its pearl-smooth face was not entirely blank—there was the ghost of a mouth, a seam barely visible, shaped around something it had never finished saying. It raised a hand of pale wood, and in the gesture he recognized, horribly, the formal address of a warden's rank ceremony. This one had been a warden once too. Or loved one. Or buried one.

*We do not want your explanations, keeper. We had those. We want the other thing. The thing your order never learned.*

"What thing?"

*Regret that costs something.* The spirit's hand turned palm-up. *You come seeking the stone. The stone was entrusted to us by the last swordspeaker who understood what was being broken—she swam down into this nave with her own arms and gave it to our keeping, and she said: only give it back to one who can sing true sorrow. Not performance. Not politics. Sorrow that has been carried so long it has grown roots.*

The chord shifted again, and now Sefu could hear what the temple had been holding all these centuries: not just the dragons' old songs, but the voices of the drowned themselves, layered beneath, singing the same grief in smaller registers. Grief for a world that had traded wonder for inventory. Grief for fires put out one careful bottle at a time.

*SING,* said the chorus, every spirit at once, and the word hit the water like a depth-charge. *Sing us your failure, warden. All of it. Hold nothing back for dignity's sake. We will know.*

Behind him, Isandro started to move. "Sefu, you don't have to—"

"It's exactly what I have to do," he said quietly. "It's the only thing I've had to do in twenty years."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about singing to the drowned: the water carries everything. Every break in your voice. Every place where you reach for a comfortable phrase and find it hollow. There is nowhere to hide a false note, because the drowned have spent six hundred years learning what truth sounds like when it moves through water, and they can taste the difference the way a fisherman tastes a change in the current.

So Sefu stood chest-deep in the ruined nave of the Ninefold Chorus, in the last grey light, and he did not perform.

He sang the way he had wept on the night he finally left the wardens' citadel—badly, privately, with the door barred. He sang the first theft he had witnessed, a young dragon named Serelith whose fire was drawn for a governor's wedding gift, and how he had stood at his post outside the cage and recited the vigilance litany to keep from hearing her cry. He sang the year he stopped visiting the cages altogether because it was easier. He sang the letter he wrote to his superiors detailing everything—the bottlings, the burnings-out, the dragon at Vaskarran who died when they took too much—and how he addressed the envelope and then burned it in his brazier rather than send it, because sending it would mean losing everything he had, and he was a coward in a good coat.

He sang Ashveth's name, and how he had dreamed of her for years without once coming to find her, because coming to find her would have required becoming someone who could bear what she had to say.

The water took all of it. The columns caught his broken notes and returned them changed, braided into the drowned choir's long chord, until it was hard to tell where his grief ended and theirs began—and maybe, he thought dimly, that was the point. Maybe that had always been the point of temples.

When he reached the end—the night the last warden post was abandoned, the night he walked out with nothing but a staff and the smell of smoke he'd carry forever—he found he had nothing left to sing. So he simply said, into the silence:

"I am sorry. Not for how it looks. For how it was. And I have spent every day since trying to become someone whose apology means anything, and if I have not yet arrived, I am at least walking."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and texture, that presses on the skin.

Then the nearest spirit—the one with the warden's salute still folded into its hand—drifted forward until it stood directly before him. Its faceless head inclined. And gently, almost tenderly, it reached out and touched two wooden fingers to his sternum, right over the ache his song had left there.

*Rooted enough,* it said.

Around the nave, the chord resolved. The spirits began to sink, folding back down into the green dark like hands closing, and the water level seemed to rise—or perhaps they sank—to meet the altar, and the third oath-stone rose from the stone table of its own accord, dripping, humming, bright as a coal wrapped in silk.

Isandro swam for it. Of course she did. She reached the altar and lifted the stone with both hands and held it above the water, and the light of it ran down her arms and lit her face from below, and for a moment she looked less like a fugitive clerk-turned-thief and more like exactly what the prophecy kept insisting she was.

The last spirit lingered beside Sefu. Its seam-of-a-mouth had changed shape. It was, he realized, smiling—or the memory of smiling, preserved like a fossil.

*Your song had weeds growing through it, keeper,* it said. *Old ones. Deep ones. Good. A sorrow with no roots blows away with the first kind wind.* It began to sink. *Tell the swordspeaker: four stones make a circle, but a covenant is made of what fills the space between them. Tell her to read the old words all the way to the end. The founders did not.*

Then it was gone, and the temple sang one final low exhale through its nine throats—a sigh, Sefu thought, six hundred years long, finally let out—and the lagoon went still and ordinary and full of nothing but evening.

* * *

They camped that night on the temple's roof, on a shelf of ancient masonry above the waterline, because after that none of them wanted to be anywhere with walls. Nour built a fire out of driftwood and declared herself a genius twice. Tam produced, from somewhere in his endless clerk's coat, a tin of candied ginger that he portioned out with the gravity of a man distributing state secrets, and for a while nobody talked at all, which was its own kind of prayer.

It was Isandro who broke it.

"He said to read the old words all the way to the end." She had the prophecy book open on her knees, the water-damaged pages soft as cloth. "The founders did not." Her brow furrowed. "I've read this book cover to cover three times since the Salt Gate. There's no end. It just... stops. After the reforging rite, the rest is water damage. Blank."

"Nothing's ever blank," Tam said comfortably, around a piece of ginger. "In my experience, 'blank' means either 'someone paid to make it blank' or 'you haven't found the right light.'"

Sefu looked at her—at the book, at the stone glowing softly in its wrap beside her, at the firelight catching the fine lines around her eyes that hadn't been there a season ago—and felt the old warden instinct stir, the one that read weather in small signs.

"Show me the rite," he said. "All of it. Tonight. Before we sail for the Emberhold."

Isandro looked down at the page. Something flickered across her face—quick, quickly mastered, gone. A ledger entry refused to be written.

"Tomorrow," she said lightly. "It's been a long day, and the tide's turning, and honestly, Sefu, if I read one more line of archaic covenant-law tonight I'm going to throw this book into the lagoon and let the choir have it."

Nour laughed. Tam chuckled. The moment passed, smooth as a card slid back into a deck.

But Sefu had spent twenty years among creatures who communicated in pressure and absence, and he had just sung his worst self to an audience that could taste a false note in moving water.

He smiled, and passed her the ginger, and said nothing at all.

And all night, as his companions slept and the temple breathed its empty chords below, he lay awake watching the prophecy book where Isandro had tucked it—watching the way she'd turned it face-down, the way her hand had rested on its cover a moment longer than a hand rests on a book that holds nothing.

There was something in those pages she didn't want them to read.

And tomorrow, one way or another, he intended to find out what it cost her to keep it there.
