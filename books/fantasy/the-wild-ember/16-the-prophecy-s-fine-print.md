## 16. The Prophecy's Fine Print

The prophecy book smelled like a drowned library.

Isandro had dried it as best she could over three days — pages spread flat on warm stones, weighted with river pebbles, turned with the tenderness one reserves for sleeping children and things that might bite. The water damage had done strange work on it. Some pages had bloomed into soft gray pulp. Others had gone strangely crisp, as if the flood had been a forge instead of a river. And a few — she was beginning to suspect deliberately, though she had no proof — had fused together at the margins, hiding whatever was written beneath in a fold of paper that refused to be opened by anything less than patience and a bone folder.

She had been avoiding those pages for three days.

Not consciously. She told herself she was being careful. She told herself the group needed rest first, that Tam's fever needed breaking, that Nour needed sleep after selling — after *losing* — the only memory of her mother's face. She told herself a great many things while she sharpened her sword, checked their food stores twice, mended a strap on Sefu's pack that he hadn't asked her to mend, and generally behaved like a woman with nothing whatsoever gnawing at the back of her skull.

It was Tam who finally called it.

"You're hovering," they said, from their sickbed by the barge's little stove. Their voice was still gravelly from the fever, but their eyes were clear again, which meant they'd recovered enough to be insufferable. "You've hovered past me four times in an hour. I counted."

"I'm checking on you."

"I'm a clerk. I know what an audit looks like when someone's afraid of the findings." Tam pushed themselves up on one elbow. "You haven't touched the last folio. The stuck one. You've dusted around it like it's a relative you owe money to."

Isandro opened her mouth to lie — and found, to her mild horror, that she couldn't do it anymore. Not to Tam. Not since the pawnshop, not since Tam had stood in front of his old lover with shaking hands and confessed everything, and been forgiven so easily it seemed to hurt him worse than any punishment would have. There was something about a person who had already burned themselves down to honesty that made lying to them feel like spitting in soup you'd both eaten from.

"The pages are fused," she said. "I'll need to steam them apart. It takes time."

"Then start tonight," Tam said. "We reach the Emberhold in six days, Isandro. Whatever's in there, I'd rather we read it here than learn it standing in a ruined fortress with Veyra's army coming up the mountain."

* * *

She steamed the pages open alone, in the small hours, because some part of her still believed — some deep, guild-trained part — that bad news should be carried by the person best armored to carry it.

That was the lie at the center of her, she realized later. The oldest one. The one the Ledger Guild had built her out of.

The barge was moored in a reed-choked bend of the river, two days downstream of the Salt Gate ambush, far enough that the enforcers would have lost the scent. Sefu slept on deck under the stars because he claimed ceilings made him itch, which Isandro privately suspected was a warden's habit of never sleeping anywhere with only one exit. Nour slept curled against the stove like a cat who had decided, provisionally, that these people were hers. Tam slept in the berth below, breathing easy for the first time in days.

And Isandro sat in the galley with a kettle of steam and a bone folder and the prophecy book open before her like a patient on a table.

The fused folio parted with a sound like a sigh. Inside, the ink had run in places, but the script was old formal covenant-hand — the kind the guild still used for founding documents, all vertical strokes and deliberate flourishes. She could read it. She had copied ledgers in this hand for fifteen years without once asking herself why a trading guild wrote its books in the alphabet of oaths.

Now she knew why. Now she wished she didn't.

The passage began conventionally enough. She read it twice to be sure, mouthing the words, translating the archaic constructions into plain speech the way she'd translate a client's contract into what it actually obligated them to:

*When the fourth stone returns to the hand of the last speaker, let the reforging begin at the Emberhold where the first oath was spoken. Let the stones be laid upon the anvil-heart, and let flame be given to wake them.*

Fine. Good. Straightforward. Flame given — dragon-flame, presumably, Ashveth's own fire, guided by the swordspeaker's voice. A ritual. A ceremony. Difficult, dangerous, but survivable. She felt her shoulders loosen half an inch.

Then came the next line. And the next. And the fine print.

Isandro Okafor had spent her whole career reading contracts, and if there was one thing the Ledger Guild had taught her — the one lesson worth the price of admission — it was that the truth of any agreement lived in the lines everyone skimmed. The clause after the signature space. The footnote in smaller script. The sentence that assumed you'd stop reading because you'd gotten what you wanted two paragraphs ago.

*Let flame be given,* the text continued, *and mark this well: the stones know stolen fire from offered, and will drink only what is freely yielded. The speaker may yield her own life-flame entire — and the covenant shall hold, and the speaker shall be spent.*

*Spent.* She stared at the word. In covenant-hand, *spent* did not mean tired. It meant *used up*. A candle burned down to the socket. It was the same verb used in the oldest ledgers for accounts closed permanently, for debts discharged by the only coin that settled them absolutely.

Her heart did something strange and quiet, like a door closing gently in an empty house.

She kept reading. Because she was Isandro Okafor, and she always read to the end, and because some traitor corner of her mind whispered: *there has to be another way, there's always another way, look for the other way.*

There was another way.

*Or,* said the text, *let the speaker stand surrounded by those bound to her by no ledger and no law — four hearts who have chosen her, and she them — and let each give willingly a portion of their flame, no portion compelled, no portion taken, each knowing fully the cost of what is given. Then shall the covenant be reforged, and all shall live to bear it.*

Four hearts. Knowing fully the cost. Willingly.

Nour, who had already paid more than anyone should pay, who had sold her mother's face so they could buy passage, whose flame was small and bright and had already been spent on love once. Tam, still pale from a crossbow bolt taken shielding that same girl. Sefu, the last warden, who had already lost everything once to this same covenant and followed its ghost across half the world anyway.

No. Absolutely not.

Isandro sat back in the dark galley, and the kettle ticked as it cooled, and she looked at the sleeping shapes through the doorway — Nour a comma of blanket, Tam's steady breath, Sefu's silhouette upright even in sleep, the man who never stopped watching exits — and she made a decision with the clean, terrible efficiency of a woman balancing a ledger.

She would tell them the ritual required her life-flame. That part was true, and they'd need to prepare her, and say goodbyes, and she wanted — gods, how she wanted — to have that time. But the alternative, the clause about four willing flames shared among companions? That stayed in the book. Buried. Her secret to keep, in the finest tradition of her guild.

Because here was the arithmetic, and it was simple, and she was very good at simple arithmetic: one life against five. One person who had spent years making herself invisible, unimportant, a woman built entirely of competent solitude, against four people who had — despite her best efforts, despite every wall she'd ever maintained — become the only family she'd ever had.

It wasn't even a hard calculation. That was the horrifying part. Veyra would have understood it instantly. Veyra, who loved her like a daughter, who had taught her that a ledger-keeper's highest virtue was protecting the whole by any means necessary. For the first time in her life, Isandro was going to use her guildmaster's ethics, and it felt exactly as filthy as she'd expected.

She copied the passage. All of it, even the parts she meant to hide — because she couldn't bring herself to destroy text, not after a lifetime of reverence for books, but she copied it into her private notebook in her own shorthand cipher, the one she'd invented at fourteen and never taught another living soul. The original page she left intact. If anyone else ever learned to steam a folio properly, well. She'd be beyond consequences by then.

Then she banked the stove, climbed the ladder to the deck, and stood looking at the stars until the sky went gray, rehearsing lies to the people she loved most.

* * *

"You didn't sleep," Sefu said, at dawn.

He was crouched at the rail, feeding the stove with reed-cuttings, and he didn't look at her when he said it, which was his way of giving her room to lie if she wanted it. He was very good at that. It was one of the thousand things about him she was trying very hard not to think about, given the circumstances.

"I slept enough."

"Mm." He fed the fire. "You have a face you make when you're carrying something heavy. Your jaw does a thing."

"My jaw does a thing."

"On the left. Like you're biting down on a word." He finally glanced at her, and his eyes were warm and worried and unbearably perceptive. "Is it the book?"

"It's the book." True. Safe. "I finished reading it."

"And?"

Here it was. The first performance. Isandro discovered that lying to Sefu was harder than lying to Tam had been, and infinitely harder than lying to Veyra had been, and she catalogued this information in the place where she kept things too dangerous to examine.

"The reforging requires a swordspeaker's life-flame," she said. "Given wholly. At the anvil-heart of the Emberhold. The stones won't accept anything less than everything." She let him hear the steadiness in her voice, and hated herself a little for how easily it came. Fifteen years of professional composure, finally put to honest work. "So now you know."

Sefu went very still. Reed-smoke drifted between them.

"No," he said.

"No?"

"There's always a second clause. Everything old is written twice — once for hope and once for truth. My teachers—" He stopped. His teachers were dead, all of them, wardens murdered alongside the dragons they served. He started again, quieter. "The wardens memorized the covenant texts. Every recitation ended with a warning verse: *what one cannot give, four may offer.* It was a saying about burden-sharing. About grief. But it came from somewhere, Isandro. Sayings come from somewhere."

Her stomach dropped through the bottom of the barge, through the river mud, into the cold stone of the world. "It's a proverb. Proverbs aren't clauses."

"Read me the passage again. Word for word."

"I just told you—"

"Word. For. Word." He rose, and there was something in his face she'd only seen once before, in the warehouse full of bottled fire: grief with a blade behind it. "Please. I have watched my people's legacy get hidden in fine print for a hundred years. Humor me."

And Isandro Okafor, who had lied to her guildmaster, who was planning to lie to everyone she loved, who had just executed a flawless deception thirty seconds ago — Isandro looked at Sefu's face and heard herself say:

"*What one cannot give, four may offer,*" and then stopped, appalled, because her mouth had apparently defected.

Sefu exhaled slowly. "So it *is* in the book."

"That's not — I misspoke. It's a variant phrasing. The operative text requires the speaker's flame entire. The other line is commentary. Scholastic gloss. It doesn't apply."

"Doesn't apply," he repeated.

"Conditions don't hold. Four willing givers, all knowing the cost, all bound by choice rather than law — Sefu, name me four people who meet that standard, because I can't." This was, technically, also a lie, delivered with the specific cruelty of using true facts to build a false conclusion. The Guild would have given her a commendation. She wanted to wash her hands.

Sefu studied her for a long moment. The stove popped. Somewhere below, Nour murmured in her sleep, a small sound, a child sound, and Isandro saw it land on Sefu too — saw him glance toward the companionway, saw his jaw tighten.

"All right," he said at last.

All right?

"I believe that you believe it," he said, which was somehow much worse than being caught. "But I'm going to read that book myself before we reach the mountain. No offense intended."

"Some offense taken."

"Good." He almost smiled. It was a terrible, tired smile. "You're a better liar than I am, Isandro. That's not a compliment. Wardens are trained to hear what people don't say, and you are the quietest person I have ever met. So I'm going to need to check."

He went back to feeding the stove. Conversation over. Isandro stood at the rail with the wind pulling at her hair and the taste of victory and ash in the same mouthful.

She should have steamed the folio further shut. She should have burned the page. She should have done a dozen ruthless, sensible, Veyran things, and instead she had handed a fugitive warden an appointment with the truth, because some stubborn ember in her chest refused to go out no matter how hard she smothered it.

*One life,* she reminded herself, walking away. *Mine. That's the trade. That's the whole trade, and I'm the only one who has to agree to it, and that makes it the first honest contract the Okafor family has signed in generations.*

Below decks, the prophecy book waited on the table, open, patient, smelling of drowned libraries and old fires. And in her private notebook, in cipher no living soul could read, Isandro had written out the hidden clause in full — including the final line, the one she had not mentioned to Sefu, the one that had been sitting in her chest like swallowed glass since midnight:

*For a flame given in ignorance of its cost is not freely given, and the stones will refuse it, and the covenant will crack.*

Which meant that if any of them ever found out — if Nour or Tam or Sefu ever learned what she'd hidden and tried to help her anyway — their sacrifice wouldn't just fail.

It would damn the covenant forever.

She had six days to keep four loving, stubborn, impossible people from finding out the truth. And the first of them was already reaching for the book.

* * *

Tam found the folio before noon.

Isandro heard the cry from the deck — Tam's voice cracking upward, half triumph, half alarm: "Isandro! The pages — the ones you steamed — they're *re-fusing*, they're sealing themselves shut, what does that *mean*—"

She closed her eyes at the rail and breathed the river air, and thought: *of course.* Of course the book protected itself. Of course the truth fought back. Prophecies, like contracts, like families, kept their own ledgers.

Down in the galley, Tam was holding the sealed folio up to the light, turning it, frowning at it, while Nour peered over their shoulder and Sefu stood in the doorway with his arms crossed and an expression of terrible, patient certainty.

"So," Sefu said pleasantly, meeting her eyes across the little cabin. "About that proverb."
