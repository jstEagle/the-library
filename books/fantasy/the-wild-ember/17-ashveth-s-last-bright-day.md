## 17. Ashveth's Last Bright Day

Ashveth dreamed in centuries.

This was not a metaphor. Dragons dreamed slowly, the way mountains eroded and rivers decided where they wished to go, and so her dreams had a weight that human sleep could never hold. She had been dreaming of the harbor for ninety years—the salt pressing against her scales like a second skin, the rusted hulls of ships settling above her like patient gravestones—and within the dream she had been dreaming of something older still: a mountain with no name, a circle of five swords planted point-down in black earth, and fire rising from the ground like a greeting between old friends.

It was into this older dream that Isandro Okafor arrived.

Ashveth felt her before she saw her—a small warm disturbance at the edge of the dream, the way a candle announces itself to a dark room long before anyone looks at it. The dragon turned her attention toward it, slow as a season changing, and found a woman standing on the shore of a memory-sea that did not exist, wearing the bewildered expression of someone who had fallen asleep expecting nothing and woken inside everything.

"You're smaller than I imagined," Ashveth said.

Her voice in the dream was not sound, exactly. It was pressure, and warmth, and the particular feeling of standing too close to a hearth on a winter night. Isandro's hand went instinctively to her hip where a sword would be, found none, and settled instead on folding her arms—which was, Ashveth thought, its own kind of weapon, though a poorly made one.

"I'm not asleep," Isandro said. "I don't think I'm asleep."

"No one ever thinks they are. That is the first thing every dreamer says, and the second thing is usually *where am I*, and the third—" Ashveth let the pause stretch the way honey stretches off a spoon— "is usually a question about whether I intend to eat them. You've skipped ahead. How refreshing."

"Would you?"

"Eat you?" The dragon considered this with genuine scholarly interest, tilting her great head until one amber eye filled half the sky of the dream. "No. Not from appetite. If I ate you it would be from principle, which is entirely different, and I will confess the possibility has not been ruled out. Come. Walk with me. We have little time and I have spent most of mine already."

* * *

They walked along the shore of the memory-sea, and because it was Ashveth's dream, walking meant something slightly different than walking. Each step carried them further back. The salt smell thinned. The light changed from harbor-gray to the gold of late afternoon in a year so distant that even the sun had been younger.

"This was the day," Ashveth said quietly. "The first covenant. Watch."

The black earth rose around them, and five figures stood upon it: four humans and a dragon, though the dragon was young then, her scales bright as hammered copper, her wings unmarked by rust or salt or sorrow. And beside them, faint as breath on glass, the shapes of other dragons—hundreds, thousands, filling the sky like migrating birds, like weather.

"We were dying even then," Ashveth said. "Do you understand? Our flame does not renew the way yours renews. It spends. It has always spent. The first dragons burned forests to clear them, burned armies to turn them, burned and burned and burned, and each burning left us less. By the age your historians call the Founding, there were perhaps nine hundred of us left in all the world, and we were frightened, and frightened dragons are dangerous in the way frightened kings are dangerous—we had power enough to destroy everything and wisdom enough only sometimes to refrain."

The five figures knelt. The young dragon extended one foreleg, and upon it rested a stone—not cut, not polished, rough as an egg straight from the nest, but glowing from within like a coal wrapped in cloth.

"The oath-stones," Isandro whispered.

"One for each of us who swore. Five stones, five speakers, one covenant. Do you know what was actually written in it, child of ledgers? Your guild keeps its copies locked in vaults. Let me tell you what they are hiding." Ashveth's voice dropped into something older than the dream itself. "It was eleven words. *What burns in me shall warm you, and what grows in you shall shelter me.* Eleven words. No clauses. No penalties. No fine print about inheritance or enforcement or who profits when a signatory dies. Just that. A promise made face to face, witnessed aloud by everyone present, because a promise hidden in a book is only ink, and ink can be owned."

Isandro was very still. "And your stone. Where is yours?"

"Here." Ashveth touched her own chest with one claw, gently, the way one touches a bruise. "I never surrendered mine. Which is why I am the last, and why the covenant cannot simply die—it must be *broken* before it ends, and breaking it requires a swordspeaker's hand. Your guild has been trying for a hundred years to find a way to break it without me noticing. They failed. I notice everything now. Dying makes you attentive; there is so little left to attend to."

She watched the girl absorb this. Watched the ledger-keeper's mind do what ledger-keeper's minds do—reach for the entry, the balance, the number. And beneath that reaching, something else, something warmer and more frightened, which was the thing Ashveth had actually brought her here to see.

"But enough history. You didn't come for history. You came because Sefu told you I judge hearts, and you want to know what I see when I look at yours." Ashveth lowered her head until her breath stirred Isandro's hair—breath that smelled, even in dream, of salt and iron and long patience. "So let us look. Not just at you. At all of you. A covenant is not forged by one heart alone, whatever your guild believed."

* * *

The dream shifted, and now they stood somewhere high and cold, watching four small figures move through a mountain pass far below—so small they might have been ants, except that ants do not argue with such evident affection.

"Sefu first," Ashveth said. "The warden. Watch him walk."

Below, the fugitive moved with his shoulders hunched and his eyes constantly moving, cataloguing exits, counting shadows. But twice—only twice—he stopped entirely, closed his eyes, and lifted his face into the wind as if listening to something no one else could hear.

"What is he doing?" Isandro asked.

"Listening for us. He has done it every day since he fled the wardens, though he would deny it if asked. He was raised to guard sleeping dragons, you know. Nine years in the high roosts, reading our breathing the way your monks read scripture. He knows the sound of a healthy flame and the sound of a failing one, and for six years he has heard nothing but silence, and silence has been eating him alive." Ashveth's eye gleamed. "He grieves like a man who believes the grief is his fault. It is not, quite. But he carries it as if it were, and men who carry grief that way will walk into fire for the smallest chance of setting it down. He will give you everything. His judgment: *true*. But fragile, the way the truest things always are. Handle him gently, swordspeaker, or he will shatter in your hand and blame himself for the sharpness."

The dream turned again. Now a rooftop, rain-slicked, and a small quick figure moving along the eaves with a sack of someone else's belongings and an expression of fierce concentration.

"Nour." Isandro smiled despite herself, and Ashveth noted the smile, filed it away among the evidence.

"She steals," Ashveth observed, "constantly, compulsively, and often pointlessly. Last week she stole a spoon she had no use for and could not explain afterward. And yet." The dream zoomed, close enough now that they could see the girl's face in profile, tight with something unsaid. "Every coin she lifts goes into a fund she pretends is for herself. Passage, she tells Tam. Escape money. But I have seen her dream, child—I visit the dreams of those who carry my stone, and she sleeps near it now—and in her dream there is a house with a blue door, and she buys it over and over, and every time she opens the door her mother is standing there, and every time she wakes before they speak." The dragon's voice softened to embers. "She sold her mother's face to buy your passage. Do you know this?"

Isandro went white. "She—what?"

"Mm. So you didn't know. Interesting." Ashveth let that sit precisely long enough to become uncomfortable. "Her judgment: *true*, and truer than she wants anyone to see. Thieves make the best covenant-keepers, in my long experience. They understand better than anyone what it costs to take what isn't given."

The dream turned once more—to a fever-haze in a barge's hold, a pale clerk sweating through nightmares, muttering numbers in his sleep. Even unconscious, Tam's hands kept making small precise motions, as though correcting errors in documents that existed only in his delirium.

"He betrayed you," Ashveth said. It was not a question.

"He confessed. He'd been reporting our movements to the guild for weeks before he understood what Veyra really was. He nearly got us killed at the Salt Gate."

"And he took a crossbow bolt meant for a thief he'd known less than a month. Yes, I know. I know everything that happens near my stones; it is the one advantage of being ancient and immobile." Ashveth studied the sweating figure below with something dangerously close to fondness. "Your clerks are trained from childhood to believe that a record is more real than the thing recorded. That a person who appears in a ledger as a debt *is* a debt. Tam spent thirty years believing that, and then he met a girl who steals spoons and a woman who lies badly, and somewhere in the middle of it the ledger fell out of his hands and he discovered that people weigh more than entries. His judgment: *true*, and newly so—new truth burns brighter than old truth, like green wood. It also smokes terribly. Be patient with the smoke."

* * *

Then the dream stopped turning, and they were alone again on the shore of the memory-sea, and Ashveth knew—it was past time, and the knowing sat in her chest like swallowed stone.

"And you," Isandro said, before the dragon could speak. "What do you see in me? You've been circling it all night."

"I see a woman keeping two sets of books."

The words landed like a dropped anvil. Isandro flinched as though struck.

"There is the ledger you show your family," Ashveth continued, gentle as falling snow and just as unstoppable. "And there is the one you keep behind your ribs. In the second ledger there is an entry you made three nights ago, in the drowned choir's temple, while Sefu sang and the others slept. You read the prophecy to the end, Isandro Okafor. All of it. Including the part about what the reforging requires."

"Don't."

"*A swordspeaker's life-flame,*" Ashveth recited, merciless as tide, "*or the willing flame of four companions sharing the burden.* And you read it, and you looked at your sleeping family—your strange, stolen, magnificent family—and you decided, alone, in the dark, that their lives weighed more than yours, and that the kindest arithmetic was the one in which they never learned the sum." The dragon sighed, and the sigh moved the sea. "You resolved to hide it. To walk into the Emberhold carrying a secret the size of your own death."

"They'd try to share it." Isandro's voice cracked down the middle. "Nour already gave up her mother's *face*. Tam took a bolt. Sefu has nothing left to give but his grief and his years. If I tell them, they'll insist. They'll *insist*, and I can't—I won't watch them burn themselves down to save me. I'm the swordspeaker. The prophecy names *me*. It should be my—"

"Stop." And Ashveth spoke the word not loudly but with the full weight of a hundred centuries behind it, and the whole dream stilled—even the sea held its waves mid-curl. "Child. Listen to me, because I am the last creature alive who was there when the first words were spoken, and I will say this once.

"A lie kept from family will crack any covenant. Not because the lie is large—yours is, but that is incidental—but because a covenant is not a contract. A contract survives concealment; contracts are designed by people who expect to be cheated. A covenant is a *shared flame*. It lives only in the open, passed mouth to mouth, hand to hand, and the moment one keeper decides the others cannot be trusted with the truth, the flame begins to gutter in their hands even as it burns in hers. Your guild's founders learned this. They hid the covenant inside ledgers and laws, told themselves it was safer there, protected—and look what their protection made. Four murdered speakers. A thousand thefts. A dragon in a rusted shell beneath a harbor, waiting a hundred years for someone honest enough to say *I was wrong* out loud."

Isandro had begun, very quietly, to cry. She did it the way she did everything—competently, invisibly, as though crying were a trade skill she preferred not to display. Ashveth found this both admirable and heartbreaking.

"I'm trying to protect them," Isandro whispered.

"I know. Protection is the most seductive form of lying. It wears love's clothes and borrows love's voice, and by the time you notice it has picked your pocket, you are standing alone at an altar with everyone you love locked safely outside, wondering why the ceremony feels like a funeral." The dragon lowered her head again, and this time her eye was level with Isandro's, amber and vast and impossibly kind. "Here is my judgment, swordspeaker, since you asked for it and since judgments are what I have left to give.

"Your heart is *true*. Truer than any I have weighed since the first forging. You love without conditions and lead without cruelty and you balance books because balancing is how you pray. But truth is not enough—not for this. The covenant will not answer a lone hero. It never has. It answers *witnesses*: people who have seen each other wholly and stayed anyway. If you go to the Emberhold carrying your secret, the stones will know. Fire always knows. And the reforging will fail—not because you lack courage, but because you lack company, and company is the entire point."

A long silence. Waves that remembered being mountains lapped at shores that remembered being nothing at all.

"If I tell them," Isandro said at last, "they'll share the burden. Four flames. They could die."

"Yes."

"That's the whole of your counsel? *Yes?*"

"It is the whole of mine, and it is more than your guild ever gave you." Ashveth smiled—a terrible and wonderful sight, a landscape rearranging itself into joy. "But since you seem to want more: they will not die, little ledger. Flames freely given are not consumed the way flames taken are. This is the secret your guild never learned, because they only ever took. What is given returns. What is shared multiplies. Ask your pickpocket sometime why the stolen wonders in the guildhouse warehouses always dimmed, year over year, no matter how carefully they were stored—and then ask yourself why the bread Nour shares always tastes better than the bread she eats alone. The law is the same at every scale. It has always been the same."

The dream was thinning now. Ashveth could feel the morning coming, feel the small bodies above the harbor stirring, feel the barge turning toward the mountain road and the ruined fortress waiting at its end. Time to finish.

"One last thing," she said, as the gold light of the first forging dissolved around them like sugar in water. "Tomorrow—or the day after, roads being what they are—you will stand in the Emberhold, and Veyra will arrive with her army, and everything I have shown you will be tested in fire. When that moment comes, and you feel the old habit rise in you—the habit of carrying the weight alone, of protecting your family from the truth of your own heart—I want you to remember this shore. Remember an old dragon who waited a hundred years to say eleven words to someone who would finally understand them."

Isandro, already fading, already half-awake in the hold of a river barge, caught the words like a thrown rope:

"*What burns in me shall warm you.*"

Pause.

"*What grows in you shall shelter me.*"

Pause. The sea going gray, the mountain dissolving, the dragon's eye the last light in the dark—

"Now wake up, Isandro Okafor. Wake up, and tell them everything. Before the road runs out and the choice is made for you."

* * *

Above the harbor, in the shell of salt and rust, Ashveth opened one ancient eye.

Far above, through fathoms of gray water and the keels of dead ships, she felt the barge change course—felt, with senses that needed neither light nor air, four hearts quicken at once aboard it. One waking from a dream with tears drying on her cheeks. One sitting bolt upright, already asking questions. One reaching for a map. One holding a candle steady while his hands shook.

And one small flame—the swordspeaker's—flickering, wavering on the edge of confession, gathering itself the way a diver gathers breath.

Ashveth settled her chin back onto her folded claws and permitted herself, for the first time in ninety years, something that was almost hope.

"Soon," she rumbled, to no one, to everyone, to the dark water pressing close as a blanket. "Come soon, children. Bring your truths with you. I have waited long enough to hear them spoken aloud."
