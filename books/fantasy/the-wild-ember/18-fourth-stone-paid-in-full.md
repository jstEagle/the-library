## 18. Fourth Stone, Paid in Full

Nour had stolen a lot of things in her life. A silver hairpin from a duchess's boudoir. A wheel of cheese the size of her torso from a monastery that had clearly been blessed by some god of dairy. Once, briefly and by accident, a baby (she'd returned it; the mother hadn't even noticed until Nour pointed it out, which said something about that family).

But she had never once tried to steal something back from a man wearing full guild plate who was standing in front of the last thing standing between them and saving the world.

"I'm just saying," she whispered to Isandro, crouched behind a stack of shipping crates on the Salt Gate road, "of all of us, why me?"

"Because he knows Tam's face," Tam murmured from her other side. His voice was still rough from the fever, his shoulder still bandaged where the crossbow bolt had gone through. He'd insisted on coming anyway, propped up on a crate like an invalid king holding court. "And he knows mine. And Isandro is—" He gestured vaguely at Isandro, who was doing something with her sword that made the air around it hum like a struck bell.

"Isandro is what?" Nour asked.

"Wanted," said Sefu simply.

Nour looked at the enforcer-captain again. Captain Dario Voss. Even she had heard of him, back when she'd been picking pockets around the guild quarter. The man was a wall with a conscience, they used to say. He'd once carried a drunk apprentice home through a hailstorm instead of arresting him. He'd also put down the Ferrier Street riot alone, unarmed, without killing anyone, and then stood at the gate afterward and personally apologized to every widow.

He stood now in the middle of the road to the Emberhold with six armed enforcers behind him, and he did not look like a man who could be apologized past.

"So your plan," Nour said slowly, "is for me to walk out there, alone, and—what? Talk him to death?"

"Not talk," Isandro said. She wasn't looking at Nour. She was looking at the small bundle wrapped in oilcloth at Nour's hip, and her expression did something complicated—the same expression she'd worn ever since the night by the canal fire, when Nour had finally told her what she'd sold to buy their passage. "Give."

Nour's hand went to the bundle before she could stop it. Her heart did its usual stupid lurching thing.

"No," she said. "Absolutely not. Do you know how long I've had this? Do you know what it cost me? I can't even *remember* what it cost me, that's the whole point, that's the whole horrible point—"

"Nour." Isandro turned to face her fully. In the grey pre-dawn light, with rain beading in her dark hair, she looked tired and scared and absolutely certain, all at once. "I read the ledger entry. Two years ago. A woman pawned a memory to the guildhouse on Candlemaker's Row. No name given. The clerk noted the memory as 'a face, much loved, origin unknown.' You were sixteen." She paused. "You got four silver marks and a hot meal."

"It bought me a winter," Nour snapped. "It kept me alive. That memory was the only thing my mother ever gave me that I knew was real, and it was the only proof she ever existed, and I traded it because we were starving, so forgive me if I'm not eager to—"

"To give it away?" Isandro's voice went soft. "No. Not give it away."

She reached out and touched the oilcloth bundle, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"Dario Voss pawned a memory too. Eleven years ago. Right after the Ferrier Street riots, right after he broke his own oath to the guild by refusing an order he couldn't live with. He pawned the only thing he had left worth anything." She met Nour's eyes. "He pawned the memory of his daughter's face. And he's been walking around for eleven years knowing he loved someone and being unable to remember why."

The rain ticked against the crates. Somewhere behind them, Sefu shifted his weight, and Tam said nothing, and Nour felt the whole plan settle over her shoulders like a wet cloak.

"He keeps it in a lockbox under his bunk at the garrison," Isandro went on. "Guild regulation: enforcer-captains surrender all personal effects above sentimental value. He declared the memory sentimental. They took it anyway. It's been sitting in the unclaimed-pawn vault for eleven years because nobody else wants it and he won't buy it back because buying back his own daughter would mean admitting the guild robbed him."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard," Nour said, "and I once watched a man cry over a taxidermied cat."

"We stole it," Tam said quietly. "Three nights ago. Before the ambush. I pulled the vault record myself." He held out a second bundle, smaller, wrapped in plain cloth. "We've had it the whole time. We were always going to try this. But he'll never take it from any of us. He'll think it's a trick. A bribe. A trap." He nodded at Nour. "From you, he might believe it."

"Why me?"

"Because you know what it's like," Isandro said. "To have something taken and be told it was fair. To be told the price was honest." She smiled, and it was a crooked, tired smile, the kind that made Nour want to both hug her and throw something at her. "You're the only one of us who's ever been on the other side of a ledger."

* * *

Walking out into the open road felt like walking onto a stage with no idea of the play.

Captain Dario Voss watched her come. Up close, out of armor's shadow, he was smaller than legend—broad, grey at the temples, with the deep-creased face of a man who had spent twenty years frowning at things worth frowning at. His hand rested on his sword but did not grip it. Behind him, his six enforcers shifted nervously.

"You're the pickpocket," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "Nour. Of the canal district. Forty-one counts of petty theft, nine counts of burglary, one count of—"

"One count of baby-napping, yes, it was a misunderstanding, the pram looked identical to another pram—"

"—and one count of impersonating a tax collector, which frankly showed initiative." Something flickered at the corner of his mouth and was suppressed. "You're traveling with a traitor swordspeaker, a fugitive warden, a disgraced clerk, and a stolen oath-stone. You're going to the Emberhold. I'm here to stop you."

"Are you?" Nour asked. "Or are you here because Guildmaster Veyra told you to be, and you're hoping we give you a reason not to obey?"

His jaw tightened. Good. Tightening meant thinking.

"You've heard stories," Nour pressed. She walked closer, boots squelching in the mud, close enough that she could see the rain running off the edge of his pauldron. "About the covenant. About what the guild's been selling all these years. Maybe you believe them, maybe you don't. Doesn't matter. Because I'm not here to argue with you, Captain. I've seen what happens when people argue with you. They lose politely."

"What are you here for?"

Nour held out the cloth-wrapped bundle.

"For you to hold something."

Voss didn't move. Behind him, an enforcer muttered, "Sir, it could be—"

"A weapon?" Nour snorted. "Look at it. It's the size of a plum. If I wanted to kill you, Captain, I'd have picked your pocket already—you didn't even notice me until I was ten feet away, and I've done it to duchesses, so let's not pretend today is your best work."

That got a twitch out of two of the enforcers. Voss silenced them with a look, and then, slowly, he held out his hand.

Nour set the bundle in his palm.

He unwrapped it with the careful hands of a man defusing something. Inside, glowing faintly with the amber light of preserved memory, sat a small glass vial sealed with guild wax—and beside it, a folded slip of paper, yellowed with age. Nour recognized the paper. Every pawned memory came with one: the clerk's description, written at the moment of pawning, in the owner's own dictated words.

*One (1) memory. Subject: my daughter, Lira, aged six, laughing, on the morning she lost her first tooth and decided it meant she was becoming a dragon. Submitted by D. Voss, Enforcer-Captain. Reason for pawning: to pay for her mother's funeral. Note: subject reports the memory is 'the only thing I have left that proves I was happy once.'*

The rain fell. Nobody moved. Voss read it twice, three times, his lips shaping the words like a man tasting something he'd forgotten the flavor of.

"This is stolen," he said hoarsely. "This is from the vault."

"Yeah," Nour said. "We stole it. From the guild. Who stole it first, from you. So really we're just returning it. Consider us very delayed couriers."

"You expect me to believe thieves returned something."

"No," Nour said honestly. "I don't care what you believe. Believe whatever gets you through the next minute." She nodded at the vial. "Open it. Or don't. Keep it locked up for another eleven years, keep paying the guild for the privilege of forgetting your own kid. That's your choice. But you should know what you'd be choosing."

"And what am I choosing between?"

"Lira laughing," Nour said, "or Lira gone."

Voss looked at the vial for a long time. Then, with hands that were not quite steady, he thumbed the wax seal and pulled the stopper.

The memory rose out of the glass like smoke with a heartbeat in it. For a moment it hung there, shapeless, amber, searching—

And then it found him.

Nour had seen memories restored exactly once before, from the outside: a rich man in a pawnshop who'd bought back his dead brother's voice, and had sat down hard on the floor and wept while the clerk pretended not to notice. This was worse. Or better. She genuinely couldn't tell.

Dario Voss went to his knees in the mud. Not collapsed—knelt, deliberate, like a man arriving somewhere. The vial slipped from his fingers. His eyes were open but seeing something eleven years and a hundred miles away: a kitchen, maybe, or a garden, a gap-toothed girl roaring at him with her arms out like wings, *look Papa, look, I'm a dragon.*

When he finally spoke, his voice came out in pieces.

"She had a freckle," he said. "On her left cheek. Just under her eye. Like a little star falling." He pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth. "I forgot the freckle. How do you forget a freckle. She's going to be seventeen this spring and I forgot the freckle."

Behind him, one of the enforcers made a small sound and looked away. Another had quietly lowered his crossbow entirely.

Nour crouched down in front of the captain, heedless of the mud ruining her only good trousers.

"Now you know how I feel," she said gently. "Except I never got mine back. I sold my mother's face when I was sixteen, and I've spent every day since knowing there was a hole in my head shaped exactly like someone I loved, and I couldn't even tell you her laugh." She swallowed. It was harder than she'd expected, saying it out loud in the rain to a kneeling wall of a man. "So I'm going to tell you something a friend told me. Mercy isn't weakness. Mercy is what strength is *for*. Any idiot can swing a sword. It takes somebody strong to kneel down in the mud and admit the ledger was wrong."

Voss raised his head. His eyes were red-rimmed and furious and grateful all at once, and Nour thought, with a strange lurch, that he looked like every father she'd ever pickpocketed outside a schoolyard: dangerous, and helpless, and full of love with nowhere to put it.

"The guildmaster will send more than six," he rasped. "When I don't report. She'll send the whole company. Then the reserve. Then whatever she keeps in the cellar under the guildhouse that I've never been cleared to see." He looked at the oath-stone slung across Isandro's back, visible now beyond the crates, humming faintly like a struck bell that refused to fade. "Four stones. You have three. The fourth—"

"Yours," Nour said.

Voss stared at her.

Then he laughed—a short, cracked, astonished sound, half sob. "Of course," he said. "Of course it's mine. Twenty-two years of service and the final stone of the old covenant sits in my kit bag. She gave it to me herself, three years ago. Told me it was a 'guild relic' and to guard it with my life." He wiped his face with a gauntleted hand, smearing mud and rain alike. "She knew. Gods help me, she knew, and she handed it to the one man too honest to ever look inside."

He reached up, unbuckled the heavy satchel at his hip, and drew out a case of black iron. When it opened, the fourth oath-stone lay inside on rotted velvet, and it blazed—not glowing, *blazing*, like a coal remembering it used to be a star, and the light of it washed across all their faces and made the rain look golden.

Isandro came out from behind the crates. So did Tam, leaning on Sefu, and Sefu with his scarred hands open and empty, showing peace.

Voss stood up. He looked at each of them in turn, and last of all he looked down at Nour, and he did something no guild captain had ever done in the entire recorded history of the Ledger Guild.

He bowed. Low. To a pickpocket with muddy trousers.

"Paid in full," he said roughly. "All debts. Mine included." He straightened, and turned to his six enforcers, who were looking anywhere else with tremendous discipline. "Men. There's a decision here. Anyone who wants to go back to the guildhouse and report me, go now, no shame in it. Anyone who wants to see what a covenant looks like when it's reforged properly—" he shouldered the iron case, "—follow me. I know a shortcut to the Emberhold through the quarry roads. We'll beat her army there by half a day."

Nobody went back. Six crossbows stayed slung. One young enforcer, barely older than Nour had been when she sold her mother's face, whispered, "My sister pawned a memory last winter. To pay for my apprenticeship fees." He looked at Voss. "Could we... after? Could we get it back? All of them?"

"After," said Voss, and the word landed like a vow.

They moved out together, seven guild soldiers and four thieves, up the quarry road toward the ruined fortress on the mountain, the fourth stone blazing between them like a small captive dawn.

Nour fell into step beside Isandro, who bumped her shoulder gently against hers.

"You okay?" Isandro asked quietly.

Nour thought about it honestly. Her legs were shaking. Her chest hurt. Somewhere in her head there was still a hole shaped like a woman she couldn't remember, and it always would be.

"Yeah," she said. And then, because it was true, and because the whole day had been about saying true things: "Ask me again after we save the world. Maybe by then I'll have learned to stop giving away the important stuff for free."

Ahead, the Emberhold's broken towers caught the first true light of sunrise, and beneath the harbor, unseen, something vast and dying lifted its head—as if it smelled four stones coming home, and one old flame about to burn.
