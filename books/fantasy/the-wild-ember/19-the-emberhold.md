## 19. The Emberhold

The Emberhold had not been a fortress in Isandro's lifetime. It was a wound in the mountainside now, all black ribs and broken throat, the great forge-tower collapsed inward like a lung that had given up breathing. Moss grew over the anvil-stones the size of wagons. Wind came down from the peaks and moved through the shattered arches, and it made a sound—not quite moaning, not quite singing—that Sefu said was the mountain remembering heat.

"It sounds like my grandmother's kettle," Nour said.

"It sounds," Tam said, "like we are about to die in a very scenic location."

"You always say that."

"Because it keeps being true."

Isandro let them talk. It was easier than thinking. They had climbed for two days out of the river country, Tam's wound stitched and re-stitched, his fever finally broken on the third morning when he woke up complaining about the quality of camp porridge, which everyone agreed was the surest sign he'd live. The four oath-stones rode in a padded case against Isandro's chest, and they hummed against her sternum like four small impatient hearts. When she stopped walking, they thumped harder, as if to say *keep going, keep going.*

Ashveth could not climb with them. The dragon remained beneath the harbor, three hundred miles back, guttering. But every night since the drowned choir, Isandro dreamed of salt water and a voice like banked coals: *The Emberhold. Hurry, little ledger-keeper. Even I have only so many pages left.*

So here they were, at dusk, standing in the ruin where the world's oldest promise had been made and then murdered.

"All right." Sefu set down his pack and began unpacking it with the terrible efficiency of a man who had rehearsed this moment for twenty years. "The forge-heart is below. There's a stair under the main hall—the wardens used it during the last reforging. We need the stones placed at the four compass points of the hearth-ring, and then—" He stopped. He looked at Isandro. "And then you speak the oath. You're the swordspeaker. That part has always been yours."

"Yes," Isandro said.

Her voice did something strange on the word. She heard it herself—heard the hairline crack in it—and prayed none of them did.

Nour heard it. Of course Nour heard it. The girl had made a career of noticing what people tried to hide in their pockets. Her eyes narrowed, quick as a magpie's, but she said nothing yet.

*Just get through the ritual,* Isandro told herself. *Get through tonight, and they never have to know what the fine print costs.*

She had found it eleven days ago, reading the prophecy by candlelight while the others slept: the passage she'd skimmed a hundred times because it was written in the oldest script, the covenant-script, the one that took her an hour per line. *The flame that seals must be freely given. One swordspeaker may give all.* And beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic, the way lawyers write things they hope no one reads:

*A single flame suffices. It does not survive.*

One life. Hers. Four stones, one oath, one fire—and the fire would take its fuel from whoever spoke the binding. Or, if the words were shared—

No. She hadn't let herself read past that part. Sharing meant asking. Asking meant they'd say yes, and she had spent her whole life keeping other people's ledgers balanced; she was not going to spend their lives balancing hers.

The stair down was narrow and smelled of cold iron and older smoke. Sefu went first with a lantern, then Tam, who insisted on carrying the case of stones despite his healing shoulder, then Nour, then Isandro last, the way she had walked through the guildhouse for years—rear guard, invisible, watching everyone's backs because no one watched hers.

Except now four people kept glancing back at her. It was the most disorienting thing that had happened all week, including the drowned choir.

The forge-heart opened beneath them like the inside of a bell. A circular chamber, vast, its ceiling lost in dark, ringed by four colossal hearths facing one another across a floor of fused glass—sand melted to mirror by heat that had died a century ago. In the center stood the hearth-ring: a low circle of black stone carved with script older than the guild, older than the realms, older, Sefu said, than most gods anyone still bothered to worship.

"It's beautiful," Nour whispered, and her voice bounced around the chamber and came back sounding like five girls whispering.

"The wardens called it the First Kitchen," Sefu said, and even his reverence cracked into a smile. "Dragons have no poetry about their own works. Ashveth says it was where the first bread was baked for the first human delegation, and that it burned twice before they got the recipe right."

Tam laughed, then winced, then laughed again anyway.

They worked. That was the mercy of ritual—it gave you tasks. Sefu directed them through the placements: north stone in the northern hearth, and so on, each oath-stone settling into its cradle with a sound like a struck bell underwater. With each placement the air changed. Warmer. Denser. Isandro felt the hum of the stones move out of her chest and into the room, felt the whole chamber begin, slowly, to remember what it was for.

When the fourth stone rang home, every piece of fused glass on the floor lit faintly gold, reflecting four small fires that did not exist yet.

"Now," Sefu said softly. "Now you speak it, Isandro. However much you've prepared. However you mean it."

She stepped into the hearth-ring. She unslung her sword—the old guild blade she hadn't surrendered, the one whose speech she alone could hear—and laid it flat across the black stone. The blade hummed against the prophecy-script, recognizing home.

And Isandro opened her mouth to lie to the four people she loved most in the world, using true words.

That was the trick of it, she thought wildly. That was the filthy genius of the old covenant. She didn't have to speak anything false. Every word of the oath was honest. Only the speaker needed to be hiding something, and the fire would find it anyway, because fire finds everything.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, looking at their faces—Sefu steady and grieving and hopeful, Tam pale but upright, Nour trying to look casual and failing completely. *I'm sorry. This is the last entry I'll ever balance, and I'm doing it the way I do everything: alone.*

She drew breath.

Torches bloomed above them.

Not torches—lanterns, dozens, mounted on poles, carried by ranks of figures filling the ruined hall above the forge-heart. Guild grey. Crossbows. The sigil of the Ledger Guild catching the light: scales, and beneath the scales, a sword.

And at their head, walking to the edge of the gallery with her hands folded and unhurried, stood Veyra.

* * *

"Isandro." Veyra's voice carried down into the bell of the chamber without effort. It always had. She had never once needed to shout in her life; rooms simply cooperated. "You've brought stolen property to a stolen place. Put down the stones and come home, child. No one else needs to be hurt."

Behind her visor of shadow, Isandro saw the enforcers array themselves along the gallery—forty? fifty?—crossbows leveled down at four small figures standing in a ring of dead fires.

"Well," said Tam. "This is familiar."

"We escaped this exact situation two weeks ago," Nour said. "It's like she doesn't think we practice."

"I have a plan," Isandro said quietly. "All of you stay behind me."

"That's the problem," Sefu murmured. "That is exactly the problem, isn't it."

Veyra descended the outer stair alone. Of course she did. She trusted her crossbows to hold position and her own presence to do the rest, and the awful truth was that for nineteen years that calculation had been correct. She came down into the forge-heart the way she entered every room she'd ever owned, and she looked at the lit hearth-cradles, at the humming stones, at the sword laid on the ancient script, and Isandro watched her teacher's face do something complicated.

"You actually found them," Veyra said. "All four." A pause. "I only ever located two, in thirty years of looking. I confess I'm impressed."

"They were never lost, Veyra. They were *sold*. Your guild sold them."

"My guild bought peace." Veyra's voice stayed gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Isandro. Look at them." She gestured at the companions with real tenderness, which was worse still. "A pickpocket. A disgraced clerk. A warden who abandoned his order. And you—a woman who hid inside my house for nineteen years because she knew, somewhere in her bones, that she didn't belong to any of it. These are not the people to carry a covenant. These are people who run."

"We run toward things now," Nour said. "It's a whole new system. Very innovative."

"Nour." Veyra turned to her, and Isandro saw the guildmaster deploy her deadliest weapon: sincerity. "I have read your file, child. I know what the guild took from you without your knowing—the memory brokered through three intermediaries so you'd never trace it. When this is done, I will personally see it returned to you. Whatever happens tonight, that I promise."

Something flickered across Nour's face—hurt, hope, fury, all in one heartbeat—and then she planted her feet. "Too late," she said. "Somebody already beat you to it. Somebody already gave it back. That's kind of our whole thing now. You should try it."

Veyra's gaze came back to Isandro, and there it was at last, the thing underneath everything: not anger. Fear. "The prophecy requires a life, Isandro. I've read it too—I've had it longer than you have. Did you think I let you keep that book because I was careless?" She stepped closer. "I have spent thirty years finding another way. Thirty years, and there isn't one. So put the stones down, and we will go on looking together, the way we should have done from the start, instead of you throwing yourself into a furnace for a dragon who would—"

"She wouldn't," Isandro said. "But I would. And that's the difference between us."

Silence. Above, fifty crossbows creaked as fifty hands tightened.

"What do you mean, you would," Tam said slowly.

And Isandro understood, with the peculiar clarity of a person stepping off a ledge, that the moment had arrived either way. Veyra had dragged it into the open. The choice was hers now: lie cleanly, quickly, with an army watching—or tell the truth in front of the people she'd been protecting from it.

She was so tired. Nineteen years tired. And they were all looking at her, and the fire in the cradles had begun, very faintly, to glow, as if it already knew what she intended to feed it.

"I mean," Isandro said, "that the reforging takes a flame freely given. One swordspeaker can give all of it." She kept her voice level, ledger-clerk flat, the voice she'd used for ten thousand entries. "I read the fine print. I've known for eleven days. I came here anyway."

* * *

The silence afterward had texture. Isandro stood in it and felt it press against her skin.

Then Nour hit her.

Not hard—Nour was a professional, and professionals calibrated—but a solid open-palm shove to the shoulder that rocked Isandro back a step. "Eleven *days*?" Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "Eleven days we slept ten feet away from you! Eleven days you watched Tam get shot! You watched me sell my mother's face, you held my hand after, you—did you know then? Was it already in your pocket?"

"No. I found it after." Which was true, and also the smallest possible answer to the question being asked.

"Oh, well, *after*," Nour snarled, and her eyes were streaming. "That makes it fine, then. Ledger-keeper of the year."

"Isandro." Sefu's voice was quiet in a way she had learned to dread, because Sefu's quiet was the quiet of deep water. "Say the rest. All of it. There's more—I've read enough covenant-law to smell an omission."

Isandro looked at the glowing cradles. At the sword. Anywhere but their faces.

"There's an alternative clause," she said. "Old script. If four companions each give a portion of their flame—willingly, equally—the burden is shared, and no one dies. It's how the original five bound the covenant. Five flames, one promise."

"And you decided," Tam said, each word laid down like a stone, "that our lives were yours to spend or spare. That we weren't entitled to the same choice you made for yourself."

"You were shot," Isandro burst out, and her ledger-flatness shattered all at once, nineteen years of it coming apart in her mouth. "You took a bolt for her, Tam, you nearly *died*, and Nour sold the only memory of her mother she'll ever have, and Sefu has lost everyone he ever served beside, and I thought—if it costs one more thing, let it cost me. Let it be mine. I've spent my whole life keeping everyone else's accounts and taking nothing, and I wanted—"

Her voice broke.

"I wanted one entry that was mine. One debt I paid alone. Because if I asked you, you'd say yes, and I couldn't bear it. I couldn't bear owing my life to people I love. It's easier to owe a fire."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere above, an enforcer shifted, and Veyra raised one hand and held her entire army still, because Veyra understood better than anyone alive that this moment belonged to the four of them, and because—Isandro realized, watching her teacher's face—because Veyra was listening too. Learning something. Her eyes had gone wet and furious.

Sefu moved first. He crossed the hearth-ring, took Isandro gently by both shoulders, and touched his forehead to hers, the old warden's greeting, the one given between equals.

"You absolute fool," he said warmly. "You wonderful, catastrophic fool. Do you know what a covenant *is*? It isn't a payment. It was never a payment. Ashveth told you herself—she's been telling you for weeks, and you filed it under 'later' like every difficult entry you've ever avoided." He pulled back to look at her. "A covenant is people choosing each other in front of witnesses. If you bind it alone, it isn't a covenant. It's just another guild. One member. One sacrifice. One ledger." His smile was sad and blazing. "We didn't follow you three hundred miles to watch you become Veyra."

"Low blow," said Veyra, from the edge of the ring.

"Ma'am, with respect, you chased us three hundred miles, so sit tight." Sefu hadn't even turned around.

Nour wiped her face with her sleeve, sniffed enormously, and stuck out her hand. "New terms. Nobody pays alone. Not for stones, not for memories, not for fire. That's the deal or I pickpocket the oath right out of your hand."

"That's not how oaths work," Isandro managed.

"Neither is lying to your family for eleven days, and you managed that fine."

Tam limped forward and put his hand on top of Nour's, wincing, grinning, fever-bright. "For the record, I object strongly to dying. I've recently developed plans. But I object far more strongly to being protected from my own choices. I'm a clerk, Isandro. Reading the fine print and then deciding anyway is literally my entire profession. Include me."

Four hands. Then, unbidden, Isandro's own rose and covered them, and the moment her palm met theirs, the four oath-stones flared—all at once, gold light pouring up out of the cradles and running along the ancient script in the floor like dawn moving across a map—and the whole forge-heart woke with a sound like an enormous inhale.

Above them, fifty crossbows wavered.

And Veyra stared at the light—at the light that answered *shared* hands, that had sat cold and dim through every word of Isandro's solo intention—and Isandro watched thirty years of certainty begin, very quietly, to crack.

"That's impossible," Veyra said. "The old records say—"

"Your records were written by murderers," Sefu said, "to justify what they'd done. Maybe check the sources."

Far away—three hundred miles beneath a harbor—something ancient exhaled, and the wind moving through the broken arches of the Emberhold changed key. It sounded less like a kettle now. It sounded like a choir tuning up.

Isandro looked at her family's hands stacked on hers, and at the army above, and at the woman who had been her whole world for nineteen years standing at the edge of the firelight with her soldiers and her fear and her dawning, terrible doubt.

"The guild is advancing or it isn't," Isandro said. "So, Veyra. Last chance to watch something impossible. Either draw—or put down your weapon, and I'll read you the oath my family wrote."

Above them, in the dark, a single crossbow clattered to the stone floor.

Then another.

Veyra closed her eyes.
