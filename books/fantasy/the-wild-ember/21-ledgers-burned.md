## 21. Ledgers Burned

The fire took three days to eat the ledgers.

Veyra watched it from the guildhouse steps with her hands folded behind her back, the way she had stood through a hundred inspections, a hundred audits, a hundred small ceremonies of order. Only now there was nothing to inspect. The great counting hall stood open to the sky where the roof had burned through, and the fire that had done it was not really fire at all—it was Ashveth's flame, given freely, and it did not behave like ordinary fire. It ate paper and left stone. It ate ink and left shelves. It had taken the west wall on the second day in a slow, deliberate crawl, like a reader working through a long document, and Veyra had begun to suspect the dragon was reading.

"You could help," said Tam.

He came up beside her with two cups of chicory, which was what passed for coffee now that the guild's accounts were frozen and nobody could buy anything. He held one out without ceremony. His arm was still bound from the bolt at the Salt Gate; he held the cup awkwardly against his chest, and Veyra took it because refusing kindness seemed, suddenly, like the last luxury she could not afford.

"I am helping," she said.

"You're standing."

"Standing is a discipline." She sipped. It was terrible. She drank more of it. "Someone should witness this properly. A hundred and forty years of accounts. Every impossible thing we ever sold, every debt we recorded, every entry written in a steady hand by someone who believed they were doing good work." She looked at him over the rim of the cup. "You were one of those someones, Clerk Tam."

"Disgraced clerk," he corrected mildly.

"Disgraced by me. I signed the order myself." She remembered writing it—remembered the particular satisfaction of a clean line drawn through a clean name. "I'd like to say I regretted it immediately, but honesty is apparently the fashion now, so: I regretted it eleven days later, when your replacement transposed two columns and nearly sold a bottled grief to a man who already owned one."

Tam made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled in its cradle. "That was Nour's doing. She'd fenced the first grief back into our own inventory."

"Yes. The child is a menace."

"She's twenty-two."

"The child is a menace," Veyra repeated, with feeling, and this time Tam did laugh, short and startled, as if laughter were another thing he'd been exiled from and hadn't expected to be readmitted to.

Below them, in the courtyard, the work of un-becoming went on. Guild clerks—the ones who had stayed, which was perhaps half, which was more than Veyra deserved—carried crates out of the counting hall and stacked them for cataloguing. Each crate was labeled now not with a lot number but with a destination. *To the river-people, the drowned choir's song-jars.* *To the widow Ammas, one memory of a daughter, returned.* *To the harbor, all remaining salt.* It was slow, painstaking, humiliating work, and Veyra had insisted on designing the labels herself, in her own hand, so that every returned wonder would carry her handwriting like a signature on a confession.

* * *

She had expected exile. She had prepared a speech for it, actually—she was honest enough with herself to admit she had drafted it twice, once in dignified sorrow and once, late at night, in genuine fury—and when Isandro had come to her on the morning after the Emberhold, Veyra had braced for it the way one braces for a door slammed in the face.

Instead Isandro had sat down across from her in the ruined refectory, looking younger and older than any twenty-eight-year-old had a right to look, and said, "The covenant binds realms together, not people to punishments. Ashveth says vengeance just makes new debts. So." She'd spread her hands. "No exile. No trial. But you're stripped of office, and you'll help return everything. All of it. Every entry."

"All fourteen thousand?" Veyra had asked.

"All fourteen thousand, four hundred and six," Isandro said, and there—there it was, the girl Veyra had raised, the swordspeaker's precision, the love of exactness that Veyra recognized because she'd taught it herself. "I counted."

"You always counted."

"You taught me to count."

And that was the wound of it, Veyra thought, watching the smoke rise from her life's work. Not the defeat. Not the stripping of office. It was that everything Isandro was—the rigor, the stubbornness, the terrible habit of keeping promises even unto burning—Veyra had put there. You could break a stranger and feel only triumph. Breaking your own handiwork was another arithmetic entirely.

* * *

On the fourth day, the fire reached the sealed annex.

Veyra felt it before anyone told her—a wrongness in the air, a pressure behind the ears, like the moment before lightning. She was halfway down the corridor before she'd decided to move, which was unlike her; Veyra Okafor-Ishikawa had never in her life moved before deciding to. But her feet knew what was in the annex. Her feet had carried her past that door ten thousand times.

Sefu was already there, standing in front of the vaulted iron door with his palm flat against it, and beyond the metal something was screaming. Not loudly. That was the worst part—it screamed the way an animal too exhausted to scream properly screams, a thin thread of sound, patient, ancient.

"It's in there?" Sefu asked, without turning. He didn't need to ask. His whole body was turned toward the door like a compass needle.

"The First Flame," Veyra said. "Or what we kept of it. The founders called it the Reserve. We drew on it for—" she stopped. Recalculated. Told the truth, because lying had become physically difficult since the Emberhold, since she had seen fire answer freely given love and understood that everything she'd built ran on the opposite currency. "We stole it. We drew on stolen fire for a hundred and forty years. Every storm in every bottle. Every borrowed memory. All of it was this, diluted and portioned and sold."

The screaming thinned. Sefu's jaw worked. When he spoke, his voice had the careful flatness of a man holding a door shut against grief. "Isandro says you'll open it."

"If she asks me to."

"She's asking you to."

They opened it together, which neither of them commented on. Inside, in a lead-lined chamber that had cost the guild a fortune and its conscience, the First Flame guttered in a basin of black iron—no bigger than a cooking fire, no brighter, and dying. Around it the air shimmered with everything it had been: storms, summers, the memory of wings, a whole world's worth of wonder compressed to a coal the size of two fists.

Sefu knelt. Veyra stood. She found, examining herself, that she could not kneel—not from pride, exactly, but because kneeling would be a performance, and she had sworn off performances somewhere between the Emberhold and the chicory.

"We have to bring it to her," Sefu whispered. "Ashveth. It has to go home or it dies here, and if it dies here—"

"Then every bottle empties," Veyra finished. "Every returned wonder gutters out mid-return. Yes. I did the accounting."

For the first time he looked at her directly, and his eyes were wet and furious and old. "How can you do that? Stand there with your sums while it screams?"

"Because the sums are how I know how much time we have." Her voice came out level, though something under it was shaking. "Fourteen hours, Warden Sefu, at current burn rate, less if we jostle it. The harbor is six hours' walk. Which leaves us eight hours to explain to a dying dragon why the woman who ran the theft is carrying her fire home." She set down her cup of chicory on the floor, deliberately, like closing a ledger. "So pick up the basin. And let us not waste my best remaining talent on recriminations."

* * *

They walked it through the city at dusk, and the city watched.

Veyra had expected stones. She had, privately, stocked up on dignity against them. But the rain-slicked streets emptied instead—shopkeeps stepping back into their doorways, children pulled up onto balconies, a fishwife crossing herself with a wet hand—and the silence that followed them was worse and better than hatred. It was the silence of people watching a woman carry a small screaming fire toward the harbor, and understanding, in their bones, what it meant about everything they'd ever bought.

Nour fell in step beside her somewhere around Tanner's Row, walking backward so she could face Veyra, hands in her pockets.

"So," Nour said. "Guildmaster."

"Not anymore."

"Right. So, *not-Guildmaster*. Question." She jerked her chin at the basin Sefu carried between them, wrapped now in Veyra's own ceremonial cloak, which Veyra had given up without comment because the cloak was lined with wool and wool, presumably, soothed stolen fire. "When you bottled things. Storms. Memories. My mother's face—I bought that back, by the way, don't think I've forgotten whose inventory it came out of—"

"I remember the lot number," Veyra said quietly. "Two-one-nine. I approved the sale personally."

Nour blinked. Whatever she'd expected—deflection, justification—it wasn't the lot number. She stopped walking backward. "You *approved* it?"

"I approved fourteen thousand, four hundred and six sales. Yours was one of them. I will not insult you by pretending yours stands out, but I will tell you this: it shouldn't have needed to stand out. One was already too many, and I knew it, and I did the arithmetic anyway, and the arithmetic always came out in favor of the guild." Veyra kept her eyes forward, on the harbor lights. "You want to know how it happened. That's how. Not monsters. Bookkeeping."

Nour was silent for half a street. Then, in a smaller voice: "My mother's face. Did you ever see it? In the bottle."

"No. We never looked at what we sold. Looking made clerks quit."

"*Looking made clerks quit,*" Nour repeated, softly, tasting each word like something spoiled. Then she laughed—one sharp note, not kind—and lengthened her stride to walk beside Sefu instead, and Veyra let her go, and understood that this too was a payment, and that she would be making payments of this kind for the rest of her life, and that this was correct.

* * *

Ashveth received the First Flame beneath the harbor, in the salt-crusted dark, and what happened then Veyra would spend the rest of her life failing to describe.

The dragon did not roar. She *inhaled*—a breath that lasted a full minute, drawing the flame home the way a tide draws water—and the rusted shell around her rang like a struck bell, and every bottle in the city, every jar and vial and stoppered phial in every returned crate, sang back in answer. Veyra heard it through the stone of the harbor stairs. She heard the widow Ammas's memory going home. She heard the drowned choir's song-jars filling. Somewhere in the counting hall, she was certain, the last unburnt ledgers were curling at their edges, their ink lifting off the pages like birds startled into flight.

Then Ashveth turned her head, and one eye the color of banked coals found Veyra in the gloom, and the dragon spoke inside her skull with a voice like a landslide deciding to be gentle.

*"The bookkeeper."*

"Former bookkeeper."

*"You kept my fire in a box for forty years and you are carrying it back in a cloak. Explain yourself."*

Veyra opened her mouth to give the speech—the drafted one, the dignified one—and found she couldn't. What came out instead was smaller and true. "I believed order was worth anything. I learned tonight that it wasn't. There is no explanation that isn't an excuse, so I won't offer one. I'll offer the work instead. Fourteen thousand returns. Catalogued, verified, delivered. My hand on every label."

The eye regarded her for a long time. Coal-light flickered in it. Somewhere far above, gulls complained about the disturbance, because gulls are gulls in every age.

*"Hm,"* said Ashveth, which was somehow both the most terrifying and most forgiving sound Veyra had ever heard. *"The girl chose her family well. Even the difficult ones."*

* * *

The last crate went out on the ninth day.

It was a small one—two items only—and Veyra carried it herself to a narrow house on Bellwether Lane, because the label was addressed in her own hand and she had promised her hand would finish what it started. She knocked. The door opened, and Isandro stood there in the lamplight with flour on her sleeves, because apparently the last swordspeaker in the world spent her evenings baking, and the domesticity of it undid something in Veyra's chest that she had no ledger column for.

"Bellwether Lane," Isandro said. "You remembered."

"I remember everything. It's my curse." Veyra held out the crate. "One memory of a mother's kitchen, pawned in the famine year by a woman who needed bread money. And one promise, unwritten, which the ledger never had a column for and therefore lost. I'm returning both."

Isandro took the crate. For a moment neither of them spoke. Down the lane, someone was playing a fiddle badly, and the smell of Veyra's own burnt ledgers still hung faintly over the rooftops like incense.

"The waystation," Veyra said finally. "The one you're planning. Out past the Salt Gate, where the roads fork. Nour talks about it constantly when she thinks I'm not listening, and I am always listening, it's the other curse."

"What about it?"

"I can catalogue." Veyra's voice was dry, but her hands were not quite steady, and she hated that Isandro noticed. "Fourteen thousand entries taught me what everything in this world is worth. I find I'd like to spend whatever years remain learning what things are worth when nobody writes them down. Promises witnessed aloud, I'm told. No ledgers." She paused. "I'm told the staff is difficult. A menace of a pickpocket. A disgraced clerk. A warden who cries at warehouses."

"And a former guildmaster," Isandro said.

"If she's wanted."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment—the woman who had raised her, lied to her, hunted her, and carried fire home in a wool-lined cloak—and then stepped aside from the doorway, and the light and the smell of baking spilled out into the lane like something returned.

"There's a spare room," Isandro said. "It's small. The window sticks."

"In my experience," said Veyra Okafor-Ishikawa, former guildmaster of the Ledger Guild, stepping across the threshold of the smallest, warmest room of her life, "all the best rooms have a flaw in the contract."

Behind them, far away beneath the harbor, something vast and ancient turned over in its sleep, and its flame burned bright and free and belonged to no one—and in the morning, the road out past the Salt Gate would be waiting, and the forks in it, and everyone who stayed.
