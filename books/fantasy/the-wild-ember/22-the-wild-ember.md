## 22. The Wild Ember

The first snow of the season arrived the way good news always did at the waystation: quietly, without asking permission.

Isandro noticed it from the kitchen window while kneading bread—flakes drifting past the glass like ash that had forgotten how to burn. She wiped her hands on her apron and stood there a moment, watching the mountain road below fill with white. Three years ago, that road had been nothing but goat tracks and old fear. Now it was worn smooth by cart wheels, by pilgrims, by dragons who preferred walking when they had somewhere unhurried to be.

"You'll ruin it," Tam called from the doorway behind her, "if you keep opening the door in this cold."

"I'm not opening the door."

"You're thinking about it. Your shoulders do a thing."

"My shoulders don't do things."

"They absolutely do." He came up beside her, gray at the temples now, spectacles perpetually smudged, holding two cups of cider he'd clearly made for himself and was grudgingly willing to share. "You have expressive shoulders. It's how I knew you'd stay, back at the canal house. You had shoulders like someone deciding."

Isandro took the cider because arguing with Tam about her own body had never once gone well. "I was deciding whether to throw you out of my flophouse."

"And look at you now. Throwing me bread instead." He nodded toward the loaves cooling on the rack. "Growth."

She laughed despite herself—the easy laugh, the one she hadn't owned before all of this—and went back to the dough. Outside, the wind came down off the peaks carrying woodsmoke and pine and, faintly, the deep mineral smell of dragon. That last part never quite left the air here anymore. The whole valley breathed it, the way a house breathes the person who lives in it.

The waystation sat where the Emberhold's ruins used to be. They'd kept the bones of it—the great hearth-wall, some of the oath-hall columns—and built the rest out of salvaged guild timber and Nour's shameless scavenging. It wasn't grand. It was a wide-roofed hall with bunks along one side, a long trading table down the middle, a stable that could accommodate anything from a mule to a young wyvern, and a porch swing that Sefu had installed for reasons he refused to explain and everyone else pretended not to find endearing.

No ledgers. That had been the first rule, written into the new covenant itself: *no accounts kept against the soul*. Trades happened across the table in plain sight, witnessed aloud by whoever happened to be present, sealed by a spoken promise and, if both parties were feeling ceremonial, a drop of wine or tea shared from the same cup. Isandro had expected chaos. She had spent her whole life believing that without ledgers there would be only theft.

Instead there was mostly tea, arguments about fair prices conducted with surprising courtesy, and an elderly stonemason from the lowlands who came every month to trade songs with a river-drake and always left weeping with joy.

"Company on the road," Tam said, peering past her through the frosted pane.

Isandro followed his gaze. Two figures were climbing the switchbacks—one human-sized, wrapped in a coat that had been fashionable in another decade, the other enormous and slow and the color of banked coals.

"Ashveth," Isandro said, already untying her apron.

"She walked all the way up in the snow? Her joints—"

"She likes the walk. She says it gives her time to think about what she'll complain about when she arrives."

* * *

Ashveth came through the gate as the light was failing, shaking snow from her wings with a sound like a hundred shutters slamming, and the whole waystation seemed to lean toward her warmth. Even after three years, even knowing her—knowing her jokes, her terrible opinions about human music, the way she snored hard enough to rattle the shutters—there was still a moment, every time, when Isandro's chest tightened with something between awe and grief. The dragon who should have died guttering under the harbor. The flame that four people had carried back to life, one piece each.

"Small swordspeaker," Ashveth rumbled, lowering her great head so their eyes were level. Her voice filled the courtyard the way thunder fills a valley. "I have traveled nine days through your terrible mountains to deliver a message of vital importance."

Isandro braced. "What is it?"

"The marmalade you sent with the autumn caravan was inferior to last year's. I am deeply wounded. I may never recover."

"It was the same recipe!"

"Then the oranges were disloyal." But her eyes—old gold, patient, seeing far too much—softened as she said it, and she touched her brow gently to Isandro's forehead, which was Ashveth's way of hugging and also of checking, Isandro suspected, whether she'd been sleeping enough. "It is good to see you, Isandro Okafor. You are eating. Good. You are smiling. Better."

Behind the dragon, the second traveler pushed back her hood, and Isandro felt her heart do the complicated thing it always did.

Veyra looked older. Of course she did; they all did. But the hardness in her face had been slowly replaced by something more weathered, more honest—like driftwood that had finally stopped fighting the sea. She still wore gray. Some habits were load-bearing. But the gray coat was patched at the elbows and stained with travel dust, and she carried her own pack, and three years ago none of those things would have been possible.

"Guildmaster," Isandro said, because teasing Veyra about her former title never stopped being worth it.

"Retired guildmaster," Veyra corrected, stamping snow off her boots with the precision of a woman who would be orderly about something if it killed her. "And I'm told I'm late for dinner, which is the sort of scandal that follows a person for decades in small communities."

"You're not late. Dinner's not ready."

"Then I'm early, which is worse." Veyra handed over a wrapped bundle with stiff formality, as though surrendering a weapon. "From the lowlands. The restoration catalogs. The final volume is printed."

Isandro took it carefully. The *Catalogue of Returns*—every stolen wonder traced to its source, every debt mapped for giving back. Four years of Veyra's penance, done voluntarily, meticulously, with the same terrifying competence she'd once brought to hunting Isandro through the rain. They were nearly finished. A handful of items remained unreturned—a bottled storm whose original sky no longer existed, a few memories whose owners had died—and Veyra had taken to visiting those orphans personally, keeping them company, as if objects could feel abandoned.

Isandro was fairly sure they could.

"Dinner in an hour," she said. "Wash up. Both of you. And Ashveth—you're not sleeping in the courtyard again, it's snowing."

"I am a creature of ancient majesty."

"You're sleeping in the barn where the stove is."

The dragon sighed with such theatrical suffering that the nearest window rattled. "...Where the stove is," she conceded.

* * *

Dinner at the waystation was, by design and by accident, the opposite of everything the Ledger Guild had been.

The long table had no head. Whoever cooked served first—which tonight meant Isandro, which meant Ashveth got the first plate and pretended this was merely correct rather than arranged. Nour sat sideways on the bench with her boots tucked under her, telling the story of the salt-gate merchant who'd tried to shortchange a pilgrim until Nour had quietly returned the man's own pawned memory of his wedding day, mid-argument, just to watch him forget what he was angry about.

"He cried," she reported, delighted. "Right there in the market. Big grown man, crying over his wife's face. Then he paid the pilgrim double and apologized to a chicken."

"That's not the trade we teach," Tam said mildly.

"It's exactly the trade we teach. Mercy as strength. I read the plaque."

"I wrote the plaque."

"And it's very good. You've grown as a writer."

Sefu, at the far end, laughed low in his chest—the laugh he'd learned from Ashveth, or maybe she'd learned it from him; at this point nobody could remember. The warden had filled out over the years, his grief settling into something that lived beside him instead of inside him. He still went down to the harbor sometimes, to the place where Ashveth's shell of salt and rust had been, and sat awhile with the water. But he came back lighter each time. Last spring he'd come back with a plan for teaching harbor-fishermen the old warding songs, so the sea itself would know its debts were paid.

"So," Nour said, when the plates were cleared and the cider came around. "Solstice is next week. Everyone's coming?"

"Everyone who can," Sefu said. "The northern clans will send word by drake-flight. The drowned choir asked to attend—they've been practicing. On land. Ishandro, I want you to know that in advance, so you can prepare emotionally for the sound."

"How bad?"

"There are forty of them. They have not yet mastered air."

"Forty," Isandro said faintly, and Tam patted her hand with genuine sympathy.

They talked logistics a while—the extra bunks, the stores, whether the barn stove could survive Ashveth plus forty ghosts plus whatever strays wandered in from the mountain roads, because strays always did. The waystation had developed a reputation among a certain kind of person: the displaced, the odd, the ones who didn't fit the ledgers anymore. A woman who traded weather-wisdom for stories. Twin brothers who spoke only in rhyme since the war. A retired enforcer-captain named Doran who showed up every solstice with honey cakes and left every solstice having cried at least twice, and whom nobody ever mentioned crying, because that was the deal.

Later, when the fire burned low and Ashveth's breathing had gone slow and tidal in the barn, and Tam and Veyra argued amiably over a game of stones, Isandro slipped away to the small room at the end of the hall that had once been the guildhouse strongroom.

It was her study now. One desk. One lamp. And on the desk, a book.

It had started as a joke, or maybe a dare—Nour's idea, naturally. *"You were the best bookkeeper the guild ever had. Write us a ledger. A real one."* So Isandro had bought a blank book from a paper-seller in the valley town, good heavy pages, and for three years she had tried to write in it.

Not a ledger. She couldn't. Her hands refused; the old training rose up in her fingers like scar tissue whenever she tried to set numbers against names. What belonged in a book that wasn't a ledger, in a life that wasn't an accounting? She'd written fragments. The recipe for the bread that always worked. The words of the new covenant, spoken at the reforging, copied out in her careful clerk's hand:

*We owe each other our care, freely given and freely received. No debt shall bind a soul that love has not already bound. What is witnessed aloud is true. What is returned is ours together.*

The words of the four flames, too—Nour's mercy, Tam's confession, Sefu's song, Veyra's letting go. She'd interviewed each of them about that night at the Emberhold, formally, taking notes, and they'd teased her mercilessly for it and then, one by one, come to her quietly afterward to make sure she'd gotten it right. Because it mattered to them too. Because stories were the only inheritance anyone had offered them that they'd wanted to keep.

Tonight, with the snow falling soft past the window and the sounds of her family murmuring down the hall, Isandro sat at the desk and turned to the last blank page.

She thought about writing the ending. The covenant reforged, the ledgers burned, the guild dissolved into something kinder. The wild ember passed from hand to hand and somehow never going out. She thought about summing it up, closing the covers, setting the book on the shelf with the weight of a completed thing.

But endings were a kind of ledger too, weren't they? A final entry that refused further entries. And she had spent enough of her life inside closed books.

Down the hall, Nour laughed at something—her real laugh, the loud one, the one she'd reclaimed along with everything else. The floorboards creaked under Tam's pacing. Wind moved through the valley, and beneath it, faint and constant as a heartbeat, the great warm presence of a dragon who was supposed to be dead, snoring in the barn.

Isandro dipped her pen.

She wrote one line, in the center of the page, and let the rest stand open—snowfall and lamplight and all the unwritten mornings pressing gently against the margins:

***This story belongs to everyone who stayed.***

She sat back and read it, and found, to her complete lack of surprise, that it was true. Not balanced—true. There was a difference, and she'd crossed a continent and burned a guild to learn it.

A knock at the doorframe. Veyra stood there, gray-haired, lantern in hand, looking for all the world like a woman checking on a daughter who'd stayed up too late working.

"Come to bed," Veyra said. "The mountain isn't going anywhere. Neither is the book."

"No," Isandro agreed, capping the ink. "But tomorrow might surprise us."

Veyra smiled—the real smile, still rare, still worth waiting for—and held the lantern high, and together they walked down the hall toward the firelight, leaving the page open behind them, waiting, like the road, like the snow, like everything, for morning.
