## 2. The Crown's Long Shadow

Ivo woke to the smell of beeswax and someone humming badly.

The humming was the first good sign. Hunters did not hum. Hunters, in his limited and painful experience, preferred silence, torchlight, and the particular creak of a bowstring being drawn back at dawn. Humming meant a kitchen, or an infirmary, or — he opened his eyes a slit and confirmed it — a temple infirmary, all whitewashed stone and shelves of labeled jars, with sunlight falling through high windows in dusty golden columns.

The second good sign was that his hands were empty but unbound.

The third was the box. Someone had set the Emberbox on the table beside his cot, exactly where he'd fallen clutching it, as though it were a personal effect and not the most dangerous object in the known world. It sat there, small and iron-banded and utterly ordinary-looking, radiating nothing at all, which was its own kind of miracle. Whatever wards the valley wove through everything here, they were holding.

His shoulder, on the other hand, was not holding anything so gracefully. It throbbed in slow, sick pulses, and when he shifted, the bandages there pulled tight over skin that felt wrong — hot in patches, cold in others, like weather moving through him instead of blood.

"You're awake," said the bad hummer.

She was old and round and had flour on her sleeves despite clearly being no baker, and she looked at him the way people look at a stray dog that has come indoors during a storm: tolerant, wary, already calculating how much it will cost to keep him.

"Sister Ondra," she said, tapping her own chest. "You collapsed at our gate three days ago. Fevered. Talking to your box."

"It talks first," Ivo rasped.

Ondra's eyebrows went up, then came down in the flat, deliberate way of a woman deciding not to have heard something. "Drink this." She pressed a cup into his hands. It tasted of willow bark and honey and something faintly metallic underneath, which worried him more than the fever had. "The elders want you fit enough to answer questions by tomorrow. So get fit."

"Questions," Ivo said. "From whom?"

"The Conclave of Elders." She said it the way one says *the weather* — a fact of the valley, neither good nor bad. Then, softer, almost against her will: "And the scribe who dragged you inside. Paloma. They carried half your weight up from the gate and got burned for it. Their hands blistered where they touched you. Fever burns, we called it, when such things happened long ago." She fixed him with a look. "Nothing like that happens in Vell anymore. Understand? Nothing. Whatever you brought through our passes, you'll leave it at the door when you go."

"And if I can't?"

Ondra didn't answer that. She gathered her jars, and at the doorway she paused, back to him, and said, "Eat the bread before it goes stale. Wasting bread in this house is a sin worse than whatever you are," and left.

Ivo lay back and considered the ceiling.

*Whatever you are.* He'd been many things in thirty-one years: courier, liar, son of a burned woman, keeper of a box that whispered. He had never before been a patient, and he found he disliked it intensely. Patients waited. Waiting was how people died.

He ate the bread anyway. It was very good bread, which made everything worse somehow. Places with good bread deserved better than what was coming for them.

* * *

They came for him at dusk — two elders flanking the sister, and behind them, hovering in the doorway with the specific posture of someone who has been told to stay out and is ignoring it, the scribe.

Paloma. He remembered fragments: narrow hands, ink-stained fingers, an expression caught between terror and stubbornness as they'd hauled him upright at the gate. Now, upright and fever-free, they stood with a ledger hugged to their chest like a shield and watched him with eyes too sharp for a temple scribe. Eyes that kept flicking to the Emberbox and away again, quick as a tongue testing a broken tooth.

"I am Elder Corvane," said the tall one, gray-robed, voice like dry paper. "This is Elder Mott."

"We know what you carry," said Mott, who was short and wide and had the face of a man perpetually chewing on a sentence he couldn't afford to swallow.

"Do you?" Ivo sat up slowly, favoring the shoulder. "Because I've met six people who claimed to, and five of them were wrong, and the sixth is why my shoulder looks like this."

"Show us," Corvane said.

Ivo looked at the box. The box, being iron and secrets, said nothing. "It doesn't open for just anyone," he said. "That's rather the point of it."

"Then tell us what it is."

And here was the fork in the road, the one he'd been lying awake mapping since dawn. He could lie — say it was documents, heirlooms, a dead man's letters. Lies bought time; time was the only currency he'd ever reliably earned. Or he could tell these quiet, wax-smelling people the truth and watch their faces do what faces always did when the old war walked into the room wearing its Sunday clothes.

He thought about Paloma's hands. Blistered. *Fever burns.*

He told the truth.

"It's called the Emberbox," he said. "It predates the war. Some say it started the war — both sides claim that, which tells you something. Inside it is the last seed of wild magic. Not temple-blessed, not crown-licensed, not valley-hidden. Wild. The kind that used to grow everywhere, before it was uprooted and burned and fenced into reservations and finally, mostly, killed." He let that sit. "The crown wants it destroyed. The hunters want it destroyed. A great many gifted want it destroyed too, because a seed can be planted, and planted things grow in directions nobody controls. I was asked to bring it somewhere it might survive. That's all I am. A gardener with poor judgment."

Silence. Mott had stopped chewing his sentence. Corvane's hands were folded, knuckles white.

"That's not all," said Paloma, from the doorway.

Everyone turned. The scribe had gone pale but held their ground, ledger clutched tighter. "You said 'asked.' Asked by whom? And the men chasing you — they're not bandits. I saw their colors at the pass yesterday. Gray and silver. That's the Crown's Long Shadow." They pronounced it carefully, a phrase learned from books. "Witch-hunters don't chase smugglers."

Corvane closed his eyes briefly, as if asking some valley-specific deity for patience. "Thank you, Paloma. You may return to the scriptorium."

"They reached the passes," Paloma said, not moving. "Elder. Nobody reaches the passes. That's the whole— everyone says the valley can't be found by—" They stopped themselves, but the shape of the sentence hung in the air, unfinished and terrible.

*By anything that hunts magic,* it would have ended. Everyone said it. Ivo had been counting on it.

"Three days ago," Corvane said heavily, "a trapper reported fires in the high pass. Cook-fires. Disciplined ones. Yesterday, a rider in gray turned back at the border stones without crossing — without *needing* to cross, because the trapper also reports they were drawing maps." He opened his eyes and looked at Ivo, and there was no anger in it, only a vast tired grief, and somehow that was worse. "So. You will tell us plainly. If they take you, and the box — what happens to Vell?"

Ivo weighed it. He was, by trade and temperament, a man who lied to protect people; it was practically a devotional practice. But Corvane had asked plainly, and there are lies so large they curdle in the mouth.

"They won't burn the valley," he said. "Burning is for the guilty. They'll do worse. They'll make it useful. A valley where magic cannot follow is worth more standing than ash — a vault, a prison, a leash-length. They'll garrison the passes, tax the temple into dependence, and every gifted soul born here will grow up never knowing what they are, because knowing would be inconvenient." He paused. "Some of them already don't know, do they? What this place does?"

Paloma made a small sound. No one looked at them.

"Elder Mott," Corvane said quietly, "call the Conclave. Full vote. Tonight."

* * *

Ivo was not invited to the vote, which surprised no one, least of all him. He spent it sitting on his cot listening to the muffled rise and fall of voices through two walls, trying to read the argument the way you'd read weather by the sound of wind. There were long stretches of low murmur — procedure, probably, the comfortable liturgy of committees everywhere — punctuated by sharper bursts. Once, a woman's voice, cracked and furious: *"—took them in, Anselm, we took them IN—"* And once, terribly, laughter. Nervous, apologetic laughter, the kind that means someone has made a joke about the unthinkable to make it thinkable.

The Emberbox sat on the table beside him. Under the bandages, his shoulder had gone from throbbing to burning, a slow crawl of heat down toward his elbow, and he knew what that was. He'd watched it take Marta the same way, in the reed marshes, four days after the arrow. The wound wasn't a wound. It was a question the poison kept asking — *where is the seed, where is the seed* — and the box answered it, feeding him just enough life to keep the question from becoming a verdict. Which meant that if the elders voted to surrender him, they would have to surrender the box too, because he would die without it, and he was not entirely sure he'd mind except that dead couriers make terrible gardeners and the seed deserved better.

He was still composing arguments when the doors below opened and the murmur resolved into footsteps, dispersing, and then one set of footsteps climbed toward him and paused outside his door.

It was Paloma. Of course it was Paloma. They stood in the corridor holding a candle, ink still on their fingers at this hour, and their face had gone through several weathers since dusk.

"They voted," Ivo said.

"At dawn, formally. But it's decided." Paloma's voice was flat, rehearsed-flat, the flatness of someone holding a lid on a pot. "Surrender both strangers to the crown's representatives at the border stones. In exchange, the hunters withdraw from the passes and the valley's charter is renewed for another hundred years." A breath. "Peace. They're calling it peace."

Ivo nodded slowly. It was a fair bargain, honestly. Two lives against a hundred years of safety for however many hundreds lived here. Any reasonable person would—

"They're going to give me to them too," Paloma said, and now the lid came off the pot, just a crack. "Not because I did anything. Because I touched you. Because my hands blistered, and Sister Ondra reported it, because she *had* to, it's the law here, and now there's a record, and the elders say a recorded gift in a charter valley is a liability, and—" They stopped. Pressed the heel of their hand against their eye. "I've lived here twenty-two years. I copied ledgers until my debt was supposed to end next spring. Next *spring.*"

The candle flame stood perfectly still between them, and Ivo realized, with a small cold drop in his stomach, that it was standing still on purpose. That the air around it had gone taut, the way air does around the box sometimes. Paloma hadn't noticed. Paloma was looking at the floor.

"I'm sorry," Ivo said, and meant it more than he'd meant most things. "For the blisters. For all of it."

"Don't apologize." Paloma looked up, and their eyes had that sharpness again, that broken-tooth glance, aimed now at the Emberbox. "Tell me the truth instead. You're good at that, apparently, when it matters. Tell me what happens to a debt-bound scribe when the witch-hunters collect them. Tell me exactly, because I've read the histories, and the histories use words like *questioned* and *processed* and nobody ever writes down what comes after."

Ivo opened his mouth to lie — gently, kindly, the way you'd pull a splinter —

and the candle went out.

Not blew out. Went out, snuffed by nothing, and in the dark he heard Paloma's breath catch, and heard them whisper, half to him and half to the dark itself:

"Dawn," they said. "They come at dawn. That gives us one night."

The word *us* landed in the darkness between them, warm and terrified and entirely accidental, and neither of them took it back.
