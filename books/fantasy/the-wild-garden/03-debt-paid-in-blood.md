## 3. Debt Paid in Blood

Mother Osha found Paloma in the copying room at the hour when the candles guttered and the ink went sluggish, which was to say: exactly when she knew Paloma would be alone, and exactly when Paloma's hands would be too tired to argue.

She did not knock. She never knocked. She had raised Paloma from the age of six in this valley of Vell, where doors were for outsiders and mothers came and went like weather.

"You're still on the Harrow ledgers," she said, peering at the page. "You've been on them three days."

"The columns don't balance," Paloma said.

"Ledgers never balance here. That's rather the point of a debt." She settled onto the stool across the desk with the grace of a woman who had sat through forty years of council meetings and could make stillness look like a weapon. Her hands folded. Her eyes — pale as river-washed stones — did not fold at all.

Paloma kept writing. The quill scratched. Somewhere below, in the infirmary, Ivo slept or didn't sleep, fevered under wool blankets, one hand even unconscious curled protectively over that sealed box he'd carried through the gates like a man carrying his own heart in a lockbox.

"He's going to die," Mother Osha said conversationally. "The wound, the fever, the box. Any of the three will do it. The hunters coming down the pass will only hurry matters along."

"I know."

"And you've been sitting up with him. Feeding him willow bark and lies about how the elders are deliberating."

"I wouldn't call it lying. I'd call it optimistic summarizing."

Something flickered at the corner of Mother Osha's mouth — the ghost of the woman who had once let six-year-old Paloma put a frog in the collection basin because "the gods appreciate variety" — and then it was gone, pressed flat beneath duty like a flower in a ledger.

"Here is what the council has decided," she said. "Tomorrow at dawn, two strangers walk out of Vell to meet the crown's riders. In exchange, the valley keeps its silence, and we keep our children. It is not a decision anyone loves. It is simply the smallest number of funerals available to us."

Paloma set down the quill very carefully, because if they set it down carelessly it might snap, and then their hands would have nothing to do, and then everyone present would see what they were actually feeling.

"And me?" they asked.

"You're not part of the arrangement. You're ours." She paused, and her voice gentled into something worse than cruelty: kindness. "But there is another matter. Your debt. Eleven years of copying, and you're perhaps a third paid. Another twenty years, at the rate you go. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless your account were closed by an act of exceptional service to the temple." She let that sit in the air between them, fat and patient as a spider. "Bring the box to the council tonight. Hand over the man who carries it, quietly, before dawn, so no one has to see him walk. And tomorrow you wake free, Paloma. No ledgers. No debt. Your own name on your own life."

The candle between them guttered — and relit itself, flame leaping back up without being asked, the way it had been doing around Paloma for years now, small impossible courtesies the temple pretended not to notice.

Mother Osha looked at the candle. Then at Paloma. Something moved behind her stone-pale eyes that Paloma couldn't read, and had never been able to read, and suddenly understood they had run out of time to learn how.

"You always said magic couldn't cross into Vell," Paloma said quietly.

"I said what kept us safe," Mother Osha said. "Those are not always the same sentence."

She rose, smoothed her robes, and left the door open behind her — whether in invitation or in warning, Paloma genuinely could not tell.

* * *

Ivo was awake when Paloma came down, propped against the wall of the infirmary cot, gray-faced and sweating but with his eyes tracking everything like a cat pretending to be asleep.

"Well?" he rasped. "Did the kindly grandmothers vote to murder me, or just deport me?"

"They voted for a quiet version of both. There's a discount for efficiency." Paloma sat on the edge of the cot and told him all of it — the dawn surrender, the trade, the debt, the offer. Their voice stayed level right up until it didn't. "Twenty years, Ivo. Twenty more years of copying other people's accounts while my whole life sits in someone else's column. All I have to do is carry a box downstairs."

Ivo studied them for a long moment. He had a way of looking at people as though reading fine print.

"But you're telling me instead," he said.

"Don't read anything into it."

"Wouldn't dream of it." A cough bent him double; when he straightened, there was blood on his lips and a terrible brightness in his face. "For the record — if you carry that box downstairs, I won't fight you. I'm barely a person anymore, I'm mostly infection with opinions. But the box doesn't go to the crown. Whatever else happens. Swear it or don't, but know that I'll crawl to the ravine first."

"Why? What's in it?"

He almost smiled. "A reason the world used to be bigger. And a reason certain people want it smaller." His hand slid off the box, trembling. "Ask me again when I'm not leaking. Ask me —"

His eyes rolled back. The fever took him mid-sentence, and Paloma caught him before his head hit the wall, and held him, and felt the strange warmth in their own palms — that hum, that itch of light under the skin they'd spent eleven years pretending was a nervous condition — reach toward him like a plant toward a window.

The wound in his side, where the first arrow had gone, glowed faintly under the bandage. Not healing. *Feeding.* Or being fed on. Paloma couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing frightened them more than the hunters did.

They laid him down. They stood up. They walked out of the infirmary, and instead of turning right, toward the council chamber and their freedom, they turned left, toward the scriptorium, where the travel seals were kept in a locked drawer that Paloma had copied the key for four years ago purely out of scribe's boredom.

Guilt walked beside them the whole way, wearing Mother Osha's face. Exhilaration ran ahead, laughing. Paloma had never known the two could share a body. Apparently theirs had room.

* * *

The night markets of Vell opened at moonrise and were gone by dawn, stalls of smuggled cinnamon and secondhand miracles pitched in the temple's long shadow by vendors who owed the temple nothing and liked it that way. Paloma moved through them with Ivo half-dressed in a borrowed acolyte's robe, his arm over their shoulders, both of them doing a remarkable impression of pilgrims.

"That one's watching us," Ivo murmured.

"The pickle seller?"

"The pickle seller is a professional. I mean the one by the lantern stall. Gray coat. Been parallel to us for three turns."

"Maybe he likes pickles."

"Nobody likes pickles that much."

They cut through the tannery row, where the smell discouraged pursuit and conversation equally, and came out at the old grain gate — unwatched, because no one left Vell, that was the whole architecture of the place. The gate's lock was ceremonial; the seals were the real door. Paloma pressed the stolen temple seals against the post and whispered the activation words they'd copied a hundred times without ever expecting to use.

The seal flared green. The gate sighed open like an old man lowering into a bath.

"Oh," said Ivo softly, staring at the dark road beyond, the first road out of the valley either of them had seen. "Oh, that's — I haven't been outside since —" He stopped. Swallowed whatever memory it was. "Lead on, scribe."

They made it four hundred yards.

The arrow came out of the dark with a sound like a snapped harp string and took Ivo high in the shoulder, spinning him against Paloma, and the night tore open with shouts — two, three hunters rising from the ditches where gray coats wait, bows drawn, and behind them lanterns swinging up the road from the direction of the temple, because of course Mother Osha had sent word ahead, or the council had, or the valley itself had smelled betrayal the way it smelled everything.

Paloma did the only thing their body knew how to do.

They screamed — and the scream came out *lit*. Light poured off their skin like water off a struck bell, wild and white and utterly unpracticed, and the nearest hunter's torch died, and his bowstring snapped like it had been cut, and the horses downstream panicked and bolted, and for three glorious, horrifying seconds the night belonged entirely to Paloma Vane, temple scribe, debt-bound nobody, blazing like a small furious sun.

Then it was out, and they were running, dragging Ivo, whose blood soaked warm and wrong through the borrowed robe.

"Left," Ivo gasped. "Ravine path — the hunters won't —" Another cough, wetter than the last. "Won't follow past the border stones. They think the valley eats —"

"Eats what?"

"Everything. Go!"

They went. Down through bracken and shale, Ivo's weight increasing with every step as his legs quit cooperating, until they reached the ravine floor and the old border stones, moss-eaten things carved with warnings in a language the temple called decorative.

Paloma hauled Ivo behind the largest stone and got the robe open and found the wound.

It was bad. Worse than bad. The arrow had torn deep, and the flesh around it was gray-veined, black at the edges, creeping outward even as Paloma watched — and no light answered Paloma's hands this time. They pressed their palms to the wound and pulled at that inner warmth with everything they had, begging it, ordering it, and it rose sluggishly, dimly, like a lamp running out of oil, and the wound drank every drop and gave back nothing.

"It won't close," Paloma whispered. "Why won't it close?"

Ivo laughed — a thin, awful sound — and fumbled the sealed box around, pressing it weakly against his own chest above the wound. The gray veins slowed. Did not stop. Slowed.

"Because we crossed the border," he breathed, "and the land behind us is still drinking. Because the thing keeping me alive isn't your gift, scribe. It's this." He tapped the box with two fingers that barely worked. "So we have a problem. I need what's inside. The crown needs what's inside. And you—" his fever-bright eyes found Paloma's, and despite everything, he grinned, "—you just lit up like a festival lantern in front of three witnesses. So I'd say we're even. Debt paid in blood, and all that."

Somewhere above them, along the rim of the ravine, a hunting horn sounded — long, patient, and answered by another much closer than it should have been.

Paloma looked at the box. At the dying stranger. At the road south, where rumor said a smuggler-cartographer sold passage to anyone with coin or a good story, and Paloma had neither, but they did have a stolen seal, a burning secret, and — for the first time in eleven years — a choice already made.

"Hold on," they told Ivo, and hoisted him up. "We're going to find someone who sells maps and bad decisions."

The horn sounded again. Closer.

They ran.
