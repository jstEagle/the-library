## 4. Where Magic Cannot Follow

The border of Vell was, in all Paloma's twenty-three years, a thing they had never once seen.

It was marked by nothing so grand as a wall or a gate. Just a line of standing stones — eleven of them, gray and lichen-eaten, marching up the shoulder of the mountain like old men who had forgotten why they'd gathered. The temple maps called it the Quiet Line. The temple elders called it protection. *Nothing crosses uninvited*, they said. *Nothing gifted can follow you here.*

Paloma had believed it the way one believes the sun rises: without thought, without gratitude, because it had simply always been true.

They paused at the first stone, Ivo leaning heavily against their shoulder, and felt something they had no word for yet. A hum. Deep under the soil, under the stones, under their own ribs. Like standing too close to a beehive made of something that was not bees.

"Do you feel that?" they whispered.

Ivo didn't answer at first. He was gray around the mouth, and his left arm hung wrong against his side where the arrow had gone through. Three days of fever, three days of the wound weeping black instead of red, and only the sealed box strapped to his chest seemed to keep him upright — he clutched it now like a child clutching a mother's skirt.

"I feel," he managed, "like I'm being *read*."

He wasn't wrong. That was the terrible thing, though neither of them knew it yet.

* * *

Ivo had crossed borders before. He'd crossed the Salt Marches with soldiers on his heels, crossed the Weeping Pass in winter with frostbite claiming two of his toes, crossed a river once by holding onto the corpse of a drowned horse because it floated and he did not. He was, by his own accounting, very good at crossing things.

But he had never crossed into a place that looked back.

The moment his boot touched the ground past the eleventh stone, something reached for him. Not a hand. Not teeth. Something older and more patient than either — the way ivy is patient, the way rot is patient. It slid through the arrow wound in his shoulder and found the ember of gift that lived in every fiber of him, trained and banked and hidden for years, and it began to *drink*.

He cried out. His knees went. Paloma caught him, staggered, caught him again.

"What is it? What's happening?"

"It's—" Ivo laughed, and the laugh came out cracked and horrified. "The valley. Your beautiful, blessed valley. Paloma, it's not keeping magic *out*. It never was."

The truth arrived in his body before it arrived in words. Every gift he carried — the small warm magics the crown called heresy, the ones that let him coax a lock or still a bleeding or whisper to a dying fire — were being pulled out of him through the wound like thread off a spool. And the land drank them down and gave nothing back.

"Then what does it do?" Paloma's voice was very small.

"It *eats* it." He forced himself upright against a stone, gasping. "That's what the Quiet Line is. That's what your temple built. Not a shield. A *mouth*."

* * *

Paloma wanted to argue. They opened their mouth to argue, and then they remembered.

They remembered the ledgers. Ten years of ledgers, copied in ink that sometimes moved when no one watched, in a script that sometimes rearranged itself into truths no scribe had written. They remembered how, every time the ink moved, every time the candle relit itself from nothing, every time their gift flickered up through their fingers like a fish rising toward light —

— the feeling afterward. The hollowed-out feeling. The exhaustion the elders called *piety's price*, the weakness they said was the spirit being cleansed. Sister Anthea pressing a cool hand to their forehead and saying, gently, *the valley takes your burden, child, so the gift cannot take root.*

The valley takes your burden.

The valley takes.

"Oh," Paloma said. It was not a scream. It was worse than a scream — it was the quiet, reasonable tone of someone doing arithmetic. "Oh, that's what it's been doing. All this time. To me. To everyone."

They thought of Brother Callum, who could once make bread rise with a song and who had grown so tired, so *piously* tired, that he'd taken vows of silence just to save the strength. They thought of old Mother Wren in the infirmary, whose hands used to warm feverish children, and who had died small and faded at sixty, worn thin as prayer-paper. Generation after generation of the mildly gifted, drawn to Vell's safety, welcomed in, debt-bound, and slowly, lovingly, *drained*.

"They weren't protecting us," Paloma said. "They were *farming* us."

The stones said nothing. The stones had been saying nothing for four hundred years, and they were very good at it.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the last stone, because Ivo could not walk another step, and because Paloma could not bear to leave the valley's shadow while the light lasted.

The fever had changed. Paloma had seen fevers — had nursed them, charted them, copied out remedies in careful columns. This was not a fever. This was a *negotiation*. The black threads spreading from Ivo's wound moved with purpose, crawling up toward his heart and stopping, always stopping, at the edge of his chest. Where the Emberbox sat.

At night the box grew warm. At night Ivo stopped shivering. In the morning, when dawn came gray through the trees, the box would have cooled and the black threads would creep again, patient as the stones.

"It's feeding me," Ivo said. He said it the way a man admits a shameful appetite. "The Box. Whatever's inside it — it's the only reason the wound isn't winning. The valley drained everything else I had. This is all that's left."

"Then we don't open it."

"We may not have a choice." He tried to smile and didn't quite make it. "You know what's funny? I spent six years running from this thing. Sleeping in ditches. Watching my back at every well. And the whole time, the most dangerous place in the world was the one everyone said was safest. Your elders could have just — let me in. Let the valley do the work. No hunters needed. I'd have been an empty husk by spring, and the Box would've just... sat there, waiting for someone else to carry."

Paloma wrapped their arms around their knees and looked back the way they had come. Far below, past the dark folds of the hills, Vell lay hidden in its hollow — the bell tower, the candle windows, the little lights of the night markets where they'd bought travel seals with hands that shook. Home.

A mouth, lying in the grass, smiling.

"I keep waiting to feel guilty," they said. "For running. For stealing the seals. For leaving them. And I keep finding — this instead. This *fury*. Is that wicked?"

"If it is, be wicked thoroughly," Ivo said. "Guilt is for people who were lied to politely. You were lied to on your knees, for ten years, while they ate your lunch."

Despite everything — the fever-stink, the black threads, the hollow ache where Paloma's own gift should have been singing — despite all of it, Paloma laughed. It startled out of them, bright and inappropriate, and Ivo grinned like he'd won something.

"There she is," he said.

"*There who* is?"

"The person who moves ink when nobody's watching. I was starting to think the valley got all of you."

Paloma reached for that place behind their breastbone where the gift lived — or had lived. Where it should have been. They found it there, faint but present, like a coal under ash, and when they breathed toward it, they felt the pull again. The leech-tug of the land behind them, drinking at a distance, greedy even across miles of stone.

But the coal did not go out.

Small, stubborn, and *their own*.

"We keep moving tomorrow," they said. "North. You mentioned — before the fever took your tongue — you mentioned people. Keepers. Someone who knows what's in the Box."

Ivo's grin faded into something more careful. "The Ashen Conclave. If they still exist. They're not friendly, exactly. More like — a locked door with opinions."

"Wonderful. I've spent ten years copying letters for a locked door with opinions. I know how to be polite to one."

* * *

They broke camp at first light, and it was as they crested the ridge beyond the stones that Paloma saw them: figures on the southern pass, tiny with distance but unmistakable. Steel catching sun. The slow, deliberate formation of people who hunt for a living.

Hunters. Inside the passes. Inside the place magic supposedly could not follow.

"They're crossing the Line," Ivo breathed, hand going to the Box. "They can't — the valley eats them too if they're gifted, they'll be—"

"No." Paloma's stomach turned cold as the arithmetic finished itself. "Don't you see? The valley doesn't eat *them*. It eats *us*. The gifted. The ones it was fed on. The hunters walk through like it's any other road — because to the valley, they're not food. They're *shepherds*."

Below, the figures paused at the standing stones. One of them raised something — glass, perhaps, or a spyglass — and even at that distance, Paloma felt certain, bone-certain, that the lens was pointed directly at them.

And far behind those figures, deep in the green and gentle hollow of home, the valley's hunger turned over in its sleep — and found itself, for the first time in four hundred years, missing a meal.
