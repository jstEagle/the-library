## 5. The Cartographer's Daughter

Sefa Marrow had three rules, and she kept them the way other people kept faith.

One: never draw a map you wouldn't be willing to burn. Two: never take a client who cried at the negotiating table. Three: never, under any circumstance, work for anyone whose business involved the crown, the temple, or the word *destiny* spoken without irony.

So naturally, on the third gray morning of a rain that had settled over Saltcross like a debt collector, she opened her door to find a temple scribe, a bleeding smuggler-turned-courier, and the word *Conclave* sitting in the air between them like an unexploded shell.

"No," she said, and began to close the door.

"We can pay," said the scribe — Paloma, they'd called themselves, in a voice that tried very hard to be steady and mostly succeeded. "Not much. But we can."

"I don't want money. I want my morning."

The courier, Ivo, chose that exact moment to fold at the knees. Paloma caught him — barely, all elbows and desperation — and Sefa watched the boy's hand flare with a soft gold light that sank into Ivo's shoulder like water into cracked clay, and felt forty years of careful rules rearrange themselves behind her ribs.

"Ah," she said. "Well. Come in before someone sees that. And wipe your boots."

* * *

Her shop sat above a chandler's on Tallow Row, which meant it always smelled faintly of beeswax and always sounded faintly of the chandler arguing with his wife about whether tallow or wax made better candles (tallow, obviously, but the wife was right about the smell). Sefa cleared the drafting table of its current commission — a wine merchant's fantasy of the Amber Coast, all coastline smoothed into flattery — and laid Ivo down on it, because the table was the only flat surface in the room she hadn't already decided to keep.

He was worse than he'd let himself show at the door. She could tell by how little he complained. In her experience, men who stopped joking were either dying or in love, and this one looked too tired for love.

The wound in his shoulder was a black feathering under the skin, like ink dropped in water going the wrong direction. Sefa had seen arrow-wounds, knife-wounds, one memorable wound from an aggrieved swan. She had not seen anything that moved while you watched it.

"That's not infection," she said.

"It is," said Ivo, "technically. It's infecting me with being dead." He managed half a smile. "The hunters use barbed heads dipped in something out of the old war. Ash-rot, we called it. There's no cure south of the mountains."

"There's always a cure," Sefa said. "It just costs more than people expect."

Paloma stood at the edge of the lamplight with their hands knotted together, and Sefa watched them watch the wound, and thought: *there it is*. That look. She'd worn it once, across a different face, in a different lifetime, when a girl named Wren used to trace roads on Sefa's maps and say *someday we'll walk every line on this.* The crown had burned Wren in the market square at Corvane for the crime of lighting a lamp with her finger when the wicks ran out. Sefa still couldn't light lamps normally without her hands shaking, which was inconvenient in a candle shop's neighborhood.

She poured three cups of plum brandy and gave the largest to herself.

"So," she said. "The Conclave."

"You know of them?" Paloma leaned forward.

"Everyone knows of them. That's the problem. Rumor's the only thing keeping them alive — nobody hunts what might be a story." She turned her cup slowly. "I know where the rumors point. A monastery above the Greywash Falls, up past the Spine. I've drawn the approaches a dozen times for clients who never came back to collect. Either they found it and stayed, or they found nothing and were embarrassed. Both options appeal to me more than the truth, usually."

"And now?" Ivo asked from the table, voice fraying.

Sefa looked at him a long moment. At the box chained to his belt — a plain iron thing the size of a loaf of bread, radiating nothing, humming nothing, and yet her candle flames all leaned toward it slightly, like flowers toward sun, and had been doing so since it entered her shop.

"Now," she said, "I'm going to make an indecent offer, and you're both going to pretend it's normal."

* * *

Here was the thing about Sefa Marrow that almost no one living knew: her mother had been the crown's cartographer before her. The great Aldith Marrow, who mapped the passes during the last years of the war, who vanished afterward with an entire folio of charts that had never appeared in any archive, and whom history remembered as either a traitor or a ghost depending on which side of a border you stood.

Aldith had raised her daughter on two lessons. The first: *a map is a promise you make to strangers, so make it carefully.* The second she'd said only once, the night before she disappeared, with her hands on Sefa's shoulders and her eyes wet: *If anyone ever comes to you carrying a box that makes fire lean toward it, you open the casing. Do you hear me? Whatever else happens, you open the casing.*

Sefa had been nine. She'd spent thirty-one years deciding it was a fever dream.

She set down her cup, crossed to the drafting table, and crouched beside Ivo. "The box. May I?"

Ivo's jaw tightened. Paloma started to say something — a warning, probably, or a plea — and he lifted his free hand to quiet them.

"Sefa Marrow," he said slowly, tasting the name. "Your mother drew the Vell Pass charts. The ones the valley elders burned copies of."

"She drew everything worth drawing."

"They said she stole from the crown."

"They say that about everyone the crown loses," Sefa said. "May I touch the box, or do you want to bleed out philosophically?"

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly bitten off by pain — and unhooked the chain. "Careful. It opens for exactly one kind of person, and as far as I know, I'm not it."

"I'm not trying to open it." She turned the iron casing in her hands, feeling along the seams, the rivets, the false weight of it. Her fingers found what they'd been trained at nine years old to find: a hairline ridge beneath the base plate, running in a pattern that wasn't decoration. It was linework. Contours. A river delta rendered in metal.

"Oh, you clever, impossible woman," Sefa breathed. "It's not a casing. It's a folio cover."

"What?" Paloma came closer, lamp in hand.

"The whole box. It's built around maps — pressed plates, layered. My mother hid the routes inside the relic itself, where no archivist would think to look and no thief would dare to pry." Her throat went tight. She kept her voice level through sheer professional spite. "There's a passage map in here. To the Conclave, if I'm reading the contours right. Maybe others. Roads nobody else has."

Ivo had gone very still. "You're saying you can read it."

"I'm saying my mother left me a letter thirty-one years overdue, and it happens to be addressed in geography." She looked up at him. "So here's my indecent offer. I guide you to the Conclave — my routes, my contacts, my extremely illegal shortcuts. In exchange, when this is done, the maps come out of the casing and they're mine. All of them. You keep your relic; I keep her handwriting."

"That's it?" Paloma said. "That's all you want?"

Sefa laughed, and it came out rougher than intended. "Child, I have wanted nothing for thirty years because wanting gets people burned. This is the most I've asked for since I was nine years old. Take it or leave it."

They took it. Of course they took it. They were exhausted and hunted and the courier was leaking ash-rot onto her good table, and besides — she'd watched Paloma's face when she said *her handwriting*, and there was something in it that recognized the shape of a parent-shaped hole.

* * *

They spent four days in the shop while Sefa prepared, and those four days did something to her that she would later refuse to examine closely.

Ivo, propped against her map cabinets with his fever cycling, turned out to be funny in the specific way of people who had decided death was a colleague rather than a threat. He critiqued her route choices from horizontal ("Are we really going through the Wend? The Wend killed my cousin." "Your cousin walked it in winter with no guide." "He also died." "From the rot, not the Wend."), and taught Paloma dice games that were transparently designed to separate a scribe from their remaining coin, and lost gracefully, twice, before Sefa realized he was losing on purpose and confiscated the dice.

Paloma, meanwhile, practiced.

Sefa caught them at it the second night — standing in the back room among the rolled charts, holding their palm out, coaxing a little bloom of gold light to life like a man teaching a bird to trust his hand. It guttered. Steadied. Guttered again.

"You're apologizing to it," Sefa said from the doorway.

Paloma flinched; the light snuffed. "What?"

"The gift. You're asking permission for something that's already yours. I used to watch—" She stopped. Redirected. "My mother said magic isn't a dog you beg. It's a river you learn to read. You don't apologize to a river. You learn where the rocks are."

"And if I hit every rock?"

"Then you hit every rock and get better." She came in, pulled a chart from the rack — an old current-map of the Greywash, all eddies and deadfalls — and spread it across the desk. "Read it to me. Not the labels. The water."

Paloma bent over it, frowning, tracing channels with one hesitant fingertip. "The main channel bends left here, but... there's a back-current. It pushes against the bend. If you steered with the surface you'd end up in the shallows."

"Mm. Now do that with the light."

It took until near dawn. But somewhere past the third hour, the gold in Paloma's palm stopped flickering and simply *was* — a small steady warmth that lit the chart edges and caught in their eyes, and Sefa, watching, felt something crack gently open in her chest that she'd caulked over decades ago.

Hope. Cautious, suspicious, badly-socialized hope.

* * *

On the fifth day they left before first light, three figures and one mule named Duchess (the mule had come with the name and refused to answer to anything else), slipping out through Saltcross's east gate while the gatekeeper was still arguing with his breakfast.

At the top of the switchback road, Sefa paused and looked back at the town — at Tallow Row, at the chandler's window where the eternal marital argument was surely resuming — and permitted herself exactly four seconds of grief before turning away.

"Well," said Ivo, grey-faced in the saddle but grinning. "The famous smuggler-cartographer. Leading us into the mountains after a legendary order of magic monks. Any chance this ends well?"

"Statistically? No."

"Then why are you smiling?"

"Because," Sefa said, nudging Duchess forward, "my mother drew the next hundred miles, and for the first time in thirty-one years, I get to find out what she wanted to tell me."

Behind them, far down the valley road, a column of riders crested the hill into Saltcross — black cloaks, white sigils, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who always, eventually, arrived.

Paloma saw them first. "Sefa. Hunters."

She didn't look back. She'd already counted the hours in her head, and the math was ugly.

"Hold on to something," she said. "Both of you. We're about to test whether my mother's shortcuts are legend or libel."
