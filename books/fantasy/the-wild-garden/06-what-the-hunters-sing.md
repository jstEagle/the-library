## 6. What the Hunters Sing

They took Ivo at dawn, which he thought was almost polite of them.

He had been on watch, or pretending to be on watch, while Paloma slept with their back against a fallen birch and Sefa dozed sitting up the way old soldiers and old smugglers do — like a woman who could be running before her eyes finished opening. The fire was down to embers. The forest above the valley rim was thin here, all silver bark and long pale light, and that was how they came: not crashing through the brush like the stories said witch-hunters did, but quiet, in grey wool, six of them, moving like men who had done this a hundred times and expected to do it a hundred more.

Ivo considered waking the others. He even got as far as drawing breath to do it.

Then a hand closed over his mouth from behind, gentle as a mother's, and a voice said near his ear, "Don't. You'll only get the scribe hurt, and they seem nice."

So Ivo went. He let them bind his wrists with waxed cord and take the Emberbox off his chest where it rode in its sling, and the moment it left him he felt the absence like a tooth pulled — a dull throbbing hollow, an ache that knew exactly what it wanted back. The box's warmth had been keeping something in his blood quiet for weeks now. He'd stopped asking himself whether that thing was the hunters' arrow-wound or something older. It was easier not to name it.

They marched him downhill through the mist for half the morning. His captors barely spoke. One of them, a young man with a chapped face, hummed under his breath as they walked — a low, wandering tune, three notes rising and falling like something you'd sing over a cradle or a grave.

"What is that?" Ivo asked finally, because his nerves had always been the kind that filled silence.

The young man looked embarrassed. "Captain doesn't like us singing it."

"Then why do you?"

"Because it's true," the young man said simply, and would say nothing else.

* * *

They brought him to a hunting lodge built into the shoulder of a hill, stone-faced and smoke-stained, older than the crown it served. They sat him in a chair by the hearth — sat him, which unsettled him more than chains would have — and after an hour that stretched like taffy, the door opened and Captain Maren Voss came in carrying two cups of broth.

She was smaller than Ivo had imagined, and older. Her greying hair was cut practical-short, and there were lines around her eyes carved deep enough to hold rain. She set one cup in his bound hands.

"You'll want to drink that," she said. "The Box takes more out of you every day. I've seen it."

"You've seen—" Ivo stared at her. "You've carried it?"

"No." She sat across from him, holding her own cup without drinking, turning it slowly. "But I've hunted four people who did. And I watched each of them go grey and quiet and die of nothing anyone could name, except that when we pried the Box from their cold fingers, their fingers looked like they'd been holding a coal for years." She glanced up. "You know what it's doing to you. Don't pretend you don't."

Ivo drank the broth because his hands were shaking and he told himself it was from cold. "If you know it's killing me," he said, "why take it? Why not just let me die on the mountain and save the crown the rope?"

"Because the crown doesn't want the Box, Ivo Ferren." She used his full name the way you'd lay a card face-down on a table. "Yes, I know who you are. Courier for the Ashen Conclave, third generation. Your grandmother ran the same road. The crown wants the Box *buried*, somewhere no one will ever dig. My orders are to retrieve it and burn whoever carried it, so the secret burns too." She said it flatly, a woman reciting inventory.

"And you?" Ivo asked. "What does Maren Voss want?"

For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she set her cup down, reached into her collar, and drew out a small cord — and on the cord, two wooden beads, worn smooth, the kind children carve at midsummer fairs.

"My daughters made these," she said. "Eleven and eight. That was nineteen years ago, in Corren's Ford, the year the gifted came through the lowlands and the ungifted armies came after them, and the two of them met in the middle where my house happened to be standing." Her voice didn't shake. That was somehow the worst part. "The war you're about to help restart killed my children, courier. Not a battle. Just — the war. The hunger winter after. The fever that walked in behind it. Everything the world became once people started choosing sides again."

"The Conclave isn't choosing sides," Ivo said. "We're trying to end the argument. Open the Box properly, put magic back into the world evenly—"

"*Evenly.*" She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as a snapped branch. "There is no evenly. There never was. That's what your Conclave's histories leave out. Before the war, the gifted weren't persecuted — they were *kings*. And everyone else was dirt under their feet. My great-grandparents sang songs about it. Do you know why my hunters sing?"

She nodded toward the door, toward the chapped-faced young man and his wandering tune.

"That song is four hundred years old. Ungifted mothers sang it to their children during the old kingdoms: *sleep, sleep, the bright ones can't see you if you're small.* It's a song about hiding from magic. And then the war came, and the gifted lost, and for four hundred years *my* people have been the ones doing the burning — and now you come down off a mountain with a seed of everything that burned my family, and you tell me it's mercy." She leaned forward. "I don't think you're evil, courier. I think you're kind, and being kind, and being wrong, and those together have killed more people than cruelty ever managed."

Ivo looked at the two smooth beads on their cord, and found that he couldn't summon anger. He tried. It would have been easier. But all he could see was a woman who had buried two children and then spent nineteen years making sure no one ever dug up the thing that buried them.

"If I give you the Box," he said quietly, "you'll bury it. And the valley keeps devouring. And every gifted child born outside a cage gets hunted until your order finds them. That's not peace, Captain. That's just the war, won so thoroughly nobody remembers it's still going."

"I know," Maren Voss said.

And that — that *I know*, said without flinching — was worse than any argument. Because he understood her then, completely, the way you understand grief: not from the outside but from underneath. She wasn't wrong about the danger. She was right, and it didn't matter, because right wasn't enough anymore for either of them.

She stood, collected the cups, and paused at the door. "The scribe and the map-woman. Are they worth dying for?"

"Yes," Ivo said, without hesitation, and was surprised to find it was true.

"Then I'm sorry," said Maren Voss, and meant it, and left him alone with the fire and the hollow ache in his chest where the Box should be.

* * *

They were careless that night — or perhaps, Ivo thought later, they simply trusted that a dying man wouldn't run far from the warmth keeping him alive. The chapped-faced young man brought him supper and forgot to re-tie the wrist cords after checking them. Ivo waited until the lodge settled into its night sounds, then slipped out through the woodshed where they'd stashed the Box in a locked strongbox — locked, but the shed wall was older than the lock, and grief makes people careful with iron and careless with timber.

He took the Box back against his chest and felt the warmth flood in like water into a cracked vessel, and hated how much he loved it, and ran.

He ran badly, coughing, weaving, half-blind in the dark, and it didn't matter, because someone found him first. A hand caught his elbow; a familiar voice hissed his name; and there was Sefa, materializing out of the treeline like a rumor, with Paloma pale and wide-eyed behind her.

"How—" Ivo gasped.

"Sefa tracked the patrol for two days," Paloma said. "She says hunters are predictable. She said it in words I shouldn't repeat."

"She's not wrong," Sefa said. "Voss runs a clean patrol. Clean means scheduled. Scheduled means ambushable. Come on, we've got maybe an hour before they notice the empty bed, and I'd rather be a valley away."

As they moved uphill through the dark, Ivo noticed Paloma glancing at him sideways, again and again, working up to something.

"They sang," Paloma said finally. "When I was listening for you. The hunters. That little tune."

"You heard it too?"

"At the temple, sometimes. Old sisters humming it over the wash-basins." Paloma's voice was strange. "I always thought it was a lullaby."

"It is," Ivo said. "That's what frightens me. Songs survive wars. People don't."

* * *

Two days later, camped in a hollow beneath a shelf of grey stone with rain drumming overhead, Sefa decided Paloma was ready.

Paloma disagreed loudly.

"I'm telling you," Paloma said, huddled in their cloak, "the last time I did this on purpose, I lit a candle with my mind and then spent a week waiting for the temple elders to notice. Deliberate is not better. Deliberate is just faster ways to get caught."

"And yet," Sefa said, crouched across the tiny fire with her scarred hands spread, "every time you're frightened, your gift leaks. Ink moves. Embers jump. I've watched you warm Ivo's bandages without touching them, twice, while talking about the weather." She tilted her head. "Your problem isn't power, scribe. It's that you think of it as something happening *to* you. So it only comes out crooked, sideways, when you're too scared to stop it. We're going to make it something you *do*."

"And if I do it and it goes wrong?"

"Then it goes wrong in front of friends," Sefa said, "which is the entire difference between a gift and a curse, if you ask me."

She taught the way she mapped: practically, patiently, with an occasional profanity when Paloma apologized too much. She had Paloma hold a dry twig and feel for the warmth in their own chest — not push, not grab, just *notice*, the way you notice your own heartbeat once someone names it. "It's already yours," she kept saying. "It's been yours since you were born. All you're learning is its address."

The first hour produced nothing but frustration and one sneezing fit. The second hour, the twig warmed. The third, its tip glowed faintly, trembling like a held breath — and Paloma made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob, because it hadn't felt like seizing control of anything. It had felt like being *allowed*.

"There," Sefa said softly. "See? It's not a wolf. It's a dog that's been tied up too long. Be gentle with it and it'll follow you anywhere."

From his bedroll, Ivo watched the small flame bloom steady above Paloma's palm — watched their face, lit gold, wearing an expression he recognized from mirrors: the look of someone discovering that a thing they feared about themselves might, instead, be the best thing about them. Something in his chest tightened. The Box pulsed against his ribs, patient and hungry, and for the first time he wondered whether the Conclave had chosen him to carry it, or chosen him to *spend*.

Later, past midnight, he woke to voices and lay still, listening.

Sefa: "—the pass at Cael Hollow, two days hard. After that, Conclave territory."

Paloma: "And if Voss reaches the pass first?"

A pause. Rain. Then Sefa, quieter: "Then we learn what she's willing to burn to keep the world asleep."

Ivo lay in the dark with the Emberbox warm against his heart, thinking of two wooden beads on a cord, and a song about hiding, and a captain who said *I know* without flinching.

He was beginning to suspect that the hardest part of this journey would not be outrunning Maren Voss.

It would be deciding, when the moment came, whether she deserved to lose.
