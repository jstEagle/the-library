## 7. The Ashen Conclave

The Ashen Conclave did not look like the seat of an ancient order. It looked, Paloma thought as they climbed the last switchback, like a pile of very old stones that had given up pretending to be anything else.

"Is that it?" Ivo asked from behind her. He had one hand pressed to his shoulder, where Sefa's poultices slowed but could not stop the slow gray creep of the hunter's wound. His voice was thin. "It looks like my grandmother's ruin. The one she used to blame on goats."

"The goats did do it," Paloma said. "I've read about those goats."

"You have not."

"Sefa's maps said so." They both looked at the empty space where Sefa should have been walking, and neither of them said anything else for a while.

The mountain path had taken them four days north of the valley rim, through pine forest that smelled of resin and cold, up switchbacks carved by hands long dead. Twice they'd passed cairns stacked with burnt offerings — feathers, wax, a child's wooden bird — and twice Ivo had gone quiet in the way he went quiet when the Emberbox shifted against his chest like something breathing.

Now the path ended at a wall of stone with no door in it at all. Just a slab of granite taller than three men, streaked white with centuries of birdlime, utterly blank.

Paloma set down their pack. "I expected more fanfare. Trumpets. A suspicious old man stroking a beard."

"Give it a moment," Ivo said. He was leaning against a boulder, gray-faced. "Everything ancient is disappointing at first. It's how you know it's real."

Paloma stepped forward anyway, because standing still felt like admitting they had no idea what they were doing, which was true but need not be advertised. They raised a hand to the stone — and stopped.

Their fingers were glowing.

Not brightly. A soft amber light, the color of candleflame through honey, pooling in their palm and spilling onto the granite like water finding a crack. The ink-light, Sefa had called it. The gift that had lived hidden under Paloma's skin for twenty-six years, leaking out in small impossible ways — moving ink, relighting candles, ledgers whose numbers rearranged themselves into kinder sums — now blazing openly because there was nothing left to hide behind. No temple walls. No silence. No valley.

The granite drank the light. And then, with a groan like a ship changing its mind, the wall split along a seam no eye could find, and swung inward.

Warm air rolled out, smelling of woodsmoke and rosemary and old paper. Paloma heard Ivo exhale behind them.

"After you," they said, because their voice had decided to be brave without consulting them first.

* * *

Inside, the Conclave was a hollowed mountain: a great round chamber open to the sky through a smoke-hole far above, terraces of stone descending to a fire pit where perhaps thirty people sat or stood or stirred pots. Most of them were old. Some were very old. One woman appeared to be mostly blanket. But every single one of them turned when Paloma entered, and every single one of them looked — not at Ivo, not at the box strapped beneath his coat, but at Paloma's still-faintly-glowing hands.

"Well," said a voice near the fire, "the ledger-keeper arrives."

An old man rose from beside the flames. He was tall once and had settled, the way houses settle, everything slightly askew but sound at the core. His robes were ash-gray and patched at both elbows. His eyes were the pale gold of weak tea, and they held Paloma's with the unnerving patience of someone who had been waiting a very long time and intended to enjoy the wait.

"I'm not a ledger-keeper anymore," Paloma said.

"No," the old man agreed pleasantly. "You're a debt-bound soul who stole her own freedom out of a locked drawer and ran. We do keep track, child. Poorly, and with much argument, but we keep it." He spread his arms. "I am Orsen. Welcome to the Conclave, such as it is. Mind the third step; it bites."

The third step did, in fact, rock alarmingly under Paloma's foot, and someone on a nearby terrace snorted into their soup.

They got Ivo seated by the fire, and a young woman with flour on her forearms brought broth and bread that tasted of rye and rosemary and was, Paloma thought with a small ache, better than anything the temple kitchens had ever produced. Ivo ate like a man apologizing to the food. Then Orsen knelt, unwrapped the bandage on Ivo's shoulder, and went very still.

"That's a Voss arrow," he said quietly. "Iron-and-blessing shafts. Made to kill the gifted and linger in everyone else." He glanced up at Ivo. "And you're carrying the Box against your heart, which is the only reason you're alive. It feeds what the arrow starves. You understand that when the Box leaves you, the wound will finish its work."

"I understand," Ivo said. "I've done the arithmetic." He tried to make it a joke and didn't quite manage.

Orsen looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at Paloma, and Paloma felt the look go through them like a needle through cloth, taking a thread with it.

"Come," Orsen said. "There are things you should see before anyone says anything difficult."

* * *

The things he showed them were below.

A stair spiraled down from the edge of the chamber, cut into living rock, lit at intervals by lamps that burned without oil — Paloma stared at these until Orsen gently steered them onward. At the bottom lay a hall of murals, floor to ceiling, painted in pigments that still glowed faintly after what Orsen claimed was nine hundred years.

"This is the war," Orsen said simply.

Paloma walked it slowly. The first panels showed a world drenched in wild magic — rivers that sang, forests that walked, people with light in their palms trading it freely like bread. Ungifted and gifted side by side. Then, panel by panel, the light dimmed. Towers burning. Hunters in crown colors. Gifted folk fleeing into mountains. And in the final mural, a small figure kneeling before a closed box, hands raised above it, while all around the figure the world went dark and quiet and safe.

"The Emberbox," Paloma said. Their voice came out strange.

"The last seed of wild magic," Orsen confirmed. "When the war ended, the gifted gathered every scrap of living power they could carry and bound it into a single vessel rather than let the crown harvest it for weapons. The Box has slept ever since. Passed hand to hand, hidden, guarded. Your friend Ivo is merely the latest in a long line of couriers, most of whom died badly."

"And if it opens?"

Orsen was quiet for a moment. In the lamplight, the murals seemed to breathe.

"If it opens freely — truly opens — the seed takes root again. Magic returns to the world. Not the tame, hoarded, feared thing it was. Wild. Everywhere. In soil and blood and ordinary people who never asked for it." He turned to face Paloma fully. "But a thing like that cannot simply be unsealed. It must be *opened*. Willingly. By the right person. Otherwise it shatters, and what comes out is not a seed but a firestorm."

"And the right person is—"

"Someone who has given everything," Orsen said, "and chosen nothing."

The words landed in Paloma's chest like a stone dropped down a well. They heard the splash a long time later.

"A debt-bound soul," Orson continued, gently now, the way you speak to someone standing at a cliff edge. "Someone who never owned their own life. Who worked off obligations they never incurred. Whose every choice was made for them — by the temple, by the debt, by the valley." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, and somehow that made it worse. "You qualify perfectly, child. I'm sorry. I watched your face hoping I'd be wrong."

* * *

They sat afterward on a terrace overlooking the fire pit, Paloma with their knees drawn up and Ivo beside them, wrapped in a borrowed cloak, the Box a hard shape between them like a third person nobody wanted to introduce.

"Perfectly," Paloma said at last. "He said perfectly. Like it's a compliment."

"It wasn't," Ivo said. "I noticed."

"Twenty-six years of being nobody's choice, and it turns out that's a *qualification*." Paloma laughed, and it came out wrong, too loud and too sharp, and a couple of Conclave elders glanced over with the mild interest of people who had seen many breakdowns and found most of them educational. Paloma lowered their voice. "Do you know what the elders used to say? Back home. When I finished a ledger early or the candles behaved. They'd say, *the valley keeps you humble.* Humble. As if the smallness was a virtue I'd earned."

Ivo said nothing. He was good at that — saying nothing in a way that made room.

"And now some ash-robed stranger tells me the reason I can open the Box is that I've never once been allowed to want anything." Paloma's throat tightened. "Being chosen feels exactly like being erased, Ivo. Did you know that? It's the same shape. Everyone pointing at me going *yes, this one, this one fits*, and none of them asking whether I fit anywhere myself."

"I'm asking," Ivo said.

Paloma looked at him. Firelight moved on his face, and under it he was still gray, still dying by inches, still stubbornly, infuriatingly kind about it.

"Then ask," they said.

"What do you want?" Ivo said. "Not the Box. Not the valley. Not the debt or the Conclave or Maren Voss and her terrible arrows. You. What does Paloma Vane want?"

And the horrible thing — the thing that made Paloma's eyes sting — was that the question had no ready answer. Twenty-six years of copying other people's accounts, and they had never once kept their own.

"I want to find out," they said finally. "That's the whole of it. That's embarrassingly the whole of it."

"Good enough," said Ivo. "Best answer anyone's given on this mountain in years, probably. Look at their faces."

* * *

It was near midnight when Orsen came to find them, and Paloma knew before he spoke. Some things arrive in the body before they arrive in the ears — a coldness in the gut, a stillness in the fire.

"There's more," Orsen said. He sat down across from them with the care of a man whose knees had opinions. "About what opening the Box actually costs. I owed you the truth of the seed first. This part I owed you sleep."

"Tell us," Paloma said.

Orsen folded his hands. "Wild magic isn't only a gift, child. It's an appetite. It eats, and it grows, and the world learned that the hard way — that's why the war happened, whatever the songs say. The valley you fled doesn't block magic. It *feeds*. It has been drinking the gift out of every soul born inside it for generations, slowly, kindly, so kindly no one ever felt the teeth. There are places like it scattered across the world — cages built by frightened people who told themselves they were sanctuaries."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "Then opening the seed—"

"Would set magic loose everywhere," Orsen said. "Including there. Including into that starving old ground. And a devouring thing, offered a feast after nine hundred years of scraps—" He met Paloma's eyes and did not flinch, which Paloma would later realize was its own act of mercy. "The valley will burn its hunger out from the inside. The land will die. Everything rooted in it. Everyone still living there."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, wind moved through the smoke-hole with a sound almost like singing.

"My mother is still in Vell," Paloma said quietly. "Elder Maerin. She raised me. She taught me my letters. She—" Their voice caught. "She offered to erase my debt if I handed Ivo over. And I ran anyway. And she stayed."

"Yes," Orsen said.

"So the price of freeing the world is everyone I grew up with."

"The price," Orsen said, "is exactly what you already knew it would be. It always is. That's why it waits for someone who has practiced losing."

Paloma looked at their hands. The faint amber light was still there, patient as ever, waiting to be aimed at something. Below them, the murals glowed in the dark — the world bright, the world burning, the world quiet and safe and starving.

Ivo's hand found theirs. His grip was fever-warm and steady.

Above the mountain, far to the south, past four days of pine and stone, the valley of Vell lay sleeping in its gentle, hungry dark — and Paloma understood, with terrible clarity, that dawn was coming either way.

The only question left was what it would rise on.
