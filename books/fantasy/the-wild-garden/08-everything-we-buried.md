## 8. Everything We Buried

Sefa had drawn this pass forty times before she ever walked it, and she'd drawn it wrong every single time.

That was the joke she kept to herself, crouched behind a shelf of fallen stone while the first light came gray over the Ashen Teeth. Forty drafts in her youth, all of them beautiful, all of them useless, because her father — the great cartographer, the man whose name was on the royal charts — had never once crossed the mountains himself. He drew the world from other men's mouths. Sefa had spent thirty years learning that the world only tells you its true shape when you put your boots on it.

And now the world was about to tell her its truest shape yet: the shape of an ending.

She counted the torches below. Eleven. No — twelve. Maren Voss did not travel small, and she did not travel stupid. The hunters moved through the lower switchbacks with the discipline of people who had done this many times and never once enjoyed it. Sefa knew that look. She'd loved a woman with that look once, back before the pyres, back when loving someone marked was still just love and not a death sentence with better weather.

"Twelve," she murmured.

Paloma crouched beside her, ink-stained fingers white-knuckled on a walking staff they clearly didn't know how to use. "Twelve what? Bad things? Please don't say twelve bad things."

"Hunters. Coming up the Widow's Stair." Sefa passed her spyglass — a chipped thing she'd traded two maps for in Kesselport and never regretted once — and watched Paloma's face do the arithmetic of doom. "They'll reach the Conclave door by midmorning."

"Can't we go out the back? There's always a back. You're a map person. Map people know where the backs are."

"There's a back," Sefa admitted. "It's called the Hollow Stair, it drops three hundred feet onto scree, and Ivo cannot currently walk to breakfast without leaning on furniture."

As if summoned by slander, Ivo appeared at the mouth of the cave, one hand pressed to his bandaged shoulder, the Emberbox slung against his chest like a second heart. Which, Sefa reflected, it essentially was. The fever had carved hollows under his eyes, but his jaw was set in the particular way of a man who has decided to be stubborn about something.

"I heard my name," he said.

"You heard the word *furniture*," Paloma said. "We were discussing your many talents."

"I have exactly two talents," Ivo said, lowering himself onto a rock with the grace of a collapsing tent. "Carrying this box, and dying slowly. And the first one is negotiable."

Sefa liked him enormously. That was the problem. She'd promised herself, after Lira, after the smoke and the singing crowds and the ash that got into everything including her grief — she'd promised she would stop liking people. People were a liability with opinions. And now here she was, three weeks into knowing these two disaster children, already composing their eulogies and hating herself for how much practice she was getting.

"The Conclave elders say the Box must not be opened here," Ivo said. "The chamber beneath is warded for study, not release."

"And Maren Voss says the Box must not be opened anywhere," Sefa said. "And the crown says it must be opened *for them*, in a nice controlled setting, by people who will then control everything forever." She shrugged. "Everyone wants a say in your box, Ivo."

"That's what she said," Paloma whispered, and then went scarlet, because temple scribes apparently blushed even at the end of the world.

Ivo almost smiled. Then the smile died as the sound reached them — boots on stone, many boots, and beneath it the low chant the hunters sang on approach. Sefa had heard it before, years ago, through a window, while the street burned. *What sleeps will wake. What wakes will burn.* They sang it like a lullaby. They probably thought it was merciful.

"They're not stopping at the door," Sefa said quietly. "Look. They've split. Six to the front, six going wide — they mean to take the whole mountain."

* * *

Inside the Conclave, the argument had already started, which Sefa supposed meant the day was proceeding normally. The Ashen Conclave was a place built for arguments; the walls were lined with alcoves so scholars could disagree in comfort across the centuries.

"We bury it," Ivo said. He had the Box on the table between them, and his hand rested on its casing the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping child. "Deep. Somewhere no chart reaches. Sefa — you're the best cartographer alive. Find me a blank spot on the world."

"There are no blank spots," Sefa said. "There are only spots nobody's bothered to lie about yet."

"Sefa."

"If we hide it," Paloma said, "the valley keeps eating. Every gifted child born inside those borders gets drained a little more every year, and nobody outside ever knows why the valley folk have such calm, empty eyes. I grew up there, Ivo. I *was* one of those children. I just didn't know I was being eaten."

"So we open it," Ivo said. "And the war starts again. I've seen what that looks like. I carried bodies out of it for a decade. Voss hasn't wrong about everything — she's wrong about most things, but not that. Open the Box and the burning starts, and this time there won't be anyone left to write the histories."

"And if we don't," Sefa said, "it burns slowly instead of quickly. Slower is not the same as kinder. I've read enough ledgers to know that."

Paloma looked at her. "You think we should open it."

Sefa thought about Lira's hands, quick and clever, drawing warding sigils in flour on the kitchen table because real ink cost too much. She thought about the soldiers who'd come, and how polite they'd been, and how the crowd had sung. She thought about thirty years of maps and not one of them showing where the ash landed.

"I think," she said, "that I'm tired of drawing the world as it is. But that's a conversation for after we survive the morning."

Which was when the first horn sounded from below, and the morning stopped surviving anybody.

* * *

The Hollow Stair was out. The front door was worse. That left the Chimney — a vertical crack in the mountain's spine that the Conclave used for hauling supplies, wide enough for a determined goat or a desperate person, and it emptied onto the high shoulder of the pass, which meant whoever took it could go east toward the lowlands or west into the Teeth.

It also meant someone had to make sure the hunters coming up the Widow's Stair didn't simply follow them up the mountain and end the conversation permanently.

Sefa stood at the mouth of the Chimney and did the math. It wasn't complicated math. It was the kind of math you did with your heart instead of your head, and the answer had been sitting in her chest since the night she'd watched the pyres and sworn she'd never be helpless again.

"Paloma takes the Chimney first," she said. "Then Ivo. I anchor the rope from above—"

"No," said Paloma.

"—and then I drop the rope and take the ledge path around to—"

"No," said both of them.

"You're both very sweet," Sefa said, "and neither of you can hold a rearguard. Paloma, your gift is three weeks old. Ivo, you faint if you stand up quickly. I, however, have spent thirty years learning these mountains, I know every chokepoint on the Widow's Stair, and I own—" she patted her satchel fondly "—a truly irresponsible quantity of flashpowder and caltrops. This isn't sacrifice. It's delegation."

"It's suicide," Ivo said.

"It's *cartography*," Sefa said. "The difference is mostly attitude." She saw the protest building in Paloma's face and held up a hand. "Listen to me. Both of you. I have been running from that night for thirty years. Every map I drew, every mile I walked, I told myself I was documenting the world. I wasn't. I was outrunning it." She smiled, and found, to her surprise, that it didn't hurt. "You two gave me something to run *toward*. Let me buy you the road. That's not dying. That's finally making the maps mean something."

Paloma was crying. Sefa pulled them into a hug, quick and fierce, and felt the young scribe's gift flicker against her skin like a candle deciding to live — warm, startled, hopeful. Good. Hold onto that.

"Ivo," she said. "Take care of them. And when this is over — whatever you decide about the Box — decide it together. That's the only instruction that matters." She gripped his good shoulder. "And Ivo? The false bottom of my satchel. Third pouch. My master charts. Every route I never published, every road the crown doesn't know exists. Use them. Make them mean something too."

"Sefa—"

"Go," she said. "*Go.*"

She watched them climb until they were gone, two shapes swallowed by the Chimney's dark, and then she turned to face the stairs, alone with her satchel, her powder, and thirty years of postponed courage.

"Well," she told the mountain. "Let's put me on the map properly."

* * *

She held the Widow's Stair for the length of three hymns.

The first hymn was easy — caltrops and a collapsed scree chute bought her an hour while six hunters dug themselves out and reconsidered their career choices. The second was harder; they'd flanked her, as she'd known they would, and she met them at the Narrows with flashpowder and a voice full of every curse Kesselport had taught her, which was a great many, several in languages the hunters' grandmothers would have blushed at.

During the third hymn, Maren Voss came up the stairs herself.

Sefa knew her by reputation and by silhouette — the captain walked like a woman carrying a house she couldn't put down. They faced each other across twenty feet of broken stair, and Sefa, bleeding from a graze along her ribs, leaned on her staff and laughed.

"Captain Voss. I'd offer tea, but I drank it."

"You're the smuggler," Maren said. "The cartographer. We have your work in the crown archives. It's very good."

"Flattery. From a witch-hunter. Lira would have loved this." Sefa shifted her weight off the bad leg. "Your people dead?"

"Wounded. All of them. You fight like someone with nothing left to lose."

"I fought like someone with something left to give. Different thing entirely." Below them, past the hunters, the dawn was coming up gold over the lowlands, and Sefa thought: *she'll see it. She'll draw it. Somewhere, someday, someone will chart this exact light, and it will be because of today.* "Captain, ask me anything. One question. You look like a woman with questions."

Maren was silent a long moment. "Does it hurt them?" she said. "The magic. The ones who carry it."

"Yes," Sefa said. "Like everything worth carrying does."

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them, something in her face had cracked along a fault line that had been waiting years for permission. "Hold the pass if you like," she said quietly. "I won't order them past you. But I'm coming for that box eventually. Understand that."

"I understand," Sefa said. "So do they. That's why they're going to open it."

The shout came from above — from the Chimney, from the direction she'd sent her children — and Sefa's heart dropped through the floor of the world. Hunters. A second team. She hadn't counted on a second team, because she had spent the last hour being brave instead of being careful, and brave was just careful's prettier, stupider sister.

She ran. Up the ledge path, ribs screaming, satchel banging, taking shortcuts only she knew because the mountain had told her its secrets and she had kept faith with it. She rounded the final spur and saw them: four hunters on the high shelf, and beyond them, at the Chimney's mouth, Paloma and Ivo — visible, exposed, Ivo half-collapsed, Paloma standing in front of him with their hands raised and light gathering between their fingers like a storm learning its own name.

Sefa did the math one last time.

Four hunters. One old woman. One narrow ledge above a very long drop. One satchel full of flashpowder, and — she checked, because a professional always checks — one fire-salt charge, the big one, the one she'd been saving for a bridge or a gate or some other obstacle that deserved it.

A ledge worked too.

"Hey!" she bellowed, loud enough to crack stone, loud enough that thirty years of silence broke all at once. "You're standing on my map!"

They turned. Of course they turned. And Sefa Galloway, daughter of the great cartographer, author of nothing and everything, ran at them laughing — actually laughing, because the joke was hers at last, the punchline thirty years in the telling — and lit the fuse as she went.

The mountain answered.

The ledge went. The hunters went. Sefa went — but not all at once, not cleanly, the blast throwing her sideways onto the surviving shelf rather than into the dark, and she lay there watching the sky do something enormous and orange, and she thought, distantly, *well, that was excessive.*

Footsteps. Hands. Paloma's face above hers, streaked with soot, saying her name over and over like a word that had stopped working.

"Shh." Sefa's mouth tasted of copper and powder smoke. Her legs wouldn't answer, and she understood, with a cartographer's precision, exactly what that meant. "Listen. Listen to me. The satchel — third pouch — the charts. All of them. Every secret road."

"I have them," Paloma sobbed. "I have them, I'm not leaving you—"

"You are. You absolutely are, because I am about to be extremely embarrassing and I refuse to have witnesses." Sefa caught their wrist. Her grip was already failing, her hands going quiet the way hands did. "Paloma. Make them mean something. The maps. Make every line I ever drew mean something. Don't hide the Box and don't hand it over — *open* it, open it and let the whole wild stupid world bloom wrong and wonderful, and put me on the map. Right there. First page. 'Sefa was here, and she was right.'"

"I promise," Paloma whispered. "I promise, I promise—"

"Good girl. Good—" She searched past them, found Ivo's pale face. "Both of you. Family. Tend it. That's all family is, it's what you tend—" The sky was very beautiful. She'd have to draw it later. "Now *run*. Before I say something sincere."

They ran. Through the smoke, down the eastern shoulder, two shapes growing smaller, and Sefa lay back against the warm stone and watched the morning come up gold over the lowlands, and thought about Lira's hands in the flour, and laughed once, softly, at the excellent joke the world had finally let her finish.

The last thing she saw was the light.

The last thing she heard was Paloma screaming her name into the wind, and the wind, for once, carrying it onward instead of swallowing it — carrying it down the mountain, down the long road east, toward everything that came next.
