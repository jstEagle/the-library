## 10. A Garden Gone Wild

The beans came up wrong.

Paloma noticed it on a Tuesday morning, kneeling in the dirt plot behind the old waystation with their hands still dusty from turning the beds. Wrong, and wonderful: each bean vine had climbed its trellis in a spiral that spiraled *inward*, curling like a fern frond frozen mid-thought, and where the flowers should have been white there were instead small lanterns of pale gold that glowed faintly after sundown. They didn't light anything useful. They just glowed, the way a cat purrs — for no reason except that it could.

"Your beans are showing off," Ivo said from the porch, where he sat wrapped in two blankets despite the mild air. His shoulder wound had finally closed, weeks after the Emberbox opened, but the scar tissue had come in silver, and he tired quickly. He tired, Paloma had learned, the way convalescents do: dramatically, performatively, milking every ounce of sympathy available.

"They're not mine," Paloma said. "Nobody's. That's rather the point."

The wild magic had seeped into ordinary soil the way rain finds cracks. It hadn't asked anyone's permission. In the three weeks since Paloma had broken the seed open above the valley rim — since the devouring heart of Vell had died screaming under their hands while Maren Voss stood witness with her bow lowered — the land itself had begun to dream. Wheat grew in cursive. A farmer outside Thornhatch reported that his well water tasted faintly of honey on Thursdays. Children born since the opening were being watched very carefully by midwives who had suddenly remembered old words their grandmothers swore they'd never learned.

And here, at the abandoned waystation halfway between the dead valley and the living world, a handful of people who had no business trusting one another were learning to grow things.

* * *

Maren Voss arrived before breakfast, as she always did, walking the perimeter with the unhurried stride of a woman who had spent twenty years hunting people and now spent her mornings counting fence posts instead.

She had brought six hunters with her when she defected. Two had drifted away within the month, unable to stomach planting turnips beside the gifted. The remaining four lived in the waystation's bunkhouse and had developed strong opinions about compost. Dagny, the youngest, had discovered she could coax earthworms to the surface by humming, and treated this as a state secret.

"East fence is holding," Maren reported, setting her bow against the doorframe — a habit she'd adopted deliberately, Paloma suspected, the way reformed smokers chew mint. "Dagny talked up a molehill again. There are worms everywhere. It looks like a tiny battlefield."

"She says it helps the soil."

"It does help the soil. It also looks deranged." Maren lowered herself onto the porch step, joints cracking, and studied the bean vines with the expression of a woman auditing an enemy. "They're glowing."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"No reason. They just do."

Maren was quiet a moment. "In the old stories," she said, "magic was a war. Gifted against ungifted, fire against stone, everything burning. My grandmother used to tell me that if magic ever came back, we'd all be ash by winter." She reached out and touched one of the golden bean-flowers, gently, the way you'd touch something sleeping. "Nobody told me it might just be *beans*."

"That's what I keep trying to tell everyone," Ivo said. "I carried that box across half the world expecting it to be a weapon. It was a seed. Seeds don't take sides." He shifted his blankets. "Seeds mostly want dirt and patience."

"You'd know," Maren said dryly. "You've needed both long enough."

Paloma hid a smile and went back to the beds. This was how it went, most mornings now — bickering as liturgy, complaint as prayer. Sefa would have loved it. Sefa would have mapped the whole argument, labeled it, sold copies in three towns.

The thought still caught sometimes, a fishhook behind the ribs. But it caught less often than it used to, and when it caught now, Paloma found themselves thinking not only of the loss but of her last instruction: *make my maps mean something.* The maps were spread across the table inside, inked over in Paloma's careful scribe's hand, marking every place the wild magic had surfaced so far. Someday, maybe, travelers would use them. Someday, maybe, the world would need a map of where wonder grew.

Someday was starting to look possible. That was new.

* * *

Midmorning, the first of the valley survivors came down the road.

They had been coming for weeks — the people of Vell, freed when the devouring land died, blinking in a sun that no longer fed on them. Some had cursed Paloma. One had thrown a stone. Most simply wandered out of the valley gates like sleepers waking, squinting at a horizon they'd been taught was poison, asking in small voices what they were allowed to do now.

Elder Sorrel came today. The elder who had raised Paloma. The elder who had offered, once, to erase a child's debt if the child would only hand over a dying stranger — and who had watched that child choose otherwise.

Paloma straightened slowly, dirt on their knees, and waited.

Sorrel looked older than the mountains. She walked to the garden's edge and stood among the wrong-wonderful bean vines without touching them, her hands folded, her face doing the complicated arithmetic of a person who has been wrong about the most important thing in her life.

"The temple voted," she said. "We're dissolving the debt-rolls. All of them. Every ledger." Her voice caught. "It took us forty years to understand what you understood in one night."

"I didn't understand anything," Paloma said. "I ran."

"You chose," Sorrel corrected. "There's a difference, and it turns out it matters more than anything else." She looked around at the waystation — at Maren leaning in the doorway, at Ivo bundled on the porch, at Dagny humming worms out of the ground in the far field. At the strange, improbable household of it. "You have a family here."

It wasn't quite a question, but Paloma answered it anyway.

"I'm making one," they said. "That's different from having one. You have to tend it. Like the beans."

Sorrel nodded, and something in her shoulders loosened — a knot untied after decades. "Then I'll say what I came to say. The valley is empty, Paloma. Truly empty, for the first time in six hundred years. The land isn't hungry anymore. It's just... land." Her eyes glistened. "Some of us want to go back. Not to hide. To plant."

Paloma felt the old fear rise — the valley as cage, as mouth, as leech — and let it pass through and out, the way Sefa had taught them to let feelings move. What remained underneath was simpler: soil was soil. Even forgiven soil. Especially forgiven soil.

"Send word when the first crop comes up," Paloma said. "I'll want to see whether your beans show off too."

Sorrel laughed — wetly, wonderfully — and pressed something into Paloma's hand before turning back down the road. It was the old debt-ledger, the one with Paloma's name inked across forty years of columns. Torn in half.

Paloma stood holding the pieces for a long time after the elder vanished down the road. Debt-free. Rootless by choice. It should have felt like falling. Instead it felt like standing in turned earth, barefoot, waiting for something to grow.

* * *

Evening came down gold and violet, and the bean-lanterns lit themselves one by one, and the household gathered for the meal that had become their unbroken ritual: Ivo telling a story at twice its natural length, Dagny arguing about seasoning, Maren pretending not to enjoy any of it while taking seconds and thirds.

Afterward, Paloma stayed out in the garden alone, as they liked to. Their gift moved through their hands easily now — candle-flames bending toward them like curious birds, the watering can refilling itself with a sulky little gurgle, a single bean-vine curling affectionately around their wrist like a green bracelet. Small impossible things. No longer hidden. No longer feared.

Family is what you tend, they thought. Not where you're kept.

From the east, where the road climbed toward the dead-and-forgiving valley, a light appeared. Not a lantern — lanterns flickered, and this light burned steady and blue-white, moving fast along the ridge. Behind it, faint on the wind, came a sound Paloma had hoped never to hear again: the hunters' signal-call, three notes, high and urgent.

But Maren's hunters were all here, accounted for, safe.

Which meant someone else was using the crown's signals now. Someone coming down out of the hills toward the waystation, carrying a light that burned like the inside of the Emberbox.

Paloma set down the watering can and went to wake the others.
