## 2. A Stranger at the Loading Door

The closing bell at Hollow Guild rang twice: once for the counting house, once for the yard. Zephyrine heard both through the loading door's iron grille, where she stood with her satchel of finished carvings and her ledger of accounts not yet closed, and she thought — as she did every evening — that the second bell sounded like something being shut in a room.

She was not wrong. She rarely was, about sounds.

The yard lay in blue dusk, cobbles slick with the day's meltwater from the ice-house. Crates of traded things waited under oilcloth: a barrel of borrowed shadows bound for a widow who could no longer bear her own; a crate of years, pale and fibrous as pork fat, sold by a man too tired to live them; a small locked box whose manifest read only *one name, used*. The porters had gone. The tally-clerk had gone. Even the yard-cat, a scarred grey thing with an accountant's indifference to all living creatures, had gone.

Zephyrine stayed because she always stayed. Someone had to make certain the numbers and the objects agreed with one another, and no one else in the guild could hear what the objects said when they disagreed.

She was checking the seal on the shadow-barrel — the shadows inside had begun to murmur, which meant the wax needed refreshing — when the gate groaned.

Not opened. Groaned. A body's weight dragged across it from the outside, wood scraping stone, and then a hand appeared through the gap between gate and post, black against the blue, and gripped the iron ring so hard she heard the tendons crack.

"Help," said a voice like wet gravel. "Guild. Please. It has to be — it has to be the ledger-keeper."

Zephyrine did not move. This was the first rule of the counting house, drilled into every apprentice before they were allowed to touch bone: *the guild does not open its doors after bell.* Debts came in daylight. Grief made bad arithmetic.

But the voice knew her title. Not *carver*. Not *clerk*. *Ledger-keeper* — the word only guild members used, and only for her.

She crossed the yard at a walk, because running would have been an admission of fear, and lifted the bar.

He fell through the gap rather than stepped. A courier — she knew the breed instantly, the road-worn boots, the doublet with its riding slits, the leather document-case strapped flat against his chest the way couriers wore it so a knife thrust would have to go through the message first. He was maybe forty, grey-stubbled, and he was bleeding from three places she could count and one she couldn't: a slice along his scalp, a puncture below the ribs that wept darkly, and his left hand, which had been — she looked away, then made herself look back — which had been *opened*, cut palm to wrist in long deliberate lines, the way you'd gut a fish or flay a book.

Someone had been looking for what he carried.

"Lock it," he gasped. "Behind me. Lock the gate."

She hesitated. The dead she trusted; the living were another arithmetic entirely. But his blood was already pooling on her clean cobbles, spreading in a slow dark bloom like ink dropped into water, and some older instinct than guild law put her hands on the gate and drove the bar home.

"There," she said. "Now tell me—"

"No time." He caught her wrist with his ruined hand and the grip was shocking, iron-hot, and she felt the blood slide between her fingers, absurdly warm. "You're Vasquez. The bone-carver. They say you hear them." His eyes were fever-bright and terribly focused, the focus of a man who had spent the last hours deciding exactly what to do with his last minutes. "I need you to hear this."

"I'm not a priest," she said. "I keep accounts."

"So do I," he said, and laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that brought up red. "God help us both. Ledger-keeper, listen. Listen with whatever's left of you."

From the case against his chest, with fingers that shook and slipped and left smears, he drew a folded thing wrapped in oilcloth, and around it a cord, and at the cord's center a seal of black wax stamped with a device she had never seen and knew instantly, the way you know a face in a funeral photograph: a circle broken in seven places, seven small gaps like missing teeth.

"The covenant seals," he rasped. "All of them. Where they stand, which are broken, what got through. I carried it from—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. Names, she understood, were debts too. "It can't stay with me. They'll take it off my body. They'll be here soon — they let me run, don't you see, they *let* me, because they wanted to see who I ran to—"

His eyes went past her shoulder, to the dark windows of the counting house above the yard, and Zephyrine felt the hair rise on her arms. The guild quarter was not empty. The guild quarter was never empty. There were eyes in those windows, there were always eyes—

"No," she said. "You're wrong. No one—"

"They'll call me a thief," he said. "Whatever they say of me, know this: I stole nothing. I *carried*." He pressed the bundle into her hands, folding her fingers over it with his opened ones, and the wax seal touched her skin —

and burned cold.

That was the only way she could ever describe it afterward, though cold does not burn and wax is neither. It was the sensation of gripping an iron fence rail in deep winter, except the winter was inside the object, a cold with intent behind it, a cold that *noticed her*. The dead spoke to her through carved things all day long; this thing had not been carved yet, and still it spoke, one syllable repeated over and over like a knell: *soon, soon, soon.*

"Take it," the courier said. "Hide it in your work. You're a carver — hide it where only you would think to look. And when you've read it — when you understand what's coming at the turning of the year — find someone who still remembers the Warden's Oath. Not the guild's oath. The *Warden's*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"I don't understand any of this," Zephyrine said. It came out steadier than she felt. "Who broke the seals? Who cut your hand? Who—"

"The Warden's Oath still binds," he said.

And then, as if those six words had been the only cargo he'd needed to deliver and everything else was packing straw, he let go.

Not dramatically. That was the terrible part. He didn't clutch at her or cry out or grasp at the air. He simply sat back against the gate he'd made her lock, exhaled once, and became a man-shaped absence. The blood kept moving without him, creeping toward the drain-grate in thin black fingers. Above them the first stars came out over the counting house roof, indifferent as tally-marks.

Zephyrine knelt in the blood holding a bundle that burned cold, and found, to her horror, that she was listening.

She always listened. It was the affliction of her life. The dying left echoes the way footprints left dust, and hers were the ears they settled in. She braced for his echo — the usual grief-noise, the unfinished sentence, the *tell my wife, tell my son, I never said, I never—*

It didn't come.

Where his echo should have been there was silence, clean and complete, and beneath the silence something else: the bundle in her hands, whispering *soon, soon, soon,* and farther off, faint as a bell rung underwater, a sound she hadn't heard since childhood and had hoped never to hear again.

Chains. Many chains, far away, beginning to slip their hooks.

* * *

She had perhaps four minutes. She spent them badly and precisely.

First she folded the courier's hands — what remained of his — over the case on his chest, so he lay like a man asleep on the road. She could do nothing about the blood; blood tells its own story and would not be edited. Then she pressed the bundle flat against her stomach, inside her coat, where the satchel strap held it, and made herself breathe until the cold against her skin stopped feeling like a mouth.

Then the bells began again — not the closing bell but the watch-bell, quick and urgent, from the guild quarter's east wall — and torchlight swung into the lane beyond the gate.

"Open in the Guild's name!" A fist hammered the wood. "We have report of a man on the lane! Open!"

Zephyrine stood very still. Her mind, that reliable abacus, laid out her options with terrible clarity. *Open the gate, surrender the bundle, tell the truth:* then she was a woman who had received stolen covenant-secrets from a dying traitor, and the guild's justice was swift and mostly quiet. *Lie:* she had never lied in her life. Lying required a kind of ear she did not trust herself to have.

But the courier's blood was cooling on her shoes, and the bundle whispered *soon* against her ribs, and somewhere far off the chains slipped another hook.

She unbarred the gate.

Four enforcers came through with drawn steel and lanterns — guild livery, grey-and-bone, faces she half-knew from the corridors. Their sergeant, a heavy woman named Brakka with a face like a slammed door, took in the scene in one sweep: body, blood, Zephyrine.

"Carver." Brakka's voice was flat. "What happened here?"

"He came through the gate at closing bell," Zephyrine said. Each word was placed like a coin counted out onto a table. "Bleeding. He asked for help. He died before I could fetch anyone."

"Did he say anything?"

Here it was, then. The whole of her life balanced on the sergeant's next breath. Zephyrine looked down at the courier's ruined hand, at the lines cut into it, deliberate as script.

"He said," she heard herself reply, "that it was too late."

Brakka studied her for a long moment. Lantern-light moved across both their faces. Somewhere in the dark above, a window shutter eased closed — softly, deliberately, someone who had watched everything and wished to seem to have watched nothing.

"Search him," Brakka said finally, turning away. "Careful. Couriers carry more than paper."

They searched him while Zephyrine stood witness, hands folded, heart hammering against the hidden bundle as if it might answer their questions for her. They found the document-case. Empty. They found coin, a tin of matches, a worn prayer-card to a god whose name had been struck through with ink. They found nothing else, because there was nothing else to find; everything he had died carrying now rode against a bone-carver's stomach, wrapped in oilcloth, whispering.

"Empty," one of the enforcers said. "Robbed on the road?"

"Or delivered," Brakka said, and her eyes came up and found Zephyrine's and held them, and in them Zephyrine saw something that frightened her worse than steel: not suspicion.

*Patience.*

"Take the body to the mortuary crypt," Brakka ordered. "Carver Vasquez — the guildmaster will want your account in the morning. First audit bell. Don't be late." She paused at the gate, half-turned. "And Zephyrine. Whatever a dying stranger presses into your hands — the guild weighs all debts eventually. Remember whose scales you're standing on."

Then they were gone, taking the courier with them, leaving Zephyrine alone in the yard with the blood and the stars and the locked gate and the thing against her ribs saying *soon, soon, soon* in a voice like winter iron.

She stood a long time in the empty yard. Then, because the alternative was to scream, she went inside and climbed to her workroom and lit one lamp and unwrapped the bundle on her carving bench.

Inside the oilcloth lay a map — no, not quite a map. A chart of circles, seven of them, linked by lines of fine cramped script she did not recognize and yet somehow could *almost* hear, the way you almost hear speech through a wall. Three of the seven circles had been marked with a carver's scribe, deep deliberate cuts: broken. Around the margins, in a different hand, someone had written a single line in trade-code, the guild's own cipher, the cipher only ledger-keepers learned:

*The Warden's Oath still binds. Ask the bones who wrote it.*

Zephyrine sat down slowly on her stool. Outside, the year leaned toward its turning, and far beneath the city something vast shifted its weight against doors built to hold it, and in her hands the map burned cold, and burned, and burned.

In the morning she would lie to the guildmaster. Tonight, first, she would need a bone large enough to hide a secret in.

Her eyes went, without her willing it, to the half-carved femur on the shelf — and stopped.

Because under the lamp, in fresh scribe-cuts she had not made, the femur had already begun to answer.
