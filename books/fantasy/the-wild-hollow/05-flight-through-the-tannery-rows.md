## 5. Flight Through the Tannery Rows

The brand came at dawn, and it did not touch her skin.

Guildmaster Vasse had no need for irons. By first bell, every ledger in the counting house carried a new line in red wax-seal ink: *Zephyrine Vasquez, bone-carver of the third bench — thief of guild property, suspected murderer of courier Ivo Merrin.* By second bell, the line had been read aloud in the mess hall, the assay room, the loading yard. By third bell, the enforcers were knocking on her door in the carvers' dormitory with a politeness that frightened her more than shouting would have.

She was already gone.

She had gone out through the bone-dust chute, the one she'd cleaned every week for six years without once wondering where it emptied. It emptied into the rendering yard, behind a wall of hanging hides, and from there the guild quarter opened before her like a book she suddenly could not read. Streets she had walked ten thousand times now walked themselves toward her, each corner a hand reaching to close around her wrist. A pair of assay-clerks looked up as she passed and did not greet her. That was how she knew it was real. In six years they had never once failed to nod.

*Run,* said the bones.

They had been saying it since before dawn — the little congregation in her satchel, the relics she'd been carrying home to finish: a knuckle-bone of a ferryman, a finger joint of a seamstress, three vertebrae of an old soldier whose name she'd never learned because he hadn't wanted her to have it. They didn't speak in words, exactly. Speaking was what the living did, clumsily, in air. What the dead did was press meaning against the inside of her skull like thumbs against wet clay. And all morning they had pressed one thing only.

*Run. Run. Run.*

So she ran.

* * *

The tannery rows began where the guild quarter ended, a mile of low stone sheds strung along the river's stink like beads on a rotten cord. The dye-vats stood open to the sky, great sunken cauldrons of madder-red and woad-blue and a green so deep it seemed to have opinions. Smoke from the smoke-sheds lay over everything in a grey wool blanket. Nobody honest came here. Nobody dishonest needed to — the tanners kept their own law, which was mostly the law of not asking.

Zephyrine moved down the row with her hood up and her satchel clutched to her chest like a child.

The dead were quieter here. Not silent — they were never silent — but muffled, the way voices are muffled underwater. She felt them shifting against her hip as she ran, restless, and she understood why when she saw the smoke-sheds: hides curing on frames, and beneath each frame, a shallow trench of ash. Ash from burned bones. The tanneries bought their lime and their curing-ash cheap, and cheap ash came from the bone-yards, and the bone-yards took whatever the city gave them, named or unnamed. Every shed she passed was full of the unquiet residue of people who had been rendered into chemistry.

*I'm sorry,* she thought at them, uselessly, the way you apologize to weather. *I'm sorry, I'm sorry.* She had never been able to fix all of them. No one could hear them all. That had always been the shape of her life: a woman standing in rain, catching what drops she could in cupped hands, while the rest fell through her fingers everywhere.

Behind her, bells. Not the closing bell, not the assay bell. The hunt bell — a short ugly clangor rung only twice in her memory, both times for men who had stolen years from the guild's stores. It rolled over the tannery rows and made the smoke tremble.

*Left,* pressed the ferryman's knuckle-bone, sharp as a struck flint. *Left, left, left.*

She went left, between two vats of madder, and not four breaths later heard boots on the stone where she would have been — two pairs, maybe three, moving fast, unhurried in the way of people who know the exits are closed. She pressed herself into the shadow between a vat and the shed wall and put her hand over her own mouth, because her breathing sounded enormous to her, a bellows, a forge.

The boots passed. One of the walkers paused at the mouth of the lane and stood there, and she watched his shadow stretch across the wet cobbles toward her, long and thin as a drawn blade, and she thought: *he can see me, he is going to turn, this is where I die in a tannery yard among strangers' ashes.*

Then the seamstress's finger joint pressed something against the inside of her skull — not a word, a picture. A drain-grate. Below. She looked down. There it was under her feet, half hidden by dye-stain, an iron grate over a culvert channel that ran beneath the rows carrying away the vats' overflow. She eased it up with fingers that shook, lowered herself into cold water to her waist, and drew the grate back over her head just as the shadow detached itself from its owner's boots and swept across the spot where she had been standing.

In the dark under the rows, chest-deep in dyer's water, she finally let herself shake.

*Good girl,* said the old soldier's vertebrae, and for once the dead sounded almost kind.

* * *

The culverts ran everywhere under the tannery rows; everyone knew that and no one knew their map. The water carried her — or rather she carried herself through it, wading, sometimes swimming short black stretches, guided now less by sight than by the satchel. The dead had opinions about direction. The ferryman, who had spent forty years reading rivers, was loudest: *left at the fork, right where the water smells of iron, stop — stop — someone above.*

Twice she stopped and doused her small lantern (she had taken it from the rendering-yard hook; theft upon theft; let them add it to my ledger) and stood in total darkness listening to footsteps cross some grate overhead. Twice the footsteps passed.

Once, worse than footsteps: a voice. Sable Fen's voice, she was almost sure, though she had only heard the guildmaster's chief enforcer speak perhaps a dozen times in her life, always in the cadence of someone reading a sentence they intended to finish later. "—checked the smoke-sheds. Nothing." A pause. "She'll go to ground somewhere she knows. Bone-carver. They're all the same. Watch the bone-yards."

Then the voice faded, taking its boots with it, and Zephyrine stood in the dark water and discovered a new thing about fear: that it has layers, and the deepest layer is not terror but recognition. *Watch the bone-yards.* Fen knew her. Knew her the way you know a tool you've used for years. And Zephyrine realized she had spent six years believing herself invisible inside the guild — a woman at a bench, dust on her sleeves, important only to the dead — when in fact she had been visible the whole time, catalogued, anticipated, *watchable*. You cannot be invisible in a ledger. Everything in a ledger is counted. She had simply never considered herself among the things worth counting.

*You are counted now,* said the dead, without cruelty. Just fact. The dead were very good at fact.

She waded on.

Her legs had gone from cold to numb to a distant ache when the culvert opened at last into a wider channel, and the channel into a sort of vaulted junction where three drains met under a dome of old brick furred white with mineral bloom. Grey light came down through a broken grate far above, falling in a dusty column onto the water. It was almost beautiful. Almost peaceful.

That was when the water ahead of her moved wrong.

It was subtle — a ripple against the current's grain, a suggestion of bulk where there should have been openness. Then a lantern flared at the far side of the junction, and she saw them: three figures, waders to the thigh, spaced wide, blocking the channel where it narrowed toward the river gate. Guild livery under oilskin. Crossbows, wrapped against the damp, but wrapped the way you unwrap a thing you mean to use.

"Bone-carver," said the middle one, and it was Fen after all — Sable Fen in person, grey-eyed, patient, terrible. Her voice carried flat off the wet brick. "Guildmaster says come up quiet and we'll talk about the page. She says you're not a thief. She says she'll make the ledger say so."

Every instinct Zephyrine had ever trusted told her this was mercy. Six years of Odile Vasse's gentle voice, of protection offered at reasonable prices, rose in her like warm water. *Take the bargain. Go up quiet. Be a tool again, a well-kept tool, alive.*

But she had lied to the guildmaster once now, and the lie had changed her the way a first cut changes a blade. She knew what *talk about the page* meant. She had seen what happened to couriers who carried things Odile wanted.

"Ivo Merrin," Zephyrine said. Her voice echoed strangely in the dome. "He bled out in our loading yard holding a map. Your guildmaster wants me to believe she'll write me innocent."

"Guildmaster," Fen repeated, tasting the word, "writes everything eventually."

The dead screamed — all of them at once, a pressure like a wall falling — and Zephyrine dropped flat into the water a half-heartbeat before the crossbow string snapped. The bolt went over her, chipped brick somewhere in the dark, and the junction erupted into noise and lantern-light and the sound of wading boots driving toward her through water that suddenly seemed determined to hold her, to slow her, to be the medium of her drowning.

She got two strokes. Maybe three. Her satchel caught on something — a grate-tooth, a fallen bar — and she turned to free it and saw Fen close, terribly close, raising the crossbow again at a range no one could miss, and Zephyrine thought with strange clarity: *the dead will finally get to speak to me directly.*

A shape fell out of the dark overhead.

It came off the junction wall or the broken grate above — she never saw exactly — a big man in a ragged oilcloak, and he hit the water between her and Fen like a dropped beam, and the splash alone put the lanterns out. Then there was fighting in the dark, and the fighting was brief and brutal and full of sounds she would remember: the crack of a crossbow stock used as a club, a man's grunt folding into water, Fen's voice — calm even then, calm the way ice is calm — saying, "Corvane," in a tone that held more history than anger. "Of course. Of course it's you."

"Hello, Sable," said the man, breathing hard. "Tell your mistress the wardens aren't all dead. Some of us are just unemployed."

Boots retreated. Lantern-light swung away up the channel, shrinking. The junction went quiet except for water dripping off brick and the ragged breathing of a stranger.

Zephyrine stayed flat in the water, satchel crushed under her, heart slamming, and stared at the tall silhouette against the column of grey light. He was looking down at her. She couldn't see his face, only that he was still, controlled, the stillness of a man who had done violence recently and knew exactly how much.

"You're bleeding," she said, because it was the first true thing she found. There was blood thinning into the water near him; she could smell the copper under the dye-stink, and the dead in her satchel had gone abruptly attentive, the way dogs point.

"A scratch." His voice was rough gravel, unhurried. "You're the carver. Vasquez. The one Merrin died reaching."

The name landed in her chest like a stone in still water. "How do you know that name?"

"Because I trained him." He waded closer, slowly, hands visible, empty — a courtesy, or a performance of one. "Ivo Merrin. Covenant-warden, second class. I signed his commission myself, back when I still had a rank to sign with." He stopped an arm's length away. Water dripped from him. Up close, in the grey light, she saw a face built for scowling and currently doing something rarer: deciding. "And I know what he was carrying, because I'm the one who told him where to carry it."

"The map," she whispered. Against her hip, inside the satchel, the half-carved femur with the map folded in its hollow core felt suddenly, enormously heavy — a coal, a star, a debt.

His eyes went to the satchel as if the word had weight enough to bend toward. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into something older than anger. Reverence, maybe, if reverence could rot.

"Seven seals bind the Hollow, bone-carver. Three are broken, and the guild knows, and the guild has decided that knowing is cheaper than telling." He held out his hand, palm up, over the dark water. "Merrin died getting that map to someone who could read bone-script. Everyone said there was one such person left in the city."

The dead pressed against her, all of them at once — the ferryman, the seamstress, the nameless soldier, and beneath them, faint and cold as a coin at the bottom of a well, the lingering echo of Ivo Merrin himself, saying what he had said with his last wet breath:

*The Warden's Oath still binds.*

Zephyrine looked at the offered hand. She thought of the counting house, of Odile's smile, of the ledger line written in red wax-seal ink that made her a thief in both realms. She thought: *I have trusted the living exactly once today, and the living shot at me.*

But she also thought: *the dead sent me left at the fork, and left at the fork was true.*

She did not take his hand. She stood, water streaming off her, and held the satchel against her chest, and said, "You'll tell me everything first. All of it. Before I show you anything."

Something moved across his face that might, on a younger man, have been hope.

"That," Corvane said, "is the first honest bargain anyone's offered me in six years."

Above them, faint through the broken grate, the hunt bell began to ring again.
