## 8. Market of Borrowed Years

The road to the orchard ran through the one place in the Hollow's borderlands where the guild had never needed to hide its work.

Corvane smelled it before he saw it — tallow and wet copper and something sweeter underneath, like fruit rotting in a locked room. Zephyrine walked a half-step behind him with her satchel of carvings pressed to her ribs, and every few paces her hand drifted to it the way other people touched a wound.

"The Tallowmarket," he said. "We go through it or we lose two days climbing the scree, and we don't have two days."

"I know what it is." Her voice was flat. "I kept its ledgers for six years. I know exactly what it is."

He had learned that about her in three days on the road: she did not flinch at horror. She itemized it. It should have made her cold. Instead it made him want to put himself between her and everything they were walking toward, which was an impulse he distrusted profoundly, because impulses like that got wardens killed, and he was no longer a warden. He was an exile with a scarred oath-hand and five days' worth of food, following a bone-carver toward a seal that had been broken by something neither of them could yet name.

"Rules," he said. "Don't trade anything you can't see weighed. Don't give your name. Don't eat, don't drink, don't sit down anywhere. And if anyone asks what your gift is—"

"Lie," she said. "I've had practice lately."

The market opened below them like a wound in the hillside.

It had been built inside the ribcage of something enormous — a leviathan, maybe, or one of the older gods the Hollow had eaten when it was young. Ribs arched overhead as pale as cathedral stone, hung with lanterns that burned without flame, each one glowing a different color, and Corvane knew — because he had enforced the covenant long enough to know — that every lantern was a year. Someone's year. Bought, bottled, and burned for light so that traders could see the faces of the people they were robbing.

Stalls lined the spaces between ribs. Not stalls of goods: stalls of hours. Traders sat behind brass scales, and the customers sat across from them looking calm and terrible, rolling up their sleeves or opening their collars, while thin silver instruments slid under their skin and drew out something that shimmered like heat off summer stone.

"They're awake during it," Zephyrine said quietly. "The extraction. The guild's own regulations require a numbing agent, but out here—" She stopped. He watched her jaw set. "Out here it costs less if the seller feels all of it. Pain is proof of authenticity. They call it *witnessing*."

"You don't have to look."

"Yes, I do." She lifted her chin. "That's the whole point of me, isn't it? I'm the one who has to look. Who always has to look."

They descended into the market together.

* * *

The first stall-keeper who approached them wore guild colors gone gray at the seams — a former enforcer's tabard cut down into a merchant's apron. Corvane recognized the type immediately. He had been the type once: someone the institution used up and discarded, now selling back to strangers the only thing the guild had ever taught them to take.

"You two are walking wrong," the trader said. His teeth were stained blue from chewing covenant-moss, the cheap kind, the kind that let you dream in someone else's memories. "Walking like folk with somewhere to be. Folk with somewhere to be pay the toll."

"There's no toll on this road," Corvane said. "There hasn't been since the Warden's Charter."

The trader smiled. "And how would you know about charters, friend?"

Corvane kept his face still. Three days ago he would have answered with his rank and watched the man's spine bend. But rank was a coin that spent badly here; exiled wardens were worse than strangers. Strangers were merely unknown. Exiles were known and found wanting.

Zephyrine saved him without seeming to. She stepped forward and produced, from some deep pocket, a bone token — small, carved, unmistakably guild-work — and turned it over in her fingers with the bored fluency of a woman handling inventory. "Counting-house courier," she said. "Ledger-line seven. We're auditing the tolls, actually. So either show me your books, or get out of the way of the people whose books those are."

It was a beautiful lie. It was also, Corvane noted with something uncomfortably close to admiration, built entirely from truth: she *had* been a counting-house keeper, there *was* a ledger-line seven, and the token was real. She lied the way good accountants balanced columns — every falsehood offset by a verifiable fact.

The trader's smile died. He bowed — shallowly, resentfully — and melted back between the ribs.

"You didn't tell me you carried tokens," Corvane murmured as they moved on.

"I carried forty years of guild paperwork. You'd be amazed what gets left in the lining of a satchel when a woman flees her entire life in one afternoon." She glanced sideways at him. "You didn't tell me you knew the Warden's Charter well enough to cite it to a smuggler. We're even."

"We're not even," he said, "but we're close enough."

* * *

Deeper in, the market changed character, the way water changes when it stops being a stream and becomes a flood.

The stalls here sold larger things. A woman with a ledger of her own offered thirty years for "any honest labor in the Hollow, terms negotiable, no questions asked past the gate." A boy of perhaps fifteen stood in line to sell his childhood — literally, a vial labeled in careful script *SUMMER, AGE SEVEN TO ELEVEN* — and Corvane had to physically stop himself from dragging the child out of the queue, because he remembered what happened the last time a warden intervened in a voluntary sale. The buyer had simply waited until the warden moved on. Nothing had changed except that the boy had been beaten afterward, for making trouble.

That memory had a face attached to it. Most of his memories did, these days.

"Corvane." Zephyrine's voice pulled him back. She had stopped beside an archway between two ribs, where the lantern-light fell differently — redder, thicker. Through the arch he could see the second half of the market: quieter, carpeted, the scales here made of glass instead of brass. The buyers' side.

"That's where the purchased years go," she said. "That's where they're *spent*. Look at them." And he looked, though he wished he hadn't. Old men drinking young blood-warm decades from cups. A merchant woman with the unlined face of a girl and eyes like a ledger's margins, empty and ruled. One figure sat alone in a curtained booth, and around the booth the air itself seemed younger — greener, softer, as if time pooled there the way water pools in a low place.

"The guild doesn't run this end," Zephyrine said. "I checked the accounts myself, years ago. There's a whole revenue stream here that never touches the ledgers. I flagged it twice. Both times my flag came back stamped *authorized above line*." Her hands had curled into fists. "I thought I'd found an embezzlement. I think I found the guild's actual business."

"Odile knows about this place," Corvane said. "Odile *built* this place. The seals started failing within a year of this market opening. I told the council that. They buried my report in the same drawer they bury everything that inconveniences the quarterly balance." He heard his own voice come out harder than he intended, and made himself lower it. "Every year bought here is a year taken out of the world's account. The covenant is a wall. This market is a door cut through it, and the guild holds the hinge."

"So we burn it down after," Zephyrine said. Not a question. Not quite a request.

"After," he agreed. "First the seals."

A bell rang somewhere among the upper ribs — not the market's bell. A guild bell, the two-tone chime used for official traffic. Heads turned all down the row. Corvane felt the crowd's attention slide over them like oil over water, checking, dismissing — and then catching, briefly, on Zephyrine's satchel, on the shape of it, before moving on.

But not everyone moved on. At the far end of the aisle, near the red-lit archway, a man in a gray traveling cloak had stopped pretending to browse.

Corvane knew him. That was the problem with serving twenty years in one institution: you could not walk through any market in the borderlands without meeting your own past wearing someone else's livery. Doss Merrow. Third-rank tracker. Competent, patient, and loyal in the specific way of men whose loyalty had never once been tested, and who therefore believed it was limitless.

Doss was not looking at Corvane. He was looking at Zephyrine. And he was reaching, slowly, casually, into his cloak.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and Corvane would remember it afterward in pieces, the way he remembered all his worst days.

Doss whistled — two notes, the tracker's signal for *target sighted, hold position*. Down the aisle, two figures in undyed wool detached themselves from a stall and began moving with the loose-hipped walk of guild hunters off the leash.

"Walk," Corvane said to Zephyrine, without turning his head. "Don't run. Runners get chased."

"Where?"

"Anywhere crowded. The extraction rows. They won't draw blades mid-sale — the buyers complain."

She walked. He walked beside her, angling his body so that his shoulder and the bulk of his coat broke Doss's line of sight. Behind them the whistle sounded again, sharper, closer, and then a third note he did not like at all: *assistance required.*

They reached the extraction rows.

Up close the stalls were worse than they had seemed from a distance. The silver instruments were not instruments at all but living wire — hollow-bone worms, covenant-spawn, bred to taste the difference between a year honestly lived and a year stolen, and to refuse the stolen ones. Each seller lay on a slanted bench while the worm slid beneath the skin of the forearm and drank. The sound they made was the worst part. Not screaming. Something smaller than screaming. A rhythmic, involuntary *hnn, hnn, hnn*, like a person trying to breathe through a stitch in the side, going on and on, minute after minute, while a clerk at the bench's foot marked the quantity drawn in a column that never, ever showed a remainder.

Zephyrine stopped dead in the middle of the row.

One of the sellers was a child. Ten, maybe eleven, lying on the bench with her sleeve rolled up, watching the worm go under her skin with the terrible patience children have when adults have explained that this is simply how things are done. Her mother stood at the bench's head holding her free hand. On the mother's other wrist: a fresh extraction scar, weeks old, still pink.

"She's paying our passage," Zephyrine whispered. "Look at the clerk's book. Look at the entry."

Corvane looked. The clerk wrote in standard guild shorthand, and Corvane had spent enough years reading confiscated ledgers to parse it upside down: *Passage, two travelers, hill road to the Orchard, paid in full — five (5) years, adult female, witnessed.* Beside it, the child's entry: *five (5) years, juvenile, witnessed, premium rate.*

Two entries. Two travelers. Their description matched to the color of Zephyrine's coat.

"They already know we're coming," Corvane breathed. "Someone sold word ahead. The toll's been posted against us." Which meant Doss wasn't tracking them at all. Doss was *collecting* — and the mother buying her daughter's passage with ten combined years was just the mechanism, the poor blunt instrument the market used to move debts around. When the hunters took them at the far end of the row, the toll-collector would collect from the mother, and the mother would have sold her childhood and her daughter's for nothing.

Unless no one collected.

"Change of plan," Corvane said. "The toll needs to be paid before we reach the gate, or the collectors take it out of her. So we pay it ourselves. Now. At this bench. Before the hunters close the distance."

"With what?" Zephyrine's face had gone white. "We have no years to sell. I have *nothing*, Corvane, I fled my life with a satchel of dead people's grievances—"

"I have something," he said.

And he understood, in the moment he said it, that he had understood this for the last hundred yards and had said nothing, and that he was going to keep saying nothing, because there was no version of this conversation in which Zephyrine Vasquez allowed him to do what he was about to do. She would argue. She would offer herself instead — and her years were not hers to sell. Whatever else her gift was, it was threaded through her lifetime like dye through wool; pull out a year of it and you might pull out the hearing, the map-reading, the only thing standing between the Hollow and the city. He had seen the guild test that theory once, early in his service, on a listener in the western houses. He still dreamed about what the man had become afterward: alive, healthy, and silent, carving bones that said nothing, weeping at the silence like a man locked out of his own house.

His years, though. His years were just years. He was forty-six years old, exiled, wounded, useful for perhaps one more campaign. Five years was nothing against four broken seals and a city of strangers he had sworn, in whatever remained of his oath, to keep.

"Sit down," he told the clerk, "and bring the worm."

* * *

The bench was warm from the child before him. That was the detail that stayed with him afterward — not the pain, though the pain was considerable, a cold threading sensation that climbed from his wrist toward his elbow like ice water finding the veins — but the warmth of the wood, left there by a ten-year-old girl who had just sold five summers so that two strangers could pass along a road.

The clerk inked the entry. *Five (5) years, adult male, witnessed.* Corvane watched the worm drink and made himself count, the way he had counted during the interrogation after his report was buried, the way he counted during everything unbearable: *one, two, three, four.* The extracted year came up the instrument's throat as a bead of light, gold-white, trembling. The clerk decanted it into a vial and held it up to the lantern.

"Good vintage," the clerk said, with the cheerful obscenity of all middlemen. "Clean years. You'll feel it tomorrow — bit of gray coming back early, joints talking to you. Nothing serious."

"Keep the change," Corvane said, and slid off the bench.

Zephyrine was staring at him. Not at the transaction — at *him*, with an expression he had not seen on her before and could not read, something between suspicion and grief, as if she were listening to a frequency he couldn't hear. Of course she was. She was always listening to frequencies he couldn't hear. Perhaps the dead had told her. Perhaps the dead told her everything, and she was kind enough, most of the time, not to say.

"What did you give them?" she asked.

"Passage," he said. "Same as anyone."

"Corvane."

"Hunters," he said, nodding down the row, because it was true — Doss and his two companions had entered the extraction rows and were working toward them through the benches, unhurried, professional — and the lie of redirection worked exactly as well as lies always worked on people who trusted you, which is to say: temporarily, at cost.

They moved. Ahead, the market's far gate opened onto the hill road, and beyond the road, faint in the evening haze, he could see the dark line of trees that marked the Orchard's boundary — the oaths-grown ground where the second seal slept beneath roots that fed on spoken promises. The toll-clerk waved them through without looking up. Somewhere behind them the mother gathered up her daughter, both of them spared, both of them lighter by five years apiece, walking away into the lantern-dark.

Five years. He flexed his right hand and found it aching in a way it hadn't ached that morning, a fine cold ache, like frost arriving ahead of schedule. Tomorrow the gray would come in early. In ten years he would feel sixty. If there was a ten years. He found, examining himself honestly, that he could not make himself mind. There was a strange arithmetic in it: five years traded for a woman who heard the dead, walking toward an orchard of teeth, and for one moment on the bench the child's mother had squeezed his hand and mouthed *thank you* with such naked relief that it had been worth more than the years, more than any reinstatement, more than the oath he had broken and the one he was still, quietly, without permission, keeping.

Loyalty, he was discovering, did not announce itself. It took root the way the covenant-oaths had taken root in the Orchard's soil — silently, underground, in the dark, feeding on things you had said without meaning to say them.

They passed under the gate's arch. The lantern-light of the market fell away behind them, and the night opened ahead, cold and starless, the stars smothered by the same creeping haze that had followed them since the drowned chapel.

Zephyrine stopped at the roadside and turned to face him fully, and the look on her face had resolved into something he liked much less than suspicion.

"The clerk's book," she said. "When you paid. I read the entry over his shoulder." Her voice was very quiet. "Five years, Corvane. Adult male. Witnessed." The wind moved through the satchel at her hip, and from inside it, faintly, one of the carved relics began — very softly — to sing.

"And before you decide what to say next," she went on, "you should know that I can hear the dead. And the year you just sold them?" She nodded back toward the market, toward the vial already shelved in the buyers' booths, already cooling toward someone else's cup. "It's still singing too. And it remembers exactly whose heartbeat it grew in."

Behind them, far down the hill road, torchlight bloomed — Sable Fen's outriders, or something worse, closing the distance they had just bought with everything the purchase had cost.

"Run now," Zephyrine said. "Explain while we run."
