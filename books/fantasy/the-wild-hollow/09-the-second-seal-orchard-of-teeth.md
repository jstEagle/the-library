## 9. The Second Seal: Orchard of Teeth

The orchard announced itself by smell before sight — a sweetness like overripe pears and something beneath it, coppery and wrong, the way blood smells when it has been sitting too long in a warm room.

Zephyrine stopped at the crest of the hill and looked down into the valley where the map's bone-script had promised the second seal. What she saw made her hand drift to Corvane's sleeve without her permission.

The trees were beautiful. That was the first obscenity of it. Row upon row of them, silver-barked, their branches heavy with pale fruit that caught the grey morning light and held it, glowing faintly from within. Each fruit was the size of a plum, smooth as riverstone, white as milk.

Each one was a tooth. A human tooth, grown to impossible size, root and all, hanging from the branch like a lantern.

"They're oath-fruits," Corvane said quietly. He had gone still in the way he did before danger — not tense, but emptied, a man setting everything nonessential down. "I told you the covenant-oaths were living things. When the first wardens swore, their words were planted. This is what grew."

"Words don't grow teeth."

"These did." He started down the slope, and she followed because following him had become, somewhere in the last two weeks, the shape her days took. "Every promise ever sworn on the covenant passed through this ground. The seal is beneath it. The trees are the lock, and the fruit is the keyhole — but you have to put your tongue in it first."

She understood then why the sweetness carried copper underneath. "Eating one does something to you."

"It makes you honest." He said it flatly, without looking back. "Completely. Irreversibly for a time. The old wardens called it *paying the toll*. You cannot approach the second seal with a lie in your mouth. The ground will know. The trees will know." He paused at the first row of trees, close enough now that Zephyrine could see the fine grain of each enormous tooth, the faint translucence at the crown, and something dark coiled in every root system below — threads running underground from tree to tree to tree, a lattice of them, like veins. Like the roots were holding hands.

"You've eaten one," she said.

"I've eaten three." He still didn't look at her. "Once to be sworn in. Once to testify against my own commander. And once—" his jaw worked, "—once to prove to a tribunal that I hadn't broken my oath, when they'd already decided I had. Truth doesn't save you, Zephyrine. It only makes sure everyone watches clearly while they do it anyway."

The dead murmured in her satchel — the little carved relics she carried, each one holding an echo, each one a voice she couldn't refuse. She found herself listening past them, to the orchard itself. And there — God help her — the orchard was *full*. Not the dead exactly, but something adjacent: the residue of ten thousand oaths, promises worn into the soil like footprints into stone. *I swear. I swear. I so swear.* It sounded like rain. It sounded like a congregation.

"I can hear them," she said. "The oaths. They're all still... saying themselves."

Corvane turned. For a moment his face did something unguarded — a flicker of pity, or fear for her, or both braided together. "Then you already know more about this place than any warden alive. Can you tell which tree holds the seal?"

She closed her eyes and let the murmur wash through her. Most of the voices were formless, ancient, worn smooth as pebbles. But beneath them ran a deeper note, one thread in the root-lattice that pulsed slow and strong, and all the other oaths bent toward it slightly, the way iron filings lean toward a lodestone.

"There." She pointed to a tree near the orchard's heart, broader than the others, its fruit denser, its crown heavy enough that the branches bowed. "That one. The others are leaning toward it."

"The anchor-tree." He exhaled. "Good. Then we only need to get there, dig to the seal, and read the bone-script. Which means eating the fruit."

They stood a moment in the shadow of the first row. Above them the teeth glowed softly, patiently, like they could wait forever. They probably could.

"How long does the honesty last?" Zephyrine asked.

"Hours. Maybe a day." Now he looked at her, and his grey eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "I won't lie to you about what's coming, even though in a few minutes neither of us will be able to. There are things I would rather carry to my grave than say aloud. After the fruit, I won't have that choice. Neither will you. So if there's anything you need to keep — keep it now. Hold it behind your teeth. The toll takes what's in your mouth, not what's in your head."

*There is one thing,* she thought. *One thing I've never said to anyone.*

Her mother's name lived in her chest like a splinter too deep to draw. Not the death — the guild had recorded the death, neat as arithmetic, fever in the winter of Zephyrine's fourteenth year, account closed. No. The thing she kept was what happened after. The thing she had never confessed, not to Odile, not to the guild's confessors, not to herself in the honest dark hours: that among the hundred thousand voices of the dead, one voice had been there since the beginning. That she knew her mother's whisper the way you know your own heartbeat. And that her mother *begged*, every day, in words Zephyrine had spent twenty years refusing to translate.

She looked up at the glowing fruit and felt, for the first time since the courier died in her arms, genuine terror.

"All right," she said.

They picked the fruit together. It came off the branch with a sound like a sigh, warm in her palm, heavier than it should have been. Up close she could see whorls in its surface — tiny lines, script-fine. She touched one and flinched. It was writing. Each tooth was carved, impossibly, with someone's oath in letters too small for any eye.

"Swear nothing as you eat it," Corvane said. "Not even casually. Not even *I swear I'll follow you*. The ground collects."

He bit into his first. The sound was horrible — dense, wet, like biting through ivory. He chewed, swallowed, and stood with his eyes closed, waiting, a man letting a flood take him.

Zephyrine lifted hers to her mouth. It smelled of pear and copper. Somewhere under her ribs, the dead went quiet — all of them, every voice in her satchel, hushing as if attending a service.

She bit down.

The taste was sweet, then salt, then — beneath both — the taste of a word being spoken very slowly by someone who meant it. It went down cold and spread through her chest like water finding low places.

And then the truth came out of her like blood from a cut, whether she wanted it or not.

"I'm afraid of you," she heard herself say, clear and calm, to Corvane, who had opened his eyes. "Not of what you'll do. Of how much I've started to want you standing next to me. I haven't wanted anything beside my duty in eleven years and it terrifies me."

The words hung in the orchard air. The roots below seemed to drink them in; she felt the lattice shiver.

Corvane stared at her. His own mouth moved before his pride could stop it. "I reported the breaches knowing they'd destroy me for it," he said, "because I thought duty was all I was, and if I lost it I'd be empty. Then I met you, and I found out the emptiness was already there, and you were the first thing that fit inside it. I would trade the rest of my life for another month of walking beside you, and I hate myself for it, because a warden shouldn't—"

He stopped himself. Or tried. The orchard wouldn't let him. "A warden shouldn't need anything," he finished, quieter. "But I need you. There. The ground has it now. It keeps everything."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The teeth glowed above them. The oaths rained down their endless patient litany. Zephyrine felt stripped open, skinned of every careful layer she'd worn since girlhood, and underneath — instead of the shame she'd braced for — there was something almost like relief. Like setting down a load she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"So that's what clean air feels like," she said.

"Yes." He almost smiled. It transformed his whole face and frightened her more than the hunters had. "Come on. The seal won't wait, and neither will whatever else wants out of us."

* * *

They walked the rows toward the anchor-tree, and the orchard taxed them the whole way.

It came in bursts, ungovernable, the truth leaping out of their mouths at odd intervals like sparks off a flint. Corvane admitted he slept with a knife under his pillow because quiet reminded him of the tribunal chamber. Zephyrine admitted she talked to her carvings while she worked, full conversations, and had done since childhood, and had once been caught doing it by a guild tutor who called her touched and recommended her for reassignment — and that Odile had intervened, had protected her, and Zephyrine had never known why until she began to suspect the answer was *leverage*.

"I resented the dead for years," she heard herself say as they ducked under a low bough, its fruit brushing her hair like cold fingers. "Everyone thinks the gift is a haunting. It's worse. It's a queue. They line up. They wait their turn. And they're all so *reasonable* about their suffering, they present their cases like clerks, and every single one of them is right, and I have never once in my life been able to say no."

"Why not?" Corvane asked. And then, because the orchard owned his tongue too: "Why do you never refuse them?"

She stopped walking.

The question sat in the air between the trees, and the whole orchard seemed to lean in — the roots, the lattice, the ten thousand buried oaths, all of them waiting on her answer with the patience of things that had waited centuries already.

"Because of my mother," she said.

It came out simply. That was the horror of it — no sob, no breaking, just plain speech, the way you'd report a ledger figure.

"She's in the queue. She has always been in the queue. From the first night after she died, when the voices came to me and I thought I was losing my mind — she was there, at the back, patient as the rest of them. And she never pushed forward. Never demanded. She just waits, and every time the queue thins and it might be her turn, she steps back and lets someone else go first. Twenty years of that. Twenty years of hearing her wait her turn behind strangers." Her voice stayed level; her hands shook. "Because she knows what she wants to ask me for. And she knows I'd do it. Whatever it is. She knows I'd burn the covenant itself to give it to her, and she loves me too much to ask. So she waits. And I let her wait, because as long as she's waiting, she's still here. As long as I never finish her business, I never lose her twice."

The orchard drank it in. She felt the words go down through her feet into the root-lattice, joining the archive, sworn and kept forever.

Corvane didn't touch her. She understood later that this restraint was the most intimate thing he could have done — that he stood one pace away, in the glow of the teeth, and gave her the dignity of her own grief.

"My mother sold years," he said finally. "To feed us. Guild-contracted, legal, eleven years off the end of her life. She died at fifty-one looking ninety, and the guild sent a form letter thanking her family for her contribution. I joined the wardens to make sure the ledger balanced for people like her. And then I found out who was keeping the ledger." His jaw tightened. "So I understand queues. I understand unfinished business. Mine has been unfinished for nine years."

"We're a pair of accounts that won't close," Zephyrine said.

"The worst kind." He glanced at her sidelong, and there it was again — the almost-smile, warmer this time, helpless. "Neither of us is going to survive being this honest with each other."

"No," she agreed. Her heart was doing something reckless and unbudgeted. "Probably not."

They reached the anchor-tree as the light began to fail.

* * *

Up close it was vast, its trunk wide as a well-mouth, and its fruit hung so thickly the branches had bowed to the ground, forming a kind of bower — an archway of glowing teeth leading inward to the tree's base, where the roots parted around a hollow in the earth. Cold air rose from it, smelling of deep stone.

"The seal's below," Corvane said. "Bone-script on covenant-stone. Your gift reads what my eyes can't. I dig, you listen."

He worked with a knife and his hands, prying at the root-lattice where it framed the hollow. The roots fought him — slowly, grudgingly, the way old men rise from chairs — but the toll had been paid, the truth was in their mouths, and the orchard honored its own law. Root by root, the way opened.

Zephyrine knelt at the lip of the hollow and listened down.

The second seal sang. That was the only word for it — a low sustained tone, covenant-stone humming the note it had held since the first swearing. But the note was frayed. She could hear the flaw in it the way a carver hears the flaw in a femur before the blade finds it: a hairline of silence running through the sound, widening year by year. Something had been gnawing at this seal from the Hollow side. Something patient. Something that knew the guild had stopped sending anyone to listen.

"I can hear it breaking," she said. "Slowly. It needs reforging — carving, binding, the same as the drowned bell chapel. Give me till dawn and I can—"

She stopped.

Because beneath the seal's frayed song, in the deep place where the Hollow pressed against the world like a face against glass, she heard a new voice. One she knew. One she had known since she was fourteen years old, waiting its turn at the back of an endless queue.

It wasn't waiting anymore.

*Zephyrine.* Her mother's whisper came up through the crack in the second seal, thin as wire, urgent as fire. *Little carver. Listen to me. It's coming through here — the thing that broke the bell, the thing that broke the orchard's edge — it's using the cracks to reach the queue. To reach us. It eats the waiting dead, Zeph, it eats us while we wait, and I am so tired, and I cannot hold my place much longer—*

The satchel's voices rose all at once, dozens of the dead crying out in overlapping alarm, and beyond them, distantly, she heard the queue itself — thousands strong — shifting, stirring, breaking formation for the first time in centuries.

Corvane saw her face. "What is it? What's wrong?"

She gripped the root-frame with both hands. "It's my mother. The breach is reaching into the land of the dead itself — the dead aren't safe on *either side* anymore." She looked up at him, and the last of the fruit's honesty poured out of her like a tide. "I have to carve the binding tonight, right now, or the crack widens and everything in that queue gets devoured. But Corvane — the script down there, the binding formula — part of it's been altered. Deliberately. Someone recut this seal from our side, years ago, to make it fail slowly instead of break loudly." Her breath came ragged. "And I know the hand. I've balanced ledgers in that hand for eleven years."

Above them, at the orchard's far edge, a lantern-kindled light flickered among the silver trunks — several lights, moving in formation, disciplined as a audit-line.

Hunters. In livery.

Corvane drew his knife and stepped between her and the lights.
