## 10. Hunters in Livery

Corvane counted the hunters before he heard them.

That was the old training, the thing the guild had ground into him across twenty years of service and could never grind back out: you learn a country by its silences. The birds had gone quiet along the hedgerow. Not startled-quiet — *organized*-quiet, the silence of small things making themselves small on purpose. And the wind had shifted, carrying up from the south a smell that did not belong to autumn pastures and distant tanneries.

Lamp-oil. Boot-grease. Guild-issue leather, cured with ash and tallow in the vats beneath the counting house. He would have known it blindfolded in a crowd of ten thousand.

"Zephyrine." He said it low, without turning his head. She was three paces behind him on the cart-track, her satchel of bone carvings clutched to her chest like a sleeping child, and she had learned in two days' flight to read his voice when it dropped into that register. "Off the road. Now."

She didn't argue. That was the other thing she'd learned.

He walked her off the track at the next gap in the hedge, down into a shallow fold of land where an old field-drain ran dry under a lip of turf, and pressed her down into its shadow with a hand flat between her shoulder blades. Then he climbed back up to the hedge-line alone, because whatever was coming was coming for the road, and the road was where he wanted its eyes.

Through the woven branches he watched them come.

Six riders in guild livery — the dark grey coats, the crossed-bone badge stitched in white thread at the shoulder, the long cloaks that were cut for riding but weighted at the hem for walking kills. They came at an unhurried trot, spread across the track in a line too wide for travelers and too neat for bandits, and at their head rode a woman Corvane knew better than he had ever known anyone wearing that coat.

Sable Fen.

She sat her horse the way she did everything: precisely, without flourish, without a single motion she hadn't decided on in advance. Her hair was bound back tight. Her crossbow was slung but loaded — he could tell from the angle of the quarrel against her thigh. Twenty years ago he'd taught her that trick himself, loading before you sling, so your first shot costs you nothing but the reach. She had been the best student the guild's hunting arm ever produced. He had written the commendation in his own hand.

Behind Fen rode five others, faces he half-knew, and one face he knew completely: Dree Halloran, his own second-in-command for six years, the man who had once taken a knife through the palm rather than loose his grip on a prisoner's collar. Halloran looked older. Tired. Like a man who had spent two years carrying something heavy and being told it wasn't heavy.

*They found us fast*, Corvane thought. *Too fast.* Which meant the market at Grayhollow had sold them, or the ferryman had, or some farmer whose memory Odile's silver had loosened. It didn't matter which. What mattered was the arithmetic: two of them, one of them wounded-walking, against six riders who knew this country and had warrants for both their heads.

And Zephyrine was crouched in a ditch below him with a satchel full of dead people's voices, trusting him to keep her alive.

He found, examining the feeling, that he didn't resent the trust. That was new. That was hers.

* *

The riders drew level with the gap in the hedge and stopped. Of course they stopped. Fen had eyes like a fishhawk; she'd marked the broken turf at the field-gap, or the absence of birds, or nothing at all except the shape of the day telling her that today was a killing day. She raised one gloved hand, and the line of riders halted as though she'd pulled a string.

"Corvane." Her voice carried across the flat fields the way a bell carries — clean, unloving, impossible to mistake for anything else. "We know you're in the hedge. We've known since the ford."

He weighed it. Running was death; the crossbows would take Zephyrine in the open before she covered fifty yards. Staying hidden was slower death. So he did the third thing, the thing the guild had trained out of everyone but him: he stood up out of cover in plain view, hands loose at his sides, and stepped over the hedge into the open road like a man stepping out to greet a neighbor.

"Hallo, Sable."

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise; he doubted anything surprised her anymore. Recognition, maybe. Grief filed under procedure. "You look thin," she said.

"I've been eating badly."

"You've been eating nothing. Halloran told me you'd let yourself go to ruin." Her gaze flicked once toward the hedge, toward the ditch beyond it, then back. She didn't order anyone to move on Zephyrine. Not yet. That restraint had a price tag on it, and Corvane had spent enough years at Odile Vasse's right hand to read the ledger. "Dismount," Fen said, and swung down herself, and the five riders followed in a clatter of tack and leather. They arranged themselves without being told — two to flank the hedge, two behind Fen, Halloran standing apart, slightly forward, looking at the ground.

"Dree," Corvane said.

"Don't." Halloran's voice cracked on the word. He still wouldn't lift his head. "Don't talk to me like we're in the barracks, Corvane. I'm here to bring you in."

"Are you?"

"I signed for it." Now he looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and furious, mostly at himself. "Your warrant. My name's on the recovery order. Do you know what that means? It means if you die resisting, it's my name the tribunal reads out. Mine. So please—" and there it was, the boy he'd recruited out of the river-docks, underneath all the grey cloth, "—please don't make me watch them kill you."

The words landed harder than any bolt. Corvane let them. Some wounds you owed people.

"Fen," he said instead, turning back to her, keeping his voice level. "You've read the recovery order. Whose signature?"

"The guildmaster's."

"And beneath hers?"

Fen was quiet a moment. Wind moved the hedge; somewhere behind it, he knew, Zephyrine was listening to every word, and probably listening to things beyond the words — the dead didn't stop talking just because the living had business. "There are no countersignatures," Fen said carefully. "Guildmaster's seal only."

"Because no warden would sign it." He took one step closer, and four crossbows came up, and Fen lifted two fingers and put them back down again. "Because what's in that order isn't recovery, Sable, it's erasure. Two fugitives, no trial date, transfer to 'guild custody' — you know which cellar that means. You've stood post outside it. I trained you outside it."

"I know what my orders say."

"Do you know what they *cost*?" He let that sit. Then: "Three seals broken, Fen. Three. I filed the report myself — the drowned chapel, the orchard, the empty hall. Odile buried it. Every breach lets something through, and every week she delays, the Hollow pushes harder against the seams, and when it goes — not *if*, when — it won't be the guild quarter that drowns first. It'll be the tanneries. The docks. Your mother's street in Lowmarsh."

Something flickered across Halloran's face. Lowmarsh. Everyone from the hunting arm had someone in Lowmarsh.

"You're a traitor," Fen said, but she said it like a woman checking a knot, testing whether it still held.

"I'm a warden whose oath is still attached to something real. Yours is attached to a woman selling doors to the dark." He opened his hands wider. "I know how this looks from inside the coat. I wore it for twenty years and it fit perfectly and I thought it *was* me. Take it from someone who's been out in the cold: the fit isn't the same as the truth."

Fen looked at him for a long moment. Behind her, Halloran shifted his weight, and one of the flanking riders — young, freckled, nervous — glanced sideways at his partner in a way that meant doubt was already loose among them, moving like smoke.

Then Fen smiled, thinly, and Corvane understood that the conversation had never really been the conversation.

"Odile anticipated you," she said. "Word for word, almost. She said you'd talk about the seals. She said you'd say *the Hollow comes for everyone*. So here's what the guild offers, Corvane, and hear all of it before you answer, because I'll only say it once."

She nodded, and two riders moved to the hedge gap, and a crossbow leveled at the ditch, and Zephyrine rose out of it with her hands raised and her satchel held high like a surrendering flag, mud to the knees, chin up, furious and magnificent.

"No—" Corvane started forward and stopped, because three bolts tracked his chest, and Fen's hand was flat in the air.

"The offer," Fen continued, as though nothing had interrupted, "is reinstatement. Full rank, full honors, your name struck from the exile rolls — Odile keeps the original report you filed, Corvane. She kept it. Why do you think that is? Because she always intended to need you again someday. You walk back through the counting-house door tomorrow morning as Warden-Captain, first chair, your old office, your old command — Halloran under you, where he belongs." She paused. "The condition is the bone-carver. Alive, intact, and silent. She goes to the vaults. Not the cellar. The vaults. She will be treated gently, fed well, and never harmed. You have my oath on it."

"And after the year turns?" Corvane asked quietly. "After the covenant fails and the Hollow comes through? What happens to her then?"

"That's above my pay," Fen said, "and yours, if you take it."

Zephyrine spoke for the first time. Her voice was steadier than he expected, and it went through him like a blade going through a shield-gap. "You should know," she said to Fen, conversationally, almost kindly, "that the dead in my satchel can hear everything you're saying. And one of them says she knew your father. A ferryman. He wants me to tell you he was proud of you, and sorry about the winter he missed."

The freckled rider made a small involuntary sound. Halloran closed his eyes.

Fen's composure didn't crack — but it *settled*, like snow finding its weight on a branch. "Witch-talk," she said.

"Bone-work," Zephyrine corrected. "And it doesn't matter whether you believe it. It matters whether they do." She tilted her head at the riders. "Ask them, later tonight, in the barracks, whether they slept. The dead get around."

Corvane felt it then — the whole encounter tilting, the moment balancing on its edge. Fen had come with an offer because Odile wanted Corvane back; a warden-captain who recanted publicly was worth more than a warden-captain in a grave. But offers expire the moment they become inconvenient, and Zephyrine had just made six armed professionals wonder whether their employer's prize might be exactly what the guild's whole machinery feared most: a woman who carried testimony from the other side of death.

"Fen," he said softly. "Last chance. Walk away. Take your people home. Tell Odile you lost us in the hills. Nobody has to bleed today."

"You know I can't."

"I know," he said. "I hoped anyway. Hoping's the part of the oath they couldn't train out either."

Fen exhaled. "Corvane. Surrender her. Please. It's the only version of today where everyone walks away."

"No," he said. "It isn't. It's just the only version where *you* don't have to choose."

He saw her decide. It happened in her shoulders first, then her jaw, then her hand — the sequence he'd watched a hundred times across a hundred hard mornings, a woman arriving at the worst available option and committing to it fully, because commitment was the only mercy discipline offered. Her fingers curled, and she gave the smallest nod, and Corvane was already moving.

He wasn't moving toward cover. Cover was for men who intended to survive by hiding. He moved toward *Zephyrine* — four strides, arms out, turning his back to the crossbows — because the shot he'd seen gathering in Fen's eyes since the moment she dismounted had never been aimed at him.

It was aimed past him.

"Sable, *no*—" Halloran's shout, cracking across the field.

The bolt took him high in the left side, under the shoulder blade, punching through with a sound like ice splitting on a pond. He had time to register that Fen had deflected her aim by a hand's width at the last instant — the bolt that was meant to kill would have gone through his heart, and instead it tore through muscle and gristle and came out the front of his chest trailing heat — and then the ground came up and the sky went sideways and Zephyrine was screaming his name, and the last thing he saw before the world folded down to a bright point was Sable Fen lowering her crossbow with an expression on her face he had never once seen in twenty years of service.

Horror. Plain, undisciplined, human horror.

Good, he thought, from very far away. Let it cost her too. Everything true costs.

* *

Sound came back in pieces. Shouting — Halloran's voice, raw, arguing with Fen, the argument dissolving into orders barked too fast. Hoofbeats scattering. A rider galloping hard north, toward the counting house — for a surgeon, or for Odile, he couldn't tell and couldn't care. Hands on him, small strong hands, tearing his coat open, pressing cloth hard against the exit wound until the pain arrived like a struck bell and rang through his whole skull.

"Stay with me." Zephyrine's face swam above him, streaked with mud, lit ferocious from within. "Look at me. Corvane. *Look at me.*"

"Did they—" His voice was a ruin. "Did anyone—"

"They're gone. All of them. Halloran threw down his crossbow and walked off — *walked off*, Corvane, in front of everyone. Fen let him. She just stood there holding that bow like it had bitten her." She laughed, a wet, shocked sound with no humor in it anywhere. "Your guild is cracking down the middle and you did it lying in the dirt. Don't you dare be smug about it while you're dying."

"Not dying," he managed. "Been... ordered worse wounds than this."

"Liar." But her voice broke on it, and she bent lower, and he felt her forehead press against his, cold and muddy and alive. "The dead are telling me things," she whispered, so low only he could hear it, so low perhaps even she barely heard herself. "About bleeding out. About how fast it goes. I've never refused them, Corvane. Never once, in thirty years. But I'm refusing this one. Do you understand me? This one I refuse."

He wanted to tell her that refusing the dead was the bravest thing he'd ever heard of. He wanted to say something worthy of the moment, something a warden should know how to say. What came out, faintly, blood-frothed, was:

"Shepherd's bothy. Half a mile... south of the drain. Off the... drover's track. There's straw. And..."

"And what?"

"A key. Under the... third stone of the wall. I hid it there, two years ago." A bubble of dark laughter shook him and hurt him. "In case I ever needed to run. Never thought I'd be... carried in."

Zephyrine stared at him — at this exiled man who had prepared a refuge he never believed he'd use, the way she'd carved bones she never believed she'd stop hearing — and something passed between them that neither of them named, because naming it would have required breath neither of them had to spare.

Then she hauled his arm across her shoulders, took his weight the way the dead had taught her bodies could bear weight, and began to walk him south through the long grass, leaving a trail a child could follow — and trusting, with the whole reckless faith of the newly defiant, that by the time the guild regrouped, they would both be gone.

Behind them, in the trampled grass where the riders had stood, lay a single discarded item: Dree Halloran's crossed-bone badge, torn from his own shoulder and dropped in the mud like a man dropping a stone he'd finally realized he no longer needed to carry.
