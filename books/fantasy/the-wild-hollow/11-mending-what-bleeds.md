## 11. Mending What Bleeds

The bothy had been empty since lambing season, if the smell of it was anything to go by — old straw, old smoke, old rain coming through the turf roof in slow brown threads. Zephyrine got Corvane through the low door more by will than strength, his arm over her shoulders, his weight a thing she would feel in her spine for days.

"Down," she said, and he went down, onto the straw pallet against the wall, with less complaint than she expected from a man who had taken a hunter's blade meant for his heart.

It had missed the heart by the width of two fingers. She knew because she'd seen the blood soak through his coat in the culvert's mouth, black in the moonlight, and because for one terrible moment she had thought — absurdly, uselessly — that she might hear him among her voices. That she might already know what unfinished thing would be left behind.

She hadn't heard anything. That was how she knew he still lived. The dead were never quiet.

"Lamp," he managed.

"I know." Her hands were shaking as she dug through her satchel, past the wrapped carvings that murmured softly to themselves like sleeping children, until she found the tallow stub and flint. Light bloomed small and yellow, and she wished it hadn't. By lamplight the wound looked worse than it had in the dark: a long diagonal opening below his collarbone, edges already stiffening, blood gone tacky on the skin around it.

"You should have let Sable Fen's blade find someone who deserves it," Corvane said.

"Shut up." She said it without heat, the way you speak to a child or a bleeding man, and he almost smiled.

She worked in silence for a while. Boiled water from the rain barrel outside, snow-melt still cold enough to make him hiss. Clean linen torn from the hem of her shift because neither of them had thought to pack bandages and pride was a luxury for people who weren't leaking. A needle she used for fine carving work, its point sterilized in flame. She stitched the way she carved — small, even, unhurried — and somewhere in the third stitch she realized his eyes were open, watching her hands instead of the wound.

"You're good at that," he said.

"Bones don't bleed much when they're cut right. Skin is easier."

"That's not what I meant."

She tied off the last stitch and sat back on her heels, wiping her hands on the straw. Outside, the wind moved through the bare rowans along the shepherd's fence, and far away — too far to be real, near enough to be memory — something in her satchel whispered.

*The tall one,* said the voice of a woman dead forty years, whose finger-bone Zephyrine had carved into a ring clasp three winters ago. *Is the tall one dying?*

"No," Zephyrine murmured.

Corvane's gaze sharpened. "Who are you talking to?"

"Nobody living." She reached for the satchel, hesitated, then drew out the little collection of wrappings and set them in a row between them on the pallet's edge, like a merchant laying out wares. Seven pieces. A ring-clasp of finger bone. A button carved from a kneecap. A whistle made of rib, which she never blew. "If we're going to trust each other, you should know what you're trusting."

He said nothing. He was good at saying nothing; she was beginning to understand it was not emptiness but discipline.

"The guild teaches that bone-carving is accounting," she said. "That relics are records. You carve a name into a bone, the ledger keeps the trade, everyone balances." She turned the ring-clasp over in her palm. "That's a lie. Or half of one. Bones aren't records, Corvane. They're *doors*. And the people on the other side aren't entries in a book. They're still there. Still waiting."

"How long?"

"Since I was six. Since my mother died." The words came out flat, rehearsed by years of never saying them. "I picked up one of her hairpins — just bone, just a plain pin — and she spoke to me. Not a ghost standing at the foot of the bed. Not a dream. Her voice, in the bone, clear as a bell rung inside my skull. She asked me to tell my father where she'd hidden the money he'd need for the winter. Under the third floorboard, left of the hearth. And I told him. And after that—" She shrugged. "After that, every piece of bone I touched had someone in it."

Corvane's face did the thing she was learning to read on him: nothing moved, but everything shifted behind it, like current under ice. "Every warden's manual says the hearing-gift is rare. One in a generation. It says you perceive echoes. Residue. Impressions." His voice was careful. "It does not say conversations."

"The manual was written by people who needed the gift to be a tool." She laughed, and there was no humor in it. "Do you want to know what it actually is? Do you want to know what I've been doing for twenty years?"

"Tell me."

"They beg." The word came out of her like a splinter drawn. "They don't chat. They don't reminisce. They *beg*. Every single one of them. Because the dead can't finish anything — that's what death is, it's the door closing before the sentence ends — and I'm the only hand that reaches across. *Tell my daughter I forgave her.* *Bury me under the rowan, not the yew.* *My brother took the blame for what I did; tell him before he dies carrying it.* Twenty years, Corvane. Hundreds of them. Thousands. I have never once refused."

The lamp guttered. Rain found a new seam in the roof and began a thin, patient dripping into the corner.

"And now?" he asked quietly.

She looked down at her hands. They were steady again, which felt like a betrayal of everything she was about to say.

"Now there's a map in a hollowed femur in my satchel, and four seals breaking, and a city that will drown in the Hollow by year's turn unless we move faster than any of the begging voices can follow." She met his eyes. "For the first time in my life, they're asking and I'm not answering. There's a woman in this satchel whose son doesn't know she kept his letters. There's a drowned man who wants his wedding ring returned to a wife who has probably remarried. There's a child—"

Her voice stopped working. She pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth for a moment, an old habit, the gesture of a girl swallowing grief at a workbench so the other carvers wouldn't see.

"There's a child who wants someone to know she hid under the stairs during the fire, so no one would think she ran away. And I am going to fail her. I am going to fail all of them, because if I stop to carry every message across, the Hollow comes through and there won't be anyone left alive to receive them." She exhaled. "And the worst part — the truly obscene part — is that it feels *good*. Choosing. Refusing. I didn't know it could feel like this. Like setting down a weight I forgot I was born holding."

Corvane was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had a roughness she hadn't heard in it before.

"When I took the Warden's Oath, they told us duty was a chain we chose," he said. "That choosing it once was enough, forever. That doubt was rot." He touched the bandage at his collarbone, gingerly. "I believed it for nineteen years. Then I found the breaches and reported them, and learned that the chain only holds in one direction. It binds the sworn, never the swearing." He looked at the row of small bones between them, each wrapped like something holy. "You've been bound since you were six years old. And you never swore anything."

"No one asks the tool for its oath."

"I'm asking."

The rain ticked. Somewhere out in the dark, a fox screamed once, high and brief, and fell silent.

"It's not a gift," she said. "Everyone calls it a gift. The guild called it an asset. My mother—" she caught herself, steadied. "My mother called it a burden I'd grow strong enough to carry. But none of those words fit. It's just... listening. Terrible, endless listening. And listening without the power to answer back is its own kind of wound." She turned the rib-whistle in her fingers. "Until these last few days, I never understood that refusing could be mercy too. For them. Some of them need to be let go more than they need to be answered. I just never had the right to decide."

"And now?"

"Now I'm deciding anyway." She said it simply, and heard, as she said it, how much of her life had bent toward this moment — every refused holiday, every night spent at the bench until dawn finishing a stranger's last request, every friendship thinned to thread because she trusted the dead more than the living, since the dead at least told her plainly what they wanted. "Duty was never mine, Corvane. It was handed to me before I could walk. This—" she gestured at the wound, the map, the dark road ahead, "—this is the first thing I've ever chosen. Which means it's the first thing I can be blamed for. I find I'd rather be blamed than owned."

Something changed in his face then. Not softening exactly — Corvane's face didn't soften, it *cleared*, like fog lifting off stone — but she watched him look at her as though recalibrating some long-held estimate, and finding it short.

"You could have run," he said. "When Odile branded you. You had the map, you had the skill to hide it anywhere in the world. Instead you came to a flooded chapel full of the unquiet dead and carved peace into a child's rib while I held the door." He paused. "Why?"

Zephyrine looked at the rib-whistle. Inside it, faintly, a voice was humming a lullaby it had hummed for eleven years, patient as water.

"Because she asked me to," she said. "And because for the first time, answering her and saving the world were the same errand." She wrapped the whistle carefully and put it away. "Don't mistake it for nobility. I'm still a ledger-keeper. I've just finally found an account worth balancing."

He huffed — nearly a laugh. Then the laugh caught on pain and became a grimace, and she was leaning forward again with fresh linen, checking the stitching, her fingers competent and cool against skin that was fever-warm. Too warm. She noted it and said nothing. There would be time enough to worry at dawn, if dawn found them.

"Hold still," she murmured.

"I'm holding very still."

"You're holding still the way a fortress holds. Relax or the stitches pull."

He tried. She watched the effort travel through him — a man unlearning vigilance muscle by muscle, finding no instruction for it anywhere in his body's memory. She softened her voice without deciding to.

"Talk to me. About anything. It helps."

"What do you want to hear?"

"Something true."

A pause. Rain. The fox again, farther off.

"I sold five years of my life in that market," Corvane said suddenly, staring at the roof beams, "so we could pass through to the orchard. I didn't tell you because you'd have argued, and we didn't have time to argue." The words came out level, an accounting rendered. "Five years. I'm telling you now because you asked for truth, and because—" his jaw worked, "—because I've decided you get the truth whether it costs me or not. That's new for me. Nineteen years of oath taught me to spend myself silently. I'm trying to learn another way."

Zephyrine sat back slowly. In her chest, something that had been clenched since the culvert loosened by one degree — not fully, not yet, but measurably, like ice remembering spring.

"You idiot," she said gently.

"Yes."

"We'll discuss it properly when you're not bleeding."

"Yes, carver."

She banked the lamp low and settled herself against the wall beside the pallet, within reach of him if the fever climbed, within reach of the satchel if the voices grew loud. The dead murmured their patience. The rain mended its slow argument with the roof. And Zephyrine Vasquez, who had spent twenty years being the door between worlds, closed her eyes and discovered that for once she was simply a person in a room with a wounded man she was beginning, against every professional instinct, to care about keeping alive.

She was nearly asleep when his voice came again, quiet, stripped of everything but fact:

"The map moved tonight. While you were stitching. I saw it through the wrapping — the ink crawling like ants." A pause. "There's a fourth line drawing itself, Zeph. Heading south. Toward the guild quarter."

She opened her eyes. Outside, far off across the dark fields, the bells of the city began to toll — not the hour, not the alarm, but something older, a rhythm she recognized from ledgers she had balanced all her life.

The year-turning audit bell. Ringing early.

Odile had begun to count.
