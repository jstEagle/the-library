## 12. The Third Seal: The Empty Throne

The road to the Swearing Hall climbed through a country that had forgotten its own name.

Corvane felt it first as a wrongness in his boots — the packed earth of the old pilgrim track gone soft, giving beneath each step like flesh that had been left too long in water. Then the trees began to lean. Not wind-lean; they leaned *toward* the ridge ahead, every birch and black pine canted like men craning to hear a distant conversation. And the birds had stopped. Three days out from the Orchard of Teeth they had thinned, and now the only sound was their own breathing, the creak of Zephyrine's satchel of carvings, and — when the wind came down off the heights — something underneath the wind that might have been voices, or might have been his own guilt finding new ways to speak.

He had walked this road before. That was the thing he could not stop telling himself. Twenty-two years ago he had walked it as a young warden with a fresh oath still warm on his tongue, escorting an auditor who never once looked up from her ledger. He remembered the hall at the top of the ridge as a place of terrible order: banners, censers, the seven wardens of the first covenant standing witness with their staves planted like fence posts against the dark.

Now the banners were rags fused to the walls by rain and time, and the doors — when they finally reached them near dusk — stood open on a throat of shadow, exhaling cold.

"We should make camp," Corvane said. "Approach it at dawn. The dead are strongest at night, and that place is mostly dead."

Zephyrine didn't answer at once. She had been quieter since the orchard, quieter since the truth-fruit, and Corvane had learned to read her silences the way she read bone: by what was absent. This silence wasn't grief. It was listening.

"They're already talking about us," she said.

"The dead?"

"The signatories." She touched the strap of her satchel without looking at it. "They've been talking since we crossed the tree line. They know why we've come." A pause, and then, flatly, as if setting a coin on a table: "One of them says we owe a voice."

The word went through him like a splinter working toward the heart. "Owe."

"That's the word I heard." She turned to look at him at last, and in the failing light her eyes were the color of the flooded chapel's water. "Corvane. What exactly did the original covenant cost?"

He opened his mouth to say *I don't know* — the honest answer, the shameful one — and found he couldn't. Because he did know. He had always known. It was written in the oldest training text, the one novices were given and then quietly discouraged from reading twice, the passage every warden memorized and none discussed: *The covenant is bought and kept with voice; let those who swear it understand that speech is the coin.*

"Everything," he said. "Or close enough. The seven who swore it gave up—" He stopped. Started again. "They didn't die for the covenant, Zeph. They died *into* it. Their bodies were buried with honors and their voices were taken into the seal-stones so the binding would renew itself with every turning of the year. The ghosts you're hearing aren't haunting the hall. They're *maintaining* it. They've been holding the third seal shut for four hundred years with nothing but their will and their words."

Zephyrine looked back at the open doors. The cold breath of the hall stirred the loose hair at her temple.

"And now the seals are breaking," she said, "because the voices are wearing out."

"Because someone has been feeding on them," Corvane said, and hated how steady his voice stayed. "A seal that renews itself doesn't fail. It gets spent. Odile's guild has been drawing credit against the covenant for decades — every impossible trade, every borrowed shadow, every year sold twice. The ledgers weren't just records, Zeph. They were withdrawals."

She absorbed that the way she absorbed everything, silently, completely, the way stone absorbs cold. Then she shouldered her satchel higher and walked past him through the doors.

"We'll need the light," she said. "And I'd rather owe them an honest visit than a cowardly one."

* * *

Inside, the Swearing Hall was a held breath.

It was vast — built to hold hundreds, and once it had, if the tiered benches carved along the curving walls were any measure — but emptiness had a weight here that furniture never achieved. Moonlight fell through a shattered roof-lantern in a single pale column and struck the floor at the center of the hall, where seven stones were set in a ring like the points of a broken crown. Covenant-stone. Even across the distance Corvane felt them, a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like bitten copper. The stones were dark. Two of them were cracked clean through.

The ghosts stood among them.

Not drifted, not hovering — standing, the way sentries stand, feet planted, spines straight, in the postures of people who had been at attention so long that attention had become their shape. There were six visible. Seven places in the ring, six figures, and the empty seventh spot made Corvane's skin crawl worse than any of the others, because absence in a place like this was not absence at all. It was a wound wearing the shape of a person.

They had been great once. You could see it in the bones of them, in the remnants of office: a woman whose robes still carried the ghost-pattern of a chancellor's chain; a broad man with a warden's staff fused to his grip; a small figure, ageless, whose hands were a carver's hands, scarred and precise. Their faces were worn smooth as river stones. Faces erode, Corvane thought, when all you have left to express is duty.

Zephyrine stopped three paces from the ring and did the thing that still astonished him after everything: she bowed. Not the shallow courtesy of a guild functionary. She bent from the waist, low, the bow of an apprentice to a master, and spoke in a voice pitched to carry without echoing.

"I'm Zephyrine Vasquez, bone-carver of the Hollow Guild. This is Corvane, once Warden-Commander of the Third Watch. We come about the seals."

The silence stretched. Then the woman with the chancellor's chain turned her head — slowly, the movement grinding like a millstone — and looked at Corvane.

"You," she said. Her voice was the strangest thing yet: not airy, not hollow, but *dense*, a voice with mass, a voice that pressed on the ear like deep water. "You wear the oath-shape. You wore it once. Why do you wear it broken?"

"Because I reported the breaches and my own guild erased my report and cast me out," Corvane said. "The oath I keep. The institution broke around it."

"You lie badly," said the broad man with the staff, and there might have been humor in it, or contempt; four hundred years had sanded the difference away. "You lie well. Both. You have told the truth so long inside yourself with no one to say it to that it has gone strange in your mouth."

"He's telling the truth," Zephyrine said quietly. "I'd know. Truth is most of what I hear."

The carver-figure raised its head. Of all of them its face was least worn — or perhaps most deliberately blank, smoothed not by time but by choice — and when it spoke, Corvane felt the words land in his sternum.

"A listener," it said. "At last. Four hundred years we have spoken into stone and no one came who could hear. Do you know what we are, child?"

"The signatories of the first covenant," Zephyrine said.

"We are the price of it." The carver spread scarred hands. "Seven voices poured into seven stones, that the door between the worlds might be latched from both sides. We do not rest. We do not wander. We hold. Every turning of the year, we speak the binding again. Or we spoke it. Now two of us are cracked and the seventh—" the figure's head turned toward the empty place in the ring, and even a ghost's gesture can convey grief, Corvane discovered; a particular slow, heavy grief, like a bell tolling underwater "—the seventh gave out eleven winters past, and we have covered the gap by speaking louder, and louder wears us, and the guild above our heads has been drinking the runoff for decades and calling itself the covenant's servant."

"The guildmaster knows," Corvane said. "We believe she intends not to repair the covenant but to control it. To become the toll-keeper between the realms."

"We know," said the chancellor. "Her agents came. Twice. They measured us." The word *measured* came out of her like a curse. "They brought instruments of bone and brass and they took readings, as though we were ore. One of them asked how much voice remained in the stones." Her worn face turned fully to Zephyrine then, and Corvane saw the ghost do something he had not believed possible: she hesitated. "You carry death on your hip, little carver. A satchel full of finished lives. Speak to us honestly, because we are bound to judge whoever comes before these stones, and judgment here is not a metaphor. Ask what you have come to ask."

Zephyrine's hand found the satchel strap again. Corvane watched her gather herself — he had learned her tells, the small brace of the shoulders, the breath drawn through the nose — and braced alongside her.

"The map shows three seals broken," she said. "The drowned chapel. The orchard. And this one, failing. The courier died bringing word that the covenant fails entirely at the year-turning unless it's reforged. So I'm asking: how is it reforged? What does repair require?"

The six ghosts looked at one another. It was a terrible thing to watch — the whole conversation passing between them in some medium deeper than air, faces too worn to show much, yet the weight of their deliberation pressing visibly on the moonlit column of dust between them.

Then the carver stepped forward, out of the ring, and the temperature of the hall dropped as though a door had opened onto winter.

"It requires what it required," the carver said. "A voice. Freely given, truly surrendered, poured into the stone that stands empty. The covenant is a latch, and a latch must be held from within. Whoever gives their voice joins us. Speaks with us. Holds with us. Until the covenant ends or the world does." A pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: "You are wondering whether it hurts. It does not hurt. It is worse than pain. It is being heard for the last time, and knowing it, and saying yes anyway."

Corvane moved before he decided to move — one step, putting himself half between the carver and Zephyrine, an instinct older than thought. "There has to be another way."

"There is always another way," said the broad man. "It costs more."

"Then name the other ways."

"Break the fourth seal and let the Hollow through," said the chancellor flatly. "No more covenant, no more price, no more holding. Also no more city, in time. The Hollow does not hate the living. It simply dissolves distinctions, and you are a distinction." She folded her translucent hands. "Or find seven new volunteers, which is what the founders hoped would happen — that the covenant would be renewed generation by generation, that the wards would call for keepers and keepers would answer. No one answered. The guild grew rich instead, and taught the world that the border was merchandise." Something moved under her worn features, and Corvane understood he was watching the ghost equivalent of fury, banked and ancient. "So. The price stands. One voice, freely given. We cannot take it — understand that. Taking is the guild's trade, not ours. It must be offered, at these stones, by someone who knows exactly what they offer. That is the whole of the law we are permitted."

The hall rang with silence after that — real silence, Corvane realized, except for the wind through the broken roof and, faintly, from the direction of Zephyrine's satchel, the whisper of the dead relics stirring like sleepers hearing their names.

Zephyrine stepped around him. She looked up at the carver — the ghost nearest her in shape and trade, the one whose hands bore the same scars hers someday would — and asked the question Corvane had been dreading since the tree line.

"Why did the seventh give out?" Her voice was very steady. "Eleven winters ago. Was it age? Or was it taken?"

The ghosts' deliberation passed between them again, faster this time, sharper. When the carver answered, every word came down like a hammer on a nail.

"Taken. Drained through a channel cut in the stone's foundation — a siphon of ledger-work, elegant, patient. Eleven winters ago the counting house above us began a new account. They called it the Reserve." The carver's worn face turned upward, toward the ruined roof, toward the distant city, toward the guild. "Your guildmaster drinks a dead founder's voice, little carver. She has been drinking it for eleven years. And the seal cracks because of it, and the drowned walk because of it, and the orchard grows teeth because of it, and at the year-turning there will be nothing left to hold."

Corvane felt the pieces lock together with an almost audible click. The Reserve — he had seen the entry once, years ago, in an audit he'd been permitted only partially: a fund with no stated purpose, drawing revenue from every division, answerable to no one but the guildmaster. He had assumed corruption. Ordinary, human corruption. Not this. Not *thrift applied to the souls of founders*.

"Then reforging isn't enough," he said aloud. "We seal the third stone tonight and Odile's siphon keeps draining it. We have to break the channel. We have to go back—"

"To the counting house," Zephyrine said.

They looked at each other, and the whole plan assembled itself in the space between their eyes: the audit, the codes, her knowledge of the ledger-lines, the fourth seal the map hadn't shown them yet but that was surely waiting beneath the vaults like a spider at the center of a web. But that was tomorrow's dread. First—

First the price, standing in the moonlight, waiting.

The carver returned to the ring. The six ghosts resumed their posts with the terrible patience of things that had waited four hundred years and could wait four hundred more. But as Zephyrine turned to go, the chancellor spoke once more, softly, and the softness was the worst sound in the whole hollow hall.

"Think carefully, listener. You above all know what voices are worth." A pause. "You hear the dead beg. Imagine joining them and being unable to answer anyone — ever — except in this single duty, forever. That is what we ask. That is what we *are*. Come back before the year-turning, or don't come back at all."

They walked out through the doors into the blue dark of the ridge, and neither spoke until the hall was well behind them and camp was struck and the fire was small and safe. Corvane sat sharpening a knife he didn't need sharpened, because his hands wanted work and his mind wanted anything but its own company.

"She'll volunteer," he said finally. "You. Before she lets anyone else pay it. I've known you eight days and I already know that."

Zephyrine was turning one of her carvings over in her fingers — a sparrow's skull, polished smooth, some small dead thing that had ridden in her satchel since the tannery rows. In the firelight her face was unreadable, which from her was its own kind of honesty.

"I know," she said. "That's what frightens me. Not the price." She looked up at him across the flames, and her eyes were clear and calm and absolutely certain. "How easily I'm willing to pay it. How much of me has always been ready to be spent, because spending myself was the only way I knew to matter." A faint, tired smile. "But the year-turning comes through the counting house first, Corvane. Whatever I choose — we have a guildmaster to ruin."

Far below the ridge, in the black fold of the valley, the city showed a scatter of lights — and among them, Corvane knew, the counting house burned bright with preparation for the audit, and somewhere beneath its foundations a stolen voice was being drunk down to the dregs.

Three nights until the year turned.

The knife whispered against the stone, and he sharpened it anyway.
