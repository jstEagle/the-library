## 1. Ash on the Wind

The trouble with burning a grimoire, Theo Dunmore discovered on the third evening, was that it fought back.

Not with fire — the pages took flame easily enough, curling and blackening like any other paper. It fought back with *memory*. Every page he fed to the hearth came apart in a bloom of scent: crushed feverfew and beeswax, tallow smoke and river mud, the particular smell of Mistress Odile's kitchen on a winter morning when she'd been up since before dawn brewing something that steamed and muttered to itself in the pot. Each page burned like a small funeral, and Theo had been holding funerals for three days straight.

"You'd have hated this," he told her, because there was no one else to tell, and because the silence in the cottage had gotten so thick it needed breaking. "You spent forty years writing this. Forty years. And I'm feeding it to the fire one page at a time like a man eating his own boots."

From her chair by the window, where she had not sat for nine days now, Mistress Odile said nothing. She was very good at saying nothing lately. She had gotten frighteningly accomplished at it.

Theo turned another page. *On the Soothing of Beasts*, written in her crabbed hand, annotated in three colors of ink, with a marginal note that read *do NOT try this on goats, they remember* — a note that had cost him a bruised shin and a great deal of dignity when he was fourteen. He held it over the flames until the corner caught, watched the words go orange at their edges, then black, then gone.

"Goats remember," he said softly, as it burned. "That's true. That's so true."

The firelight moved across the room. The kettle on its hook had gone cold hours ago; he kept forgetting to fill it, then filling it and forgetting to boil it, as if some part of him still expected her to appear in the doorway and scold him for wasting good water. *Water doesn't grow on trees, boy.* Nothing grew on trees here except pine and lichen, which was precisely why she'd said it — one of perhaps four jokes she permitted herself per year, each one rationed out like medicine.

Nine days since the riders had come through the pass below. Nine days since she'd shoved him into the cellar and told him, in a voice he had never heard her use, *whatever you hear up there, boy, you stay down and you stay quiet.* Nine days of shouting, of hooves, of something that howled — not a dog, not a wolf, something that made his teeth ache in their roots — and then a silence so complete he'd lain in the dark for what felt like an hour before daring to climb the ladder.

He had found her on the doorstep, facing down the mountain. Her staff was scorched to a stub. There was frost on her eyelashes even though it wasn't cold enough for frost, which meant she had spent everything she had, right down past the bottom of herself. And she had been smiling. Mistress Odile, who smiled roughly once per fiscal quarter, had died smiling at the mountains, like she'd won something.

He hadn't been able to bury her properly — no spade would cut the frozen ground, and he hadn't dared light a pyre big enough for anything but this. So he'd wrapped her in her good cloak, the blue one, and laid her in the woodshed where the cold would keep her, and told himself he'd come back in spring. Told himself it like a debt he fully intended to honor.

And then he'd started burning the grimoire, because that was the other half of her last instruction, delivered sometime between the howling and the end, pressed against his ear while she gripped his wrist hard enough to leave marks:

*Remember nothing. Forget nothing.*

Which made no sense whatsoever, and was also, in his experience, exactly the kind of thing old women said when they were right.

* * *

By the fifth night the grimoire was a third gone, and Theo's hands had gone strange.

It started small. He'd reach for the poker and find the coals already banked perfectly. He'd think about tea and the kettle would be singing before he remembered putting it on. Small kindnesses from nowhere, the house quietly tidying itself around him like an anxious host. He tried not to notice. Noticing, he suspected, was how it got worse.

Because that was the thing about the gift — his gift, the one Mistress Odile had spent eleven years teaching him to keep folded up small and quiet inside himself, tucked away like contraband. It didn't like being ignored. It sat under his ribs like a cat locked in a room, and the longer the door stayed shut, the more things got knocked over.

"None of that," he murmured, pressing his palm flat against his sternum, a gesture she'd taught him without ever explaining. *When it stirs, put your hand there and think of something dull. Think of mending fences. Think of taxes.* "We're staying boring. Boring is survival."

The warmth under his ribs subsided, sullenly, the way a cat subsides when you pick it up mid-pounce.

He went back to the hearth and turned to the next page. *On Weather-Sense and the Reading of Clouds.* Her handwriting here was younger, loopier, from before whatever had happened to make her letters go small and tight. In the margin: *clouds lie more often than men, but less beautifully.*

He laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, surprised sound in the empty cottage — and then the laugh turned into something else halfway through and he had to sit down on the hearthstone and let it be whatever it was going to be. Grief did that. It ambushed you in the gaps between tasks, in the space between one page and the next, and all you could do was let it wash through and wait for your legs to work again.

She'd taken him in when he was eleven, a scrawny orphan off the relief cart from Lowmarsh, all elbows and appetite and no family to speak of. Everyone in Hollowmere assumed she'd wanted a pair of hands for the chores. Nobody — least of all Theo — understood why she'd chosen him specifically, why she'd spent over a decade teaching him herbcraft and letters and how to fold his gift down flat, why she'd drilled him on the mountain paths until he knew every smuggler's track and goat trail better than the roads themselves.

*Because someday, boy, someone will come looking for what I've hidden,* she'd said once, apropos of absolutely nothing, while they were digging wormwood roots. And when he'd asked what she'd hidden, she'd looked at him for a long moment with those pale, unsettling eyes, and said, *Pass me that root.*

He burned the weather page. Then the next. Then the next.

Outside, snow had begun coming down soft and thick, the kind of snowfall that erased the world politely, layer by layer. Good. Snow meant tracks covered. Snow meant the pass road would close and stay closed, and anyone coming up from the lowlands would have to *want* it badly.

He was reaching for page thirty-something — *On the Mending of Broken Things*, which hurt worse than most, because her notes there were full of little asides about him, *the boy mends fences well, has a knack, wasted on fences* — when he heard the bells.

Hollowmere had two bells. The chapel bell, which rang for services and funerals, and the square bell, which rang only for fire, flood, or the tax collector. This was neither of those. This was a bell rung hard and fast and frightened, over and over, and beneath it Theo could hear voices, many voices, all talking at once the way a village talks when something has arrived that nobody invited.

He set the page down. His hand had begun, very slightly, to shake.

Through the frosted window he saw light in the square — torchlight, moving wrong, too many sources, too orderly. And shapes. Horses. Riders in the colors he knew from proclamations nailed to the waystation door twice a year and ignored twice a year: the black and gold of the crown of Thornfell.

They weren't supposed to come up here. Hollowmere was a pimple on the spine of the mountains, a place where nothing happened except weather and the occasional disputed goat. Crown riders had come maybe six times in his whole life, and always for something tedious — a census, a tithe dispute, once a runaway tax clerk who'd been hiding in the chapel loft for a month and had grown a magnificent beard.

But Mistress Odile was dead, and dead women couldn't hide what they'd been hiding anymore.

Theo blew out the candle, though the hearth still lit the room like a confession. He pulled on his coat, his boots, the scarf she'd knitted that was too long and too itchy and that he'd have fought a warden to keep. At the door he paused, looked back at the grimoire — half-burned, half-alive, still full of everything she'd been — and made a decision that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

There wasn't time to burn the rest. And taking it was death, if they searched him. But leaving it—

Leaving it felt like leaving *her*.

He took the remaining pages anyway, wrapped them in oilcloth, and shoved them inside his coat, against his chest, where they lay against his heartbeat like something sleeping. Then he stepped out into the snow and went toward the square, because apparently he was an idiot. Because he needed to see. Because knowing was a habit eleven years of Mistress Odile had never managed to break him of, and dying wouldn't change it either.

* * *

The square was full.

Full of villagers, first — nearly all of Hollowmere, bundled in wool, faces lit orange and uneasy. And in front of them, in a half-moon formation, sat six riders on horses far too fine for this altitude, steam rising off the animals like kettles. The riders wore the black and gold, visors down, and one of them — the tall one, the one whose horse stood a head above the rest — was nailing something to the old posting-oak with precise, unhurried strikes of a hammer. *Thock. Thock. Thock.* Each strike landed like a verdict.

The village headman, a round and ordinarily cheerful man named Aldous Pike who sold cheese and owed everyone money, was reading the proclamation aloud in the booming voice he used for market day. His voice was doing the booming part. His face was doing something else entirely.

"—by order of His Majesty, King Aldric the Second, rightful sovereign of Thornfell and its marches: be it known that a great treason festers among us. There are those who carry the old gift, the wild oath, working sorcery outside the crown's license—"

Theo stopped breathing.

"—and be it further known that the crown offers *reward* for their discovery. One hundred crowns of silver for information leading to the taking of any unlicensed practitioner. Five hundred for a hedge-witch or cunning-man. One thousand—"

Aldous's voice faltered. Cleared itself. Went on.

"—one thousand for word of any *master* of the art, living or dead."

The square went very quiet. Quiet enough that Theo could hear the snow landing on his shoulders, could hear his own heart doing something clumsy and loud. Around him the villagers shifted, and he watched — with a clarity that felt like standing outside his own body — the arithmetic happen behind their eyes. He saw it in the miller's face. In the widow Carn's. In young Tam Fletcher's, who used to steal apples with him off Odile's tree and laugh about it.

One hundred crowns. A hundred crowns was a winter's worth of feed, a new roof, debts paid. People he'd known his whole life were doing sums, and he was the sum.

*Stay calm,* he told himself. *Put your hand on your chest. Think of taxes. Think of fences. Think of—*

The tall rider finished nailing the proclamation and turned his horse slowly along the line of villagers, and Theo saw, with a lurch, that the man's visor wasn't raised — and that beneath the edge of it, where his jaw should have been, the skin was *wrong*. Smooth. Unmoving. Like a mask painted by someone who had seen a face described to them secondhand.

And the thing inside Theo — the folded-down, locked-room, buried-under-ribs thing that he had spent eleven years keeping quiet — woke up.

It didn't stir. It *blazed*.

Heat roared up through his chest and out along his arms faster than he could clamp down on it, faster than any fence-mending thought in the world could smother. Light bloomed. Every lantern in the square — every hanging lamp, every shuttered window-candle, every guttering torch in the riders' brackets — flared at once, white and joyful and utterly unmistakable, throwing shadows like noon.

Sixty people turned to look at the light. Then, following it, they looked at Theo.

The tall rider's horse swung toward him. Under the false-smooth jaw, something opened. Theo couldn't say what — there was no mouth, exactly, just a darkness that widened — but he felt it *notice* him, felt it the way a rabbit feels the hawk's shadow slide across the grass, an ancient and total attention pinning him to the snow.

"There," the rider said, in a voice like a door opening in an empty house. "Bearer."

Theo ran.

Later he would never remember deciding to. His legs simply committed treason on his behalf, and the crowd parted — whether from fear of him or for him he'd never know — and he was sprinting through knee-deep snow toward the alley behind the smithy, lanterns blazing wildly overhead as he passed them, lighting his way like a procession of traitors, like the whole village's lights had conspired to point at him. Behind him: shouting, a horn blowing one long flat note, hooves finding their footing in the churned snow.

He knew these lanes. Thank every patient god, he knew these lanes — every shortcut, every woodpile to vault, every gap in the fences that he and Tam had widened years ago for apple-thieving purposes. Bless you, criminal childhood. He went through the gap behind the Fletcher barn, down the frozen streambed where the ice would hold a running man if he stayed to the shadowed side, up through the alder scrub where no horse could follow, and out onto the lower flank of the mountain where the trees closed dark and thick around him.

The flare in his chest was fading, guttering, punishing him now — a headache splitting open behind his eyes like the sky cracking, the worst one yet, pain with a strange texture to it, almost like something *tearing loose*. He stumbled against a pine trunk and pressed his forehead to the bark and breathed, and breathed, and somewhere in the roaring of his own blood he heard her voice, clear as if she stood behind him:

*Run the high paths, boy. Remember nothing. Forget nothing. And Theo —*

And there the memory frayed, the way it had frayed every time he reached for it these nine days, dissolving just before the last word like a dream at dawn. As if something in his mind had been sewn shut, and the stitches were pulling.

Hooves below. Torchlight weaving through the trunks, downhill and to the left. They hadn't lost him. They weren't going to lose him.

Theo pushed off the tree, clutched the oilcloth bundle of grimoire pages against his chest, and turned uphill — away from the lowlands, away from everything, into the high passes of the Thornfells where the smugglers' roads wound thin and secret through country that ate the unwary.

Behind him, Hollowmere's lanterns still burned too bright, marking everything he was leaving.

Ahead, the mountain waited, cold and enormous and, tonight at least, the only friend he had left.
