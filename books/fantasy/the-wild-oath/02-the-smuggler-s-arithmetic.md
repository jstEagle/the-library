## 2. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

The smugglers' road through the Grayteeth Pass was, in Theo's considerable experience, the only honest thing in Thornfell.

It lied about nothing. It didn't pretend to be safe, or kind, or passable in winter. It simply was what it was: a knife-slash of track cut into the mountainside by men who'd needed to move brandy and salt without paying the king his tithe, three hundred years before anyone had thought to call that a crime. The crown had never paved it, patrolled it properly, or put it on any map worth owning — which meant the road had outlived every kingdom that ignored it, and would likely outlive this one too.

Theo walked it with his collar up and his hands buried in his sleeves, counting his assets the way other men counted prayers.

One pair of boots, resoled twice but sound. One coat, his mistress's, too big for him and smelling faintly of rosemary and woodsmoke no matter how many times he washed it. One satchel containing: a change of shirt, a heel of black bread gone hard as scripture, four feet of copper wire, a stub of tallow candle, and eleven charms.

Eleven. He'd started with thirty-one when he'd left Hollowmere with ash still on his cuffs. Twenty sold, bartered, or spent outright since. A charm of finding, traded for a night in a shepherd's bothy. Two charms of quiet-foot, which he regretted parting with every single time the scree shifted under him. And yesterday, at the crossroads inn where the lowland road met the high one, a charm of sweet dreams to a carter whose wife hadn't slept properly since their boy went off to soldier — the man had wept a little, pressed a silver bit into Theo's hand, and told him not to let the wardens catch him, son, whatever it is you're carrying.

*Whatever it is.* If only the man knew it was mostly arithmetic.

That was the joke of it, Theo supposed, if you were the sort of person who found anything funny anymore. The Proclamation nailed to the Hollowmere posthouse had promised fire for anyone bearing "the old gift," and here he was, surviving on the gift the way a miser survived on coin — spending it carefully, hoarding it desperately, hating himself a little more with each transaction. Every charm he made was a flare lit over open country. Somewhere behind him, surely, there were men whose trade was watching for exactly that flicker.

So he rationed. He did the sums every morning, breath steaming, fingers numb inside his gloves.

*Can I make it to the next waystation on bread alone? Yes, if the weather holds. Will the weather hold? No. Therefore: sell or starve.*

It was honest work, in its way. His mistress would have approved of the discipline and despaired of everything else.

He crested the shoulder of the pass near midday and saw, below him in the frozen fold of the valley, the caravan.

He knew it at once for what it was, because there was only one kind of train that moved through the Grayteeth in the dead of winter: smugglers going up with southern wool and coming back down with northern furs, paying no tolls because they paid instead in knowledge — which passes were watched, which wardens took bribes, which bridges would hold and which would drop a wagon into a ravine and ask no questions after. Twelve sledges, heavy-laden, drawn by shaggy mountain oxen with ice crusted in their brows. Thirty-odd figures in fur and oilskin, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the road they stood on.

At their head rode a broad woman on a broad gray horse, wearing a coat that had probably cost more than Hollowmere's entire annual wool crop.

Theo crouched behind a boulder and did the arithmetic again, faster now.

Option one: avoid them. Cost: two days' detour over the high shelf, in weather that was already turning mean, with his bread gone and his body running on stubbornness. Risk: moderate. Probability of freezing to death alone in a snowfield: uncomfortably high.

Option two: approach. Cost: one charm of warmth — his best remaining piece, the one he'd been saving against exactly this kind of cold. Gain: passage, hot food, and the near-invisibility of being one small face among thirty. Risk: they might hand him to the wardens for the bounty. Probability of that: lower than freezing, if he read the room right, because smugglers despised the crown's men even more than they despised cold, and because nobody in the free traffic liked the Hunt. It made their business worse. It made everyone nervous. Nervous people paid less.

Option two, then. He just had to be worth more to them warm and working than turned in and dead.

He'd been poor his whole life; he knew how to make that argument. You didn't lead with magic. You led with hands. *I can drive oxen, patch harness, read the sky.* The charm came later, offered like a secret, priced like one.

He stood, brushed the snow from his knees, and started down.

* * *

The caravan master's name was Odile Vass, though everyone called her Mother Vass, and she looked at Theo the way a butcher looks at a carcass she intends to buy cheap.

"Runaway," she said. Not a question.

"Apprentice," Theo said. "My mistress died in the autumn. There's nothing left for me in Hollowmere."

"Uh." She chewed on that, and on something else besides; her jaw worked constantly, like a woman who kept nuts in her cheek for emergencies. "Hedge-witch, was she?"

"Herb-wife," Theo said, which was true, and safer, and also true.

"Herb-wives don't get Proclamations written about them." Odile Vass had a voice like gravel poured slowly. "Herb-wives die quiet in their beds and nobody burns anything. I've seen the notices, boy. *The old gift.* They're pulling hedge-folk out of their kitchens all down the valley. So either your mistress was more than an herb-wife, or you are, or both. I don't much care which." She leaned forward in the saddle. "I care whether you're useful and whether you're dangerous, in that order. Convince me of the first, and I'll decide about the second."

"I can drive oxen," Theo said. "I can set a bone, draw a tooth, and tell you within a day when the weather turns. I can keep your frostbite from costing you fingers. And—" here it was, the secret, offered low, "—I can sell you this."

He drew the charm from inside his glove. It was a modest thing: a disc of river slate, threaded on leather, into which he'd folded heat the way a cook folds butter into dough — patiently, in layers, until the stone held a hearth's worth of warmth and gave it back slow, all winter, to whoever wore it against the skin.

Mother Vass took it, weighed it in her palm, and went very still.

"Warm," she said, almost accusingly. Then, quieter, to herself: "Gods below. My hands haven't been warm since the Feast of Candles." She curled her fingers around it and looked at him with new arithmetic in her eyes — not the butcher's look anymore, but something closer to the look you give a strange dog that has just demonstrated it can count.

"What do you want for it?"

"Passage to the lowland gate. Food. To walk among your people and not be asked questions."

"And if my people start asking anyway?"

"They won't get answers."

She snorted. "Everyone says that. Most of them crack inside a week." She tucked the slate inside her coat, against her chest, and Theo watched her shoulders drop half an inch as the warmth reached them — watched twenty years fall off her posture in a single breath. That was the thing about his craft that no Proclamation understood, he thought. It wasn't fireballs. It was this. It was warm hands in a cold country. It was worth more than gold to exactly the people who had the least of it.

"One condition," Mother Vass said. "You make another of these before we reach the gate. For me. And you teach my second girl to draw teeth."

"Done."

"Then welcome to the Vass traffic, apprentice. Try not to be interesting." She wheeled the gray horse around and bellowed down the line, "We move! Light's dying and so will we if we sit!"

And that was that. In the space of ten minutes Theo had acquired employment, supper, and a place in a moving crowd of thirty strangers — which was, he reflected, falling in beside the nearest sledge, the closest thing to safety the world currently had on offer. There is a particular shelter in a caravan: you become cargo. Cargo isn't questioned. Cargo doesn't have a past worth examining. He pulled his mistress's coat tight around him and let the rhythm of the road take him.

Supper was barley porridge with salt pork, served from a cauldron slung under a sledge, and it was the finest meal Theo had eaten since the night his mistress died, which was not saying much but said enough. He ate sitting on the runners between a fur-trapper named Brakka who smelled of pine tar and a boy of maybe fifteen called Wick who talked the entire time about horses. Nobody asked him questions. Mother Vass had evidently communicated the policy.

It was, for perhaps two hours, the closest thing to peace he'd felt since Hollowmere burned its lanterns.

Then the headache came.

* * *

It always began behind his left eye.

Not pain, exactly — not at first. More like pressure, like something leaning on the inside of his skull, patient and enormous, the way deep water leans on a diver. The old headaches, the ones he'd carried for years, had been like storms: sudden, blinding, done by morning, leaving nothing behind but a bad taste. Mistress Aldith had fed him willow bark and feverfew for those and told him they'd ease when he grew, and he'd grown, and they hadn't eased, and eventually she'd stopped pretending and started watching him with that expression — the one he'd only understood after she was dead. The expression of a woman doing sums she hated.

But the headaches since her death were different. These had texture. These had *direction*, as though something behind his eye were a door, and someone on the other side were testing the handle.

Tonight, around the banked fire while the sentries called the hours, the handle turned.

He was sitting wrapped in a blanket, half-listening to Wick explain the political implications of a horse named Duchess, when the pressure bloomed and split — and for one white instant Theo was somewhere else entirely.

Snow. A different snow than this, older, deeper, lying on a road he didn't know. A woman's voice saying words in a language he shouldn't have known and somehow did, each syllable landing in his chest like stones dropped down a well. His own hands, younger, holding something small and warm that screamed light—

Gone.

He was on his knees in the trampled snow by the fire, and Wick was shouting for Brakka, and Mother Vass was striding toward him with the slate-charm glowing faintly through her coat seams like a coal.

"What was that?" she demanded. "What did you do?"

"Nothing," Theo said. Which was true. That was the whole horror of it. "Nothing. It's — a sickness. Since my mistress died. Sometimes I—"

He stopped, because he could not finish that sentence honestly. *Sometimes I see things I sealed away? Sometimes something in my head knocks?*

Mother Vass studied him for a long moment, firelight jumping in her eyes. Around them the caravan had gone quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet — not silent, but listening, every conversation angled toward him like grass bending toward wind.

"There's a physician," she said finally, "in the capital. Before she disgraced herself. Maren — Maren something. They say she could look at a man's skull and tell you what was wrong with his thoughts. They say the king dismissed her for asking questions about the king's own health." She spat into the fire. "They say a lot of things. But if I had something wrong in my head, boy, she's the one I'd want looking at it. She winters in the lowlands, last I heard. Waystations along the river road, moving every few weeks, staying ahead of the wardens same as us."

"I'm not sick."

"No," Mother Vass agreed, and her voice was, for the first time, almost gentle. "Sick things don't glow."

Theo's hand went to his temple. Behind his eye, the pressure settled back — patient, enormous, waiting — and beneath it, faint as a watermark, he heard his mistress's voice, or the memory of it, worn smooth by a hundred repetitions:

*Remember nothing, Theo. Forget nothing. Both halves, and mind you keep them separate, and mind you never let them touch.*

He'd thought, all these weeks, that he understood those words. Remember nothing: don't dig at what she'd hidden in him. Forget nothing: don't lose the rest of yourself, the ordinary self, the self that drove oxen and counted coins. Keep the seal intact and the man intact, and never let the two meet.

But lying awake that night in the lee of a sledge, watching his breath freeze on the blanket's edge, a colder reading crept in. What if the words weren't instructions? What if they were a diagnosis? What if *remember nothing* wasn't advice about caution, but a description of what the binding would eventually take from him — and *forget nothing* a plea, a hope, a prayer against exactly that?

And what if the door behind his left eye wasn't locked at all?

What if it had only ever been latched — and the latch was rusting through, and every mile north brought him closer to whatever stood on the other side waiting?

Near dawn, the sentry called the hour, and the mountains stood black against a sky going gray, and Theo Dunmore lay very still and did the last sum of the night. Eleven charms. One coat. One name he was increasingly afraid to say aloud, in case saying it cost him something.

And somewhere south, in the lowlands, along the river road — a disgraced physician who could look into a man's skull and read what was written there.

He didn't believe in fate. His mistress hadn't taught fate; she'd taught arithmetic. But the numbers kept coming out the same way, and by the time the caravan lurched into motion, Theo had already decided that at the lowland gate he would leave the Vass traffic and find her.

Behind his eye, soft as a knock at a far-off door, something turned the handle again — and this time, distinctly, patiently, it knocked.
