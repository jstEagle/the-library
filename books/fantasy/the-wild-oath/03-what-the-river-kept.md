## 3. What the River Kept

The river called the Greywife ran cold enough to kill a man in the time it took him to regret falling in, which was one of the many reasons Theo Dunmore liked traveling beside it. Smugglers' roads followed rivers. Rivers didn't ask questions, didn't post proclamations, and—most importantly—didn't contain lanterns that his magic could light up like a festival.

Four days out of Hollowmere, and he had not slept properly since. Every time he closed his eyes he saw the square: forty lanterns blooming gold at once, all of them lit by nothing but a runaway apprentice's terror, and old Tam Weller looking up from his ale and saying, very slowly, "Theo. Lad. Why are your hands on fire?"

They hadn't been on fire. That was the humiliating part. His *shadow* had been on fire, briefly, before he'd smothered it under his coat and run. Tam would tell the wardens. Tam would have no choice. The Proclamation offered a full purse for gift-bearers and a hanging for those who sheltered them, and Tam had six children and a roof that leaked.

So Theo walked the Greywife road with his collar up and his charm-bag emptied of everything that smelled of craft, and told himself the story he'd been telling himself since Hollowmere: *you're just a courier now. A tired courier with a headache. Couriers don't light lanterns.*

His head had been aching since the night his mistress died—a deep, slow pressure behind the eyes, like someone leaning on a door from the other side. He'd assumed it was grief. Grief was allowed to do that. But lately the ache had developed opinions. It flared when he thought too hard about his childhood, and it flared when he tried, and it had begun—this was the part he did not say aloud even to himself—to skip. Little gaps. He'd lose a half-minute of road and find himself further along without any memory of walking it.

*Remember nothing, forget nothing,* she'd whispered at the end, gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise. It had sounded like an old woman's confusion. Now it sounded like instructions.

He was rehearsing the arithmetic of his purse—eleven coppers, a warm-charm worth maybe thirty if he found a buyer who didn't ask where he got it, and one silver he was saving against catastrophe—when the hair on his arms stood up.

Not cold. Something else. A smell, almost, though there was nothing in the air but pine and river-stone. It was the feeling of being looked at by something that wasn't using eyes.

"Courier," said a voice behind him, pleasant as a knife going into bread. "You're leaking."

Theo turned. The man stood perhaps ten paces off, dressed like a drover but standing like a soldier, and he had a long nose that kept twitching, tasting the air. A bounty-taker. Theo had heard about them from caravan men—crown-hired hounds with just enough hedge-craft in their blood to smell the gift the way you'd smell woodsmoke on someone's coat.

"Leaking what?" Theo said. His hand drifted toward his belt-knife, uselessly. Knives were for other knives.

"Gift." The man smiled. His teeth were bad. "It's coming off you like sweat, lad. Been following your stink up this river for two days. You lit some lanterns in Hollowmere, I hear. Forty-one of them. Wardens paid me a bonus for the count."

Theo's mind, which had been skipping like a scratched record all week, went abruptly, horribly clear. There was a bend in the road ahead, twenty paces past the man. There was the river, close and freezing on his left. And there was the warmth-charm in his pocket, which was not a weapon, unless—

Unless he took it apart. Which would hurt. Which might work.

"I think you've got the wrong man," Theo said. "I'm a courier. I carry invoices."

"You carry invoices," the man agreed, ambling closer, nose twitching. "And underneath them you carry a binding older than anything I've smelled in ten years of this work. Whatever's wrapped round your skull, lad, somebody spent real craft on it. Real *old* craft." His smile widened. "The crown pays extra for curiosities."

Theo pulled the warmth-charm apart.

It was designed to hold heat gently, like a cat in a basket. Stripped of its gentling, it became what it had always secretly wanted to be: a small, furious sun. Light burst out of his fist, white and total, and the bounty-taker reeled back with a shout, both hands over his eyes—and Theo turned and *ran*, because he was an apprentice and not a fool, and apprentices survived by running.

He made eleven steps.

Twelve, if you counted the stumble.

Then something hit the back of his knees and the world went over sideways into the shingle, and the man's weight landed on him, knee between his shoulder blades, breath hot and doggish against his ear.

"Cute," the bounty-taker wheezed, half-blind and furious. "Real cute. Now hold still while I put these irons—"

There was a sound like a goose being struck by a door.

It took Theo's rattled mind a moment to identify it as the sound of a heavy leather physician's satchel, swung two-handed, connecting with the back of a man's skull.

The weight lifted off him. The bounty-taker got halfway up, turned around, and beheld a tall woman in a travel-stained grey coat holding a satchel like a mace, and said, with the last of his professional composure, "Madam, you have no idea what you're—"

She hit him again. He sat down in the shallows. Then she drew a slim blade from her boot—Theo would learn later it was a lancet, of all things, honed to a wickedness its makers never intended—and did something quick and final and quiet, and the river took the bounty-taker the way rivers take everything, without comment.

Silence. Water sounds. Theo lay in the shingle, blinking at the sky, and decided not to move until he understood more.

From the bank above the ford, Maren Voss watched the boy in the shingle decide not to move until he understood more, and approved of it. Sensible instinct. Rare, in boys his age.

Her heart was going like a drum in a parade, which annoyed her. She had spent nine years as a royal physician; she had held dying men's chests open with her own hands; she had once talked a furious duke out of having her flogged. And here she was, four days into exile, heart hammering because she'd killed a bounty-taker with a bag of instruments she'd spent a decade perfecting.

Well. She'd always said the lancets were underused.

The boy still hadn't moved. Good. She crouched, checked the dead man's pockets out of habit—two coppers, iron manacles, and a folded warrant with the crown's stag stamped in black wax—and then looked at the boy properly.

That was when she stopped breathing.

She knew the signs of a bound mind. Every court physician did, in theory; oath-work was old law, mostly legend. But she had spent three years watching the king sleep—or not sleep—and she had learned what a sealed mind looked like from the outside, in the rare moments the thing wearing him let its attention slip. The pupils go wrong. Not blind-wrong, not drunk-wrong. *Sealed*-wrong, like windows painted over from the inside, with light moving faintly behind the paint.

This boy's eyes were painted over. And behind the paint, something enormous shifted its weight, slow as a whale under ice.

"Oh," Maren said quietly. "Oh, you poor creature."

The boy finally moved—scrambled backward through the wet shingle, hand finding his belt-knife, which nearly made her laugh. "Stay back," he said. "I—I don't know what you want, but I've got nothing worth—"

"You've got a binding on your mind that's eating you alive," Maren said. "Your eyes are the worst I've seen outside the capital, and I've seen things in the capital that made me leave my whole life behind at a dead run. So either sit there and bleed out your secrets at me, or get up and walk downriver with me, because that man has partners and they'll find this spot by morning."

The boy stared at her. Up close he was maybe nineteen, river-soaked, with the hollowed look of someone who hadn't eaten properly in days. "Who *are* you?"

"Maren Voss. Formerly physician to the royal household, currently a woman with a body to dispose of and a limited amount of daylight." She stood, shouldered her satchel, and considered him. "You can call me a bandit if it helps. Bandits saved your life."

"That was—" He swallowed. "You killed him."

"He was going to iron you and sell you. Yes." She said it plainly, because kindness delayed was cruelty compounded. "I'll grieve him later, if he turns out to deserve it. Come on."

"Why?" The word cracked out of him. "Why would you— you don't know me. Nobody does anything for nobody. Everyone's pricing everyone since the Proclamation. What's your price?"

It was a fair question, asked with the flat bitterness of someone who had already done that arithmetic himself. Maren liked him slightly better for it.

"My price," she said, "is that three days ago I watched a thing wear our king like a coat, and I ran instead of fighting, and running sits in my stomach like bad shellfish. You," she pointed the lancet at his painted-over eyes, before remembering she shouldn't know about the paint, and adjusting smoothly, "you look like a person things get interested in. I intend to find out why. That's my price. Curiosity and guilt. The two cheapest currencies in the world, and I'm rich in both."

The boy looked at the dead bounty-taker, at the river, at the road south toward nowhere, and Theo Dunmore—who trusted no one, who had been taught by a dying witch to remember nothing and forget nothing, whose head had been leaning against a locked door for weeks—made the first foolish, necessary decision of his new life.

"Theo," he said. "My name's Theo. And I think I need to sit down."

"You already are sitting down."

"Yes," Theo said. "I expect it's going to get worse."

* * *

Maren built a fire small enough to hide under a coat and large enough to boil willow bark, because whatever else was true, the boy had a fever riding on top of whatever else was wrong with him. They'd walked two miles downstream to a drover's shelter—a lean-to, really, three walls and a memory of a roof—where the dead man wouldn't be found until the river or the ravens got ambitious.

Theo sat wrapped in her spare blanket, shivering, watching her work with the expression of a man trying to memorize a room he expected to be thrown out of.

"You're a real physician," he said. "A royal one."

"Formerly."

"What happened?"

Maren stirred the pot. This was the moment, she supposed, where a sensible liar invented a backstory. She found she didn't have the energy for invention. "I told you I watched a thing wear the king. Let me be precise. Three years ago the king stopped sleeping. Not insomnia—the complete absence of it. He lies awake, all night, every night, and smiles. His physicians bled him, purged him, drugged him. Nothing touched it. And the ones who tended him longest started forgetting him." She glanced up. "I mean it literally. The chamberlain couldn't describe his king's face after eight years of service. Drew it wrong, every time, confident as a priest. I wrote notes. By morning my notes described a different face than I remembered writing about."

Theo had gone very still. "What did you see? When you looked at him?"

"That's the interesting question, isn't it." She handed him the willow brew. "Drink. Bitter as a tax collector, but it'll bring the fever down. When I looked at him—on my last night, the night I ran—I saw what's behind *your* eyes. Paint over glass. Something sealed. Except with the king, the seal isn't keeping something out." Her voice dropped. "It's keeping something *in*. Or worn, rather. Like a glove."

The fire popped. Downstream, the Greywife went on keeping whatever it kept.

"And you ran from that," Theo said quietly.

"I ran from that." Maren sat back on her heels. "Nine years building a name, gone in one night, because my body decided before my mind did that staying meant dying. I've been ashamed of it ever since. And then today I saw a boy pull a warmth-charm apart like a man tearing a shirt off a wound, and I saw his eyes, and I thought: *there*. The same craft. The same maker's hand, if I'm not mad. Whatever sealed that thing behind the king's face—someone put its twin on you."

"That's insane," Theo said.

"Almost certainly."

"I'm an apprentice. Was. My mistress was a hedge-witch in a village so small the census taker got lost twice. She died in the autumn. Before she died she—" He stopped. His hand rose to his temple, pressing, and Maren watched the pain move across his face like weather. "She said words over me. I thought she was praying. She grabbed my wrist and said *remember nothing, forget nothing*, and I thought she was confused, she was dying, old women say—"

"Old women who bind minds with their last breath are not confused," Maren said. "They're spending themselves. That kind of working costs the maker everything. She bought you time with her life, and the interest is coming due." She leaned forward. "How long has your head hurt, Theo?"

"Since she died."

"How long has it been *skipping*?"

He didn't answer right away. The firelight moved on his painted-over eyes, and behind the paint, that vast slow thing shifted again, and for one absurd moment Maren felt it notice her—felt it look at her the way a patient looks at a new physician, taking her measure, deciding whether she was worth the trouble.

"The last week," Theo admitted. "Little gaps. Half a minute of road, gone. Once I lost the taste of breakfast. I keep thinking I'm forgetting things on purpose. Like she asked."

"She asked you to forget what would lead them to you," Maren said. "Not everything. Not yourself." She reached out and, before he could flinch, tilted his chin up with two fingers, examining his eyes the way she'd examine any patient, because procedure was a kind of courage. "This binding is a cage, Theo. A good one—old craft, lovingly made—but it's burning fuel to stay shut, and the fuel is *you*. Your memories. Your years. If we do nothing, in a month you won't remember your mistress at all. In two, you won't remember why you're running. And the cage will still be there, empty, humming, waiting for whatever it was built to keep out to come collect."

Theo pulled away from her fingers. For a long moment he said nothing, and Maren let him have the silence, because she'd learned in nine years of sickrooms that people need a moment to hate the truth before they can carry it.

"If I take it off," he said finally. "The binding. Can you take it off?"

"Not tonight. Not alone. Maybe not at all—I'm a physician, not a witch, and this is witch-work of a pedigree I've only read rumors of." She fed a stick to the fire. "But I know someone who might. And I know a road to them. And I know that the thing wearing our king is missing exactly one piece of itself, and I'd bet my remaining reputation—which is to say, nothing—that piece is sitting across my fire wrapped in my spare blanket."

Theo stared into the flames. Maren watched him do the arithmetic—he was a boy who did arithmetic, she could see it, survival measured out in coins and charms and steps of road—and she waited, because the answer had to be his.

"Eleven coppers," he said at last, apropos of nothing.

"What?"

"My fortune. Eleven coppers, a silver against catastrophe, and half a torn-up warmth-charm." He laughed, short and unsteady. "My mistress used to say the world runs on debts written small. Fine. Physician. You killed a man for me, so I owe you a death. I don't like owing deaths. They compound." He stuck out his hand across the fire, thin and shaking and stubborn. "Downriver, then. But I set the pace on the smuggler's roads, and you follow my rules: we trust each other exactly as far as the next mile, and we renegotiate daily."

Maren shook it. His grip was colder than the river.

"Daily renegotiation," she agreed. "Best contract I've signed all year."

They doused the fire and walked. Behind them the Greywife slid past the drover's shelter, dark and patient, carrying its one small secret seaward—and ahead, somewhere south where the roads thickened into the capital's web, something behind a painted window felt its missing piece begin, at last, to move toward it.
