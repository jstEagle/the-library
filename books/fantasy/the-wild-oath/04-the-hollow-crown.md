## 4. The Hollow Crown

The waystation had been a chapel once, before the road moved and left it to the crows. Now its roof leaked snowmelt in three places, its altar had been chopped into firewood by some pragmatist with a grudge, and its single window stared out at the pass like an eye that had given up blinking. Maren Voss, formerly Physician Royal of Thornfell, third of her name to hold the post, currently a woman with no name worth speaking, sat on the broken altar steps and watched Theo Dunmore sleep.

He slept like a man who expected to be woken badly. Even unconscious, his hands kept curling toward the satchel at his side, checking on it, guarding it. Every few minutes his brow creased, and he murmured something too soft to catch—once she thought it might have been *mistress*, once, unmistakably, *no, not the lanterns*.

She should have let the bounty-taker have him.

That was what survival demanded. Survival was arithmetic; she'd learned that in the capital's upper wards, where a misplaced kindness could cost you your license, your position, occasionally your pulse. She had spent eleven years learning exactly how much mercy she could afford, and the answer had always been: less than you want.

And then a boy with a binding-sigil burning behind his eyes had looked up at her from the mud with a dead hunter's blood cooling between them, and Maren had heard herself say, *can you walk*, as if she were still the sort of person who asked.

She pulled off one boot and flexed her toes toward the fire, wincing. Her feet had opinions about today. So did her conscience, but consciences could be bandaged later.

The hunter's knife was still in her coat pocket. She took it out and set it on the altar step beside her, hilt toward Theo, where he'd see it when he woke. A physician's honesty: *I killed for you; here is the instrument; judge me if you must.* It was the closest thing to an apology the road allowed.

Theo stirred at the fire's crackle. His eyes opened—dark, wary, and beneath the wariness, for just a heartbeat before he blinked it away, a flicker of gold light that moved wrong, spiraling inward like water finding a drain.

There it was again. That signature. She had seen it once before, through a door she should never have opened.

"You're awake," she said. "Good. Drink this."

"What is it?"

"Willow bark, ginger, and melted snow. If I wanted to poison you I'd have done it while you were snoring."

"I don't snore."

"You snore like a landslide arguing with itself." She pressed the tin cup into his hands and watched him drink, cataloguing him the way she catalogued every patient: underfed, underslept, hands steady despite exhaustion—which meant either courage or long practice at being afraid—and a tremor in the left eyelid that spoke of pressure behind the eyes. Pressure like that came from swelling, or from magic wearing a mind thin as wet paper.

"How long since your headaches started?" she asked.

His cup paused halfway to his mouth. "Who says I get headaches?"

"The squint. The way you keep touching your temple like it owes you money. Also," she added, "when the bounty-taker grabbed you, you made a sound I've only heard from men with skull fractures. You haven't got one. So it's not bone. It's something else pressing."

He said nothing for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere in the rafters a crow rearranged itself with the air of a landlord inspecting delinquent tenants.

"My mistress died in autumn," Theo said finally. "Since then, it's been... bad. Some days I lose hours. Some days I lose words. Yesterday I couldn't remember the word for *east* for half the morning. Just stood there on the road pointing at the sunrise like an idiot." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "You're a physician. Is it a growth? Something eating my—"

"Mind," Maren said quietly. "Something eating your mind. Yes. I believe it runs in the kingdom."

* * *

She told herself she wouldn't tell him. She told herself it all through the evening meal—barley porridge with a strip of dried mutton, eaten cross-legged by the fire while the wind tested the shutters like a debt collector. There was no reason to tell him. He was a runaway apprentice with a stolen page's worth of hedge-craft and a head full of someone else's secrets. He was cargo she was escorting out of coincidence, nothing more. In two days they'd reach Gallow's Rest and part ways, and whatever ate him would eat him far from her bedroll.

But then he banked the fire with a small gesture—not quite a spell, just a nudge, the way you'd settle a blanket over a sleeping child—and the flames dimmed obediently, and the sigil flared gold behind his eyes for a full second, and Maren thought: *three years*. Three years she had watched that same light, that same wrong spiral, in the eyes of a king who did not sleep.

"Tell me about your mistress," she said instead. "The one whose book you burned."

He went very still. "How do you know I burned a book?"

"Ash in the seams of your cuffs. Ink ghosting on your fingertips—you scrubbed, but grimoire ink bites deep. And you smell faintly of burnt rosemary, which hedge-witches use to bind protective spells." She shrugged. "Also you talk in your sleep, and you apologized to somebody named Ellara four times."

The color drained from his face so thoroughly she almost regretted her diagnostic thoroughness. Almost. A physician who spared feelings got patients killed; a physician who spared truths got kingdoms killed, though she hadn't known that until recently.

"Ellara Vane," he said. "She raised me from fourteen. She was—" His jaw worked. "She taught me charms. Small ones. Warmth, finding, mending. Nothing that should matter to anyone. And when the riders came to Hollowmere, she hid me somehow. Told me to run north and remember nothing and forget nothing, which makes no sense, both together like that. And now things are coming apart inside my head and I think—" He looked at his own hands. "I think whatever she did to hide me is what's hurting me."

"Yes," Maren said.

"And you know that how?"

"Because I've seen a binding like yours before." She fed a split log to the fire and bought herself a moment watching it catch. "In the capital. In the palace."

"In the—" He laughed. "What, you tended some lordling's cursed son?"

"No." The word came out flatter than she intended. "I tended the king."

* * *

Maren had rehearsed this confession for the road, in case she ever met anyone worth confessing to, and discovered now that rehearsal was useless. The words came out anyway, one after another, like stones rolling downhill.

"I was appointed to the royal household six years ago. Four years ago, the king stopped sleeping. Not insomnia—I've treated insomnia, it has patterns, it has *mercy*. King Aldric has not closed his eyes in three years. Not once. I have sat at his bedside through nights no physician should witness, and his eyes stay open, and they are dry, and they are patient in a way human eyes are not.

"At first we called it illness. I bled him—useless. I tried poppy, valerian, everything in the college formulary and several things not in it. Nothing touches him. He doesn't need sleep. He doesn't need food, though he eats, beautifully, publicly, at every feast—he eats the way a man reads a letter aloud in a language he doesn't speak.

"Then I noticed the shadow." Maren held her hands up to the firelight, casting her own shadow against the chapel wall—a normal shadow, attached, obedient. "The king's shadow moves wrong. Delayed. Sometimes it moves *first*. Once, during a court function, I watched his shadow turn its head toward a young gift-sensitive in the crowd—a kitchen girl, sixteen, could light candles with a touch—while the king himself was looking elsewhere. Two days later the girl was taken to the palace 'for service.' I never saw her again.

"And the last thing. The thing that ended me." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Everyone who tends the king forgets his face. Every morning, without fail, the chambermaids, the guards, the physicians—we wake and we know our duties but the face itself slides away, like a name on the tip of the tongue that never comes. We keep mirrors and portraits in the servants' passages to relearn him daily. I started keeping notes. Drawings. I drew his face forty times in a year, and every drawing was different, and every morning I looked at yesterday's drawing and felt nothing, the way you feel looking at a stranger.

"A physician keeps records. So I kept records. And one night I followed the night-nurse's rotation chart down to the old crypt levels—the pre-coronation levels, the ones sealed since the founding—and I found the fourth—" She stopped.

"The fourth what?" Theo asked. His voice had gone very quiet.

"A stone," Maren said. "An oath-stone, older than the dynasty, buried beneath the throne room with the seal-marks of nine witches cut into it. Nine marks. Eight of them worn smooth, spent. One still bright." She turned and looked directly at the sigil-light behind his eyes, which had begun, without his knowing, to glow faintly in the dark. "One still burning, Theo, and it burns in the shape of the binding in your head. Your mistress wasn't hiding you from the hunt. She was *hiding the seal in the only place the thing hunting it couldn't reach*—inside a memory it couldn't find because she locked it away from you too."

"That's mad," Theo said. But his hand had drifted to his temple again.

"Three years ago the king renewed the coronation oath at midwinter, alone, in the old crypt, with no witnesses. Afterward the sleeplessness began. I believe something answered the oath. Something old, something that was bound beneath the capital by nine witches centuries ago—an oath-eater, a thing that feeds on gifted minds the way a leech feeds on blood. It wore the oath like a glove and it has been wearing the king ever since. The hunt isn't a purge, Theo. Purges are about fear. This is about *appetite*. They're not killing the gifted—they're collecting them, herding them to the capital, and I think I know why they leave the strong ones alive."

Theo had gone pale in the firelight. "Because it eats them slowly."

"Because it eats them *carefully*," Maren said. "And because one mind it cannot eat. Yours. Whatever your mistress bound into you—whatever piece of the great seal sleeps behind those headaches—it's the counter-oath. The lock's key was cut from the same metal as the lock. You are the one thing in Thornfell that thing cannot digest."

Silence filled the chapel. The crow on the rafter, sensibly, said nothing at all.

Theo stood up. Sat back down. Stood up again, pacing the width of the ruined nave, boots ringing on old flagstones. "No. No, absolutely not. I'm an apprentice. I burned charms for farmers' rheumatism. I ran away from *one rider* and I cried about it afterward. You've got the wrong boy. My mistress was a hedge-witch, not—not one of nine legendary—she kept goats, Maren. Goats. One of them hated me specifically."

"Grief wears many coats," Maren said gently. "Goats included."

"This is—" He stopped pacing. Turned. And she saw it happen, the exact moment the horror finished dawning and became something worse: purpose. She recognized the expression because she'd caught it in her own mirror a year ago, the morning she'd fled the palace with her drawings sewn into her coat lining. It looked like a man reading his own death warrant and noticing, in the corner, a map.

"If what you're saying is true," Theo said slowly, "then everyone the wardens take—everyone I ran from, everyone faster or slower or more frightened than me—"

"—is being carried toward it," Maren said. "Yes."

"And my memories. The ones going missing. The word for east. My—" His voice cracked and steadied. "It's not the binding killing me, is it? Or—it is, but it's spending me. Like coin. Something is spending me to keep me hidden, and eventually the purse runs empty."

"Which is why you need to decide what you'll be when it does," Maren said. "A spent purse. Or a spent *weapon*. Because there are people in the capital working against this thing—small, frightened, careful people—and every one of them has been waiting for one impossible fact: a bearer the eater can't touch. I didn't save you from that hunter out of charity, Theo. I'm not built for charity. I saved you because you may be the only counter-oath in existence, and I am a physician, and my king is dying of a parasite, and I intend to treat him."

"The king," Theo repeated. "The man who ordered the hunt."

"The man the hunt is *for*," Maren said. "Or against. I admit the grammar gets difficult."

He laughed—a short, cracked, astonished laugh, the kind that escapes when terror and absurdity arrive holding hands. Then he rubbed his face with both hands and, when he lowered them, the sigil-light was steady in his eyes, gold and slow and spiraling inward, and for a moment he seemed to be listening to something very far away.

"There's a stone," he said, in a voice not entirely his own cadence. "Under the roots. Under the roots of the mountain, there's a stone that remembers my na—"

He stopped. Blinked. Shook his head hard, like a dog shedding water.

"Migraine aura," Maren said quickly, crossing to him, taking his wrist—pulse hammering, then settling. "Sit. Breathe."

"It talked," Theo whispered. "Something in my head just talked. Or—remembered out loud. Maren, what did she *put* in me?"

"That," Maren said, settling him back down by the fire, her heart performing maneuvers no physician would endorse, "is what we're going to find out. Carefully. Starting tomorrow. There's a diagnostic rite—old college work, pre-Prohibition—that can open a sealed memory without shattering the rest. It will hurt. It may show us things neither of us wants to see. But if your mistress left instructions in you, Theo—if she left a *road*—then we need to read it before whatever hunts you reads it first."

Theo looked into the fire for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice was small and steady and terribly adult.

"She told me to forget nothing and remember nothing. Both. I always thought she was just... confused, at the end. Old women get confused." He swallowed. "She was giving me orders, wasn't she. Forget nothing—keep myself. Remember nothing—keep the seal hidden. And I've been failing the second order so badly that I'm about to fail the first one forever."

Maren reached over and, with a physician's brisk tenderness, tucked the blanket around his shoulders.

"We fail forward," she said. "It's the only direction available. Sleep now. Tomorrow we do the rite, and tomorrow you learn the first truth your mistress paid to bury—and fair warning, boy: in my experience, buried truths come up angry."

Above them, unnoticed by either, the crow shifted on its rafter, and its shadow on the wall—for just a heartbeat before the fire guttered—cast the shape of wings that were far too large, and folded, and waited.

Far to the south, in a palace that did not sleep, a king opened dry, patient eyes in the dark, and smiled at nothing, and the shadow on the wall behind him turned its head—first—toward the north.
