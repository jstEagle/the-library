## 5. Debts Written in Skin

The waystation had once been a chapel, which explained the roof that mostly worked and the pews someone had thoughtfully chopped up for firewood. It sat in a fold of hills like an old dog too stubborn to lie down, and it was the first place in three days where Maren said the word "safe" without immediately qualifying it.

"Relatively safe," she amended, dumping her satchel on a pew-stump and beginning to lay out instruments in a row so precise it made Theo's teeth ache. "Nothing is safe anymore. But this is at least *quietly* dangerous, and I've been craving quietly dangerous for weeks."

"You have a strange relationship with danger," Theo said.

"I was married to it for eleven years. We're comfortable together." She held a small copper bowl up to the grey light coming through the window and squinted at its interior like it had insulted her. "Sit down."

He didn't sit down. He'd learned that much about himself on the road: when Maren said *sit down* in that physician's voice — the one that had probably talked kings into swallowing unpleasant things — his whole body wanted to do the opposite.

"What rite?" he asked.

"A diagnostic rite. Very old. Very boring. Mostly it involves me looking at you while you feel slightly foolish." She began winding a length of thread around her fingers. "Your headaches are getting worse. You know they are. The gaps between them are shrinking, and yesterday you called me by your mistress's name twice, and this morning you put salt in your tea because you'd forgotten tea isn't soup."

"That happened *once*."

"It happened once that I saw. There were probably other times." She looked up at him, and her face did something complicated — the brisk competence sliding aside for a moment to show something older and more tired underneath. "Theo. I fled the capital because I watched a man forget his own face over the course of a year, and everyone told me it was grief. It wasn't grief. I know what unraveling looks like. And you—" she gestured at him with the thread, taking in all of him, the hollows under his eyes and the way his hands wouldn't stay still, "—you are unraveling at a speed I find professionally offensive."

"Offensive," Theo repeated.

"Medicine has standards. Whatever is eating you is doing it *badly*, with no craft whatsoever, like a child gnawing the corners off a book instead of reading it." She snapped the thread taut. "So I'm going to look. Properly. Underneath whatever your mistress stitched over you. Sit. Down."

And there it was again — the flare of resistance, hot and animal, rising in his chest before his brain could weigh in. He stood in the cold chapel with his arms crossed like a sulking teenager and heard himself say: "She said never to let anyone look. She said—"

"She's dead, Theo."

The words landed harder than Maren clearly intended. Her mouth pressed flat; she hadn't meant it as cruelty, just surgery. But the truth of it went through him anyway, clean as a lancet: *she's dead, and she left a lock on your mind and no key and no explanation, and you have been carrying her silence around inside your skull like a stone.*

He sat down.

* * *

The rite was, as promised, mostly boring.

Maren lit three candles that smelled of tallow and old churches. She had him hold the copper bowl in his lap while she tied the thread — red, frayed at one end, salvaged from who knew what — loosely around his wrist. She murmured under her breath in a language that sounded like water arguing with stones, and Theo felt absolutely nothing except a growing conviction that he looked ridiculous.

"For the record," he said, "in Hollowmere we diagnosed people by feeding them nettle soup and seeing if they complained."

"Mm. And what was your survival rate?"

"Surprisingly good, if they complained."

"There, you see. Empiricism." But she wasn't really listening anymore. She was leaning close, peering into his eyes with the fixed attention she gave to wounds, and whatever she saw there made her go very still. "Theo. When your mistress bound you. Did she tell you what she was binding?"

"No. She said—" He stopped. The memory was there, he could reach it, but reaching for it was like reaching into a drawer full of needles. "*Remember nothing, forget nothing.* That's all. That's all she ever said about it."

"*Remember nothing, forget nothing,*" Maren repeated slowly. "Do you understand what an ugly piece of work that is? Remember nothing — seal it all away. Forget nothing — but keep the shape of the loss, so you grieve things you can't name." She sat back on her heels. "That's not protection. Protection would let you heal. This is a cage built to be lived in."

"You don't know that."

"No," she agreed. "I don't. That's why we're doing the rite." She took the bowl gently out of his hands. "I'm going to look at the first seam. Just the first one. If it hurts, you say so, and I stop, and we find another way. Agreed?"

He agreed, because agreeing was easier than admitting that some treacherous part of him — the part that woke at night with his heart hammering and no reason in his head, only the *shape* of a reason — wanted to know. Wanted it the way a starving man wants bread he's been told is poisoned.

Maren dipped her fingers in the bowl. The water in it shouldn't have been glowing. It was ordinary well-water; he'd watched her fill it from the barrel outside. But now light moved in it, slow and greenish, like something swimming far below ice, and when her fingers touched his temples the world went quiet in a way that was worse than noise.

Then the seam tore.

* * *

It was summer. He was six years old, and the square at Millbrook smelled of bread and pig and hot dust, and his mother was holding his hand too tightly.

He knew, even inside the memory, even knowing nothing, that she was holding his hand too tightly. That was the thing about being six: you noticed the grip long before you understood why.

They'd come to market for flour and honeycomb, and she'd bought both, and then she'd knelt down in front of him right there among the barrows, flour already spilling from the torn corner of the sack, and she'd smiled at him. He remembered the smile with terrible clarity. It was the smile you give someone when you want their last sight of your face to be a good one.

"Theo," she said. "You remember the hedge-lady? The one who makes the good tea?"

"Mistress Yew," he said, proud of himself.

"When she comes, you go with her. You go with her and you don't look back. Not once. Can you do that? Can you be my brave boy and not look back?"

"Are you coming later?"

The pause lasted half a heartbeat and a whole lifetime. "Yes," she lied, beautifully. "Later."

Then the bells started — not the market bells, the other ones, the ones Theo's body knew before his mind did — and the crowd changed its texture. It stopped being a crowd and became a wall, and the wall had faces, and the faces had torches, and somewhere a man was shouting about abomination in a voice cracked with something that might have been fear or might have been pleasure.

His mother pushed him through a gap between two stalls. He ran. He was six and he ran, and he did not look back, because she had asked him to be brave and not look back, and he was brave, he was so brave, he was brave his entire life —

Except that the memory didn't stop where a memory should stop. Because he was six, but he was also twenty-six, sitting in a ruined chapel with green light on his face, and the child's body ran forward while the man's knowledge ran backward, and so he heard what the six-year-old hadn't stayed to hear. The roar going up. The specific quality of a crowd's noise when fire catches. And threaded through it, thin and rising and cut off far too soon:

His mother's voice. Saying his name. Not screaming it. Saying it the way you say a prayer you don't expect to be answered — *Theo* — two syllables, and the second one bending upward, unfinished, forever unfinished, hanging in the smoke above Millbrook square for twenty years while he slept safe behind his mistress's lock and called the nightmares *just dreams*—

"—stop, STOP—"

He came back to himself on the chapel floor. He didn't remember falling. His wrists ached where Maren had gripped them, and her face above his was white as the candles, and the copper bowl had cracked clean in two.

"Say something," she said. "Theo. Say anything."

He said his mother's name. Then he couldn't stop saying it, or rather he couldn't stop hearing it, that bent unfinished second syllable looping in his skull like a bell with a crack in it. He got up off the floor because staying down was unbearable, and he walked to the far wall, and he put his hand against the cold stone, and he shook.

Maren let him shake. To her credit — and he would think, much later, that this was the moment he decided to trust her — she didn't touch him, didn't murmur comfort, didn't do any of the soft useless things. She picked up the broken halves of the bowl, set them down neatly, and waited, the way you wait for a fever to break.

"She burned," Theo said finally. His voice came out scraped. "She burned and I ran and I didn't look back and everyone always said what a brave boy I was."

"Yes."

"And she—" His breath hitched. "The binding. It kept that. All these years it kept that behind the lock, whole, perfect, every detail, keeping it *fresh*—" He turned around. "Why? Why would Mistress Yew keep that? What kind of mercy is—"

"It isn't mercy," Maren said quietly. "I told you. It's a cage. And I think I know now what it's caging." She rose, brushed off her skirts, and met his eyes with the flat honesty he was learning to dread and rely on in equal measure. "A cage keeps things in, Theo. But you build one that strong when you're more afraid of something getting *out*. Or in. Think about what your gift does — it calls. Lanterns light when you're frightened. Things answer you. Your mistress didn't hide you from the crown's hunters."

The cold went down his spine ahead of her words, the way frost crosses a window.

"She hid you from the thing that hunts the gifted," Maren finished. "The thing I ran from. The thing wearing the king. And a hiding-place that costs this much blood—" she touched her own temple, mirroring his headache, "—isn't a hiding-place anymore. It's a debt. Being paid in installments. Out of *you*."

* * *

He packed his bag. He did it fast and badly, shoving in the charm-wire and the flint and the map he'd traded two days' warmth-charms for, and Maren watched him do it without moving to stop him, which somehow made it worse.

"You're leaving," she said.

"I'm thinking."

"You're leaving *angrily*, which is thinking's stupid cousin." She leaned against the pew-stump, arms folded. "Go on, then. Walk out into the snow with a hole torn open in your mind and a debt eating you alive, and see how far the smuggler's roads carry a man who can't remember which roads they are."

"I'll manage."

"You'll manage beautifully until the second headache. Then you'll manage the way a lamp manages oil." She sighed, and for a moment the physician fell away entirely and there was just a tired woman in a cold church who had killed a man three days ago and hadn't slept properly since. "Theo. I'm not your enemy. I'm not even your friend yet — friends take longer than four days. But I am the only person standing in this frozen ruin who knows what's inside your head, and I'm telling you: the binding is failing. Not weakening — *failing*. There's a difference. A dam weakens slowly. A dam *fails* all at once, and everything it held comes through at once, and everything it was holding back comes looking for whoever broke it."

He stood in the doorway. Snow was starting again, fine and dry, hissing against the stones. Behind him, the three candles guttered. Ahead of him, the passes he knew like his own pulse, and solitude, and the arithmetic he was good at: alone, he might last a month. Maybe six weeks.

And behind him, that bent syllable. *Theo.* Twenty years of it waiting in the dark of him, patient as a creditor.

"If she loved me," he said, not turning around, "she should have told me. She should have let me choose."

"Perhaps," Maren said, "she was choosing for you. Mothers and mistresses do that. Kings do it to whole kingdoms." A pause. "Or perhaps there was a reason she couldn't tell you. Perhaps telling you would have been the same as opening the door."

He hated her a little for saying it, because it was exactly what the sensible voice in his own skull had been whispering since the memory tore loose, and he hated most the truths he'd almost reached himself.

His hand was on the door-latch. The snow waited. The road home — the road to *nothing*, to no name, no mother's face, no grave, just miles and headaches — waited.

And then, from deep beneath the floor of the chapel — from deeper than the floor, deeper than the hill, from somewhere old — he felt it.

Not heard. Felt: a slow attention turning toward him, vast and unhurried, the way a fisherman feels the line go tight. Three days' ride south, something behind a king's eyes lifted its head. And every lantern in the ruined chapel — three candles, snuffed minutes ago, drowned in their own wax — relit themselves, all together, all at once, burning green.

Maren stared at them. Then at him.

"Theo," she said carefully, "how far away did you say the capital was?"

"Four days' ride," he whispered, "if the weather holds."

Outside, the wind changed direction. It had been blowing from the north all week.

It was blowing from the south now.
