## 6. Thornfell's Teeth

Theo heard the mountain before he saw it.

That was the thing about Hollowmere's passes—twenty years of running charcoal and untaxed brandy through them taught a body to listen. The smuggler's road had a voice: wind in the scree like a kettle left on the fire, the tick of freezing stone settling into its bones. A healthy mountain grumbled. It did not *groan*.

"Stop," Theo said.

Maren stopped. She'd learned that much about him in three days on the road together—that when Theo Dunmore said a word like that, flat as a slate, you obeyed it and asked questions after.

Ahead of them, where the pass narrowed between two shoulders of granite, the groan became a roar. The cliff face above the road shrugged off half of itself with the casual violence of a man knocking ash from his pipe, and forty tons of rock came down across the path like a door slamming shut.

Dust boiled out. Pebbles rained past their boots. Somewhere under all that stone was the road, and the road was not going to be seeing daylight again this winter.

"Well," Maren said at last, brushing grit from her sleeve with the fastidious air of a woman who had once been addressed as *Master Physician* by men who owned silver forks. "I suppose we climb."

"We don't. Scree slope on the north face goes for six hundred feet into a ravine full of things I'd rather not name before breakfast."

"You said you knew these roads."

"I said I knew them." Theo stared at the slide. His head had begun to ache—the deep, structural ache behind his eyes that had been his constant companion since Mistress Vael died, like someone rebuilding a wall inside his skull one bad brick at a time. "Knowing a road and it existing are two different contracts."

He walked toward the rockfall anyway. There was a smell under the dust that didn't belong—cold iron and burnt hair, sharp as a struck flint. He'd smelled it once before, years ago, when a rival crew had cut a rope bridge rather than let a cargo reach the coast. Sabotage had a scent. Mountains falling on their own schedule smelled like rain and old stone. Mountains pushed smelled like *this*.

"Maren," he said quietly. "Look up. Not at the rocks—at the edges of them. Where they broke."

She climbed partway up the talus without hesitation, which Theo was learning was simply what Maren did: she treated every danger as a patient presenting symptoms, and patients were meant to be examined, not fled from. She pressed her palm against a sheared face of stone, then frowned.

"There's no weathering on the fracture lines," she said. "And—" her fingers came away grey-black, "—this isn't soot. This is residue. Ritual grade. Someone burned something here. Recently."

"Wardens use gift-hounds," Theo said. "Hounds can't dig through forty feet of rock. But wardens who know a gifted person is coming through a pass—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Her expression finished it for him, and it was not an encouraging expression.

"They're not chasing us," Maren said slowly. "They're *herding* us. Cut the road ahead, leave the valley open behind, and any sensible quarry turns around and runs straight back down into the net."

Theo's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. Behind them, three days back down the trail, lay the ford where the bounty-taker had ambushed him—and where Maren had done what she did with the knife. If anyone had found that body, the hunt had a direction now. And if the hunt had a direction, then the only way out was the way nobody sensible would ever go.

"I know a route," he said.

"Of course you do."

"It's a mine."

"Of course it is."

* * *

Maren had a theory about fear, developed over eleven years of medicine and one spectacular disgrace: fear was just the body doing arithmetic too loudly. Heart rate up, breath shallow, hands unsteady—the mind shouting numbers at itself until it couldn't hear anything else. The cure, when there was one, was to give it better sums.

So she counted as they climbed the goat track above the rockfall: two hundred steps to the mine mouth Theo swore existed, one lantern between them, zero reasonable alternatives. The arithmetic steadied her. What did not steady her was watching Theo count along with her under his breath, because she hadn't told him she was counting, and when she glanced over he gave her a small crooked smile and said, "Old habit. You count your steps in the high country or you stop trusting them."

The mine mouth was exactly where he'd said—a black slot in the hillside, timbered with beams gone soft as bread, half-swallowed by snowdrift. A rusted chain hung across it, bearing a plaque some crown clerk had nailed up twenty years ago:

**CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE KING'S MINING WARDEN. PERIL OF COLLAPSE. TRESPASSERS ANSWER TO THE CROWN.**

"Peril of collapse," Maren read aloud.

"The whole mountain's a peril of collapse right now. At least in here the collapse will be honest about its intentions." Theo snapped the chain with a twist of his hands that made her wince—it should have taken bolt cutters—and ducked inside.

The tunnel swallowed them. Cold air moved through it in long slow breaths, like the belly of something sleeping. Maren held the lantern high and tried not to think about how far underground they were, or how the timber overhead groaned in a register suspiciously similar to the mountain's groan outside.

"How do you even know this place?" she asked, mostly to keep her own fear from doing loud arithmetic.

"Smugglers used it before my time. My mistress knew the man who mapped it." Something crossed Theo's face when he mentioned her—the flicker that always crossed it, grief and headache arriving together like unwelcome cousins. He pressed two fingers to his temple. "There's a cross-cut about half a mile in that comes up on the far side of the ridge. Wardens will be watching the road below. They won't be watching a dead mine."

"And if they are?"

"Then we'll improvise. I'm told physicians are good at improvising."

"I'm told smugglers are good at dying," Maren said sweetly, and was rewarded with a laugh—short, startled, real. Three days of trudging through frozen silence, and she was beginning to collect those laughs the way a miser collects coins. They were rare currency. She intended to hoard them.

They walked. The tunnel bent, branched, bent again. Theo navigated without hesitation, hand trailing along the left-hand wall, and Maren realized he wasn't reading the passages—he was *remembering* them, walking through a map drawn somewhere behind his eyes. Then, at a junction marked by a rotted surveyor's stake, he stopped dead.

"This way," he said.

"That's a collapse."

"That's a collapse *now*. Last time I walked it, there was timber bracing." He stood very still, and Maren saw it happen—the small stutter in his face, like a candle guttering. "There was timber. There were... there were marks chalked on the beam. An arrow. A joke. Someone wrote..." His brow furrowed hard. "Someone wrote something funny on the beam. I can't—I could swear I remember laughing at it."

"Theo."

"It's fine." He said it too quickly, the way people say *it's fine* when it is precisely not fine. "It's been fifteen years. Things fall out of a mind. That's normal. That's what minds *do*."

It was not, in Maren's medical opinion, normal. She had watched memories fail in fevers and age and the grey rot of certain poisons, and none of them failed like this—not in clean holes, not selectively, not leaving the map intact while the margin-notes vanished. Whatever Mistress Vael had sewn into this boy's head, it was unraveling, and it was taking him with it, stitch by stitch.

She didn't say any of that. Instead she took the lantern and stepped forward onto the collapsed section, testing each footing like a surgeon testing tissue, and said, "Then we climb carefully and you tell me about the joke later. When you remember it."

Behind her, silence. Then, quietly: "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me when we're above ground and I haven't broken an ankle."

* * *

The hounds found the mine mouth an hour after they entered it.

Theo felt them before he heard them—that was the cruelest joke of his blood. Gift answered gift across distance like one tuning fork waking another, and whatever foul alchemy lived in the wardens' hounds, it reached into his skull and plucked a string. Pain lanced through the ache behind his eyes and set every sealed door in his memory rattling on its hinges.

He staggered. Caught himself on the wall. Kept walking, because stopping was dying, but kept one hand pressed flat against the cold stone and fed the mountain a thread of warmth as he went—just a whisper of working, enough to settle the old timbers, to coax the frozen ground into holding its grip. Small magics. Cheap ones. He told himself they were cheap.

The tunnel disagreed.

At the second junction the ceiling shed a sheet of frost with a crack like a musket shot, and Theo's memory shed something with it. He was mid-sentence—telling Maren about a winter crossing, a mule named Duchess, a blizzard—when the story simply ended. Not trailed off. *Ended*, mid-word, the way a scratched song skips.

"—and Duchess planted all four feet and refused, and the blizzard—" Nothing. White nothing. He stood in the dark with his mouth still shaped around a word that no longer had a story attached, feeling along the edge of the absence like a tongue finding a missing tooth.

"What happened to Duchess?" Maren asked.

"I don't know." His voice came out very level, which frightened him more than shaking would have. "I was telling you about her. I could see her. Grey ears, one folded. There was a joke about her and a keg of brandy, and I—" He laughed, or started to, and the laugh turned into something else. "There it is again. The same hole. Duchess and the beam and the joke. All the funny parts, Maren. It's eating the funny parts first."

"Binding magic is economical," Maren said softly. "It takes what costs the least to burn. Joy is cheap fuel, Theo. Grief is heavy; it doesn't burn well." She said it like a diagnosis, gentle and terrible, and then she put her hand on his arm—briefly, briskly, physician-fashion—and pointed up the passage. "Come on. Duchess would want us to keep moving."

"You don't even know if she was real."

"Neither am I, some days. Walk."

* * *

From her place at the rear, Maren watched the boy fight a war nobody could see, and revised her opinion of him upward for the fourth time that day.

She had known gifted folk before—had stitched wounds for hedge-witches and delivered babies for charm-workers back when such work hadn't cost a person her name and career. They had all carried their gift the way one carries water: carefully, resentfully, aware of the weight. Theo carried his like a leak he was pretending not to notice, bailing with both hands while smiling and making jokes about mules.

Now the bailer was failing. She could see it in the skips. Twice more in the following hour his stories dropped out from under him—one about a fence in Gallow's Rest who sold enchanted bootlaces, one about his mistress teaching him letters with a stick in cold ash. Each time, he lost the middle and kept the edges, and each time, he paused one beat longer before covering it with humor.

The third time, he didn't cover it at all.

"She's killing me slowly," he said to the darkness ahead of them, conversational as a remark about the weather. "Or saving me slowly. Depends on the day, I expect. Today feels like the first one."

Maren thought about the king. Three years sleepless. A shadow that moved wrong. Physicians who forgot his face by morning—she'd written the observation in her own journal four times, in four different hands, and read it each dawn like a stranger's notes, and that was the night she'd understood what wore the crown, and run. She thought about the binding-signature she'd seen flare in Theo's eyes when the bounty-tacker died: the old circle's knotwork, the seal-work of Thornfell's founding witches, alive and starving in a boy's head.

"You may be both," she said. "Saving and killing aren't opposites in this kingdom. They've been the same act since before either of us was born."

"Comforting."

"I'm a physician. Comforting is extra."

He laughed—the third coin in her collection—and then the tunnel ahead opened into the cross-cut he'd promised, and everything got worse at speed.

* * *

The cross-cut was collapsing. Not had collapsed—was collapsing, in slow motion, a seam of rotten quartz letting go grain by grain somewhere above them with a sound like sand in an hourglass the size of a cathedral. Theo stood beneath it and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the rockslide closed the pass.

Option one: turn back. Back meant the hounds. The hounds meant wardens, and wardens meant a cell, and a cell meant the thing wearing the king getting what it wanted, which—per Maren's white-faced telling of it—was him, alive, soon.

Option two: go through. Through meant magic. Real magic, not whispers—holding a thousand tons of hungry mountain back while two people crossed a hundred feet of dying tunnel. And his reserves were already spent down to fumes from the small workings, and the binding ate every scrap he raised like a toll-keeper with an iron scale.

His head throbbed. In the throb, almost kindly, rose the memory of Mistress Vael's last lesson—*remember nothing, forget nothing*—and beneath it, fainter, the shape of a warning she'd never quite spoken aloud.

"Maren," he said. "When we come out the other side, ask me something. Anything about my childhood. Promise me."

"Why?"

"So I'll know how much is left." He rolled up his sleeves. "Get behind me and walk when I say walk, not when you think it's safe. I'm going to be busy."

He laid both palms against the tunnel floor and pulled.

Magic came up through him like water through a cracked pipe—his gift answering, the binding clamping down, the two of them grinding against each other inside his skull with a pain that whited out the world. He held anyway. He built the working the way his mistress had taught him to build anything: honestly, from the bottom, no shortcuts. Warmth into the stone. Stillness into the seams. A promise made to a mountain: *not yet. Hold. HOLD.*

The tunnel held.

They crossed it in ninety seconds that felt like ninety years, Maren moving quick and low under raining grit while Theo stood rooted, arms shaking, blood or something like it seeping from his nose, feeding the working everything he had and then reaching for things he didn't know he had. The mountain screamed. He screamed back, silently, in the language of heat and pressure and stubbornness, and the mountain—being older, being wiser—eventually blinked first.

"Go!" he gasped, and Maren hauled him the last ten yards by the collar as the working failed and the cross-cut came down behind them in a thunderclap of dust and ruin.

They emerged into grey daylight on the mountainside, snow beginning to fall, and Theo sat down in a drift because standing had stopped being among his available options.

Maren knelt in front of him. Her face was pale and set. She took his chin in her hand, physician-fashion, tilted his head toward the light, and looked at his eyes for a long moment. Whatever she saw there made her jaw tighten.

"You asked me to test you," she said gently. "Here's the test. Your mother. Tell me about your mother."

"My mother." Theo reached for it.

He found the shape of the hole first—that was worst, that was always worst, knowing the exact dimensions of the absence the way you know a missing stair in the dark. He had a mother. He knew he had a mother. There was a kitchen, there was smoke, there was a word he associated with her that might have been a name or might have been a prayer, and beyond that—

Beyond that, snow. Clean white nothing, drifting where a person should be.

"I don't," he said. His voice cracked down the middle. "I don't remember her face. Maren, I don't remember whether I—whether she—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. The binding pulsed behind them, sated, quiet, like a dog that had eaten. "Was she tall? Did she sing? Somebody has to know. *You* don't know. Nobody living knows. It's just gone."

Maren did the only thing she had left. She sat down in the snow beside him, put an arm around his shaking shoulders, and let him grieve for a woman he could no longer name, in sight of a pass they'd bought with a piece of himself, while somewhere below them the hounds bayed at the sealed mine mouth like the world's most persistent creditors.

"We rest tonight," she said at last, when the shivering slowed. "There's a village in the next valley. Off every chart the crown keeps, if the stories hold true."

"What village?"

"Hollowbrook? Hollow-something. A mapmaker lives there. Blind, they say, and the best cartographer in three provinces, and her father charted the old oath-stones before the crown pretended they never existed." Maren stood, brushed the snow from her coat, and offered him her hand. "You need answers about what's eating you. I need answers about what's eating the king. She may be the only soul alive holding both maps."

Theo looked at her hand. Then up the valley, where the snow was thickening into evening.

"Duchess," he said suddenly. "The mule. I remembered—the joke. She wouldn't cross the ice, so we drank the brandy waiting for her to change her mind, and the joke was—" He smiled, wet-eyed, and it was a real smile, crooked and stubborn. "It's back. Some of it's back."

"Some," Maren agreed. "Which means some can return." She hauled him upright. "Walk, seal-bearer. We're not done paying, but I hear the interest rate improves ahead."

And behind them, far below, the hounds went silent—which was somehow worse than the baying—and the mountain, patient as a debt, began to snow.
