## 7. The Cartographer's Daughter

The house announced itself before we saw it, which was a mercy, because by then I could barely see anything at all.

Snow had been falling for six hours—the fine, malicious kind that finds the seam between collar and skin—and the pass had narrowed to a corridor of grey rock and greyer sky. Theo had stopped talking somewhere around the fourth hour, which frightened me more than any silence ought to. A talkative man going quiet is a man conserving something he cannot spare.

Then the smell of woodsmoke, and then—impossibly, absurdly—a signpost. It stood at a fork in what could barely be called a road, and it was carved with an arrow pointing left, beneath which someone had chiseled: *WARMTH THIS WAY. COLD THAT WAY. CHOOSE WISELY.*

"Charming," I said.

"That's her," Theo said, and something in his voice loosened for the first time since the mine. "That's Sera's humor. Her father cut the post; she painted the words." He squinted through the snow. "Or she had someone paint them. She's—" He hesitated, and I watched him arrive carefully at the word, the way you'd step over ice. "She's blind."

I looked at him. He looked at the snow.

"You know her."

"I knew her father. Old Aldric charted half the smuggler's roads in the northern passes—the wardens use his maps without knowing they're using them." A pause. "He died two winters back. I sent a charm of safe passage. I never... I didn't come."

"Why not?"

"Because people who come to say thank-you bring questions, and questions bring attention, and I was very committed to being no one." He said it lightly, but his hand had drifted to his temple again—that gesture I was coming to dread, fingers pressed to the bone behind his ear as though holding shut a door that wanted to open. The headaches were worse. I'd been timing them without telling him. Every four hours now, down from six.

"Well," I said, "you're someone tonight, or you're a corpse in a snowdrift, and only one of those gets a warm bed. Come on."

* * *

Sera Voss opened her door before we knocked.

I would learn later that this was simply how she answered: she heard footsteps on the frost from thirty paces, counted the stride, judged the weight. But standing there in the doorway, snow whirling past her shoulders into a hall lit gold and orange, with a kettle already sighing on the stove behind her—as if she'd put it on when she heard us enter the valley, hours ago—it seemed less like skill and more like the house itself had told her we were coming.

"You'll be the physician," she said to me, before Theo had said a single word. Her eyes were pale grey, lovely and unfixed, aimed somewhere near my left shoulder. "And you'll be Theo Dunmore, who owes my father a visit he is eleven years late paying, and who will therefore carry in the firewood without complaint."

Theo's mouth opened. Closed. "How did you—"

"Theo." She stepped aside to let us in. "You have the same boots. You've had the same boots since you were nineteen. My father mended them twice and complained about your ankles both times." Then, to me, with a smile that rearranged her whole face into something quick and bright: "Physician, because you walk like someone cataloguing symptoms of everything, including the doorstep. Am I close?"

"She does this," Theo muttered, hauling the first armload of firewood. "Fair warning. She does this constantly."

"I do it because sighted people lie with their faces," Sera said serenely, shutting out the storm. "Feet are honest. Now sit down before you fall down, both of you. There's stew. There will be arguments later, but first there is stew."

* * *

The stew was barley and mountain turnip and something gamey I chose not to identify, and it was the best thing I had eaten in three years, and I told her so, and she said, "Yes, everyone does," in a tone of great satisfaction.

We ate. The stove ticked. Snow flung itself against the shutters with increasing personal grievance. And I watched Theo Dunmore begin, very slowly, to relax—which was precisely when I began, very quietly, to worry.

Because here is what I saw, physician's habit, cataloguing even at dinner: the tremor in his left hand when he thought no one was looking. The way his gaze snagged on objects and slid off them, like a man reading a page and retaining none of it. The moment, halfway through the meal, when he paused with his spoon raised and said, "Did I ever tell you how I met your father?"—and Sera said, gently, "Twice, Theo," and he laughed, and the laugh had a hairline crack running through it.

He was losing time. Small pieces, small enough to laugh off. I had seen the beginning of this pattern once before, in the palace, in a different context entirely, and my stomach had not yet forgiven me for it.

"So," Sera said, setting down her bowl. She turned her face toward me with the unnerving accuracy of her listening. "Maren Vael. Formerly First Physician to the Crown of Thornfell. Struck from the college rolls eight months ago. Fled the capital under a cloud the shape of a noose." She folded her hands. "My father kept correspondence with half the kingdom's quiet people. Word travels. So. Tell me what the king looks like these days."

"Sera—" Theo started.

"I'm asking her, not you." But her voice held no sharpness, only a terrible patience. "Because I've been waiting two winters for someone to walk through that door who had actually stood in the same room as him. And because my father spent forty years mapping stones that everyone else insists are folklore, and he died convinced the folklore was about to wake up. And I would like to know if he was right."

I set down my spoon.

What came out of me then was not the polished report I had rehearsed across a hundred miles of frozen road. It was messier than that. I told her about the king who had not slept in three years—not one hour, not once, the sleepless vigil documented by physicians who were rotated every fortnight so that no one tended him long enough to notice what happened to their own minds. I told her about his shadow, which lagged. Not much. A heartbeat's delay, visible only in candlelight, only if you knew to look—but I had known to look, because I had trained myself on stranger things. I told her how each attendant forgot his face by morning: not the office, never the office, they remembered *the king*, the crown, the function—but ask them to describe the man and their descriptions slid apart like water. One said grey-bearded. One said young. One, weeping, insisted the king had her dead son's face, and could not be reasoned out of it.

"And you concluded?" Sera asked.

"I concluded I was going mad, and said so to the wrong person, and was struck from the rolls within the week." I wrapped my hands around my cup. "But I had read the old texts. The ones the college calls superstition. There is a family of entities the old witches called oath-eaters. They don't possess, exactly. Possession implies a fight, a theft, something broken into. This is worse. They are *invited*. They attach to an oath—a coronation vow, a marriage vow, a healer's vow—and they wear it. Feed on it. And when the host mind runs thin..." I made myself say it plainly. "They eat the gifted instead. Gifted minds keep them fat. A kingdom with a hunt for gifted folk isn't persecuting anyone, Lady Voss. It's *harvesting*."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse expressed an opinion.

"And the binding," Sera said, turning to Theo. Her voice changed—softened into something that made him flinch worse than an accusation would have. "The one old Mother Caspenn stitched into your head. The headaches. The gaps. Tell me."

"I don't know what you mean," Theo said, too fast.

"Theo Dunmore. You burn charms to hide your gift, you trade warmth-spells like coin, and you've been pressing your hand behind your left ear since you walked in my door, which is where a mind-binding anchors when it's laid by someone right-handed. My father taught me the anatomy of working as he taught me the anatomy of roads." She leaned forward. "You are not hiding your gift. You are *caged* in it. And the cage is eating you. Yes?"

I watched him try to hold his face together, and fail.

"Yes," he whispered. "Since she died. Remember nothing, forget nothing—that's what she told me, at the end, and I thought it was grief-madness, an old woman's riddle. But the headaches started the night after her funeral and they haven't stopped. And lately things go missing. Hours. Names. I couldn't remember the name of the pass I've walked since I was fifteen. I had to *read the signpost*." His voice cracked on the absurdity of it. "I burned her grimoire, Sera. Page by page. To hide. And I think—I think maybe that was the cruelest thing I've done, because I think the answers were *in it*."

"They were," Sera said. "Some of them. But not all." She rose, unhurried, and crossed to the far wall—the wall I had taken for shelving. It was shelving, but what it shelved was maps. Hundreds of them, rolled and flat, inked and re-inked, generations of careful hands. She found one without searching, the way a musician finds a string. "My father didn't chart smuggler's roads. That was his cover, and a good one, and it paid for this roof. What he charted was this."

She unrolled it across the table, weighting the corners with salt cellars and my abandoned spoon.

It was the kingdom—but wrong. Thornfell as I had never seen it drawn. Beneath the familiar geography ran another layer, in faded violet ink: a web of lines connecting points across the whole country. Mountain passes. River fords. And at the center, at the capital, a knot of converging lines dense enough to look like a wound.

"Oath-stones," Sera said. "Standing stones, boundary markers, foundation-stones—things the modern world walks past without seeing. Fourteen of them, major. Dozens minor. My grandfather mapped nine. My father finished the work. And the oldest record he ever found—older than the kingdom, older than the crown—says they were raised by a circle of thirteen witches to bind something hungry beneath the capital. Something that had eaten its way through three kingdoms before this one." Her finger traced the violet lines inward. "The stones don't cage it. Stones can't cage a thing like that. They form an *oath*. A counter-vow, woven into the land itself. And an oath needs bearers. Living ones. The circle bound themselves into it, and their heirs after them, generation on generation, each carrying a piece of the seal in their own blood and bone—usually without knowing it."

The room had gone very still.

"How many heirs are left?" I asked, though I already felt the answer arriving like weather.

"Three, as of last summer." Sera's voice was gentle and merciless. "One was a woman called Caspenn, in Hollowmere, who the records list as hedge-witch and midwife and keeper of the northern line." She turned her pale eyes toward Theo, though they could not see him, and somehow saw him completely. "She died protecting her heir. Who is sitting in my kitchen refusing to meet my face, which is rude, since I can't meet yours either, and we might as well be even."

Theo stood up so fast his chair went over.

"No." He backed toward the door. "No, you've got the wrong—I'm an apprentice. A smuggler's runner. I make charms of *warmth*, I'm barely—she never told me any of this, she told me to *run and hide*, why would she tell me to hide if I was—"

"Because a seal-bearer who knows he's a seal-bearer radiates like a bonfire," Sera said. "Every gift-hound, every warden, every hollowed thing wearing a nobleman's smile would feel you from a hundred miles. She caged your knowledge *and* your gift inside the binding itself, because the binding wasn't just protection, Theo. It was concealment of the deepest kind. Your piece of the great seal—your inheritance—has been locked inside your own memory, behind your own name, all these years. Hidden so well that even you couldn't find it." She paused. "And hidden so well that the only key is the binding itself. Which is unraveling. Which is why you're losing hours. The cage is dissolving, and what's inside it is starting to leak."

"The counter-oath," I breathed. It assembled itself in my head with the horrible click of a diagnosis finally landing. "The entity wears the coronation oath. It can't be fought, starved, or exorcised while the oath holds—it *is* the oath. But the witches' seal is an older vow, a prior claim. If a living bearer stands at the stones and speaks the counter-oath—" I looked at Theo, and hated what I was about to say, and said it anyway, because he deserved the truth more than he deserved comfort. "You wouldn't be fighting it. You'd be *outvowing* it. Unmaking its claim. And the price of speaking that oath is written into the old texts too. A seal-bearer pays with what the seal holds. Their memories. All of them. Everything that makes them themselves."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind threw snow against the shutters like a beggar demanding entry.

Theo stood with his hand against the door, half-turned to flee, and I watched him weigh it—I watched him actually weigh it, the door against the table with the map on it, escape against purpose—and I understood, in that moment, that I would follow this stubborn, frightened, dishonest young man into whatever came next, because I had spent eight months running from truth myself, and I recognized the posture.

"My mother," he said at last, very quietly. "She was burned when I was six. For bearing the gift. Is that—is that what this is? Was she—"

"I don't know," Sera said softly. "Maybe. The blood runs quiet for generations sometimes, then surfaces. Or maybe she was simply gifted, and you were always meant to be the vessel of something older. The stones would know. They remember what people forget—that was their whole design."

"The stones." He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "You want me to go stand on a rock and let it eat my life."

"I want," Sera said, "nothing. I'm a mapmaker. I show people where things are; what they do when they get there has never once been my business." She gathered the map's edges with careful fingers. "But I'll tell you what my father wrote in his last letter, because I've had two winters to decide whether he was right, and I've decided he was. He wrote: *The seal doesn't want a hero, Sera. Heroes break seals. It wants a witness—someone willing to see the whole of it, all the way to the end, and pay honestly.*" She rolled the map and held it against her chest. "Whatever you choose, Theo, choose it knowing. That's all any of us owe anyone."

* * *

He didn't leave that night. He sat up by the stove until the small hours, and I sat up pretending to sleep and watching him, and at some point he took out the little carved anchor-charm he carried—he thought I hadn't noticed it—and held it in his cupped palms like a bird with a broken wing.

Near dawn he slept, fitfully, and dreamed loudly enough that I caught fragments: a woman's voice saying *remember nothing, forget nothing*; a square full of lanterns lighting all at once; fire.

I woke properly to the smell of bread and to Sera's voice, crisp as frost, coming from the kitchen.

"—no, Theo, listen to me. If the binding is failing, you have days, not weeks. And the first stone is nine days' hard travel south-southeast, and the pass road is crawling with wardens since the mine collapse, so you'll take the low route through Gallow's Rest, and *yes*, I know what Gallow's Rest is, I've mapped its cellars if not its morals—"

I came around the corner in time to see Theo staring at her with an expression I was beginning to know well: the look of a man discovering that the world has been arranged around him without his consent, by people who love him, competently.

"Gallow's Rest," I said. "The free-town."

"The fence markets," Sera confirmed cheerfully. "Where a clever boy could buy a memory-anchor charm strong enough to hold one thread of himself safe, no matter what the stones take. If he were foolish enough to walk into a town full of informants with a price on his head, which he is, because he's Theo."

"I hate this plan," Theo said.

"Mm," said Sera, slicing bread. "And yet you're already reaching for your boots."

He looked down. He was.
