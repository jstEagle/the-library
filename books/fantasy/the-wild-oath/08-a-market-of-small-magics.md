## 8. A Market of Small Magics

Gallow's Rest smelled the way all free-towns smelled: woodsmoke, wet wool, frying onions, and the faint metallic tang of too much magic bought and sold in too small a space. Theo came down off the ridge road at dusk with his hood up, his coin purse light as a dead man's conscience, and a headache behind his left eye that had been keeping time like a drummer who hated him personally.

The headache was worse today. He'd woken on the mountainside not knowing which way he'd been walking, or for how long. There had been frost on his shoulders and a hole in his memory the size of an afternoon, and when he'd tried to look into that hole directly, it had looked back—coldly, patiently, the way a trapdoor spider looks at lunch.

*Remember nothing,* Mistress Vell had told him, in the last hour of her life, gripping his wrist hard enough to leave bruises he still carried. *Forget nothing.* He had spent three years deciding those instructions contradicted each other, and lately he'd begun to suspect they were both failing.

He pushed through the gate arch of the town, where a bored guard with a crown badge pinned crookedly over his heart glanced at him, glanced past him, and decided he wasn't worth the trouble of looking *at*. That was the arithmetic of Gallow's Rest: everyone worth anything here had already paid someone else to stop seeing them.

The free-town had grown up around an old gallows hill—the name was honest, if unflattering—and it had never quite managed to be respectable since. Three streets fanned out from the market square like spilled ale, and every one of them was lined with stalls selling things the crown had outlawed in Thornfell proper. Luck-knots. Weather-bones. Love charms that worked about as often as dice rolled honestly. A woman selling bottled dreams swore hers were all true ones; her neighbor, selling bottled sleep, swore the dream-seller's were lies. Both seemed to consider this good advertising.

Theo kept his head down and did what he'd done in a hundred towns: he counted exits, counted coins, counted the crown badges. Two guards by the well, one at the gate, none near the tannery lane. Good enough. Nothing was ever good enough anymore, but good enough would do.

The fence he wanted worked out of a wagon at the end of Tinker's Row—a narrow, blue-painted cart that had clearly once been a fortune-teller's wagon and now pretended to sell buttons and buckles, with the fortune-telling stock hidden under a false floor that any child in the town could find in under a minute, because that was part of the game.

The fence himself was called Quill, though no one believed it was his name, including possibly Quill. He was small and round and gray as a moth, with spectacles so thick his eyes looked like two startled coins, and he had the particular stillness of a man who had survived forty years of dangerous commerce by never being the most interesting thing in any room.

"Buckles," said Theo, in the flat voice buyers used.

"Buttons," Quill agreed, in the flat voice sellers used. "And if you're after something under the floor, you'll want to say the word 'buckles' again but mean it."

"Buckles," said Theo, meaning it.

Quill's face did not change, but his hands began to move, lifting trays of honest tinware off a panel that swung up smooth as a wink. Beneath: velvet-lined slots, each holding something that hummed faintly against the evening air. A compass that pointed at whatever you'd lost rather than where north was. A ring that made the wearer briefly, harmlessly forgettable—three minutes at most, Quill's card claimed, though cards lied. A jar of fireflies that were not fireflies, glowing with a soft green light that Theo's skin recognized before his mind did, and he stepped back from it without meaning to.

"Gifted stock," Quill murmured, watching him step back. "You flinched."

"I'm flinchy. It's a condition."

"Mm." Quill put the jar away with the delicacy of a man handling a sleeping snake. "What are you actually after? You've got the look. Road-worn, hood-worn, eye-twitchy. And—" his coin-eyes flicked up, down, up again, "—you're leaking."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Leaking." Quill tapped his own temple. "Magic doesn't sit still in a person who's trying to hold it down. It seeps. Most folk can't smell it. I can, because most of my customers could, back when they were alive and buying. You, my boy, are seeping like a cracked jug. Which means either you're carrying something powerful—" he paused meaningfully, "—or something powerful is carrying you."

Theo's hand drifted to the strap of his pack, where Mistress Vell's last charm rode sewn into the lining. "I want a memory-anchor," he said quietly. "Something to hold what's mine. So it stops sliding."

For the first time, Quill went genuinely still. The market noise went on around them—the onion-frying, the haggling, a drunk singing something about a miller's daughter with anatomical optimism—but inside the little wagon there was a held breath.

"You know what those are," Quill said. It wasn't a question.

"I know what they cost."

"They cost more than coin." Quill took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which gave him somewhere to look that wasn't Theo's face. "A proper anchor takes a piece of the buyer to make. Not blood, nothing so dramatic. A memory. A true one, freely given. The charm holds your shape the way a peg holds a coat—so when the wind tries to blow your mind off its hooks, something stays hung up." He set the spectacles back on. "You understand what I'm telling you. To keep what you have, you have to give some of it away first. That's the arithmetic. Nobody ever likes the arithmetic."

Theo laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and bitter at the edges. "I've been doing that arithmetic my whole life. Give this to save that. Burn this page so they don't find the next one." He thought of the grimoire going up page by page in Hollowmere, Mistress Vell's life's work curling into ash while he wept into his sleeves like a child, and how even grief had felt like a transaction: *mourn later, run now.*

"Who taught you that?" Quill asked, and there was something almost kind under the merchant's wariness.

"A witch," Theo said. "The best kind. The kind who dies so you don't have to."

Quill looked at him a long moment. Then he reached deep into the false floor, deeper than the velvet slots, and brought out a thing wrapped in oilcloth. He unwrapped it on the counter between them.

It was a charm the size of a walnut, carved from rowan wood, strung on a leather cord gone dark with age. Inside its carving—tiny, precise work—Theo could see a lattice like frost ferns, and threaded through the lattice, faint as breath on glass, a shimmer of stored light. His own magic stirred toward it before he could stop it, the way a dog stirs toward a familiar whistle.

"Made it myself, thirty years ago," Quill said. "For a girl who was running from the same hunt that runs today. Different king, same hunger." His voice had gone quiet and dry as old paper. "She never came back for it. Anchors don't keep. The charge fades unless the maker feeds them, and I've fed this one longer than I should have, out of sentiment, which is the one luxury I've never learned to afford." He pushed it across the counter. "It wants a memory. One true memory, freely given, and it will hold the rest of you together for a year. Maybe two, if you don't do anything stupid."

"Stupid," Theo repeated. "Such as?"

"Running from crown wardens through collapsing mines. Bargaining with oath-things. Falling in love with anyone whose survival depends on yours." Quill shrugged. "The usual."

Theo stared at the little wooden nut in its cradle of oilcloth. A memory. Freely given.

Here was the ugly truth he'd been circling for days, the way a tongue circles a broken tooth: his memories were already leaving him. Skipping, slipping, going soft at the edges like wet paper. He'd lost an afternoon on the mountain. Last week he'd stood in front of a stream he knew he'd fished as a boy and could not remember fishing it. The binding in his skull was eating him from the inside, patient as rust, and every day the man called Theo Dunmore was a slightly smaller edition of himself.

What was one more memory, given on purpose instead of stolen?

But which one?

That was the trap in the arithmetic. Every memory felt load-bearing when you picked it up. The taste of Mistress Vell's nettle tea. The sound of rain on the caravan canvas. His mother's hands—no. Not that one. He flinched away from that shelf of himself so fast he nearly knocked over a tray of buttons.

"The price is fair," Quill said gently. "Take tonight to choose. Come back at—"

"I'll pay now."

Because if he took tonight, he would talk himself out of it. If he took tonight, tomorrow's Theo might wake up having already forgotten why the choice mattered. The binding didn't wait for convenient moments. It took what it liked, whenever it liked, and it never gave receipts.

He closed his eyes and let his mind walk its own halls, looking for a room he could bear to empty.

He found one. Small, worn, not precious—that was the trick of it, he realized, the mercy of it. Not his mother. Not his mistress. Something ordinary, stubborn, human. A summer afternoon when he was perhaps ten, before everything, sitting on the workshop steps shelling peas with a woman who might have been his mother's friend or might have been nobody at all, just a neighbor, just kindness passing through. Sun on the step. Peas popping under his thumbs. Her laughing at how many he ate raw. Nothing in it but warmth and the particular peace of a child who does not yet know the world keeps accounts.

He held it out—not with his hands, but the other way, the way Mistress Vell had shown him in a lesson he suddenly remembered with terrible clarity, because she'd taught it saying *one day you may need to give a piece of yourself away, and I pray to every god I've annoyed that you never do.*

The charm drank it. He felt the moment go—felt the afternoon lift out of him like a splinter drawn clean—and for a heartbeat he grieved, and then the grief slid off something smooth in his mind where the afternoon used to be, and he understood with cold precision that by tomorrow he wouldn't even know what shape the hole was.

"That's the whole trick of them," Quill said softly, winding the cord and pressing the anchor into Theo's palm. "You can't miss what you gave. Only what's left notices the room's a bit emptier." He closed Theo's fingers over the rowan wood. "Wear it under your shirt. Feed it a drop of blood on the new moon. And boy—if anyone asks, you bought buttons."

Theo laughed again, and this time it came out almost right. "How much do I owe you?"

"For the anchor? Paid in full." Quill was already restoring the false floor, the trays, the honest tinware, erasing the evidence of his own generosity with practiced speed. "For the buttons, two coppers, because I do have standards."

Theo paid the two coppers. He was turning to go—hood up, heart lighter and heavier in the same measure—when the crowd at the mouth of Tinker's Row parted around a figure walking toward him, and the world performed a small, obscene joke.

She was taller than he remembered. Thinner. Crown colors at her collar, discreet as a whisper: a gray sash, a silver pin shaped like an open eye. Her hair was still that improbable copper, though cropped short now, soldier-fashion. She walked like someone who had learned that streets are made of eyes.

Ilsa Vane. Fellow apprentice. The other half of the only childhood friendship he'd had. The girl who'd held his hand at Mistress Vell's funeral-that-wasn't, before the crowns came and everything burned. Three years dead to him, and here she was, wearing the enemy's silver, standing six feet away, staring at him with her mouth open.

"*Theo,*" she said. Just that. His name, in her voice, in the middle of Gallow's Rest, where names were currency and his was priced at the gallows.

Every instinct he owned screamed at once, a choir of idiots. *Run. Deny. Lie.* But his feet had gone stupid, and worse, worse than anything, some traitorous warm corner of him that hadn't heard its name spoken kindly in three years leaned toward her like a plant toward sun.

"Ilsa," he managed. "You look—"

"Crown?" She touched the silver pin as if noticing it for the first time, and something moved across her face—shame, maybe, moving fast, like weather. "It pays. It always pays. That's the whole sermon, isn't it? Everyone's got a price, and the crown's is just the highest bidder in town." Her eyes were doing something complicated—flicking to the wagon, to Quill's carefully blank face, to the crowd, counting watchers the way he counted exits. Old habit. They'd both been raised by a woman who taught them to count.

"You should go," Theo said low. "Whatever you saw here, you didn't see it."

"Theo Dunmore." She said the full name very quietly, tasting it, and he watched her weigh it—watched her do the arithmetic in real time, the horrible transparent arithmetic of the age. A captured seal-bearer, if that's what the proclamations meant when they spoke in riddles, would buy her a house. A pardon. A life with the fear taken out of it. All she had to do was raise her voice, turn her head, nod at the guards by the well.

Her hand moved toward the silver pin. Stopped. Moved again. Stopped.

"Do you remember," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle like cheap ice, "the night we stole the honey wine? From the cellar? And Vell caught us, and she didn't whip us, she made us drink the *whole flagon*, both of us, till we were sick in the garden, and she sat between us on the grass and said, 'Now you know why theft is a bad idea, and also why it's tempting,' and then she told us the stars' real names, the old ones, the ones the crown banned?"

Theo's throat closed. "I remember."

"It's the only night I think about anymore," Ilsa said. "I've been feeding the informants' ledger for two years, Theo. Names, faces, routes. People I've sent to the wardens. People who are probably in cells right now, or worse, and I tell myself the arithmetic every morning—my life against theirs, my life against theirs—and every morning the sum comes out the same and I hate it and I do it anyway." Her jaw tightened. "So don't ask me what I saw. Because I don't know yet. Ask me tomorrow. Pray to every god she annoyed that I'm still the girl from the garden by tomorrow."

"Ilsa—"

"Go." It came out sharp, a blade laid flat across his path. "Out the tannery gate, not the main one. Take the river road south. Don't stop in Duncarrow, it's crawling. And Theo—" she was already turning, already putting her mask back on piece by piece, the informant's blankness sliding over the girl from the garden like a shutter, "—whatever you're carrying, whoever bound it into you, they chose well. Vell never chose wrong. Don't make her wrong by getting caught."

Then she walked away down Tinker's Row, gray sash and copper hair, and the crowd closed over her like water, and Theo stood in the lamplight with a memory-anchor warm against his chest and the sick knowledge settling into his bones that he had just been spared—or sentenced—by a woman doing sums with his life.

Quill appeared at his elbow, button-tray in hand, wearing the expression of a man who had watched forty years of exactly this.

"Well," the fence said mildly. "There's your answer about whether the hunt reaches Gallow's Rest."

"She won't betray me," Theo said. Then, honestly, "She might. She doesn't know yet herself."

"No one knows themselves until the price is on the table." Quill began stacking buttons with great care. "That's the whole trade, boy. Crowns, wards, fences, sweethearts—we're all just pricing each other. Only difference is what currency we take." He glanced up, coin-eyes catching the lamplight. "Run along now. Buy your buttons elsewhere."

Theo bought his buttons. He walked out through the tannery gate as instructed, into the dark, onto the river road south, and he did not look back at the town where the only friend of his childhood was sitting somewhere alone, weighing his name in her hand like a coin she couldn't decide to spend.

He made four miles by moonlight before the headache struck him down at the roadside—worse than it had ever been, a white-hot spike driving through the left side of his skull—and when the world stopped spinning he was on his knees in the frozen mud, and he could not, for the life of him, remember which direction south was.

The anchor charm pulsed once against his chest, steady as a second heartbeat, holding on.

Behind him, far back along the river road, a horn sounded. Long, clear, and unmistakably crown-made.

Someone, it seemed, had finished their arithmetic.
