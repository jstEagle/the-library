## 9. The Price of a Name

The morning Gallow's Rest betrayed him, Theo woke with the taste of copper in his mouth and the memory-anchor charm warm against his sternum.

He'd slept badly, in the way he'd been sleeping for a week—snatches of it, shallow as a stream in high summer, always with one ear listening. The room above the cooper's shop was small and smelled of oak shavings and lamp oil. Maren had taken the cot by the window; she slept like a soldier even now, on her side, one hand curled near the little knife she kept under her pillow. Old habits from the capital, she'd said. Or new ones. She'd stopped distinguishing.

He'd told her about Ilsa two days ago. He still wasn't sure why. Perhaps because keeping secrets had begun to feel like carrying stones in his pockets—all of them his own choosing, all of them heavy, and the binding in his head growing heavier every day to make room.

"She'll sell you," Maren had said, not unkindly. She'd been grinding something in her mortar at the time, and hadn't looked up. "Not because she's wicked. Because she's frightened, and frightened people do arithmetic."

"I know the arithmetic," Theo had said. "I've been doing it my whole life."

"Then do this sum: what is your life worth to her?"

"A purse of crown silver and a pardon, if the wardens keep their word. Which they won't."

"And hers?"

"That's just it." He'd turned the charm over in his fingers, feeling its faint warmth, the way it tugged at him like a hook behind the breastbone. "I don't think they'll keep their word either way. That's what I can't make her see. The hunt isn't a market. It's a net. You don't buy fish back out of a net by showing them where the other fish are."

Maren had finally looked up then, mortar and pestle stilled in her hands, and given him a long, level look—the look she reserved for patients who insisted they were fine. "No," she'd said. "But that's not how frightened people count, Theo. They count what's in front of them. Silver is in front of them. Nets are for later."

* * *

He went down to the well before dawn because he couldn't bear the ceiling anymore, and because the headaches had come back—the deep, grinding ones, like someone turning a key slowly in the lock of his skull. Cold water helped. Not much. But cold water was honest, and honesty was getting scarce.

The free-town woke around him in its usual disorder: gulls arguing over the fish-cribs, a tinker swearing at his cart axle, the baker's boy hauling loaves with the solemnity of a man moving crown treasure. Gallow's Rest had no walls and no lord, only an understanding—that everyone here had come from somewhere else, and nobody asked too closely from where. It was the sort of place Theo had loved since he was fifteen and first walked out of the passes with empty pockets and a mistress's warning ringing in his ears: *remember nothing, forget nothing.*

Ilsa found him at the well.

He heard her boots first—she'd always walked like she was apologizing to the ground—and his stomach dropped before he even turned, because some part of him had known since the moment he'd seen her face in the fence's stall yesterday that this was coming. She stood at the corner of the cooper's lane with her hood back and her hands jammed in her pockets, looking exactly like the girl who'd sat beside him at Mistress Ellery's knee twelve years ago, copying sigils wrong on purpose to make him laugh.

"You shouldn't have come here, Theo," she said.

"No," he agreed. "Probably not."

"They posted your likeness in the market square. Bad likeness. But I knew the way you stand." Her jaw worked. "Like you're listening to something under the floor."

"I am, mostly. It's called a headache."

That got half a laugh out of her, strangled at birth. She glanced up the lane, down the lane. Empty, except for the gulls. "There are wardens in the town. Four. And a hound-handler who came in on last night's barge. They're asking after a woman too—a physician. Gray cloak, capital accent."

Theo's blood went very quiet. "Where are they now?"

"Asking at the harbor offices. They'll work round to the cooper's street within the hour; it's on their list. I saw the list." She pulled something from her pocket and pressed it into his hand—a folded paper, grease-spotted, covered in her cramped, slanted handwriting. "Names. Every informant working the coast road between here and the capital. You were going to learn it eventually anyway, so—" She shrugged, a terrible casual shrug, the kind people wear when they've already done the unforgivable thing and are trying to fit their shoulders around it. "Consider it a discount on the betrayal."

"Ilsa."

"Don't." Her voice cracked clean through. "Don't say my name like that. I did the sums, Theo. I told you I would. If I don't report, someone reports me, and then we're both in the net, and then nobody warns anybody ever again. At least this way—" she gestured at the paper in his fist "—at least this way somebody gets out."

"The wardens won't honor it. Whatever they promised you—"

"I know." She said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse than tears. "I'm not stupid, Theo. I was second in our class, remember? You were first, but only because you cheated off me." A ghost of the old grin. Then it died. "Go. Out the salt-gate, north along the tideline, take the cart-track past the lime kilns. Don't use magic unless you mean it. Every spark is a shout."

She turned to go, and he caught her sleeve—an impulse, foolish, dangerous.

"When they ask you where I went. What will you say?"

She didn't look back. "Whatever keeps them off the tideline longest." A pause. "It's not nothing, Theo. It's the only thing I've got left to give you."

Then she was gone down the lane, walking like an apology, and Theo stood at the well with a list of names in his hand and the certain knowledge that the clock had started.

* * *

Maren was already packing when he burst into the room. She'd heard the gulls change their argument, she said later, though Theo suspected she simply never fully slept anymore. Her gray cloak was pinned, her satchel buckled, her face set in the expression she wore for bad prognoses.

"Tideline?" she said.

"Tideline. Salt-gate. Now."

They made the gate as the harbor bells rang the hour. Behind them, in the market square, Theo could hear a warden's voice, clear and bored, reading names off a list to a knot of early risers. He didn't hear his own name. He heard Ilsa's instead, steady and flat, answering questions—and he made himself keep walking, because turning around would burn time he didn't have, and time was the only coin he had left that wasn't cursed.

The tideline was gray and cold and beautiful, the tide far out, exposing the ribbed sand and the wreck of some ancient hull picked clean by decades of salt. They walked fast, Maren's breath smoking, the lime kilns rising ahead like pale broken teeth against the dunes.

"We should split," Maren said.

"No."

"Theo. Listen to me. If they take us both, there's no one left who knows what you carry. One of us has to reach Sera's maps and the third stone. That was always the arithmetic."

"I hate your arithmetic."

"I know." For the first time since he'd met her, bleeding on a riverbank with a bounty-taker's knife at his throat, Maren smiled—small, tired, real. "It's a terrible thing, being right. My whole career was built on it, and look where it landed me."

That was when the dogs bayed.

Not behind them. Ahead—around the point past the kilns, where the cart-track bent inland toward the dunes. Two shapes cresting the shingle bar, and behind them, lanterns. Wardens had come down the coast road in the night, or been landed from a boat; either way, the net had closed from the north while Ilsa held the south.

"Fool," Theo breathed. "She gave them everything."

"No," Maren said sharply, already pulling him down behind the ribs of the old wreck. "Look at their spacing. They came straight here, no casting about. She bought us the whole night, Theo. Whatever she told them, she made it late and thin." Her eyes were doing their physician's quick accounting—two handlers, four wardens, the hounds straining at leads, the light strengthening mercilessly. "Can you run?"

"On sand? I can negotiate with sand."

"Then listen." Her hands gripped his collar, and her voice dropped to the register she used for truths too large for normal volume. "They want you alive. They'll take me however they can get me. So you go east, into the dunes, low, until the ridge cuts their sightline. I go west, loud, and give them a physician to catch."

"Maren—"

"It's the arithmetic again. I'm sorry. I know you hate it." Her grip tightened. "And Theo—the charm. If it comes to the worst. If you have to spend memory to get clear. Spend the cheap ones first. The rain on the ferry. The taste of bad stew. Save the ones that make you *you* for as long as you possibly can."

"How will I know which ones those are?"

Her smile flickered again, grief hiding inside it. "You won't. That's rather the point."

Then she stepped out from behind the wreck, drew herself up to her full unimpressive height, and walked west along the tideline like a woman late for an appointment with destiny, and Theo—heart hammering, throat closed—went east into the dunes.

* * *

It nearly worked.

For twenty minutes it worked. He ran bent double through the marram grass, following the hollows, the charm hot against his chest, the headache flaring white with every heartbeat. Behind him he heard Maren's voice raised in imperious protest—*do you know who I am, I demand you check with the college, this is an outrage*—a performance worthy of the capital stage, and the sounds of pursuit bending away from him, following her, and he hated himself for the relief.

Then the hound-handler, whoever he was, made the mistake of letting the hound think for itself.

The cry came from his left, close, too close—one of the beasts had swung wide along the dune ridge, and now it came down the slope toward him in great bounding arcs, joyous and terrible, and behind it a warden in crown gray, shouting.

Theo did the sums. There were no good numbers.

He could run for the kilns and hope the lime smoke masked him—but the wind was wrong. He could fight, and lose; the binding had eaten too much of him already, his magic arriving these days like a messenger who kept forgetting half the letter. He could stand, and be taken, and everything—Maren in chains, Sera's maps, the stones, the thing wearing the king—would end in a cell beneath the keep.

Or he could pay.

He stopped running. Closed his eyes. Reached inward past the grinding ache, past the sealed doors of himself, to the vault where his mother lived.

He'd gotten her face back five days ago, at the first oath-stone, when the stone had breathed a fragment of sealed memory into him like air into drowned lungs: a kitchen, woodsmoke, flour on her hands, her laugh low in her chest, her face—gods, her face, brown-eyed and sun-lined, tilted toward him as she sang some nonsense song about a blackbird. Six years old, he had been, sitting on the table so he'd be tall enough. He had wept for an hour afterward, silently, so Maren wouldn't hear.

*I have her,* he thought, addressing the memory itself, the way you'd address a person. *I only got you back. I'm so sorry.*

He took her face and burned it.

Magic has many flavors, and most of them get described wrong. Fire-magic isn't heat; it's hunger with direction. Memory-burning is worse—it's spending yourself as fuel, feeding the working with the only currency that matters. The concealment rose out of him like fog off cold water, wrapping him, dulling his scent, his gift-sparkle, his outline, folding him into the dunescape until the hound skidded to a stop ten feet away, sneezed, and lost him entirely—lost him the way you lose a word on the tip of your tongue.

The warden arrived moments later, cursing, dragging the hound in circles. Theo stood three feet from the man, wrapped in the ash of his mother's face, and watched the warden's gaze slide across him like water off glass.

*Move along,* Theo thought fiercely. *Nothing here but sand and a dead boy's mother.*

The warden moved along.

* * *

He found Maren's satchel strap torn loose near the kilns, and blood—not enough blood, a scuffed smear of it—and boot prints going north toward the coast road, and he understood with a clarity that felt like falling: they had her.

He should have raged. He meant to rage. Instead he sat down in the lee of the cold lime kiln with the wind combing the dune grass around him, and tried to remember his mother's face, and discovered that the drawer was not merely empty—it had been *unmade*. No kitchen. No blackbird song. No flour-dusted hands. Just a shape where a shape used to be, a doorway opening onto a wall, and a grief sitting in his chest with no object to attach to, vast and orphaned, mourning a woman he could no longer prove had existed.

He cried anyway. It seemed important. Someone had to mourn her, and apparently he was the only candidate, and he couldn't even remember why.

When the tears burned out—which took longer than he'd have guessed—he sat with the anchor charm in his palm. Warm. Patient. Holding its single thread: the feel of mountain passes under his boots. Everything else of him was negotiable now, and he knew it, and knowing it changed nothing except the temperature of his fear.

"So that's the price," he said aloud, to the kiln, to the gulls, to nobody. His voice came out scraped and strange. "That's the whole game. In the end I feed myself to the seal, memory by memory, until there's nothing left worth hunting." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Mistress Ellery, you absolute horror. You knew. All those years, *remember nothing, forget nothing*—you weren't teaching me caution. You were teaching me how to say goodbye."

The wind said nothing. The gulls argued on. Somewhere north, Maren was in a cage, buying time with that same terrible competence, trusting him to be worth the purchase.

He stood up. Brushed the sand off. Did the last sum available to him: Maren captive in the capital meant eyes inside the keep. It meant the ticking clock had a witness. It meant the only road left ran straight into the lion's mouth—and Sera's maps showed a third oath-stone on the way, up in the hills where the ghosts of the old circle waited with their patient, awful question he still couldn't answer.

Would he trade his past for the realm?

He'd just answered it, hadn't he. One face at a time.

Theo turned south, toward the hills and Sera and the stones, and began to walk—carrying a grief he couldn't name, for a woman he couldn't remember, toward a future that would cost everything he had left.

Behind him, far off across the dunes, a warden's horn sounded once—the signal that the physician had been secured, and the hunt for the seal-bearer was moving to new ground.

He walked faster.
