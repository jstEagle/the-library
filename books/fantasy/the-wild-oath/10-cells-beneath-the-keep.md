## 10. Cells Beneath the Keep

The dungeons of Thornfell Keep smelled of wet stone and old iron and, beneath that, something sweeter—like honey left too long in a sunlit window. Maren catalogued the smell the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was what kept her hands from shaking. Rot. Not of flesh. Of *oath*.

They had taken her satchel at the gate, her knives at the second gate, her boots at the third, which struck her as excessive even by capital standards. Now she descended in stockinged feet behind a warden whose shoulders were broad enough to shelve books on, past cell after cell where the prisoners watched her go with the flat, incurious eyes of people who had stopped expecting anything.

"Comfortable accommodations," she said to the warden's back.

He didn't answer. Most of them didn't, down here. She'd noticed it on the walk from the gate—the guards spoke less the deeper they went, as though the keep itself were drinking their words. By the third turning, two of her escort had fallen into a silence so complete she might have been walking with furniture.

The sweet rot thickened.

*Three years,* she thought. *Three years without sleep.* She had seen men go mad from three days without sleep. Whatever wore the crown did not sleep because whatever wore the crown was not a man, and men were the only things she knew of that needed rest. Everything else simply fed.

They stopped before a door that had no business existing in a dungeon. It was oak, iron-banded, and *clean*—the wood oiled, the hinges gleaming, a lamp burning beside it with a flame that burned without flickering, which was either excellent craftsmanship or a very bad sign. Maren had learned, in her years at court, that the two were frequently the same thing.

"He'll see you now," said the broad-shouldered warden, and his voice came out wrong. Flat. Like a man reading words he didn't understand off the inside of his own skull.

The door opened onto warmth.

Maren had expected dark. She got candlelight—a chamber carved from the living rock but furnished like a physician's study, shelves of jars, a table of instruments, a bed with clean linens. Her instruments. She recognized the brass ear-funnel with the dent she'd made dropping it during the Sallow Fever winter. They had brought her life down here and arranged it like an apology.

And on the bed, propped against pillows, sat the King of Thornfell.

He looked worse than the last time she'd seen him, which had been through a doorway, at a distance, four years ago, when he had still been merely ill rather than *wrong*. He was thin in the way that wax is thin—smoothed, softened, features blurring toward some average of all faces. His skin had the gray-gold pallor of old parchment. His hands lay on the blanket like things set down and forgotten.

But his eyes—

His eyes were open, and they were awake, and they were looking at her with an attention so total it felt like being flayed politely.

"Physician," the king said. The voice was the king's voice; she remembered it from coronation day. But the *cadence* was off, each word placed with the care of someone handling borrowed tools. "Maren Voss. Formerly First Physician to this court. Disgraced over the matter of the prince's fever." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Which was not your fault."

Her spine went cold. That detail was sealed. Court records sealed, witnesses dispersed, her own tongue bound by oath and fear. No informant network reached that deep.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said. "You know my sins and I don't know yours."

Something moved behind the king's face—not a smile, exactly. The *idea* of a smile, worn briefly, like a coat tried on for fit. "I know many things. I have eaten many minds, and they are all still in here, arguing." The eyes glittered. "Yours will argue beautifully. You think in lists."

"I do not—" Maren began, and then stopped, because she had, in fact, just begun composing a list.

The thing laughed. It used the king's laugh poorly, the way a musician plays an instrument left out in the rain.

"Sit," it said, gesturing to a chair that had been placed precisely across from the bed. Placed recently. Placed for *her*. "We should discuss terms. I am given to understand you respond well to terms."

She sat. Stockinged feet on cold stone, hands folded in her lap to hide their tremor, chin up. Somewhere above them, presumably, the court dined and danced and told itself stories about its beloved, tireless king.

"Terms," she repeated. "For what?"

"For your old life. Your name restored, your seals broken, your college chair waiting—you would be surprised how quickly a vacancy can be manufactured. In exchange: the boy. Theo Dunmore. Seal-bearer. You know where he is, or you can know, or you can find him. Deliver him, and everything the Hunt took from you is returned with interest."

The candles did not flicker. Maren realized, distantly, that none of them had flickered since she entered. Flames held perfectly still, like painted flames, like flames that had forgotten what fire was for.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

The entity tilted the king's head, considering. "Then I return you to your cell, and in a week you will not remember why you came, and in a month you will not remember your college, and in a season you will be one more blank-faced servant carrying trays to this room and wondering, sometimes, why the work feels important." A beat. "Or you may simply die. I am flexible. Eternity teaches flexibility."

It said it pleasantly. That was the worst part. It offered her erasure or death the way a host offers tea or wine.

"I notice," Maren said carefully, "that you haven't simply taken the knowledge out of my head. If you can eat minds, mine should be a light supper. So either you can't reach what I know about the boy—and I've hidden it well—or taking it costs you something."

The stillness that followed had texture. Weight. She had spent thirty years reading bodies across sickbeds, learning to hear what patients didn't say, and she heard it now in the king's borrowed silence: the small involuntary tightening around the eyes. The flicker of hunger meeting arithmetic.

"You are clever," the entity said at last. "That is not a compliment. Cleverness in my experience is mostly a disease of the frightened."

"And you're hungry." She leaned forward. "Look at your own hands. Look at the tray of food by the bed—I'd wager nothing on it has been touched in weeks. You're wearing a king and the king is wearing out, and no matter how many gifted minds you swallow, the seal-work underneath isn't feeding you anymore. Is it."

The thing wearing the crown looked at her for a long moment. Then, with a movement that seemed to cost it, it raised one hand and drew back the king's sleeve.

Maren had treated wasting diseases all her life. Consumption, fluxes, the slow gray leaching of age. She knew every shape that dying took. What she saw on the king's forearm was none of them and all of them: the flesh gone translucent near the wrist, and beneath the skin, faint as watermark, the ghost of *script*—dense, interlocking lines of oath-script sinking into the arm like ink into water, spreading upward toward the shoulder, toward the heart, toward whatever passed for a mind in there.

The great seal. The binding laid centuries ago beneath the capital by a circle of witches. It wasn't holding the oath-eater *down* anymore. It was holding it *together*—and coming apart.

"You see," the entity said quietly. "Your predecessor's circle built well. Too well. Their seal is woven into the coronation oath itself; I cannot remove it without unmaking the throne, and I cannot feed properly while it frays, and every year it frays faster." The eyes lifted to hers, and for one unguarded instant there was nothing regal in them at all—only vast, ravening want, the want of a starving animal dressed in state robes. "I need the last seal-bearer. The boy carries a living fragment of the binding. His mind is a key cut to this lock. With him, I finish the dissolution cleanly. Without him—"

He spread the king's hands, eloquent.

"—the crown crumbles. And not gracefully. When the oath fails, everything sworn upon it fails. Every vow of fealty ever spoken to this throne unravels at once. Do you know what a kingdom looks like, physician, when ten thousand oaths let go in a single hour? I do. I have seen it. Twice."

*Twice.* The word dropped into Maren's stomach like a stone into a well, falling a long way before it landed.

"How long?" she asked. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "How long before it fails?"

The entity studied her. And then—it wanted her to play along, and it was wise enough to pay for cooperation—it answered.

"A season," it said. "Perhaps two. The Feast of the Oath comes in forty days; the whole court renews its vows, and the oath draws strength from that. It will buy me time. But the fraying accelerates. By midwinter, the first wardens' bonds will loosen. By spring, the lords'. I must complete the dissolution before the Feast, or spend the next century putting down fires in a kingdom of oathless men." A thin smile moved across the king's mouth. "So. Forty days, physician. Find me the boy within them."

Forty days. Maren turned it over, and under it, and through it, the way she turned symptoms. Because here was the thing the creature had not noticed—or had noticed and dismissed, because kings dismiss small people, and it was wearing a king now, and forgetting how to be anything else:

A deadline was a weapon. Deadlines could be missed. Deadlines could be *made* to be missed. Roads washed out. Stones stayed hidden. Boys stayed lost. She had spent four years learning exactly how slowly the machinery of the realm could turn when the right hands rested on the right wheels.

"I'll need resources," she said. "Access to the archives. Freedom of the lower levels. My instruments returned."

"Granted."

"And my boots."

The entity blinked—slowly, oddly, like a lizard. "Your boots?"

"You took them at the third gate. It was excessive."

For a moment something almost human crossed the king's face, and Maren understood, with a lurch, that some sliver of the actual man was still in there, buried deep, and that he might have found the boot demand funny. Then the moment drowned again in that vast patient want.

"They will be returned," it said. "You have until the Feast, physician. Do not mistake patience for mercy. If the boy is not delivered, I will begin feeding differently—wider, coarser. There are gifted folk hiding in every village of this kingdom, and my Hunt knows how to find them, and I no longer require them *whole*."

"There," Maren said, rising, "is the salesmanship I remember from court."

The guards escorted her up. At the third gate her boots were waiting, lined up neatly, laces tucked inside—which unsettled her more than any threat had. At the second gate they gave back her knives. At the first gate, a clerk pressed a token into her hand: bone-white, warm, humming faintly. Access to the archives. The keys to the kingdom's memory, handed to a disgraced woman by a thing that needed her to find one boy.

In her narrow cell that night—comfortable, they had said, and insultingly it was—Maren sat on the cot with her knees drawn up and did what she always did. She made a list.

One: the entity was starving, and starving things grew careless.

Two: the seal failed at midwinter unless renewed at the Feast, which meant the entity's true deadline was softer than it claimed. It needed the Feast's renewal as much as it feared the fraying. Which meant the Feast was not only its opportunity. It was its *dependency*.

Three: somewhere between here and the mountain passes, a stubborn apprentice with smuggler's roads in his boots was walking toward a fate both of them dreaded, and every day she delayed was a day he gained.

Four: she had until the Feast of the Oath to decide which side of her own nature she served—the physician who healed whoever was in front of her, or the woman who had already run from this court once with her conscience in her satchel.

There was a fifth item, the one she wrote smallest, in the corner of her mind where she kept truths too sharp to handle often:

Five: the king was still alive in there. Buried, starved, worn thin as vellum—but alive. And if the seal could be unbound rightly, by the right key, in the right hands, then the entity could be cast out without killing the man it wore. The entity had told her the dissolution could only be finished its way. But liars always described the lock as though they owned the only key.

She blew out the candle and lay down in the dark, listening to the keep breathe around her—the drip of water, the far-off clank of the changing watch, and beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat underground, that sweet rotting-honey smell seeping up through the stones.

Forty days.

Somewhere above her, in the dark of the royal apartments, the thing that wore the crown sat sleepless among its eaten minds—and did not know that its physician was already counting.

* * *

On the third morning, they brought her breakfast and, with the tray, a folded scrap of paper she was meant to believe had slipped in by accident. The guard who delivered it had flat eyes and a flat voice, and stood watching while she read it.

The handwriting was elegant, antique, precise:

*The fourth stone sleeps beneath the throne. Tell no one you know this. — S.*

Maren read it twice, burned it in the candle flame, and began, calmly, to plan how a disgraced physician might steal a kingdom back in thirty-seven days.
