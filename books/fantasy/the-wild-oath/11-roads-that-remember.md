## 11. Roads That Remember

The maps Sera had given him were works of art, which was a problem, because Theo could not read them.

Not the way she meant them to be read, anyway. They weren't drawn so much as *woven*—roads rendered as threads of silk stitched into cloth, mountains raised in little puckered knots, rivers in cool blue braid. Sera had run her fingers over the whole thing twice while explaining it, quick and sure, like a woman reading a letter from an old friend.

"The red thread is the old drover's road," she'd said. "It remembers where it goes even when the snow doesn't. When you reach the first oath-stone, you'll feel it before you see it. Like standing too close to a bell that hasn't been rung yet."

"And if I feel something worse than that?" Theo had asked.

"Then you'll know you're almost there," she'd said, and smiled, blind eyes aimed at some middle distance where he apparently lived now, a smudge of fate on her father's charts.

That had been six days ago. Since then: one burned bridge, two frozen nights, half a wheel of hard cheese, and a headache that had graduated from nuisance to resident squatter, pounding at the door of his skull day and night and refusing to be evicted.

He sat now in the mouth of a drover's cave above the Greywater ford, chewing the last of the cheese and consulting the map by touch, because his hands were steadier than his head and because looking at the thing for too long made the colors swim. His memories had started skipping. That was the honest word for it, though he hated the word. He would be walking along, thinking about nothing, and then—*skip*—he was somewhere else mid-stride, three hours later, with no accounting for the road between. Once he'd found himself humming a lullaby in a language he didn't know and couldn't stop humming for a mile.

His mistress had done this to him. He was trying very hard not to think that thought. He was failing.

"Remember nothing," he muttered, mimicking her cracked old voice, "forget nothing." He threw the cheese rind into the dark. "Well, which is it, you infuriating woman? You never did answer a question straight in your life."

The map, being cloth, offered no opinion. The mountain wind, which had opinions about everything, howled agreement anyway.

* * *

The drover's road was real. That much Sera had promised truly. It ran under the snow like a vein under skin, and Theo found it the way smugglers find everything—not by sight but by habit: a cairn shaped wrong, a fence post grown through rather than planted beside, the particular way pines leaned off a track that carts had worn into the world a hundred years before anyone drew a border there.

He walked it north by east, toward the notch in the ridgeline Sera called the Shepherd's Tooth, and the headache climbed with him, knot by knot, until by midafternoon he was stopping every hundred paces to press his palms against his eyes and breathe through his teeth.

And then he felt it.

Sera had said *a bell that hasn't been rung*. She was wrong, or her father's language had worn smooth with retelling, because what Theo felt was not anticipation. It was *recognition*. Something ahead of him knew him. Had always known him. Was waiting with the terrible patience of stone for him to arrive, the way a lock waits for its key, and the knowing slid down his spine like cold water and settled in his knees.

He nearly turned around.

He wants it noted—he wanted this recorded, wherever such things get recorded—that he genuinely considered it. There was a side valley. There was a low pass south that a determined man might cross in four days. There was a whole world full of places where no ancient buried thing was waiting to open him up like a letter.

But there was also Maren, in a warden wagon somewhere behind him with iron on her wrists, taken because she'd stood between him and a bounty-taker's knife. There was Ilsa's face, pale with guilt, turning away from him in Gallow's Rest. And there was the small, humiliating truth that whatever was eating him from the inside was going to finish the job whether he faced it or not.

"All right," he told the mountain. "All right. But if you kill me, I want it known I complained the entire time."

The mountain, being a mountain, declined to negotiate.

* * *

The first oath-stone stood in a hollow below the Shepherd's Tooth, in a ring of rowan trees that should not have survived this altitude and plainly knew it and were showing off. Snow lay everywhere except in a perfect circle around the stone, ten feet across, bare brown earth scattered with rowan berries like spilled garnets. The stone itself was taller than a man, black, unmarked—except that when Theo looked at it directly, marks swam up from inside it like fish rising to bread, glyphs and knots and spirals, and when he looked away they sank again, shy.

His hands were shaking. He noticed this with detached interest, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

"Right," he said. "Remember nothing. Forget nothing."

He touched the stone.

* * *

The world went out like a snuffed candle, and another world came in.

He was nine years old, kneeling on a kitchen floor he knew better than his own heartbeat, scrubbing a cauldron while his mistress scolded him for salting the tincture instead of the stew.

"You'll poison a duke someday, boy, mark me."

"Why would I ever cook for a duke?"

"You'll do it out of spite. It runs in your line." But she said it softly, and her hand came down on his hair, heavy and warm, and stayed there longer than scolding required.

The memory let him go gently, set him back in the snow like a mother setting down a sleeping child—and then took him again before he'd finished gasping:

He was fourteen, caught in a whiteout on the high pass, and she'd wrapped him in her own cloak and walked behind him all night singing, badly, keeping him moving, keeping him warm, and in the morning she denied the whole thing. *Cloak? What cloak? You've been wearing that cloak for years, boy, are you simple?*

Skip—

He was sixteen, and a crown rider had come to Hollowmere asking after hedge-witches, and she'd fed him stew and lies with equal warmth and sent him off with a charm against rheumatism, and only afterward did Theo see her hands shaking as she banked the fire. Only afterward did she say, quietly, to the hearth more than to him: "They're getting closer every year. The net's drawing in."

Skip—

And then the last night. Her deathbed, or near enough—the narrow room, the smell of yarrow and tallow, her hand like a bird's skull in his.

"Theo," she said. "Theo Dunmore. Say it."

"Theo Dunmore," he said, laughing because she frightened him. "What is this?"

"I'm going to do something to you, and you're going to hate me, and you're going to be right to hate me." Her eyes were bright and terrible and wet. "There's a thing in the capital, child. An old thing. Old as oaths. It eats minds like yours—gifted minds, bright ones—it eats them and wears what's left. And it's hunting for what's left of the seal, the great seal, the circle that bound it under the city before either of us was born. I am one of the circle's heirs. The last who can still work the craft. And I am dying, and the seal needs a bearer, and I—" her voice broke, and reassembled itself out of sheer stubbornness, "—I have chosen you, and I am sorry, and I will pay for it in whatever comes after, gladly."

"Mistress—"

"Hold still."

She put her hands on either side of his face and she *took*. Not his gift—she left him that, cruelly, mercifully—but the knowledge of it, the shape of it, the memory of every working he'd ever done, every lesson, every night of wonder, folded away into a room in his mind and locked, and locked again, and the lock was made of her, of her love and her guilt braided together into something stronger than iron, and it would burn her own life to keep shut, and it was burning it, and she knew—

"Forget nothing," she whispered, forehead pressed to his, tears on both their faces now. "Because I won't forget. Someone has to remember what I've done to you. Remember nothing, so it can't find you through your own mind. Forget nothing, because you deserved better than to lose it. Both at once. I know. I'm sorry. Forgive me or don't, but live."

*Live,* she said, and the memory ended, and Theo was on his knees in the ring of bare earth, screaming, or maybe just breathing very loudly; he couldn't tell the difference.

* * *

He didn't know how long he knelt there. The light had changed. The rowans had dropped berries on his shoulders like a benediction or a mockery.

Anger came first. Anger was easy, anger fit his hands well, he'd carried anger since Hollowmere since the lanterns since the proclamation nailed to the post like a knife through the village's heart. *She chose me.* Sixteen years of her roof and her lessons and her terrible cooking, and she'd spent him like coin. She'd looked at a boy scrubbing her cauldron and seen armor. Seen a shield for a kingdom that had never once sheltered either of them.

"You could have asked," he said aloud, to her, to the stone, to nobody. His voice came out wrecked. "One question. *Would you?* That's all. One question, and I'd have—I'd have said yes, you old horror, you must have known I'd have said yes—"

And there it was. The crack in the anger, right down the middle, exactly where she'd predicted.

Because he would have. That was the unforgivable part, the part that dissolved the fury faster than it could burn: she hadn't needed to ask because the answer was already written in him. She'd raised him. She'd known. And she'd still apologized, still wept, still damned herself rather than pretend it cost her nothing—when pretending was the one magic she'd never had any talent for.

She loved him enough to damn him. He turned the thought over and over, looking for the trap in it, and there wasn't one. There was just an old woman in a narrow room spending her last strength building a cage around the boy she loved, and weeping while she built it, and locking herself in with him.

The headache had stopped. That was the strange thing. For the first time in months the space behind his eyes was quiet, and in the quiet he could feel the sealed room in his mind—not smaller, but *steadier*, as if the stone had set a shoulder under the load. As if the binding recognized where it had come from, and rested.

"Forgive me or don't," she'd said.

"Don't," Theo told the stone, wiping his face with a sleeve already frozen stiff. "Not yet. Ask me again at the second one. Maybe by then I'll have worked up to it."

The stone said nothing. But the circle of rowans rustled, though there was no wind inside the hollow, and he chose—with the deliberate, self-aware foolishness of a man who has just learned his whole life was someone else's plan—to take that as agreement.

* * *

He camped that night in the hollow, which was warmer than it had any right to be, and slept without skipping once. In the morning Sera's map showed him what he'd missed before: stitched faintly into the cloth beneath the Shepherd's Tooth, a second thread, darker than the rest, running south by west toward the low country. Toward the second stone.

Toward, if Ilsa's dying confession meant anything, the teeth of the hunt.

He packed the cheese rind out of habit, found it, and ate it anyway on principle. Then he stood at the edge of the hollow, looking back once at the black stone standing patient among its impossible rowans.

"Two more," he said. "And then we settle accounts, all of us. You, me, her, and whatever's wearing the king."

The road remembered where it went. He followed it down out of the mountains, and far to the south, where the lowlands smoked with woodsmoke that was not hearth-smoke, something old turned its attention toward the foothills—and began, patiently, hungrily, to wait.
