## 12. The Second Stone

The second oath-stone stood in a hollow that no honest map admitted to, in the crook of the Greywater fork where the river went quiet and sullen under ice. Theo found it the way Sera's map had promised: not by looking for it, but by looking for what *didn't* belong. A ring of alder trees too old for the soil they grew in. A silence with edges to it, like a held breath. And at the center, a stone the size of a sleeping ox, black-veined, wearing snow that had never quite decided to settle on it.

He'd been walking three days since the first stone gave back his mistress's voice—*I'm sorry, little wren, I loved you enough to damn you*—and he'd spent those three days doing something he was not good at: nothing. Sitting with it. Turning it over like a river stone until the sharp edges wore down to something he could carry.

It hadn't fully worked. He suspected it wouldn't fully work ever again.

"Right," he said aloud, because talking to stones was apparently who he was now. "You're the one with answers, then. The last one only had apologies."

The stone said nothing, which he'd expected, but its cold reached out and pressed against the binding behind his eyes all the same—a pressure like a thumb finding a bruise, familiar now, almost companionable. His sealed memories stirred beneath their cage, restless as mice under floorboards.

He knelt in the snow and laid his bare palm against it.

The world tilted.

*—a kitchen, flour on his hands, Ilsa laughing at him because he'd salted the honeycakes—*

*Ilsa.*

*—her voice through the dormer wall, past midnight: "If they ever come for us, Theo, promise me you'll run. Don't be brave. Brave people are the ones they burn—"*

He came back to himself gasping, hand frozen to the stone, tears freezing in the creases of his face. Not grief this time. Worse than grief. It was a *name*, arriving whole, dragging years of it behind it—Ilsa Vane, fellow apprentice, sharper-tongued than any blade in Mistress Aldery's cupboard, the girl who'd once convinced an entire village that the miller's cat could predict weather so she wouldn't have to pay for bread.

She'd sold him. He knew that the way he knew his own hands. In Gallow's Rest, in the market of small magics, when he'd bought the memory-anchor from the fence—she'd been there. She'd seen him. And the crown's silver had done what old friendship couldn't.

The stone pulsed under his palm, patient, expectant. *Touch me again,* it seemed to say. *There is more where that came from.*

"Not yet," Theo muttered, prying his fingers free. "Let me have a moment to hate someone properly first."

But the moment wouldn't come. That was the trouble with memory—it didn't serve itself up in tidy portions. Every recollection of Ilsa came bundled with warmth: her stealing the good quill and blaming the ravens; her sitting with him the whole night after his mother's execution, not saying anything, just being a small furious presence beside him; her terrible singing, which she inflicted on everyone equally, as a form of justice.

And underneath all of it, the thing he'd missed until now, the thing the first stone had pried loose without him understanding: the night Mistress Aldery bound him, Ilsa had been *there*. At the edge of the circle. Crying. Begging the old woman to choose her instead.

Theo sat down hard in the snow.

"Oh," he said quietly. "Oh, you stupid, brave, *awful* woman."

The hounds began baying a quarter hour later.

* * *

They came down the eastern slope in a wedge, six wardens in grey-and-crimson, and two gift-hounds ahead of them—lean, wrong-eyed things bred to smell the gift like sharks smelled blood. Theo heard them before he saw them, because the mountain told him. Three years of smuggler's roads had taught his body a language his mind had forgotten how to read: the particular quality of birdsong when birds were fleeing uphill, the way snowmelt under the ice changed its tune when boots crossed the ridge above.

He did the arithmetic he always did. Six wardens, two hounds, open ground, one half-trained hedge-witch running on a magic budget that would embarrass a church mouse. The answer was the mine.

Old Corveth's Delve had honeycombed this hillside a century ago and been abandoned when the vein ran out—or so the story went. Theo knew better. The smugglers' guild had kept the lower galleries open for moving goods the crown preferred not to see, and more importantly for moving *people*, and the entrance was forty yards south, behind a curtain of dead bramble that looked like every other curtain of dead bramble on the mountain.

He was ten steps toward it when the crossbow bolt took the bramble beside his head.

"Seal-bearer!" The shout rolled down the slope. "By order of the crown, stand and be taken! Your cooperation will be noted!"

"Will it?" Theo shouted back, already running. "In writing?"

He heard laughter among the wardens—they always laughed; fear needed somewhere to go—and then the hounds' voices dropped into that register that made his spine try to climb out of his body. Not baying anymore. Hunting.

The bramble curtain tore at his coat like an old woman scolding. Behind it, the mine mouth gaped, breathing cold air that smelled of iron and deep stone. He plunged in, counted his steps the way he'd been taught—seven, twelve, nineteen—and put his shoulder to the timber brace that opened the smuggler's gallery. The counterweight groaned. The wall of rubble behind it shivered.

Then the light behind him changed, and he turned, and Ilsa Vane stepped into the mine mouth with a drawn crossbow and a face like winter coming early.

"Ilsa," Theo said. Stupidly. As though they might discuss the weather.

"Theo." Her voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone holding a door shut from inside. "Don't run. Please. They want you alive—that's the only reason you're still breathing. Walk out with me and I swear on whatever you've got left to swear on, I'll get you to the capital in one piece."

"In one piece," Theo repeated. "That's generous. Last time anyone went to the capital in one piece, they came back as ash on the wind."

"You think I don't know that?" For just a moment the flatness cracked, and underneath it was the girl from the dormer, seventeen years old and furious at a world that burned people like his mother. "You think I sleep? You think the silver spends well? Theo, they had my sister's name. Do you understand? They had her name and her village and a warrant with her description on it, and a man with kind eyes explained to me exactly what happens to families of unregistered gift-bearers if I declined to cooperate."

Theo's throat worked. "So you sold me."

"So I sold your *location*." She flinched at her own words. "That's what I've been telling myself for eleven days. Location isn't the man. Location isn't the memories. I wrote it down like a sum that balances, and it doesn't balance, Theo, it has never balanced—"

Behind her, upslope, the hounds screamed.

And then the screaming stopped.

That was worse. Hounds that stopped mid-cry were hounds that had found something, or met something. Ilsa spun toward the daylight, crossbow up, and Theo saw the moment her training caught up with her instincts—the moment she realized that six wardens should not go silent all at once, that silence in the Thornfell passes meant rockslide or warg or—

The first warden came through the mine mouth backwards, which is a difficult way for anyone to travel, and landed very still.

What followed, Theo would spend years trying to describe and never quite manage. The gallery lit with grey-green light—not fire, nothing so honest as fire—but a crawling luminescence that poured down the tunnel like water finding its level. Wardens shouted. Crossbows fired into glow. And in the middle of it all moved a figure Theo's sealed memories recognized before his waking mind did: tall, robed in road-dust, moving with the precise economy of someone who had learned surgery and unlearned mercy in the same year.

Maren. Except Maren had gone north to the third stone's approaches to scout a safe road for Sera. Maren was two days away.

Except here she was, and her hands were full of light, and the light was killing men.

"*Run!*" she snarled at him, and it was definitely Maren—no mistaking that voice, the voice that had told him three separate times that his survival instincts were "medically interesting." "*The shaft, Theo, DOWN—*"

He ran. So did Ilsa—which surprised him, surprised him so much he nearly stopped—but she ran *with* him, away from her own handlers, down the smuggler's gallery into the dark, and behind them the green light bloomed once, twice, and guttered.

The secondary shaft was where the maps said it was, because Sera's father had charted these hills the way other men charted coastlines, and his daughter remembered every line. Theo hit the ladder, dropped eight feet, ten, fifteen, and came up in the lower gallery with his lungs full of stone dust and his heart going like a drum in a parade.

Ilsa landed beside him. Her crossbow clattered on the rock.

For a moment they just breathed at each other in the dark.

"You're the informant," Theo said.

"You're the seal-bearer," she said.

"Yes. Well. One of us has an excuse."

Something happened to her face then—not a smile, nothing so simple, but the ghost of the expression that used to precede her worst ideas. "You always did joke when you were terrified."

"You always did notice. Then sell tickets."

"That's not fair."

"No," Theo agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. You had a sister. I had a mistress who died for me and a mother who burned for me, and the crown has a hunt, and none of it balances. I know about sums that don't balance, Ilsa. I'm carrying the biggest one ever written."

Footsteps above. Maren, descending fast, breathing hard, a smear of blood across her cheek that Theo prayed belonged to someone else. She took in the scene—Theo, the stranger, the dropped crossbow—with the rapid triage glance he'd come to know.

"This is Ilsa," Theo said. "My—"

"We're settling accounts later," Maren said. "Both of you. There's a warden sergeant still alive up there and he'll have runners out by now. This gallery floods in spring but it's January, so our exit is the flooded adit, and I need everyone moving before I change my mind about how many people I just killed for."

They moved. And that was when everything went wrong at once, the way things do—as if calamity, like water, simply waits for the lowest point.

* * *

The flooded adit was a lie of a name; in January it was shin-deep and viciously cold, a hundred yards of black water over black stone, with the roof dipping low enough in places that they waded bent double. Theo went first—he knew these galleries, or his body did, remembering routes his mind had surrendered. Twenty yards in, thirty, the dark pressing close and the binding behind his eyes pulsing with each step like a second heart.

At forty yards, Ilsa made a sound.

Not a cry. Ilsa Vane had never cried in her life; she'd once set her own broken finger rather than visit the physician's college and admit to weakness. The sound she made was smaller than a cry—a soft expulsion of breath, a question mark made of pain.

Theo turned. The grey-green light Maren carried showed him everything at once, the way disaster prefers: Ilsa upright in the water, one hand pressed high against her ribs, and between her fingers a bloom of red that the light turned a terrible, glowing green.

"Bolt," she said, almost conversational. "From the sergeant. Up top. I thought—" She frowned, as if genuinely working it out. "I thought it had gone wide. It didn't go wide."

"It went wide," Theo said. "It went wide, Ilsa, we just have to—"

"Wren." She used his old name, the one only she and the dead were allowed. "Be quiet a moment. Let me do sums."

Maren was already there, hands replacing Ilsa's own, and Theo watched the physician's face do the thing faces do when they read a verdict they can't rewrite. She didn't say anything. She was a good enough liar to fool a king, but her hands shook, and Ilsa felt it, and smiled.

"Don't waste the craft on me," Ilsa said. "I've seen your stores. You've got maybe three workings left in you and a king to unbind. Spend them there."

"Shut up," said Theo.

"See, this is why I sold your location. Insufferable."

"*Shut up.*" His voice cracked on it. The binding flared behind his eyes, and for one vertiginous moment he felt every sealed memory strain toward her at once—all the years of her, the salted honeycakes and the stolen quill and the night she'd begged to take his place—pressing against the cage like a crowd against a locked gate.

They got her onto a dry ledge where the gallery rose above the waterline, an old ore-loading platform, and Maren did what she could, which was warmth and pressure and a tincture for pain, and Theo held Ilsa's hand, which was already colder than the flood water.

"Theo." Her eyes found him in the dimness, and they were clear—terribly clear, the clarity of someone past the reach of panic. "Listen to me. I need to tell you what I know, and I need you to believe it, because I paid for it in the worst currency there is and I won't have it wasted."

"Ilsa—"

"*Listen.*" A bubble of blood at her lip, wiped away with the back of her wrist, briskly, like brushing off crumbs. "The informant network. All of us—me, the fence in Gallow's Rest, the harbormaster at Greywater, the tithe-collector, the lot. We thought we were hunters. We thought we were finding the gifted for the crown's registers." Her breath hitched; she rode through it. "We're not hunters. We're *nerves*. Every report we file goes to the Office of the Hunt, yes—but the Office reports to the Lord Chamberlain, and the Chamberlain doesn't sleep, and his clerks copy everything into a ledger bound in white leather, and I saw it once, Theo. I saw the ledger. It's not records. It's *him*. The thing in the king. Every gifted soul we find, every seal-signature, every scrap of the old circle's bloodline—it reads us like we're pages, and it moves the hunt accordingly."

Theo felt the world tilt again, worse than the stone's touch. "Moves it how?"

"Toward you." Her grip tightened, surprising strength in it. "Why do you think the wardens were on this hill today, of all hills? Why do you think the bounty notices started appearing along exactly the roads Sera's maps follow? The hunt was never about finding the gifted, you idiot. The gifted are chaff. It's about *you*—the seal-bearers, the heirs of the circle. It can't find the stones themselves; they're hidden inside old oaths it can't digest. But it can herd the people who carry pieces of the seal. Drive you, road by road, ambush by ambush, always toward the capital." Her laugh was thin and awful. "Every warden chasing you, every informant selling you, every kindness and betrayal on every road—we're all just sheepdogs, Theo. And the flock is you."

The gallery was very quiet. Somewhere far off, water dripped, counting seconds.

"They wanted me to bring you in myself," Ilsa whispered. "Special commission. Kind-eyed man said I'd be paid double, and my sister's warrant would burn. I said yes. I walked up that hill meaning to do it." Her eyes were shining now, and she let them. "And then I saw you kneeling at the stone, and you were crying, and I remembered the dormer, and I thought: Ilsa Vane, you have priced everything your whole life, and this is the one sum that comes due today."

"Ilsa." His voice was wrecked beyond salvage. "Ilsa, wait—"

"Tell my sister—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "No. Don't tell her anything. Let her think I died a stranger. It's kinder." Another bubble of red, wiped away. "Wren. The fourth stone. When you find out where it is—you'll find out, the stones will show you—remember that the road to it was built by sheepdogs. Every step. Make the last step count for something."

Her hand went slack in his by degrees, the way evening does, so slowly that he couldn't name the moment it became different.

Maren closed her eyes. Theo didn't. He owed her that much—to watch, to witness, to be the one who stayed while the light went out of the sharpest, bravest, most infuriating person he had ever known.

Afterward, they buried her above the waterline, in a niche in the gallery wall, with a cairn of loose stone because there was no earth and no time. Theo marked it the only way he could: a charm of warmth, woven weak and small, set into the topmost rock so that anyone sheltering there in the cold would feel, without knowing why, that this spot was kindly. It was the least he could do and the most. It took nearly everything he had left.

Maren stood over the cairn a long moment. "I killed six men today," she said quietly, "to save a stranger. And she killed herself, in the end, to save the same stranger. There's an arithmetic in this business that I don't care for."

"She'd have liked you," Theo said.

"I know," said Maren. "That's what worries me."

They waded out through the flooded adit into grey afternoon light, and the mountain received them without comment, and Theo walked with a cairn behind him and a truth ahead of him that rearranged everything: there had never been anywhere to hide. Not the passes, not the free-towns, not the smallest smuggler's track. The whole kingdom was a net, and every thread of it led inward, to a throne, to a white-leather ledger, to a hungry thing wearing a king.

Which meant, he understood as the pass opened before them, that the only direction left was the one the net was built to drive him.

Forward. To the capital. To the fourth stone, wherever it slept.

He touched the memory-anchor charm at his throat—his mother's face, bought back at the price of another—and began, deliberately, to walk the road they had built for him.

Behind them, far up the hillside, the warmth-charm on Ilsa's cairn glowed faintly against the gathering snow, a small stubborn light in a kingdom of wolves.

And ahead, on the southern horizon, the spires of the capital caught the last sun and gleamed like teeth.
