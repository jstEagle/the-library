## 13. What Wears the King

Maren had been a liar for eleven days now, and she was getting better at it, which frightened her more than anything else in the keep.

She had expected the dungeons. She had expected, in her darker imaginings, the block. What she had not expected was to be given back her physician's satchel, assigned a chamber two doors from the king's own, and greeted by name by servants who seemed genuinely pleased that "Doctor Vess had come home." The entity wearing the crown had decided she was useful, and usefulness, Maren was learning, was its own species of cage—gilded, comfortable, and locked from the outside.

"You look terrible, Doctor," said Tam, the young under-steward who brought her breakfast and opinions in equal measure.

"I'm honored you noticed. Most people are too polite."

"Oh, I'm never polite before noon." He set down the tray of tea she would not drink—she had taken to pouring it into the ornamental fern, which was thriving in a way no fern should—and lowered his voice. "Is it true what they're saying? That you were dismissed from the college for poisoning a duke?"

"I refused to poison a duke. It was considered unprofessional."

Tam grinned and went off to spread the correction, which would probably be worse. Maren drank water from her own flask, checked the little wax tablet hidden beneath her mattress where she recorded her observations in physician's shorthand no one at court could read, and prepared for morning rounds with the king.

The routine was always the same. Two guards at the door, both of them wrong in small ways—their breathing too even, their eyes too still, like men who had forgotten blinking was optional. The king seated at his window, watching the capital with an expression of mild appetite. And the greeting.

"Good morning, Doctor Vess," the king would say. "How is your mother?"

Her mother had been dead nine years. Every morning it asked. Every morning she answered something different, and every morning she watched to see whether it remembered the previous answer. It never did. It wasn't collecting facts; it was collecting *her*—the texture of her lies, the flavor of her fear, the particular way grief sat in a person's voice. Feeding, in tiny spoonfuls, the way a gourmand samples a dish before committing to the whole plate.

"She's well," Maren said this morning. "She sends her love."

"How kind," said the thing behind the king's eyes, and smiled with the king's mouth, which was the worst part. It used his face well. Too well. A living man's face has friction in it—small hesitations, asymmetries, the honest clumsiness of muscle and mood. This face moved like silk drawn over a hand.

"Shall we begin?" she asked, opening her satchel. Her hands were steady. Eleven days of practice.

"We shall."

* * *

The examinations were theater, and both performers knew it. She listened to a heart that beat exactly sixty times a minute, waking or sleeping, joy or terror—a metronome where a man should be. She took blood that came out dark as old wine and clotted into shapes that made her want to burn her notes. She asked questions about sleep, appetite, dreams, and received answers polished smooth as river stones.

But underneath the theater, Maren was doing what she did best: mapping. Not the body—the body was a coat rack. She was mapping the *architecture*, and after eleven days she had begun to understand what she was looking at, and understanding had made everything worse.

It started with the oath itself.

Every crowned sovereign of Thornfell swore the Coronation Oath at the high altar: *By root and crown, by stone and star, I bind my breath to Thornfell's keeping.* Everyone knew the words. What no one outside the old colleges knew—and what Maren knew because she had once, foolishly, proudly, been trusted with the college's sealed archives—was that the Oath was not words. It was a working. The founding witches had built it as a vessel, a covenant-shaped container meant to hold the sovereign's life in trust for the kingdom's sake. The king's breath literally belonged to the land. That was why no trueborn monarch of Thornfell could be assassinated by ordinary means: the Oath held them like a fist holds a bird.

And something had gotten inside the fist.

She had confirmed it on the fourth day, during a bleed-and-balance rite that let her read the deep currents of a patient's vital hum. Beneath the metronome heart she had found the Oath's structure—root and crown, stone and star, four great anchors of power running through the king like the frame of a house. And wrapped around all four anchors, fused to them so thoroughly that she could not find the seam, was the entity. It hadn't possessed the king the way a squatter possesses a house. It had grown into the load-bearing walls.

Which meant that tearing it out might bring down the whole structure.

She had spent three nights with that problem, pacing her chamber, eating nothing, drinking tea she didn't pour into the fern. Because there was a corollary, and the corollary was obscene: if the oath-eater died while fused to the anchors, the Oath might collapse entirely—and the Oath, she suspected, was doing more than holding one man. The stones in the capital's foundations hummed with it. The ward-lines that kept certain older, hungrier things out of the lowlands drew their strength from the coronation vessel. Kill the parasite carelessly, and you didn't free the kingdom. You left the door open, the locks broken, and posted a sign reading *vacant*.

*Or worse,* she wrote on her wax tablet, pressing hard enough to snap the stylus, *you leave the throne itself empty, and an empty throne is an invitation.*

There had to be a counter-oath. There had to be a way to fill the vessel even as the parasite was evicted—something poured in behind the blade so the walls didn't cave. Theo's stolen memories. The seal he carried. She thought about the boy daily, wondered where he was, whether he was alive, whether he hated her for leaving him on the road with half a truth. Probably. She'd have hated her too.

She was composing her fifteenth mental letter of apology when the summons came.

* * *

"The king wishes to observe a healing," Tam told her, wringing his cap. He looked ill. "Down in the undercroft. He asks—Doctor, he asks specifically for *your* craft. Says the others make the patient worse."

"What patient?"

"A child." Tam's voice cracked on the word. "A girl. They pulled her off a caravan two days ago. Gift-bearing."

Maren felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does when a ship changes course toward rocks. "And the court physicians have been... attending her?"

"Nobody will go near her anymore." Tam wouldn't meet her eyes. "They say she's—" He stopped, swallowed. "You'll see."

She saw.

* * *

The undercroft of the royal keep was a vaulted space of black granite, older than the keep above it, older, Maren suspected, than the city. The founding witches' work was everywhere here—in the anchor-stones set into the pillars, in the geometry of the arches, in the faint hum she felt in her teeth. Someone had hung lamps. Lamps, for a dungeon, as though decoration could disguise function.

The girl lay on a stone table in the center of the room. Eight years old, maybe nine. Brown hair cut short and raggedly, the way soldiers cut it. Her wrists were bound with silk—silk, Maren noted, because silk conducted the gift's current without burning it, someone here understood the mechanics—and she was awake, and she was looking at Maren with the flat, calculating gaze of an animal that has already priced its odds.

"Hello," Maren said gently, crossing to her. "I'm a doctor. My name is Vess."

"They said you'd come," the girl whispered. "The tall one with the nice voice. He said a real doctor would come, and then I'd feel better, and then I could go home."

Maren's stomach dropped through the floor. She turned.

The king stood at the edge of the lamplight with two of the still-eyed guards, and he was smiling, and it was the worst smile she had yet seen on that borrowed face—not the silken court smile but something warmer, grandfatherlier, a smile designed by something that had studied human kindness the way a forger studies signatures.

"Doctor Vess. Thank you for coming." He gestured at the girl with genuine-seeming tenderness. "This poor mite burns with fever. Her gift is raw, undisciplined—it's consuming her. You've seen it before, I'm sure, in your travels. The hedge-talents, the untrained ones. They gutter like bad candles." He shook the king's head sadly. "I only wish to help her. To *settle* her."

Every instinct Maren owned was screaming. She made herself walk forward, made herself kneel by the table, made herself take the girl's pulse—racing, thready, terrified—and made herself say, in a level professional voice:

"May I examine her properly first?"

"Of course. Thoroughness is your reputation." The smile widened a fraction. "Take whatever time you need. We have such an abundance of time, you and I."

The girl's skin was clammy. Her pupils were blown. And around her, in the air, Maren could see—with her physician's sight, the sight that had gotten her disgraced and nearly hanged—the girl's gift flickering like a guttering flame, being *drawn* somewhere. Downward. Into the stone beneath the table.

Into the anchor-stone.

"No," Maren breathed, before she could stop herself.

"Hm?" The king tilted his head, attentive, benign.

"Fever patterns," she covered, quickly. "I need to check her against the cold stone. Standard practice." She pressed her palm flat to the granite beside the girl and felt it: a current, slow and rhythmic as digestion, flowing from the child into the stone, up through the foundations, into the vessel wearing the crown above them.

It wasn't going to heal the girl. It was going to eat her. Slowly, luxuriously, the way it ate everyone—except that the courtiers and servants it fed on gave up scraps, dregs, the foam of a mind. A gifted mind was a feast. A child's gifted mind was—

The girl grabbed Maren's wrist. Small fingers, iron grip.

"He lied," the girl whispered. "He said my mama sent me to a school. There's no school, is there."

Maren looked at her. Nine years old, bound in silk, lying in a witch-built vault being drained drop by drop, and asking the question with the clear-eyed pragmatism of the very poor, who learn early that institutions lie.

"No," Maren said quietly. "There's no school."

The girl nodded, unsurprised, and Maren felt something in her chest tear cleanly down the middle.

She stood. She turned to face the king. And for one long moment she stood at the exact edge of it—the edge she had walked every day for eleven days, the line between useful cowardice and useless courage. If she spoke now, denounced it, threw her satchel at his head, she would die in this room, and the girl would still be eaten, and Theo would never learn what she'd found, and the kingdom would burn with nobody holding the map. Useless courage. The most expensive kind.

The entity watched her decide. She could see it watching—could see, behind the king's patient eyes, the attention sharpening like a cat tracking a bird's flight path, waiting to see if she'd flush from cover.

"Your Majesty," Maren heard herself say, "the girl's condition requires sustained treatment. Daily attendance. I'll take responsibility for her care personally."

A pause. Then the warm smile again, satisfied. "I hoped you would. Compassion is so rare in your profession." He turned to go, then paused, as if remembering something charming. "Oh—one more thing. The girl's name. Have you asked her? Patients do better when we know their names."

"It's Pip," the girl said. "Pip Wrenfield. From Marsh Hollow."

"Pip Wrenfield, from Marsh Hollow," the king repeated softly, tasting it. "Lovely. Names are such nourishing things, don't you find, Doctor?"

He left. The lamps guttered in his wake, all together, like a held breath released.

* * *

Maren worked on Pip for two hours with every trick she had—cooling compresses, steadying draughts, a minor shielding rite disguised as a fever protocol. She could not stop the drain. The current ran through silk and stone and foundation, and cutting it would require breaking the binding openly, which would announce her betrayal to a creature that had just demonstrated how patiently it watched.

What she could do was slow it. She anchored the girl's gift to a knotwork charm from her satchel—a cheap traveler's ward, the sort sold at fairs—and buried the charm in the girl's bedding. It would act as a false bottom to the well. The entity would drink and drink and get mostly dross, and grow impatient, and impatience, Maren gambled, might mean haste, and haste meant mistakes.

"You're going to get me out of here," Pip said. It wasn't a question. Children like Pip didn't ask questions they couldn't afford the answer to.

"I'm going to try."

"My mama's waiting at the crossroads inn. She told me if anyone asks, I'm from Marsh Hollow, and my name's Pip, and I'm eight." A pause. "I'm actually seven. But she says eight sounds more capable."

Despite everything, Maren laughed—a wet, cracked sound. "Your mother sounds like a very capable woman herself."

"She's smarter than the king," Pip said matter-of-factly. "That one's not a real person, you know."

Maren went very still. "What do you mean?"

The girl shrugged, seven years old and ancient. "When it looks at me, there's nothing behind it. Like a window with the shutters painted on. My gran could see auras and she taught me the trick of it before—" Pip stopped, and for the first time since arriving, the flat pragmatism wavered, and underneath it was a seven-year-old far from home. "Before the riders came."

Maren took the small hand. "Listen to me, Pip Wrenfield, aged eight, of Marsh Hollow. I have a friend coming. He's stubborn and reckless and he knows these roads better than the roads know themselves, and when he arrives, things are going to move very fast. When they do, you stay close to me and you do exactly what I say, even if it seems mad. Especially then. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Pip considered. "Is your friend also not a real person? Because honestly, at this point—"

"He's painfully real. It's his worst quality."

"Good," said Pip, with the solemnity of a judge. "Real people can be trusted to be scared. It's the ones that aren't scared you have to watch."

Maren finished her rounds, logged her notes, poured out her tea, and stood a long time at her window looking down at the city, where the Feast of the Oath preparations were underway—banners going up, ovens firing, the whole capital polishing itself for a ceremony she now understood was nothing of the kind. A feeding. A great public feeding, with the entire realm as the table.

The entity needed the Feast, she realized suddenly. Needed it the way a starving man needs market day. Its hunger was failing—she'd seen it in the readings, in the thinning of the currents, in the fact that it had resorted to draining children in cellars. Three years without true rest, three years of eating scraps, and now it had scheduled itself a banquet and invited a kingdom.

Which meant the clock was ticking, and it was ticking fast.

She pulled out her wax tablet, smoothed the surface, and wrote the message she would entrust to the laundress who smuggled letters for extra coin—the message she prayed would reach a boy with headaches and a smuggler's stride before the feast-day bells rang.

*The seal must come to the fourth stone before the Feast. The vessel cannot stand empty. Come soon or not at all — and if not at all, forgive me.*

Below it, after a moment, she added the only other thing that mattered:

*Tell him his mistress loved him. Tell him she knew.*

Then she blew out her lamp, and in the dark she let herself, briefly, privately, be afraid—because tomorrow she would put Pip's supper in front of her, and smile, and lie some more, and hope that somewhere on a frozen road, a runaway apprentice was moving fast.
