## 14. The Circle's Last Heirs

The third oath-stone stood at the crown of a hill that had no name on any of Sera's maps, which was how Theo knew it was the right one.

"Everything worth hiding gets left off the maps," Sera said, when he'd pointed this out to her. She walked beside him with a white hazel staff tapping the frozen ground ahead of her, reading the world through it the way other people read it through their eyes. "My father used to say a map is just an argument about where things are. The stones don't argue. They simply refuse to be found by anyone who isn't already lost."

"Well," Theo said, "I'm thoroughly qualified."

He was more than lost. He was frayed. The skipping had gotten worse since the mine — three days now, and twice in that time he'd come back to himself standing somewhere with no idea how long he'd been gone, his boots muddy, his fingers cold, whole minutes missing like pages torn from a ledger. Once he'd found himself humming a lullaby he didn't know and couldn't stop. Maren would have called it a symptom. Maren wasn't there; she was in a warden's cell beneath the capital, and every hour he spent climbing hills instead of riding toward her was an hour he'd have to answer for.

But the mistress's instructions had been clear as mountain water: *the first stone to remember me, the second to remember you, the third to remember us all.* And so they climbed.

The hilltop opened like a held breath. Wind-scoured grass, brown-gold even under frost, bent in slow waves around a ring of nine smaller stones — each no taller than a man's knee — encircling one great pillar of grey rock that rose out of the earth like a tooth. Snow wouldn't settle on any of them. He noticed that only after noticing the quiet: no birds, no insects, not even wind at the top, though the slopes below were loud with it.

Sera stopped at the edge of the ring. Her staff went still.

"You feel it," she said. It wasn't a question.

"It feels like being *watched*," Theo said. "By someone very patient."

"My father charted eleven of these before they took his sight for the trouble." Sera's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "He said the old circle didn't build wards. They built *judgments*. Go on, then. I'll wait here."

"Aren't you coming?"

"I'm a mapmaker's daughter, not an heir." She turned her face toward him — those cloud-grey eyes that saw nothing and somehow saw everything. "Whatever's in there will know the difference. Best not to insult it."

Theo stepped into the ring.

* * *

The moment his boot crossed between two of the small stones, the world changed key.

It was subtle at first — the light going amber, thick as honey, the frost on the grass glittering without melting. Then the great pillar breathed. Not cracked, not shifted — *breathed*, once, slowly, like something waking from a sleep measured in centuries. And out of its grey surface rose figures the way heat rises off a summer road.

Nine of them. Women and men both, robed in styles four hundred years dead, their edges soft and flickering like candle flames seen through smoke. Memory-echoes, Sera had called them, from what the map-fathers wrote down: not ghosts, exactly. Ghosts remembered being alive. These remembered being *useful*.

The one who came forward was small and brown-faced, with laugh lines carved deep around her eyes and iron-grey hair braided with river-clay beads. Something about the set of her shoulders snagged at a place behind Theo's sternum — a familiarity with no memory attached, like recognizing a house you've never entered.

"You are the last heir's boy," she said. Her voice was dry and warm, like paper left in the sun.

"Theo," he managed. "Theo Dunmore."

"Dunmore." She tasted it. "She kept the name, then. Even hidden."

"You knew her?" Hope leapt up in him, ugly and immediate. "Mistress Ellery Vane — she died in Hollowmere, they burned her grimoire, I burned her grimoire, I—"

"We knew her," the echo said gently. "We knew her mother, and hers, back to the woman who stood on this hill and gave nine years of her life to make what you carry. We have watched every heir since. We watched your mistress bind the seal-piece into a child too young to consent, because there was no one older, and the hunt was coming, and she chose." The echo's flicker steadied, sharpened. "We did not interfere. Consider what that tells you about our mercy."

"I'd rather you told me," Theo said, "than made me guess. Everyone keeps making me guess. My whole life is guessing."

Something moved across the circle then — not laughter exactly, but the shape of it, passing echo to echo like a cup passed down a table.

"He asks," said one near the back, an old man whose beard was full of frost that never fell.

"He asks," agreed another, young, sharp-faced, impatient even in death. "The last three heirs argued with us for a day before they asked anything."

The brown-faced woman raised a hand, and the amber light gathered itself, drew inward, and became — a door. A door of light standing alone on the hilltop, tall and plain and utterly unremarkable, the kind of door you'd find in any farmhouse in Thornfell.

"Then we will not guess with you either," she said. "One question. Answer truly, and the fourth stone opens to you. Answer falsely, or not at all, and you walk away whole — we do not punish honesty, boy, whatever else we are. But you will leave here carrying what you carry, and the thing in the capital will keep eating, and the hunt will keep hunting, until someone answers better than you did."

Theo looked at the door. His hands were shaking. He noticed them shaking with a strange detachment, the way he'd been noticing everything lately — as though parts of him were already packing up and leaving.

"What's behind the door?" he asked.

"The question," said the frost-bearded echo. "Behind every door, the question. That is all doors ever hold."

And the door swung open.

* * *

Inside was his kitchen in Hollowmere.

Not a vision of it — it. The crooked shelf with the chipped blue bowls. The smell of bread and bitterroot tea. The window with the crack running through it like a lightning bolt, which the mistress had never fixed because, she claimed, a cracked window kept the weather honest. Evening light came through that crack and lay in a bright bar across the table, and at the table sat Mistress Vane, younger than he'd ever known her, grinding herbs with a mortar worn smooth as a riverstone, humming.

And there — small, six years old, knees scabbed, clutching a wooden horse with one ear chewed off — a boy. Himself.

"No," Theo said aloud, to the amber light, to the patient echoes. "This isn't fair. You can't just — show me things I paid to forget."

"We are showing you nothing," the brown-faced echo said, beside him now, though he hadn't heard her move. "The door shows each soul its own weight. Look. Or don't. The question does not care whether you watch."

But of course he watched. Of course he did.

Young-him sat at the table swinging his feet, and the mistress slid him tea sweetened with too much honey, and said, in a voice Theo had spent months failing to forget, "Now then, little thief. Tell me what you took from my cupboard."

"Nothing," said young-him, with the transparent guilt of the very young.

"Mm. And if I said the jar you took wasn't sweets but a binding-charm, and you've just eaten half of it?"

Young-him froze mid-swing. Theo, watching, felt the memory land in his body before his mind — the sour panic, the certainty of doom, the way children believe themselves capable of infinite catastrophe.

The young mistress laughed. It rang through the little kitchen, and it was such a warm sound, such an ordinary human sound, that Theo's throat closed. Then she knelt, and took the boy's scraped hands in hers, and said — softly, seriously, the humor folding away like a fan:

"Hear me, Theo. One day someone will ask you to give up everything you remember of me. Of your mother. Of every road you ever walked and every person who was kind to you. And you'll have to decide whether the world is worth more to you than your own self." She tucked a strand of hair behind his ear, clumsy and tender. "You won't be able to answer today. That's all right. Today you're only six. But promise me you'll think about it honestly, every year, so that when the day comes, you'll know your own heart instead of guessing at it."

"And if my heart says no?" young-him asked.

"Then your heart says no," she said. "And I'll love you anyway, and the world can go hang itself."

The kitchen began to fade at the edges, honey-light thinning to amber to grey. Young-him laughed at something. The mistress smiled. And the last thing Theo saw, before the door shut, was her hand resting on the boy's head — the same gesture, he realized with a jolt, that she'd made in the final hour of her life, when she pressed her palm to his skull and whispered *remember nothing, forget nothing*, and lit every ward she had between him and the dark.

The door was gone. The ring of stones waited. Nine echoes looked at him with four hundred years of patience.

"Would you trade your past for the realm?" the brown-faced echo asked, and her voice had lost all its warmth, become simply true, the way a blade is true. "Answer."

* * *

He tried.

That was the worst part, afterward — knowing he had genuinely tried. He stood on the hilltop with the question pressing against him like deep water, and he reached for yes, and yes was there, of course it was there, he'd burned his mother's face to escape Gallow's Rest, he'd fed pieces of himself to the road like a man feeding coins to a toll-keeper, surely he could say the word—

And the word would not come.

Because underneath the yes was the kitchen. Underneath the yes was the sound of her laugh, and he could not remember most of his life but he could remember *that*, and it rose up in him like floodwater and drowned the word before it reached his lips.

"I—" he said. "Yes. No. I don't—"

His voice failed. The amber light dimmed, politely, the way a host dims lamps when a guest has fainted. And the great pillar breathed again — one long exhalation — and the echoes began to fold back into the stone, one by one, unhurried, unsurprised.

"No!" Theo lurched forward, grabbed at the pillar's rough grey flank. "Wait — tell me what to say. Tell me the right answer. There has to be a right answer, everyone always wants something, just tell me what it costs and I'll pay it, I always pay—"

The brown-faced echo paused at the threshold of the stone. She looked back at him, and her expression was the expression his mistress had worn the day she died: sorrow, and love, and the terrible discipline of not fixing what must not be fixed.

"There is no right answer," she said. "There never was. That is the test."

And she was gone, and the ring went quiet, and Theo Dunmore slid down the side of the pillar onto the frostless grass and put his head in his hands.

* * *

Sera found him there some time later. He didn't know how long. Time had gotten slippery again; he was learning not to trust it, the way you learn not to trust ice in late spring.

She tapped her way across the ring — the staff clicking once on each small stone, deliberate, like a knock at a door — and sat down beside him without ceremony, folding her skirts, laying the staff across her knees. For a while she said nothing, which was a skill Theo was beginning to suspect was rarer and more valuable than magic.

"She refused you," Sera said finally. Not a question. She'd felt it from the edge; of course she had.

"The stone refused *me*." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Four hundred years of witches, and I'm the first one who couldn't say yes to saving everyone. Some heir I am. Maybe the hunt's got the right idea. Maybe people like me should be—"

"Don't finish that sentence." Mild, but absolute. "It's a stupid sentence and you're tired, and tired men say stupid sentences and then believe them later."

Theo huffed. Despite everything, despite the hollowed-out feeling in his chest, something in him warmed. "You know, you're the second person to boss me around like that. The first one bound her entire soul into my head without asking."

"Yes, and look how well that turned out," Sera said. "You're alive, the seal holds, and you're insufferably dramatic. Clearly the method works."

He snorted. It surprised him — the snort, the small animal warmth of it. Then the weight came back, and he stared at the great grey pillar that had looked at the whole of his soul and found it wanting.

"Why?" he asked her. Quietly, now. Honestly, which was harder. "Why couldn't I say it? I meant to. I wanted to. Every hour Maren sits in a cell is an hour I—"

"Because you answered the wrong question," Sera said.

He turned to look at her — her blind grey eyes aimed at some middle distance only she could see, her hands folded on her staff, frost melting on her shoulders because the snow still refused the stones but not the girl sitting among them.

"The seal doesn't want a hero," she said. "My father figured that out, near the end, after they took his eyes. He said everyone who came to the stones came asking *am I strong enough, am I good enough, am I willing enough* — and the stones turned them all away, because strength runs out and goodness bends and willingness is just fear wearing armor." She turned her face to him. "The seal doesn't want someone willing to burn. Anyone can burn. Burning is easy; it's over fast. It wants a witness. Someone who stays, and sees, and remembers — even the parts that hurt. Especially those."

"A witness," Theo repeated. The word landed strangely, heavily, like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was there.

"Think about what you're carrying," Sera said. "Not the magic. The *memories*. A kingdom's worth of stolen lives, Maren says, waiting to pour back through you. The entity eats minds because it cannot bear them — because remembering is the one thing it can't do. And what does your mistress's binding do? What have you been doing since Hollowmere, since the lanterns, since the pass? Remembering. Badly, painfully, in pieces, but *doing it*. Skipping like a scratched record, you said. But a scratched record still plays." She smiled then, small and fierce. "You weren't refused because you failed a test of courage, Theo. You were refused because you offered the wrong coin. They didn't want your past. They wanted to see whether you understood that your past was never yours to trade. It's theirs — everyone's — every life the thing in the crown has swallowed. You're not the payment. You're the *ledger*."

Theo sat with that. The wind had returned to the hilltop, quietly, as if given permission, stirring the brown-gold grass around the nine kneeling stones. Somewhere below, a bird resumed singing, tentative, testing whether the world had ended.

"So what do I do?" he asked. "Just — come back when I've learned my lesson? Meditate on it? There isn't time, Sera. Maren's in a cell. The feast is coming — the Feast of the Oath, the whole court gathered around that *thing* —"

"I know." Sera's smile faded into something graver. "And I know what my father's charts say lies beneath the throne room. The fourth stone. Where all of this ends." She reached out and found his hand with unerring accuracy, and gripped it, hard, her fingers cold as the staff. "So hear me, Theo Dunmore, because I'll only say it once, and I say it as a mapmaker's daughter, which means I say it about territory I've actually surveyed."

He waited.

"Heroes die gloriously and are done. Witnesses have to *live* with it. Every memory you give to that seal, you'll have to stand inside while it burns. Every stolen life that pours back through you, you'll have to hold open the door for. It will not be quick and it will not be clean and no one will write songs about the middle of it." Her grip tightened. "Can you do the middle of it? Don't answer me. Answer the hill. Come back tomorrow, or the next day, and answer the hill. Old magic is patient magic. Let it be patient with you."

They sat together on the nameless hill as the light went long and gold, and Theo found, to his astonishment, that the dread in his chest had changed shape. Not smaller. Just — aimed differently. Less like a wall and more like a door.

* * *

They came down off the hill at dusk and made camp in the lee of the slope, and it was Sera who heard the hooves first — her staff flat against the earth, her head tilting like a bird's.

"Riders," she said. "Many. Coming up the valley road, fast. Toward Gallow's Rest."

Theo was on his feet before she finished, scanning the darkening valley. Far below, strung along the road like embers dragged through ash, torchlight moved in numbers that made his stomach drop. Crown riders didn't travel in that force for one runaway apprentice. Crown riders traveled in that force for examples.

"Gallow's Rest," he said. "Ilsa's there. The fence, the market, all of them—"

"And Maren's captors will want the informant network questioned," Sera said grimly, rising, staff already pointing the way down. "Questioning, in the crown's grammar, means fire."

Theo looked back once at the hilltop, at the grey pillar catching the last light, patient as centuries.

"Hold on," he told it — not a plea, not quite a prayer. "I'll bring you the right answer. Whatever it is."

Then he ran, down into the valley, toward the glow that was beginning — even at that distance, even in the dark — to look less like torchlight and more like a town, burning.
