## 15. Gallows Rest Burns

Theo smelled the smoke three miles out, and for one foolish heartbeat he thought: someone's baking.

That was the thing about hope. It reached for the kindest explanation the way a cold hand reaches for a stove.

By the second mile he could taste it — not bread, not hearthwood, but the greasy black reek of pitch and thatch and everything inside them. By the third mile, Maren reined up beside him on the ridge, and neither of them said anything at all, because there was nothing left to say that the valley below wasn't already saying in the loudest voice it had.

Gallow's Rest burned. Not burning — *burned*. The fire had come through days ago, eaten its fill, and moved on, leaving behind a skeleton of chimneys standing in the snow like grave markers someone had forgotten to carve names into.

"We're late," Theo said.

"No," Maren said quietly. "We were always going to be late. The question is what we do now that we know what lateness costs."

He looked at her. She was watching the town with the flat, clinical gaze she wore when feeling something too large to let all the way in. He'd learned to read her by then — three weeks on the road together since Sera's maps had put them back on the same path, three weeks of her dry commentary and his bad jokes and both of them pretending they hadn't each left someone behind in this war already.

"Your fence," she said. "Odo. He might have gotten out."

"He didn't get out."

"You can't know that."

"I know Odo." Theo nudged his horse down the slope before she could argue, because if he stopped moving he'd have to feel it, and feeling it could wait until there was work to do. Work was a kind of mercy. His mistress had taught him that, back when he still remembered being taught things.

*Back when I still remember her face,* some traitorous corner of him added, and he shut that corner like a door on a winter night.

* * *

Maren had been in burned places before. Plague towns mostly, in her old life — the ones where the sickness outran the remedies and the magistrates decided fire was cheaper than physicians. She remembered the particular silence of them. Not true quiet, because ruins creaked and settled and wept snowmelt, but the silence of an absence so total it rang in the ears.

What struck her now, walking Gallow's Rest's main street, was how orderly it wasn't. The crown's burnings in the capital were tidy affairs: condemned structures, cleared streets, proclamations nailed up after. This had been a slaughter that happened to involve fire. She counted doorways as she passed. Here a scorch-shadow on a wall where something — someone — had stood close when the flame took it. There a child's boot, small and singular and asking a question Maren refused to answer.

"They didn't even pretend," Theo said hoarsely. "No trial. No proclamation first. They just—"

"They made an example," Maren said. "That's what free-towns are *for*, from a crown's perspective. Places without walls are lessons waiting to happen."

She said it in her lecture-voice, the one she used to keep her hands steady, and she saw him recognize the trick because he'd started using it himself. Two physicians' tricks, though only one of them had ever held the title. The irony was not lost on her. Very little was lost on her these days; that was the trouble with being the only person awake in a kingdom of sleepwalkers.

They found the first body of the network within the hour, and it told them everything. A woman pinned under a fallen beam near the well, and in her dead hand, clenched so tight the knuckles had gone white, a scrap of parchment bearing a list of names — half crossed out. The informant ledger. Someone had died trying to destroy it.

"The hunt isn't a net," Theo said softly. "It's a web. And they're cutting the strands."

"Or we're seeing the strands cut *for* us." Maren knelt, pried the parchment loose with a gentleness she saved for the dead. "Look at the crossings-out. Some of these weren't burned with the town. These are marks — someone came through after and settled accounts. Ilsa's network turned on itself, or the wardens tidied up loose ends. Either way—" she folded the list away, "—the entity doesn't leave witnesses. Even useful ones."

"Theo!" Her head came up sharp. He'd gone rigid in the doorway of what had been Odo's shop, and he was staring at nothing, or at something behind nothing, and his eyes had gone glassy-bright in the way she'd learned meant the binding was chewing on him.

"What do you see?" she asked, crossing to him fast.

"A memory that isn't mine yet." His voice came out strange, level as a frozen pond. "The shop. Full of stock. Lanterns, charms, a jar of pickled eggs Odo swore were lucky." He blinked, and the glassiness cracked, and underneath it was grief with nowhere to go. "I'm remembering it *before* it happened. Like the stones gave me a preview and forgot to mention the ending."

Maren took his arm. Old habit now; new tenderness under it, though neither of them named that either. "Come on," she said. "Let's find your friend."

* * *

They found Odo where Odo had always been: behind his counter, in the flooded ruin of his shop, propped against the shelving with a warden's crossbow bolt through him and a smile on his ruined face like he'd won the argument anyway.

He was dying. Not dead — dying, which was worse and better at once, because dying men got last words and dead men only got regrets. Theo dropped to his knees in the ash-water and took the old fence's hand, and Maren knelt across from him, already reading the wound with her physician's eye and knowing, the way you know weather, that no craft of hers would matter here.

"There's my boy," Odo rasped. "Knew you'd come back. You've got the look. Everyone who survives this business gets the look eventually. Yours just arrived early."

"Hold still. Let me—"

"Sit down, sit *down*, boy, you'll wrinkle my dignity." Odo's laugh became a cough became a wet, rattling thing. "I sold you a charm two months ago and lied about its price. Consider us square."

"Odo—"

"I *know*. I know what you want to say, and I haven't got the hours for it, so listen instead." The old man's grip tightened, shockingly strong. From somewhere inside his coat he drew a shape wrapped in oilcloth, and pressed it into Theo's palm, folding the boy's fingers over it with his own ruined ones. "Anchor charm. The one you asked after. I charged it special — took me three nights and a favor I owed a witch in Lowmarsh, rest her, she's ash now too probably."

Theo unwrapped it with shaking hands. Inside lay a small brass compass, needleless, its face etched with the circle-and-line sigil of the old oath-stones. It should have pointed nowhere. Instead it trembled, steady as a heartbeat, toward the southeast.

Toward the capital.

"It doesn't point north," Odo whispered. "It points to the fourth stone. The one your mistress's circle never wrote down, because writing it down would've been signing the whole realm's death warrant." His breath hitched. "Beneath the throne, boy. Under the coronation chair itself, where every king since the founding has sat down and sworn the oath. That's the root. That's where the great seal was driven home. And that's where your thing has been gnawing for three hundred years like a rat in a granary."

"Beneath the throne," Theo repeated dumbly.

"Every feast, every coronation, every oath sworn in that hall feeds straight down into it. Clever, eh? The founders built the lock *into the keyhole*." Odo's eyes drifted, found Maren. "You. Physician. Keep him alive long enough to be stupid. That's the whole job description. Don't let him be sensible — sensible men hide, and hiding's over."

"I'll consider it," Maren said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it.

Odo smiled again. Looked back at Theo. "I priced that charm fair, in the end. First honest transaction of my life. Tell them — tell whoever's left to tell — that Gallows Rest paid its debts." His grip loosened. "Go on, then. Shop's closed."

And that was all. The hand went slack in Theo's, the way hands do, suddenly just a hand, and the fire's silence rushed back in around them like water around a sinking stone.

* * *

Maren gave them both a minute. Then she did the thing physicians do, which is stand up and make grief wait outside while the living get sorted.

Theo stayed kneeling a long moment. Then he closed Odo's eyes, took the compass, and rose, and when he turned around his face had changed. She'd seen him afraid, angry, determined, heartbroken — she'd catalogued all of it over three weeks of hard road. This was none of those. This was a man setting down a weight he'd been carrying wrong and picking it up correctly.

"They knew," he said. "The crown knew the network was here and they burned it rather than turn it. Why? Ilsa's people were *useful* to them."

"Were," Maren said. "Until Ilsa broke. Until word spread that informants were turning their coats. A web whose strands start choosing sides is more dangerous than no web at all." She hesitated, then gave him the truth plain, because he'd earned plainness. "Or the entity ordered it. It's been feeding on fear for centuries, Theo. A kingdom-wide hunt produces fear the way a mill produces flour. But a *burned town* — that produces terror. Terror concentrated, pure, harvestable. I think we just walked into a field after the reaping."

Theo looked out over the ruined street, at the chimney-graves and the child's boot and the scorch-shadows, and Maren watched him decide — visibly, the way a man decides to lift something he knows will hurt his back — to carry it.

"Then it fed well," he said. "And it's still hungry, because Odo said so and because the king hasn't slept in three years and because every road leads southeast." He held up the compass. Its needleless face trembled toward the capital, patient as scripture. "Beneath the throne. My mother was burned in that city. My mistress bound me to keep me from it. And the answer's been sitting under the chair the whole time, laughing at us."

"That's usually where answers sit," Maren said. "Underneath whatever we're too polite to move."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face — the first since the ridge, thin but real. "You've been saving that one."

"I've been saving several. You're difficult to cheer."

"I'm easy to cheer. You just have to burn down everything I love first and then hand me a compass."

"There it is," Maren said. "There's the boy I nearly died for in a river ambush. Insufferable. Good. Insufferable means you're planning to live."

But the humor thinned fast, because humor was a coat and the wind was coming through. They worked through the afternoon among the ashes — Maren doing what she could for two survivors pulled from a root cellar, a grandmother and a boy of maybe ten with a flicker of the gift in him that he'd need to unlearn fast, and Theo walking the streets with the ledger-names, marking doors, saying names aloud to the snow. A record. A witness. Sera's word from the third stone came back to him unbidden: *the seal doesn't want a hero — it wants a witness.* Well. He was witnessing.

At dusk they stood at the town's edge, where the gallows-tree that had given the place its name still stood, scorched but living, stubbornly green at the tips of one high branch. Somebody — Theo suspected the grandmother — had hung a strip of red cloth from it. Old custom. Mourning and defiance in one knot.

"They'll call this a warning," Theo said. "Every survivor they let crawl away is carrying the message: *this is what the gift costs.*"

"Then we carry a different message." Maren mounted up. "Same roads, opposite direction. Let the next town they threaten learn that two people walked out of Gallows Rest still breathing, still armed, and still — what's the word you smugglers use?"

"Determined?"

"I was going to say *stubborn*."

"Stubborn works." Theo swung up beside her, the compass in his breast pocket, tapping against his heart like a second pulse. Southeast. Beneath the throne. Through the Lion's Mouth, past the feast, into the dark under the coronation chair where three hundred years of oaths had rotted into a den.

"One condition," Maren said as they rode. "When this is done — when the thing's unraveled and the king is his own man again or dust — you and I are going somewhere with ovens. Real bread. I refuse to end this story smelling of smoke."

Theo laughed — actually laughed, there in the shadow of the gallows-tree, and the sound went up into the winter sky like something offered rather than stolen.

"It's a bargain," he said. "But Maren — the Feast of the Oath is in nine days."

She went very still.

"And the compass isn't pointing at the throne," Theo said slowly, watching the brass face in his palm. The trembling needle had shifted. Still southeast, still toward the capital — but lower now, deeper, angrier, quivering like a plumb line dropped into a well that went down much, much further than any of them had guessed.

"It's pointing *under* it. Maren — whatever's beneath the throne, it knows we're coming. I think it just started pulling."
