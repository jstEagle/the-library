## 16. Entering the Lion's Mouth

The capital of Thornfell announced itself long before we could see it—not with walls or spires, though those came soon enough, but with a smell. Woodsmoke and roasting meat and horse dung and river mud, all of it braided together into something that could only be called *city*. I had been told once that a man could live his whole life in Hollowmere and die happy. Standing on the last hill before the King's Road widened into cobblestone, I decided that whoever had said it had never smelled a city at festival time.

"Feast of the Oath," Maren said beside me, squinting down at the traffic of carts and pilgrims and merchants flowing toward the gates. "Every noble house in the kingdom in one square, every guard tripled, every gate searched twice." She paused. "I want you to appreciate the irony that this was your plan."

"It's a good plan."

"It's a terrible plan. It's the worst plan I've heard since a physician proposed treating lung-rot by blowing pipe smoke up the patient's nose." She patted the satchel at her hip, where her stolen credentials sat wrapped in oilcloth like something fragile and slightly shameful. "Fortunately, it is also our only plan, which makes it brilliant."

That was Maren all over. She insulted a thing and used it in the same breath, the way a surgeon insults a wound while stitching it shut.

We had been walking for six days since Gallow's Rest, and we had not talked much about the ashes. Some things you carry better in silence. But every night when I closed my eyes I saw the fence's face, gray as paper, pressing the memory-anchor charm into my palm. *The fourth stone is beneath the throne itself,* he'd rasped, and then he'd looked past me at the smoke where his whole livelihood had been, and he hadn't said anything else, because there wasn't anything else to say.

The charm hung now against my chest under my shirt, warm as a held coin. It didn't hold memories anymore—just one instruction, one thread of someone else's dying certainty. Beneath the throne. Of course it was beneath the throne. Where else would an oath-eater keep the door to its own cage but under the chair it sat in?

"They'll search us at the gate," I said.

"Yes."

"And my gift flares when I'm frightened."

"I've noticed." She handed me a small clay vial from her satchel. "Tincture of valerian and something else. Drink it. It won't stop the magic, but it will slow your pulse enough that you might pass for a man who is merely nervous rather than a man about to wet himself."

"Comforting."

"I'm a physician. Comfort is above my pay grade. Survival is what they pay me for, and technically they don't pay me anymore because I'm disgraced." She smiled, thin and sharp. "Drink."

* * *

The gate was exactly as bad as promised.

Two wardens in crown black stood at the arch, and between them a third figure in plain gray robes with a chain of small iron bells around her neck—a *listener*, one of the entity's new priest-caste, trained to hear the hum of the gift the way a tuner hears a sour string. The line of people waiting shuffled forward in the gray morning light, and every person in it had the particular stiffness of folk trying very hard to look innocent, which is the least innocent way to look there is.

"Credentials," said the warden when we reached the front.

Maren produced her papers with the bored efficiency of a woman who had done this a hundred times—which, until three years ago, she had. "Royal College of Physicians, traveling warrant, Feast sanitation detail," she recited. "We're to inspect the feast hall drains before the crowds arrive. You do not want what happens to a court that eats bad shellfish, sergeant. Believe me. I've seen it. There were *buckets*."

The warden's mustache twitched. Nobody wants to hear about the buckets.

"And him?"

"My assistant. Strong back, no opinions." She jerked her thumb at me without looking. "Don't ask him questions; he'll answer them."

It was a joke, mostly. Mostly. The warden grunted and turned to me, and that was when the listener's head came up.

She turned slowly, like a weathervane finding wind, and her eyes—milk-pale, unfocused—drifted across the crowd and settled somewhere near me. Not quite at me. Near. The iron bells shivered though she hadn't moved.

My heart slammed. And my heart slamming meant my gift woke, the way it always did, heat crawling up my arms, and I felt it reach for the nearest light—the warden's lantern—and I thought, *no, no, not here, not now,* and pressed it down, down, down into the place inside me where I kept it buried, the same place my mistress had built her cage, and the pressure of holding it there sent a spike of pain through my skull so bright I saw white.

The lantern guttered. Just barely. A hiccup of flame.

The listener frowned. Her head tilted toward the lantern instead of me, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she was hearing *me*, but the binding in my mind was scattering the sound—she couldn't find the source. The cage my mistress built to hide me was still doing its work, even as it ate me alive.

"Something?" the warden asked her.

"A rat in the walls," she murmured, and her pale eyes slid off the lantern and moved on, sweeping the crowd behind us. "Old stone. Old echoes. Nothing living."

Maren's hand found my elbow, grip light as a bird's foot and twice as strong. "Move along," she told the warden pleasantly, and we moved along.

Only when we were through the arch and swallowed by the feast-day crowd did I let out the breath I'd been holding. My legs felt strange, borrowed.

"She heard something," I whispered.

"She heard you. And she couldn't find you." Maren steered me left, down a lane of fish stalls, speaking low and quick. "Do you understand what that means? Three years ago, a flare like that would have lit you up like a bonfire to anyone with ears. Whatever your mistress wove into you—it isn't just hiding your memories. It's *scattering* them. Making you sound like old stone." She glanced sideways at me. "Theo. Your cage is killing you, yes. But it is also the finest concealment working I have ever encountered, and I studied under the best in the kingdom."

"That's very touching. Can I throw up now?"

"After the feast hall."

* * *

Maren's credentials got us into the palace through the service gate, which was its own particular education. I had expected grandeur. What I got was grandeur with its skirts tucked up: scullions hauling barrels, chambermaids arguing about linens, a steward with a voice like a cracked bell directing forty people at once and being obeyed by none of them. Power, it turns out, smells like tallow candles and other people's labor.

And underneath it all—underneath everything, so faint I might have imagined it—I felt the stones humming.

I stopped dead in a corridor of grey granite and put my hand against the wall before I knew I was going to.

"Theo." Maren's warning voice, distant.

But the stones were *singing*. Low, wordless, patient as mountains. And threaded through the hum, rising unbidden from the sealed dark of me, came images: a woman's hands sorting herbs by candlelight. A road through mountain fog. A fire, enormous, and screaming—

I snatched my hand back as if the wall had burned me. Which, in a sense, it had.

"This stone," I managed. "It remembers something."

"Everything here remembers something," Maren said quietly. "This city is four hundred years old and half of that history happened in fires. Keep walking."

But I knew. The knowing settled in my stomach like a swallowed stone of its own. We were walking the same halls where they had burned the gift-bearers. Where they had burned *her*. Somewhere in these foundations was ash that had been my mother, packed into mortar by men who called it justice, and the stones had held the memory of it ever since, humming it to each other in the dark like a lullaby nobody wanted.

And the terrible thing—the thing I would not tell Maren until much later—was that I wasn't afraid anymore.

Somewhere in the last six days, terror had burned itself down to coals, and the coals had gone strange and calm. Walking these halls, I felt less like an intruder than like a letter finally arriving at its address. Every step deeper made the humming in my blood a little louder, a little more familiar, and I understood that this was not an accident of architecture. My mistress had bound the great seal into my memory, and the great seal lived in these stones, and stones remember their own. I had run from Hollowmere thinking I was fleeing my fate. I had simply been walking toward it the long way around, taking the scenic route through smugglers' passes and burned towns and graves.

I was always meant to come back. That should have horrified me. Instead it steadied me, the way the mistress's voice had always steadied me, even when she was telling me I'd mended the kettle wrong again.

"You've gone quiet," Maren said as we descended a stair toward the feast hall's undercrofts. "Quieter than usual, even, and you're a champion of quiet."

"Just listening."

"To what?"

"To the building." I tried a smile. It felt odd on my face, like wearing someone else's hat. "It's saying hello."

Maren gave me a long look, physician's look, cataloguing symptoms. "If you start answering it, tell me first. I'd like to be holding something sharp."

The stair opened into the undercroft beneath the feast hall, all vaulted shadow and stacked casks, and there—past a row of wine barrels older than the dynasty—we found the drain access Maren's warrant entitled us to inspect, and beyond the drains, half-hidden behind a false wall that Maren spotted because disgraced physicians apparently develop a nose for hidden doors the way cats develop a taste for fish, a narrow stair going *down*.

Down past the cellars. Down past the deepest cellar. Down to where the torchlight didn't bother reaching.

The air changed. Colder, and tasting of iron, and the humming in the stones rose to something I could feel in my teeth. Against my chest, the memory-anchor charm went hot enough that I pulled it out on its cord, and its surface—dead clay for weeks—had begun to glow faintly, the color of embers under ash.

"Beneath the throne," I whispered. "Directly beneath."

Maren stood at the top of the stair, looking down into the dark, and for once she had nothing wry to say. When she finally spoke, her voice was very quiet.

"When we go down there, everything changes. You understand that. Whatever happens after tonight, neither of us comes back up the same person."

"I haven't been the same person in months," I said. "Ask anyone. Except everyone who knew me is either dead, disgraced, or sold me to the crown." I touched the charm, warm as a heartbeat. "Besides. I think it already knows I'm here. I think—" I laughed, soft and disbelieving, the sound echoing off stones that hummed back at me like a struck bell. "I think it's been expecting me. Maybe since before I was born. Maybe that's what the whole hunt was. Not searching. *Setting the table.*"

"Then let's not keep it waiting," Maren said grimly, and drew the little knife from her sleeve, the one she'd taken off the bounty-taker's body, the one she cleaned more often than she cleaned anything else.

We started down.

And ten steps into the dark, the humming stopped—all at once, everywhere, like a held breath—and a voice that was not a voice at all but a pressure against the inside of my skull said, gently, almost kindly:

***Welcome home, seal-bearer. We have kept your seat warm.***

Above us, far above, the first trumpets of the Feast of the Oath began to play.
