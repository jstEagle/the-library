## 17. The Feast of the Oath

The Great Hall of Thornfell had been built to make people feel small, and in Maren's professional opinion it over-prescribed. She had studied anatomy for thirty years; she knew exactly how small a human being was, and had never before been made to feel it by the architecture. Vaulted ceiling like the inside of a whale's ribcage. Banners of every province hanging from iron hooks that had once, she suspected, held other things. And at the far end, on a dais beneath the empty throne — the king's chair itself stood vacant tonight, which no one remarked upon, which told her everything — the long table where the Feast of the Oath would be served.

She walked through it all in stolen silk.

The physician's robes had been easy enough to acquire; half the capital's tailors owed their livelihoods to forged credentials in one direction or another. The harder part was the face. Not disguising it — a little powder, a change of parting in the hair, and the woman who had fled the palace three years ago became simply an aging court doctor from the northern provinces, one of forty summoned to attend His Majesty during the feast, because His Majesty had not slept in three years and everyone pretended this was a matter of diet.

"Doctor Vess." A chamberlain with a voice like wet parchment consulted his list. "You are seated below the salt. Do not approach the dais unless bidden."

"I wouldn't dream of it," Maren said, and meant: *I have dreamed of nothing else for six weeks.*

Below the salt. Good. The salt line ran down the center of the hall like a scar, and everything above it belonged to the thing wearing the king. Everything below it still, nominally, belonged to people.

She found her seat between a fat baron who smelled of pickled herring and ambition, and a young countess who kept glancing toward the dais with an expression Maren recognized from hospital wards — the look of someone watching a patient they knew was dying and had decided not to say so.

"You're staring," Maren said pleasantly, arranging her napkin.

"I'm admiring," the countess said. "His Majesty looks *wonderful*, doesn't he? Better every year."

Maren followed her gaze to where Aldric IV stood greeting the first arrivals, and felt her stomach perform a small, controlled revolt.

He did look wonderful. That was the horror of it. Three years without sleep should have left him a gray husk, and instead the king glowed — skin smooth as candle wax, eyes bright and black and slightly too wet, like river stones after rain. He moved beautifully. He moved *too* beautifully, each gesture arriving a half-second before the reason for it, as though the music were following the dancer instead of the reverse.

And when his shadow shifted against the banners behind him, it lagged. Just slightly. Just enough that only someone who had spent years studying how bodies worked would notice that his body was being operated from somewhere else.

"He does," Maren agreed. "Whatever is his secret?"

"They say he has found a new physician," the countess whispered, delighted. "Someone very skilled."

*They say I am very skilled,* Maren thought. *They say it in the dungeons, too.*

She had bought her place at this feast with three weeks of careful, poisonous service — tending the entity's household guards for minor complaints, listening, mapping. She now knew the rotation of the door wardens to within a quarter hour. She knew which of the kitchen staff could be bribed with coin and which required flattery, and that the head cook required neither because she hated the crown on principle and asked no questions of anyone carrying a covered basket. She knew that Theo and Sera were already inside — Sera in the map-room galleries above, fingers reading the carved railings like braille, and Theo—

Theo was below the salt too, three tables down, wearing a servant's livery that fit him badly and a waiter's blankness that fit him well. Their eyes met once across the crowd, briefly, the way two strangers' eyes meet. In that glance he managed to communicate: *I'm fine, don't worry,* which was a lie, and *I'm frightened,* which was not, and *the wine here is terrible,* which she chose to believe.

It steadied her more than she liked to admit. Fear shared out is fear halved; that was arithmetic even the smugglers knew.

* * *

The feast began with trumpets, because everything in Thornfell began with trumpets; Maren privately suspected the kingdom would announce breakfast by trumpet if the budget allowed.

The courses came in procession. Boar glazed dark as lacquer. Eels from the southern marshes. A swan re-dressed in its own feathers, which struck Maren as either reverence or mockery, depending on what one believed sat on the throne these days. The wine flowed upward from below the salt, and downward from above it, and the noise of the hall rose until it became a kind of weather.

Maren ate sparingly, drank less, and watched.

That was when she began to count.

It started as a physician's habit — cataloguing symptoms in a room. But the symptom she was cataloguing was *wrongness*, and it was everywhere above the salt. The Duke of Carrow laughing a beat too long at jokes that hadn't finished. The Lady Amethyne, whose husband had died last spring, blinking at intervals so perfectly regular that Maren timed them against her own pulse — every fourth beat, precisely, like a mechanism. The Lord Chancellor pouring wine for a neighbor without looking at the glass, without looking at anything, his gaze fixed on the middle distance where perhaps instructions lived.

She counted forty-one nobles above the salt line.

Twenty-two of them blinked in time.

Her hand had gone cold around her cup before she understood why. Not servants. Not prisoners. These were the great houses of Thornfell, the ancient blood, the ones whose names anchored treaties and marriages and the whole rotten lattice of the realm — and they were hollowed. Worn. Vessels with something else poured into them, keeping warm, keeping *position*, the way a squatter keeps a fire burning in a stolen house.

Half the court, she thought. Half the court, and no one below the salt could see it, because grief and drink and the general dimness of feasting had settled over everyone like snow.

Except the countess beside her had seen something. And the boy who had just entered bearing the second dessert course had seen everything.

Theo crossed the hall with a tray of candied plums, moving in the unhurried glide of someone who had carried heavy things past dangerous people since childhood. He was pale under the livery. As he passed behind the king's chair — close, closer than any servant was meant to come, though no warden stirred to stop him, and Maren's heart climbed into her throat — the entity turned its borrowed head and looked up at him.

The hall went on eating. The trumpets went on being awful. But between the king and the serving boy, something passed that had weight, like a rope thrown across a chasm.

"Ah," said Aldric IV, in a voice like warm honey poured over cold iron. "There you are."

He said it loudly enough for the near tables to hear, warmly, as a king might greet a favorite servant. But Maren heard the second sentence underneath, the one spoken in no air at all:

*Seal-bearer. You brought yourself to me. How economical.*

Theo set down the tray. His hands did not shake. Later, Maren would think about that — about where a boy learned to keep his hands still while the world's largest predator looked at him with its whole attention — and she would decide it was the same place he'd learned everything else: the hard way, early, alone.

"Your Majesty," Theo said, and bowed the exact right depth for a servant, and Maren saw the entity's smile widen by one tooth.

*Sit,* it said, in that voice beneath the voice. *No. Stand there. Let me look at you.*

And it looked. The whole hall felt it without knowing — a pressure like the moment before thunder, dogs whining in distant kennels, candles guttering in unison down forty feet of table. The fat baron beside Maren paused with a plum halfway to his mouth and said, "Odd draft."

"It's the doors," said the countess.

"It's not the doors," said Maren.

Above the salt, the entity rose. It wore the king's body graciously, the way a good actor wears a costume, and it lifted its hands and the hall quieted the way a room quiets when the physician enters.

"My friends," it said, in Aldric's voice, pitched to carry. "Tonight we celebrate the Oath — the old vow between crown and country, older than any of our names. And tonight I find my heart full. For look—" it gestured, gracious, at the hall, at the banners, at the sea of faces— "look how many of you are *already mine.*"

Laughter, polite and puzzled, rippled below the salt. Above it, twenty-two nobles laughed in perfect unison, a single sound from twenty-two throats, and the laughter below faltered and died because the ear is an honest creature and knows when harmony is wrong.

"Do you know what an oath-eater eats?" the king went on, conversational now, strolling along the dais. "Not flesh. Not souls — souls are gristle, mostly water. It eats *minding*. The part of you that says *I remember, therefore I am*. Every mind it takes, it keeps. Whole lives, shelved like jars of preserves." It smiled at the hall. "I have a magnificent pantry."

"The king is mad," the fat baron whispered, with the relief of a man finding a tidy explanation.

"The king," said Maren quietly, "is not in the building."

The entity stopped at the head of the steps. Below the salt, Theo stood among the serving folk with his tray and his still hands, and Maren watched him do the bravest thing she had ever seen a person do: nothing. Wait. Let the trap close around himself rather than run, because running would spend magic he needed, and because Sera was in the galleries and Maren was at the table and the plan had a shape that required him to stand still inside the wolf's grin.

"Come here, boy," the king said gently. "You've carried those plums quite far enough."

The hall watched a servant climb the dais steps. Murmurs — *who is he? what favor is this?* — and the countess beside Maren leaned in, thrilled: "A promotion, perhaps."

"Yes," Maren said. "Something like that."

At the top of the steps, the entity took Theo's chin in one royal hand and tilted his face to the light, the way Maren herself had done in a frozen riverbed a lifetime ago, checking a patient's eyes. And it spoke — aloud now, letting the whole court hear, because secrecy had stopped serving it the moment its seal-bearer walked in wearing a servant's collar.

"Do you know what you're carrying?" it asked. "Of course you do. The witch told you *nothing*, which is the same as telling you *everything*. A piece of the old seal, stitched into your memory, hidden in the one place I could never safely dig." Its thumb stroked Theo's jaw, almost tender. "You have been in pain since she died. Headaches. Gaps. Hours gone like torn pages." Theo's breath caught — audible even to Maren, three tables down. "That is not the binding killing you, child. That is *me*, trying to reach my property through a locked door, every day, for years. I apologize for the inconvenience. Truly. It must be like living with a debt collector in your skull."

"You could stop," Theo said. His voice was steady. Young, but steady.

"I could," the entity agreed, "if you opened the door. And here is my offer — and mark it, all of you, for I have never made it twice." It turned to the hall, arms wide, magnanimous as a festival. "Surrender the seal, and I will return every memory I have ever taken. Every life in my pantry, poured back. Including yours — all of them. Your mother's face." 

The hall went silent in a different key.

"You burned her image to hide from my wardens," the entity said softly, only to Theo now, though every word carried. "You woke and could not recall her, and you grieved a grief you couldn't name. I *have* it, boy. I have her laugh, her hands, the song she sang when you were feverish at four years old. I have your mistress's last night, every word, every apology. I have six hundred years of the dead, filed and waiting. Give me the seal, and walk out of this hall richer than any man born. Or refuse—" and here its voice dropped into something vast and patient and cold as bedrock— "and I will take the seal anyway, and take everything else besides, and you will leave this world with less than a beggar owns."

Silence. Real silence, the kind that has weight.

Maren's knuckles were white on the table edge. *Refuse*, she willed across the distance. *Oh, you stubborn, mule-hearted boy, refuse — but carefully, carefully—*

Because she had seen his face when the offer landed. She had seen the hunger there, raw and naked as a wound — the longing of a man offered back the pieces of himself he'd paid away. No torture could have broken him like that tenderness did. The entity knew it. Six hundred years old, and it had learned that the surest way through a locked door is to promise the person behind it the key to their own heart.

Theo looked up at the thing wearing his king.

"No," he said.

Just that. Quietly. Almost kindly, the way one declines a second helping.

"No?" The entity's smile stayed fixed while everything behind it stopped moving.

"No," Theo repeated. "My mother's face isn't yours to sell. Neither is my mistress's apology. You didn't *keep* their memories, whatever you tell yourself. You stole them. And stolen things—" he stepped back, out of the royal grip, and Maren saw his hand slide into his sleeve where the anchor charm hung against his wrist— "stolen things have to be given *back*. Not traded. Returned. That's the difference between us, I think. You'd never understand it, but it's the whole oath. The real one. The wild one."

For one heartbeat, the entity simply stared at him — six centuries of appetite meeting a boy with a servant's collar and a thief's straight spine — and Maren thought, absurdly, *it looks almost impressed.*

Then the candles went out. All of them, every flame in the hall, in one breath, as if the darkness had been waiting behind the light all evening with its hand raised.

In the black, the entity's voice filled the vaulted dark like water filling a hull:

"*Take him.*"

* * *

Chaos is a physician's native country. Maren moved through the screaming dark the way she'd moved through the plague wards — low, fast, counting exits. Someone knocked over the salt cellar; she heard it scatter like sleet. The twenty-two hollowed nobles had risen in unison and stood motionless among the panicking guests, heads tilted, listening to orders no one else could hear.

She reached the dais steps as the first wardens' torches flared at the doors — and found Theo already gone, swallowed into the press of bodies below the salt, moving toward the kitchens, toward Sera, toward the fourth stone sleeping somewhere beneath all this marble. Toward the counter-oath they had planned in a blind woman's kitchen over maps drawn from memory.

But as the torchlight spread, she saw the king at the top of the steps, and the king was not pursuing. He was standing very still, one hand pressed flat against his own chest — against Aldric's chest — and his face had changed. The wax-smoothness had cracked, and underneath, brief as lightning, was something old and exhausted and *frightened*, some buried remainder of the man who had once worn this crown, surfacing for one instant like a drowning sailor's hand.

*"Physician,"* it gasped — in a voice that was not honey, not iron, but hoarse and human and nearly gone— *"beneath the throne — the stone — do not let it finish the binding in reverse — it will not release the oath — it will swallow the—"*

The hand dropped. The smoothness returned. Black eyes found Maren across the hall and smiled with every tooth the king possessed.

"Doctor Vess," the entity called pleasantly, before the whole court, as wardens closed around her position. "How fortunate. I find myself suddenly in need of a physician. My king's heart is troubling him."

Guards stepped out of the crowd — six of them, closing in a ring, and Maren understood with terrible clarity that her three weeks of careful work had been visible all along, permitted, *arranged*, the mouse allowed its maze because the cat enjoyed the geometry.

She straightened her stolen silk, lifted her chin, and prepared to buy time the only way left to her: by being indispensable, and lying magnificently.

Above her, in the dark galleries, she heard — faint, impossible, unmistakable — the tap of a blind girl's cane beginning to move.
