## 18. Unbinding

The fourth stone was not a stone at all, not anymore.

Three hundred years of kings had built over it—flagstones, marble, a throne room above and cellars below and somewhere between them a chamber no architect's plan admitted to, where the rock still remembered being rock. Theo lay on it with his wrists bound in oath-cord and his spine pressed flat against its surface, and the stone hummed through him like a plucked string that refused to stop.

"You're comfortable," Sera said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm *bound to an ancient sealing-stone beneath a stolen throne while an immortal hunger chews on the kingdom overhead*," Theo said. "I'm having a wonderful time. Send word to my mistress."

"She'd have liked you," Sera murmured. Her fingers moved over the chalk circle at the chamber's edge, quick and sure despite eyes that had never seen it. She didn't need them for this work. The stones spoke to her hands. "She had your mouth on her, I think. The old ones always did. Wit as armor."

"It's not armor," Theo said. "It's load-bearing."

Maren knelt on his left, checking the cord at his wrist for the third time. Her hands were steady, which was more than he could say for his own. "The binding will hurt," she said, in the voice she used for delivering diagnoses—the one that meant she'd already decided lying would be a waste of both their time. "I want to be honest about that before we begin."

"You've been honest about it four times."

"And you've been brave about it three times. We'll see if the streak holds." She touched his forehead, briefly, the way a physician touches a patient whose fever they cannot break. "The counter-oath isn't a spell, Theo. Spells can be interrupted. This is a transaction. You give; the seal takes. The only way out is through, and the only thing you carry through is what you choose to keep hold of."

"And what I choose to keep hold of is," he said, "approximately nothing."

"Approximately nothing," she agreed gently. "The anchor charm holds the passes. The feel of the road under your boots. That's your thread back."

"My thread," Theo said, "is a pair of boots."

Sera laughed—a small, startled sound, quickly smothered against her sleeve. "Oh, you *are* hers."

Above them, muffled by thirty feet of foundation stone, the Feast of the Oath was reaching its crescendo. He could hear it when the wind shifted through the vents: music, laughter, the bright hollow ringing of a thousand goblets raised to a king who had not slept in three years and did not eat and whose shadow moved a half-second behind his body, like a man walking beside his own echo.

Somewhere up there, the entity knew they were here. It had known since the moment Theo crossed the threshold of the keep. It had let them come. It was, Maren thought, *curious*—and curiosity in a thing that old was worse than rage.

"The outer circle holds us or nothing holds us," Maren said now, rising. "When you start pouring, we pour with you. Whatever you feel, whatever it shows you—we take some of the weight. That's the whole craft of it. Three people remembering together so one person doesn't drown alone."

Theo looked from her to Sera to the dark curve of the ceiling above. "And if I drown anyway?"

"Then we'll be very annoyed with you," Sera said. "We walked all this way."

He laughed. It came out cracked, but it came. And then, because there was nothing left between him and the doing of the thing, he closed his eyes and began.

* * *

The counter-oath started, absurdly, with a memory of bread.

His mistress's kitchen. The smell of rye loaves cooling on the sill, steam curling off the crusts. He had been fourteen. He remembered wanting to steal one before supper and knowing he'd be caught, because she caught everything, and choosing to steal it anyway, because some lessons you have to learn twice—

The seal drank it.

Not erased—*poured*. The memory ran out of him like water finding a channel, down through his spine into the humming stone, and he felt it settle somewhere far below, a single warm brick laid in a wall. He gasped. His hands spasmed against the cords.

"That's it," Maren said quietly. "Again. Give it the next one."

"Which next one? There are—" He stopped. There were thousands. Tens of thousands. A life is made of moments too small to count: every loaf of bread, every frostbitten morning, every time someone had laughed at something he said. All of it was currency now. All of it was fuel.

He gave it the frostbitten morning. He gave it his first charm, botched, that had set his sleeves alight and taught him to respect fire. He gave it the smuggler's road at dusk, the taste of cheap wine in Gallow's Rest, the sound of Sera laughing at his joke about boots.

Each memory went down like a coin into a well, and the well kept drinking, and the stone hummed louder, and the wall grew.

Then the entity noticed.

It came into his mind the way cold comes under a door—not entering so much as *arriving*, everywhere at once, a pressure with intent. And it opened what it carried.

*A kingdom's worth of stolen lives.*

They flooded him. Not his memories—*theirs*. A farmer's wedding in a valley he'd never seen, forty years eaten away. A hedge-witch's apprenticeship, her first poultice, her last breath. A soldier's whole boyhood. A midwife's thousand deliveries, each child's cry stacked inside him like bells. The entity had fed for centuries, and it dumped the feast over him all at once, drowning, drowning—

"*Maren—*"

"We have you." Her voice came from very far away and also directly inside his skull. "Theo. Listen. These aren't yours to carry. Let them pass through. You're a riverbed, not a reservoir. *Let them flow.*"

He tried. He tried to be a riverbed. But the memories were so *heavy*, and each one wanted to be held, wanted to be mourned, and grief has no respect for whose grief it is—

A hand found his. Small, sure, scarred with mapmaker's ink. Sera.

"I'm here," she said. "You don't have to know these lives. Just witness them. That's all the seal ever asked. *Witness.*"

So he witnessed. He stopped trying to hold the farmer's wedding and simply watched it go by, and it passed through him cleaner, lighter, and settled in the wall below with the rest. The midwife's thousand cries rang once through his chest and flowed onward. The soldier's boyhood. The witch's last breath.

Brick by brick. Memory by memory. Not only his now—he was pouring himself out *and* serving as the channel for everything the thing had ever taken, a conduit for centuries of stolen living, and the seal rose around it all like a dam built from the raw material of human days.

The entity screamed.

Not with a mouth. With *absence*—every sound in the chamber cut dead at once, even Theo's own heartbeat, a silence so total it was a wound. And in that silence it tore at him, looking for the seams, hunting for anything left loose to rip away.

It found the anchor thread.

*The mountain passes,* it crooned, in a voice assembled from a thousand stolen voices, some of them kind, which was the worst part. *Boots on frozen road. Shall I take the boots too, little seal-bearer? Shall I leave you nothing at all?*

Theo, who could no longer remember his mother's face or his mistress's name or why bread mattered, held onto two things: the phantom weight of his own boots on stone, and the grip of a blind woman's hand.

"No," he said aloud, and heard how thin his voice had gone, and laughed anyway, because humor was load-bearing and he intended to die bearing it. "You can't have the boots. They're—hah—they're *the only thing I'm wearing*—"

"*Theo,*" Maren warned.

"He started it," Sera said serenely, and squeezed his hand, and poured another armful of herself into the circle.

Because that was the trick of it, he realized, in the lucid flashes between floods—that was Maren's whole craft. The outer circle wasn't just holding the ritual. They were feeding it. Maren gave it her disgrace, the fall from the college, the long bitter walk out of the capital—she poured it in without flinching, and the seal took it gladly. Sera gave it her father's maps, the years of darkness after the blindness, the loneliness—and then, softer, the day a stranger with a smart mouth had stumbled into her workshop and made her laugh for the first time in a year.

Three people remembering together so one doesn't drown alone. But it worked both ways. They were remembering *him* too, holding the shape of him from the outside while the inside emptied, and that was the only reason any of Theo remained when the last of the flood came.

* * *

Near the end—it might have been hours later; time had gone soft as wet paper—the entity changed tactics.

It offered.

*Your mother's face,* it whispered, gentle as snowfall. *I have it. She stood right there, in the square where they burned her, and she looked up, and I drank the look off her face like wine. I can give it back. Every memory. Your mistress's name. Your whole life, restored, whole, yours again. Only stop. Only close the channel. Only let me keep the kingdom.*

Theo hung in the roaring dark of the almost-empty self, and he understood, distantly, that this was the true test—not the tearing, not the flood, but this: temptation offered at the exact moment he had nothing left to resist *with*.

His mother's face. He couldn't remember it. He had burned it himself, weeks ago, to hide from wardens, and he had woken not knowing what he'd lost except that the loss had a shape, and the shape was everything.

*I could have her back,* he thought. *One face. One memory. Is one memory worth a kingdom?*

And then, from far away, through stone and silence and the ruins of himself, he heard Sera singing. An old round, tuneless, half-remembered—the kind of song mapmakers sang on the road to stay warm. Ordinary. Stubborn. Unglamorous and endless.

*Ordinary,* he thought. *Stubborn.*

"No," Theo said—or thought, or was; there wasn't much difference left. "You don't get to sell me my own life back in pieces. It was never yours. None of it was ever yours. Take the kingdom's worth of grief. Choke on it."

And he opened the channel wide, and gave the seal the last of himself: the boots on the frozen road, the feel of the passes under him, the one thread, the whole remaining country of Theo Dunmore, poured out like water into stone—

* * *

The silence afterward lasted three heartbeats. Maybe four.

In that silence, the hum of the fourth stone changed pitch—rose, steadied, and became something like a chord, vast and low, the sound of a wall completing itself around a thing that could never dig out. The pressure in the chamber lifted. The cold under the door withdrew.

Theo lay on the stone and felt absolutely nothing where his life used to be, and found, to his mild surprise, that he was still breathing. Still *someone*. A small someone. A boot-shaped someone.

"Theo?" Maren's face swam over him, tear-tracked and grimy and alive. "Theo, talk to me. What's your name?"

He searched. He searched honestly, thoroughly, the way she'd taught him to search a wound. There was a scar where the answer used to be, smooth and clean.

"Don't know," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. Someone younger. Someone with no past at all. "Don't suppose it matters much. Do I at least get the boots?"

Above them, faintly, through thirty feet of foundation stone, the Feast of the Oath stopped.

All at once. Every goblet, every laugh, every note of music—cut dead, the same total silence the entity had worn like a glove, rolling upward through the keep as the thing inside the king felt its meal turn to stone in its throat and understood, at last, what had been done to it.

Maren went white. "It's unbound," she breathed. "Theo—it's *loose*—"

And the throne room overhead erupted into screaming.
