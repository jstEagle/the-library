## 19. The Empty Throne

Maren had spent eleven years in the royal physician's college learning that the body always told the truth eventually. Hearts lied to their owners. Lungs lied on command. But a pulse under her fingers, a pupil shrinking against candlelight—those never lied. It was why she had trusted bodies over people for most of her adult life, and it was why, standing in the ritual chamber beneath the throne with Theo Dunmore's blood cooling on the fourth oath-stone, she knew before anyone else did that something had gone terribly wrong.

The circle was holding. That was the first thing. Sera stood at the eastern point with her father's maps pressed flat against the stone wall and her blind eyes turned inward, murmuring coordinates like a woman reciting a grocery list. Maren herself held the western point, palms bleeding from the rite's opening cuts, chanting the counter-oath in the old cadence that no living tongue should have remembered but which lived, apparently, in the marrow of every physician trained in the capital's deep archives—another debt the entity would answer for, if it could be made to answer for anything.

And at the center, bound to the stone by silver wire and older law, Theo was pouring himself out.

She could see it happening. Not metaphorically—she could *see* it, the way one sees heat shimmer off summer cobbles. Thin ribbons of light lifted off him like breath in winter: small, unremarkable lights, the color of tallow candles and river water and bread going stale on a counter. Not the blazing gold of kingship memories or the deep violet of old oaths. Just... ordinary light. A lifetime of small things.

"Is he supposed to look like that?" Sera asked quietly.

"Like what?"

"Like someone reading a very long letter and finding it boring."

"He's emptying," Maren said. "It's supposed to hurt more than it looks."

"It looks like laundry day."

"Laundry day," Maren said, "can be very dangerous if you don't respect the hot water."

Above them, muffled through thirty feet of mountain granite and royal masonry, the Feast of the Oath continued. She could hear the faint music of it, the tramp of feet, and beneath it all a sound that had been growing for the past hour—a low vibration in the stone itself, like a hive the size of a cathedral waking up.

The entity knew what they were doing. Of course it did. It had known since Theo walked into the feast hall wearing a dead man's credentials, and it had let him come, because it was arrogant, and arrogance was the one disease Maren knew how to treat.

The vibration stopped.

Then the throne room above them screamed—not with voices, but with architecture. Stone groaned. Somewhere far overhead, a chandelier fell; she heard it as a crystalline shudder through the floor.

"Here we go," said Sera, and turned another page of her map.

* * *

The thing came down into the chamber the way winter comes down a mountainside: not walking, but *arriving*. One moment the stairwell was empty. The next, the king stood upon it—or the shape that had worn the king for three years, tall and correct and terrible, its shadow moving half a heartbeat out of step with its body, the way it always had. Its face was the king's face, but the expression on it belonged to nothing human, the way an expression painted onto a mask belongs to nothing at all.

"You are interrupting a coronation," it said. Its voice was every voice it had ever eaten, layered like sediment. "Three hundred years I have waited behind this seal. Three hundred years of sipping when I was built to *feast*. And you bring me—" it looked at Theo with something like hunger and something like confusion, "—this?"

"This," Maren agreed pleasantly, "is the last seal-bearer. He's very sorry to disappoint you. He mentioned it several times during the descent."

"Theo," Sera said softly, without turning her head. "Keep going. Whatever happens at the edges of the circle, keep going."

Because that was the truth of the rite: the outer circle was not a weapon. It was a fence. All three of them together could not have scratched the thing. They were sheepdogs, and their entire purpose was to keep the wolf talking while the lamb bled itself dry in the middle of the field.

The entity descended the last steps and stopped at the circle's boundary. Maren watched it test the edge—one fingertip extended toward the line of salt and blood and old words—and watched the fingertip blacken and withdraw.

"Clever," it said. "The witches' work. Dead three centuries and still lecturing me." It turned its attention fully to Theo, and even across the chamber, even inside the circle, Maren felt the weight of that gaze like a hand pressing down on her skull. "Boy. You are giving away everything you are. Do you know what I am giving you instead? *Everything.* Every stolen face. Every devoured mind. Your mother is in here." It tapped its borrowed temple. "I can return her to you whole. Her voice, her hands, the smell of her hair. You burned her memory to hide yourself in Gallow's Rest—you don't even know her face anymore. I do. Shall I trade?"

For one horrible moment, the ribbons of light rising from Theo stuttered.

"Don't listen to it," Maren said, and hated how thin her voice came out.

"I wasn't talking to you, physician." The king's mouth smiled. "I remember you. Little Maren Voss, who asked too many questions about the king's sleep. I ate your predecessor in four bites. He tasted of brandy and cowardice. You'd taste of vinegar and stubbornness—I do so hate the flavor."

"I've been told my personality is an acquired taste," Maren said. "Fortunately, tonight isn't about me."

"No." The entity's smile widened, and the smile went on widening past any reasonable boundary of a human mouth. "Tonight is about the meal."

* * *

What happened next, Maren would spend the rest of her life failing to describe accurately.

The entity broke its own patience. It did not cross the circle—it *poured*, streaming over the boundary like smoke finding a crack, and the counter-oath flared white, and for a moment the whole chamber was a held breath. Then it reached Theo, and instead of devouring him, it began to drink the seal directly—drinking what he poured out, gulping down the ribbons of light as fast as they rose, trying to swallow the counter-oath mid-working and turn his sacrifice into its feast.

"No," Maren breathed. "No, no—that's not how—"

Sera's head snapped up. "What? What's happening?"

"It's eating the offering. If it digests the seal before it closes—"

"Can it?"

"I don't know!" Maren admitted, and the honesty of it curdled in her throat. Eleven years of medicine and she had learned exactly one universal lesson: *the body tells the truth eventually*. But she had never treated a patient like this one, and she had no idea whether the thing currently gorging itself on a boy's childhood was capable of choking.

They watched it feed. The king-shape swelled, its shadow spreading across the ceiling like spilled ink, drinking down Theo's small gray-gold lights by the hundreds—the taste of rain on smuggler roads, the weight of a grimoire page curling in fire, a hedge-witch's laugh, six years of a mother whose name was already ash. It drank greedily, gluttonously, and Maren braced for the seal to fail—

And then the entity stopped moving.

Its shadow shuddered. Its borrowed face contorted into an expression no king had ever worn: bafflement. Genuine, almost childlike bafflement.

"What," it said, "is *this*?"

"What?" Sera whispered.

"It's—" The thing gagged. Actually gagged, the king's body bending double at the waist like a drunk after bad shellfish. "It won't go down. None of it goes *down*."

Maren stared, and then—slowly, wonderfully—began to understand.

The entity fed on gifted minds. On power. On the bright, concentrated distillations of magic: potent, rich, *dense*. That was its diet, its nature, its three-hundred-year discipline. And Theo Dunmore—apprentice smuggler, hedge-witch's boy, a young man of modest talent and considerable stubbornness—was feeding it a diet of *everything else*. The feel of cold boots. The boredom of long roads. The exact quality of grief for a woman whose face you could no longer picture, and the smaller griefs stacked underneath it: burnt porridge, a dog that died, a friend who chose coin over loyalty, the thousand tiny undramatic weights that make up an actual human life.

Power was a banquet. This was *grain*. Endless, humble, indigestible grain.

"It can't digest him," Maren said aloud, and started laughing, and couldn't stop, because it was either laugh or weep and there was still work to do. "Sera—he's not powerful enough! His whole gift wouldn't fill a thimble! So it's getting *him*. All of him. Every mundane, stubborn, unglamorous piece, and there is no bottom to it!"

"How much of him is there?" Sera demanded.

"I don't think anyone's ever measured!"

Inside the circle, the entity convulsed. The king's body swelled and shrank, swelled and shrank, its shadow thrashing against the walls, and from somewhere inside it came sounds—not screams, worse: *complaints*. The whining of a creature encountering, for the first time in three centuries, something it could neither use nor escape.

"Stop," it rasped. "*Stop.* Take your memories back. Take the bargain—your mother's face, boy, her *voice*, I'll give you—"

Theo didn't answer. Couldn't, probably; his lips had gone gray and his breathing had gone shallow, but his hands stayed flat on the oath-stone, and the little lights kept rising off him like steam off a morning field. Steady. Patient. Stubborn as a mule in January.

"He's not listening," Sera reported, with something dangerously close to pride. "He's doing the thing where he doesn't listen. I only met him yesterday and I already know the thing."

"Yes," Maren said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The unraveling took nine minutes. Maren counted, because counting was what she did when the alternative was screaming.

It began at the entity's edges. The shadow frayed first, wisps of darkness peeling away and dissolving into nothing—not destroyed, just *unwanted*, the way fog unwants itself at noon. Then the king's body began to shed the wrongness: the too-wide smile collapsing back into merely a mouth, the half-beat-late shadow snapping back into alignment, vertebra by vertebra, until what stood in the circle was simply a man. Old. Gaunt. Hollowed to the walls and shaking, but *his own*, and weeping without any sound at all, tears running down a face that hadn't been his own face for three years.

The entity made one last sound as it went. Maren expected rage. She got something quieter and far more unsettling: bewilderment. A vast, ancient hunger asking, in the voice of a child presented with a plate of boiled turnips, *why would anyone want to be this?*

Then it came apart entirely, thread by thread, and the chamber was full of drifting motes of ordinary light, and silence, and the smell of ozone and old stone.

The king—the man—crumpled.

Maren broke the circle and ran.

* * *

He weighed almost nothing. Three years of being fed upon will do that; she had charted the decline herself once, in her former life, recording a monarch who ate sumptuously and starved anyway. Now she knelt on cold stone with his head in her lap and found his pulse: rapid, thready, arrhythmic—but *present*. Humanly present. A pulse with no second heartbeat hiding underneath it, no wrongness in the rhythm, just a dying man's honest heart doing its failing best.

"Your Majesty," she said. "Look at me. How many fingers?"

His eyes opened. For the first time since she'd fled the capital, they were only eyes—tired, frightened, mortal eyes. He looked at her raised hand with genuine effort.

"Three," he croaked. "Who... who are you?"

"Maren Voss. I used to be your physician. Before you fired me for noticing you didn't sleep."

"I don't remember firing you."

"No," Maren said gently. "You weren't home."

Something moved in his face—horror arriving ahead of understanding, understanding arriving ahead of grief. His hand crept up and touched his own cheek, as though checking that it belonged to him.

"How long?" he whispered.

"Three years."

"Three *years*." He closed his eyes. When he opened them again they were wet. "There was a feast. There was—there's always a feast. Did I..." His gaze drifted to the center of the chamber, to the oath-stone, to the boy still bound to it.

Maren followed his gaze, and the triumph in her chest turned to ice.

"Sera," she said. "Sera, check Theo."

But Sera was already there, kneeling, two fingers pressed to Theo's throat, her map forgotten beside her. She was very quiet for a long moment. Long enough that Maren's heart performed maneuvers it had no business surviving.

"He has a pulse," Sera said finally. "Slow. Very slow. Like... like a river in winter. Under the ice." She looked up, and her blind eyes were aimed precisely, unbearably, at Maren. "How much of him poured out?"

"All of it was supposed to pour out. That's the rite. He gives the seal his memories so the thing can't—" Maren stopped. Because the rite had specified a surrender, yes, but a *shaped* surrender, a sealed vessel poured along a channel—and the entity had interrupted the shaping. Had forced Theo to dump everything at once, unsorted, unchanneled. Everything. Not the portion the seal required. *Everything.*

She crawled to him. Her knees left bloody prints on the stone, and she didn't care.

"Theo," she said. "Theo Dunmore. Can you hear me?"

His eyelids fluttered. For a moment she thought he surfaced—and then his brow furrowed, and he said, in a voice slurred and distant and terribly, terribly polite:

"Who's Theo?"

The chamber went silent except for the king's ragged breathing.

"That's normal," Maren said quickly—to Sera, to the king, mostly to herself. "Disorientation after a working is normal. Memory returns in stages. Give him a minute. Give him—Theo. It's Maren. We escaped wardens together. You led us through a collapsing mine and complained about it the entire time, which frankly was excessive given that you insisted on the mine."

He blinked at her. There was nothing behind the blink. No wariness, no recognition, none of the wary competence she had come to rely on like a load-bearing wall.

"Do I know you?" he asked. "You seem... upset. I'm sorry. Should I know you?"

Sera made a small sound. Maren realized she was gripping Theo's wrist hard enough to bruise and made herself let go.

"Give him time," she said again. "The anchor charm—he bought a memory-anchor in Gallow's Rest, before Ilsa sold us out. Check his pockets. Whatever it holds, whatever thread it kept—"

Sera's hands moved over him, quick and practiced, and stopped at the cord around his neck. She lifted the charm free. It was warm, faintly glowing, holding—she pressed it into Theo's palm and folded his fingers closed around it, guiding his touch the way she guided everything, patiently, without sight.

"What do you feel?" Sera asked him.

Theo frowned. Frowned harder. Then, slowly, wonderingly:

"Cold air. High places. Stone under my boots, and a long way down on either side. The passes." He opened his eyes. "Why do I know the passes? Who taught me the passes?"

"You did," Sera said softly. "You taught yourself. It's the one thing nobody gave you and nothing took away."

He looked at the charm in his fist. He looked at the two women kneeling over him and the ancient king weeping on the floor. And then he did the most Theo-like thing Maren had ever witnessed, in the body of a boy who no longer knew his own name: he sat up, wincing, and asked,

"Is everyone else alright? Only, everyone keeps crying, and I feel like I should be checking."

Maren laughed. It came out cracked and wet and closer to the other thing than she would ever admit.

"We're fine," she lied, warmly, professionally. "Everyone is completely fine."

Overhead, through thirty feet of stone, the music of the Feast of the Oath had stopped. In its place: shouting. Running feet. The particular chaos of a court discovering, all at once and mid-toast, that its king had fallen—and that half the nobles present were sagging in their chairs like puppets with cut strings, hollowed vessels suddenly empty, their patron unraveled.

The kingdom, Maren thought, hauling Theo upright with one arm and supporting a dying king with the other, while Sera gathered her maps, was about to have a great many questions. About the hunt. About the Proclamation. About what to do with a throne that had finally, truly, emptied.

And the only person alive who understood the answers had just asked her, with genuine courtesy, what her name was.

"Come on," she said, steering them all toward the stairs. "Up we get. The hard part's over."

She believed it, too, right up until the doors at the top of the stairwell burst open.
