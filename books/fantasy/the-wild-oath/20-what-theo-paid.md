## 20. What Theo Paid

The first thing was the smell.

Beeswax and boiled linen, crushed yarrow, something bitter underneath—willow bark, maybe, or whatever physicians steeped when they wanted a room to smell like they were winning. Theo knew it the way you know a song's chorus without knowing its verses. His body understood it. The rest of him was a locked house with all the furniture missing.

He opened his eyes.

A ceiling. Whitewashed beams, one of them cracked like a river delta. A window with frost ferning up the glass. Somewhere below, a kettle argued with a fire.

*You are in a bed*, he told himself. *You are alive.* Both facts arrived separately, neither willing to vouch for the other.

He tried to remember his name.

That should have been easy. That should have been the first thing anyone owns, the coin sewn into the hem of the self. He reached for it and found a scar instead—not on his skin but somewhere behind his eyes, a smooth place where something had been cut away and the edges drawn neatly together. He knew it was a name-shaped absence the way an amputee knows weather coming. It ached in a specific place for a specific reason, even though the reason itself was gone.

*Theo,* said a voice that wasn't a voice, deep in the marrow. Not remembered. Just... left behind, like chalk dust on a slate someone wiped too thoroughly.

"Theo," he said aloud, testing it. It fit his mouth. It did not fit anything else.

The door opened.

* * *

She was tall and gray-streaked and moved like a woman who had spent years being the calmest person in rooms full of screaming, which meant she moved like a physician. She stopped when she saw him sitting up. Something crossed her face—relief, and under the relief, grief being held very carefully by the scruff of its neck.

"You're awake." She set down her tray with the deliberateness of someone who has dropped trays before. "How much do you have?"

"That depends," Theo said. His voice came out rusted. "On how much there was."

"Ah." She pulled a stool to the bedside and sat, folding her hands as if settling in for a long diagnosis. "Then let's do this properly. I'm Maren. You know me—or you did. I'd ask if that means anything, but your face is already answering no."

"I'm sorry," Theo said, because it seemed like the thing to say, and then, because honesty felt suddenly precious—the only currency he had any of—"I don't know why I'm sorry. I just feel like I owe you."

"You don't owe me." Her mouth did something complicated. "You paid enough for both of us. More than both of us."

"What did I pay?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Outside, wind worried at the eaves. Somewhere in the building a door opened and closed, footsteps, a murmur of voices—life going on around them with the cheerful indifference life is famous for.

"You don't remember the throne room," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"No."

"The stones beneath it?"

"No."

"Do you remember—" she hesitated, and he watched her decide, deliberately, not to finish the sentence. "Never mind. One thing at a time. What *do* you have? Tell me everything. Small things count double."

Theo closed his eyes and took inventory the way a man takes inventory after a fire, lifting charred boards to see what survived underneath.

"Boots," he said slowly. "I have boots. I mean—I remember boots. Walking. Mountain passes. Snow squeaking underfoot, and the way the road smells after rockfall, like struck flint. There's a smuggler's switchback above a frozen river where you can hear the ice talking to itself at night. I don't know how I know that. I just do. My feet know every mile of it."

"Good," Maren said softly. "That's good."

"There's a woman." He frowned. "Not—you. Someone else. I can't see her face. But I know she laughed like she was getting away with something, and she smelled of woodsmoke and pennyroyal, and she taught me..." He reached. The reaching hurt. "...something. Something important. Her hands were always ink-stained." His eyes stung. "I don't know her name."

"Don't force it," Maren said quickly. "Theo—may I call you Theo? The charm gave us that much."

"Theo. Yes. It's apparently mine."

"It is yours." She said it firmly, like setting a bone. "Whatever else went into the seal, that stayed. Hold onto it."

"There was a fire," he said. "I think. A burning. And a mother—" The word came up out of him raw and bleeding. "I had a mother. I know I had a mother the way I know I have hands. But there's nothing attached to it. No face. No voice. Just the fact of her, like a nail hole in a wall where a picture used to hang." He pressed the heel of his palm against his eyes. "Why does it hurt more, not remembering? Shouldn't it hurt less?"

Maren was quiet for a while. When she spoke, her voice had gone gentle in a way that suggested gentleness cost her something.

"Grief doesn't live in the memories," she said. "It lives deeper than that. You can burn the library, but the foundations remember the weight."

* * *

They took stock together over the following days, the way survivors take stock of granaries. Maren kept a ledger—an actual paper ledger, ruled and neat—and each morning she would read back yesterday's findings and ask what remained.

The results were grim arithmetic.

His mistress: gone. He knew the word *mistress* applied, knew it the way you know a coat once fit you, but the woman inside it had been carried off whole. His childhood: mostly gone, a few splinters—a taste of honey on bread, a dog barking somewhere, the color of a door. His mother: the nail hole. The road years: strangely intact, the smuggler's passes living in his legs like birds in a hollow tree. Gallow's Rest—he tested the name and got only smoke. Sera: a name that produced a shape, a blind mapmaker, fingers reading ridges of paper like braille—but no conversations, no kindnesses, no her.

And the war itself. The oath-eater. The king. The feast. The stone beneath the throne. All of it mist, all of it secondhand now, a story Maren told him about a stranger who happened to wear his body and sleep in his bed.

"So I won," Theo said one evening, staring at the ceiling. "You keep saying I won."

"You won."

"I saved the kingdom. Unbound a thing wearing the king's face. Filled some great seal with—" he waved vaguely at his own head "—whatever I had left."

"With ordinary memory," Maren said. "Unglamorous and endless. It couldn't digest it. Nothing so hungry can stomach the ordinary—it wants the shining stuff, the gift, the oath. You fed it bread when it wanted gold, and it starved." A pause. "Sera's phrase. She says it better."

"And the price was—" he gestured at himself, at the locked house, the empty rooms "—this."

"Yes."

"Hm." He considered the ceiling a while longer. "Do you know what the worst part is? It's not the losing. It's that I can't even be proud. Some man won a kingdom, and I'm just the fellow renting his bones afterward. It's like arriving late to my own funeral and finding everyone agreed it was a lovely service."

Maren laughed—short, surprised at herself, quickly smothered. But she laughed. "There he is," she murmured, almost to herself.

"Who?"

"Nothing." She rose, gathered her tray. "Eat your broth. Healing is tedious work and you're behind schedule."

* * *

The anchor charm hung on a cord around his neck, and it was the strangest object he owned, which was impressive considering he owned almost nothing and could account for all of it.

It was small—a knot of mountain iron and rowan wood, worn smooth by thumbs that weren't his anymore. When he held it, one thread ran through him, bright and singular: the passes. Boots on frozen road. The flint smell. Ice talking to itself at night. Whoever had charged it had known exactly which thread to save, and Theo suspected—without evidence, without memory, purely on the evidence of his own stubborn feet—that the man who made that choice had chosen the part of himself he could least bear to lose.

He asked Maren about it once.

"You charged it yourself," she said. "Before the end. The fence in Gallow's Rest—" she caught herself, saw the blankness, and rerouted without flinching. "Someone in Gallow's Rest finished it. But the thread inside is yours. Your own working. We found it still warm when we dug you out from under the throne."

"Dug me out?"

"You were holding the stone," Maren said. "Both hands. Like a man drowning holds a spar." She looked away, toward the window, toward the frost. "We thought for three days we'd buried you along with the thing. Sera wouldn't leave. She sat with you and talked to you—told you about maps, about her father, about anything, everything. Said hearing was the last sense to go and she'd be damned if the last thing you heard was silence."

"She sounds..." He searched for the shape of her again, got the fingers, the paper, the patience. "She sounds like someone I would have liked very much."

"You did," Maren said. "Very much. She'll remind you of all of it. She's already started. She keeps a list."

"A list of what?"

"Of things you did that you'll want back someday." Maren's mouth curved. "Currently it runs to forty-one items. Item nine is 'the time he sang badly on purpose to make a dying fence laugh.' She swears you'll want that one most of all."

Theo turned the charm over in his fingers. Warm iron, patient wood.

"Will I?" he asked quietly. "Want them back?"

"You'll earn new ones," Maren said. "It's slower. But they hold better."

* * *

At night, sometimes, the dreams came.

They weren't memories—that was the cruel trick of them. They were the *shapes* of memories, the holes where memories had been, and his sleeping mind walked through them like a man walking his own house in the dark, hand trailing along walls, finding doorways that opened onto nothing.

In one dream there was a grimoire burning page by page in a mountain square, ash rising past lanterns, and someone was watching from a doorway with love and terror braided together in their posture, and Theo woke with ash-taste in his mouth and tears already cold on his cheeks, mourning a person whose face he had never been given the chance to forget because he'd never been given the chance to keep it.

In another, a market. Smoke. Someone calling a name across the chaos—*his* name—and a woman turning, and then white, and then nothing, forever nothing, and he woke gripping the charm hard enough to bruise, whispering *I'm sorry, I'm sorry* to someone he could not picture, for something he could not recall, and meaning it down to the floor of himself.

Grief without content. Mourning as pure form. It was, he decided, either the cruelest thing he had ever endured or the proof that he had ever loved at all—possibly both, probably both.

He learned to sit with the dreams rather than fight them. Learned their geography. Learned that if he lay still and let them wash through, they left something small behind—not knowledge, exactly, but orientation. The way a compass doesn't tell you where home is, only which direction it lies in.

Home, apparently, was mountains. Home was a road under his boots and snow-squeak and ice talking to itself. Whatever else had been burned out of him, that thread held taut.

* * *

On the twelfth day, Sera came.

He heard her before he saw her—her stick tapping the ward's flagstones in a rhythm too confident for blindness, more like a drummer keeping time than a cane feeling for cracks. She was small and sharp-faced and her eyes, unfocused, fixed on him with unnerving precision anyway, as if she navigated by some sense that didn't require light.

She stopped at the foot of his bed. Looked at him—or aimed herself at him—for a long moment.

"Well," she said. "You're taller than forty-one items' worth of trouble ought to be."

"Sera," Theo said. The name came out steady. The shape of her—fingers, paper, patience—stood up inside him and put on a hat, ready to work, even without a face or a voice to fill it.

"You know my name."

"Maren told me. And there's—" he touched his sternum. "There's something. Not a memory. Just the impression that knowing you was good. Is that strange?"

Sera's expression did something complicated and tender. "No," she said. "That's the truest thing left in you, probably." She dragged a stool over with her stick, hooked it, sat. "Right. Item one. When you first came to my father's old workshop, soaked through and half-frozen, you apologized for dripping on the maps. Dripping. On the maps. As if the maps were the tragedy of the day."

"I wouldn't do that."

"You absolutely would. Item two. You called the oath-eater 'a large unpleasant landlord' to its face during the unbinding, and the entire ritual circle nearly collapsed because Sera—" she tapped her own chest "—laughed at a critical juncture and Maren had to shout a corrective verse."

Theo blinked. Then, helplessly, he grinned. It felt strange in his face, like a tool recovered from a drawer, slightly rusted but fundamentally sound. "I said that? To the thing wearing the king?"

"To the thing wearing the king. While bound to a stone. Bleeding." Sera leaned forward. "Do you see what I'm doing, Theo? I'm telling you who you are, since you can't. And here's the secret nobody tells you about memory—" she raised one finger "—it isn't only what's in your head. It's also what's in everyone who loves you. You lost your library. Fine. We are all of us walking shelves, and between us we can lend you most of a book."

Theo looked down at the charm in his hands, at the thread of mountain road running through it, taut and bright.

"And when the lending runs out?" he asked. "When you've told me everything and it's all just stories about a stranger? Stories aren't the same as—"

"No," Sera agreed. "They aren't. But here's item forty-two." Her voice changed; the humor dropped away like a cloak, and underneath it was something fierce and certain. "This morning, before dawn, a rider came up from the capital. The king is dying—truly dying now, mortal and human and out of time. And the council Maren's been building, the one the king wants to trust the realm to—they've sent for you. By name. *Theo*. Because whatever else is missing, the gifted folk coming out of hiding these past weeks keep asking for one man by name, a man none of them have ever met, and the stones in the old roads hum when you walk past them, and Sera's maps show a line of light running from this ward straight to every freed oath-stone in the kingdom."

She stood. Took up her stick.

"They're asking for the man who won," she said gently. "And I intend to introduce them to the man who's *here*—whoever he turns out to be. So drink your broth, item forty-three is that you hate broth but eat it anyway because Maren glares, and tomorrow we start walking. You still know the passes, don't you?"

Theo's feet answered before his head could. He could feel them: snow-squeak, flint-smell, ice talking to itself at night, a road going on and on into the high white country, waiting for him like a friend who never asks questions.

"Yeah," he said, and found he was smiling at the frost on the window, at the wide white world beyond it that he couldn't remember and didn't need to. "Yeah. My boots know the way."
