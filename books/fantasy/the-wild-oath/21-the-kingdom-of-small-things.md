## 21. The Kingdom of Small Things

### I.

Sera Vane had mapped coastlines she'd never seen and mountain ranges she would never climb, but the strangest cartography of her life began the morning the Proclamation came down off the post in Gallow's Rest.

She knew it had happened before anyone read it aloud to her. The town made a sound—a collective exhale, like a house settling—and beneath her palms the paper of her newest map trembled with the footsteps of people running, not away this time, but *toward* something. She could feel crowds through her feet if she paid attention. She'd learned that trick from her father, who claimed a good mapmaker listened for where the weight of the world gathered.

"Read it to me again," she said to the boy who'd brought the news, mostly because he clearly wanted to be asked.

"'By order of the Council Regent,'" the boy recited, breathless, "'the Proclamation of the Hunt is rescinded in its entirety. All persons accused under its articles are pardoned. All wards upon the practice of the old gift are lifted, save such as protect the unwilling—'" He stumbled over the next part. "There's more. It says the stones are free. The oath-stones. It says they're *singing*."

Sera ran her thumb along the edge of her table. Beneath the floorboards of her shop, faint as a pulse through wool, something was indeed humming. It had been humming since midwinter, when a boy with half his mind burned out of him had stood on the fourth stone beneath a throne and poured everything he was into the dark.

"They've been singing for months," she said. "The rest of you are only just learning to listen."

She spent that first week doing what she always did: drawing. But the map that grew under her stylus was unlike any her father had ever taught her to make. She marked the first stone in the high passes, where the snow never quite buried the warmth. The second, in the ruined mine country, where wardens' hounds still occasionally wandered in circles, confused by roads that no longer led where they used to. The third, on the moor where ghosts had once asked a frightened boy an impossible question. And at the center of it all, drawn small because she did not yet trust herself to draw it large, the fourth stone beneath the capital—the one that had cost Theo Dunmore nearly everything he was.

Around each stone, she drew circles. Not decorative ones. Each morning she went out—or sent the boy, or bribed a neighbor's child with honey bread—to ask the folk nearby what they'd dreamed, what they remembered better than they used to, what griefs had gone strangely lighter overnight. The circles grew around the stones like rings in wood.

"It's not healing," Maren said when Sera showed her the work, weeks later, in the capital. "Or—it is, but not the way physicians mean it. It's more like... scar tissue forming. The kingdom was cut open. This is the cut closing."

"Then my map is a scar," Sera said, pleased. "My father would be furious. He thought maps should show things as they ought to be."

"And what do you think?"

"I think," Sera said, tracing the ring around the third stone where a woman had reported remembering her grandmother's lullaby for the first time in thirty years, "that ought-to-be starts with what actually happened. Somebody has to write it all down before it smooths over."

That was Sera's gift, though she'd have laughed at the word: she noticed. She noticed the gifted cooper from Thornfell parish who came to the capital asking, hesitantly, whether there was work for someone who could hear timber groan before it split. She noticed the two sisters from the fen country who arrived with nothing but a kettle that never boiled dry and the cautious look of animals deciding whether a clearing was safe. She noticed that they all, without exception, asked first not about pardon or property or justice, but about *work*—because a hunger for usefulness is the last thing persecution burns out of a person, and sometimes the first thing that grows back.

She started a ledger. On the cover she wrote, in her careful cartographer's hand: *A Map of What Remains.*

The boy asked her why she didn't call it something grander.

"Because grander comes later," Sera said, "if we're lucky. First you chart what's actually there." She smiled at the page. "And what's actually there is a kettle, a cooper, two sisters, and a great many people learning to sleep through the night again. That's a kingdom too. Arguably the important one."

### II.

Maren Coltheart had been a disgraced royal physician for exactly as long as it took the Council Regent to realize they needed one, at which point she became, by unanimous and slightly terrified vote, *Royal Physician Emerita, Restorer of the College, and Advisor on Matters Pertaining to the Oath*—a title so long she suspected someone had designed it as a joke.

She kept the disgrace pinned above her desk. Literally: the old writ of banishment, framed in cheap pine, hanging where petitioners could see it.

"People ask me about it," she told her first class of students—eleven of them, three of them openly gifted, four of them formerly hunted, all of them nervous. "They think it's bitterness. It isn't. It's a reminder that institutions fail, and that the failure is rarely the institution's fault. Institutions don't decide anything. People do. Usually tired people. Usually frightened ones." She tapped the frame. "You will be tired and frightened someday, and someone will hand you a writ, and you will have to choose. I'm hoping the frame helps you choose better than I did."

It was, she reflected, strange how quickly the college reassembled once permission existed. The building itself had stood empty only two years, but it had been gutted—records burned, instruments confiscated, the anatomy theater converted to a granary. Yet within a month of the rescinding, former students appeared at her door like seedlings after rain. Some came openly. Some came hooded, at night, and stood in the doorway a long time before knocking.

One of those was a woman named Bree Halloway, who had spent six years as a hedge-healer in the Thornfell passes, delivering babies and setting bones with hands that warmed themselves, which was either a miracle or evidence, depending on who was watching. She stood in Maren's office twisting her cap and said, "I don't know if I'm allowed. There wasn't ever a rule against me exactly, but there weren't rules *for* me either."

"There are now," Maren said. "I'm writing them myself, which should terrify you. Sit down."

"What are the rules?"

"Rule one: nobody gets burned for what they were born being. Rule two: nobody gets trusted simply for it either. Gift is a tool. So is a scalpel. Both can save or kill, and neither makes you wise." Maren leaned back. "Rule three, and this is the hard one: the college admits what it doesn't know. For centuries we pretended the gift didn't exist, which meant we know almost nothing true about it. Half your education will be unlearning lies, and the other half will be admitting ignorance politely. Can you live with that?"

Bree considered. "Better than living with the other thing," she said, and sat down.

The hardest work was not the teaching. The hardest work was the archive. In the dungeons below the keep, in the records rooms of the old hunt, Maren found lists—hundreds of names, gathered by informants over years, the arithmetic of betrayal written in a clerk's neat hand. Most of the named were dead. Some had fled. A handful had informed on others to buy their own safety, and their names sat in both columns, accuser and accused, which Maren found she could not judge as cleanly as she wished.

She did not burn the lists. Sera would have approved of that; Sera believed in writing down what actually happened.

Instead Maren copied every name into a new book, both columns together, no verdict attached, and titled it *Debts Written in Skin*, after something a boy had once said to her on a frozen road. Someday, she wrote in the preface, someone would need to reckon with this honestly—not to punish, not to forgive, but to understand. She suspected it would take longer than any of them had.

In the evenings she climbed the keep stairs to the king's chambers, where the dying man who had been a hollow vessel for three years was learning, slowly, painfully, to be a person again.

"How is the pain today?" she asked, as she asked every evening.

"Loud," said King Aldric—or the man who had been Aldric before the oath ate him, and who was himself again but for the holes. He managed a smile. "But loud is mine. Do you understand? Three years of silence, and now everything hurts and I am *grateful*, because the hurting is happening to somebody."

"You're the worst patient I've ever had," Maren said fondly, "and I once treated a man who insisted his leg was haunted."

"It probably was. You were there for worse." He looked out the window at the city. "Coltheart. When I'm gone—they'll want a coronation. They'll want it badly. Crowns make people feel safe."

"And?"

"And I'm going to tell them no." He said it lightly, but Maren heard the iron under it. "I wore a crown while something else looked out of my eyes. I watched—I remember fragments, Coltheart, I remember *fragments* of what it did with my hands. No child of mine, no cousin, no convenient duke. Not until we understand what the oath is and who it feeds. Let the council hold it together. Let them argue. Argument is healthier than worship."

"You'll have the whole nobility in revolt."

"Half of them are still recovering from being hollowed," the king said. "Let them revolt quietly, after supper."

### III.

The council convened for the first time in the spring, in the old audience hall, and it went approximately as well as seven proud people in one room ever goes, which is to say: terribly, then wonderfully, then terribly again, in a rhythm Maren privately called the weather.

The regency council of seven: a stonemason from the guilds, a fen-farmer elected by nothing except that the farmers refused to send anyone else, a warden captain who had resigned rather than continue the hunt, a scholar from what remained of the university, Sera Vane—who attended by letter at first, then in person once she discovered the council chamber had excellent acoustics—an elderly priestess of the small shrines whom nobody had invited and everybody was afraid to dismiss, and, sitting in the corner with his boots muddy from the road, taking notes nobody had assigned him, a young man with a stranger's calm in his eyes.

The mason and the farmer fought about grain tariffs. The warden captain and the priestess fought about whether the wardens should swear new oaths or none at all. The scholar proposed a committee to study the question of committees, and was, remarkably, not laughed out of the room, because everyone was too exhausted.

Through it all, the king watched from a wheeled chair near the fire, wrapped in blankets, looking like a man attending his own wake and enjoying it immensely.

On the third day of argument, the priestess—Mother Cassia, who had a voice like gravel in honey—stood up during a dispute over succession law and said, "You're all trying to build a roof before you've agreed on walls."

"We've agreed on walls," the mason said. "The walls are the council."

"The walls are *nothing*," Mother Cassia said, "until you decide what the crown is *for*. You keep arguing positions as though the throne were empty because the king is dying. Children, the throne has been empty for years. Something sat in it, but nothing *reigned*. Before you fight about who inherits, you must answer: inherit *what*? Power? Then you're breeding another monster. Service? Then stop calling it a crown. Stewardship?" She looked around the room. "Decide. Slowly. Wrongly, even, the first few times. But decide *on purpose*."

A silence followed, the good kind, the kind where thinking happens.

It was the fen-farmer, of all people, who broke it. "My wife keeps bees," he said. "Everyone always asks whose honey it is. Wrong question. Ask who tends the hive." He shrugged. "Maybe that's mad. But I've seen a crown up close lately, and I'll tell you what I saw: a hole."

So it was decided—not finally, because nothing in that room was ever final—but provisionally, experimentally, the way a physician tries a treatment and watches: the council would rule, the crown would wait vacant, guarded, studied, and perhaps in ten years or fifty the kingdom would decide whether anyone should wear it at all. The king, asked for his blessing, laughed until he coughed.

"My blessing? I'm giving you my *apology*. I sat in that chair and let a thing eat my kingdom while I slept. Guard the empty seat better than I guarded the full one." He paused, and his voice gentled. "And thank the boy. Whatever else you decide, decide it knowing that a boy with no name left bought you the time to have this argument. Don't waste it on grain tariffs forever."

"We'll rotate the tariff arguments," the scholar offered. "Seasonally."

"There," the king said. "Statesmanship."

### IV.

Theo Dunmore lived at the edge of things.

Not unhappily—he'd been surprised to discover that edges suited him. He kept a room above a farrier's shop in the low city, took work hauling and repairing and occasionally, when the farrier wasn't looking, coaxing a stubborn forge hotter with a fingertip and a word the mistress had taught him, back when his memory was whole enough to hold words. He was known in the neighborhood as a quiet fellow, reliable, odd about doors—always touching the lintel on his way past, some habit nobody questioned—and possessed of a laugh that startled people because it arrived so rarely and then all at once.

He did not remember most of what he'd done. He knew it the way you know a story about someone else, told by many tellers who disagreed on details. Maren told him pieces. Sera told him more, with maps. The king himself had summoned him once, early on, and gripped his hand with both of the king's thin ones and said, "I don't know what I owe you. Nobody does. That's the shape of it, isn't it? A debt too large to see." And Theo had said, "Then don't pay it. Spend it forward," a phrase he did not recognize as his own until it was already out of his mouth, and which had apparently been his mother's saying, according to Sera, who had researched everything.

Grief without memories was a strange country. He mourned his mistress the way you mourn a house you lived in as an infant: with certainty of love and no pictures. He mourned his mother the same way, plus one sound—one single sound, held somewhere deeper than memory, that he did not speak of to anyone, because they would want to examine it, and he wanted only to keep it.

What he had instead of his past was other people's gratitude, which is a poor substitute and also, he was learning, not nothing. The cooper from Thornfell sought him out to shake his hand and couldn't explain why. The kettle-sisters left soup outside his door for a month. Children in the street, who had decided among themselves that he was somebody important, followed him at a respectful distance in a solemn pack, and he had given up objecting.

He attended the council sessions because Sera asked him to, and because listening felt like a way of paying attention to a life he couldn't recall living. He took notes in a hand he half-recognized. Sometimes a phrase in the debates snagged on something in him—a word about oaths, a mention of the passes—and he'd feel a tug behind his sternum, like a hook in deep water.

"You could have a place here," Maren told him once, walking back from the hall. "A real one. The college needs someone who understands the stones from the inside. The council listens to you more than they admit. You could—" She stopped herself, which was unlike her.

"I could become somebody," Theo said. "New name, new work, new history. I know." He smiled at her. "Give me time. I'm only just finished being nobody. It turns out nobody is a profession, and I was very good at it."

"You were terrible at it," Maren said. "You got found by every bounty-taker between here and the moor."

"That's fair."

They walked in companionable silence past the rebuilt market stalls, past the shrine where someone had left flowers on the fourth stone's marker, past a knot of gifted children—actual children, playing openly, sparks jumping between their fingers like a game of catch—being loudly scolded by a grandmother who was herself making the sparks jump higher.

"Do you regret it?" Maren asked quietly. "What you gave?"

Theo considered it honestly, which was the only way he knew how to consider anything anymore, having lost the capacity for self-deception along with everything else.

"I can't miss what I can't remember," he said. "Some days that feels like mercy. Some days it feels like the cruelest bargain ever struck—grieving a life I can't even prove was mine. But then..." He touched his chest, over the sternum, where the tug lived. "Something in me knows. Knows it was worth it. Not the details. Just—the worth. That survived. Isn't that strange? Everything else went, and the *worth* stayed."

"No," Maren said. "I don't think that's strange at all. I think that might be the whole point of you."

### V.

Spring turned toward summer, and the kingdom of small things put down roots the way small things do: stubbornly, sideways, in cracks.

The cooper installed himself in a yard by the river and became, within a season, famous among shipwrights for timbers that never failed. The kettle-sisters opened a tea house that was quietly understood to be the safest room in the city for anyone with shaking hands and secrets. Bree Halloway graduated first in the college's restored class and took a post in the Thornfell passes, of all places, sending back letters that described delivering babies by lantern-light with warm hands, and added, in a postscript that made Maren laugh aloud: *The mountains are singing. You get used to it. Eventually you find yourself singing back, and then you stop apologizing for it.*

The king declined, week by week, with enormous dignity and an ongoing feud with his physician over the matter of broth. ("Emerita Coltheart. If I die, let it be of something interesting.") The council argued its way into competence. Sera's map of what remains filled three tables and required its own room, and visitors came to stand before it silently, finding their own villages, their own stones, their own names in her ledger of the returned.

And on a bright morning in early summer, Theo woke with the tug behind his sternum pulling harder than it had pulled in months.

He lay still and let it speak. It wasn't pain. It was older than pain—something like a road remembering a traveler. He dressed, touched the lintel on his way out, and walked without deciding to walk, up through the low city, out past the gates, onto the north road, his boots finding a pace his mind had no opinion about.

At the fork where the north road met the old smuggler's track—the track itself long legal now, renamed, signed, ruined with respectability—his feet stopped. The mountains stood up against the sky, blue and patient. The first stone was up there, in the high passes, under snow that no longer quite covered the warmth.

He didn't know why he had come here. He didn't know why today.

But standing at the fork, hand resting on the old milestone, Theo felt something loosen in him—some knot tied a year ago and never untied—and heard, very distinctly, though no one was near, a sound like wind through a high pass shaped almost like a voice. Almost like a woman's voice. Almost like—

He laughed, surprising a flock of pigeons, wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist, and turned up the smuggler's road, boots finding the old rhythm, body remembering what his mind could not.

Behind him, in the city, the bells rang noon. Ahead, the mountains waited, singing.

He walked.
