## 22. The Wild Oath

The pass above Hollowmere smelled the same as it had a year ago: cold stone, pine resin, and the faint iron tang of snowmelt running somewhere beneath the ice. Theo Dunmore stood at the mouth of the smuggler's road with his pack on his shoulder and no memory whatsoever of why this particular bend in the mountain felt like coming home.

His boots knew.

That was the strange arithmetic of his life now. His mind was a house with most of its rooms emptied and the doors painted over, but his body had kept a private ledger of everything he'd lost. His feet found the safe footing on scree without looking down. His hands went to the handhold on the cliff face before his eyes found it. When the wind shifted and carried the smell of woodsmoke up from the valley, something behind his sternum loosened—grief or relief or both—and he had to stand very still for a moment until it passed.

"You're doing it again," said Sera, catching up beside him, her walking stick tapping a quick rhythm against the stone.

"Doing what?"

"Listening to yourself." She tilted her head, blind eyes turned toward him with that unsettling accuracy she had. "You go quiet and stare at the middle distance like you're waiting for someone to walk out of it."

"Maybe I am." He shrugged. "I'm told I used to be somebody. I keep hoping he'll introduce himself."

Sera snorted—a sound entirely without sympathy, which was one of the many reasons he liked her. "If he does, make him carry his own pack. You've been complaining about yours since Gallow's Rest."

"I have *not*—" He stopped. Considered. "Have I?"

"Twice yesterday. Once about the straps, once about the weight, and once—I counted, because Maren dared me—about how your boots 'remember things your head won't tell you,' delivered in a voice of tremendous tragedy."

Theo laughed despite himself. It still surprised him sometimes, that laughing came so easily now. He'd expected, in the gray weeks after he woke in the physician's ward, to spend the rest of his life as a kind of ghost—present, breathing, but belonging to no one. Instead there had been Sera's dry commentary and Maren's terrible cooking and the slow, patient work of building a person out of what remained.

He had a name. That was more than some got. Whether it was *his* name—whether Theo Dunmore had ever truly been anyone, or only the sum of memories now poured into a seal beneath an empty throne—that question he'd stopped asking around midwinter. The answer changed nothing. The name fit his mouth. He wore it the way he wore his boots: broken in by use, whatever their history.

They walked on. The road narrowed where the mountain pinched it against the sky, and Theo's body did what it always did here—it *sang*. Not magic, exactly. Something older than magic. The smuggler's roads of Thornfell had been worn into the rock by generations of people who could not afford to be seen, and every switchback and hidden shelf of them had been pressed into Theo's muscles during years he could not recall. He knew where the wardens' patrol lanterns hung (a rusted bracket, third bend). He knew which boulder hid a cache (it didn't anymore; he checked; someone had taken the oilcloth and left a courteous cairn). He knew, without knowing how he knew, that if they cut left at the frozen waterfall they would find the shepherd's stair down into the high valley, and that the fourth step from the top was loose, and that you stepped over it, never on it.

He stepped over it.

"There," Sera said softly, behind him. "There it is again. You just moved like water."

"Like an old man with bad knees."

"Like water," she insisted. "Maren says the body keeps what the mind spends. She's writing a paper on it. A very long paper. She read me four pages."

"Heaven help the college."

"Heaven help *me*, she wants me to check her citations."

* * *

Hollowmere came upon them gradually, the way mountain villages do—one crooked roofline, then another, then all of them at once, huddled in the valley like sheep against weather. Theo stopped at the last rise and looked down at it for a long time.

He did not remember living here. He knew he had—the whole kingdom knew the story by now, told and retold in markets and taprooms with more embroidery each season: the apprentice who burned the witch's grimoire page by page, whose buried gift flared and lit every lantern in the square when the Proclamation riders came. In the popular versions he fled barefoot through burning snow pursued by nine wardens. In one version he turned into a stag.

"The truth is less interesting," he'd told a tavern full of listeners in Gallow's Rest, when a ballad-singer had cornered him. "I ran in good boots, and nobody chased me, because nobody knew I mattered yet."

The crowd had booed. Kindly, but they'd booed. People wanted their heroes barefoot.

Below him, Hollowmere was hanging lanterns for the festival—the anniversary of the rescinding of the Proclamation, which the villages of the high country celebrated with a stubbornness that made the capital's official observance look like a tax form. Strings of lights were going up between eaves. Children were hauling sledges of firewood. Someone was already playing a fiddle badly enough that it echoed beautifully off the mountainsides.

And in the square—Theo's breath caught, just slightly—in the square stood the old well, and beside the well, a small stone marker he could see even from this distance, because it had been dressed with ribbons and winter-blooming heather.

Mistress Aldery's grave.

He hadn't known, arriving, that she was buried here. He knew it now the way he knew the loose stair—the knowledge had simply arrived and settled, the way facts about Theo Dunmore tended to do, delivered by strangers or by Sera's maps or by his own hands doing things. She had been buried in Hollowmere by neighbors who owed her deliveries at midwife's fees and curses lifted off cattle, and the village had kept the grave weeded all year, and today they had dressed it in ribbons.

"Come on," Sera said gently. "You can grieve at the bottom of the hill same as the top, and my knees are younger than yours."

"They are not, you're twenty-six."

"And smug about it."

* * *

The village knew him on sight, which was its own strange burden. An old woman pressed a heel of honey bread into his hand and gripped his wrist with fingers like tree roots and said, "She'd be proud, lad," and Theo thanked her and meant it and understood perhaps half of it. A boy of maybe ten fell into step beside him and asked, in the reverent whisper children reserve for people who have survived stories, whether it was true he'd eaten a warden's shadow. Theo considered saying no. Then he considered the boy's face, glowing with the possibility that shadows were edible, and said, "Only the one, and it gave me heartburn for a week."

The boy would carry that to his grave. Good. Let the record show variety.

At Mistress Aldery's grave, the noise of the village fell away—not literally; the fiddle scraped on, the children shouted—but inside Theo, everything went quiet, the way it did in the empty rooms of his mind.

He knelt. Set his palm flat on the cold stone.

*Thank you,* he thought, and then, because it was true and he was tired of pretending otherwise: *I'm angry with you. Still. Sometimes. At three in the morning, mostly.* She had loved him enough to damn him—had caged his gift and sealed his memories and let the binding eat him alive rather than let the oath-eater find him. It had been the right choice. It had nearly killed him. Both of those things lived side by side in him now, like two travelers sharing a narrow bed, each convinced the other was taking too much blanket.

*I forgive you,* he thought. *I think I forgave you at the second stone, when Ilsa died. Or maybe at the first. The stones know. Ask them.*

A ribbon on the marker fluttered. Wind, certainly. Almost certainly wind.

He stayed there a while anyway, kneeling in the trampled snow, while behind him the village prepared its festival and the fiddle found, briefly, the right key.

* * *

Maren met them at the inn at dusk, having come up from the capital two days behind—she never traveled with them anymore, citing the need for "deniability," which everyone understood to mean she couldn't bear to watch Theo visit the graves of people she'd failed to save. She looked tired and sharp-eyed and triumphant, in the specific manner of a woman carrying documents.

"Well?" Theo said.

"Well." She dropped onto the bench across from him, accepted cider without being offered it, and produced a folded packet sealed with the college's new sigil—a mortar and pestle wreathed in rowan, chosen by committee, hated by everyone, which made it perfect. "The council ratified the charter. All seven of them, even Vessing, who argued for three hours that licensing hedge-magic would 'invite disorder.' Sera's maps settled it. Showed them forty villages where the gifted had been quietly keeping wells clean and calves alive for two hundred years, and asked Vessing which of those he'd like to outlaw first."

Theo grinned. "What did he say?"

"He said, and I quote, 'This is not the same thing,' which in council-speak is surrender." She drank, wiped her mouth, and looked at him over the rim of the cup with the expression she saved for diagnoses she didn't like delivering. "There's other news. The king is failing."

Theo set down his bread. "How long?"

"A season. Perhaps two. He's lucid most days now—finally, gloriously his own, after three years of being worn like a coat—but the unbinding took the marrow out of him. He knows. He's cheerful about it, which is somehow worse." Maren turned the cup in her hands. "Yesterday he asked me to sit with him and tell him about the roads. Not state business. Roads. What the passes smell like in thaw. He said he'd ruled a kingdom he never saw and wanted to at least borrow someone else's memory of it."

Theo was quiet a moment. "Did you tell him?"

"I told him about the frozen waterfall, and the shepherd's stair, and the loose fourth step." Her mouth twitched. "He said, 'You're making that up.' I said, 'Your Majesty, I learned it from a man whose body remembers everything his mind has lost, and if that isn't the definition of a kingdom, I don't know what is.'"

"He liked that?"

"He laughed until he coughed, then made me write it down for the council. So congratulations. You're in the historical record as a loose stair."

* * *

They walked out to the first oath-stone the next morning, alone—all three of them, though Sera waited back a respectful dozen paces, because some conversations belonged to fewer people. The anniversary proper. One year since the Feast of the Oath. One year since Theo had knelt beneath the throne and poured himself out, memory by memory, into the seal, until the entity that had worn the king tried to swallow what he gave it and found it could not digest ordinary human remembering—the stubborn, unglamorous, endless flood of a single life honestly given—and unraveled like thread off a spindle.

The stone stood where it had always stood, in its clearing above the village, wearing frost like lace. To anyone else it was a standing rock with old carvings, lichen-eaten, unremarkable. Theo put his hand on it.

Warmth. There was always warmth, now. Since the unbinding, the stones weren't seals anymore—just open doors, humming with the freed circle's echo, the ghosts of those old witches lingering in their work the way a tune lingers in a room after the singer leaves.

"Hello," Theo said. His breath fogged. "It's me. Again. I know I come by too often. Maren says it's grief. I say it's habit. We've agreed to disagree."

The echo stirred, the way it always did—not words, exactly, but a pressure, a presence, old and patient and amused in the way of grandmothers. It had tested him here, a lifetime ago, it seemed. *Would he trade his past for the realm?* And he had failed to answer, and the stone had refused him, and Sera had told him: *the seal doesn't want a hero. It wants a witness.*

In the end, under the throne, it had gotten both.

"I brought you something," Theo said, and dug in his pack, and drew out a small bundle of oilcloth. Inside was a page—not from the grimoire; that ash was long scattered—but written in the same hand, in the careful looping script he'd seen reproduced in Maren's archives: a recipe for a charm of warmth, the very charm he'd traded a caravan master for passage, a hundred years ago last winter. Sera's father's charts had led them to the caravan master's daughter, who had kept her father's ledger, who had kept the charm itself, unused, a curiosity. Theo had copied it out fresh and charged it with a thread of his own gift—the gift that was his again now, unbound, small and warm and *his*.

He tucked the page into the crevice at the stone's base. "For whoever needs it next. Passengers only. No heroes."

The echo moved through the clearing like sunlight through fog, and Theo laughed—because it *was* amusement, unmistakable, the circle's echo approving of his terms.

Then it gave him something.

It had never done this. In a year of visits, the stones had hummed and warmed and occasionally, he'd have sworn, sighed at his jokes. But they had returned nothing, because there was nothing left to return—he had poured it all out, and what the seal held beneath the empty throne was not his anymore, was no one's, was simply the realm's memory now, vast and common and safe.

But the echo gathered itself, and the clearing grew very still, and the frost on the grass glittered like it was listening—

—and Theo heard his mother's voice.

Not a memory. He had no memories; that door was painted shut. This was new. This was a gift, unasked, spun out of the echo's long patience and whatever scraps of the circle's old love could be woven into sound:

*"Theo."*

Just that. His name, in a voice he had never heard and recognized instantly, completely, the way you recognize your own heartbeat. Warm. A little rough, as if she'd been working. Amused about something—amused about him, probably, in the way mothers are, forever, about everything their sons do.

*"Theo, love. Come in out of the cold."*

The clearing blurred. He was aware distantly that he had sunk to his knees in the snow, that his shoulders were shaking, that he was laughing too—laughing and weeping in the same breath, the sounds tangled together, ridiculous, unstoppable. Somewhere behind him Sera had turned away to give him privacy and was absolutely listening anyway, because she was Sera.

The voice faded the way warmth fades from a cup, slowly, leaving the shape of itself.

Theo knelt in the snow for a long time.

When he finally stood, his face was wet and freezing and he didn't care. He pressed his hand once more to the stone. "Thank you," he managed. His voice cracked on the second word. He cleared his throat, tried again. "That wasn't hers, was it. Her actual voice. You built it. From the seal, from—"

The echo pulsed, gentle and honest: *built, yes; true, also.*

"Yeah," Theo said thickly. "Yeah. Close enough. Closer than close enough."

* * *

They stayed in Hollowmere through the festival—there was no leaving once the villagers discovered the famous Theo Dunmore could be cornered, fed, and made to dance, and Maren betrayed him utterly by pleading a fictional headache and abandoning him to the fiddle. He danced badly. A girl of six corrected his footwork with the gravity of a drill sergeant. The old woman with the root-like fingers told him three times, at increasing volumes, that he had her late husband's chin, which was impossible and which he agreed with each time, because some gifts cost nothing and are worth everything.

On the third morning, they packed to go.

Theo stood at the edge of the village square while Sera argued with a mule and Maren pretended not to be buying more of the honey bread for the road, and he took stock of himself the way he'd learned to: a quiet audit, item by item.

Name: present, worn smooth with use. Face: present, unfamiliar in mirrors, growing familiar. Gift: present, modest, warm as banked coals. Mother: gone, except for one word of voice, carried like an ember in a closed fist. Mistress Aldery: gone, except everywhere. Ilsa: gone, except whenever he chose a stranger's side in an argument, which was often. The years on the road: gone, except in his boots, his hands, his spine, his laugh.

It was not the life he'd been born with. It was better than the one he'd woken with. And it was, for the first time, *chosen*—every day of it picked up and put on deliberately, the way you choose a coat in cold weather. A past given is a past you can lose. A past you build is a past you own.

"All right," Sera announced, returning with the mule, who looked mutinous. "Capital? Or are we doing the sentimental detour past the second stone again? Because Maren will pretend to have a headache, and then she'll secretly walk out there at dawn like she always does, and we'll all waste a morning pretending we don't know."

"The capital," Theo said. "The king asked Maren to bring him the smell of thaw, and thaw comes late to the passes. We should hurry."

They took the smuggler's road south, because it was faster, and because it was his, in the only way anything was his now—and because, though he'd have denied it under oath, his body sang on those roads, and he had decided long ago that a man ought to live where his bones feel at home.

Halfway down the frozen waterfall stair, stepping neatly over the loose fourth step without looking, Theo paused. The wind had shifted, carrying up from the valley the smell of woodsmoke and pine and the first faint promise of melt.

For no reason he could name—except every reason—he said it aloud, to the mountain, to the stone far above, to the woman whose voice he'd never really heard and would never forget:

"Coming in out of the cold now."

And he walked on, whistling, into the thaw.
