## 1. The Charm Seller's Hour

The hour before dusk was Jonah Hartwell's favorite time to work, though *work* was a generous word for what he did. The lamplighters were just beginning their rounds, and Greyhollow's market district softened into that amber half-light where people's guards came down along with the shutters of their stalls. It was the hour when grief got hungry. He'd learned that long ago: sell a man bread and he'll haggle you to coppers; sell him his dead wife's voice and he'll pay whatever you ask.

The widow's name was Maren Collick, and she had come to him three times already this season. She sat across from him at his little table in the back corner of the Drowned Kettle tavern, her hands folded around an untouched cup of chicory like it was something warm she was trying to remember.

"You said you'd found another one," she said.

"I did." Jonah kept his voice low, unhurried. Around them the tavern murmured—dockhands arguing over dice, a fishwife laughing too loudly—but the corner was theirs alone. "It took some doing. Your husband had a distinctive laugh. Most laughs I could find anywhere. His—" he tapped the little glass vial on the table between them, no longer than his thumb, its stopper sealed with grey wax, "—I had to go looking."

Maren's eyes went wet immediately. She was not subtle in her grief; that was part of what made her easy to help and easier to feel guilty about.

"May I?"

"That's rather the point of the transaction."

She picked up the vial with both hands, the way one holds a bird. Jonah watched her uncork it, watched the faint silver breath rise—the memory catching the lamplight—and watched her lean in and inhale.

He knew exactly what she was tasting. He'd bottled it himself two nights ago, working from a secondhand thread he'd bought off a sailor who'd drunk with Tam Collick a hundred times: the sound of the man's laugh, big and unguarded, the kind that started in the chest and embarrassed everyone around it. The night of the herring festival, six years back, when Collick had slipped on the greased pole and come up soaked and triumphant, laughing so hard he had to hold onto a stranger for balance.

Maren made a small sound behind her hand. Then she laughed herself—a startled, cracked thing—and pressed the stopper back in as if she might spill the rest of it into the air forever.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, that's him. That's *him*. Not the sickbed version. Him."

"That's the one I wanted you to have."

She fumbled for her purse, but Jonah shook his head. "You've paid enough this season, Mrs. Collick. Call it a festival gift."

"Ghosts don't give gifts," she said, but she was smiling through the tears now, and she pressed a coin into his palm anyway—an old silver bit, worn smooth, probably worth more than she should spare. He took it because refusing would only wound her pride.

"Same time next month?" she asked, standing, smoothing her coat.

"If I find anything worth finding."

"You always do." She paused at the edge of the lamplight, and for a moment her face did something complicated—gratitude and shame braided together. "I know they're not really his, Mr. Hartwell. I know that. But it's the closest thing to hearing him again that this city will let me have. So thank you. Whatever else you are, thank you."

Then she was gone into the crowd, and Jonah sat alone with his chicory going cold and the word *whatever else you are* sitting in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

He wasn't a fraud. That much he held onto. The memories he sold were real—he didn't fabricate, didn't counterfeit. He found them, in the strange grey country between other people's heads where he'd been able to walk since before he could name the ability. A touch, a moment of stillness, and he could lift a memory out whole: the smell of a first kitchen, the weight of a sleeping child, a dead man's laugh on a festival night. He reshaped them gently—smoothed the pain off the edges, kept the gold—and sold them in glass. Half the city thought he was a sentimental con artist. The other half, the ones who bought, knew better and told no one.

Because the other thing about Jonah Hartwell's trade was that it was illegal. Had been illegal for a hundred years, since the Sundering, since Greyhollow cut itself off from Vael and outlawed every craft that smelled of dragon-magic. Memory-work was the most forbidden of all. The Magistrate's truth-sniffers—lean, grey-cloaked creatures that were more hound than man and more man than anyone liked to admit—could taste a stolen memory on the air like blood in water.

Jonah had survived ten years in the trade by being small. Unremarkable. Forgettable in the literal sense—he wore a charm of dullness woven into his own surface thoughts, a trick Old Bram taught him, so that even a sniffer walking past would slide its attention off him like rain off oiled canvas.

Which is why it unsettled him profoundly when the woman at the corner table looked up from her book and said, without any preamble at all:

"You seal your wax with saltwater. Interesting choice."

Jonah looked up. The woman had been there when he arrived—he'd noted her absently, a sharp-featured young woman in a charcoal coat reading a battered volume, occupying the table nearest his corner. Archivist's ink stains on her fingers. Hair pinned back in a style that said *I did this once this morning and refuse to think about it again.* Pretty in a way that seemed accidental and slightly annoyed about it.

"I'm sorry?" he said.

"The wax on your vials." She nodded at the empty bottle Maren had left behind, its grey seal still intact around the neck. "Most bottlers use plain tallow. You mix sea-salt into yours. Preserves the memory longer, doesn't it? Keeps it from evaporating." Her eyes were a pale, startling green. "Old technique. Very old."

Every instinct Jonah had went quiet and watchful, like a cat hearing a floorboard. "I think you've mistaken me for someone else, miss. I'm a spice merchant."

"You're a memory-bottler." She said it the way one says *you're bleeding*. "And you're good. I watched you sell that woman an hour of her husband, and she'll sleep well tonight for the first time in months. Do you know how rare that is? Clean work. No residue, no bleed. Whoever trained you trained you *properly*."

Jonah stood, slow and casual, gathering his coat. "This has been charming, but—"

"Don't." She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to. "There's a sniffer doing the rounds on Cordwainer Street right now—I saw it pass the window four minutes ago, working north. If I raise my voice and say the word *bottler*, you'll be running for the drains by the time it turns the corner. Sit down, Mr. Hartwell. Please. I'm not here to hurt you. I'm here because I've spent three years trying to find you."

That stopped him. Not the threat—the threat was almost comforting, threats he understood. It was the name. He hadn't given her a name.

"How do you know my name?"

She closed her book and turned it toward him, and Jonah felt the floor tilt gently beneath his chair. The book was old—not antique-old, but *forbidden*-old, its spine re-sewn with the crude careful stitching of someone who repaired contraband by lamplight. The page she'd opened to was dense with cramped annotation, and beside the text someone a century dead had sketched a small illustration in faded iron-gall ink.

It was a young man with dark hair falling over his brow, holding a small glass vial up to the light.

It was, unmistakably, him.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. It was his face—the particular crooked set of his mouth, the scar through his left eyebrow that he'd never been able to explain, even the way he tilted a bottle to catch the light. And beneath the sketch, in that same cramped hand, a single line:

*The thief with no yesterday shall come at the hour of salt.*

Jonah became aware that he had stopped breathing. "What is this?"

"It's called the Century Codex," the woman said. "It's a prophecy text, banned under the Sundering Edicts—which is why it lives in the sealed stacks of the Greyhollow Archive, and why I shouldn't be showing it to you, and why I've already lost one career over it and am presently risking the other." She extended her hand across the table, businesslike, absurdly formal given the circumstances. "Sera Voss. Junior archivist, third class, soon to be unemployed or worse. I've been cross-referencing banned prophecy fragments against census rolls and guild registries for three years, Mr. Hartwell, looking for a face. And four days ago I found a birth record that matches, and a guild apprenticeship record, and a shop lease on Tanner's Row, and then tonight I walked into the Drowned Kettle and watched a man bottle sunlight for a grieving widow."

Jonah didn't take her hand. His own had gone cold. "That drawing is a hundred years old."

"Yes."

"People don't live a hundred years."

"No," Sera agreed quietly. "They don't. Which raises a question I very much want answered, and which I suspect you want answered even more." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle. "Tell me, Mr. Hartwell. What's your earliest memory?"

The tavern noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. Jonah opened his mouth to answer—and found, to his genuine surprise, that he had to actually search. Most people know instantly. Their childhoods sit behind them like furniture.

His own past sat behind him like fog. There was Bram, and the shop, and learning the trade at nineteen—

Nineteen. Everything began at nineteen. Before that: nothing. Not darkness, not blank pages—*nothing*, a smooth wall where a life should have been, so seamless he'd never once thought to question it. Ten years of living and he had never noticed he was missing the first nineteen.

"No," he said aloud, not meaning to say it aloud.

"There it is," Sera Voss whispered, and there was no triumph in her face at all—only a kind of dread, deep and dawning, the look of someone who has pulled a thread and felt the whole garment shift. "That's what the Codex means. *The thief with no yesterday.* I hoped I was wrong. Gods help me, I hoped I was wrong."

"Wrong about what?" Jonah's voice came out flat and strange. "What does the Codex say I am?"

Sera glanced toward the window, toward the street, where somewhere in the darkening lanes of Greyhollow a truth-sniffer was moving through the crowds with its grey nose lifted, tasting the air. When she looked back at him, she was already sweeping the Codex into her satchel, already standing, already becoming a woman with somewhere urgent to be.

"Not here. Not with that thing outside. Tomorrow—there's a door in the archive's east stacks, behind the Sundering folios. Come at the change of the watch, and I'll show you what a hundred years of Magistrates have killed to keep buried." She hesitated, and something flickered across her face that might have been apology. "I'd have asked you to dinner first, under different circumstances. This isn't dinner, Mr. Hartwell. This is the rest of your life."

She left a coin for her chicory and was gone, and Jonah Hartwell sat alone in the amber light with an old silver bit in his palm and a single sentence turning over and over in the place where his childhood should have been:

*The thief with no yesterday shall come at the hour of salt.*

He looked down at his hands—the hands of a man who had never wondered where he came from, because wondering had apparently been taken from him too—and realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the evening air, that he had no idea whether to run from Sera Voss or follow her.

Outside, the lamplighter reached the end of the street, and the last window of daylight went grey as sea-salt.
