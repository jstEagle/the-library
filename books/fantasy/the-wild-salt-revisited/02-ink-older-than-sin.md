## 2. Ink Older Than Sin

The archive of Greyhollow smelled of dust and tallow and, beneath it all, something older—salt, maybe, though the sea had not touched this district in a hundred years. Jonah Hartwell stood in the antechamber with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, watching Sera Voss argue with the under-clerk in a voice pitched low enough to be polite and sharp enough to draw blood.

"Sealed stacks, Voss. You know the rule."

"I know the rule," she said, "and I know that Archivist Dennen signed my clearance himself before he took his winter cough. Are you telling me a cough outranks a signature?"

The under-clerk's ears went pink. Jonah studied the mosaic floor—a dragon coiled around a tower, its scales rendered in chips of green glass gone dull with age—and did what he did best: he made himself unremarkable. He softened the edges of his posture, let his face settle into the pleasant blankness of a man waiting on his wife. It was a small theft, the kind he'd performed ten thousand times. Nobody looks twice at a man who has decided to be furniture.

It worked. The under-clerk waved them through with the resentment of a man who would remember being annoyed but never why.

Sera led him down a corridor of iron-banded doors, her keys singing in her pocket, and Jonah felt the strangest sensation—that he had walked this hallway before. Not déjà vu exactly; he didn't believe in it. More like the corridor recognized him. The lamps guttered as they passed, one after another, like a row of bowing servants.

"You're quiet," Sera said.

"I'm always quiet. It's part of the charm."

"It's part of something." She stopped before a door black with old varnish, its lock wrapped three times over in lead wire stamped with the Magistracy's seal—the tower and the coiled beast, though here the beast's head had been hammered flat, erased. She unwound the wire with fingers that trembled only slightly. "I've been trying to decide for two weeks whether to show you this. Then you ordered wine last night and held the bottle to the light the way a memory-broker tests vintage, and I decided."

"Sera—"

"Don't." She turned, and in the lamplight her eyes were fierce and frightened in equal measure. "I've read every text this city allows and half of those it doesn't. I know what bottled memories smell like when they're fresh—I catalogued six confiscated vials my second year here. And when you smiled at me across that table, I could *smell* it on you, Jonah. Craft. Old craft. The kind they hanged people for."

The word landed in his chest like a dropped stone. *Hanged.* His trade was a misdemeanor, a fine, a confiscation. Everyone knew that. Everyone said so.

"The Sundering Laws," he said carefully, "are about theft. About breaking into minds. Selling someone else's past is—" 

"—is exactly what you do." She pushed the door open. "And yet you don't see yourself as a thief. Which means either you're lying to both of us, or you genuinely don't understand what your gift is. And Jonah—" she stepped aside, gesturing him into the dark beyond the threshold, "—I've spent two weeks hoping it's the second one. Because if it's the second, then there's something I need you to look at."

* * *

Sera Voss had learned early that the archive kept its own weather. In the sealed stacks the air hung motionless and cold, thick with the particular silence of paper that no one had loved in a long time. She lit the reading lamp with hands she pretended were steady, and watched Jonah Hartwell take in the room.

He moved like a man entering a temple he'd been told was condemned—reverent and wary at once, fingertips hovering an inch above the spines of chained folios without quite touching them. Most visitors to the sealed stacks fidgeted. He didn't. He simply *attended*, the way she imagined a priest attends an altar, and something about it made her chest ache.

She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it badly, at two in the morning, pacing her narrow rooms above the chandler's shop, holding the Codex argument out loud to an empty chair. But rehearsal dissolved now, because he had turned toward the reading table where the Century Codex lay under its cloth, and his face had changed. Not fear. Recognition. The way a man looks at a house from a childhood he can't name.

"What is it?" he asked.

"A hundred years ago, when Greyhollow was cut off from Vael, someone wrote down why." She drew back the cloth. The book beneath was massive, bound in hide gone the color of dried blood, its pages edged in silver leaf that had tarnished to black. "They call it the Century Codex because it was compiled in the century after the Sundering—testimonies, fragments, things salvaged from the burning. Most of it is grief. Some of it is lies. One page—" her mouth went dry, and she made herself finish, "—one page is prophecy."

Jonah's hand hovered over the cover. "You said banned texts. This isn't banned. It's *sealed*. There's a difference, and it's worse. Banned books get burned. Sealed books get kept by someone who wants them forgotten but not lost."

She stared at him. "That's almost word for word what Dennen used to say."

"Did it," he said quietly, "say anything about a thief?"

* * *

She turned to the page.

Jonah had expected Latin or High Cant or some scholar's dead tongue. Instead the prophecy sat in plain trade-script, ink faded to the brown of old tea, and it was short enough to fit inside a heartbeat:

*When the last fire gutters behind stone,*
*the thief with no yesterday shall come*
*wearing borrowed hours like a coat.*
*Him the realms will ask to pay*
*what was stolen; him the fire will know.*
*Beware the magistrate who keeps the flame,*
*for he feeds the lie that keeps the cage.*
*Only the heir who remembers nothing*
*can choose to give everything.*

The lamp flame leaned sideways in air that hadn't moved a moment before. Jonah became aware that Sera was speaking—her voice reaching him down a long tunnel—but the words wouldn't land. He was counting instead. Counting backward through his own life the way he counted stock in his shop, quick and ruthless.

Nineteen. Everything began at nineteen. Bram finding him half-dead in the Salt Quarter with no name he could speak and no past he could touch, just a purse of coin and a talent neither of them discussed. Ten years since. Ten years of a life with clean edges and nothing underneath, and he had called that *privacy*. He had called it a man keeping his own counsel. He had built a trade on other people's yesterdays because—

Because his own had been taken.

Or taken *out*. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth. A man whose memory was damaged grieved. A man whose memory had been *removed*—surgically, deliberately, by hands skilled enough to leave no scar—had been robbed. And robbery implied a thief, and thieves left receipts, and somewhere in his skull was the shape of an absence so complete it had worn a groove smooth as riverstone.

"Jonah." Sera's hand on his arm. Warm. Real. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing." He wasn't, quite. "Say it plainly. What does 'heir' mean? Heir to what?"

"To the covenant." She said it the way people say the names of dead gods. "Before the Sundering, Greyhollow and Vael were joined by an oath—the covenant-stones, the dragon-fire shared between realms. The memory-thieves were its keepers. That's what the old testimonies say. They could walk into any mind in either world, but the covenant bound them: they took nothing they didn't return, reshaped nothing they weren't asked to reshape. They were the proof that power could be trusted." Her laugh was thin. "Then the Sundering came. The realms tore apart, the dragons vanished, the fire went behind stone, and within a generation the Magistracy passed the laws that made the keepers into criminals. Every text that explained why was burned or sealed. Except this one."

"And the magistrate who keeps the flame." His voice came out flat, strange to his own ears. "Greyhollow has had eleven magistrates in a century. Corvane's held the seat twenty-two years."

"Corvane commissioned the sealing of this room," Sera said. "Dennen told me once—he'd had too much brandy—that the order came with a list. Pages to be moved, pages to be destroyed. The Codex survived because Dennen hid it inside a shipping manifest and let the clerks seal it by accident. He called it the most honest book in Greyhollow, and then he never spoke of it again until his last day."

The lamp guttered again. Jonah looked down at the prophecy, at the line that seemed to have been written with him in mind across a hundred years and a sea of ash: *wearing borrowed hours like a coat.*

He thought of his escape routes through the Salt Quarter—the alleys he knew blind, the courtyards, the drain-grates, knowledge he'd assumed came from ten years of running goods for Bram. He thought of how naturally the craft had come to him, as if he were remembering a skill rather than learning one. He thought of the widow yesterday, the laugh in the bottle, the ease of it, the terrible ease.

"Sera." He heard his own voice as if from another room. "I don't have anything before nineteen. No childhood. No mother's face. Nothing. I always thought—" His throat closed. He started again. "What does it mean, *him the fire will know*?"

But she wasn't looking at him anymore. She was looking past his shoulder, at the door of the sealed stacks, which stood open.

Neither of them had opened it.

* * *

In the doorway, framed against the lamplit corridor, stood a figure in the grey livery of the Magistracy—not a guard, not a clerk. One of the sniffers. Jonah knew them by reputation alone: men and women with pale eyes and still faces who could smell a lie the way sharks smell blood, who answered to no one but Corvane himself. This one's nostrils flared once, delicately, like a hound catching scent on the wind.

"Archivist," the sniffer said pleasantly. "And guest. How interesting. The Magistrate does so love it when interesting things happen in his archive."

Behind the sniffer, far off down the corridor, a bell began to ring—the evening hour, or perhaps not the evening hour at all. Jonah's hand found Sera's in the dark. Hers was ice.

"Run on my mark," he breathed, and some part of him, some part older than nineteen, already knew the way out.
