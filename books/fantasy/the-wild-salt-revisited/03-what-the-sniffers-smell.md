## 3. What the Sniffers Smell

The truth-sniffers came at the hour when archives forget themselves, when the lamps burn low and the clerks have gone home to their suppers, and the only sound in the Vellum Hall is the slow breathing of ten thousand books.

Jonah heard them before he saw them. That was the trouble with his gift—it worked both directions whether he willed it or not. Fear has a smell to a man like him: not the honest stink of sweat and panic, but something older and stranger, like copper left out in rain. The sniffers wore their fear like perfume, and it came drifting down the sealed stacks ahead of them, threading between the shelves where Jonah stood with Sera's hand still warm around his wrist.

"Down," Sera breathed, and pulled him into the gap between a case of tax ledgers and the wall.

They crouched in the dust-dark while boots rang on marble—six pairs, maybe seven, moving with the unhurried precision of men who had never once been told *no*. Jonah had seen truth-sniffers before, from across streets and through shop windows: grey-coated, clean-shaven, faces like unmarked paper. They were Magistrate Corvane's own, answerable to no one but him, and they carried the thing that made them terrible—a lantern of smoked glass called a verity-lamp, which burned with a flame that fed on lies. Set it near a hidden document and the flame would lean toward it, hungry as a compass needle. Set it near a liar, and the liar's secrets would rise off his skin like steam.

Jonah had spent nine years being invisible to such men. He had never once asked himself why.

"Third shelf, sub-level," said a voice above them, flat as slate. "The Century Codex was drawn from the seal-vault two nights past. Someone accessed it without registry."

*Two nights past.* The night Sera had first shown it to him. The night she'd turned the vellum toward the lamp and read aloud those words that had been waiting a hundred years for someone with his face:

*The thief with no yesterday shall come at the last, bearing fire in his blood and nothing behind his eyes.*

His mouth had gone dry then too.

Beside him, Sera moved—not away from danger, which would have been sensible, but toward her satchel. She drew out a bundle of papers wrapped in oilcloth: her notes, three years of them, marginalia on banned prophecy texts, transcriptions of covenant-lore that should have been ash before Corvane's grandfather was born. Jonah watched her hands as she worked. Steady. Deliberate. She uncorked a flask of lamp-oil and poured it over the papers with the tenderness of a woman washing a wound.

"Sera—" he whispered.

"They can't find these." Her voice was barely air. "If they find these, they don't just take me. They trace every hand that ever touched my work. Every archivist who looked away. The junior clerks I trained." She struck a match against the stone. "I knew this day might come. I wrote everything twice for exactly this reason."

The match touched oil and the notes went up with a soft, almost apologetic sound, pages curling like leaves in autumn. In the cramped dark, firelight played across her face, and Jonah saw what he hadn't let himself see before: she wasn't afraid. She was grieving, yes—but underneath the grief ran something harder, a current like bedrock under a river. She fed the burning papers piece by piece into a brass wastebin, controlling the flame the way he controlled memories, and he understood suddenly that they were more alike than either of them had admitted. Two people who kept dangerous things, and knew when to destroy them.

Above, the verity-lamp flared. He felt it even without seeing—the flame's hunger reaching out through the stacks like a tongue tasting the air, and finding... what? Ash. Smoke. And beneath it all, faintly, the residue of magic that clung to Jonah's skin like river-silt.

One of the sniffers made a low sound. "There's residue here. Recent."

"Recent how?"

"Ninety minutes. Perhaps less."

Ninety minutes. Since the Codex. Since Jonah had stood with his fingers an inch from the prophecy's ink, feeling it hum against his gift like a plucked string recognizing its twin.

Boots began to descend.

Sera pinched out the flame and shoved the bin deep beneath the ledger-case. Then she did something Jonah would remember long after—remember being the operative word, though he didn't know yet how literally. She took his face in both hands, oil-smoke and determination, and pressed her forehead to his.

"The loading dock," she whispered. "West side. There's a door behind the map cabinets that opens onto Cooper's Slip. Go now, go fast, and don't look back for me."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are, because if they catch us together, everything burns—me, you, the Codex, all of it. And we need all of it." Her thumbs brushed his cheekbones, brief and fierce. "You're the heir of something, Jonah Hartwell. Whatever else is true, that's true. Act like it."

He wanted to argue. Instead he kissed her knuckles, once, like sealing a promise, and went.

* * *

The door behind the map cabinets was narrow and smelled of tallow and old salt, and it opened—as she'd promised—onto Cooper's Slip, where the tide slaps the pilings and the fishing boats knock their hulls together like gossiping old men.

Jonah ran.

Not blindly. That was the strange part, the part that would keep him awake for nights after. His feet knew things his mind did not. When he reached the mouth of the slip, his body turned left before his eyes could survey the street—left, into a covered passage he had no memory of ever seeing, a slot of shadow between a chandler's store and a derelict counting-house, exactly wide enough for a thin man and exactly placed so that anyone watching the main road would see nothing at all.

He emerged on Tanner's Row and his legs chose again: down the stair-cut beside the dye-house, through the gap where a drainage grate had been pried loose years ago—*he knew* it had been pried loose years ago, could feel the knowledge sitting in him like a swallowed stone, though he could not for his life say when or how it got there. The grate scraped over his shoulders. The tunnel beyond was dry, floored with old brick, lit by cracks of lamplight from a tavern overhead, and he followed it without hesitation, left at the fork, right where a beam had fallen, up a ladder whose rungs he somehow knew were rotten on the third step and so skipped it without thinking.

Every turn was correct. Every shortcut was perfect.

And none of it was his.

By the time he climbed out through the coal-chute of a shuttered smokehouse in Saltcote Ward, his lungs were burning and his thoughts had gone very quiet and very cold. He sat in the coal-dust with his back against the chute and made himself look at it plainly, the way he'd make himself look at a wound.

He knew Greyhollow's underworld the way a fish knows water. Every fence's back room, every smuggler's cut-through, every alley where the watch didn't bother looking. He'd always assumed he'd learned it the way anyone learns a city: by living in it, by walking it, by nine years of Old Bram's errands and lessons. But Bram had raised him from nineteen. Nineteen! No boy learns a city's secret anatomy in the years since—he'd been careful, quiet, a ghost by trade. Ghosts don't collect maps.

Which meant the maps had come pre-installed.

Someone had walked these alleys for him. Someone had stolen—or been given—every route, every hiding place, every bolt-hole, and pressed them into a boy who couldn't remember receiving them. His invisibility, his perfect, effortless invisibility, wasn't talent. It was architecture. Someone had built him to move unseen through Greyhollow, the way a locksmith builds a key to fit one lock.

And there was only one man with the power and the patience to build such a key, and Jonah had eaten supper at his table last night.

"Bram," he said aloud, and the word fell into the coal-dust like a coin down a well.

Above him, gulls screamed over the harbor. Somewhere across the city, the archive's bell would be ringing the alarm; Sera would be standing in the wreckage of her notes with her chin up and her hands empty, lying beautifully to men with grey coats. And somewhere north, in the high quarter where the streets were clean, Magistrate Corvane would be hearing a report: *the residue was recent, Magistrate. We believe the thief with no yesterday exists.*

Panic rose in him then—true panic, animal and total—and he let it crest, let it shake his hands, because fighting it would only give it longer legs. And when it crested and began, slowly, to drain away, what remained underneath was not relief. It was resolve, cold and specific as a blade laid flat on a table.

They had taken his yesterday. All right. Then he would go and ask the man who'd sold it why, and what else had been in the parcel, and who had paid. Old Bram had taught him every rule of the trade except perhaps the most important one: *follow the memory to its owner.*

His childhood had an owner. And Jonah intended to collect.

He climbed out of the smokehouse into the blue evening, dusted coal from his coat, and set off through streets he finally understood were not his home but someone else's design—toward the Rookery, toward the fence-shop with the crooked sign, toward the man he had called father.

Behind him, far off, a verity-lamp flickered alight over Cooper's Slip, and began, patiently, to follow the smell of what he truly was.
