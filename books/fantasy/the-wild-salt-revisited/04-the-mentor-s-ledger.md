## 4. The Mentor's Ledger

The rain had stopped by the time Jonah reached Saltrow, but the streets still wept, water threading down the gutters in long grey ribbons that smelled of ash and old iron. He knew these alleys. That was the trouble. He knew them the way a man knows a song he's never heard sung—by heart, without origin.

He'd spent the walk from the archive testing himself, reaching back the way Sera had taught him to reach for a citation, precise and merciless. *First day of apprenticeship with Bram.* There—bright as lacquer, the smell of tallow and brass fittings, Bram's crooked grin under the lamp. *His mother's face.* Nothing. A smooth white wall where a face should be, like a page torn cleanly from a book. *The first time he bottled a memory.* There. *Learning his letters.* There—but even those felt strangely recent, as though his whole childhood had been compressed into a handful of polished stones dropped into an empty jar.

Nineteen years old. Everything before nineteen was either missing or too perfect, too curated, like rooms in a house staged for sale.

Old Bram kept shop at the crook of Tanner's Bend, behind a door painted the green of bottle-glass. Jonah had walked through it ten thousand times. Tonight he stood outside it in the dark, rain dripping off the eaves onto his collar, and found he was afraid.

Not of the sniffers. Not of Corvane. Of the answer waiting inside, which he already carried in his pocket: the scrap of paper Sera had pressed into his hand as they parted, pulled from the Century Codex's margin. A broker's mark. The same coiled-serpent sigil Old Bram stamped on every receipt in his shop. And beneath it, in faded archival ink, a date ten years gone—and the notation, dry as dust: *Acquisition, batch of nine. Source: juvenile. Buyer: office of the Magistrate.*

Jonah pushed open the door.

* * *

The bell above the frame gave its familiar cracked chime, and the smell of the shop wrapped around him—brass polish, lamp oil, the faint mineral tang of bottled memory, like lightning stored in saltwater. Shelves rose to the ceiling on every wall, thousands of vials catching the lamplight, each one holding somebody's wedding day, somebody's grief, somebody's single perfect afternoon. Bram called them inventory. Jonah had always called them people.

Bram sat at the sorting table in back, hunched over a jeweler's loupe, silver hair wild around ears too big for his head. He looked up, and his face did something complicated—relief and guilt fighting for the same ground.

"You're alive." He said it like a man setting down something heavy. "Sniffers turned over half the archive quarter. I told them nothing, but I want you gone from Greyhollow by morning, boy. There's a barge leaving on the tide—"

"Batch of nine."

Bram stopped moving. It was small, the way he stopped—a stillness in the hands, the loupe set down with exaggerated care—but Jonah had learned to read men across a bargaining table since before he could legally drink, and there was no mistaking it.

"Source: juvenile," Jonah said. His voice came out flat, unfamiliar. "Buyer: the Magistrate's office. Ten years ago. Your serpent's stamped right beside it, Bram."

"That book is archive property. Stolen property, if you're holding what I think you're holding." Bram stood, slow, joints cracking like kindling. "Sera Voss has fed you poison, Jonah. That girl reads dead men's prophecies and starts seeing them in living faces—"

"I don't remember my childhood."

He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, quiet, in the space between the lamp's flickers, and it landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"I've got maybe six memories from before I was nineteen," Jonah went on. "Six. All of them clean. All of them *nice*. Like you picked them out yourself. And I never once wondered why, because why would I? You were all I had. You fed me, taught me the craft, gave me a bed when the fever took everything else—" His throat closed. "What else did the fever take, Bram?"

For a long moment the old man said nothing. Then he crossed to the shelf behind his chair—the one Jonah had never been allowed to touch, the one Bram claimed held "spoiled stock"—and lifted down a ledger bound in cracked black leather. He set it on the table between them like a man laying a corpse on a slab.

"Read it yourself, then. Since she's already read it to you."

* * *

The ledger's columns were neat, meticulous, written in Bram's careful merchant's hand. Dates going back thirty years. Lots acquired, lots sold, prices in crowns and favors. And threaded through the early pages, entries in a different ink, older:

*Lot 41. Boy, approx. 9 yrs. Memories: 4 (mother's face; fire; name-day; sea voyage). Buyer: private. Note: subject cooperative.*

*Lot 44. Same source. Memories: 11. Includes language acquisition, first theft, grief (father). Buyer: office of the Magistracy. Price: exemption, protection, standing order for future yield.*

*Standing order,* Jonah read again. The words swam. *Future yield.* As though he were an orchard. As though his head were a cellar to be tapped each season, and each harvest shipped upriver to a man who wore a magistrate's chain and smiled from balconies while children cheered him.

Nine batches. The Codex had said nine. Nine extractions over seven years, beginning when Jonah was nine years old and ending the night before his nineteenth birthday, when the ledger recorded—final entry, circled twice—*Balance settled in full. Subject retains functional minimum. Recommend no further contact with source material. Subject must not learn.*

"Subject," Jonah said. The word tasted like copper. "You wrote *subject*."

"What would you have me write?" Bram's voice cracked, and for one naked moment he looked every year of his age, an old fence in a cold shop with rain coming through the roof. "Boy? *My boy*? You think that word would have made it cleaner?"

"Why?"

"They came for you, Jonah. Corvane's men, the night after the Sundering anniversary, when you were nine. They said the boy was flagged—prophecy-adjacent bloodline, whatever rot that means—and that the Magistrate offered a choice. Sell your past or lose your future. I could keep you, feed you, teach you, so long as the yield flowed. Or I could watch them take you somewhere no one would ever sell anything out of again." Bram's hands were shaking now, openly. "I chose. God forgive me, I chose, and then I chose it again every year for seven years, because the alternative got worse the longer I waited, and because—because you were *good*, Jonah. You took to the craft like breathing, and the better you got, the more valuable you were, and the more valuable you were, the harder it became to ever let you go."

"So you kept me ignorant. Kept me grateful. Kept me *small*."

"I kept you *alive*!" The shout rang against the bottles, and somewhere on a high shelf a vial chimed softly, some dead woman's laughter disturbed in its sleep. Bram lowered his voice to a rasp. "You don't know what Corvane is. You've seen the balcony smile. I've seen the other thing. The fire under the Magistracy. What he does down there with what he buys. If I'd left one thread loose, one memory you might pull, he'd have known, and he'd have unwound you entirely."

"And instead you wound me yourself." Jonah closed the ledger. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside, something vast was collapsing quietly, the way a pier collapses—slow, structural, irreversible. Every kindness reassembled itself into transaction. The feverbed. The lessons. The name. *Hartwell*—he checked the ledger's first entry, and there it was: *Subject renamed per buyer's instruction.* Even his name was a purchase.

"Bram." He looked up, and the old man flinched from what was in his face. "Did you love me at all? Or did you just tend the crop?"

"Don't." Bram came around the table, reaching, and Jonah stepped back, and the distance between them—three feet of dusty floorboards—might as well have been the Sundering itself. "Don't you dare make it simple. I stole your childhood and I'd do it again, because the man who'd have raised you honest is dead in a ditch somewhere off Marsh Lane, and you'd be in it beside him. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you how it was."

Jonah believed him. That was the worst of it. He could see the whole shape of it now, the trap built of love and cowardice in equal measure, and there was no version of the story where Bram was a monster, only versions where he was weak, and weak men had built Greyhollow's whole rotten peace.

"The third stone," Jonah said quietly. "Where does Corvane keep it?"

Bram went very still again. "So Voss told you about the stones."

"She didn't have to tell me about this one." Jonah tapped the ledger. "*Final payment received: one stone, covenant-class. Vaulted pending transfer.* You sold him that too. You watched him take it, and you never said a word—not when I started asking questions about the Sundering, not when I dreamed about dragons I'd never seen."

"He'd have known I told you. He always knows."

"He won't this time." Jonah pulled his coat tight and moved toward the door, and found Bram blocking it—not barring it, just standing there, an old man in the way of a storm he couldn't stop.

"Vault's under the Magistracy, third sub-level, behind the false reliquary. But Jonah—" Bram gripped his sleeve, and for once the grip wasn't commerce, wasn't craft, was just a frightened old man holding on. "It's a trap. It's been a trap since the night the sniffers missed you at the archive. Corvane doesn't chase thieves, boy. He *cultivates* them. He wants you to come for the stone. He's wanted it since you were nine years old."

"Why? Why me?"

Bram's eyes went to the window, to the dark, as if the answer might be listening. When he spoke, his voice was barely above the sound of the gutter-rain.

"Because the prophecy doesn't need a thief who steals memories, Jonah. It needs one who can give them back. And there's only one left."
