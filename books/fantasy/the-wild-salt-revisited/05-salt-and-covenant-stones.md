## 5. Salt and Covenant-Stones

Sera Voss had spent eleven years in the Greyhollow archives learning to read what the city wanted forgotten, and in all that time she had never once broken a rule she could name. She told herself that now, standing at the edge of the Saltmarch with her boots sinking into black mud and the Century Codex wrapped in oilcloth against her chest: *I am not breaking rules. The rules were broken long before me. I am only reading.*

Jonah Hartwell walked ahead of her along the causeway, his coat collar turned up against the wind off the drowned flats, and every few strides he glanced back as if to confirm she hadn't changed her mind. She almost wished he'd stop looking. Every look made it harder to pretend this was scholarship.

The Codex had given them the chapel's location in its oblique, maddening way—not an address but a riddle of tides: *where the bell still rings though no rope is pulled, where salt keeps the door that water cannot open.* Cross-referenced against the old parish surveys she'd smuggled out in her memory rather than on paper—Corvane's sniffers had made paper dangerous—it pointed to Saint Aldhelm-in-the-Salt, a chapel swallowed by the sea when the Sundering dragged half the coastline down, ninety-nine years ago. At low tide, its spire stood exposed like a bone picked clean.

The tide was going out now. The spire was already showing, gull-white and crusted, and around it the water peeled back in slow silver sheets, revealing rooftops like drowned faces, a street lamp still bolted to a wall that had not held a street in a century.

"You're quiet," Jonah said, dropping back beside her.

"I'm composing my resignation letter," Sera said. "In my head. It's very dignified. Magistrate Corvane will weep."

He laughed, but it didn't reach his eyes. He hadn't slept—she could see it in the bruised hollows under them—and he kept touching the inside of his wrist where, three nights ago, in the burning light of the archive, the Codex's ink had crawled toward his hand like iron filings finding a lodestone. *The thief with no yesterday,* the prophecy called him. Sera had catalogued banned texts for a decade and had never seen ink move before. She had never seen a man's face drawn in a book written before he was born, either.

"The stone," she said. "When we find it. What do you expect it to do?"

"I don't know." He said it honestly, which she was beginning to understand was his most practiced dishonesty—the truth deployed so plainly it stopped feeling like information. "Bram always said the old families kept tokens. Keepsakes from before the Sundering. I thought they were stories."

"Your mentor sells memories out of a shop behind the fish market. You bottled a widow's grief last week and charged her for the privilege." Sera heard the sharpness in her own voice and didn't take it back. "Stories are the only thing anyone in this city has left, Jonah. Including you. *Especially* you."

He flinched. She watched him decide not to argue, and something in her chest twisted—guilt, maybe, or the beginnings of something less convenient.

* * *

They reached the chapel as the last of the water slid from its doors.

Up close, Saint Aldhelm's was less ruined than drowned things should be. The stone was black with salt but uncracked; the arched doors stood shut, their iron fittings green with age yet bright at the edges, as if recently handled. And the bell—there was no bell in the belfry, Sera realized, only a shape of absence, a bell-sized hole in the air that hummed faintly when the wind crossed it. A bell that rang though no rope was pulled. The Codex had not been speaking in metaphor after all.

Jonah put his palm flat against the door and went very still.

"What?" Sera whispered.

"There are memories in the wood." His voice had gone strange—flattened, careful, the voice of a man walking on ice. "Old ones. Layered. Like... like sediment. Someone sealed this place with remembering. Whole decades of it, pressed into the grain." He looked at her, and for the first time since she'd met him there was something raw under the charm. "Sera, I can feel them and I don't know how. I've never—I bottle feelings secondhand, through craft, through tools. This is just my hand."

"The Codex says the heir remembers how," she said quietly. "It says the gift wakes when the stones do."

"I don't *want* it to wake." The words came out fast, frightened. Then he closed his eyes, exhaled, and pressed harder against the door—and the salt crusted across the hinges shivered, loosened, and fell away like frost in morning sun. The doors drifted inward without a sound.

Cold air rolled out, smelling of brine and beeswax and something older underneath—something hot, like a banked forge, like the moment before lightning. Sera's skin prickled. Every archive instinct she owned screamed that they were crossing a threshold no one had crossed in a hundred years, and that the thing on the other side had been waiting.

She followed him in anyway. That was the part she would remember afterward, when everything else became unbearable: that she chose the dark knowing what it would cost, and walked in with her eyes open.

* * *

Inside, the chapel breathed.

Light came from nowhere—or from everywhere, a dim amber glow rising from the flooded nave floor like sunset trapped underwater, though the water itself had receded to ankle-deep pools among the pews. Salt stalactites hung from the vaulting, glittering. And at the far end, where an altar should have been, the first covenant-stone sat on a plinth of fused glass: a fist-sized heart of grey rock veined with firelight, pulsing slowly, steadily, like something asleep and dreaming.

Sera had read ten thousand descriptions of dragon-fire. None of them prepared her for the way the sound reached her before the sight—a low resonance she felt in her sternum, in her teeth, in the small bones of her ears, as if the stone were singing a note too deep for hearing and her body had decided to listen anyway.

"It's dying," Jonah said hoarsely. "Can you feel it? It's—God, it's like holding a bird with a broken wing."

He stepped forward and Sera caught his arm. "Wait. Wards. Any text this important would be warded."

But the wards, when Jonah brushed them with his awareness, turned out not to be wards at all. "It's a lock," he said slowly, frowning. "A memory-lock. It wants—" He stopped. Went pale. "It wants me to give it something. A memory. Freely."

"Then give it one," Sera said. "A small one. Not—" She almost said *not one of the ones you have left*, because she knew now how few those were, nineteen years of borrowed life floating on a sea of erasure, but the pity in it would wound him worse than the theft already had.

Jonah knelt at the plinth. He pressed two fingers to the stone and closed his eyes, and Sera watched his face while he paid whatever price the covenant demanded—watched something leave him, some small brightness dimming behind his eyes—and then the veins of fire in the stone flared white-gold, and the whole drowned chapel rang like a struck bell, and every pool of water in the nave leapt an inch off the floor.

And someone clapped, slowly, from the shadow of the belfry stairs.

"Took you both long enough," said a voice like smoke over coals. "Ninety-nine years, by my count. But who's counting?"

* * *

She unfolded from the darkness the way heat shimmer rises off summer stone: a woman, tall, barefoot on the wet flagstones, wearing a coat stitched from something that might once have been scales and might still be, dull bronze gone grey at the seams. Her eyes were the problem. They were gold, slit-pupiled, and they caught the stone's glow and threw it back doubled. Her hands were human. Mostly.

"Wren," Jonah breathed. "You're real. Bram's stories—you're actually—"

"A half-blood exile, yes, the last one, don't make it sentimental." She descended the stairs with a predator's unhurried grace, and Sera noticed the way she moved around the pools of water—avoiding her own reflection. "Wren. Formerly of the Vaelish court of Ember-Remembering, currently of a leaking boathouse two miles north. And you're the archivist." Gold eyes fixed on Sera, and Sera felt catalogued, weighed, found interesting. "You smell like dust and defiance. Good. One of each is useful."

"You knew we were coming," Sera said.

"I knew the stone woke. Word travels in the places your Magistrate's sniffers can't follow." Wren's mouth curved, but without warmth. "The question is whether you two understood what you just did. Because that little payment of yours, thief-boy? It started a clock. There are four stones. You've lit the first. The other three felt it, and so did everything else that's been sleeping since the Sundering—including the things Corvane keeps fed and furious beneath the Magistracy."

"The fire," Jonah said. "Is it true it's guttering?"

That wiped the irony off her face. Wren looked at the stone for a long moment, and in the gold of her eyes Sera saw something she recognized from the widows and orphans who came to the archives asking for records of people the city preferred to forget: grief with nowhere to put itself.

"Guttering isn't the word," Wren said finally. "A guttering flame can be tended. This is a flame being *drunk*. Slowly. Systematically. Somewhere under this city, someone has been siphoning dragon-fire for a hundred years—thimble by thimble, year by year—enough to keep Greyhollow fat and comfortable and forgetting. Enough to power whatever sweet lies the Magistrate feeds you every festival day." Her claw-tipped fingers flexed. "There was a time the Vael burned bright enough to be seen from your shores. Now I dream of embers. *Embers.* Do you understand what that means to my kind?"

Sera thought of Corvane's face on the public screens, warm and paternal. *An era of safety,* he liked to say. She thought of the archive's sealed stacks, and which books were sealed, and who had signed the seals. The architecture of it assembled itself in her mind with terrible ease, the way a scholar's mind does—every fact clicking home like a drawer shutting.

"He's not containing the danger," she said aloud. "He *is* the danger. The ban, the Sundering laws, the sniffers—it's all a fence around his own private hearth."

"Now she's reading," Wren said approvingly.

Jonah had gone very quiet. He stood with the awakened stone cupped in both hands—when had he lifted it?—and its light climbed his face, and Sera saw that he was crying without seeming to notice. "My whole life," he said. "Every missing year. He didn't just erase me by accident. He *stockpiled* me."

"Yes," Wren said, not gently, because gentleness would have been a lie. "And here's the part you'll hate. The Codex doesn't promise you'll win, thief-boy. It promises you'll *choose*. That's what an heir is for. So choose quickly, and choose well, because the second stone sits in the vault of the man who raised you—and men who sell children's memories do not guard the merchandise out of love."

The stone pulsed in Jonah's hands. Outside, far off across the flats, Sera heard it—the first horn of the evening patrol, and beneath it, thinner, stranger, a sound like sniffing hounds tasting the wind.

They had perhaps an hour before the tide returned. Less, she suspected, before Corvane learned the first stone was awake.

Sera looked at Jonah—this charming, hollowed-out stranger with her career burning behind her like the notes she'd fed to a stove three nights ago—and found, to her own surprise, that she had already chosen. Perhaps she'd chosen the moment the ink had crawled toward his hand. Perhaps she'd chosen the day she first catalogued a book about dragons and felt homesick for a realm she'd never seen.

"Well then," she said, and was startled by how steady her voice came out. "We should go get your childhood back."

Jonah looked at her, and Wren grinned with too many teeth, and somewhere beneath the city, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.
