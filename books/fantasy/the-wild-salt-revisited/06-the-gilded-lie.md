## 6. The Gilded Lie

Magistrate Aldous Corvane had learned long ago that a city did not need to be free to be happy. It needed only to be *certain*. Certainty was the cheapest commodity in Greyhollow, and he was its sole licensed merchant.

He stood at the tall window of his chamber atop the Magistracy, watching the sun climb over the Salt Quarter's crooked roofs, and permitted himself the small vanity of admiring what he had built. Below, the morning markets were waking. Fishwives called their prices in voices roughened by brine. Children chased gulls along the seawall. A lamplighter made his drowsy rounds, snuffing flames that no longer served. It was, by every measure that mattered, a peaceful city.

That it was also a city of thieves and liars and desperate little magicians—that it sat, in fact, upon a wound in the world—was precisely why it required him.

"Magistrate." The voice came from the doorway without a knock. Only one person in Greyhollow entered his presence unannounced. "The Reservoir is holding at depth. Third siphon since midnight."

Corvane did not turn. "And the flame?"

"Flickering." Undersecretary Hale had the flat, careful voice of a man who had trained himself never to say anything worth punishing. "The archivists call it guttering. I'd prefer we not use that word in the public addresses."

"Guttering is an honest word." Corvane turned at last, folding his hands behind his back. He was sixty-one years old and wore his age like a uniform—silver at the temples, a spine kept straight by will rather than youth. His face was the face every citizen knew from the proclamations: patient, paternal, faintly sorrowful, as though order were a burden he carried for all their sakes. He had practiced that sorrow in a mirror for forty years until it stopped being practice. "Honesty is a private virtue, Hale. Publicly, we shall say *dimming*, which suggests a candle rather than a death. People forgive a candle its flicker. They do not forgive a death its cause."

Hale nodded and wrote nothing down, because there was nothing written that could survive a truth-sniffer's inspection. "The address is at noon. The Square will be full."

"The Square is always full. That is the point of the Square." Corvane crossed to the great mirror on the eastern wall—the Gilded Glass, his own private joke—and pressed his palm against its frame. Beneath the silvered surface, far deeper than any reflection, something answered: a slow pulse of heat, like a heart heard through a wall. He felt it the way a man feels his own pulse, constantly and without gratitude. "How much longer, would you estimate? Honestly."

Hale hesitated. "At current draw? A decade. Perhaps less if the winter is hard. The fire doesn't replenish anymore—not since—" He stopped himself.

"Not since the Sundering." Corvane supplied it mildly. "You may say it, Undersecretary. The Sundering. The breaking of the covenant. The severing of Greyhollow from Vael. We have a hundred years of euphemism; surely the two of us can survive the plain words between these walls." He smiled, and it was the smile from the proclamations, warm as banked coals. "Besides. There are only three people alive who know what actually broke the covenant, and two of them work for me."

"And the third?"

"The third," said Corvane, "is a fence in the Salt Quarter with a bad hip and a ledger he should have burned ten years ago. But we'll come to Bram. First, the city must be comforted."

* * *

Noon found Magistrate's Square packed shoulder to shoulder, ten thousand citizens beneath banners of grey and gold. Corvane ascended the steps of the Speaker's Rostrum to the sound of bells—real bells, rung by real hands, because the people trusted bells more than they trusted speeches and he saw no reason to spend trust when it flowed freely.

He let them look at him for a long moment before speaking. A pause, he had learned, did half the work of rhetoric. Ten thousand faces tilted upward like flowers toward a sun they believed in absolutely.

"Citizens of Greyhollow," he began, and his voice carried on the wind-channels cut into the stone, reaching even the farthest corners of the Square. "Thirty days ago, our truth-sniffers uncovered a conspiracy. Men and women dealing in stolen memory. Bottled pasts. Grief sold by the dram and joy sold by the flask. I know many of you purchased such things. I know some of you stand here now wearing borrowed happiness like a coat."

A ripple through the crowd—shame, fear, the delicious relief of confession-by-proxy. Corvane let it breathe.

"I am not angry." He spread his hands. "I am *grieved*. Because I understand why you went to those sellers. This city has wounds. We lost the dragons. We lost the old magic. We lost whatever bright certainty our grandparents had when the realms were whole. And when the world takes something from you, you want it back—even a counterfeit of it." His voice softened, and ten thousand people leaned in. "But counterfeits rot, friends. A stolen memory does not heal grief; it hollows you out and leaves the grief a larger house to live in. I have seen it happen. I have watched good citizens become strangers to their own children because they preferred a bottled yesterday to a living today."

He paused again. In the third row, a widow wept into her shawl, and Corvane noted her face for later. Gratitude was a currency too, and he collected it in every denomination.

"So today I offer you something better than a bottle." He lifted his hand, and on cue, the great Memory-Braziers flanking the rostrum roared alight—not with ordinary fire but with a soft golden luminescence that made every upturned face look young and washed and hopeful. "A new era of safety. From this day forward, the Magistracy will administer the Vigilance—a shared remembrance, freely given and honestly held, of the night we nearly destroyed ourselves. You will remember the Sundering not as a wound but as a lesson. Not as terror, but as wisdom. You will remember that we survived *together*."

What he did not say—and what no soul in the Square could have comprehended—was how the Vigilance worked. The Braziers drew from the Reservoir below, from the last hoarded vein of dragon-fire in the world, and laced it through the crowd like wine through water. Each citizen would walk home this evening carrying a small correction: the terror of the Sundering gentled into nostalgia, the rage of the dispossessed smoothed into patience, the dangerous questions filed away under a warm amber haze of *we are safe, we are cared for, we are certain*.

It was theft. Corvane knew it was theft, and he had long ago made his peace with the arithmetic of it. One hundred memories gently adjusted per citizen, ten thousand citizens, four adjustments a year—the interest compounded quietly in the dark while the principal, the great burning reserve of Vael's dying fire, drained a little lower with each administration.

They would call him a tyrant, if they ever knew. They would call him worse than the memory-thieves he hunted. Let them. The thieves stole memories for coin—for a widow's laugh in a bottle, for a drunkard's courage, for pocket change. Corvane stole for *order*. And order, he believed with a conviction that had long since calcified into conscience, was the only inheritance worth leaving. Chaos had broken the covenant. Chaos had burned Vael's sky and drowned Greyhollow's chapel and left orphans like Jonah Hartwell scrabbling in the gutters of the Salt Quarter. If a lie kept the peace, then the lie was load-bearing, and woe to the fool who pulled it out to see whether it held.

He finished the address to thundering applause, raised both arms in benediction, and descended the steps wreathed in golden light and the smell of warm amber—like honey, like summer, like safety itself. As the crowd dispersed, he watched them go: the widow dabbing dry eyes, a dockworker humming, children skipping with the untroubled lightness of the properly remembered. Every face serene. Every mind tidied.

"Beautifully done, Magistrate," murmured Hale at his elbow.

"It was necessary, which is better than beautiful." Corvane's eyes swept the dispersing crowd once, twice—then caught, like a fishhook on silk, on a figure at the Square's edge. An archivist's grey cloak. Dark hair escaping its pins. A woman standing utterly still amid the flowing contentment, her jaw set, her eyes *clear*.

Sera Voss. He'd had reports, of course—the archive raid, the burned notes, the escaped thief. Reports said she'd been questioned and released, her involvement judged incidental. Reports, Corvane reflected, were written by men whose noses were less honest than a hound's.

She was not weeping. She was not humming. She stood in the amber wash of his new era of safety like a nail refusing to be hammered down.

"She didn't take," Corvane said softly. More statement than question—he could see it in the set of her shoulders, the way she scanned the braziers rather than basking in them. Resistance. Either natural immunity, which happened perhaps one soul in ten thousand, or preparation, which meant she'd known what the Vigilance would be before he announced it. Which meant the Codex. Which meant *the heir*.

"Shall I have her followed?" Hale asked.

"No." Corvane smiled, and this smile was not for proclamations. It was private, and patient, and cold as the deep places of the Reservoir where the fire guttered in the dark. "Followers frighten clever people into running. Leave her be. A woman who resists the Vigilance will seek out others who resist it—and I have wanted an address on Jonah Hartwell for ten years." He turned from the window, from the Square, from the ten thousand safely sleeping minds below, and straightened his cuffs. "Send word to the sniffers. Quietly. Wherever Miss Voss goes next, we follow the trail, not the woman."

"And if the trail leads to the boy?"

Corvane paused at the door of his chamber, and beneath his feet—beneath ten thousand tons of granite and ceremony—the last dragon-fire pulsed once, dimmer than the year before, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"Then it leads," he said, "to the last loose thread of the Sundering. And I have waited thirty years to tie it off."

* * *

That night, alone in the Reservoir's deepest vault, Corvane descended the spiral stair by lamplight to make his accounting.

The cavern below the Magistracy had been a dragon-shrine once, back when shrines were honest things. Now it was a vault of glass and iron: a hundred vessels arrayed in concentric rings around the central basin, where the last uncontained fire of Vael swam in slow golden coils, thinning year by year like smoke deciding whether to become nothing.

He stood at the rail and watched it, and for one unguarded moment—for the first time in decades—let himself remember honestly. No Vigilance haze. No amber comfort. Just the truth, held privately, the way he held all his worst truths: close, and quiet, and never spoken.

He had been nineteen when the covenant broke. He had stood in this same cavern, younger and poorer and more afraid than he would ever admit, and he had watched a child—one small frightened child with a gift no child should carry—do the thing that sundered the worlds. Coerced, they'd called it later. Coerced by whom? By the men who found him. By the men who understood, before anyone else, what a memory-thief was truly worth. Corvane had not forced the boy's hand. He had merely arranged the room, and the fear, and the silence afterward—and then spent thirty years ensuring the arrangement held.

The fire guttered. The lie held. Somewhere in the Salt Quarter, a fence with a bad hip kept a ledger, and somewhere above it all, an archivist with clear eyes was walking toward answers Corvane had buried twice.

He pinched out the lamp and stood in the dark with the dying gold, unafraid. Fear was for cities. He had given his city every comfort he could manufacture, and taken in exchange the only payment that mattered: its certainty.

Let them sleep. Let them dream the gilded dream.

By morning, the sniffers would have their orders—and the hunt for the thief with no yesterday would begin in earnest.
