## 7. Ashes of the Second Stone

The rain had stopped by the time they reached Saltcutter Row, but the street still wept, water threading down the crooked gutters and pooling in the seams of cobblestones worn smooth by a century of smugglers' feet. Jonah pulled his collar up against the damp and tried not to look at Bram's door.

He looked anyway.

It was the same door it had been for nineteen years—warped at the bottom, iron-banded, painted a green that had long ago surrendered to grey. The same brass knocker shaped like a coiled eel. The same chipped step where he'd sat as a boy, shelling winkles while Old Bram haggled with dock thieves in the parlor. Everything was the same.

Everything except him.

*You sold me,* he thought, and the words had no heat yet, only weight, like a stone dropped into deep water still falling, waiting to hit bottom. *Ten years ago you sold me, piece by piece, and I ate supper at your table every night after.*

Beside him, Sera shifted. She'd traded her archivist's robes for a fishwife's oilskin that smelled of brine and old smoke, her white hair stuffed beneath a shapeless cap. Even disguised, she moved like someone who belonged among books, not gutted fish—careful, deliberate, as though the world were a fragile binding.

"The ledger said the vault is beneath the counting room," she murmured.

"I know where it is." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "I've known since I was twelve. Bram used to say the floorboards down there remembered everything, so best not to say anything worth remembering."

A bitter laugh escaped him before he could stop it. Sera touched his elbow—light, questioning—and he shook his head and started forward before the touch could become comfort. He wasn't sure he deserved comfort tonight. He wasn't sure Bram did either.

The eel-knocker was cold under his hand. He didn't knock. He pressed his palm flat against the wood and whispered the old thief's cadence, the one Bram had taught him when he was small enough to fit inside a chimney flue: three syllables, then silence, then two more—a rhythm with no meaning except to those who'd been let in before.

The lock turned. It always had for him.

Inside, the shop smelled of dust and lamp oil and, beneath both, something faintly burnt—the way memory itself smelled, if memory could be bottled and left too long on a shelf. Shelves climbed the walls, crowded with bottles of every color of glass, each one tagged in Bram's crabbed hand: *widow Aldren, first snowfall; tanner's boy, mother's voice; ferryman, the war, unopened.* A trade in other people's yesterdays. A trade Jonah had inherited without ever asking where the stock came from.

Now he knew. Some of it had come from him.

"Jonah." Sera closed the door behind them and dropped the bar into place. "If he's here—"

"He'll be here." Jonah lit a stub of candle from the mantel. "It's the third hour past midnight. He's always awake at this hour. Says sleep is just memory you can't bottle."

The counting room lay beyond the shop, through a curtain of oiled canvas. Jonah pushed it aside and descended the narrow stair, candle held high, and the dark below breathed back at him—cold, mineral, patient. The vault. Bram's true treasury, deeper than any bottle-shelf. Where the things too valuable or too dangerous for glass were kept.

The second covenant-stone was down there. He felt it before he saw anything, the way he sometimes felt a strong memory before he'd pried its lid loose—a pressure behind the eyes, a hum in the teeth. The Codex had called them *the salt-bound hearts of the sundered flame*, and standing at the bottom of the stairs with the candle shaking in his grip, Jonah finally understood that this was no figure of speech. The stones were warm. They were alive, in the way embers are alive.

The vault door was open.

Jonah froze on the last step. Sera bumped into his back, and he caught her arm to steady her, pressing one finger to his lips.

Bram stood in the center of the vault, lantern raised, an old man made mostly of shadow and stooped shoulders. He hadn't heard them—or pretended not to. Before him, on a table of scarred oak, the covenant-stone pulsed with a dull amber light, and around it, arranged with terrible neatness, lay papers. Ledger pages. Jonah knew them even upside down. He would know his own name written in Bram's ink anywhere.

"You came," Bram said, without turning. His voice was gravel soaked in wine. "I hoped you wouldn't. I prayed, actually. First time in forty years."

"You knew we were coming."

"I've known since Corvane's men came sniffing round my shop last week, asking after a boy with no yesterday." Now Bram turned, and the lantern light carved his face into ridges and hollows Jonah had memorized across a thousand suppers. "Sit down, lad. There's things—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of Jonah like a whip. He crossed the room in four strides and swept the ledger pages off the table. "Don't call me lad. Don't sit me down. Don't do whatever gentle thing you're about to do, because I have read your handwriting, Bram. Page by page. *Nineteen years, complete set, delivered in installments. Price agreed.* You didn't sell me a horse. You sold me *me*."

Bram's jaw worked. For a moment the old fence looked every one of his sixty-odd years, older, ancient, a man being weighed by something no scale could hold.

"Aye," he said. "I did."

"Why?" It came out as a whisper, which was worse than a shout. "Was it the money? Was it ever the money?"

"It was the money," Bram said quietly, "and then it wasn't. That's the filthiest part, boy. The first year, it was coin. Corvane paid well and asked no questions I wanted answered. But by the third installment—" his hand rose, trembling, toward Jonah's face and fell short, "—by the third, he told me what you were. What the memories were *for*. And I kept selling anyway, because he promised me that if I ever stopped, he'd take the rest himself. All at once. Leave you a husk drooling in a charity ward." Bram's eyes glistened. "So I fed you to him slow. One childhood per year. It was the only mercy left to buy."

Jonah stood very still. Somewhere above them, rain began again against the shop roof, soft as fingers.

"I don't believe you," he said.

"I know." Bram nodded slowly. "That's fair. That's more than fair." He lifted the covenant-stone from the table and held it out, amber light swimming up his wrist. "But believe this: I spent ten years trying to buy it back. Every coin, every favor, every secret I hoarded—I spent it all getting close enough to Corvane's vaults to steal you home. This stone was my collateral. My proof of value. The only thing the Magistrate wants more than your memories." His voice dropped. "Take it. Take it and run, and don't look back, and never forgive me, because forgiveness is a debt I haven't earned."

Jonah's hands wouldn't move. Grief and fury had braided themselves so tightly in his chest he couldn't tell one strand from another, couldn't tell whether he wanted to embrace the old man or put him through the wall. Loyalty said: *he raised you, he fed you, he taught you every trick you know.* Truth said: *he emptied you, year by year, and watched you thank him for supper.*

"Jonah," Sera breathed behind him. "The stairs. Do you hear—"

He heard it. Boots. Many boots, on the boards above, moving with the unhurried precision of men who already knew the layout. The bar on the shop door splintered with a sound like a bone breaking.

"Truth-sniffers," Sera whispered. "How—"

"The ledger pages," Jonah said, cold flooding him. "I scattered them. They were bait. He left them out because he knew I'd come, and he knew I'd read them, and—" He turned on Bram. "You *called* them."

"No," Bram said. And then, horribly, gently: "Yes. Both true, lad. Corvane gave me until midnight to deliver you or the stone. So I chose the only trade left to a dying fence. I sold myself instead." He shoved the stone into Jonah's hands—warm, impossibly warm, humming like a heart—and turned toward the stairs, drawing the long knife he'd carried since the Sundering riots. "Back wall. Third shelf. False bricks. Go."

"Bram—"

"*Go!*"

The door at the top of the stairs burst inward and the lantern light filled with shapes—grey cloaks, pale masks, the long brass snouts of the truth-sniffers' scenting rigs, already twitching toward the warmth of the stone. The first one came down the steps and Bram met him like a landslide, all sixty years of him, knife flashing, roaring curses in dockside cant that Jonah had learned at this man's knee.

Sera hauled Jonah toward the false bricks, but he couldn't make his legs obey. He stood rooted, watching the old man fight—watching Bram take a blade through the shoulder and keep swinging, watching him go down under three masks and rise again, blood sheeting his forearm, buying seconds with the only currency he had left.

"Lad!" Bram caught Jonah's eye across the chaos, and in that look there was no apology left to give, only a final transaction, the last honest trade of a dishonest life. "The third stone! Corvane keeps it under the Magistracy—in the *Foundry*, beneath the fire-vats, where he feeds the flame! Remember it, boy! Remember *for* me!"

Then they brought him down for the last time, and Jonah ran.

He ran because running was the only thing left that Bram had given him freely. Sera dragged him through the false bricks into the smuggling crawl beneath Saltcutter Row, and behind them the shop he'd grown up in went up in lamplight and shouting, and the amber stone burned in his fists like something grieving.

In the dark of the tunnel, pressed against wet stone with Sera's breath ragged beside him, Jonah finally wept—for the father who'd sold him, for the father who'd died buying him back, for the boy who'd loved them both and couldn't tell anymore which love was real. The stone pulsed against his chest, and in its warmth he felt, faintly, unmistakably, a memory that was not his own: an old man at a workbench, late at night, whispering over a bottle of amber glass.

*Forgive me, lad. Forgive me, forgive me, forgive me.*

Jonah wiped his face with the back of his hand. Above, through the crawl-space grate, the glow of the burning shop stained the tunnel ceiling orange, and somewhere far off, at the heart of Greyhollow, the Magistracy's spires stood black against the smoke.

The Foundry. Beneath the fire-vats.

"Tomorrow," he said to Sera, and his voice came out scraped raw and harder than he'd ever heard it. "We go tomorrow. And we take everything he has."
