## 8. The Price of Remembering

The drowned chapel had given them two stones. The third sat cold against Jonah's palm now, and the other two hummed in answer, a low triad like struck glass, and he understood—without being told, without wanting to know—that they would not give up what he needed unless he gave them something first.

*Blood is for bargains,* Wren had said, folding her scaled forearms as she watched from the chapel's broken archway. *Memory is for kin. If you mean to claim them, thief, you'll have to open every door in yourself and let them walk through.*

Sera stood beside him on the salt-crusted floor, her hand hovering near his shoulder without touching it. "You don't have to do this tonight."

"I do." His voice came out flat, strange to him. "Corvane has the fourth stone behind the Magistracy walls, and Bram died telling me where it was kept. I can't face him with holes in me. He made those holes, Sera. I want to see every one of them."

She nodded once, slowly, and stepped back. Even Wren said nothing more. The chapel held its breath around them—the sea sighing through the collapsed roof, moonlight lying in pale bars across the flooded nave, the stones' light pulsing faintly beneath his skin-warm fingers.

Jonah sat down in the shallows, because standing felt like pride and kneeling felt like prayer, and he wanted neither. Then he closed his eyes and let go.

The first door opened before he was ready for it.

* * *

He was nine years old again, and the world smelled of tallow and wet wool.

Not Greyhollow—not the city he knew, with its gaslamps and gull-cries and the perpetual grey drizzle off the Sound. This was Vael. He knew it the way you know your own name: the air itself was different here, thick with a warmth that came from below, from the deep veins of dragon-fire that ran beneath the realm like a second sun. The sky above the terraced streets was the color of brass at noon, and dragons moved across it the way clouds move—casual, immense, unhurried.

His mother's hand was in his. He could feel her calluses, the ink stain on her thumb. She had been a memory-smith—a maker, not a thief, though the trades were cousins. She sang while she worked, half-forgotten songs about the First Accord, when men and dragons had sworn their realms into balance: fire for memory, memory for fire. Each fed the other. Each needed the other.

And Jonah—he was not yet Jonah then; that name came later, salted down for hiding—but whatever he was called, he was a child who could slip into a mind like a hand into a glove, who could lift a grief out of a stranger and leave a kindness in its place, and everyone said he was the finest gift the guild had seen in three generations.

He saw himself at nineteen, too, later in the flood of years: proud, hungry, already half-corrupted by the flattery of powerful clients. He saw the night Corvane came to him—young then himself, sharp-faced, ambitious, wearing the Magistracy's new grey like a skin he hadn't grown into—and offered him a commission no one else would take.

*There is a vault beneath the Spire of Accord,* Corvane had said, in a room that smelled of cedar and lies. *A covenant-stone. The dragons keep their half of the bargain locked away where no man can touch it. But the covenant binds both realms, boy, and both realms deserve to know what's in their own house. You're going to open it for me.*

*I'm not a burglar,* young Jonah had said, with a teenager's contempt. *I'm an artist.*

*You're whatever I pay you to be.* And then, softer, the poison working in: *Do this, and I'll see your mother's debts dissolved. Refuse, and the guild learns what you really are. What you did to that magistrate's son last winter. They outlawed your kind once already, Jonah. It wouldn't take much.*

So he had gone. Nineteen years old and thinking himself clever, thinking himself owed, he had walked into the deep places beneath the Spire of Accord and put his hands on the covenant-stone, and he had done exactly what Corvane asked. He had reached into the stone's sleeping mind the way he reached into human minds, and he had *pulled*.

The Sundering, the scholars called it afterward. The night the realms tore apart.

But it wasn't a tearing, not from inside. It was a theft. One boy's greedy, frightened, coerced little theft, and the whole architecture of two worlds had come down around it like a roof whose keystone some idiot pried loose to see what held it up.

Dragons had screamed across a sky that suddenly had no bottom. His mother had come running through the collapsing guildhall, shouting his childhood name, and the last thing she ever said to him was drowned in the roar of the sundering fire—

—and then Corvane's men had taken him, and Corvane himself had leaned close and whispered, almost tenderly: *You're the only witness to what happened down there. So we're going to make sure there is no witness.* And he had brought in the guild's own butchers, the memory-eaters, and they had scraped Jonah hollow from his earliest day to the edge of the Spire, and everything they took went into jars, and the jars went into Corvane's keeping, and the empty boy was shipped across the Sundering-sea to Greyhollow with a new name and a fence named Old Bram paid to raise him and watch him.

Paid. Not chosen. *Paid.*

* * *

Jonah came back to himself screaming.

Salt water in his mouth, Sera's arms around him, the stones blazing against his chest like three small suns. Wren had recoiled to the far wall, wings half-spread, her reptilian eyes wide with something between horror and recognition.

"You," she breathed. "The Codex says *the thief with no yesterday*. We thought it meant someone born without a past. It doesn't. It means someone whose past was *taken*—by the same hand that broke the world." Her voice dropped. "By you. You broke it."

"I know." His own voice sounded scraped raw, like something dragged over gravel. "I know. I was there."

Sera held his face in her hands, forcing him to look at her. Moonlight and stone-light tangled in her dark hair. "Jonah. Listen to me. You were nineteen. He threatened your mother. He blackmailed you. That's not a choice, that's a cage."

"I still did it." The words tasted like bile. "I tell myself all day, every day, that I sell people back their own happiness—that my little bottles are mercy. And maybe they are. But underneath, Sera, underneath all of it, I'm the boy who pulled the keystone out because a man in grey told him he'd be punished if he didn't. Everything since—every charm, every bottle, every stolen laugh I've sold in that market—the whole rotten trade started with me reaching into something sacred and taking what didn't belong to me."

"Yes." She said it simply, without softening it, and somehow that was worse than any argument. "And Corvane built a cage around a child and then spent ten years making sure that child never grew up enough to remember the door. Jonah—if you'd known what you were, if you'd remembered your mother's face or the sound of Vael's sky, do you honestly believe you'd have spent a decade selling bottled comforts in a fish-market? He didn't just erase your past. He erased the man you might have become, and then left the *shape* of him behind to drift."

Jonah laughed, and it came out broken. "That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you *angry*." Her grip tightened. "Self-loathing is just another way of making it about you. He wants you small, Jonah. He wants you curled around your guilt like a snail in its shell, because a man drowning in shame never comes for the man who filled the water. Get up. Be furious instead. Fury points outward."

For a long moment he couldn't move. The memories were still settling in him like sediment after a storm—the smell of his mother's workshop, the weight of her hand, the terrible brass sky, the exact pitch of Corvane's whisper in the dark under the Spire. All of it his again. All of it his to carry.

And beneath the shame, slowly, something else began to move. Not forgiveness—not yet, maybe never entirely—but a kind of clearing weather. Because he had spent ten years believing himself empty, a man-shaped absence walking through Greyhollow, and absence cannot be guilty of anything. Now he knew: he was not empty. He was *full*—full of a mother who sang, full of a realm of brass skies and patient dragons, full of a crime committed by a terrified boy and a monstrous man, and the monstrous man was still alive, still ruling, still feeding on the fire he'd helped steal.

Guilt was a stone you carried. Purpose was what you built with the stones you were given.

"I forgive myself," Jonah said quietly, testing the words like ice on a pond. "Not because I deserve it. Because he needs me not to. Because every hour I spend hating that nineteen-year-old kid is an hour I'm not spending tearing down everything Corvane built on top of him." He looked down at the three covenant-stones in his lap, glowing softly now, warm as living things. "He took my yesterday. Fine. He can keep it. I'm going to take his tomorrow."

Wren pushed off the wall and came toward them, and for the first time since he'd met her, the half-dragon exile inclined her head—an old gesture, courtly, from a realm that no longer existed. "Then you understand what you are. Not the thief with no yesterday." Her eyes glittered. "*The heir who remembers.* The Codex has a second verse, archivist. Most scholars think it's corruption in the text. I don't." She recited it low, in a tongue that made the stones flare:

*"When the thief reclaims his name,*
*the fire will know its own,*
*and the fourth stone will wake*
*in the house of the liar."*

Sera went very still. "The fourth stone. In the Magistracy."

"In Corvane's vault," Jonah said, and the memory rose in him whole and clear—the vault he'd been led through at nineteen, the shelves of glowing jars, the stone in its iron cradle at the center, drinking light. "I've seen it. I've *been* there. It's the one place in Greyhollow I could navigate blindfolded, because I walked those corridors before Corvane wiped them from my head—and now he hasn't."

He stood, dripping, and the stones rose with him, orbiting his clenched fist like three small moons.

"Get some rest, both of you," he said. "Tomorrow night, we go to the Magistracy. Tomorrow night, I introduce the Magistrate to the only witness he failed to erase."

Above the broken chapel roof, far to the north, the spires of Greyhollow caught the moonlight—and somewhere among them, Jonah knew, the last dragon stirred in her chains, and the fourth stone waited in the dark like a heart waiting to be restarted.
