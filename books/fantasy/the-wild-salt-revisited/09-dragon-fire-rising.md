## 9. Dragon-Fire Rising

The Magistracy had no doors for men like Jonah. It had vents, drains, and a hundred small dishonesties of architecture, and he had spent three nights learning them from the inside of Sera's borrowed maps. Now, in the hour when the lamps along the Spireway guttered low and the watch changed with the sleepy ritual of men who had never once been truly needed, he climbed.

The drain shaft smelled of old salt and older lies. He moved by feel, palms flat against wet stone, and the two covenant-stones at his throat burned against his skin like twin coals that had learned his heartbeat and refused to be soothed by it. *Thief with no yesterday,* the Codex called him. Tonight the name fit like a key fits a lock—smoothly, inevitably, with the terrible click of something opening that could not be shut again.

He thought about Bram as he climbed. He tried not to, and did it anyway. The old man's last breath had been spent buying Jonah three more minutes of life, and grief was a debt you couldn't fence or sell; it simply sat in your chest accruing interest. So Jonah climbed, and let the cold stone take the heat out of him one handhold at a time.

Above him, through a grate crusted with verdigris, he could hear the sound.

It was not a roar. Roars were for creatures with room to make them. This was smaller—a long, ragged exhale, the sound of something vast breathing in a space built to hold cattle. And beneath it, fainter, a second sound: machinery. Chains. The patient mechanical slurp of dragon-fire being drawn off somewhere below, piped upward through copper veins into the heart of Greyhollow's beloved order.

Jonah pressed his eye to the grate and looked down into the Furnace Hall.

* * *

Sera Voss stood in the public gallery of the Magistracy's atrium, wearing her archivist's badge like a knife she hadn't decided whether to use.

She had walked in the front entrance. That had been Wren's idea—the half-dragon's idea, delivered in that dry rasp while sharpening a claw on the cobbles outside the drowned chapel: *"They'll search every shadow for the thief. No one searches the woman holding a legitimate pass."* So Sera had pinned on her credentials, straightened her collar, and walked past two truth-sniffers who turned their blunt snouts toward her, tasted the air, and found nothing but the honest stink of paper dust and fear.

Fear was allowed. Fear wasn't a lie.

Below her, the atrium was dressing itself for Corvane's great unveiling. Banners of grey silk. A podium draped in the city's seal—the chained dragon rampant, which everyone had always assumed was merely heraldry. Workers hauled brass piping up the walls, and Sera, watching, understood at last what she was seeing: the pipes were connected to the spire above, and the spire was connected to the Furnace Hall below, and the whole beautiful building was a still. Greyhollow's safety, its calm streets and untroubled mornings, distilled drop by drop from a captive creature's burning heart.

A bell rang. The crowd hushed.

Magistrate Corvane descended the grand stair, and Sera felt the crowd's love for him move through her like weather. He was silver-haired and warm-eyed, and he greeted a child by name. *He knows everyone's name,* she thought. *That's how you know how much he has stolen.* She had read enough of the Century Codex now to understand what the man was: not a tyrant in the old stories' mold, all fire and cruelty, but something worse—a gardener. A man who pulled memories the way one pulls weeds, and called the resulting neatness peace.

Her hand found the third covenant-stone's location written in code inside her sleeve: *beneath the dais, behind the seal, where the first lie is kept.*

All she had to do was wait for Jonah.

* * *

The Furnace Hall was vast and hot and wrong.

Chains thick as Jonah's waist ran from iron collars set into the floor—collars sized for a neck he didn't want to imagine—to a great copper engine that drank with a slow rhythmic pulse. Heat shimmered over everything. And in the center, half-lit by its own dying glow, lay the last dragon.

It was smaller than the stories. Starved down to essentials, scales gone dull as old pewter, wings folded tight against a body that had clearly stopped believing in them years ago. Its eyes were open. They were the color of the sea before a storm decides, and they fixed on Jonah the moment he dropped from the vent, and Jonah felt himself seen—truly seen, memory and all, every stolen thing laid out like goods on a market blanket.

"You," the dragon said. Its voice was rust and embers. "The little breaker returns."

Jonah's mouth went dry. "You know me."

"I knew you small." The dragon's breath rattled. "I held you on my palm when the world was whole. Then you grew, and men in grey coats came with sweet voices, and you gave them the word, child. One word from you opened the covenant's lock. You were seven. They told you it was a game."

The stones at Jonah's throat flared white-hot. He staggered—and the flood came early, unbidden: rain on a summer fair, a huge gentle eye, laughter, then the grey coats, then *say the word, boy, say the lovely word*—

"I remember," he whispered.

"Then you are nearly ready." The dragon closed its eyes. "But ready is not free. Nothing here is free. Ask your magistrate—he has built an empire on the difference."

Jonah drew his picks and went for the engine instead of the chains. Break the tap, and the theft stops. The copper was old and the wards upon it were Corvane's own design—wards built to repel force, not craft. Jonah had spent his whole life being craft.

The first ward peeled like wet paper. The second screamed.

Alarms bloomed red through the hall.

* * *

In the atrium, Sera watched Corvane pause mid-sentence, head tilting like a bird's, and knew.

"Ladies and gentlemen," the Magistrate said pleasantly, "it seems we have a rat in the cellar. Do forgive me—I must go and set a trap."

The crowd chuckled indulgently. Sera was already moving, shoulder-first through the press of bodies, toward the service stairs, toward the dais, toward the sealed panel behind the city's chained-dragon crest. Her hands shook. Her hands had never shaken in twenty years of archives. She pried the panel open with a letter-opener and there it was: the third covenant-stone, black as a drowned star, humming against her palm like a living thing recognizing kin.

Behind her, a truth-sniffer's nostrils flared. Somewhere above, steel rang on stone.

She ran.

* * *

They met at the top of the spire because the story demanded it, though neither of them would have phrased it that way. Jonah came up the inner stair three steps ahead of six guards, the freed dragon's fire roaring back to life beneath him like a sun remembering dawn—the beast had torn its own chains loose once the tap was broken, and the whole Magistracy shuddered around its rising. Sera came up the outer gallery with the third stone clutched to her chest and Wren dropping from the sky beside her like judgment arriving early.

Corvane stood at the spire's crown, alone, unhurried, silhouetted against a sky beginning to change color in ways skies should not.

"Jonah Hartwell." The Magistrate's voice carried without effort, warm as a hearth. "Do you know what's happening below us? Look."

Greyhollow spread out beneath them in the smoky dark—and it was burning. Not the buildings. The *memories*. All across the city, people had stopped mid-stride, mid-laugh, mid-embrace, clutching their heads as the false comforts Corvane had fed them for decades caught light and peeled away. A widow on the Spireway suddenly remembered her husband's real laugh—not the bottled one, the true one, cracked and imperfect—and sat down hard on the cobbles and wept. Whole neighborhoods were waking from a lullaby fifty years deep.

"They'll riot," Corvane said. "They'll burn each other. They cannot bear the truth—that is why I took it from them. I have kept this city safe since before you were born, boy. Every peaceful morning, purchased. And you—" he sighed, genuinely sorrowful, and it was the most frightening thing yet— "you are about to undo it all for a dragon and a dead man's prophecy."

"I'm going to finish what I started when I was seven," Jonah said. "One way or another."

Corvane smiled. And then he opened his hand, and in it lay a vial.

Jonah's breath stopped. He knew that glass. He'd sold ten thousand like it. But this one held something golden and enormous, something that made the air bend around it, and even at forty feet he could feel it calling to him with his own voice: *rain on a summer fair, a huge gentle eye, Bram's kitchen, his mother's hands, a birthday, a name, a whole childhood, whole and waiting—*

"Your yesterday," Corvane said softly. "All of it. Every year I bought from Bram, preserved perfectly, never spent. Hand me the stones, walk away, and wake up tomorrow a boy again on the inside—a man who remembers being loved." His voice gentled further. "No one else will ever offer you this. Not the dragon. Not the girl. Not your gods, if they exist. Me. Because only I understand what a memory is worth."

The spire wind tore at them. Below, the dragon rose against the smoke, wings unfurling for the first time in a century, and the stones at Jonah's throat pulsed like a second heartbeat.

Sera's hand found his.

"Jonah," she said quietly. "Whatever you choose—it's yours to choose. That's the whole point of you."

Corvane extended the vial across the gulf between them, and the golden light of a stolen childhood swayed there, close enough to touch, warm as a lit window on a winter night.

"Choose quickly," the Magistrate said. "The fire is rising."
