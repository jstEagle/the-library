## 1. Ink and Ash

The ink froze in the well before the third hour, as it always did in the cold months, and Ada Zhou had learned to work around it the way a man learns to walk with a stone in his boot—without complaint, and without ever quite forgetting it was there.

He sat in the scriptorium of the temple of Thornfell, third desk from the drafty window, copying the Litany of the Ninefold Tide for what he estimated was the four-hundredth time. *Blessed are the still waters. Blessed are the deep roads.* His letters were good letters. Round, patient, obedient. A scribe's letters. Nobody looked twice at a scribe, and that was precisely how Ada had arranged his life.

Nine years ago he had come down out of the mountains with frostbitten ears, a false name, and a debt that wasn't entirely his but had been pinned to him anyway—the ledger of the Zhou family's passage debts, sold and resold until it landed, like a stone dropped from a great height, on the youngest son who happened to still be findable. The temple had taken him in. The temple had also taken his labor at four coppers a day against a debt that grew interest like mold grows in a wet cellar.

So Ada had done what smugglers do: he'd found another market.

Twice a month, a carter named Brunn came to the temple gate with salt fish and left with more than salt fish. For a modest fee, paid into the hands of a moneylender in Lowmarket who did not ask questions, Ada told Brunn which passes were watched, which were washed out, which were safe for cargo that didn't want to meet a customs man. It was not glorious work. It was not even particularly dangerous work anymore—he knew the Thornfell mountains better than the king's own surveyors, because the king's own surveyors had never carried a hundredweight of untaxed tin through the Hollow Stair in February. But it kept the ledger shrinking by a finger's width every season, and it kept Ada invisible, and invisibility, he had long ago decided, was the only wealth worth hoarding.

"Blessed are the still waters," he murmured, dipping his pen.

"You're muttering again," said Brother Odo from the next desk, without looking up. Odo was seventy if he was a day, half-blind, and could apparently hear a scribe breathe wrong from across a room.

"I'm praying."

"Prayers don't have that tone. Prayers don't sound like a man arguing with his supper." Odo squinted at him with one milky eye. "You argue like your ink is expensive."

"It *is* expensive."

"Everything's expensive to you, Zhou. That's why you count everything twice." Odo returned to his own page, satisfied. He was one of the few people in the temple who had noticed anything about Ada at all, and even he had noticed only the wrong things—that Ada counted, that Ada flinched at loud doors, that Ada never spoke of before Thornfell. The right thing to notice would have been that Ada Zhou was two people wearing one robe, and neither of them entirely honest.

Ada copied. *Blessed are the deep roads.* Outside, the wind came off the mountain with its teeth showing, and somewhere below in the wash-yard, the temple's washerwomen beat linens against stones in a rhythm like a slow heart.

He was on the second column of the page when the screaming started.

It came up through the floorboards—not the sharp scream of an argument or a dropped kettle, but the low, grinding wail of pain that has been going on for a long time and expects to go on longer. The other scribes didn't so much as lift their heads. Illness in the temple was weather; you learned to copy through it. But Ada set down his pen.

He would spend the rest of his life wondering why. He had passed a hundred dying people in nine years and kept his eyes on his feet, because looking was how you got involved, and involvement was how you got seen. But something about this wail hooked under his ribs and pulled, and before he had decided anything at all, he was down the stairs and out into the gray light of the wash-yard, ink still on his fingers.

She lay on the flagstones by the washing-troughs, an old woman with arms like knotted rope and a washerwoman's apron soaked through where she'd coughed blood onto it. Two younger women knelt beside her, wringing their hands uselessly. One of them looked up at Ada with desperate, borrowed hope—the way people look at anyone in a temple robe, as though the robe itself were a kind of medicine.

"She won't let us carry her inside," the young one said. "She keeps saying the floor knows her."

"Ma'am," Ada said, crouching. "Let them help you. There's warmth inside, and Mother Cassia knows fever-work."

The old woman's hand shot out and closed around his wrist with a strength that astonished him. Her skin was cold as river-stone. Her eyes found his, and they were bright with the particular terror of someone who has spent her whole life believing exactly enough, and now, at the end, needs to believe completely and finds the cupboard bare.

"The new gods don't hear me," she said. "I've prayed to them all my life and they're polite about it, that's all. Polite. Like clerks." She pulled him closer; he could smell lye and blood on her breath. "But the old ones—the ones under the mountain—the ones my grandmother whispered to when the tide came up wrong—you know them, boy. You've walked their roads. I can see it in you, you've got the look of the high paths about you. Tell them. Someone tell them I'm here. Tell them a woman is dying who was faithful before there was a church to be faithful *to*—"

"Ma'am—" Ada tried gently to free his wrist. "There are no old gods. There's just the Nine, and the Nine are—"

"Mercy," she said. "I'm not asking for a miracle. I'm asking for somebody to *say it*. Say it out loud, so it's been said. So the mountain heard a woman ask."

And Ada—who had spent nine years making himself a man no one asked anything of, who had built his whole life out of the principle that the safest mouth is a shut one—heard himself say:

"*Hear her.*"

Except it wasn't his voice.

The words came out of his own throat, but they arrived in the yard like something surfacing from very deep water—low, layered, a voice with a current running through it, the voice of a wave that has traveled a thousand miles to end here, on these flagstones, over this woman. The two young washerwomen went pale. Ada felt his lips move and had nothing to do with it.

"*Daughter of the wash-stones,*" his mouth said, in that voice that made the trough water shiver, "*you were not forgotten. You were unheard. These are different griefs, and I will answer the second one.*"

The old woman stopped shaking. Something went out of her face—not life, exactly, but fear. She began, very quietly, to weep, the way children weep, without any attempt at dignity.

"*Your hands are clean,*" the voice said through Ada, gentle as tide over sand. "*No one can wash away what was never stained. Come up when you come up. The water will be warm.*"

Her grip on his wrist loosened. She smiled—a real smile, cracked and gap-toothed—and then her head tipped back against the trough, and she breathed once more, softly, like someone settling into sleep, and did not breathe after that.

The yard was silent except for the wind.

Ada knelt there with his arm still extended, held in the ghost of a dead woman's grip, and slowly, carefully, closed his mouth. Then opened it. Then, very deliberately, said, in his own voice, small and cracked: "That wasn't—I didn't—"

The two washerwomen were staring at him. Not with gratitude. Gratitude he could have handled. They were staring at him the way people stare at a dog that has suddenly spoken, or a wall that has begun to bleed—with the dawning, horrified understanding that the world contains less solid ground than advertised.

"He spoke," whispered the older of the two. "Brothers and sisters, he spoke with the—"

"Don't." Ada was on his feet now, backing away, hands up, palms out, the universal gesture of the man who wants no trouble. "Don't say it. Don't say whatever word you're about to say. I'm a scribe. I copy prayers. I don't—" *perform them*, his mind finished, and the word made his stomach drop through the flagstones.

They were still staring. And Ada understood, with terrible clarity, that he had just become visible for the first time in nine years. Every careful inch of his constructed life—the round obedient letters, the counted coins, the second desk from the window—had been a wall, and he had just walked straight through it in front of witnesses.

Somewhere behind him, a door opened onto the yard. Heavy steps. The specific, unhurried steps of a man who owns the building you're standing in.

Father Anselm, high priest of Thornfell, stood in the doorway with the morning's correspondence still in his hand, and his eyes went from the dead woman, to the weeping washerwomen, to Ada—ink-stained, trembling, thoroughly and catastrophically *seen*.

"Brother Zhou," Anselm said pleasantly. Too pleasantly. "A moment of your time."

* * *

Anselm led him not to the scriptorium but to the small chapel behind the reliquary, the one where the temple kept its oldest, least-visited icons—saints whose names had worn smooth, a tide-font nobody blessed anymore. He closed the door. He did not light a candle. In the gray light his face was unreadable, which was a skill Ada recognized, because it was one of his own.

"A washerwoman dies in the yard," Anselm said, "and the last words she hears come out of your mouth in a voice the whole yard swears was not yours. Two women are downstairs crying and lighting candles to gods we don't keep on our altar. So." He folded his hands. "Explain it to me. Slowly. As a friend."

"As a friend," Ada said, "I'd tell you that grief makes people hear things."

"Mm." Anselm turned one of the smooth old icons over on its shelf, idly, the way a man turns a stone to see what's underneath. "As a friend, then, let me tell you something. When I was a young priest in Saltmere, there was a fisherwoman who spoke in the voice of the deep during a storm. The church called it hysteria. The village called it miracle. She was burned for it either way—the church couldn't allow the first and the village couldn't forgive the second." He set the icon down. "Which story do you imagine they'll tell about you, Brother Zhou? And which do you imagine the archprelate in Vail will pay to hear?"

Ada's mouth was dry. "I don't know what you want from me."

"Honesty. Just once. From the man who counts everything twice." Anselm leaned close, and for the first time Ada saw that beneath the pleasantness the old priest was afraid—genuinely, deeply afraid, in the manner of a man who has read something in an old book and hoped all his life never to meet it in a courtyard. "Because three nights ago, Brother Zhou, the tide came up the river against the wind. The bells rang with no hand on them. And last night I dreamed of a woman made of salt water standing in this chapel, asking me who had been keeping her house." He straightened. "So before I decide whether to report you, protect you, or pray over you—I suggest you decide, quickly, whether you believe in the old gods."

And somewhere beneath Ada's breastbone, warm as a coal wrapped in wool, something stirred, and answered, before he could stop it:

*Ask me properly, little scribe, and I will tell you my name.*

Ada's knees nearly went out from under him. He gripped the shelf. Anselm was watching him with those unreadable eyes, waiting for an answer, while inside Ada's own body something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, patiently, to wake.
