## 2. The God in His Throat

Ada Zhou woke with salt on his lips.

This was, he noted with the detached calm of a man cataloguing his own ruin, deeply wrong. He lived four days' ride from the sea. He had never once woken tasting it. He lay very still in his narrow cell above the scriptorium and stared at the ceiling beams and thought, *I dreamed it. The washerwoman. The voice. All of it.*

His mouth disagreed. His mouth tasted like a harbor at low tide.

"Right," he said aloud, to the empty room. "Absolutely not."

He got up anyway, because a scribe's morning did not care what lived in his chest. He washed, he dressed, he went down the cold stair to the scriptorium where Brother Callum was already hunched over his desk like a question mark that had given up on punctuation.

"You look terrible," Callum said without looking up.

"I slept badly."

"You slept badly for nine years. It's called having a conscience." Callum dipped his pen. "High Priest Ovid wants you after matins. Something about the Miriam woman."

Ada's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the foundation stones, and kept going. "The washerwoman? Is she—"

"Died at dawn. Peaceful, they say. Smiling, they say." Callum finally looked up, and his eyes were sharp behind their ink stains. "Which is odd, isn't it, since she came in screaming yesterday with her lungs full of water. Odd that she died smiling. Odder still that when she grabbed your sleeve, every candle in the nave flared blue."

"I didn't notice any candles."

"No," Callum agreed mildly. "You wouldn't have."

* * *

The morning passed in a haze of copying that Ada could not do. He sat before a psalm about the steadfastness of the New Faith and found his hand writing, in the margin, small and neat: *the tide does not ask permission.* He stared at it. He scraped it away with a knife until the vellum thinned dangerously.

*You scrape me off the page,* said the voice, warm as sun-warmed stone, amused as a wave finding a new inlet. *But I am not on the page, little scribe.*

"Not now," Ada whispered into his inkwell.

*I have been bound beneath this mountain for three hundred years. You will forgive me if I lack your sense of timing.*

"I'm copying a psalm."

*You are copying lies in a pretty hand. I know. I watched you do it yesterday, and the day before, and for years. You press too hard when you don't believe the words. Did you know that? Every prayer you doubt comes out with little claws at the ends of the letters.*

Ada looked down at his work. He pressed harder when he doubted. He had always known that, in the way you know things about yourself that you refuse to examine, the way he refused to examine why he could still draw the Thornfell passes from memory, every smuggler's trail, every goat path the temple forbade, all of it filed away in his hands like contraband.

"Who are you?" he breathed.

*A name was taken from me. But the fisherfolk called me Yara. The Salt-Mother. And you, Ada Zhou, are the first mouth I have touched in three centuries that did not break.*

"Why me?"

There was a pause, and in it he felt something vast and old and terribly tired consider him.

*Because you were drowning too,* she said simply. *And I answered.*

* * *

She showed him at noon, while he sat in the chapel pretending to pray, which was easier than pretending to copy.

The vision took him like cold water takes a swimmer—not violently, but completely. He stood somewhere that was not the chapel: a great hall hung with banners, and on a throne sat King Aldric, and Ada knew it was Aldric because the king's face was on every coin Ada had ever smuggled across a border.

But it was wrong. The face was right and the man was wrong. Aldric sat very still, too still, the stillness of a puppet with one string left. And around his brow—

The crown was moving.

Not gleaming. Not catching light. *Moving*, slow as oil in water, and where its points touched the king's temples, Ada saw—or Yara made him see—the thin dark threads running inward, down through skin and skull, drinking. With each pulse of those threads, Aldric's eyes went whiter. With each pulse, the king smiled a smile that belonged to no living man.

*Three hundred years ago,* Yara said, her voice rolling through the vision like surf, *something was cast down. Not killed—we could not kill it. It was too hungry to die and too clever to starve. So we broke it, as you break a loaf, and we scattered the pieces, and we bound me beneath the mountain to sit on the largest piece and keep it sleeping.*

"And the crown?" Ada's voice sounded far away, a boy's voice, a drowned voice.

*One piece was never found. It waited. It learned patience—it is very good at patience, it has nothing else. A smith found it in a riverbed. A king put it on his head. And now it feeds, and grows, and remembers what it was, and dreams of being whole again.* The tide of her voice went dark. *It felt me wake yesterday, Ada Zhou. When I spoke through you. It knows I have a mouth in the world again. And it will come looking.*

The vision snapped shut. Ada found himself gripping the pew so hard the wood creaked, sweat rolling down his spine, and the chapel utterly ordinary around him, dust motes and candle-smell and God's honest nothing.

"No," he said.

*I beg your pardon?*

"No. No, no, no." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "I copy prayers. I owe the temple eleven marks. I once smuggled cheese, Yara. *Cheese.* I am not—I am not a vessel, I am not a hero, I am nobody. That's the whole point of me. Nobody looks at Ada Zhou. That's how I've survived."

Silence. Then, gently, the way the sea is gentle right before it takes your footing:

*Little scribe. You hid so well you forgot there was anything worth hiding. But I have been asleep under stone for three hundred years, and even I can see it—you were never nobody. You were somebody pretending very hard.*

"That's not the same thing."

*It is exactly the same thing, worn inside out.*

He put his head in his hands. Above him, the chapel bell rang for none, and he did not move.

* * *

High Priest Ovid found him at dusk in the reliquary corridor, which was where Ada went to feel guilty in historically significant surroundings.

Ovid was seventy, dry as August grass, with eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. Nine years, and Ada had never seen him hurry. He hurried now.

"Brother Zhou." Even out of breath, he managed the title like an accusation. "Miriam the washerwoman. Tell me what happened in the nave."

"She was dying. She asked the old gods for mercy. I held her hand. She died."

"The candles burned blue, brother."

"Candles do that. Wax. Drafts."

"The blue flame bent toward you, Ada." Ovid never used his name. The use of it now landed like a dropped book in a silent room. "Fourteen witnesses. And when they carried her body out, the seawater on her shroud had dried into a pattern the fishwives recognized instantly. They're calling it a blessing. They're calling it *Yara's mark.*"

"They're calling it nonsense. The old gods are stories. We say so in every homily."

"Do we." Ovid stepped closer. Up close he smelled of lamp oil and old paper, and beneath it, faintly, absurdly, of brine. "I have served this temple forty years, boy. I have read every ledger, every binding-record, every sanitized history this church ever wrote. And I will tell you a secret that has curdled in my chest for decades: our oldest records describe the Binding of Yara in language no one uses for imprisonment. They use the language of *maintenance.* Of watch-keeping. As though she were not the prisoner." His voice dropped. "As though she were the lock."

Ada's heart was slamming. "Then what's the door?"

"I don't know." For one moment Ovid looked every year of his age, and afraid, and human. "But last night something answered a dying woman through a scribe who doesn't believe, and today I received a letter from the capital bearing the seal of the Crown Inquisition. They ask after 'a vessel.' Someone talked, Ada. Someone always talks. You have perhaps two days."

"Two days until what?"

Ovid looked at him for a long moment, and then did the last thing Ada expected. He bowed. Slightly. The way you bow to someone standing in a doorway you'd thought was a wall.

"Two days until they arrive to take you," he said, "or until you run. I know which roads the inquisitors will watch. I also know—" and here the old man's mouth twitched, dry humor surviving even now, "—that a certain former novice once memorized several roads the temple does not admit exist. Roads I was ordered to expunge from our maps, and which I instead copied and locked in my desk, because I am a coward, but a thorough one."

Ada stared at him. "You knew. About me. All this time."

"I knew a young man who flinched whenever anyone mentioned the high passes, and whose hands drew mountains better than our hired cartographer. I knew, and I said nothing, because whatever you were hiding, you were also *staying*. Nine years, Ada. You could have run at any time. You stayed and paid your family's debt penny by penny. I decided that was a character reference." He turned to go, then paused. "The letter from the capital. There was one other thing in it."

"What?"

"A description of the king's recent decrees. Signed in his sleep, they say. Eyes gone white as salt." Ovid's shoulders were very straight. "Whatever wears that crown, brother—it has begun to notice the mountain again."

That night, alone in his cell, Ada did not sleep. He sat by the window with the moon on the peaks of Thornfell, silver and enormous and patient, and he argued with the god in his throat until his voice gave out.

"I can't carry you up a mountain. I get winded on the chapel stairs."

*You carried a dying woman's hope yesterday without spilling a drop,* Yara said. *Weight is a matter of practice.*

"I informed on my friends. I let people believe I was dead rather than face them. I'm not—"

*I know what you did.* The words were not cruel. They were worse; they were kind. *I have been lying under a mountain listening to the truth rot for three centuries. I know exactly what unspoken things weigh, little scribe. I am asking you to carry mine. Will you refuse to let me carry yours?*

The moon climbed. The mountain waited. And somewhere far below the capital, Ada knew, something in a crown of gold turned its blind white eyes toward Thornfell and began, slowly, to smile.

"Fine," Ada said at last, hoarse and terrified and, beneath the terror, in a place he would deny under torture, awake. "Fine. But I want it noted that I object."

*Duly noted,* said the Salt-Mother, laughing like waves against a hull. *Now sleep, Ada Zhou. Tomorrow we learn to run.*

Down in the valley, at that same hour, a rider in crown colors crossed the river ford at a hard gallop, and did not stop.
