## 3. What the Crown Wears

Maren had learned to read her brother's sleep the way other people read weather.

Aldric had always been a loud sleeper — a man who argued with his dreams, who laughed at them, who once woke the whole household by singing a fisherman's ballad with tremendous conviction and no trousers. So when she stood in the doorway of his chambers on that gray autumn morning and watched him *write*, she felt the cold arrive before her mind did.

He sat upright in bed, eyes open. Eyes open and wrong — pale as sea-foam, pale as the inside of a shell, with none of Aldric's warm brown anywhere in them. His hand moved across the ledger of state with a sureness he never possessed while waking. Aldric hated paperwork. Aldric signed things the way children eat vegetables: under protest, in small reluctant bites.

But this hand wrote steadily, page after page, in the dark before dawn.

"Brother?" Maren said softly.

The pen did not stop. The mouth smiled — a smile that fit his face the way a stranger's coat fits your shoulders.

"You shouldn't be here," Aldric said, in a voice like water running over stones. Not unkind. Worse than unkind. *Patient.* "Go back to your prayers, little sister."

She backed out of the room. She did not run until the door was closed, and then she ran all the way to her own chambers and was sick into the washbasin, and afterward sat on the floor with her back against the bed and laughed a little, helplessly, because that was what she did instead of screaming.

*Little sister.* Aldric hadn't called her that since she was nine and had fallen into the koi pond trying to rescue a frog she'd already rescued.

By noon there were three new decrees in the king's name. One dissolved the coastal granaries' winter reserves. One ordered the temple tithes doubled. And one, written in a hand almost but not quite Aldric's, appointed a High Inquisitor with powers Maren had thought belonged only to nightmares: to enter any house, question any soul, take any person who showed "signs of the old corruption."

Signs of the old corruption. She turned the phrase over all afternoon like a stone with something dead underneath it.

That night she went to the royal archive, bribed the archivist with plum wine and flattery, and asked for everything the crown owned about the mountain above Thornfell. The archive gave her dust, a map with the passes deliberately burned away, and one line in a chronicle four hundred years old:

*And they set the Salt-Mother beneath the stone, for a cage must have a keeper, and a keeper must forget why.*

Maren read it eleven times. Then she packed a single bag, told the household she was visiting an aunt whose existence she invented on the spot, and took the north road to Thornfell alone, because there was no one left she trusted enough to lie to properly.

* * *

Ada had not slept.

Three days since the washerwoman had died holding his sleeve, three days since his mouth had opened and someone else had spoken through it, and the temple had gone strange around him the way a house goes strange when you suspect a rat in the walls. Brothers glanced at him across the refectory and looked away too quickly. The high priest, Father Ossen — a kind man built like a soft old barrel — had begun asking questions shaped like pleasantries. *How are you sleeping, Ada? Any dreams? Any... voices?*

"No, Father," Ada said, copying out the Ninety-Ninth Psalm of the Nine with hands that shook only slightly. Ink-stained fingers. Honest work. A scribe's life. Nothing here but ink.

*You're a terrible liar,* said the voice in the base of his skull, warm as bathwater and twice as intrusive. *It's one of your better qualities.*

*I'm not talking to you,* Ada thought furiously at the god living in his throat.

*Of course not. You're talking to yourself. Very loudly. With excellent diction.*

Yara — the Salt-Mother, the bound one, the voice that had poured out of him over a dying woman like a tide coming in — had spent three days being exactly as patient as the ocean, which is to say not at all. She showed him things when he blinked: a hall of white stone, a king with foam-colored eyes, gold on his head that *moved*, subtly, the way a slug moves if you watch long enough. She showed him the mountain above Thornfell and made him feel its weight, the way you feel a tooth you've lost.

*I don't want this,* Ada told her, pressing the pen hard enough to blot. *I copy prayers. I pay down my father's debts. I am a completely unremarkable man and I intend to stay that way until I die quietly of paper cuts.*

*Little scribe,* Yara said, and there was something underneath the warmth now, something vast and tired and old, *I lived three hundred years in the dark listening to lies recited over my head. Do you imagine I have any patience left for yours?*

Ada put down the pen. Put his head in his hands. The psalm blurred.

"I didn't ask for you," he whispered, so quietly even the brothers nearest him heard nothing but a scribe's sigh.

*No,* the god said, gentler. *Neither did she. The woman who died. Neither did the king. We do not choose our vessels or our cages, child. We only choose what we do inside them.*

Which was, Ada reflected bitterly, exactly the sort of thing gods said when they wanted you to do the frightening thing.

* * *

Maren reached Thornfell on the fourth day, road-dusted and saddle-sore, wearing a traveling cloak over clothes too fine for it and telling herself she was incognito, which was a lie, because Maren had never been incognito in her life; she had her mother's height and her mother's way of standing as though a room should arrange itself around her.

The temple of Thornfell clung to the town's edge like a barnacle, all gray stone and blue-painted doors. She had expected priests. She had not expected the smell of the sea, faint and impossible, eighty miles from any coast — salt on the wind where no wind should carry it.

Inside, evensong was ending. She slipped into the back among farmers and fishwives, and that was where she saw him: a young man near the front, ink to the second knuckle, shoulders hunched as though trying to make himself smaller than a prayer book. He wasn't singing. His lips moved, but not to the psalm. And every few moments he winced, very slightly, like a man listening to a conversation nobody else could hear.

Maren had watched her brother wince exactly that way for two months.

Her heart began to hammer. *There. That one. Whatever's happened to Aldric, this one knows something of it.*

She waited through the benediction with the patience of a woman who had survived nineteen years of court banquets, which is considerable, and followed him out into the cloister as the blue dusk came down.

"You," she said. He jumped a foot. "Forgive me. I'm looking for answers, and I think you have them."

The young man turned. Up close he was younger than she'd guessed — mid-twenties, sharp-boned, with the wary eyes of someone who had learned early that being noticed was expensive. He looked at her fine cloak, her riding boots, her unmistakable face, and Ada Zhou — smuggler, debtor, liar of limited range — did the arithmetic instantly and hated the answer.

"You're the queen-sister," he whispered. "Maren."

"And you're the man who spoke with the old gods' voice over a dying woman last week." She stepped closer. "Half the town's saying it. Half the town's lying about saying it. Which half are you?"

"I'm the half that would very much like everyone to stop."

"Good," Maren said. "Because I need you to tell me what's happening to my brother."

Something crossed his face then — not guilt exactly, but its cousin, the one that lives next door and borrows guilt's tools. "What's happening to your brother," Ada said carefully, "is that he wears a crown."

"He's worn it six years."

"Yes," Ada said. "And lately it's been wearing him back."

For a moment neither of them moved. The cloister bell dripped its slow evening count. And Maren felt it happen — that rare, almost painful thing, rarer than love: recognition. Another watcher. Someone else who had stood in a doorway watching a beloved face become a mask, and who had told no one, because who would believe them, and who had carried it alone because carrying it alone was the only shape the truth came in.

"You've seen it," she said quietly. "Him. Or something wearing him."

"In visions I didn't ask for." Ada rubbed his ink-stained hands together, a nervous habit. "Your Majesty—"

"Maren. If anyone hears 'Your Majesty' they'll start thinking, and thinking leads to questions."

"Maren." He tried the name like a step onto thin ice. "There's a thing in the gold. Old. Hungry. It feeds on—" He stopped. Winced again, harder this time, and pressed two fingers to his temple as though holding a door shut.

"What?" Maren demanded. "What is it?"

"It says," Ada gritted, "that we are standing in a graveyard and it has finished counting the graves."

"That's cheerful."

"It's a god. They're all like this." He looked up at her, and to her surprise there was humor in his exhausted face, dry and crooked and human. "I used to be a quiet man, you know. I copied prayers for a living. I had a system."

"What was the system?"

"Avoid attention, avoid honesty, avoid mountains." He sighed. "I'm two for three and losing."

Maren laughed — a short, startled sound, the first laugh she'd had in weeks, and it cracked something loose in her chest. "You'll do," she said. "Scribe. You'll do nicely."

"My name is Ada."

"Ada." She offered her hand, court-fashion, absurdly formal between the herb beds and the blue dusk. He took it. His grip was ink-stained and steady, and she thought: *this is the first honest thing I've held in months.*

Then the bells began to ring — not evensong, not the hour. Alarm. Fast and ugly, all nine bells at once, and below in the courtyard torchlight bloomed like a rash.

They went to the cloister wall together and looked down, and there they were: riders in black and iron, the crown's sigil on their breasts, and at their head a tall figure in gray whose presence made the torches gutter, though the night was still.

"The Inquisition," Maren breathed. "Here. Already."

An amplified voice rolled up through the dark, flat as slate: *"Deliver to us the vessel of the old corruption, and the temple will be spared the full rigor of the crown's inquiry. Shelter it, and every soul within these walls will be questioned. Beginning with the oldest. Working down to the youngest."*

Beside her, Ada had gone very still and very pale. "They mean me," he said. It wasn't a question. It was a eulogy.

"They mean us both, I'd wager." Maren's mind was already moving — exits, horses, the burned-away passes on the map she'd memorized. She caught his arm. "Is there another way off this hill that isn't the front gate?"

Ada looked at her. And somewhere behind his eyes, a smuggler who had buried himself alive for nine years woke up, stretched, and remembered every hidden path in the Thornfell mountains better than his own name.

"As it happens," he said, "yes."
