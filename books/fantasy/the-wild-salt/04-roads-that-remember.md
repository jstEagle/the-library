## 4. Roads That Remember

The back gate of the temple of Thornfell opened onto the herb garden, and the herb garden opened onto a drainage culvert, and the drainage culvert—Ada had learned this nine years ago, in his first terrified month of copying prayers he didn't believe—opened onto everything.

"You're telling me," Maren said, hiked up to her knees in runoff, "that the holiest sanctuary in the northern marches empties its washwater directly into the smuggler's roads."

"The gods don't mind," Ada said. "I checked. There's no rule about it anywhere in the liturgy."

"Nine years of scripture and that's what you retained."

"It's what I *needed* to retain." He held up a hand as they reached the junction where the culvert split three ways, and let his fingers remember before his mouth did. Left was the tanners' road, watched, always watched, because it went somewhere worth watching. Right was the old pilgrim stair, which pilgrims had stopped climbing two centuries ago but which the crown's engineers had recently decided to repair, which meant lanterns and ledgers and men with lists. Straight ahead was nothing at all—a crack in the rock that most people would swear wasn't there, because Ada himself had spent three days building the illusion of not-being-there: loose scree dragged across the entrance, a dead juniper wedged just so, the smell of fox arranged with considerable effort.

He'd been proud of that work once. He'd been proud of all of it—the false walls in his memory, the routes he could walk blindfolded, the ledger of debts and favors kept in his skull where no inquisitor could read it. Pride, he understood now, walking Yara's borrowed warmth through the cold dark, was just another way of hiding. You built something beautiful so nobody looked at you.

Behind him, Maren climbed steadily, saying nothing, which he was learning was her way of saying a great deal. She had left her sister-queen's rings behind in the temple and taken instead a washerwoman's shawl off a drying line, and she wore it like she'd been born under one, chin high, eyes moving. Somewhere behind them both, High Priest Ovett was explaining to three gray-cloaked inquisitors that yes, the vessel had fled, and no, he hadn't seen which direction, and may the Nine forgive the delay in reporting this, he'd been composing his report.

Ovett would survive. Ovett survived everything; it was practically a sacrament. But the inquisitors wouldn't stay fooled for long, and when they stopped being fooled they would go where the roads went.

So Ada took them where the roads didn't.

* * *

They came out of the mountain's skirt an hour past midnight, into air that tasted of pine and snowmelt, and Ada made himself stop and breathe it, because it had been nine years since he'd smelled anything but ink and candlewax and the particular staleness of a man who has decided to be furniture.

"There." He pointed up the black shoulder of the ridge, to a place where the starlight broke differently against the rock. "The Shepherd's Cut. It'll take us above the tree line by dawn and down into the high valleys by tomorrow night. The army thinks the mountains are a wall. They're a door, if you know where the hinges are."

"And do you?" Maren asked. "Know where the hinges are?"

"I used to." He said it lightly, and heard how badly the lightness sat on the truth, and Yara shifted inside his chest like a tide adjusting itself, gentle and enormous. *You were good at doors,* she said, in the voice that lived now behind his teeth. *My salt remembers your feet on my stone.*

"Don't do that," Ada muttered.

"Do what?"

"Not you," he said to Maren, who was looking at him with the expression of a woman cataloguing symptoms. "Her. She talks like I'm a map she owns."

*I talk like you are mine,* Yara said, warm and terrible, *because for three hundred years no one has been.*

He didn't answer that. There wasn't an answer that wasn't either a prayer or a scream, and he'd done enough of both for one lifetime.

They climbed. The Shepherd's Cut was exactly as he'd left it, which was to say hostile—handholds worn smooth by goats rather than kindness, a chimney of rock that required you to trust a ledge the width of a hymnal. Maren climbed it without complaint and only one aristocratic noise of protest, and Ada found himself absurdly grateful for her, this stranger-sister who had grabbed his sleeve in the temple courtyard and said, *I don't care what you are, but my brother is dying from the inside out, and if you run I will follow you until my shoes wear through.* She'd meant it, too. He could tell. She had the same look the debt collectors used to have, right before they explained interest.

Dawn caught them in a hollow below the ridgeline, a cup of granite with a view of the whole eastern approach: the river valley silvered with morning fog, the king's road stitched along it, and on the road—

Maren saw it first. "Smoke."

Not cookfire smoke. Torch smoke, carried in daylight, which meant ceremony or menace, and given that they were fugitives, menace seemed the safer bet. Through the haze Ada counted banners, and the counting turned his stomach cold. Gray cloaks. Inquisitors' gray, moving fast, too many horses, and ahead of them on the road a riderless string of remounts that said they expected a long pursuit.

"They're not following our trail," Ada said slowly. "There is no trail. I made sure of—" And then he stopped, because he knew. He knew exactly how they knew.

The Shepherd's Cut came out onto the high valleys. The high valleys funneled into the first pass, and the first pass had exactly one place to rest, one spring, one sheltered campsite—and one person who had spent the last six years making a living knowing where people ran, because finding runners paid better than running yourself.

"Sefa," Ada said.

"Who," said Maren, "is Sefa?"

"The best friend I ever had." He got to his feet. His knees felt like borrowed things. "And the person on this earth most entitled to hate me."

* * *

He told her while they climbed, because there was nothing else to do with his hands and his mouth, and the truth came out of him in pieces, jagged, like stones pried loose.

Sefa Vance. Daughter of a fisherman who drowned, raised by a mother who dealt in everything the crown taxed. They'd met when Ada was seventeen and still Ada-of-the-harbor, quick-fingered, quicker-mouthed, the boy who could get a cart of untaxed wool over the Thornfell passes in a night and make the customs men laugh doing it. Sefa had taught him the mountains. That was the thing he couldn't stop remembering, climbing her own handholds now: every route he was using to save his life, she had put into his hands like a gift. *The mountain remembers everyone who walks it honest,* she used to say. *Walk it honest and it'll carry you.*

Then the crew had been taken. Then the questions in a cellar that lasted four days. Then—Ada's hands tightened on the rock—he had talked. Not much. Enough. Names, dates, one cache location. He'd told himself it was nothing, that everyone breaks, that the crew would understand. And then he'd told himself nothing at all, because the crew hadn't been given the chance to understand: the temple had come for *him* next, quietly, a debt called in, a scribe's robe offered, a life erased. Ada Zhou of the harbor had died of shame, and Brother Ada of the scriptorium had been born in his clothes.

He never wrote. Never sent word. Nine years.

"She thinks I abandoned her," he finished, hoarse. "Worse than abandoning. She thinks I thought she wasn't worth the risk. And now there's a bounty on the vessel of Salt-Mother Yara that would buy her mother's house twice over, and Sefa has never in her life walked past money that was also justice."

"So we go around her."

"We can't. Every other pass is watched by worse." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you see the joke? I spent nine years becoming invisible. I did it so well that the only people left in the world who can find me are the ones I hurt."

Maren was quiet for a dozen steps. Then: "When my brother started changing, I went to the court physicians, then the priests, then the archivists, and every one of them told me I was imagining it, because a worried sister is easier to dismiss than a sick king." She brushed hair from her face. "Invisibility isn't only chosen, Ada. Some of us have it imposed. The question is what you do when someone finally sees you anyway."

Ahead of them, the pass opened between two shoulders of stone like a doorway, and the morning sun fell straight down its throat—and there, seated on a boulder at the narrows with a crossbow across her knees and her boots swinging like a girl waiting for a festival, was a broad-shouldered woman with wind-burned cheeks and her hair braided tight for work.

She wasn't looking at the trail. She was whittling. She'd been expecting them for hours, because of course she had; she'd probably known the moment the inquisitors bought remounts in the valley town, because Sefa Vance read the mountains the way Ada read scripture—fluently, and without believing a word of the official version.

Ada stopped at the edge of the sunlight. Yara stirred in him, curious, ancient, reaching toward the stranger with something almost like tenderness, and he clamped down on it, because this moment belonged to him, mortal and guilty and nine years late.

"Sefa," he said.

The whittling knife stopped.

She turned, and her face did several things very quickly and settled on the worst one: nothing at all. "Well," she said, in a voice like gravel poured slow. "The ghost of Thornfell. Nine years, and you come up my pass wearing a priest's boots."

"These are scribe's boots."

"They're *coward's* boots, Ada." She stood, and the crossbow came up—not aimed, but present, the way she did everything. Behind him, Maren's breath went sharp. "Do you know what I did when you vanished? I walked every road in these mountains looking for a body. A body I could bury. I gave you a funeral, you absolute bastard. Paid for the ale myself. And now the temples are nailing papers to trees saying you're carrying a god, and the price on your head would set my whole crew free, and here you *are*, alive, standing in the sun like you've got a right to it."

"I don't," Ada said. "I know I don't."

"Good. That's something." Her eyes flicked to Maren, to the shawl, to the fine bones underneath the disguise, and narrowed. "And who's this?"

"A friend," Ada said.

"A rich friend."

"A desperate one," said Maren, with the precise dignity of a queen-sister in a stolen shawl.

Sefa looked at Ada for a long moment, and whatever she was reading in his face—the god, the guilt, the nine years—it made her jaw tighten. "Here's what happens now," she said, raising the crossbow to a full, steady aim. "You're going to sit down on that rock, both of you, and you're going to tell me why you disappeared. All of it. And then I'm going to decide whether I turn you in for the bounty or throw you off my mountain."

"That's fair," Ada said, and meant it, and sat down on the cold stone with his heart hammering and a god rising in his throat like a tide coming home, and began, at last, to speak.
