## 5. Salt and Iron

Sefa Vane had been waiting in the Greywater Pass for three days, and in those three days she had learned two things: the mountain wind had a personal grudge against her ears, and the man she'd been hired to drag down off the mountain was still, infuriatingly, predictable.

He always took the smuggler's road. Always. Nine years gone from the trade and he still cut the switchback at the split pine instead of the honest path, still stepped where the stone sang hollow, still moved like a man who'd memorized the mountain's lies and loved them anyway.

She watched him come up the trail with a woman in a fine traveling cloak—fine enough to be royal, which meant trouble—and something coiled tight under her ribs that she refused to name.

"There you are," she murmured. "You absolute ghost."

The rope took him at the ankles the way it always had, back when they'd run salt brandy through the same pass and laughed about the crown's patrols being too fat to climb. He went down hard on his elbows. The woman shrieked. And Ada Zhou—nine years older, ink-stained, thinner than a prayer book—looked up at her from the frozen mud with an expression she'd seen a thousand times on a thousand faces of men caught red-handed.

"Hello, Sefa," he said.

"Hello, Ada." She kept the crossbow level. It was harder than it should have been. "Nice weather for a hanging."

* * *

They'd been nineteen the first time they'd worked together, and she remembered it because he'd been terrible at it. All elbows and apologies, a temple charity case who'd wandered into the Salt Lantern tavern looking for work copying bills of lading and walked out with a job running contraband because he could read a mountain the way priests read scripture.

"You're sure about this route?" she'd asked him, first night, snow coming sideways.

"It remembers me," he'd said, dead serious, and then tripped over a root, and she'd laughed so hard she'd had to sit down in the snow, and after that it was just—the two of them. Sefa and Ada. The best small crew in the Thornfell ranges. He named the passes like they were friends. She taught him to cheat at cards. He taught her that the stars over the eastern ridge made a shape the old-timers called the Salt-Mother's Comb, and she'd pretended not to care and then secretly cared very much.

Then the crew got taken. Then Ada came back from three days in an inquisitor's cellar with his hands ruined and his mouth shut, and two weeks later the crown swept up every cache, every contact, every road they'd ever trusted, because someone had talked.

Because Ada had talked.

He never said so. That was the worst part. He just vanished—one morning his bunk was empty and his debt-ledger was gone and nobody would say where, and Sefa spent four years being angry before she found a way to make the anger pay: bounty work. Hunting people. Hunting, eventually, him.

Now here he was, trussed at the wrists with his own bootlaces—she'd taken his belt too; he never changed his kit—and watching her with those sad scribe's eyes.

"Sefa," he said carefully, "the woman behind me is Queen-Sister Maren."

"I know what she is. I can read a warrant."

"And I need you to know that if you turn us in, a god will be very disappointed."

Sefa stared at him. "A god."

"The Salt-Mother." He said it the way he used to announce bad weather. "She lives in my throat now. It's a whole thing. There were visions."

Maren, kneeling in the mud beside him, looked like she wanted the ground to swallow all three of them.

"Nine years," Sefa said, "and you've gotten *weirder*."

* * *

It might have ended there. Should have. She had a contract, a debt of her own, and a crossbow. But the wind shifted, and down the southern trail came the sound she knew better than her own heartbeat: the creak of inquisitor leathers and the jingle of too many men trying to walk quietly.

Six of them, cresting the ridge below. Crown grey and iron.

Sefa swore and pulled Ada and Maren off the trail into the rocks, and the fact that she did it without thinking—that her body had decided before her brain caught up—made her angrier than anything else that day.

They holed up in a crevice while the patrol passed below. Close. Too close. One of them stopped, sniffed the air like a dog, and called something up the slope, and then there was a shout and a bolt out of nowhere and Ada moved—not away from it, *into* it, shoulder turned, taking the quarrel meant for Maren's back.

He made a sound like a man biting through a coin. Went down against the rock. Blood, dark and fast, soaking through his sleeve.

And Sefa stood there with her crossbow raised and realized she was aiming at the inquisitors and not at her bounty, and that somewhere between the rope-trap and this moment she had already chosen, and hadn't asked herself's permission.

The fight was short and ugly. She'd been doing this nine years too. When it was done, two inquisitors limped back down the mountain and four didn't go anywhere, and Sefa's hands were shaking as she knelt by Ada, who was white as salt and breathing wrong.

"Hold still," she snapped, tearing open his shirt, and the wound beneath was worse than she'd thought—a ragged hole below the collarbone, welling red.

"We have to move," Maren whispered.

"He can't *move*, he'll—"

Ada's hand closed around her wrist. Weak. Cold. "Sefa," he rasped. "Let her."

"Let her *what*?"

And then the wound breathed.

There was no better word for it. The blood slowed, reversed, drew back into the torn flesh like a tide remembering the shore. The hole in him knitted closed under Sefa's staring eyes, skin sealing smooth and pink, and the smell of the air changed—brine and cold stone and something old, something patient, something that felt like standing at the sea's edge at night.

Ada exhaled, and his breath smelled of salt.

"She says thank you," he said quietly. "For choosing."

Sefa sat back on her heels in the snow and discovered she was crying, which was unacceptable, and wiped her face with a rough sleeve, which fixed nothing.

"That's cheating," she said. "That is—you can't just—" Her voice cracked. "Do you know how long I've been angry at you? Four years. I built a whole *career* on it. You don't get to bleed for someone and get healed by a fish goddess and make it all complicated."

"The Salt-Mother," said Maren faintly, "is technically the sea entire—"

"Not helping!"

But Ada was looking at her, and there it was, the thing she'd carried four years like a stone in her boot: not defiance, not guilt even—just that awful gentleness.

"You think I don't know why," he said. "You think I vanished because I was ashamed of *you*?"

"I think you talked, and couldn't look at us after."

"Yes," he said simply. "I did talk."

The word landed between them like a dropped blade.

"Three days in the cellar, Sefa. They had my hands. They had the ledger with every crew member's family listed in it—your mother's name, your sister's school. And they asked questions, and eventually I answered them, because I'm not brave, I never was brave, I was just good at maps." His voice stayed steady in the way that cost everything. "I told myself I gave them nothing that mattered. Then I watched them burn the caches I'd drawn for them, and I couldn't—I couldn't be near you and let you trust me. So I disappeared into a temple and copied prayers for nine years and paid down debts I couldn't name out loud."

Sefa found she was gripping her crossbow like it might hold her up.

"You could have *told* me."

"With what? 'Sorry I sold the roads we bled for'?" He almost smiled. "I informed on our crew. There's no version of telling you that doesn't end with exactly this face you're making."

"This face is because you're an *idiot*," Sefa said, and her voice broke clean in half. "We were children. You were tortured. And instead of letting anyone carry any of it with you, you carried it alone up a mountain for nine years and made me hunt you across half of it. Do you know what I did with the bounty money last winter? I sent it to your mother's creditors anonymously. I've been paying your family's debts to spite you for two years, Ada. *Two years.*"

Silence. Snow ticking down through the pines.

"...That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," Ada managed.

"I will shoot you in the other shoulder."

* * *

They made camp in a shepherd's hut above the treeline, and Sefa sat outside in the cold longer than she needed to, talking to the dark.

"So," she said to the Salt-Mother's Comb burning low over the eastern ridge. "Fish lady. Sea entire. Whatever you are." She rubbed her eyes. "He's a coward and a liar and the best friend I ever had, and if you break him, I will find whatever mountain they bound you under and I will complain at it until it cracks. Understood?"

The wind moved through the pass. It smelled, briefly, unmistakably, of low tide and childhood summers, and something in her chest that had been clenched since she was twenty-two loosened one careful notch.

Inside, Maren had fallen asleep sitting up. Ada was awake, watching her through the doorway, and he didn't say anything clever for once, and neither did she, and it was enough.

At dawn she packed her gear, and they assumed—she saw it on their faces—that she was leaving to report.

"Inquisitors already have the mountain routes," she said instead, shouldering her pack. "All of them. Your smuggler's roads, Ada. Someone sold them. Someone who knew them like scripture." She watched him go pale. "So the honest paths are death, and your secret ones are mapped, and the only way up that mountain now is the way nobody's ever gone—which means you need me, because I'm the only person alive meaner than this range."

"Sefa," Ada started.

"Don't." She pointed at him. "Don't thank me, don't apologize, don't do the sad eyes. We're even now. Your sins bought my bounty career; my spite paid your mother's rent. Ledger balanced." She turned toward the high, unclimbed face of the mountain, where the snowfields began and the old shrine waited, wherever it was. "Now show me the road that remembers you. Because I have a feeling," she said grimly, "that we're about to need every lie this mountain ever told."

Behind them, far below, horns sounded in the Greywater Pass—and they weren't search horns. They were assembly horns.

The army was coming up the mountain.
