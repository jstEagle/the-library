## 6. The Temple's Ledger

Maren had prayed in a hundred shrines more beautiful than this one, and never once in any of them had she felt the particular silence of a room that had been *abandoned on purpose*.

This shrine sat in the fold of the mountain like a cupped hand, half-swallowed by moss and mountain ash. Its bell had fallen from its bracket and lay in the grass, green with age. Someone had swept the threshold last—judging by the drift of pine needles across it—a very long time ago, and then simply walked away and let the mountain close the door behind them.

"Charming," Ada said, ducking under the lintel. "Drafty, haunted, and structurally optimistic. Everything a fugitive could want."

"You're not helping."

"I wasn't aware I'd been assigned to." He set down their bundle of supplies and looked around with the wariness of a man who had learned that buildings could have opinions. Yara's presence moved in him when he did it—a slow tide beneath his skin, a salt-smell where there should be none. Maren was growing used to the sight of it: the way his shadow seemed to move a half-beat late, as if something vast were learning to fit inside a scribe's body and kept missing the rhythm.

"The Salt-Mother says—" he began, then stopped, pressed two fingers to his temple like a woman trying to recall a name at a party. "She says this place was hers before it was the temple's. Or—the temple's *because* it was hers. I can't tell which. She's being cryptic."

"Gods are allowed," Maren said. "It's practically in the job description."

"She says you'd say that."

Maren smiled despite herself. Nine days on the road together, and she had catalogued the small changes in him the way she'd once catalogued her brother's: the ink stains fading from his fingers, the new habit of tilting his head as though listening, the way he flinched from holy water now—not from pain, but from politeness, the way one steps around a sleeping dog.

She lit no candles. There were none. Instead she knelt at the little altar—a slab of grey stone worn smooth by centuries of foreheads—and did what she had done every morning of her life since she was six years old. She prayed to the Ninefold Light, formally, correctly, in the old cadences her tutors had beaten into her.

The words came out and went nowhere, like coins dropped down a dry well.

Behind her, she heard Ada moving along the back wall, and then a sharp intake of breath.

"Maren. Come look at this."

* * *

The wall behind the altar was false—or rather, the wall was real but the altar was not where the builders of the shrine had intended devotion to happen. Ada had found the seam by accident, running his thumb along the mortar the way, he admitted, he used to check for hollows in smugglers' caches. The stone swung inward on greased pivots that still worked after three hundred years, which told Maren someone had expected this door to be needed again.

Beyond it, a narrow chamber. Dry air. And shelves.

Not reliquaries. Not treasure. *Shelves*—rows and rows of them, cut into the living rock, holding ledgers. Hundreds of them. Leather cracked to powder at the touch, vellum gone the color of old teeth, but intact. Intact because they had been sealed away from damp and light and, Maren understood with a cold spreading feeling, from *readers*.

"It's a records vault," she said stupidly. "In a heretical shrine. On a forbidden mountain."

"Smugglers' logic," Ada said. "You hide the valuable thing where nobody would ever think to look for value. Nobody hides truth inside a lie on purpose." He paused. "Well. Almost nobody."

He pulled out the nearest ledger and opened it with a scribe's care, and Maren watched his face change as he read. She had seen that expression before—inquisition reports, confessions, the moment when a person understands that the floor they are standing on is painted over a pit.

"What is it?"

"Read the header." He turned the book toward her. His hands were shaking slightly.

The script was archaic temple-hand, but Maren had been tutored by the best liars in the capital and could read it easily enough. The heading read:

*Ledger of the Vigil, Being the True Account of the Binding of Yara-of-the-Tides, Set Down by Order of the First Nine, That It Never Be Forgotten, Though We Are Commanded to Forget It.*

"That last part," Ada said quietly, "is doing a great deal of work."

* * *

They read by the light of Maren's travel-lamp until the lamp burned low, and then by the light of Ada's small fire-magic—he blushed furiously when it flickered up between his palms, a scribe's trick for warming cold hands in scriptoriums, nothing grander, and yet Maren thought it was the first time she had seen him make anything instead of copy it.

The ledger told the story the way a surgeon tells it: without comfort, without mercy, in columns.

Three hundred years ago, something had come down out of the dark between stars, or up out of the deep places—it was unclear, and the monks who wrote this had clearly been unclear too, and honest about it. It had no name they dared use. It wore crowns. It fed on kings. Wherever a throne grew rich and fat and cruel, there it nested, drinking the will out of the wearer until the crown sat empty on a puppet's head and the thing behind it ruled.

They could not kill it. They tried; the ledger recorded three attempts in language so restrained that the horror came through all the worse—*the Third Company did not return; the Second returned diminished*. So the Ninefold Light's founders had done the only thing left. They had bound it into sleep, and the chain they forged was not iron.

It was belief.

"Yara," Maren said aloud, testing it. Her whole life she had been taught that the Salt-Mother was a demon of the old cults, drowned and bound for humanity's sake. The founding lie, neat as a folded letter.

Ada shook his head slowly. "Not quite. Read the margin."

The marginal note, in a different, later hand, read: *We did not bind the Sea-Wife against her will. She consented. She wrapped herself around the Sleeper like a mother around a fevered child and let us nail them both beneath the mountain, because a god who sleeps in chains holds the sleeper still, and a god who wakes breaks free and leaves the child unwatched.*

"She's not the prisoner," Maren whispered. "She's the lock."

"And locks," Ada said, "only hold if somebody keeps believing in the door."

There it was. Page after page, in the later ledgers: the maintenance rites. The annual reaffirmation. The names of the vigil-keepers, generation after generation, monks whose entire sacred duty was to come to these hidden shrines and *remember correctly*—to speak Yara's true name, to recount the binding, to keep the story alive and accurate, because a story half-remembered or wrongly told loosens like a knot worked by careless fingers.

And then, eighty-seven years ago, the entries stopped.

The last vigil-keeper's final note was short. Maren read it twice, hoping she'd misread it the first time.

*By decree of Hierarch Ossian the Reformer, the cult of the Sea-Demon is declared extirpated. All vigils cease. All records of the true binding are hereby sealed. Let no faithful hand reopen them. The danger is past; the Light prevails.*

"He didn't seal them to protect anyone," Maren said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, flat and far away. "He sealed them because keeping the vigil was *embarrassing*. Because a church that preached the triumph of the Light couldn't admit its greatest victory was actually a truce that required upkeep. So he declared the war won, locked the evidence in a hole in a mountain, and—" she laughed, and hated the sound of it— "and my family has been funding that church's beautiful lies for nine generations."

Ada said nothing. He was watching her with an expression she was beginning to know: the careful, guilty tenderness of a man who has known he was lied to for years and is now watching someone else arrive at the same shore.

"The crown on my brother's head," she said. "The temple crowned him with it. They *chose* it. They've been handing that thing to kings for centuries and calling it divine right, because admitting what it really was would mean admitting Ossian's reformation was the most catastrophic act of negligence in—"

Her voice broke. She was surprised by how much it hurt—she had thought herself past this, past faith, a practical woman who attended services for appearances. But it turned out she hadn't stopped believing; she had merely believed *in spite of everything*, quietly, the way a bruised bone still carries weight. And now the weight was gone and the bone was broken and she didn't know how to stand.

"They didn't just let it rust," she said. "They taught us to be grateful for the rust. Called it piety."

Ada reached over and, awkwardly, gently, closed the ledger in her hands. "Yara wants to say something," he said. "If—if that's all right. She doesn't usually ask permission. She's asking."

Maren nodded.

Ada's posture changed subtly, the way a harp changes when a string is tuned. When he spoke again, his voice carried harmonics it shouldn't have, deep water and long tides.

*"Little queen of ash and candlelight,"* Yara said through him. *"Your church did not betray me. Betrayal requires loyalty. Your church forgot me, which is worse and also forgivable, because forgetting is what mortal things do. But hear me: I am failing. Three hundred years I have held the Sleeper, and your forgetting has thinned me to lace. Every king your temple crowns feeds it a little. Every prayer sent to the wrong address starves me a little. Soon I will not be able to hold it, and it will wake hungry in the head of a king who loves his people."*

The tide receded. Ada sagged, caught himself on the shelf, breathed.

"I'm sorry," he said. "That part takes it out of me."

"How soon?" Maren asked. "How soon does she fail?"

Ada looked at her, and Maren saw the answer in his face before he spoke, the way you see weather coming over a ridge.

"Soon," he said. "And there's more. There's a section here—" he touched the ledgers stacked beside him— "on how the binding can be undone properly. Safely. A mortal has to stand where the binding was made and speak the whole truth of it aloud. Every lie. Every erased name. All of it, in front of witnesses, while the Sleeper fights back." He swallowed. "The last page lists what happened to every mortal who attempted it and failed."

"Do I want to know?"

"No," Ada said. "But I think we need to anyway. Because the mountain where she's bound—" he looked up toward the peak above them, invisible now in the dark, but both of them felt it, a weight on the chest like deep water— "is three days' climb from here. And Maren. There are fresh boot-prints at the threshold outside. Inquisitors don't sweep their tracks."

Outside, wind moved through the mountain ash, and somewhere below them in the dark, faint as a struck bell, a hunting horn sounded twice—the signal, Maren knew from her husband's campaigns, for *found them, closing*.

Ada began stacking the ledgers into his pack with shaking, determined hands.

"Grab the oldest ones," he said. "We're going to need every word."
