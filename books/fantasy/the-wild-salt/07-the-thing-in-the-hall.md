## 7. The Thing in the Hall

The dream came again on the third night above the treeline, and this time Yara let it come.

Ada stood in a hall he had never seen but knew at once—the great chamber of the royal keep in Saltmere, all vaulted stone and banners gone gray with dust. The throne sat empty. The crown did not.

It hovered above the armrest like a thing of gold and teeth, turning slowly, and though it had no eyes, Ada felt it looking at him the way one feels weather coming through a bad tooth.

*Little scribe,* it said, and its voice was the sound of coins being counted in a dark room. *You have been busy.*

"I don't talk to parasites," Ada said, which was braver than he felt, considering he was asleep and therefore entirely made of nerves.

*A parasite.* The crown turned another slow degree. *I have worn kings before you were born. I wore a river-god. I wore a saint so holy her bones still sing in three cathedrals. Your king is simply the warmest chair available.* A pause, and then something almost like humor, if humor could be filleted and salted: *But you—you are interesting. You carry my prisoner in your mouth and you still flinch when she uses it.*

"She's not your prisoner anymore."

*No?* The hall flickered. For one horrible instant Ada saw it as the crown saw it: a ledger. Every soul in Saltmere reduced to columns of debt—what they owed, what they feared, what they would sell. And there, two-thirds down a page marked THORNFELL PASSES, a name he knew better than his own.

*Sefa Vane. Crew of four, plus a captain who talks too much. Debts of honor are the easiest kind to collect. She will bring you to me, little scribe, or she will watch them hang from the waystation beams. Tell her I said the interest has compounded.*

The hall dissolved. Ada woke with salt crusted on his lips and Maren shaking his shoulder, her face pale in the coals' last light.

"You spoke in your sleep," she said. "Not Yara's voice. Something colder."

"I know." He sat up, joints cracking like green wood. "It knows where Sefa is. It's going to make her an offer she can't survive either way."

Maren fed the fire a stick and considered him. Nine days on the mountain had worn her courtly polish down to something truer underneath—a tired woman with sharp eyes and a soldier's hands. "And you're going to tell her. Before it does."

"Am I?"

"You're going to try." She almost smiled. "You've been rehearsing it in your sleep for a week. You mumble, Ada Zhou. Like a man memorizing a confession."

He looked at the fire. "Maybe I am."

* * *

They reached the Widow's Rest waystation at dusk on the sixth day—a squat stone shelter wedged into the shoulder of the pass, built by smugglers three generations back and abandoned by everyone since except goats and ghosts. Yara liked it; Ada could feel her humming through the walls, some low contented note, like a cat finding an old sunbeam. Places that remembered honest trade, she said, were the only churches left worth visiting.

Sefa was already inside.

She sat on the far bench with her boots up and her crossbow across her knees, eating their dried plums with the unbothered air of someone who had decided the outcome in advance. Her crew stood in the doorway behind her—Rook and Tammas and little Wren, all older, all harder, all watching Ada with expressions ranging from murder to heartbreak.

"Evening," Sefa said. "Nice shrine you've got. Very rustic."

"Sefa—"

"Ah-ah." She raised a plum. "You don't get to talk first. Nine years, Ada. Nine years I carried your half of every job because you couldn't be bothered to leave so much as a note. I thought you were dead. I *buried* you—in my head, there was a whole funeral, it was very moving, Rook cried—" 

"I did not," said Rook, who absolutely had.

"—and now you turn up on my mountain playing prophet for a fish goddess, and the crown wants me to drag you down to Saltmere or dangle my people from beams." She set the plum down. Her hands weren't quite steady. That was the worst part. Sefa's hands were always steady. "So before anyone does anything stupid, you're going to sit down and tell me why."

The fire popped. Yara stirred in Ada's chest like a tide deciding whether to come in. *Let him speak,* she murmured. *This is his temple too.*

Ada sat down across from her. His mouth was dry as prayer-paper.

"The winter after we ran the Kestrel cargo," he began, "you remember. When the revenue men nearly had us at the ford."

"I remember."

"They didn't nearly have us. Someone told them where we'd cross." His voice came out small, and he hated it, and he kept going anyway. "It was me. They took me in Thornfell—two of them, off the street, into a cellar under the customs house—and I lasted four hours. Four hours, Sefa. I always told myself I'd hold out forever, and I lasted four hours, and everything I ever believed about myself went out of me through my ribs, and when they asked for the ford crossing I gave it to them like I was reading from scripture."

Nobody moved. Wren had stopped breathing.

"They let me go because I was more useful loose. And I told myself the same lie every day after—that I'd vanish to protect the crew, that disappearing was noble, that if I stayed away no one could trace anything back to you. But that wasn't it." He forced himself to look at her, because hiding his face would just be lying in a different posture. "I disappeared because I couldn't stand to be seen by people who trusted me. Being invisible hurt less than being known. That's the truth. That's the whole ugly ledger of it. I didn't run to save you. I ran because shame is a coward, and I let it drive."

The silence went on long enough that he heard the wind change outside, the mountain breathing.

Then Sefa laughed—one short, cracked sound, nothing funny in it.

"Four hours," she repeated. "Ada. You absolute idiot." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "We got caught at the ford *anyway*. Revenue men don't move on rumor alone—they'd already had scouts on the water for a week. Whatever you gave them, they had it. Nobody died. Nobody hanged. We lost the cargo, cursed the gods, and moved on, because that's the trade." Her voice roughened. "Nine years. You gave us nine years of grieving you over a sin that didn't even take."

"It took *me*," Ada said quietly.

And there it was—the strange lightness, terrible and clean, of a weight finally set down. Yara rose in him like warmth after cold, not speaking, just present, the way the sea is present in a shell. He understood suddenly why she wanted truth spoken aloud rather than merely known. Truth held inside curdles. Truth given away feeds someone.

Maren touched his shoulder, briefly, and withdrew. Sefa scrubbed a hand over her face.

"Fine," she said thickly. "Fine. You're forgiven, you're a coward, you're forgiven, we'll workshop it. Now here's my problem." She jerked her chin toward the door, toward the darkening pass. "The crown's men reached me yesterday. Not inquisitors—an army column, regulars, marching up the south road. They're a day behind me and they know exactly where this waystation is, because I told them, because they've got Wren's brother in irons in Saltmere and I had maybe ten minutes to choose." Her jaw tightened. "So whatever your fish goddess plans to do at the top of this mountain, Ada, she'd better do it fast. Because tomorrow morning I'm supposed to walk out of here with you in chains, and if I don't, they hang them."

Wren made a small sound. Rook put a hand on her shoulder.

"No," Ada said.

Sefa blinked. "No?"

"No." He stood, and Yara rose with him, filling the room—not seizing it, just arriving, the way dawn arrives. Salt-smell filled the old stones; the fire burned bluer for a moment. "You're not walking out with anyone in chains. You're taking your crew down the west goat-path tonight, the one only three people alive know, and you're getting them clear. I bought that path with nine years of studying maps nobody else bothered with. Consider it the first payment on a very large debt."

"And you?"

"We go up." He looked at Maren, who nodded once, grim and certain. Then back at Sefa. "There's a shrine under the summit where this started three hundred years ago. There's a truth that has to be spoken there, aloud, completely—and apparently I'm the only mouth available." He managed a crooked smile. "Turns out all that practice copying prayers was training after all."

Sefa stared at him for a long moment. Then, softly: "If you die up there, I swear I'll dig you up and kill you."

"Noted."

Outside, far below in the dark, torches had begun to climb the south road like embers rolling uphill—a day early. The crown-thing had lied about its schedule, or grown impatient, or simply wanted them to feel the heat at their backs.

Sefa followed his gaze to the torchlight, and her face went hard and bright as a drawn blade.

"That's not a day's march," she said. "That's hours."
