## 8. Where the Mountain Was Bound

The last stretch of the mountain did not feel like climbing so much as being swallowed.

Ada had known smuggler roads his whole life—the goat paths, the collapsed mine shafts repurposed as shortcuts, the cairns that meant *water ahead* or *turn back, fool*. But the stair they found at dawn on the ninth day was none of these. It went *down*, into the mountain's root, and it had been carved by hands that thought in centuries.

"Charming," said Maren, holding her lantern high. Soot from nine days of travel striped her face, and she'd long since stopped looking like a queen-sister and started looking like a very determined washerwoman with excellent posture. "Three hundred years of my church insisting this mountain was empty of anything but rock and weather, and it turns out there's a staircase to the underworld with decorative fish carved on every fourth step."

"Fish are Yara's," Ada said. His voice came out strange—there were two voices in his throat these days, and they took turns. "She counts them the way other gods count stars."

*I count you too,* said the voice inside him, warm as sun-heated stone. *Little scribe. You have ink behind your ears still.*

"There is not," Ada muttered, scrubbing at his ear, "and I bathed three days ago."

Maren gave him the look she'd perfected over nine days—the one that said *I know you're talking to someone who isn't me, and I've decided to find it endearing rather than alarming.* It was, Ada had come to realize, an act of considerable generosity.

The stair wound down through stone that grew warmer, then wet, then salt. Brine wept from the walls in slow beads, and where it gathered in the hollows, small pale things moved—shrimp, maybe, or the mountain's memory of shrimp. The air tasted like the harbor at Thornfell, like his childhood, like the first morning after a storm when the whole town came out to see what the sea had left behind.

He had not let himself think about the sea in nine years. The sea knew too many people he'd failed.

*You will need to breathe,* Yara said gently, when his chest went tight. *Breathe, Ada. The truth does not drown those who carry it honestly.*

"That's a lovely sentiment," he managed, "from someone who's been chained under a rock for three hundred years and didn't have to say any of it herself."

*I said it for three hundred years,* she answered, and there was something old and tired underneath the warmth. *To the dark. To the chains. No one listened. That is why it must be a mortal mouth now. A god may speak truth forever into silence. Only a man speaking truth can make other men ashamed.*

* * *

The shrine opened without warning. One moment the passage was shoulder-narrow; the next, the lantern light simply gave up and fell away into a cavern vast enough to hold weather. Maren's flame caught the edges of things: pillars like kelp stalks, a floor of black stone veined white with salt, and at the center—

Ada had expected chains. He'd been carrying the image for days, rehearsing his horror. What he found was worse and stranger: a circle of standing stones, each one wrapped in rope so old it had fused with the rock, and within the circle, a pool. Not water. Salt, pure and white and fine as flour, filling a basin the size of a village well, utterly still.

And around the rim of the pool, carved deep and then *deliberately chiseled away*, names. Hundreds of names. He could see the ghosts of them—strokes half-erased, syllables amputated mid-word.

"Oh," Maren whispered. She'd gone very pale. "Oh, we did this. My church did this." Her hand rose toward the nearest stone and stopped short, hovering, as if the chisel marks might bite. "We didn't just bind her. We *unwrote* her. Every name she ever had—"

"Except the ones people kept saying anyway," Ada said quietly. "Yara survived because fishermen can't help themselves. You can burn a book, but you can't stop a grandmother muttering thanks before she hauls a net." He heard himself smile, thin and crooked. "Turns out the temple's great enemy was always old women being stubborn."

*Four hundred and eleven names,* Yara said, and her grief moved through Ada's ribs like cold tide. *They left me one. The smallest. The one children use.*

"The Salt-Mother," Ada said aloud, testing it.

*A nickname,* she said, with something that was almost humor. *Like calling a whale 'the big fish.'*

Ada walked the circle slowly, reading the wounds in the stones, and the scribe in him—the part that had spent nine years copying prayers he no longer believed, the part that noticed everything and reported nothing—began, horribly, to *understand*. This was not vandalism. It was a system. Each erased name had been a lock-pin; the binding wasn't one chain, it was a sentence written in removal, a text made of absences. The temple hadn't imprisoned a god. They'd performed an act of editing so thorough it had become physics.

And somewhere above them, wearing a crown, the thing that had dictated the edit sat fat and patient on its throne of borrowed years.

"So I speak the names back," Ada said. His voice echoed strangely off the salt. "All of them. And the lies. The whole ledger, read aloud, in reverse."

"Yes," Maren said. Then, because she was honest even when honesty cost her: "Ada. When the temple finds out what you're going to say—they'll call it blasphemy. They'll call *you* blasphemy. Everything they built, everything that sheltered you—" She stopped. Started again, gentler. "Brother Anselm taught you your letters. He vouched for you when no one else would take a smuggler's boy with ink-stained cuffs."

"I know." Ada looked down at his hands. Nine years of ink lived in the creases; he doubted he'd ever fully wash it out, and some traitorous part of him hoped he never would. "He used to rap my knuckles with his spectacles case and say, 'Zhou, a lie copied ten thousand times is still a lie, only tired.' I thought he was talking about scripture." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "He was talking about the whole temple, wasn't he? He knew."

"Maybe," Maren said softly. "Maybe he suspected. Maybe suspecting was all he could bear."

Above them—far above, muffled through a thousand feet of stone—a sound began. At first Ada took it for the mountain settling. Then he recognized the rhythm: boots. Hundreds of boots, marching in step, descending the switchbacks of the upper passes. And threaded beneath it, thin and wrong, a hum that made his teeth ache and the salt in the pool tremble at its edges.

The crown-thing had found them.

*It sings the way it rules,* Yara said. *Flatly. Endlessly. Hoping everything else will fall asleep.*

"How long do we have?" Maren asked, all business now, pulling her knife, checking the lantern oil like a quartermaster. Somewhere in the last week she'd become terrifyingly competent at fieldwork, which Ada suspected would horrify the palace staff.

"An hour," Ada estimated. "Two if they argue about the narrow places." He turned to face the pool, and his stomach performed a maneuver usually reserved for ship decks. "Two hours to speak four hundred and eleven names and every lie ever told about them."

"You don't have to be eloquent," Maren said. "Just true."

"Just true," Ada repeated. "*Just.* As though truth were a small thing. As though it weighs nothing." He rolled up his sleeves—old habit, the washerwoman's son in him surfacing at the worst moments—and stepped to the pool's edge.

The salt did not reflect him. It reflected, faintly, the shapes of names.

*Begin anywhere,* Yara whispered. *Truth has no first line. That's what makes it different from scripture.*

* * *

He started with the easiest lie, because a drowning man grabs the nearest plank.

"My name is Ada Zhou," he said, and the cavern took the words and multiplied them, dozens of Adas speaking into the dark. "I was born in Thornfell harbor, third child of Mei Zhou, who washed other people's linens until her hands cracked, and I have lied about loving the temple since the day I entered it."

The words felt like pulling a tooth—no, like pulling nine years of teeth at once. But the salt brightened where his voice touched it, a faint glow spreading like dawn on water, and the first ghost-name on the nearest stone stirred, half-erased strokes filling in like frost forming on a window.

*Yarathanei,* the stone remembered. The glow strengthened.

Encouraged and terrified in equal measure, Ada kept going.

"The temple teaches that the old gods abandoned us first. That is a lie. The temple teaches that the binding was mercy—that Yara consented to sleep beneath the mountain to protect mankind from her storms. That is a lie so large it needs its own wing of the library." His voice steadied as he went, finding a rhythm, the rhythm of ledgers, of inventory, of debts tallied honestly at last. "The temple teaches that King Aldric's line was blessed by heaven. Aldric wears a fragment of the thing that bound her. The crown feeds on him. He signs decrees in his sleep. His eyes have gone salt-white. I have *seen* it, and I say it here where no one can unwrite it—"

Boots, closer now. The humming louder. Dust sifted from the cavern roof.

"The name Yarathanei was struck out in the year the harbors burned," Ada said, and the stone blazed. "The name Vel-of-the-Ninth-Tide was struck out—"

He lost track of time. Names came up out of him like anchors coming up out of deep water, each one trailing weed and old grief. Some he learned as he spoke them, fed directly into his mouth by the god whose throat he was borrowing. Some he invented nothing for—he simply pointed at the chisel scars and said *this was done, and it was wrong, and we knew it was wrong, because we hired the stonemasons afterward to forget, and forgetting is a wage like any other.*

Maren stood beside him the whole while, lantern high, and when his voice broke on the name of the stonemason—*Ollen, Ollen who had daughters*—she picked up the thread without hesitation, reading the half-legible carvings in her careful scholar's voice, so that between the two of them the truth never stopped flowing. He would think about that later, how easily she joined in. How little it seemed to cost her, and how much it must actually have cost.

The pool was glowing now, full-bright, and the standing stones hummed like struck bells, and the ropes that had fused with rock for three hundred years were beginning, fiber by fiber, to smoke.

Then the passage behind them filled with torchlight.

Not two hours. Not even one. They'd found the smuggler's shortcut—someone had told them, someone with a debt and a knife and a crew hanging in the balance, and Ada's heart clenched around Sefa's name like a fist around broken glass.

Soldiers poured into the shrine, ranks deepening, pikes lowering. At their center, wrapped in gold that hurt to look at, stood a figure in the king's armor wearing the king's face wearing something else entirely behind the eyes—two white coins where pupils should be, and a smile like a crack in porcelain.

"Vessel," it said, in a voice like a hymn played backward. "You are copying again."

Ada looked at the army. At Maren, knife drawn, magnificent and doomed. At the pool, blazing, nearly full—but not complete. Dozens of names still dark.

His terror did not vanish. It changed shape, the way fear does at the exact moment it becomes irrelevant—transmuting, all at once, into something with edges and weight and a terrible calm. He had hidden for nine years. He would not hide for nine more seconds.

"No," Ada Zhou said, in his own voice, in both their voices, loud enough to reach every soldier in the cavern and, through the salt, through the stone, through the roots of the world, the throne room of a haunted king. "I'm doing something much more dangerous."

* * *

He drew breath, and spoke the next name.
