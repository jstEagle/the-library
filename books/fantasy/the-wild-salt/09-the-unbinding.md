## 9. The Unbinding

### Sefa

The pass had a name, once. The old maps called it the Weeping Stair, and Sefa had always thought that was smuggler sentimentality until she stood at the top of it in the grey hour before dawn and watched an army come up through the fog like a wound opening.

Torches first — dozens of them, then hundreds, a river of orange crawling up the switchbacks below. Behind them, the sound: iron on stone, the wet cough of war-drums, and beneath all of it a wrongness in the air that made her back teeth ache. She'd felt that wrongness before, three nights ago at the waystation, when the crown's messenger had smiled at her with too many teeth and offered her crew's lives for Ada's head.

She had said yes. That was the terrible part. She had knelt and sworn it, hand on the royal seal, and walked away with her people's freedom signed in ink while her heart sat in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

Then she'd turned around.

"You could still run," said Bren, the youngest of her crew, who had insisted on coming this far and no further. He stood beside her now, hugging himself against the cold, looking at the torch-river with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. "You know that. Nobody would blame you."

"Everyone would blame me," Sefa said. "Mostly me." She rolled her shoulders and checked the straps of her gear one last time out of habit, though there was nothing left to check. She'd sold everything worth selling to buy this hour: oil for the brazier at the pass's throat, rope to cut, scree to loosen, every trick of the mountain she and Ada had ever learned together, laid out like a table set for guests who wanted them dead.

"And when they get past you? Because they will, Sef. Eventually."

"Eventually isn't today," Sefa said. "Today I only have to be slow enough to be interesting."

Bren laughed despite himself — a short, cracked sound. Then his face went serious. "Why? Really. He left you. Nine years of nothing."

Sefa looked down at the fog, at the army inside it, at the mountain where somewhere above her a scribe with ink-stained hands was about to say true things into the dark.

"He came back," she said. "That's the whole answer, Bren. It's not a good answer. But it's mine."

She hugged him once, hard, the way you hug someone when you're not sure the hug has an ending. Then she turned and walked down toward the Weeping Stair, alone, and began, very quietly, to sing the old road-songs — the ones Ada used to hum while he counted cargo, the ones their mothers sang before the temple decided the old gods were embarrassing. If the crown-thing wanted this pass, it would have to climb over a woman singing salt-songs at it.

Her hands were steady. She noted that with some surprise, the way you note weather.

### Ada

The deep shrine smelled of drowned things.

It lay at the mountain's root, past the abandoned shrine where Maren had found the ledger, down a stair that predated the temple, predated the kingdom, maybe predated the idea of stairs. The walls wept brine. Salt crusts bloomed on the stone like white flowers, and in the center of the chamber stood the chain.

Not a chain of links. A chain of names — each link carved with a word in a script older than any alphabet Ada knew, and yet he could read them, because Yara read them through him, and the reading felt like swallowing seawater: necessary and awful.

"The binding is a sentence," Maren whispered behind him. She had the ledger clutched to her chest like a shield. In the lamplight her face was hollowed out; she hadn't slept since the records showed her what her church had done. "It's not a cage, it's a *sentence*. Someone has to keep saying it or it stops meaning anything."

"And the temple stopped," Ada said. "Three hundred years of copying prayers, and nobody ever copied *this*." His voice shook. Yara moved under his skin like a tide deciding on the shore.

*Say it,* she said, in the voice that was not a voice but more like weather inside him. *Say the whole of it. Every lie. Every name they took from me. A binding held by lies rusts, little scribe. A binding held by truth — even ugly truth — holds forever.*

"What happens to Aldric?" Maren asked. "When you rise?"

*I take back what is mine,* Yara said. *And what is his, I leave him. What remains of his.*

Ada knelt before the chain of names. His knees found grooves worn into the stone by other knees, centuries of them, and the thought that he might be the last person to kneel here made his throat close.

Above them, faint as memory, came the first sounds of battle. Iron on stone. A woman's voice, raised in song, defiant and cracking.

"Sefa," Ada breathed.

*She is holding the door,* Yara said, and there was something almost gentle in the vastness of it. *She bought you minutes. Do not waste them being proud of her. Be worthy instead.*

So Ada opened his mouth, and began to speak the truth.

He spoke of the binding — how the first priests had come to the Salt-Mother not as conquerors but as beggars, because something had fallen from the sky into the king's gold and coiled itself around the crown like a tapeworm made of hunger. How Yara had agreed to be bound, to be chained beneath the mountain and named a prisoner, so that her strength could press the thing asleep. How the priests had then done the most human thing imaginable: they had lied about it. Written her down as a monster. Erased her name from every ledger but one. Taught generations that the old gods were savage and the new order merciful, because a church built on a rescue cannot admit it was built on a sacrifice.

"They called you devourer," Ada said, and his voice rang off the wet stone. "They called you storm-mother, tide-hag, the thing below. They took your name and gave it to the dark and told children to fear the dark." Tears ran freely down his face now, and he let them. "Your name is Yara. Your name is Yara. Your name is Yara."

Each repetition struck the chain of names, and each strike rang like a bell underwater.

*More,* Yara said, and her tide rose in him, and Ada understood suddenly why the temple had feared this — not the god, but the *honesty*, the way truth at scale could crack stone.

* * *

### Sefa

The first soldiers reached her at the third switchback, and she gave them a rockslide for their trouble, which bought her ten minutes and broke two of her fingers setting the trigger-rope. She sang louder after that, mostly to hear something besides her own breathing.

The crown-thing spoke through their captain eventually. It did that now — she'd seen it happen at the waystation, a soldier's jaw going slack and another voice pouring out of him like smoke from a bad chimney.

*"Turn aside, bounty hunter,"* it said through the captain's mouth, while the captain wept silently, which was somehow the worst part. *"Your debt is paid. Your crew walks free. Why die for a man who forgot you?"*

"Because he remembered," Sefa said, and pulled the next rope.

The scree came down in a white roar. It wouldn't stop them. Nothing would stop them. But it slowed them, and slowing them was the whole of her job, and she did it with everything she had — every trick, every cached tool, every mile of mountain she knew better than the army marching on it. Twice she fought. Once she ran. Always she sang, until her voice was a rasp and the songs came out as rhythm without melody, drumbeats of defiance echoing down the Weeping Stair.

Somewhere above her, faint through the stone, she heard a bell tolling. Or a chain ringing. Or both.

"Hold on, Ada," she rasped. "I'm making you a door. Walk through it."

A spear took her in the side at the fifth switchback. She didn't fall so much as sit down very suddenly, which she supposed was technically a tactical retreat. Blood spread warm under her coat, and the strangest thought surfaced through the pain: *Ada will fuss. He'll do the thing with his hands where he can't decide whether to pray or apologize.*

She laughed, which hurt, and kept singing.

* * *

### Ada

The last lie was the hardest, because it was his own.

"There was a boy," Ada said, kneeling in the salt-flowers, the chain blazing white above him, Yara rising in his chest like dawn under the sea. "A boy who knew these passes, whose family owed the temple everything, who was tortured for the names of smugglers and gave them — and who then hid, nine years, behind ink and prayers, telling himself he was paying a debt when really he was just hiding. And hiding looked so much like devotion that even the temple believed it."

*Yes,* Yara said. *That too. All of it. Truth is a whole cloth, little scribe.*

"I'm not hiding anymore," Ada said, and stood, and put his hand on the chain of names, and spoke the final word — the first word, the name that had been hers before the sky fell and the crown learned to chew:

"*Yara.*"

The mountain answered.

It began in the floor, a shudder like the earth clearing its throat. The chain of names didn't break — it *bloomed*, each link unfolding into light, and Yara rose out of Ada like the sea reclaiming a coastline, vast and silver-green and furious and *joyful*, filling the shrine, filling the stair, pouring up through the bones of the mountain faster than any army could climb down it.

*THREE HUNDRED YEARS,* she said, and her voice was surf and storm and, unmistakably, relief. *I REMEMBER EVERY NAME. INCLUDING YOURS, SEFA KARIM OF THE WEATHERCOCK CREW. HOLD, DAUGHTER. I COME.*

### Aldric

King Aldric woke on the floor of his own hall, face-down on cold marble, with a scream dying in his throat.

For nine years he had lived inside a dream of ruling — signing decrees in his sleep, smiling at councils whose faces he couldn't quite recall, watching the world through glass stained salt-white. He had known, in the way drowning men know, that something was wearing him. He had begged, some nights, into the dark. No one had heard.

Something heard him now.

Across the miles, through stone and soil and the roots of the world, he felt a tide arrive — immense, ancient, and precise as a surgeon. It did not smash the thing in his crown. It *poured* around it, the way the sea deals with a barnacle, and then it pulled.

The burning was indescribable and lasted four seconds.

What remained of the parasite flared gold-black in the circlet on his brow, shrieked in a language with no mercy in it, and burned out — a curl of smoke, a smell like scorched hair and low tide, gone.

Aldric lay on the marble and wept like a child, great ugly sobs, clutching his own face to make sure it was his. Around him, courtiers scattered or stared. Queen-Sister Maren was miles away on a mountain, but he spoke to her anyway, to the memory of her, because she was the only person he could imagine believing him.

"Maren," he sobbed. "Maren, it's me. It's *me*. Where did it go? Where did it *go*?"

Far above Thornfell, in a deep shrine flooding with silver-green light, a god rising like a tide answered across the distance, though only one man heard it:

*Home. Where I am going, it does not follow. Rise, king. You are late for your own apology.*

* * *

### Sefa

The army stopped.

Not retreated — stopped, mid-climb, mid-fight, weapons dropping from slackening hands as a green-silver light swept up the Weeping Stair like a wave finding its shore. Sefa, sitting against a boulder with a spear-wound in her side and blood in her boot, watched the light reach the wounded captain who had spoken with the crown-thing's voice. The captain gasped, sagged, and began, quietly, to cry — the ordinary crying of an exhausted man who was suddenly, entirely, only himself.

The light touched Sefa last, deliberately, like a hand.

Warmth flooded the wound. Not healing exactly — she'd carry the scar, she suspected, forever — but closing, knitting, enough. Enough to stand. Enough to walk down through an army that had forgotten what it was fighting, men and women blinking in the strange dawn-light and asking each other questions no one could answer.

At the bottom of the pass, where the fog was lifting off the valley like a curtain going up, Sefa saw the sea. She had never seen the sea from this height before. It caught the early sun and threw it back silver, and for one absurd moment she was certain it waved.

"Yeah," she told it, hoarse. "Yeah, all right. Coming."

She started up the mountain, toward the bell-sound, toward the shrine, toward a scribe who owed her nine years of explanations and a very long list of apologies.

She intended to collect every single one.
