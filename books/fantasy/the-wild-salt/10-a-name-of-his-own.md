## 10. A Name of His Own

The ledgers burned on a Tuesday, because Maren said reforms should begin on ordinary days.

"Miracles make people kneel," she told the assembled priests of Thornfell, standing in the courtyard where nine years ago I had first copied a prayer I didn't believe. "Tuesdays make them think. We have had enough kneeling."

I stood at the back with ink still under my fingernails from the last copying I would ever do for them—the public confession, in Maren's own hand, that I was merely setting down fair. Nine hundred years of temple records, and it came to eleven pages: what the binding truly was, who Yara was, what names the temple had erased, what lies it had told to keep its own power comfortable. Eleven pages. The old high priest, Brother Cassian, wept through most of the reading, though whether from grief or relief even he probably couldn't say.

And then they brought out the debt-ledgers.

Every family in Thornfell owed the temple something. Grain tithes, coin debts, years of servitude written in careful columns by careful scribes—by scribes like me, who had once been one of those columns myself. My family's name sat on page forty-one, three generations deep, the debt my parents had died still owing, the debt I had spent nine years chipping away with smuggled knowledge and copied prayers, never quite able to see the bottom of it.

Maren handed me the torch herself.

"I can't," I said, which was true, and then heard how it sounded, and said the truer thing: "I'm afraid if I start burning them I'll want to burn the whole temple."

"Then stand close to me," she said, "and aim carefully."

The paper caught fast, as paper does when it has waited centuries for someone to stop being polite about it. The crowd made a sound I hadn't expected—not cheering, exactly, but a long exhale, like a held breath finally let go. An old woman near me began to laugh and couldn't stop. A boy asked his mother if the ash could be used for bread, and his mother, crying, said no, love, and laughed too.

Yara stirred somewhere beneath my ribs, warm as sunlit water.

*You are smiling,* she observed.

*I'm allowed.*

*You were always allowed. That was rather the entire problem.*

* * *

Aldric came to Thornfell himself, which nobody had expected, least of all Aldric.

He arrived with six soldiers instead of six hundred, wearing plain wool instead of the crown—he kept the crown in a locked iron box now, carried by two men whose whole duty was making sure it stayed shut—and when he dismounted in the temple courtyard, his eyes were just eyes again. Brown. Tired. Human enough to look embarrassed.

"You're the scribe," he said to me. It wasn't a question. He'd been told everything, apparently, including the part where his own mouth had signed decrees in the night while something wore him like a glove. "Ada Zhou."

"Your Majesty."

"Don't." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked less like a king than like a man who had woken from a very long fever and found the house a mess. "Do you know what the worst of it is? I remember. Not the doing—but the watching. Like being shut behind glass in my own skull, reading letters I couldn't stop my hands from writing. Nine years." His voice cracked on the number. "It fed on me for nine years, and the whole time my own sister was trying to tell me something was wrong, and I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "She's staying here. Did you know? She's taking over the reform of the temple, and I've signed—awake, this time, and read twice—papers giving her authority to do it properly. The church answers to her now. And the crown goes into the mountain vaults, sealed, until the scholars figure out what's safe to do with a dead god's tooth."

"It wasn't a tooth," Yara murmured through me, and I felt my mouth move before I chose to allow it, salt-cool, amused. "It was a splinter. Teeth belong to something with a body, little king. Be glad you never met the body."

Aldric went pale, then—remarkably—laughed. "Does she always do that?"

"Only when she thinks it's funny."

"And is she often right?"

"About what's funny? Constantly. About everything else, more than I like."

He looked at me for a long moment then, and there was something in his face I recognized, because I'd worn it for nine years: the look of a man deciding whether to be honest. He chose honesty. "I owe you my mind," he said. "I know a debt like that can't be repaid, only acknowledged. So consider it acknowledged. If you ever need anything—from the crown, from me—you will not need to ask twice."

"He'll ask zero times," Sefa said, appearing at my elbow with the timing she'd always had, "because he's an idiot about asking for things. But I'll hold you to it, Your Majesty. For both of us."

"Sefa Vane." Aldric studied her—the new scar running from her brow to her jaw, earned holding a pass against four hundred soldiers with thirty people and a lot of rockfall; the way she stood like someone who had decided pain was negotiable but loyalty wasn't. "They tell me you held the Serpent's Gate alone for twenty minutes."

"I had help. The mountain helped. Mostly I yelled a lot and threw things downhill. Gravity did the real work."

"They also tell me gravity has offered you a commission."

"Gravity," Sefa said, "has excellent taste."

* * *

That evening Yara asked me to walk up to the shore of the high tarn, the one above the shrine where she'd been bound, where the water ran silver and tasted faintly of salt no map had ever explained. She didn't take my voice anymore unless I gave it—that had been the first gift of the unbinding, learning that communion could be conversation instead of possession. But sometimes, on quiet evenings, she liked the walking, and so did I.

"You know why I called you here," she said, when we reached the water.

"I have a guess."

"Guess anyway. You're better at it than you admit."

"The offer," I said. "To be your speaker. Properly. Voice, temple, title. The Salt-Mother's chosen mouth in the world."

"The Salt-Mother's chosen *anything*, really. Speaker is only the word the temples prefer because it sounds employable." Her presence turned over inside me like something huge and contented settling in warm shallows. "Yes. The offer stands. There is no one alive who knows me better, Ada Zhou. No one who carried me up my own mountain. No one who spoke my names with his throat full of terror and did it anyway. I could give you purpose beyond any ledger. Every shore from here to the sea would know your name."

I looked at the tarn a while. Somewhere below us, Thornfell's windows were going gold with lamplight, one by one, the way they did every evening, the way they'd done every evening of the nine years I'd spent among them trying to be invisible. I thought about how much effort invisibility took. How it was, in its way, the hardest work I'd ever done—harder than smuggling, harder than the mountain, harder than speaking truth while an army climbed toward me. Hiding meant memorizing exits. It meant keeping accounts of everyone's expectations and paying each one exactly, never over, never under. It meant being a column of ink in someone else's book.

"I'm going to say no," I said.

"I know," said Yara, sounding delighted. "Say it properly, though. I want to hear your reasons. I've been listening to your silences for months; it's time I heard a speech."

I laughed, which still felt new in my chest, like a room I'd forgotten I owned. "All right. Here's my reason: I don't want a bigger name. I want mine. Just—mine. Ada Zhou, who lives on Lantern Row above the cooper's shop, who owes Widow Merrel a loaf because I ate hers on Thursday, who argues with the ferryman about the fare every single market day and always loses. Small things. Local things. Things I can actually carry." I crouched and touched the tarn, and it was cold, and it tasted of salt when I licked my finger, and that was enough. "If I'm your speaker, I become important, and important men get hidden again—wrapped in ceremony, protected, managed. I've done hidden. I was *good* at hidden. I'd like to try visible for a while. Visible and known and—" I hesitated on the last word, because I still wasn't entirely sure I believed it, but I said it anyway: "Forgiven."

The water shivered without wind. When Yara spoke again, her voice had gone gentle, the way it did maybe once a season.

"Three hundred years I lay bound, and do you know what I dreamed of? Not freedom, at first. Freedom came later, after hope got creative. At first I dreamed of exactly what you described. A small life. A door I could open. Someone saying my name without fear in their mouth." A pause, vast and warm. "So no, I don't accept your refusal, because it isn't one. You've accepted the better version of the offer. Be my speaker in the way that matters: live loudly where I can watch. Pray badly and honestly. Argue with the ferryman. That will honor me more than any temple." Something rippled through me like laughter through deep water. "Besides. I intend to visit. Often. You won't get rid of me."

"I'd be worried if I wanted to."

"Good. Now go home. There's a woman limping up the mountain path looking for you, and she'll pretend she isn't, and you'll both waste an hour being casual about it if I don't shove you."

* * *

Sefa found me at the foot of the path, leaning on a walking stick she refused to call a cane, her scar catching the last light. The healers said the leg would mend crooked but functional. Sefa said the leg had always been crooked and was merely being honest now.

"Cassian's retiring," she said, falling into step beside me. "Properly retiring. Says he's too old to preside over a church that tells the truth—it requires stamina."

"Who takes the shrine on the south road, then?"

"Maren's appointing someone local. Someone who knows the passes and the people and the difference between faith and paperwork." She glanced sideways at me, entirely too innocent. "No idea who she has in mind."

"Sefa."

"What? I'm just relaying information. I'm a courier now, technically. Aldric gave me a commission and everything. Gravity's finest." She stopped at the bend where the path overlooked the town, and her joking fell away the way it only did up here, where the mountain kept our secrets and returned them gently. "I'm coming back, Ada. To Thornfell. To stay. I signed the lease on the old tollhouse this morning, which means either I'm a resident or I've been swindled by a widow, and knowing Widow Merrel, possibly both."

I looked at her—at the scar, the stick, the stubborn set of her jaw—and something in my chest that had been clenched for nine years finally, fully, let go. "You could go anywhere," I said. "You held a pass against an army. Kings owe you favors."

"Yeah, well." She shrugged, awkward, looking out at the lamplights kindling below. "Turns out I already had a home. Took almost dying to notice it was here." She bumped my shoulder with hers, careful of the stick. "Besides, someone has to keep you honest. You're terrible at asking for help. You'd drown in a puddle out of politeness."

"Noted."

"And someone has to argue with the ferryman. You always lose alone."

"Together we lose faster. It's efficient."

We started down, side by side, our shadows long on the path, and behind us the tarn whispered salt into the dark, and ahead the town lights multiplied like a sky that had decided to come down and live among people. My name was in no ledger now. It was in mouths instead—Merrel's, Maren's, the cooper's kids who waved when they saw me, Sefa's, spoken plainly, without debt attached.

Somewhere far off, I thought I heard the sea.

Or possibly just Yara, laughing.

Either way, I walked toward it, visible, known, and—for the first time in my life—home on purpose.
