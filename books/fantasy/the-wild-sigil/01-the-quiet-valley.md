## 1. The Quiet Valley

The dead came to Juniper through his hands.

They had never come any other way—not in dreams, not in mirrors, not in the voices of birds or the rustle of wheat, though the old stories promised all of those and more. They came when his fingers touched bone, and they came quietly, the way rain comes to a valley that has learned to expect it: without ceremony, without surprise, filling every hollow until the whole world seemed to hold its breath and listen.

He was listening now.

The workshop smelled of beeswax and cold ash, of linseed oil and the particular mineral sweetness of old bone—smells so familiar to him that he no longer noticed them the way he noticed nothing about himself anymore, having spent thirty-one years becoming as unremarkable as the furniture he sat among. Outside the single window, dusk was settling over Villalobos Hollow like a hand closing gently over a candle flame. The mountains went blue. The first chimney smoke rose straight up in the windless air. Somewhere down the lane, Widow Marsh was calling her goats in for the night, her voice cracking on the second syllable the way it always did, and Juniper thought, not for the first time, that he knew the valley's dead better than he would ever know its living—and that he preferred it that way.

In his lap, cradled in a square of undyed linen, lay a rib bone no longer than his thumb.

It had belonged to Tam Broadstairs, aged six years and four months, drowned in the Millbeck three weeks past, and it had been given to Juniper willingly by Tam's mother with both hands and averted eyes, because that was how it was done here, had been done here for as long as anyone could remember, and probably longer than that. The living gave the dead to Juniper Villalobos the way they gave bread to the priest: because their mothers had done it, and their mothers' mothers, and because the alternative—to carry grief with nowhere to set it down—was unbearable.

He turned the bone over in his fingers. It was warm. Bone was always warm in his hands, which was the first thing people found unsettling about him when they were young and new to the valley, before they grew used to it or moved away or died themselves and joined the great murmuring congregation beneath the turf.

*Careful now,* said a voice through his knuckles, dry as autumn leaves. *He's shy yet.*

"I know," Juniper murmured. "I know he is."

Tam's death had been three weeks of silence. That was usual. The newly dead often needed time to understand what they had become; some of them raged, a few of them wept, most simply drifted at the edges of Juniper's awareness like swimmers too far from shore to make out clearly. But today, when he had taken up the rib, something had shifted—a pressure behind his sternum, faint but insistent, like a knock at a door from the inside.

*I didn't mean to,* the bone whispered. The voice was small and wet-edged, the voice of a child still learning what dying meant. *The water pulled. I told Mama I'd stay on the stones.*

"The water doesn't listen to telling," Juniper said softly. He had said some version of these words perhaps four hundred times in his life, to the drowned and the fallen and the fever-taken, and each time he felt the truth of them settle into his own chest like a stone into a well. "It never has. It isn't your fault, Tam."

A tremor ran through the bone, and through the bone into Juniper's hands, and through his hands into the older presences that crowded always at the edge of his gift—the valley's accumulated dead, hundreds of years of them, farmers and shepherds and midwives and one disgraced toll-keeper whose name the family had begged everyone to forget. They pressed close when a child died. They always did. Even the dead, it seemed, could not bear the death of children without leaning in.

*Little one,* rumbled a voice like millstones, old Aldous Fen, dead two hundred years, whose femur hung by the door among Juniper's tools. *You're among friends now.*

*We all fell in somewhere,* said a woman's voice, wry and smoke-rasped—the miller's daughter, dead of a lung fever at nineteen. *Some of us just fell slower than others.*

Juniper closed his eyes and let them talk. This was the part of the work no one paid him for and no one knew about: the part where he simply held the bone and listened while the dead gathered around the newest of their number the way villagers gathered around a frightened traveler at an inn, offering bread, offering warmth, offering the slow reassurance of shared experience. *You'll get used to the quiet,* they told Tam. *You'll learn to see without eyes. And when the carver holds you, you can speak, and being heard—that's the sweetest thing there is, little one. Being heard.*

And Tam, tentative, curious, already a little less afraid: *Will it hurt? Being carved?*

*Not hurt,* said old Aldous. *More like... being made into a letter. So the ones who loved you can read you whenever they need to.*

Juniper opened his eyes and reached for his knife.

* * *

The knife had been his grandmother's, as everything worth having had been his grandmother's. Its blade was no longer than a man's finger, worn to a crescent by three generations of careful hands, its handle wrapped in leather gone dark and glassy with age. There were other tools in the kit—burnishers and rasps, a tiny drill, a set of needles fine as insect legs—but the knife was the heart of it, and when Juniper picked it up he could almost feel her hands over his, correcting his grip, patient and exacting and, in her flinty way, loving.

*Steady,* she would say. *The bone remembers hurry. Carve like you've got all the time the grave allows.*

She had been dead eleven years, and she had never once spoken to him through her own bones, and he had stopped asking why.

He set the rib against the block of tallowwood clamped to his bench and began.

Memorial charms followed a grammar older than the valley's oldest headstone. A spiral for the continuing of things. A knot for grief, tied where the thumb could worry it. For the drowned, always, a wave-line broken by three dots—the three breaths the water allowed before it took its due. He cut shallow and true, curling pale shavings away from the groove, and with each stroke of the knife the presence in the bone grew clearer, closer, less a voice heard through a wall and more a warmth against his palm.

*Mama makes bread on Thursdays,* Tam said suddenly, urgently, the way the newly dead always burst out with the things they most feared losing. *With the dark flour. She lets me—she let me have the heel. Tell her I remember the heel.*

"I'll tell her," Juniper said. His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat and kept carving. "Anything you want remembered, I'll put it in the charm. That's what the charm's for. She holds it, and it's like holding your hand."

*Can she hear me? Through it?*

"No." He hated this part. Hated it the way you hate a scar—familiar, permanent, aching only when pressed. "The charm keeps what I carve into it. Your words, your warmth. But the speaking stops when the carving ends. I'm sorry, Tam. If I could build a bridge instead of a letter, I would."

The bone was quiet a moment. Then, smaller: *Is it very lonely? Where you go after?*

And the whole valley's dead surged forward at once—not words yet, just pressure, a tide of presence pressing up through his wrists, his forearms, his aching shoulders—and then old Aldous's millstone voice again, gentle as it had ever been in life or death:

*Lonely? Lad, there's ten thousand of us under this grass. Lonely's the last thing you'll be. Now hush, and let the carver work. You want to look your best for your mama, don't you?*

Juniper carved until the candle guttered low.

When it was done, the charm was a small thing, humble as everything else in the Hollow: a rib bone the size of a thumb, spiraled and knotted and waved, drilled through with a hole for the cord, its surface polished with wax until it held the firelight like a drop of amber. He breathed on it, the old way, and whispered into it the words Tam had given him—heeled bread and Thursdays and *I didn't mean to, tell her I didn't mean to*—and felt them sink into the carved grooves the way water sinks into thirsty ground. The bone warmed once, twice, and then settled into a stillness that was not absence but completion, the way a sentence is still once its final word is placed.

He wrapped it in linen. He would deliver it tomorrow, walk down to the Broadstairs farm in the morning fog, hand it to a mother with both hands and averted eyes, and say the words: *He's at peace, and this is his peace, kept for you.* And she would weep, and thank him, and press a coin into his palm that he would try to refuse and fail to refuse, and then she would close the door, and he would walk back up the lane alone, and somewhere along that walk the world would quietly rearrange itself back into the shape he kept it in: Juniper Villalobos, carver of memorials, a thin stooped man whom nobody invited anywhere and nobody feared and nobody, if they could help it, thought about at all.

That was the craft of it, he thought, banking the candle coals. Not the carving. The invisibility.

* * *

His grandmother had taught him that too, though she had called it something less kind. *Vanishing,* she'd said. *It's half your trade, boy. Anyone with steady hands can cut a spiral. But can you stand in a room and be no more seen than the chair? Can you take a mother's dead son and give him back as a trinket and leave her thinking of the trinket and not the strange thin man who handled him? That's the trade. That's the whole of it.*

He had been nine. He had asked her why, and she had looked at him for a long moment with those gray river-stone eyes, and she had said: *Because being known is being aimed at. Remember that. Whatever else you forget, remember that.*

He had remembered. He had built his whole life inside that remembering, the way a snail builds inside its shell, the way the valley itself seemed to build inside its mountains—folded in, hidden, content to be overlooked by the world beyond the passes. The Hollow had no lord and paid no taxes that anyone could trace; its roads appeared on no map Sera Quill's kind ever drew, though he wouldn't learn that name for some months yet. Travelers sometimes wandered in—peddlers, pilgrims, the occasional lost surveyor—and they always left with the vague conviction that they'd passed through a place too small and too dull to bother recording, and Juniper had helped them arrive at that conviction, smiling his bland smile, offering directions that wound harmlessly back toward the main road.

Because being known is being aimed at.

He wondered, sometimes, in the long gray hours before dawn when the dead slept and even his hands were quiet, what exactly his grandmother had known. What she had been aiming away from. She had burned papers before she died—him watching, eleven years old, as she fed a decade of letters to the stove with hands that shook only slightly—and she had made him swear three oaths on her deathbed, and only one of them had made sense: *Never leave the valley, Juniper. The valley is the only wall between you and what our blood is owed.*

Our blood is owed. He had turned that phrase over for twenty years like a coin he couldn't read the face of. Owed by whom? To whom? The valley dead had never answered. When he asked them—casually, obliquely, the way you ask a dangerous question of a friend—they went politely vague, the way people do around a family shame, and eventually he had stopped asking, the way you stop touching a bruise.

The living asked nothing at all. That was the arrangement. They brought him their dead; he gave them back their dead transformed into comfort; and in exchange, they let him be exactly as strange as he needed to be, at exactly the distance he needed to keep. Old Petch at the mill nodded to him across the yard and never crossed it. The priest tolerated him with the wary courtesy of one professional acknowledging another whose methods differed. Children were warned not to pester him, which suited him fine; he had never learned what to do with the living that he hadn't learned from the dead, and the dead were so much easier to love.

That was the shameful arithmetic of his heart, and he knew it was shameful, and he kept it anyway: the dead wanted so little. To be heard. To be carried a little farther. To have someone say *you mattered, I remember the heel of the Thursday loaf.* The living wanted things you couldn't carve or wrap in linen—wanted conversation and reciprocity and the terrible daily risk of being perceived, being *known*, and every year the gap between what the dead required and what the living demanded grew wider, until loving the dead felt less like a calling than a refuge.

He blew out the candle. In the dark, the workshop filled with the soft rustle of the resting dead—old Aldous's femur by the door, the miller's daughter's fingerbones in their cedar box, forty other relics waiting for commissions that might come next week or next year—and the sound was so like company that his chest ached with something he refused, as always, to name.

"Goodnight, all," he said to the darkness.

*Goodnight, carver,* murmured ten thousand throats of dust and memory, tender as rain.

* * *

He woke before dawn, as he always did, to the particular quality of silence that meant snow was coming—though it was early in the season for it—and lay listening to the house tick and settle. Then he dressed, ate standing at the counter, and took up the linen-wrapped charm for the walk down to the Broadstairs farm.

The fog was thick off the Millbeck, wooling the hedgerows, muffling the world to a radius of twenty feet. His boots knew the way regardless. He walked through the white blindness feeling pleasantly erased—fog was the valley's own vanishing, laid on generous as a blessing—and it occurred to him, as it occasionally did, that he was happy, insofar as he permitted himself the word. Narrow, hidden, useful. A life measured out in memorials. There were worse shapes for a soul to fit.

The Broadstairs farmhouse emerged from the fog all at once, close enough to touch. Tam's mother was already in the yard, and she had been crying—you could tell even at distance, even in fog, by the way a person stood when they'd been crying since before dawn—and Juniper squared his shoulders, put on his bland smile, and began the practiced litany of comfort in his head.

He got within ten paces of her before the dead spoke.

Not Tam. Not Aldous, not the miller's daughter, not the ten thousand murmuring congregants of the Hollow's soil. Something else—a voice with no body attached, coming from no bone in his kit, no grave in his ken, arriving not through his hands at all but through his ears, plain as the widow calling her goats, and saying words that stopped him mid-stride with his heart slamming like a fist on a locked door:

*At last. At last. Oh, you're awake, you're finally awake—we heard you, we all heard you—*

And then, everywhere beneath him at once, across the whole valley, the dead went silent.

Not sleeping-silent. Not resting-silent. Silent the way a forest goes silent when the wolf walks through it—every voice cut off mid-word, every presence recoiling, and through his numb hands Juniper felt the last flicker of the congregation's feeling before it vanished entirely, and the feeling was terror.

Down the yard, Tam's mother had sunk to her knees in the mud, staring at the sky, and she was laughing and weeping at once, and far off toward the chapel a bell began to ring that had not rung in living memory—

—and in Juniper's silent, suddenly empty hands, the memorial charm for a drowned child had gone cold as riverbed stone.
