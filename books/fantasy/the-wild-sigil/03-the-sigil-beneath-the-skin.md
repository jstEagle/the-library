## 3. The Sigil Beneath the Skin

The rain came down on the valley like a blessing nobody had asked for, soft and steady, filling the gutters of Hollowfen with a sound Juniper had loved since childhood. He worked by lamplight in the back room of the carving shed, the one with no windows, where the air smelled of bone dust and beeswax and the particular sweetness of linseed oil worked into old wood. On his bench lay his grandmother's kit.

It had come back to him three days ago, wrapped in oilcloth and carried up from the lowlands by a cousin he barely knew, who had inherited it from an aunt who had inherited it from somewhere further back than anyone could say. The family had scattered its dead across four counties, it seemed, but the tools had always found their way home. Juniper understood why. Bone remembered hands.

He unwrapped it now, slowly, the way he did everything that mattered. The oilcloth fell away and there they were: eleven knives of varying size, their handles worn to the shape of grip after grip after grip, generations of thumbs pressed into the same hollows until the wood itself had learned to be held. A whetstone gone concave with use. A spool of copper wire gone green at the edges. A small brass caliper whose hinge still moved smoothly, oiled by someone's care long after they were gone. And beneath them all, folded flat, a square of leather studded with slots where two more tools should have sat — empty slots, worn smooth, as if whatever had lived there had been removed long ago and often.

"Hello," Juniper said softly, to the kit, because you said hello. His grandmother had taught him that. *Everything that worked this long has a soul in it somewhere, even if it's only the soul of patience.*

The dead in the walls stirred. Not the shed's dead — the shed was new, barely sixty years — but the valley's dead, who gathered wherever he worked the way birds gather where grain is threshed. They had no voices he could hear with his ears. They spoke through his hands, a language of warmth and pressure and small tugs at the tendons, and right now they felt like a congregation settling into pews. Curious. Attentive. The drowned child's charm was finished and delivered; the grief in the village had been fed, and for now the dead were simply... watching him work.

*Careful,* murmured something through his left hand, an old woman's caution, patient as frost. He didn't know her name. Most of them he never learned names. But he knew her tone. She'd been warning him about things all week — the loose board on the stairs, the kettle left too near the flame. Grandmotherly, though she wasn't his grandmother. He wondered, not for the first time, if the dead got assigned to the living, or if they simply drifted toward whoever listened.

"I'm being careful," he told her, and picked up the largest knife.

He began, as ritual demanded, with maintenance. He cleaned each blade with a rag dampened in spirits, checking the edges against his thumbnail. He rewrapped the handles where the binding had frayed. He oiled the caliper's hinge. It was meditative work, the kind of work that let his mind go quiet enough for the dead to lean close, and he lost himself in it for the better part of an hour, the rain keeping its soft time on the roof.

It was when he lifted out the leather tool-roll to clean beneath it that he found the false bottom.

The leather was stitched over a second layer, and between the layers lay a flat rectangle of horn or old ivory, thin as a wafer. He drew it out and turned it in the lamplight. It was a template — carvers used them, shapes traced onto bone before the knife ever touched it. But this was unlike any template he'd seen. It was cut into the shape of a sigil: a vertical line crossed by three descending arcs, and at its heart a small circle pierced through, with fine radiating lines like the rays of something opening. Like an eye. Like a seed split open. Like a door.

He knew every traditional pattern. Spirals for safe passage. Knots for binding grief. The interlaced V-shapes for protection of children, which he'd carved three days running for the drowned girl's mother. This was none of them.

And yet his hands knew it. That was the terrible thing. When he turned the template over, his fingers settled on its edges with the ease of long familiarity, thumb finding the circle's center, and warmth flooded up his wrist so sudden and rich that he nearly dropped it. The dead in the walls went utterly still. Not quiet — still, the way animals go still before a storm breaks.

"What is this?" he whispered.

The old woman's presence returned, faint and reluctant, pressing one word through the bones of his palm in that language without sound: *Yours.*

"That's not an answer."

But even as he said it, something cold walked up his spine, because the shape nagged at him. Three descending arcs. A pierced circle. Radiating lines. He'd seen it before. Not in any carving manual. Somewhere closer than that.

He set the template down and, hands almost steady, unbuttoned his shirt.

---

The birthmark sat below his left nipple, angled toward the ribs, a patch of skin slightly darker than the rest, the color of tea steeped too long. It had been there since birth — his mother had told him so, laughing, saying the midwife had remarked on it, saying it looked like nothing much, a stain, a shadow, a spill. He'd stopped seeing it decades ago. You stop seeing your own skin. It is the one country you never bother to map.

He leaned toward the lamp and looked at it properly for the first time in twenty years.

Three descending arcs. A circle at the center, pierced through. Fine lines radiating outward, fainter with age, but there — unmistakably there, once you knew to look. The mark on his ribs and the template in his grandmother's kit were the same shape, drawn by the same intention, separated by however many years and however many hands. Someone had copied it. Or someone had been copying it, generation after generation, carrying the design forward in leather and horn because flesh alone could not be trusted to remember.

"No," Juniper said aloud, to the empty room, to the rain, to the attentive silence of the dead. "No, that's coincidence. Birthmarks are— they're nothing. They're accidents."

His hands disagreed. His hands were trembling, and the dead were pressing against his palms from the other side of everything like faces pressed to glass.

He should have put the shirt back on. He knew that even then — some animal part of him understood that he stood at the edge of something with no path back across it, and the wisest move was retreat. But curiosity is its own kind of hunger, and Juniper had spent his whole life feeding everyone else's ghosts while starving his own questions. He reached out and touched the sigil on his skin.

It glowed.

Not reflected lamplight. Not some trick of fatigue. It *glowed* — a warm gold light rising out of the mark itself, out of him, tracing the three arcs and the pierced circle until they burned clear and bright against his skin like a brand lit from within, like a window opened in the wall of his own body. And through his hands, the dead made a sound he had never heard them make in all his years of listening: not words, not comfort, not grief.

They recoiled.

Every presence in the walls pulled back at once, a tide going out, and the sudden absence rang in him like a bell struck silent. For thirty years the dead had been his constant weather, his background hum, his strange and beloved company. Now the room held nothing but rain and lamplight and his own galloping heart, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that they hadn't merely withdrawn.

They had recognized something. And recognition, in their world, meant fear.

* * *

He sat on the floor for a long time. The lamp burned lower. The rain eased toward the small hours and then thickened again, and Juniper Villalobos, charver of memorials, keeper of the valley's quiet griefs, sat among his grandmother's tools and tried to think like a man instead of a frightened boy.

Facts, he told himself. Stick to facts.

Fact: his grandmother had owned a template of a sigil that matched the mark on his body. Fact: two slots in her kit had been emptied, deliberately, their contents removed by whoever packed it last. Fact: the mark answered touch with light, which no birthmark in the history of birthmarks had ever done. Fact: the dead feared it.

Fact: he had carried it all his life, and until tonight, nothing had happened.

That last fact was the one he held onto. Whatever the sigil was, it had slept in him for thirty-one years without stirring. Perhaps it had woken tonight because he'd touched it with intent for the first time — because he'd *looked*. Perhaps it was like anything else that lived: dormant until disturbed.

Or perhaps — and here the thought came cold and complete, arriving whole like a fish surfacing — perhaps it hadn't been sleeping at all. Perhaps it had been waiting. Marks like this weren't accidents in the old stories his grandmother told on winter nights, stories she claimed were nonsense even as she told them. In those stories, marks were *claims*. Brands placed by gods on their property. Seals pressed into wax. Signatures of ownership written into the skin of the owned before they were old enough to read the contract.

He thought of his grandmother's hands, so deft, so gentle, guiding his small fingers around a knife when he was six. *You carve for the grieving, Junny. Never for yourself. Promise me.* He'd promised. He'd thought it was humility she was teaching, or craft discipline. Now he wondered if it had been a rule with teeth behind it. Never for yourself. Because what you carry is dangerous. Because what you are belongs to something that will come looking.

The empty slots in the kit rose in his mind. Two tools removed. By his grandmother? Before she died? Had she known what slept under his skin? Had she spent her life teaching him to serve the dead precisely so the living powers would overlook him?

The dead pressed close again at the edge of his awareness — not returning, not exactly. Hovering. The way villagers hover at the edge of a yard where something wild has been seen. After a while, the old woman's presence crept back, cautious as a cat crossing ice, and laid one word into his palm:

*Hide.*

"Yes," Juniper said hoarsely. "Thank you. I'd worked that out."

But hide from whom? That was the question the night wouldn't answer. He buttoned his shirt over the mark as if cloth could argue with fate, and sat with his back against the workbench, and watched the lamp burn down, and thought about how for thirty-one years he had believed himself the most invisible man in the valley — a quiet trade, a quiet life, no wife, no land worth naming, no ambitions anyone would trouble to envy. He had built his invisibility plank by plank and called it peace.

And all the while he'd been wearing a lantern under his shirt.

Near dawn, exhausted past thinking, he did the last thing he expected himself to do. He took up the horn template, and a scrap of river-bone from his stock, and he traced the sigil onto the bone with a scribe's point — not carving it, just marking the outline, just asking the question in the only language he fully trusted. Then he set the scribe down and laid his palm flat on the marked bone.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. It was a drawing on a scrap of bone, not a prayer, not a working.

Except.

Deep in the marrow-quiet where the dead spoke to him, something very far away turned over in its sleep. He felt it the way you feel a cart pass on a distant road — not the cart itself, only the tremor in the ground, only the knowledge that weight is moving somewhere beyond sight. Something vast had registered the shape. Something patient had opened one eye.

The dead fled again, all of them, every presence in the valley pulling away from his hands like smoke from a doused fire, and in the ringing emptiness they left behind, Juniper heard — with his actual ears, in the actual silence of the grey dawn — the first crows of the morning, and beneath them, faint and rhythmic, from far off toward the ruined temple road, a sound like stone grinding against stone.

Like a door remembering how to open.

He sat very still until the sun rose, and then, because his hands needed work the way lungs need air, he picked up his smallest knife and began, with great care and enormous dread, to carve.
