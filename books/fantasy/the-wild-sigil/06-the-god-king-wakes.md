## 6. The God-King Wakes

The stone had been dreaming.

That was the only word Aurel had for it afterward, when he tried to reconstruct what a century of stillness had felt like from the inside. Dreaming was not accurate—he had not dreamed, not truly, because dreams require a dreamer who moves through them, and he had been nothing so restless as that. He had been a held breath. He had been the silence between two heartbeats, stretched out over a hundred years of rain falling on a ruined roof, of roots shouldering apart his altar, of snow drifting through broken windows and melting in spring and drifting again. The world had happened to him the way weather happens to a mountain: constantly, indifferently, without once requiring his participation.

And then, somewhere far to the north and west, across ranges he could not see and valleys whose names had rotted out of living memory, something flared.

It did not wake him gently. It was not a voice calling, or a hand on his shoulder, or any of the tender mechanisms by which mortals imagine gods are roused. It was blood. His own blood, diluted down a hundred generations into some carver's grandson with bone dust under his fingernails, and it lit up like a signal fire in the dark of him, and Aurel remembered that he had a body, and the body remembered that it had once been worshipped, and the century cracked open like an egg.

* * *

He rose badly at first. That must be said honestly, because honesty—cold, unsentimental, exacting honesty—was the discipline that had made him what he was, and a god who lies to himself about small things will soon lie about everything.

His left arm did not move. Stone does not surrender its grip gracefully; it merely decides, after enough time, that flesh and mineral have reached an agreement neither party remembers making. So he lay on his own altar, staring up at the vaulted ceiling where murals of himself had faded to smudges and shadows—the crown still legible, the face eaten away—and he began, patiently, the work of becoming again.

He flexed what would flex. He catalogued pain, because pain was information. The right hand answered; the jaw answered; the spine answered with a grinding reluctance like a door in a house long abandoned. He worked each part the way a man works a rusted lock, not with force but with attention, coaxing, insisting, until sometime in what he judged to be the deep hours of night—for the temple's broken clerestory showed stars wheeling past—he sat up.

Dust fell from him in sheets. It had accumulated in the folds of his robes, in the hollows of his throat, in the cupped palms that pilgrims had once kissed. He looked at his hands and found them gray as ash, and beneath the gray, faintly, the old gold: the sigils inked into his skin a thousand years ago by priests who had believed, correctly, that they were writing on something permanent.

Aurel breathed. It was an unnecessary act—he had not breathed in a hundred years, and would not strictly need to breathe now—but he did it anyway, because breath was how he spoke, and he intended to do a great deal of speaking.

"Century," he said aloud. His voice came out ruined, a whisper ground through gravel, and he almost smiled at it. "You are forgiven. You did not know what you were doing."

* * *

He walked his temple while the moon set.

This took a long time, because his temple was large and he was slow, and because he stopped at every station of ruin to take account. The nave had lost its roof entirely; grass grew between the flagstones where the congregation had knelt, thick and green and utterly unrepentant. A tree had come up through the baptistry, its roots wrapped around the font like fingers around a stolen thing. Bats had colonized the bell tower, which meant the bells still hung, green with verdigris, mute for want of rope and hands.

He touched each bell as he passed beneath them. Not rang—touched. There was a difference. Ranging them would have been a claim, and claims made too early were how kings became corpses. Touching them was a promise kept privately, between himself and the bronze.

In the crypts below, he found his priests.

They had not done well. That was the cost of the arrangement, and he had warned them of the cost, and they had accepted it with the particular fervor of men who believe eternity is a place rather than a duration. When the worship failed—when the last valley went quiet and the prayers thinned to nothing and then to less than nothing—the priests had chosen sleep as he had, but they had slept without divinity to hold their shapes, and so their bodies had continued their slow argument with gravity and rot even as their minds stood still. What remained in the sarcophagi was not pleasant. He opened each lid anyway, one by one, because a king counts his soldiers before a campaign, even when the count is grim.

Forty-one sarcophagi. Nine intact enough to raise. The rest had gone to bone and brown stain and the small industrious ecology of beetles, and he stood over them a while with something that was not grief but was adjacent to it, the way a mason is adjacent to a collapsed wall—not sorrow, exactly, but a professional assessment of what would need rebuilding.

"You slept faithfully," he told the bones. "I will find you better vessels."

Because that was the truth of his priesthood, the secret under the secret: the bone-priests had never been bound to their bodies any more than he was bound to his. Flesh was clothing. Faith was the thread that held the garment together. A priest who died with sufficient devotion did not stop serving; he simply waited, bodiless and patient, for cloth. And there was cloth everywhere, in the world above—there always had been. Pilgrims walked roads alone. Heretics wandered into deserts. The desperate and the devout were forever setting out toward holy places and never arriving, and every one of them was a robe waiting to be worn.

But not tonight. Tonight he needed only the nine.

He laid his palm on the first sarcophagus's rim and spoke the waking words—not loudly, not in any tongue a living congregation would recognize, but in the old liturgical register, the language of command, which sounds like prayer but is in fact the opposite: prayer asks, and this instructed.

"Brother Vessel. The century is ended. The blood has flared. Rise and be counted."

Nothing happened for the space of ten heartbeats—ten unnecessary heartbeats; he counted them anyway, out of habit, out of the old discipline—and then the sarcophagus exhaled a breath of air that had been sealed since before the border wars, and inside it something moved that had decided, very recently, to be alive.

The priest who climbed out was mostly preserved, which is to say mostly wrong: skin like tallow, eyes sunk to coins in wax, lips gone thin and dark around teeth that had yellowed toward ivory. He knelt on the crypt floor without being asked, forehead to the stone, and his first word was not *my lord* or *father* but simply:

"Where."

Not *where am I*. Just *where*—the question of a soldier waking on a field, wanting the map before anything else.

"The Vale's western marches," Aurel said. "What used to be the road to Sarnholt."

"Sarnholt fell."

"Yes."

"The kingdom—"

"Gone." Aurel watched him absorb this. It was important that the priest absorb it fully, without cushioning, because despair handled correctly becomes fuel. "The name is struck from the charts. The line that struck it thinks itself safe. They burned our temples where they could find them, salted our springs, married our princesses into their own houses to dilute the blood." He let that sit. "It did not dilute. Blood remembers what chart-makers forget."

The priest raised his head. In the tallow face, the eyes had begun to do something terrible, which was to hope.

"How many years, lord?"

"A hundred."

The priest closed his eyes. When he opened them, the hope had been rendered down into something harder and more useful, and Aurel thought, with satisfaction: *yes, there it is, that is the ore I smelt.*

"Whom shall we gather?" the priest asked.

"All of them. Wake your brothers. Then go out and find me the faithful."

* * *

By dawn—or what passed for dawn, the sky over the ruins going the color of dirty wool—eight more priests stood in the nave, arranged in the crescent formation that had been drilled into the order a thousand years ago and apparently survived death's interruption. They did not fidget. They did not whisper. They stood in their various stages of decay like a row of unfinished statues, and Aurel walked the crescent slowly, looking at each face, remembering names, assigning values.

He had missed this. That surprised him, and he examined the surprise with the same cold honesty he applied to everything: yes, he had missed this. Not power—power was a tool, and tools do not produce longing. What he had missed was *order*. The world of a century ago, whatever its faults, had understood hierarchy. It had known what a god was. Men had built these walls knowing they would never see them finished, because finishing was not the point; permanence was the point; the point was that something in the world should stand above the weather.

Then the valleys had gone quiet. Not rebellious—quiet. Quiet was worse. Rebellion he could have fed on; rebellion generates prayer of a kind, all those furious petitions hurled at heaven like stones. But the quiet centuries had simply... stopped asking. The old rites had worn down to superstition, the superstitions to folk habit, the habits to nothing at all, and the door between worlds—which had never been a door so much as a wound that he and his kind had learned to live inside—had scarred over from both sides.

He had felt it close. Even in stone, even in the held breath, he had felt it: the diminishment, the narrowing, the slow starvation of being remembered by no one. Gods are not sustained by existence. They are sustained by address. And when no one addresses you, you discover that you were never a god at all—you were a relationship, and relationships end when one party stops showing up.

Which was why the flare of blood in the night had mattered so much, and why he had risen before fully understanding it. Somewhere, a descendant of the erased line had done something. Touched something. Carved something. The sigil had woken in him like a coal breathed upon, and for one glorious moment Aurel had felt it the way a blind man feels sunlight: not seeing, but knowing, absolutely, that the source existed.

One drop. That was all the door required. One drop of royal blood, freely given or freely spilled—it preferred freely given, but doors are not famous for their pickiness—and the scar would tear open along its old seam, and what was on the other side would come through, and he would be what he had been before the erasure: not a god among gods, but the god, singular, addressed, fed.

He only had to find the heir. And heirs, he had learned across long dynasties, were like springs: you did not hunt them; you followed the water downhill. Where there was blood, there were neighbors. Where there were neighbors, there were prayers. And where there were prayers—

He smiled. It was not a warm expression. It was the smile of a man watching frost form on a window: precise, patterned, indifferent to the glass.

"—and where there are prayers," he said aloud, to the crescent of priests, "there is a door already half-open. We will not kick it. Kicking wakes the dogs. We will knock, and answer, and be welcomed."

* * *

The first miracle took three days.

He chose a village two days' walk south of the ruins, a place of thin soil and thinner luck, where the wells ran brackish in summer and the children were counted like coins because so many of them were spent early. He sent Brother Vessel—the first-raised, the most intact—to walk its road at dusk wearing the face of a traveling mendicant, which is a face any priest can make from any skull, and to sit by the well and listen.

The listening did not take long. Villages like this were aquifers of desperation; you struck water anywhere you dug.

There was a woman whose son had a fever the local remedies had failed to touch. There was a farmer whose ox had gone lame at plowing time. There was an old man who prayed every night to saints whose names he pronounced wrong, which somehow made the prayers more nourishing, the way mispronounced words reveal that they were learned in childhood, at a grandmother's knee, in intimacy.

Brother Vessel reported all this at dawn, kneeling in the wet grass outside the ruins, and Aurel considered the ledger the way a merchant considers three competing debts.

"The ox," he said finally.

The priest blinked. Tallow eyelids over coin eyes. "Lord?"

"Not the child. Everyone expects a god to save the child; saving the child buys one grateful mother and a town full of skeptics asking why the fever was permitted until now. And not the old man—his faith needs no reinforcement; it is already load-bearing. The ox." He let the word hang. "The ox is plowing time. The ox is the difference between eating next winter and not. Save the ox, and the farmer tells everyone, and everyone says *coincidence*, and then the second miracle comes, and they say *fortune*, and by the third they have stopped saying anything at all and simply begun to kneel. Gratitude is a crop. You plant it in rows and you do not overwater it."

"And the woman's son?"

Aurel looked at him for a long moment.

"Heals," he said. "On the fourth day. Naturally, as far as anyone will ever prove. Let her spend those four days learning what begging costs. Gratitude bought cheaply is gratitude squandered."

* * *

On the seventh night after his rising, he climbed the bell tower.

The bells hung silent above him, fat with verdigris, and beyond them the land rolled away dark to every horizon: forests reclaiming old fields, rivers running in channels that had shifted since anyone bothered to chart them, villages scattered like dropped seeds, each one containing its handful of hearts, each heart containing its private catalogue of wants. A hundred years of wants, accumulating unanswered. A hundred years of interest compounding on a debt no one had collected.

He put his hand flat against the nearest bell, and this time he did more than touch.

He did not ring it. Ringing was for announcing, and announcements were premature. What he did was finer: he listened through the bronze, through the metal's long memory of every peal it had ever sung, down into the ground, into the root-system of the world, the way he had once listened a thousand years ago when the world had been loud with address. And there—at the very threshold of perception, faint as heat-shimmer—something answered.

Not a voice. A direction.

Northwest. Far. Through mountains and forgotten valleys and country that had, if his priests' intelligence proved correct, removed itself from the maps of men. Something there had flared and then gone quiet again, the way a hearth dims when someone throws a rug over it—but a covered fire is still a fire, and smoke finds chimneys, and blood finds blood.

"They hid a kingdom," he murmured to the bell, to the night, to the heir sleeping unknowing under whatever roof the centuries had left him. "Hid it so thoroughly they hid it from themselves. Clever. Patient. Worthy adversaries, once." His hand tightened on the cold bronze, and verdigris flaked away beneath his fingers like dead skin. "But hiding is a posture, little heir, and postures tire. You will grow lonely in your erasure. You will wonder what you were. And when you reach for the past to answer you—" 

Below him in the nave, faintly, the eight priests had begun the old chant, the waking-liturgy, their ruined voices braiding together in the dark of the roofless temple, rising past the bats and the stars.

"—I will be there. I have always been there. I am what waits at the bottom of every prayer."

He turned from the horizon and descended the tower stairs, unhurried, patient as stone, and behind him the bells hung in their silence, holding their promise, waiting for rope and hands.

Far to the northwest, in a valley full of whispering dead, a carver's grandson slept uneasily, one hand pressed to ribs that ached like a healing bruise.

Aurel could not see him. Not yet.

But the water was already running downhill, and he knew how to follow it.
