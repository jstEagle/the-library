## 7. Bone-Priests at the Ford

The ford was the valley's one open mouth.

Every other approach wound through passes too narrow for carts, too steep for anything but goats and patience, but at the eastern end of the vale the river spread itself thin and shallow over a bed of pale stones, and there the road came down from the high country and crossed. Juniper had stood on the near bank a hundred times in his life, watching travelers arrive: tinker's wagons, wool merchants, once a whole circus that wintered two seasons and left with half the miller's daughter's heart. Pilgrims were nothing new either. The valley lay on no great road to any shrine, but pilgrims wandered like water, finding their way into every low place.

These were different from the first moment he saw them.

He had come down to the ford to gather river-cane for charm handles, a basket over his arm, his collar up against the morning damp. There were six of them, walking two by two, and they wore the gray-white robes of penitents, hooded, their faces masked not with cloth but with thin plates of carved bone, lashed at the jaw with sinew. Each mask was smooth as an egg except for a single slit for sight, and each bore a sigil burned into the brow—a spiral coiling inward, tighter and tighter, toward a point that never quite arrived.

Juniper knew bone. He had held enough of it in his hands to know its temper, its grain, the way it took a blade. These masks were human. Femurs split and sanded flat, he judged, though the workmanship was finer than anything he could do—no tool marks, no seams. The bone had been persuaded into smoothness rather than cut.

And they were silent. Not quiet—silent, in the way that a stopped clock is silent, in the way that made the silence itself audible. The dead murmured everywhere along the river; drowned souls lingered near fords the way cats lingered near warm hearths, and usually their voices washed over him in a low tide as he walked the bank. Now, as the six figures stepped off the road and onto the crossing stones, every voice within his hearing drew inward and hushed. He felt it physically, like a hand pressed flat over his ears from inside his own skull.

*Don't look at them,* said old Marta's voice, faint and fraying at the edges, from somewhere upstream where her bones slept under the willow roots. Marta had been dead sixty years and mostly spoke in fragments, but the fragment now was clear as a bell struck once. *Don't look. Walk home, boy.*

He did not walk home. He turned instead toward the reed beds, crouched as if cutting cane, and watched through the stems.

The pilgrims crossed without splashing. That was the detail his hands would remember later, when his mind tried to make the morning ordinary again: the water should have taken them mid-shin, the ford was never lower than mid-shin even in the driest weeks, but their gray hemlines broke the surface and came up dry. The stones beneath their feet did not so much as shift.

At the near bank they stopped, all six together, and the one in front—the tallest, whose mask bore its spiral slightly larger than the rest—turned its head slowly, left to right, taking in the valley the way a man takes inventory of a house he means to buy.

Then it spoke. The voice came out of the bone mask sounding like wind through a rib cage.

"We seek the hearer."

The words carried across the water with unnatural ease, arriving at Juniper's hiding place among the reeds undiminished, as if spoken directly into his ear. His hands went cold around the cane knife.

"The what?" called Tam Broadfield, who kept the ferry pole at the ford's edge for travelers who came at flood season, and who was standing now in his doorway with his breakfast still in his hand.

"The hearer," the masked figure repeated. "One who hears the dead. We have traveled far. We bring offerings." It lifted a hand, and Juniper saw that the fingers were gloved in white leather stitched with tiny knots. "Gratitude. Guidance. Rest. Every soul that speaks has something it wants said, and we are here to carry the words of the one who listens."

Tam looked at the pilgrims. Tam looked at the sky. And then Tam Broadfield—who had bought a mourning charm from Juniper when his father died, who had stood in Juniper's shed drinking juniper spirits while he wept about it, who had never once told another living soul what the charm was or who had made it—Tam laughed.

"Hearer? Friend, you've walked out of whatever fever-dream you came from. This is Hollowmere Vale. We grow barley and cheat each other at cards. Nobody here hears anything but their mothers-in-law."

The masked head tilted. For a long moment nobody moved, and the river ran loud between them, and Juniper crouched in the reeds with his heart going like a trapped bird.

"You know him," the pilgrim said. Not a question.

"I know my neighbors," Tam said. "That's all anybody knows in a valley."

Something passed among the six—a small motion, a bowing of heads, a settling—and then the leader reached into its robe and produced an object that caught the light wrong, dull where metal should shine. A coin, or something shaped like a coin, and it tossed this across the water to Tam, who caught it on reflex and immediately wished, visibly, that he hadn't.

"For your trouble," the pilgrim said. "We'll ask along the valley. Someone will remember something someone said."

They turned and walked up the east road, toward the village, dry-hemmed and silent, and Juniper stayed among the reeds until the cold of the water had soaked through both knees and his basket was full of cane he didn't remember cutting.

* * *

By evening the whole valley knew, which meant the whole valley lied, which meant—for one strange, shining day—the whole valley belonged to him.

He heard it in pieces as he made his rounds, because rounds were the thing to do, the only way to bear it: moving from farmstead to farmstead delivering charms and condolences, listening to his neighbors rehearse their innocence. At the Wellers', old Weller was telling his sons, "If they come here, you never heard of Villalobos, you don't know the name, you never bought so much as a button from him." At the mill, the miller's wife was instructing her girls with the ferocity of a general: "He's a carver. Charms for graves, that's all, that's all anyone knows." Down at the tavern, Juniper stood at the end of the bar with his hood up and listened to four men compete to see who could claim ignorance most convincingly, Brannick declaring loudly to no one, "Hearer? Never heard the word," to general appreciation.

It should have warmed him. He understood, distantly, that it was meant to warm him—that this was the valley closing around him like a hand, the same hand that had closed around his grandmother when she was alive, around her mother before her. The valley had always known what he was. It had simply agreed, generation after generation, to forget on purpose, and today the agreement was being honored in public, loudly, at cost.

But what Juniper felt standing in the tavern's smoky warmth was not warmth. It was the specific chill of watching people step in front of a cart for you. There is gratitude in it, and shame, and underneath both a rising arithmetic he could not stop calculating: six pilgrims, one valley, forty households, how long, how much.

"They went to the shrine first," Marta whispered. She was strongest near him tonight—he'd brought a fresh-cut sprig of willow for her grave, an old habit—and her voice threaded up through his palms where they rested on the cold headstone. "Asked old questions of dead stone. Got nothing, of course. Our gods quit answering before your gran's gran was born. But they wrote things down, boy. They're patient ones. Patient ones are the worst kind."

"Where are they now?" he breathed, barely moving his lips.

"The Widow Ansel's. Paying for lodging. Paying double." A pause, and when Marta spoke again her voice had gone thin as paper. "Boy. Go home. Lock your door. Don't carve tonight. Some nights the less you listen, the safer everyone is."

He went home. He did not lock the door, because locking his door would be an admission, and he did not carve, because his hands were shaking too badly to hold a blade steady—but he sat up half the night anyway, listening to the dead murmur at the edges of his hearing like surf, and somewhere in the small hours he realized what had been bothering him since the ford.

The pilgrims had asked for the hearer. Not the heir. Not the bloodline. Not any word his grandmother had ever used in her rare, grim lessons. *Hearer.* Whoever sent them knew what he could do—but not, perhaps, everything he was.

There was a sliver of mercy in that. He held onto it like a coal in cupped hands, and slept at last near dawn.

* * *

They worked outward from the village center, house by house, and the valley lied to them magnificently.

Juniper heard the reports secondhand, thirdhand, embroidered and improved with each retelling. The pilgrims paid copper for answers and received weather talk. They attended services at the little stone chapel and knelt through them with perfect, unsettling stillness. They showed everyone the coin—word of the coin traveled faster than the pilgrims themselves, a strange dull piece stamped with the inward spiral—and offered more for true speech, and got jokes instead. Old Fenwick told them the hearer was a heron that lived up the west fork, and they thanked him gravely and wrote it down, which frightened Fenwick worse than any threat could have.

On the fourth day they came to the edge of Juniper's own lane, and stood at its mouth for the better part of an hour, all six of them, looking up at his shed the way men look at a door they intend one day to open. Then they withdrew without a word. He watched from behind the shed's shuttered window, and afterward he found himself cataloguing the shed's contents—his grandmother's kit, three decades of finished charms, the little iron pot where he melted the sealing wax—and thinking, with terrible clarity, *this can all burn. This can all be replaced. The question is what else can.*

On the fifth day they spoke to Doss Harrow.

Doss was ninety-one and had been dying politely for a decade, propped in his chair by the south window like a fixture of the house. In his prime he'd been the valley's best mason, and he had built the low wall around Juniper's grandmother's grave with his own hands, refusing payment, saying only, "She mended my boy's leg-bone when the doctor wanted to take it. You don't charge a family for that." Everyone in the valley knew the story. Everyone in the valley had, at some point, needed Doss's silence or given their own, and the web of it—of who owed whom, of what had been quietly mended and quietly concealed—was older than any living memory except Doss's own.

Juniper learned what happened from the dead, which was somehow worse than hearing it from the living, because the dead told it plainly.

The pilgrims had come to the Harrow house in the late afternoon. They had not asked Doss anything. That was the cruelty of it—they had simply sat with him, all six, in his small parlor, while the light went gold and then gray, saying nothing, until the silence grew teeth. And Doss, who had spent ninety-one years being a man who fixed things, had finally grown tired of watching strangers sit in a room like unburied bones, and had said, in his cracked old voice:

"If you're hunting the hearer, you're wasting your time. Whatever you think you'll get from him, you've already decided what you'll pay for it, and it won't be enough. Take your masks off. You look ridiculous."

And the leader had leaned forward, bone face catching the last of the light, and asked—not angrily, almost gently—"Old man, who mended your son's leg?"

The dead who lingered near the Harrow house told Juniper the shape of what followed, in the way the dead tell things, without sequence and without mercy. Doss had named no names. That was certain, that was repeated by every whispering soul in the vicinity with something like awe: Doss Harrow, ninety-one years old, faced with six bone-masked strangers who knew exactly which questions to ask, had given them nothing but the truth of his own old grievances and none of the valley's secrets.

But he had also, in his pride, in his exhaustion, in his simple old-fashioned contempt for being intimidated in his own parlor—said, "You think he's the only one who ever heard anything? This valley has been keeping its own counsel since before your order had a name. Ask me again tomorrow and I'll tell you the same."

They had thanked him. That was the part that turned Juniper's stomach for days afterward. They had thanked him, and risen, and bowed, and left copper coins on his table—one for each of them, six coins in a neat row—and gone out into the dusk.

Doss Harrow was found the next morning by his granddaughter, sitting in his chair by the south window, dead. There was no wound, no mark, no disturbance. His face was peaceful. The physician from two valleys over, summoned reluctantly, pronounced it his heart, an old man's heart, a merciful ending—and then stood in the parlor frowning at the mantel, where the granddaughter had lined up six copper coins, each one stamped with a spiral that seemed, if you looked too long, to coil inward forever without arriving anywhere.

The physician took one coin for his fee. He was dead within the week, in his sleep, peacefully, in a town thirty miles away, and the news of it reached the valley the same day the remaining five coins were buried under a cairn at the crossroads by unanimous and terrified agreement.

* * *

Juniper went to the funeral because everyone went to Doss's funerals; Doss had built half the walls and most of the steps in the churchyard, and it was understood you saw him down. He stood at the back in his good coat with his hood shadowing his face, and he listened to the chaplain say the standard words over the standard hole, and he did not cry, because crying at the back of a crowd of neighbors who were lying for him felt like a theft.

*You know it was for you,* the dead murmured from all around, from under the turf, from the walls Doss had built. *Say it plainly, boy. Say it once, at least, where he can't hear it.*

"It was for me," Juniper whispered into his collar. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, old man. I'll mend the wall. I'll mend everything you ever built. I'll—"

*No,* said a chorus of voices, gentle and terrible. *You'll leave. Before they stop asking and start taking. You've seen what their gratitude does. What do you imagine their disappointment looks like?*

He stayed for the lowering, because leaving early would draw eyes, and eyes were the one thing he could not survive. Six feet of earth went over the finest mason the valley ever produced, and the valley's finest liar, though those two titles were the same title here and always had been, and Juniper Villalobos stood at the back of the crowd and understood, with the slow cold certainty of water finding a crack, that his invisibility had never been free. It had been subsidized. Decade upon decade, neighbor upon neighbor, lie upon lie—every secret kept on his behalf had been a small stone laid in a wall, and walls were Doss's trade, and now Doss lay under one, and the bill had come due in a currency Juniper had never agreed to spend.

Afterward, in the churchyard's corner, Sera Quill was waiting for him.

He had been avoiding the cartographer for days—she lodged at the inn, asking her loud foreign questions about vanished kingdoms, and she was a lit torch in a valley doing its best to be darkness. But she stood now by the yew tree with her travel cloak beaded with rain, and her sharp face was, for once, stripped of ambition. She had liked Doss. Everyone had liked Doss. She'd spent three evenings sketching the churchyard wall while he supervised from his window, shouting corrections.

"They'll come for you next," she said quietly, without greeting. "I've watched men hunt before. First they map the terrain. Then they map the people. Then they remove whoever stands between." She glanced toward the fresh-turned earth. "Your neighbors are brave, carver. Braver than they've ever been allowed to be. But courage spends faster than copper, and those masked priests have deeper purses."

"You should leave the valley," Juniper said. "Whatever you're looking for, it isn't worth what this is going to cost."

Sera smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of a woman who had already paid prices he couldn't imagine. "Neither is yours. Yet here we both stand." She pulled her cloak tight. "When you're ready to stop paying in other people's lives, carver, you know where I'm lodging."

She walked away across the wet grass, and Juniper remained alone among the graves with the rain coming down, and every dead soul in the churchyard was silent, watching him, waiting for an answer he did not yet have.

Up the east road, faint through the rain, he could see the six gray figures standing at the mouth of his lane again. Still. Patient. Writing things down.
