## 8. The Runaway Daughter

The temple of Aurel did not sleep, but it pretended to.

Nim had learned that in her eleventh year, the night she first crept from her alcove to watch the bone-priests change the watch-candles. The great hall above was always awake—hymns at every hour, censers swinging, the god-king's voice rolling through stone like a tide that never fully withdrew—but below, beneath the floors where the pilgrims knelt and wept with gratitude, the temple's bones were quiet. And quiet things could be moved past.

She had been practicing this descent for six years.

Now she stood at the mouth of the reliquary stair with a hood drawn low over her face and a shard of black glass pressed against her sternum, bound there in linen, warm as a swallowed coal. The shard had no business being warm. It had been cold for a hundred years, sealed behind three doors of bronze and one of prayer, and it should have stayed cold until the end of the world. But her father had woken, and everything he touched remembered what it was for.

*You are my hands,* he had told her on his first night risen, holding her face in palms still gray with the sediment of stone-sleep. *You are the last thing I made before I lay down.* He had said it like a benediction. She had spent every night since hearing it like a sentence.

Two priests passed the stairhead above, their masks of yellowed femur catching the candlelight, their voices a low murmur about the western roads—something about a valley, a hearer, blood answering to blood. Nim pressed herself into the shadow of an urn taller than she was and did not breathe until their footsteps faded. Her heart was a fist against the shard, and the shard pulsed back, once, like something knocking to be let out.

*Not yet,* she told it. *Not here.*

It obeyed. That was the worst part. Everything in this temple obeyed her—she was the god-king's daughter, and obedience pooled around her feet like spilled oil—and none of it had ever asked her permission first.

* * *

The reliquary itself was a long throat of a room, lined with niches, each niche holding a piece of the world her father had broken the first time. A crown of interlocking vertebrae. A bell cast from a melted army's shield-bosses, said to ring only when a city died. Jars of ash labeled in a dead script: *the people who stopped believing; the people who wouldn't stop.* Nim had grown up among these relics the way other children grew up among dolls. She had named some of them. She had apologized to others.

The door-key's cradle stood empty at the far end, its velvet recess shaped like a sunburst with one ray snapped away. The missing ray—the shard against her chest—had come free the night her father woke, cracked loose by the first thunderclap of his rising, and rolled to her feet across the flagstones as if delivered. As if chosen. She had picked it up because everyone was watching and a daughter does not flinch from her father's leavings, and she had not been able to put it down since. Not because she couldn't. Because if she set it down, someone would pick it up, and then it would be used, and she knew—had always known, the way you know the shape of your own teeth with your tongue—exactly what it would be used for.

There was a door between worlds. Her father had closed it himself, a century ago, or been closed behind it—her father told both stories, depending on which made him more magnificent—and now he needed three things to open it again: a key, a gate, and blood of the line that had locked it. The key slept against her heart. The gate waited somewhere in the mountains, under the erased kingdom, in a country that no longer appeared on maps because its own king had cut it out of them. And the blood—

The blood was a boy in a valley who carved charms from dead men's bones.

She had heard the priests whispering about him for two nights now. *The hearer. The last Villalobos. The sigil woke in him unbidden—can you imagine, unbidden, after a century of silence?* They spoke of Juniper Villalobos the way farmers speak of rain coming to a drought-stricken field. Something precious, arriving. Something to be gathered.

Nim thought of him instead the way you'd speak of a hare marked for the hunt: alive, unaware, and already running in its sleep.

She crossed the reliquary floor without letting her sandals sound. At the outer door she paused, one hand on the latch, and looked back at the empty cradle, and felt—for one absurd heartbeat—an urge to leave something in its place. An apology. A stone. A note: *I have taken nothing that was yours.* But the key was not hers to return and not her father's to reclaim; it belonged to the door, and the door belonged to no one, and she was so tired of things belonging.

She left the reliquary empty and the latch undisturbed and began, at last, to run.

* * *

The mountain took her in the way mountains take fugitives: reluctantly, then completely.

Below her, the temple spread along its ridge like a spine of lit windows, hymn-smoke rising from vents in the rock, and she did not look back at it except once, at the tree line, when the bells began. They had found the empty cradle. Of course they had found it—her father had woken after a hundred years of stone and the first miracle of his new life was to be robbed by his own daughter, and the whole mountain would shake with his anger, and she was glad. Let him shake the mountain. Let him learn what it was to reach for something and find it gone. She had been reaching for him her entire life and finding, every time, a god where a father should have stood.

But she was not glad, really. She was terrified. Gladness was just terror wearing its good clothes.

The shard burned through its linen wrapping as she climbed, and by the time the temple lights sank below the shoulder of the slope it had begun, very faintly, to sing. Not with sound—with heat, with pressure, with a pulse she felt in her molars. It wanted to go home. It wanted to go to the gate. It wanted to go to the boy with the sigil on his ribs, or perhaps to the man hunting the boy, or perhaps simply anywhere its absence was noticed, and Nim clutched it through the cloth and whispered to it like to a spooked animal:

*No. We are not going where you want. We are going where they will never think to look, and you are going to behave.*

The shard pulsed twice, sulky. Then, unbelievably, it dimmed.

Nim stopped dead on the trail, breath ragged, pine needles slick under her boots. It had never dimmed before. It had never *listened* before. She unwrapped a corner of the linen and looked at it in the moonlight—a splinter of night-colored glass, veined with silver, no bigger than her littlest finger—and it sat in her palm like a thing considering her.

"You're a piece of a door," she told it. "Doors don't want anything."

It warmed gently, once, the way a cat presses its head into a hand. And Nim, daughter of the god-king, heir to an army of the grateful, sat down hard on a cold root in the dark and laughed until the laugh turned into something else entirely, and let herself cry for exactly as long as it took the moon to clear the ridge—no longer, because crying was loud, and loudness killed—and then she wiped her face on her sleeve, rewrapped the shard, and kept climbing.

* * *

Three days later, in the market town of Ferrow's Bend, she learned how fast the world could close around a name.

She had been careful. She had bought bread with coins from a purse she'd filled years ago, saved against a morning like this one. She had kept her hair bound and her head down. She had not spoken to anyone beyond what trade required. But at the well on the second morning, an old woman selling onions looked at her too long, and then looked at the notice nailed to the post beside the well, and then looked at her again, and Nim understood with a cold drop through her stomach that her father's face had been reproduced in charcoal on a hundred posts between here and the coast—her face, not his, because the god-king did not need posters. The god-king sent priests. It was only the daughter who could be found by onion-sellers.

The notice read: *THE TEMPLE SEEKS A LOST DAUGHTER. SHE IS NOT WELL. SHE WILL BE RECEIVED WITH LOVE AND RESTORED TO HER FAMILY. REWARD: ONE ANSWERED PRAYER.*

One answered prayer. Her father had learned, in his first weeks risen, that miracles were cheaper than coin and more persuasive than threats. The baker whose ovens had failed got fire that never went cold. The widow's blighted orchard had bent double with fruit overnight. Every person in Ferrow's Bend who had ever begged the silent sky for anything was looking at that notice and doing arithmetic, and Nim was the sum.

She left town within the hour, off the road, cutting cross-country through gorse and sheep pasture, and the shard against her chest sang louder with every mile—not toward the temple, she realized. Toward the mountains. Toward the erased kingdom. The key wanted its gate even if it must pass through her disobedient hands to get there.

"Fine," she muttered, thigh-deep in gorse. "We'll go near it. Near is not through. Near is a place I can stand and decide."

Because that was the thought she had been circling for three days like a moth around a lamp she refused to enter: she could not simply hide the key forever. Hiding delayed her father; it did not defeat him. He had waited a hundred years in stone. He would wait a hundred more. Someday she would slip, or die, or grow tired, and the shard would pass to other hands, and the door would open, and the boy in the valley would bleed into it whether he ever consented or not. Unless—

Unless the door was destroyed. Unless the key was destroyed. Unless someone reached the gate first and ended the question permanently, and the only people who knew where the gate was were the ones hunting her, and the one being hunted.

Which meant, and the thought arrived with all the grace of a dropped roof beam, that she needed to find Juniper Villalobos before her father's priests did. Not to deliver him. To warn him. Possibly to help him. Possibly to stand beside a stranger she'd never met and fight the thing that had raised her, because refusing to be the instrument of his return was not the same as stopping him, and she was done confusing the two.

She would rather be hunted than used. She had said it to herself so often it had worn smooth as river glass. But there was a third thing, past hunted, past used, that she had only now turned over enough to see: useful *against* him. A daughter's betrayal as the sharpest tool in the world, because no one guards against it. Because he certainly didn't. Because he believed, with the whole serene weight of his divinity, that she was his making and would bend back to him like green wood to flame.

Let him believe it. Belief was a kind of blindness, and blindness was a door too.

* * *

On the fifth night, in the ruins of a shepherd's cot with rain needling through the collapsed roof, the dream came.

She knew it was his the moment it began, because everything went too beautiful. Real rain was gray and mean; dream-rain fell in threads of gold, and the ruined cot rebuilt itself around her beam by beam, and the hearth lit with a fire that gave light but no warmth, and her father sat beside her on a bench that had rotted to soil in the waking world. He looked as he had on the morning of his rising: magnificent, unhurried, stone-dust still ghosting his shoulders like the residue of a blessing.

"Nimara." He always used the full name, the temple name, the one that meant *beloved of the threshold.* He had named her for doors. She understood that now, and had not, at four, when it had seemed like a pretty word. "You've made things difficult for yourself."

"I've made them difficult for *you.*"

His smile was patient as erosion. "The key will come home. Keys do. You could carry it home yourself, and be received with honor, and sit beside me when the door opens and the old world pours back in—all the drowned countries, all the silenced gods, everything your grandmother's generation starved us of. You would never kneel to anyone again. No one would ever look through you again." His gold-rain eyes studied her. "I know how they look through you. I have watched it your whole life. I am the only one who has ever seen all of you."

That was the cruelty of him: everything he said was true, or nearly true, or true-shaped, and the nearly was where the poison lived. She had spent her childhood invisible in a temple full of people staring at her. He *had* seen her. That was precisely why she had run—because being seen by him felt less like warmth than like being held under glass.

"The boy," she said. "The carver. When you take his blood, does he live?"

Her father's smile did not move, and that non-movement was the answer.

"I'm not bringing you the key," Nim said. "I'm not bringing you the boy. I'm not bringing you anything. Find another door."

"There is no other door." He leaned close, and the dream-fire guttered. "And there is no other you. Think on that, beloved of the threshold. Doors swing both ways. Come home before I am forced to stop asking gently."

The cot dissolved. She woke with rain actually falling on her face and the shard burning so hot through the linen that it had scorched a small brown sunburst into the cloth, and her palm—her right palm—bore a mark like a brand in the shape of a key's tooth, thin and white and precise.

He had marked her. So that any priest with eyes could find her. So that she could run, but never anonymously, never invisibly, never as no one.

Nim sat up in the ruin, dripping, furious, and did the only thing left to do with rage that had nowhere to go: she aimed it. She dug through her pack by feel, found her knife and the stub of charcoal she used for marking trails, and on the flat underside of a fallen roof-beam, where rain could not reach, she drew what she knew of the road ahead—every town, every ford, every stretch of high moor between here and the border ranges, and at the center of it all, circled twice, the valley where a boy carved the names of the dead into bone.

She had never met Juniper Villalobos. She already owed him an apology, a warning, and possibly her life. She intended to deliver all three, in whatever order survival allowed.

Nim blew out a breath, watched it shiver in the cold, and pulled her hood up.

Five days behind her, she guessed, rode the first of the bone-priests. Ahead lay the mountains, and somewhere in them a country that had erased itself to stay free, and a boy who did not yet know he was the last of it. The shard pulsed against her heart, warm and stubborn, keeping its own counsel.

"All right," she told it, shouldering her pack in the dark. "Near is not through. But near is where I'm going."

And she stepped out of the ruin into the rain, a marked girl walking toward an unmapped country, hunted by everything she loved.
