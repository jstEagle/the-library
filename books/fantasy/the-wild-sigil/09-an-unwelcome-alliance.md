## 9. An Unwelcome Alliance

The cartographer came back on the third morning, and this time she did not knock.

Juniper found her standing in the doorway of the carving shed, her travel cloak still dusted with valley road, studying his workbench as though it were a specimen. Her fingers hovered over a half-finished charm—a knucklebone rattle for old Marta's rheumatism, its grooves not yet smoothed—and Juniper watched her want to touch it and watched her stop herself, which was more courtesy than he'd expected from a woman who had spent two days asking every soul in Hollowmere about a kingdom that did not exist.

"You're up early," he said.

"Maps keep early hours." Sera Quill turned, and the light through the shed's one window caught the scar that ran from her left ear down into her collar—the one she didn't bother hiding and never explained. "So do I, when there's something worth charting."

"There's nothing here worth charting."

"That," she said, "is the most interesting thing anyone has said to me in six years."

She set her satchel on his bench without asking, which he was learning was simply how she moved through the world, and drew out a roll of oilcloth. She unrolled it with the reverence other people reserved for scripture. It was a map—but unlike any Juniper had seen. The paper was old enough to have gone amber at the edges, and the ink had been worked over so many times, by so many hands, that the rivers seemed to shimmer. And there, pressed into the western mountains where every modern chart showed nothing but blank parchment and the words *terra incognita*—here there were mountains drawn in fine, confident lines, and valleys threaded with roads, and at the heart of it all a single word in a script Juniper did not know but somehow, horribly, almost recognized:

*Ossuary Vale.*

"I stole it," Sera said, matter-of-factly, watching his face. "From the Royal Athenaeum in Corvenne, the night before they would have burned me for treason anyway. Forty-one charts of extinct nations, and this is the only one that matters." She tapped the name. "Every cartographer who ever tried to find it died looking or gave up lying. The kingdom isn't lost, Villalobos. Lost things leave traces. This place was *erased*. Deliberately. And erasure is a kind of evidence."

Juniper looked at the map and felt the dead stir in his hands—not the valley dead, who had gone quiet and frightened since the miracles started, but something older, deeper, a pressure in his fingerbones like weather coming. He curled his fists closed.

"You asked around the village," he said. "About the Vale."

"I did."

"And what did they tell you?"

"That the Villalobos family has kept bees and carved bone for nine generations, and that you're the last of them, and that you're strange but decent, and that no one named Villalobos ever came from anywhere else." She smiled thinly. "Which is exactly what a people trying to forget would say."

"My grandmother came from over the eastern pass. There are records."

"Records can be written. Bones don't lie." Her eyes went to his hands, and Juniper felt the look like a hand on his skin. "The innkeep's daughter told me you made a charm for her mother's grave. That when she held it, she said it was warm. That she dreamed of her mother that night, and in the dream her mother told her where to find the will her father hid from the second family." Sera tilted her head. "Warm bone, Villalobos. I've handled relics from forty-one dead kingdoms. Bone only stays warm for one reason."

"And what reason is that?"

"Someone's still home."

* * *

He refused her, of course. He refused her twice that day—once in the shed, politely, and once outside the Widow Crane's fence, less politely, when she laid out the offer plainly: passage out of the valley, safe conduct across three borders, guides who knew the unmapped country, everything she had traded her honor for and more besides—in exchange for him leading her to Ossuary Vale, whose location he swore he did not know and which she swore she did not believe.

"I'm a carver," he told her. "I make charms for grieving people. I mend fences. I have bees."

"You have a sigil under your shirt that glows, if the stories out of the eastern villages are true. You have bone-priests crossing your valley asking after a hearer. And you have—" she gestured at the whole green sweep of the valley, the barley, the beehives, the smoke rising gentle from thirty chimneys, "—all of this, which is going to burn, because you're standing in the middle of it pretending to be small."

"I am small."

"No one is small to a god-king."

He walked away from her then, and hated that his hands were shaking, and hated more that she was not entirely wrong.

* * *

That evening he sat with the valley dead, or tried to. It was his habit, on the nights when the murmur through his palms grew insistent, to walk the little graveyard behind the meetinghouse and lay his bare hands on the cold ground, letting the dead say what they needed said. Most nights it was small things. A name spoken wrong on a stone. A debt unpaid. An apology owed to a sister across the valley who had died before the quarrel healed. Juniper carried these messages the way he carried everything—quietly, without witnesses, invisible as he had trained himself to be since childhood, since the first time he'd taken a dead man's femur in his hands and heard the man complain about his son-in-law.

But tonight the ground said nothing.

He knelt in the wet grass until his knees ached, palms flat against the earth, and got silence. Not the comfortable silence of contented dead—the dead were never fully silent, any more than bees were; there was always a hum, always a mutter, always the low tide-sound of voices just below hearing. This was the silence of a room where everyone has stopped talking because someone frightening has entered it.

Even the newest grave—little Tam Broadhurst, four days in the ground—said nothing, and Tam had not stopped chattering since his funeral.

Juniper stood, brushed the grass from his trousers, and told himself this too would pass. Things passed. The valley had outlasted a century of silence from the gods; it could outlast a few weeks of silence from the dead.

He was halfway home when he smelled the smoke.

* * *

It was the shed. Of course it was the shed—it was always the shed, the workshop, the place where the thing you loved lived, because whoever burned chose what you loved with a cruelty that was itself a kind of signature.

He came over the rise behind the house and saw the light of it before he saw the flames, an orange bloom against the dark, and for one stupid heartbeat he thought: *the forge*, someone's working late. Then the heat hit his face and the smell hit his throat—pitch and pine and scorched bone—and he was running, and the world narrowed to the door of the shed, which was already a wall of fire.

Three figures stood in the yard, watching. They wore pilgrim gray, hooded, masked with smooth plates of polished bone that reflected the firelight in long yellow streaks. They did not move as he ran past them. One of them inclined its head, almost courteously, the way a messenger nods after delivering a letter.

Inside, the heat drove him back twice before he got the door wrenched open. The bench was gone—already a black skeleton of itself, the vise glowing dull red. The racks of finished charms, three months of work, commissions promised to seven families: curling, blackening, weeping resin. His grandmother's tools—the kit she'd brought over the pass, iron and horn-handled, older than anything else he owned—were in their drawer, and he got the drawer open, and the handles were hot enough to bite, and he took them anyway, wrapped in his own shirt, and ran.

He made it ten steps before his legs quit and he knelt in the yard with the tools clutched to his chest, coughing, while the shed that had been his grandfather's and his grandfather's before that went up in a column of sparks that leaned east on the wind, toward the mountains. Toward, he thought distantly, wherever Ossuary Vale was hidden.

The bone-masked figures were gone. In their place, pinned to the gatepost with a sliver of white bone, was a square of gray cloth, and stitched on it in thread the color of dried blood was a single mark.

He knew the shape. He had touched it on his own ribs in the dark, more than once, tracing the raised lines of the birthmark he'd carried all his life and never understood. It was the same sigil, exact down to the smallest stroke, and seeing it there on the burning gatepost was like hearing his own name shouted by a stranger in a crowd.

Behind him, boots on gravel. Sera Quill, wrapped in her cloak, hair loose, a fire-iron from the inn still in her fist—she had come running too, then. She stopped beside him and looked at the sigil, and he watched her understand it in real time, watched the pieces settle behind her eyes.

"They know your name," she said quietly.

"They knew it before tonight."

"Then you have two choices, Villalobos. You can stay here and wait for them to come back for the rest of it—the house, the hives, the neighbors who lie for you. Or you can come with me, and we find out what you actually are before they decide it for you." She crouched, took the charred bone-splinter from the gatepost, turned it over in her fingers. "You don't have to lead me to the Vale. Not yet. Just get out of this valley alive. That's the offer. Everything else, we negotiate on the road."

Juniper looked at the fire. The roof beams fell inward with a sound like a sigh, and a swirl of sparks rose straight up into the windless dark, hanging a moment among the stars as if uncertain whether to fall or fly.

His hands ached. Not from the burns—from the silence. Somewhere out beyond the firelight, the valley dead were holding their breath, and he understood suddenly, completely, that they had not abandoned him. They had gone quiet to hide him. Every voice he had ever carried had swallowed itself, and it hadn't been enough, and it would never be enough, because the thing the priests wanted was not something he could put down. It was under his skin. It was his blood.

Staying invisible had been a craft, like carving, and he had been good at it. But a craft requires material, and the material of his invisibility had just gone up in smoke.

"The road east," he said hoarsely. "Past the ford. You said your guides know the unmapped country."

Sera rose beside him, firelight moving on her face. "They do."

"Then I'll want my grandmother's tools sharpened before we go. And I'm bringing the bees' honey—Marta Crane will see it doesn't go to waste." He stood, and his legs held, and something in him settled into a shape he did not recognize yet but suspected he would carry a long time. "And Quill—if you're taking me to a kingdom that erased itself on purpose, you should know I intend to ask it why. Whatever answer it gives, I carve it into something, and you put it on your map. All of it. No omissions."

For the first time since she'd walked into his valley, Sera Quill looked genuinely surprised. Then she smiled, slow and sharp, and offered her hand.

"No omissions," she agreed. "Deal."

Above the ruined shed, the sparks had finally chosen, and were drifting east.
