## 10. Leaving the Valley

They left before dawn, while the valley still wore its mist like a shroud, because Juniper could not bear to watch it wake.

Sera had the pack on her back and the stolen atlas wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and she moved through the dark with the certainty of a woman who had already memorized every step of a road she had never walked. Nim trailed behind them both, hood up, hands buried in her sleeves, glancing over her shoulder at intervals so regular they might have been prayer. Juniper came last. He told himself it was caution—rearguard was sensible, rearguard was practical—but the truth sat in his chest like a stone: he wanted to look as long as he could.

The valley did not look like a place worth dying for. He knew that. From the rim, from any of the high passes, it was just another fold in the hills—a ribbon of barley and pasture stitched along a slow brown river, roofed in slate and thatch, hedged in hawthorn. No castle. No cathedral. The bell tower of the meetinghouse leaned two degrees east of true and no one had ever found the money or the will to straighten it. It had never appeared on a map. Sera had told him that the first night, almost apologetically, the way you tell someone their house is built on an old grave. *Your valley isn't anywhere,* she'd said. *I've looked. I've looked everywhere.* He had laughed then. It had seemed a fine thing, to be nowhere.

Now men wearing bone masks had crossed his river asking after him, and Old Marta lay in her bed with her jaw wired shut and her eyes open too long, and being nowhere had stopped meaning safe.

He paused at the last stile before the sheep track began to climb. Below him, half-lost in the mist, he could see the smoke of first fires rising from the chimneys—Tamsin's bakery, the Hollis farm, the little crooked house where he had been born and where his grandmother had taught him to hold a knife against bone the way you hold a hand against a frightened animal. Someone would find the ashes of his carving shed today. Someone would stand in the wet black ruin of it and shake their heads and say his name, and the name would travel down the valley faster than the river ran, and by evening every soul there would know that Juniper Villalobos had run rather than be found.

He hoped they would lie about which way he'd gone.

"Juniper." Sera's voice, low. Not impatient—that was the thing he was learning about her; beneath the ambition she carried like a blade under her coat, she had patience the way other people had scars. "Light's coming. We want the ridge crossed before full day."

"I know."

"You keep saying that."

"And I keep meaning it."

Nim made a small sound that might have been a laugh, quickly swallowed. She hadn't spoken much since the shed burned. She watched him sometimes with an expression he couldn't read—pity, maybe, or recognition. She had left something behind too. Everyone walking this road had left something burning.

He took one last breath of valley air—woodsmoke, barley dust, the mineral cold of the river—and turned uphill.

* * *

The dead came with him.

Of course they did. They were not bound to the valley; they were bound to him. He felt them the way you feel your own pulse, a murmuration at the edge of thought, dozens of voices braided into a single low hum that he had known so long he had forgotten it was a sound at all. Grandmother Ilse was there, closest, warmest, her presence like a hand resting between his shoulder blades. The drowned child Tam—the one whose memorial charm he had carved in what felt like another life, ten days ago, a hundred years—kept pace beside him without weight or footsteps, humming a skipping rhyme in a voice like water over stones. The older ones drifted further out: farmers dead four generations, a soldier who had crawled home to die in 1189, a girl who had never been born at all but whom her mother had loved so fiercely and grieved so long that she had acquired a kind of ghosthood anyway, all potential and no memory.

They murmured comfort as they climbed. That was mostly what they gave him now. *Steady, lad. Watch the loose stone. Your grandmother says mind the loose stone.* The soldier hummed a marching song from a country that no longer existed, and the unborn girl hummed harmony, badly, and Tam giggled at her, and the whole invisible company of them sounded, to Juniper, like home.

The path steepened. Hawthorn gave way to bracken, bracken to grey scree, and the mist fell away below them like a sheet being drawn off a body. Behind, the valley revealed itself whole and small and golden in the early light, and ahead—

Ahead the world simply ended.

Not literally. Sera had explained it three times with maps sketched in dirt, with growing frustration at his inability to grasp what she considered elementary. But looking at it, Juniper understood none of her cartography and all of its terror. The valley rim was the edge of everything he knew, and beyond it the land rolled away in folds and swells toward mountains he had seen only as blue smudges on clear days, toward distances that had no names in any tongue he spoke. There was no road marked. There was no road at all, at first—just sheep tracks fading into grass, grass into stone, stone into the kind of country that existed before anyone had bothered giving it edges.

"The border," Sera said quietly, coming to stand beside him. She said it the way pilgrims say holy words. "The old border. What used to be Ossuary Vale's frontier." Her fingers twitched against her thigh; he had noticed she did that when she wanted to draw. "No modern chart acknowledges it. But the land remembers borders even when maps don't. Borders are wounds. They scar."

"And we cross it," Nim said. Not a question. She stood slightly apart from them, arms crossed, looking at the far mountains with an expression Juniper now recognized: the look of someone walking toward the thing that hunted them.

"We cross it," Sera agreed.

Juniper nodded. His mouth was dry. He told himself it was the climb.

* * *

It happened at the cairn.

There was an old boundary cairn at the top of the pass—heaped stones, lichen-eaten, older than any living thing in the valley, older probably than any living thing anywhere near. Sera went to it immediately, reverent, brushing moss from its flank with her fingertips, hunting for marks. Nim hung back, watching the road behind them. And Juniper stepped over the line.

He didn't see the line. There was nothing to see—no wall, no post, no painted stroke. But he felt it, the way you feel a doorframe across your shoulders when you're tall for a room.

And the dead stopped.

Not faded. Not thinned. *Stopped.* Mid-syllable. Grandmother Ilse's voice—which had been saying something, he would realize later, some last small ordinary thing, *mind the scree, love, wear your scarf*—cut off as cleanly as if a hand had closed around a throat. Tam's humming ended. The soldier's marching song ended. The unborn girl's bad harmony ended. One instant they filled the world the way light fills a room, and the next instant there was nothing, and the nothing had a shape, and the shape was silence.

Juniper stood very still on the far side of the cairn.

"Wait," he said. His voice came out wrong. Thin. "Wait."

He stepped back. One stride, two, back over the invisible line, back onto valley stone—

They rushed in like water into a broken hull. All of them at once, all their voices, relief and alarm tumbling over each other, *lad, lad, what happened, where did you go, we couldn't feel you, we couldn't hear—* and underneath it Grandmother Ilse, steady as bedrock: *Hush now. Hush. He knows.*

He stepped forward again. Over the line.

Silence.

Complete. Total. Not the silence of a room when people stop talking—rooms are never truly silent, rooms breathe and settle and remember. This was the silence of a grave. Of the space between stars. It pressed against his eardrums. It had texture. It had weight. He realized he was hearing his own heartbeat for perhaps the first time in thirty years, and it sounded enormous and obscene, a drum banging in an empty house.

"Juniper." Sera had turned. She was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse—concern layered over something else, something sharper. The cartographer seeing a phenomenon. "What is it?"

"They can't follow." His own voice sounded foreign in the dead air. "The—the voices. They stop here." He touched the cairn. Lichen, cold stone, nothing more. "This is the border. This is what you meant. The kingdom erased itself and the magic—" His voice failed. He tried again. "Whatever my gift is, whatever I am—it belongs to the valley. It can't leave."

"That's not possible," Sera started, in her map-logic voice, *borders don't work like that, magic doesn't respect lines on stone* — and then she saw his face and stopped.

Nim said nothing. But she looked at the cairn a long time, and her face did something complicated, and later he would understand that she recognized this too—that gods also had territories, that daughters also got left behind at boundaries—but that morning she only stepped closer to him, wordless, and stood at his shoulder like a wall between him and the view.

He stepped back into the valley one more time. Just once more.

They flooded in again—all of them, and now they knew, and they were grieving, he could feel it, the whole murmuration of them keening softly like wind through a keyhole. Grandmother Ilse came closest. *Oh, my love. Oh, my darling boy. We wondered when you'd find the edge of us.* Her voice was warm and breaking. *Listen to me. Listen. It isn't dying. Do you hear me? You'll carry us anyway. In here.* He felt her press, immaterially, against the place where his heart beat. *You learned our faces before you ever heard our voices. You'll remember. Remembering is yours. It doesn't belong to any valley.*

"How do I do this," he whispered. Out loud, in front of the living, and he was past caring. "How do I walk out of my own—"

*The way we did,* she said. *One foot. Then the next. That's all anything ever was, sweetheart. That's the whole secret. Nobody ever tells you that's the whole secret.*

Tam pressed close, water-voiced, singing his skipping rhyme one last time, and the unborn girl harmonized, badly, beautifully, and the old soldier brought up the rear with his marching song from a vanished country, and Juniper stood inside the sound of them for one final moment, letting it fill him the way rain fills a dry field, memorizing the pitch of each voice, the weave of the braid, the exact warmth of his grandmother's presence between his shoulder blades.

Then he kissed his own fingertips and laid them on the cairn, a carver's farewell, a craftsman's blessing on finished work.

And he walked out of the valley.

* * *

The grief hit him halfway down the far slope.

That was the cruel geometry of it—crossing over had been the easy part, all adrenaline and horror, and then the path descended gently through bracken and birch into a green foreign country under a mild morning sky, birdsong, sunlight, beauty, and Juniper sat down on a rock in the middle of it all and broke.

It wasn't crying, exactly. Or it was crying, but crying was only the smallest part. The rest was amputation. Thirty years of constant companionship, torn out at the root—not silenced, *gone*, and gone in a way his body kept refusing to believe. He kept reaching for them. Every few minutes, involuntarily, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth: he reached, and found nothing, and flinched, and reached again. When a bird called overhead he turned his head before he could stop himself, half-expecting the dead to comment on it. When he stumbled on a root, he waited for his grandmother's voice—*watch the path, love*—and the waiting itself was agony, a held breath that never ended.

He had loved them more easily than the living. He had known that about himself for years, had worn it like a private shame. The living were complicated; they wanted things; they lied and forgave and needed and disappointed. The dead only wanted to be remembered, and remembering was the one thing Juniper had always been rich in. With the dead he had never once been lonely.

Now he was the loneliest man alive, sitting in sunlight in a green country, and his companions were watching him come apart and could do nothing, because there was nothing to do. Grief without a corpse. Mourning without a grave. He couldn't bury them. He couldn't visit them. They weren't anywhere except behind him, on the other side of a heap of mossy stones, and they hadn't died—they were exactly as they had always been, just beyond reach forever, and somehow that was worse than death. Death, at least, ends. This would go on. Every hour of every day for the rest of his life, his hands would rise to carve and find themselves listening out of habit, and hear nothing, and grieve again, freshly, each time.

Sera sat down beside him on the rock. She didn't touch him—she wasn't a touching woman—but she sat close, shoulder nearly to shoulder, and let the silence be what it was instead of trying to fix it. After a long time she said, quietly:

"My father died when I was nine. I still set two cups out, some mornings. Ten years, and I still do it." A pause. "It doesn't get smaller. You just get bigger around it."

"That's not comforting," Juniper managed.

"No," she agreed. "But it's true, and I've noticed you prefer true things even when they're terrible."

He laughed—a cracked, awful sound, but a laugh. Nim, perched on a stone nearby with her knees drawn up, said, "My mother sang to the door every night before it was sealed. I used to think it was madness." She picked at the bark of a stick. "Now I think she was saying goodbye for forty years in advance. Maybe that's wisdom. Saying goodbye slowly."

"I didn't say goodbye," Juniper said. "I kissed a rock."

"It was a very respectful rock," Nim said gravely, and Sera snorted, and the terrible pressure in Juniper's chest loosened by one degree, one small degree, enough to breathe around.

They walked on. The country unrolled ahead of them, nameless and green, and Juniper learned the new arithmetic of his life. Every sound was now merely a sound. Birds were birds. Wind was wind. Nothing meant anything beyond itself, and the freedom of that was real—he could feel it, faint and strange, like sun through fog. No chorus demanding his attention. No centuries of unfinished business pressing on his skull. For the first time since childhood his head held only his own thoughts, and his own thoughts were quiet, and simple, and unbearably alone.

By late afternoon they crested a second ridge and saw, in a fold of the hills below, a road—an actual road, rutted and real, running toward a bridge over a brown river, and beyond the bridge, smoke rising from chimney pots. People. A town. The first town of the rest of his life.

Sera unfolded a corner of the oilcloth and checked her atlas by the failing light, and Juniper saw her frown.

"What?"

"This road," she said slowly. "According to every source I have, there should be a river crossing here. There is. Fine. But the town on the far bank—" She turned the page. Turned it back. Turned it again. "The town on the far bank shouldn't exist. It's not on any chart I've ever copied. And the road leading to it—" her finger traced a line that visibly, impossibly, did not match the ground, "—this road is going somewhere that was erased."

Nim had gone very still. "Then it's going somewhere important," she said.

Below them, evening settling blue over the water, the unmapped town lit its lamps one by one—and Juniper, reaching out of thirty years of habit toward the dead of that place and finding, for the first time, not silence but something else, something faint and listening and aware of him—felt the hair rise on his arms.

Something on the far side of the bridge was waiting to ask him his name.
