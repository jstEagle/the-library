## 12. The Toll of Names

The bridge appeared at dusk on the third day out of the valley, and it had no business existing.

Sera said so twice, once in the flat voice she used for facts and once louder, as if volume could argue geography into behaving. She stood at the lip of the ravine with her stolen atlas open across both forearms, turning pages with a thumb gone white at the knuckle, and the ravine stared back at her, two hundred feet of shadowed granite dropping to a river that ran the color of old pewter.

"It isn't here," she said. "This crossing was destroyed in the Border Wars. I've seen the survey. Burned pilings, a ferry concession, nothing else. And now—" She gestured at the thing spanning the gorge as though it were an insult personally delivered.

"Now there's a bridge," Nim said. She sat cross-legged on a flat stone, turning an apple over in her hands without eating it. "There's been a bridge every night since we left the wheat field. Haven't you noticed? Roads too. The land keeps rearranging itself like furniture while we sleep."

"I noticed," Sera said, with the tone of someone who had noticed and resented it deeply.

Juniper said nothing. He was listening—or trying to—and what he heard made the skin crawl along his arms. In the valley, the dead had been a murmur under everything, a low tide of voices he'd learned to sort like a farmer sorting grain. Out here the murmur was thinner, stranger, full of accents and griefs he didn't know, and beneath the bridge the river carried its own drowned congregation, hundreds of them, maybe thousands, all of them singing the same three notes over and over in a language his hands understood but his ears did not.

*It wants names,* they sang. *It wants names.*

"There's a toll-keeper," Juniper said.

Sera looked up sharply. "You can see one?"

He could. A small figure stood at the center of the span, where the last light still touched the stones—a hunched shape wrapped in gray, holding something long and thin that might have been a staff or a scale-beam. But that wasn't how he knew. He knew because the dead below were afraid of it, and the dead were almost never afraid.

"Someone's standing on it," he said. "Middle of the bridge."

Nim set down the apple. "Then we camp here and go around."

"There is no around." Sera snapped the atlas shut. "I told you—the ravine runs forty miles to the south before the walls come close enough to climb, and north it feeds into the Karst, which is a hole in the world with weather. This is the crossing. It has always been the crossing. That's why it bothers me that it keeps appearing and disappearing. Bridges don't do that. Roads don't do that. Unless—"

"Unless the roads are remembering," Nim finished quietly.

"Unless someone is unremembering them." Sera rubbed the bridge of her nose. "Every map I own disagrees with itself about this region. I assumed error. Cartographers assume error the way priests assume sin—it's the water we swim in. But errors don't repeat. Errors don't *avoid*. Something has been editing this country for a hundred years, and it edits around this bridge like a tongue finding the gap where a tooth used to be."

Juniper watched the toll-keeper through the failing light. It hadn't moved. He doubted it breathed.

"We cross at dawn," he said. "Whatever it is, I'd rather meet it with the sun behind us than ahead."

Neither of them argued. They made cold camp above the ravine, and Juniper lay awake most of the night listening to the river-dead sing their three notes, and toward morning he realized what they were singing, or at least what it meant: *we paid, we paid, we paid.* Every soul in that pewter water had crossed the bridge once and never come back off it. The toll-keeper had not let them leave.

He didn't mention this to the others. There was nothing to be gained by it, and besides—he was beginning to understand—there are truths you carry alone the way you carry an injury, because speaking them makes them heavier, not lighter.

* * *

Dawn came up gray-gold, mist rising off the river like breath from a sleeping animal. The bridge resolved in the light into something worse than shadow had suggested: ancient black stone, older than any masonry Juniper knew, each block fitted so precisely there was no mortar and no gap, carved along its rails with rows of figures that might have been people bowing, or people drowning, depending on how long you looked.

The toll-keeper stood at the center. Closer now, it resolved into an old woman—human-shaped, human-sized—wrapped in layered gray wool gone the color of river fog. Her face was deeply lined and utterly calm, the calm of deep water. In her hands she held a bronze pan-balance, empty on both sides.

"Three travelers," she said when they stopped before her. Her voice was dry and unhurried, like paper being folded. "Good morning. The toll is a true name."

Sera stepped forward with the easy authority she wore like armor. "We're pilgrims heading to the shrine at—"

"Madam." The toll-keeper did not raise her voice. She simply continued, the way a river continues around a stone. "The toll is a true name. Not a name you answer to. Not a name your mother called you in from the fields. A true name. The one that is written in you." Her eyes, pale as frost, moved across them. "You will know the difference when you speak it. So will I."

"Names aren't currency," Sera said. "They're not things you can take."

"Everything is currency somewhere." The old woman adjusted the balance with one finger; the empty pans swayed, then stilled. "You have been spending yours all your life, dear. Every time someone says your name with love, you spend a little. Every time someone curses it, more. Most people die owing themselves nothing but the sound of it. Some few"—her gaze drifted to Juniper, and stayed there, weightless and terrible—"some few are worth a great deal."

Juniper's mouth went dry. Beside him, Nim had gone very still, her hand drifting toward the shard hidden inside her coat, and he caught her wrist, barely moving, and felt her swallow her panic down.

"What happens if we don't pay?" Sera asked.

"You turn back. Or you swim." The toll-keeper nodded toward the river, and the mist parted briefly, and Juniper saw the shapes below the surface—not bodies exactly, but impressions of bodies, like stains, hundreds of them, drifting with the current in slow procession. "Most pay."

"And what do you *do* with them?" Nim asked. "The names?"

"That is not your business, child." For just a moment something moved behind the old woman's calm—not cruelty, nothing so simple. Tedium. The tedium of ten thousand years at the same post. "I keep the ledger. What is written must be kept somewhere. Names are heavy. They sink. You can hear them, sometimes, on quiet nights, if you lean over the rail."

Juniper thought of the three notes. *We paid, we paid, we paid.*

Sera squared her shoulders. He watched her decide—watched the calculation move behind her eyes, quick and cold and practiced, the same look he'd glimpsed when she talked about the atlas, about what maps cost. Then she smiled her best smile, the one that opened doors in cities.

"Mara Voss," she said clearly. "Of Callow's Ferry. Daughter of Edmun Voss, shipwright."

The balance did not move. The toll-keeper regarded her with an expression of vast, gentle disappointment, the kind a grandmother wears watching a child lie about stolen sweets.

"Madam," she said. "That name belongs to a girl who drowned in the Vell at age nine. I have her here." One gray hand rested lightly on the bridge rail, over the pewter water. "She is quite content. Do not insult us both."

Color rose in Sera's cheeks. Juniper watched her try again—another name, another city, another dead girl's identity stripped and worn—and watched the balance sit unmoved through all of it, until finally Sera ran out of lies, or courage, and stood there breathing hard with her fists clenched at her sides.

"You can't have mine," she said at last, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "Do you understand? I don't have much left. I sold pieces of myself years ago, more than you'd believe, and my name is the last thing that's actually *mine*. Everything else I traded. Not that."

"No one takes," the toll-keeper said. "You give. That is the entire point of a toll. If I took, I would be a thief, and thieves are struck from the ledger." She turned her pale eyes to Nim.

Nim lifted her chin. Whatever name she considered, Juniper saw her weigh it and find it too dangerous even to counterfeit—she was the god-king's daughter; any name attached to her was a beacon. "Pass," she said. "I'll swim."

"The river keeps those who refuse, child. It does not keep those who cannot pay. Look at me." The old woman leaned forward, and for a moment her face seemed younger, or older, or both. "You are already marked by something far worse than me. Cross. The bridge knows debt when it sees it. Your father's claim on you outweighs anything in my ledger—you are collateral, not currency. Go."

Nim went, fast, not looking back, her boots ringing on the black stone, and she did not stop until she reached the far side, where she turned and waited with her arms wrapped around herself.

Which left Juniper.

The toll-keeper looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something he would remember for the rest of his life: she bowed. Shallowly, formally, the way one greets a superior whose rank one resents.

"Heir," she said.

"Don't call me that."

"You mistake me. I am not calling you. I am *noting* you." She straightened. "Your family's accounts were closed a century ago, closed deliberately, sealed with a rite I have never seen performed before or since. Every Villalobos came off my ledger in a single night. Do you know what that means, boy? It means for a hundred years, your line has owed nothing—to anyone. To death, to memory, to the gods, to me. Free and clear. The only free-and-clear souls in the world." Her frost-pale eyes narrowed slightly. "Until you were born."

"I'm a carver," Juniper said. "I make memorial charms. My mother sold eggs. There's no account."

"Then say your name and prove it."

He should have lied. Sera was signaling frantically behind him, mouthing some invented name, and he could have done it—could have spent an hour feeding the old woman false identities until he found one that stuck, the way con men find locks that yield. He was good at being no one. He had built his whole life on it, layer by careful layer, the deliberate invisibility of a man who hears the dead and doesn't want the living asking why.

But the dead below the bridge were listening. He could feel them, hundreds of sunken names pressed against the underside of the stone like faces against ice, and they had gone silent now, all of them, waiting. And somewhere deeper, fainter, the valley dead he'd carried with him in memory—old Marta with her arthritic hands, the drowned child whose charm he'd carved in the first chapter of all this—they were listening too. He could not lie in front of the dead. It was the one rule his gift had ever taught him. You can lie to the living all day; they want it half the time. But the dead had given him everything—his trade, his purpose, the only love he'd ever found uncomplicated—and they deserved the truth the way a patient deserves the knife straight.

Besides. Some tired, honest corner of him was done running from his own name.

"Juniper Villalobos," he said.

The word left his mouth and the world changed.

It was subtle at first—a pressure in the air, a hum in the black stone under his boots. Then the carvings along the rails began to move. All those bowing, drowning figures stirred, turning their heads in unison to look at him, and the balance in the toll-keeper's hands swung hard to one side, the empty pan dropping, the other rising, though nothing had been placed upon either.

The toll-keeper reached into her gray wrappings and drew out a stylus of bone, and before Juniper could step back she caught his right hand, turned it palm-up, and wrote.

It didn't cut. That was the horror of it—it didn't cut at all. The stylus moved over his skin and the mark sank *inward*, a thin silver line curling into his palm like smoke sinking into water, and it burned the way frost burns, cold instead of hot, and Juniper heard himself cry out and heard his own voice come back to him doubled, trebled, as if the whole ravine were repeating it.

"There," the toll-keeper said, releasing him. "Paid in full. The name Juniper Villalobos, entered in the ledger of crossings, marked and witnessed. Wherever you are claimed from now on—by kings, by gods, by men with contracts and chains—this mark proves you gave yourself first. Freely. No one may take what has already been surrendered." Almost gently, she added: "It also means you can never be anonymous again. You understand that? A named man casts a shadow in every world, including the ones that don't have suns."

Juniper stared at his palm. The sigil gleamed there, fine as a spider's thread, and it pulsed—once, twice—in rhythm with his heartbeat. Beneath it, faint but unmistakable, he felt the older mark on his ribs stir and press against the new one, two signatures recognizing each other across the distance of his own body.

"What did it cost?" he managed. "A name. What's it worth?"

The toll-keeper was already folding back into her gray, becoming less, the way dusk becomes night. "Depends who comes collecting," she said. "For most, nothing. A mother calls it across a market square and it costs you nothing at all." Her eyes glinted. "But you are not most, heir of the erased kingdom. Somewhere east of here, a man is waking up who would trade a province for your name. And now it is *written*. Written things can be read. Read things can be summoned." She smiled then, and it was the kindest expression he'd seen on her and the worst. "Cross quickly, Juniper Villalobos. And when the bone-priests come—and they will come—I suggest you remember that you chose this. No one took it. Whatever happens next, that was yours."

He crossed. The stones rang under his feet like the inside of a bell, and the sunken names below rose as he passed—not bodies, but sounds, a slow swell of whispered syllables pressing up through the black rock, brushing against the soles of his boots like fish investigating a lure. His name among them now, someday. He understood that. Everyone who crossed joined the choir eventually, unless—

Unless they erased themselves first. Like his ancestors had. Like he might yet.

On the far bank, Sera grabbed his hand and turned it over, and her breath caught when she saw the mark. "What did you *do*?" she whispered. "You had a way out. We could have circled, we could have—"

"They already knew what I was, Sera. The bridge, the river, everything downstream of this ravine probably knows. Lying would have just made it angry." He closed his fist over the silver line. It pulsed against his fingers like a second pulse. "Now it's official. Now it's on record. Whatever claims me has to compete with the fact that I gave myself away first."

Nim stood a little apart, looking back at the bridge, where the toll-keeper had already dissolved into the morning fog. When she spoke, her voice was low and rough. "My father will feel that."

Juniper looked at her.

"The ledger," she said. "It's not just a book. Names written there ring, in certain places, in certain ears. He has an ear." She met his eyes, and he saw she was terrified and sorry and absolutely certain, all at once. "Juniper. He knows your name now. Truly knows it. By tonight he'll be able to say it, and when a god says your true name, you hear it in your bones. Wherever you are. However far you run."

The wind came up the ravine, cold off the pewter water, carrying the faint three-note song of the paid and sunk. In his palm, the new mark pulsed steadily on, patient as a lit fuse, and far to the east—too far to see, not nearly far enough to matter—something ancient turned its attention westward like a lighthouse swinging its beam.

Juniper closed his eyes and listened, one last time, for the comfort of the dead.

What he heard instead, thin and clear and getting closer, was hoofbeats.
