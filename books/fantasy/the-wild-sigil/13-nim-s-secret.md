## 13. Nim's Secret

Nim had been counting the days since she'd hidden in the cart, and the number had grown teeth.

Seven nights sleeping in ditches and hedgerows while Juniper and the mapmaker argued about roads. Seven nights of pressing her father's shard against her sternum where it burned like a swallowed coal, warm through three layers of wool, warm enough that she sometimes woke certain her ribs were glowing, certain that any priest with half an eye would see the light leaking from her seams like a lantern with a cracked shade. Seven nights of listening to Sera Quill talk about contour lines and river fords as though the world were a problem of measurement, while Juniper Villalobos sat apart from the fire, turning his hands over and over in the light as if he expected to find something written on them.

She should have told them on the first night. She knew that now. She had told herself it was caution—*they'd leave me at the next crossroads, they'd hand me back to buy their own safety*—but honesty required her to admit, in the small hours when no one could hear, that it was fear. Not of them. Of what they would see when they looked at her afterward. She had spent her whole life being looked at as the god-king's daughter, as the vessel, as the instrument, and she had run a hundred miles to stop being a thing that was *for* something, and telling them would make her a thing again. A burden. A trap walking alongside them wearing her face.

But tonight the fire was low and the wheat fields had given way to scrubland, and Sera had gone to check the snares, and Juniper sat sharpening his carving knives with the particular tenderness he brought to all his tools, and Nim understood that she could not carry it one more night. The secret had begun to weigh more than the truth ever would.

"Juniper."

He didn't look up. "If you're going to complain about my snoring again, I'll point out that Sera snores like a landslide."

"I don't snore," came Sera's voice from the dark, dry as old vellum, and then her footsteps crunched closer, because of course she'd heard—of course she'd been listening; the woman heard everything, it was how she'd survived exile.

Nim's mouth went dry. She had rehearsed this speech forty times and every version dissolved now, leaving only the plain ugly truth. So she said it plainly.

"My father isn't hunting you to kill you."

The whetstone stopped mid-stroke. In the firelight Juniper's face did something complicated—relief and suspicion arriving together and canceling each other out into a careful blankness.

"He wants you alive." The words came out of her faster now, the dam broken, everything spilling. "He needs you alive. That's why the priests keep asking after 'the hearer' instead of just burning villages until you surface. That's why they took the toll-keeper's daughter instead of the toll-keeper, why they ask questions before they use force. Dead blood is useless to him. He needs yours *living*, and—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, hated herself, "—and I'm the reason he knows which valley you were in at all."

* * *

The silence after that lasted long enough for the fire to spit twice.

Sera moved first—not toward Nim but around her, circling to put herself between Nim and Juniper without seeming to, an old habit of a woman who had learned the hard way which direction danger came from. Her hand rested near the knife at her belt, not on it. "Say that last part again," Sera said quietly, "and slower."

"The night I ran," Nim said, "I left the temple through the reliquary. There are listening bones there—ribs strung on wire, from the first priests. They carry voices to whoever feeds them. My father feeds them himself, every dawn. And when I passed, I was crying, and I said your name. Both your names. Out loud. I thought—I thought no one was listening. But the bones are always listening. That's what they're *for*." She wrapped her arms around herself. "He's known since the second day. Everything—the ford, the bridge, the roads we've taken. He's let us run because running keeps you moving toward the mountain, and the mountain is where he needs you."

"You led him to me." Juniper's voice was very even, which was worse than shouting. He set down the whetstone with exaggerated care, the way he handled things when he was holding himself together with both hands. "At the bridge. You watched me give my true name to the toll-keeper, and you said nothing."

"Yes."

"And the bone-priests at the wheat field—they found us too easily."

"They weren't tracking you." Nim felt tears coming and forced them down; her father's priests read tears like scripture, and she would not give anyone here that text. "They were tracking *me*. The shard reacts when it gets close to the sigil. It's why I kept my distance those first days. It's why I slept upwind." She pulled open the collar of her shirt.

The shard hung on a cord against her sternum: a sliver of pale material the length of a finger, neither stone nor bone nor anything she had words for, its edges worn smooth by centuries of handling. In the firelight it caught the orange glow and gave back something else—a faint gold shimmer, like light remembered rather than reflected. Even now, even eight feet away, Nim could see it trembling slightly, humming against her skin, reaching toward the man by the fire the way iron filings reach for a lodestone.

"That," Sera breathed. She crossed the camp in three strides and crouched, close but not touching, her cartographer's eyes narrowing. "That's from the door. That's from *the* door." Her voice changed entirely, all its irony stripped away. "I've seen its shape in the master atlas. The old kingdom's charts mark a keyway in the mountain above Ossuary Vale, drawn in gold leaf, and the marginalia says—" she recited it from memory, of course she did, "—'*sealed with the king's own substance, to be opened by none but his blood.*'"

"His blood," Nim repeated. "Yes. My father's blood opened it once. But the door has been closed so long, sealed from inside by people who wanted it shut forever, that his blood alone isn't enough anymore. The seal grew roots. It became part of the world's own bones." She finally made herself look at Juniper directly. "It needs new blood. Blood descended from the ones who closed it. Yours. You're the heir, Juniper. The last heir of Ossuary Vale. Your blood doesn't just open the door—it *unseals* it. It dissolves what your ancestors built. And on the other side of that door is everything my father lost. His priesthood. His worship. His army of answered prayers. A century of hunger waiting behind one lock, and your body is the key."

* * *

Juniper stood. He walked to the edge of the firelight and stood there with his back to them, and Nim watched the muscles of his shoulders move with breath that was too controlled, the breathing of a man managing pain.

When he spoke, he didn't turn around. "The dead went silent around me two weeks before Sera ever arrived. Did you know that? A lifetime of voices in my hands, and then nothing. I thought I'd done something wrong. I thought I'd finally carved one charm too many and they'd abandoned me." A short, terrible laugh. "They hadn't abandoned me. Something was *pulling* on them. Something waking up under the world, drawing all the quiet toward itself like water down a drain. That was you, wasn't it? Or him. Through you."

"The shard wakes when it's near the sigil," Nim whispered. "I think... I think some part of you has always been the door's other half. The valley dead could feel it. That's why they fell quiet. They were afraid of what you're becoming."

"And my grandmother's kit. The sigil on my ribs. The corpse that turned to ash at my touch." He turned now, and his eyes in the firelight were the eyes of a man taking inventory of a ruined house. "You've known since the cart. Since before the cart—you knew who I was when you found me. So tell me the rest, since we're doing honesty tonight. Tell me what happens when he gets what he wants."

"You die." Nim didn't flinch away from it; she owed him the unflinching. "Not quickly. The rite takes days. They'll keep you alive while the door drinks you. And when it's finished, there won't be enough of you left to bury. My father will call it an honor. He'll mean it. That's the worst part—he genuinely believes he's restoring a sacred order, that everyone will be grateful. He answered ten thousand prayers during the famine years and never understood that gratitude given under duress is just another kind of chain." She swallowed. "I know because he chained me with it first."

Sera straightened slowly. Nim braced for the accusation, the blade of it—but when Sera spoke, her voice was strangely flat, almost gentle. "I sold a refugee column's location to fund my atlas. Eleven years ago. Forty-three people went into the salt mines because I needed parchment and patronage." She looked at Juniper. "So I know exactly what it is to sit across a fire from someone who fed you to the wolves, and I know the arithmetic of forgiveness doesn't balance. It never does. You forgive anyway or you travel alone, and alone, we all die in a wheat field."

"Nim," Juniper said.

She made herself meet his eyes.

"Why?" Simple word. Enormous word. "Why run? Why warn me? What do you want?"

And there it was, the question underneath all questions, the one she'd fled a thousand miles rather than answer, and lying now—after all this—would be the one betrayal that couldn't be survived. So she told the truth one more time.

"I want to be no one's daughter. I want the door to stay shut so hard shut that even his name forgets it. I can't kill him—he's a god, or near enough. But you can starve him. Your ancestors figured it out: erase yourselves, take away his key, and he sleeps. I ran because I'm the only person alive who knows how the rite of sealing works, and I'd rather teach it to the man he's hunting than watch him use it on the world." Her voice finally broke and she let it. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to use me. Point me at what needs breaking and I'll break it, and when this is over you never have to see my face again."

* * *

For a long moment the only sound was the fire and, far off, the wind moving through the scrub like something vast turning over in its sleep.

Then Juniper crossed the camp, held out his hand—and Nim saw, with a lurch, the fresh burn marking his palm, the mark her father pressed into men's dreams. He'd refused Aurel already, then. Refused him formally, knowing the cost.

He took the cord at her neck and lifted the shard free of her skin, and where it had rested, her own flesh was marked with a faint gold shadow in the shape of the sigil on his ribs. Twin marks. Key and lock.

"We keep it between us," he said. "Wrapped in your spare shirt, in the bottom of Sera's map case. No one carries it against their skin. No one carries it alone." He looked at Sera. "We're three days from the mountain road, by her charts. Two, if the roads behave, and they won't." Then, to Nim, quieter: "I don't forgive you yet. But I believe you. That has to be enough for tonight."

"It's more than I hoped for," Nim said honestly.

Sera was already repacking the map case, her movements quick and precise, but she paused with her hand flat on the leather and said, without looking up, "The atlas margin. '*Opened by none but his blood.*'" Her jaw tightened. "There's a second line underneath the gold leaf. I've never been able to read it—it's scraped almost to nothing. But I copied what remained into my working book years ago, and I've stared at it a thousand times wondering. '*And closed again by—*'"

She looked up, and the firelight caught her eyes, and Nim saw the exact moment the cartographer understood what she'd been carrying in her notes all along.

"*And closed again by the heir's own hand,*" Nim finished softly. "Willingly. From his own bone."

Juniper went very still. Somewhere east, beyond the dark hills, torches were moving along roads that bent themselves obligingly toward the mountain, and the shard in the map case hummed once, softly, like something answering a call it had waited a century to hear.

"We walk tonight," Juniper said.
