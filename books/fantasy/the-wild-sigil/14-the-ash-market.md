## 14. The Ash Market

The market announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and old paper, tallow and something sweeter beneath it all—the scent of things that had outlived their owners. Sera Quill smelled it from the ridge above the river bend and felt her heart quicken like a girl's.

"Is that it?" Nim asked, shading her eyes. Below them, strung along a dry floodplain where three dead rivers met, hundreds of tents rippled in colors that did not belong together—madder red beside salt-stain blue beside a yellow so pale it looked like cloth that had given up. Smoke rose from cookfires and braziers and one enormous pyre at the far end that burned green and never seemed to diminish.

"That's the Ash Market," Sera said. She heard the reverence in her own voice and hated it slightly. "Where nations go to be sold."

Juniper stood a little apart from them, as he had for days now, his shoulders drawn up as though the wind were something said about him personally. Since the bridge, since the toll-keeper had taken his name and marked him with it, he had been quieter than quiet—a silence with edges. He looked down at the bazaar the way a man looks at deep water when he cannot swim.

"There will be bone-sellers there," he said. It was not quite a question.

"There are bone-sellers everywhere," Sera answered, gentler than she meant to be. "But this is the safest place in the world for you today, if anything is. Here, everyone's past is merchandise. No one looks twice at what anyone else carries."

She was not sure she believed it. She was not sure she believed much of what she said these days. But Juniper nodded once, and Nim hitched her bundle higher under her cloak—the shard wrapped in wool against her spine, though Sera still only knew it as *the thing Nim would not explain*—and they went down into the smoke.

* * *

The Ash Market had no streets, only currents. Sera led them through them by instinct learned years ago, before her exile, when she had come here first as a student with her master's coin purse and her master's disapproval. You moved with the flow of bodies until you found your current: the tent-row of relic-mongers who dealt in door hinges from fallen cities; the row of singers who sold last words, transcribed from dying men of extinct tribes at ruinous prices; the row of map-sellers, which was smaller every year, because who bought maps of places that no longer existed?

Who but her. Who but the disgraced, the obsessed, the ones who could not let a country die just because everyone else had agreed to forget it.

The stalls here sold memory in every form. A woman with silver rings on every finger offered vials of ash labeled in a dozen dead scripts—*Last Hearthfire of Kellin-Moor. Ashes of the Library at Vess, unsorted.* A boy no older than ten sold teeth arranged on velvet by nation, each little white crescent tagged with a painted flag. A fat merchant in robes of impossible orange displayed a single boot, cracked and enormous, claiming it had walked out of Ostry-on-the-Water the night the sea took it, and had kept walking for nine miles afterward.

Sera felt Juniper flinch beside her at that one—at relics that moved, that remembered motion—and touched his elbow without thinking. He glanced at her hand, then at her face, and something in his expression made her withdraw it.

"I'm fine," he said, before she asked.

"You keep saying that."

"It keeps being mostly true."

Nim had gone strange and watchful the moment they'd entered the market, hood up, moving close to Juniper's shadow. Sera pretended not to notice. There were enough secrets among the three of them to pave a road to the sea.

What Sera wanted lay deeper in, past the ash-vendors and the tooth-boy, in the row they called the Quiet Quarter, where the tents had no banners and the sellers did not call out. That was where you found people who traded in answers rather than objects. And answers, she knew, were the most expensive goods in any market.

* * *

The map she meant to barter lay against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, where it had lain for eleven years. She had taken it from the Royal Cartographic Hall of Vess the night the city fell—not stolen, she told herself still, after all this time, *rescued*, though the distinction had never once fooled the dreams that came after—and it was the finest thing she would ever touch: the Handscroll of the Western Reaches, six feet of treated silk on which some anonymous master had rendered a thousand miles of coast and mountain in inks ground from lapis and crushed pearl. Her master atlas needed it. Her life's work needed it. She had carried it through exile and disgrace and the burning of two safehouses, and she had sworn she would part with it over her corpse.

But the atlas had a hole in its heart, and the hole was shaped like a valley.

They set up camp for the night in the market's edge-country, among the tents of those too poor or too proud for the interior rows. Sera left Juniper mending a strap with Nim hovering nearby—she had noticed how the girl positioned herself between Juniper and strangers lately, always casually, always deniably—and went alone into the Quiet Quarter with the scroll heavy against her chest.

The stall she wanted was not a stall. It was a tent of undyed canvas with no sign, guarded by nothing but an old man asleep in a chair, or seeming to sleep, with his hands folded on a walking stick of black wood. Sera stood before him for a long count of ten until one eye opened, gray as weather.

"You're the cartographer," he said. His voice sounded like pages turning. "The one who asks after Vale."

Her mouth went dry. "You know me?"

"I know everyone who asks after Ossuary Vale." He unfolded himself from the chair with the care of a man whose joints were also heirlooms. "There have been eleven of you in my lifetime. Scholars, treasure-hunters, a poet once, gods help us. They all wanted the same thing. Coordinates."

"And what do you want?"

He smiled without warmth. "To see what you'll pay."

She unwrapped the oilcloth right there, in the smoky lamplight, and saw the old man's breath catch—which was, she thought, the first honest reaction anyone in this market had shown her. The Handscroll unrolled across his table like a captured river, pearls and lapis catching flame-color.

"This is worth more than this entire row of tents," he said quietly.

"Yes."

"And you'd trade it. For news."

"For truth," Sera said, and heard the hunger crack her voice open. She had rehearsed this haggling, planned to open low and feign indifference, and watched all of it burn away in a single breath. "Eleven years I've chased a country that isn't on any chart made in the last century. Every record says it was destroyed—burned, drowned, depopulated, pick your catastrophe, the sources disagree because the sources are lies. I've been to where the maps say it stood. There's nothing there. Not ruins. Nothing. Land doesn't heal over that clean. So either every cartographer of the last hundred years failed, or—" She stopped. Made herself say it. "Or the country was never destroyed at all. It was *removed*."

The old man looked at her for a long moment. Then he began rolling up the scroll with hands that were, she noticed, trembling very slightly.

"Sit," he said. "Drink. What I'm going to tell you cost me forty years and both my sponsors, so you'll forgive me if I take the silk first."

* * *

He poured a tea that tasted of iron and honey, and while it steeped he talked, and Sera forgot to drink.

"The histories will tell you Ossuary Vale died in the Sundering Wars, same as thirty other kingdoms. Burned by Aurel's priests, they say. But I served a house that kept older papers, papers predating the wars, and I'll tell you what those papers say." He leaned forward. "They say that in the last year of the war, the king of the Vale did something no king has done before or since. He didn't sue for peace. He didn't scatter his army. He gathered his cartographers, his chroniclers, his genealogists—every soul whose trade was *remembering*—into the mountain vaults, and he ordered them to erase him."

Sera's skin prickled. "Erase—"

"Himself. His line. His kingdom. Every road renamed, every boundary redrawn, every grave-marker struck blank, every song rewritten with the names cut out like rotten teeth. The Vale didn't fall, girl. The Vale *amputated itself* from the world's memory. Deliberately. Completely. The greatest act of cartography ever performed wasn't drawing a country—it was unpublishing one." The old man's eyes glittered. "And the question that has eaten forty years of my life is: what was their king so afraid of being *found* by?"

Sera thought of Juniper's face at the toll-bridge. Thought of the sigil she had glimpsed, once, when his shirt had pulled aside—that mark like a brand, like a seal, glowing faintly under his skin. Thought of a god-king rising from stone after a century of silence, and bone-priests asking after a hearer.

"Aurel," she said slowly. "The god-king. If he needs something from the Vale—if the Vale held something he wanted badly enough—then hiding the whole country would be—"

"Hiding the *door*," the old man finished softly. "Whatever the Vale was, whatever sat under that mountain, the king decided that the only way to keep it shut was to make sure no one alive could find it. Including, perhaps especially, himself. Erasure as survival. A kingdom that chose to be forgotten so it could choose to exist." He pushed the rolled scroll back toward her, and Sera stared at it, uncomprehending.

"Take it," he said. "I deal in truths, not silks. Besides—" the ghost of a smile again, sadder now, "—you'll need it. Because there's one more thing, and it's the reason I waited for you instead of selling to the treasure-hunters. The papers spoke of the erasure having a price. Blood-price, the phrase was. The royal line couldn't just be forgotten by the world—they had to be forgotten *by themselves*. Sealed inside their own descendants, generation after generation, sleeping in the blood." He tapped the table once, hard. "And three weeks ago, every relic-trader in this market felt it happen: something old woke up in the west. Something with a birthmark like a crown."

The lamplight guttered. Somewhere out in the currents of the market, a bell rang the hour, and Sera thought of Juniper sitting by their small fire, mending a strap with careful hands, hearing the dead murmur through his bones, carrying a kingdom under his ribs without knowing its name.

"He doesn't know," she whispered. "He has no idea what he—"

"No," the old man agreed. "And neither, I think, does the thing hunting him. Not yet. Which means you have a head start, cartographer. I'd run faster."

* * *

She walked back through the currents without seeing them. The green pyre at the market's heart threw its colorless light over tents and sellers and the boy with his teeth on velvet, and none of it touched her. Eleven years. Eleven years of exile and burned safehouses and a master's disappointed ghost, all spent chasing coordinates—and the answer had never been a place on a map. The answer was a man who carved charms from other people's bones and apologized to the wood when he whittled it.

Ossuary Vale was not destroyed. Ossuary Vale was *hidden*—by its own king, at the cost of its own name. And its king's blood was sitting by a fire, waiting for her, trusting her.

Trusting her. Sera stopped between tents and pressed the oilcloth roll against her sternum, over the place where the scroll had lived for eleven years, and made herself look squarely at the thought she had been refusing since the bridge:

She had sold things before. Locations. Names. A refugee column, once, to buy the atlas itself—and she had never told him, and might never tell him, and the shame of it sat in her like a swallowed stone. What would she sell to keep him safe? What wouldn't she? And which of those answers was the better person's?

When she reached their fire, Juniper looked up, and his face did the thing it always did—that terrible, gentle reading of her, as if grief had taught him to hear living hearts too.

"You found something," he said.

Sera sat down across from him, the fire between them, the scroll in her lap heavier than silk had any right to be. Behind Nim's shoulder, far off at the market's dark edge, a lantern flared in a tent that had flown no banner an hour ago, and a figure in a bone-white mask turned its head toward their fire as though it had heard its name.

"Yes," Sera said. "And you're not going to believe what it costs."
