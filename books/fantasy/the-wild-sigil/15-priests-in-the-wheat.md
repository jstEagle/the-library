## 15. Priests in the Wheat

The wheat was taller than Nim, which she took as a personal affront.

"It's obscene," she said, pushing through it with both arms. "Grain shouldn't come up to a person's chest. Where I'm from, nothing grows this tall unless it's been *encouraged*."

"Where you're from," Sera said without turning around, "nothing grows at all unless someone's praying over it."

Juniper let the argument wash past him. He had learned in three weeks of travel that the two of them bickered the way rivers ran downhill—constantly, and with no particular destination—and that interrupting only made the current stronger. He walked at the rear of their little line with his hood back and his hands loose at his sides, and he tried to feel the land instead of hearing it.

That was the discipline of it now. The valley's dead were far behind him, out of range of whatever sense had carried their voices through his bones since childhood, but the world between here and the border was not silent. It was never entirely silent. Every field they crossed had its sediment of the dead—farmers under hedgerows, soldiers under fords, children under the crooked stones people pretended were just rocks. Their voices came to him faintly here, muffled, like speech heard through a wall, or through water, or through the long green hush of wheat.

This field was quieter than most. That should have told him something.

He noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth: an absence with edges. For the last mile the dead had murmured their fragmentary gossip—the old woman under the oak complaining about the drainage, the drowned boy in the millpond singing half a hymn on a loop—but as they waded deeper into the wheat those voices thinned, and thinned, and stopped, not fading so much as *withdrawing*, the way birds go quiet before a storm.

Juniper stopped walking.

Sera was ten paces ahead, her stolen master atlas wrapped in oilcloth across her back like a shield. She'd taken to wearing it there even while sleeping, and Juniper had stopped commenting on it, because he understood. A map could be a kind of spine. He had carried his gift the same way, once.

Nim turned when she felt him stop. "What?"

"I don't know." Which was true, and useless, and he said it anyway because lying to these two had become harder than telling the truth, and that frightened him more than anything chasing them did.

The wheat stood golden and heavy-headed all around them, moving in slow pulses where the wind combed through it. It was beautiful. It was the kind of field a painter would choose, all light and abundance, and Juniper found himself hating it with a sudden animal clarity—not the wheat itself but the *stillness* underneath it, the way the stalks nearest him leaned slightly away, as if the ground beneath them had flinched.

"Sera," he said. "How far to the river?"

"Two hours, if we push." She'd gone still in the way she went still when reading terrain, her eyes doing that quick triangulating flick across the horizon. "There's a farmstead ahead somewhere—I marked a chimney smoke an hour ago. We could ask for a barn."

"We're not asking anyone for anything," Nim said. "We're crossing and not being seen. That was the whole plan. It was a very short plan. I liked it."

"The plan," Sera said, "assumed the roads would keep behaving."

The roads had not kept behaving. That was the other thing living in Juniper's mind these weeks, alongside the grief of silence and the spreading warmth beneath his ribs: the geography itself seemed to be arguing with Sera's atlas. Paths that appeared on her maps led into ravines that weren't charted. Bridges showed up where bridges had no business being. Twice they'd followed a road that simply gave up mid-stride and became sheep track, then bramble, then nothing, as though the land were trying to forget them specifically. Sera had started muttering about *erasure fields*, about how a kingdom hidden by its own king might leave scars in the shape of its hiding, and she said it with the hungry brightness of a woman who loved a mystery more than her own safety.

Juniper loved neither mysteries nor safety particularly. What he loved—if he was honest, standing in that wheat, listening to the absence of the dead—was the small daily fact of being unremarkable. A carver. A quiet man with sawdust in his hair. Every mile from the valley made that harder to hold onto.

"Something's wrong," he said. "The dead have pulled back."

Nim went pale in the specific way she went pale whenever the dead were mentioned, which was often, and which Juniper had decided was not fear exactly but recognition—she'd grown up in a temple full of bone-priests who spoke *for* the dead, and whatever she'd heard there had taught her that the dead deserved better spokesmen.

"Pulled back from what?" she asked. "From us?"

"From *here*." He crouched, pressed two fingers to the soil between the wheat rows. Warmth. Not sunlight-warm. Something underneath, like a hearthstone in a house where no fire had burned for a hundred years. "Like animals before a flood. They know something is coming up the road behind us."

Sera swore softly and fluently, a cartographer's vocabulary of curses organized by direction. Then she pointed. "Dust," she said. "West side. Moving fast. Not a cart—riders, maybe six. Maybe eight."

Juniper looked and couldn't see it at first, and then could: a smudge against the glare where the field met the sunken lane, dust rising in a column too straight to be wind-blown.

"They've been behind us since the ford," Nim whispered. "I told you. I *told* you at the ford. Nobody crosses a toll-bridge that fast unless they already know which names to say."

At the bridge, three days ago, the toll-keeper had demanded true names, and Sera had lied beautifully, inventing a widow and a wool merchant and a niece, and Juniper—who had spent his whole life teaching himself to be no one—had looked at the toll-keeper's patient gray face and told the truth. His name. Just the name, plain, like setting down a stone. And the bridge had hummed under his feet, and a mark like a frost-fern had bloomed briefly on the toll-post, and Sera hadn't spoken to him for a day afterward, which was the angriest she knew how to be.

Now he understood what the mark had been. An address. You cannot send a letter without one.

"We run for the river," Sera said. "Wet ground doesn't burn. If they have fire—"

"They don't have fire," Nim said. Her voice had gone very flat, very young. "They have worse than fire."

The riders came out of the sunken lane and into the field's edge, and Juniper saw them clearly for the first time and felt his stomach drop through the warm earth into some colder country below.

They wore pilgrim white, but the white was wrong—stained at the seams, yellowed like bone left in earth, and their faces were masked in polished plates of carved femur, featureless except for eye-holes, so that eight identical skulls rode toward them through the gold wheat. Bone-priests. He'd only seen them once before, at the ford, asking after *the hearer*, and his neighbors had lied for him with such easy, generous courage, and old Tam Vetch had paid for it with a broken arm and a burned barn, and Juniper had run rather than watch the rest.

He was done running through wheat. There was nowhere to run *to*. The river was two hours; the priests were four minutes; the math closed like a door.

"They've seen us," Sera said. "Scatter?"

"No," Juniper said, and didn't know he was going to say it until it was out. "No. They want me. If we scatter they take the others and make me watch."

"Then what's your suggestion, carver?" Sera snapped, but her hand was already finding his sleeve, holding on, and he realized she wasn't asking him as a strategist. She was asking him the way people ask a thing they've decided to trust.

Nim had gone very still. She was looking at his chest—at his ribs, where under shirt and skin and bone the sigil lived now, the birthmark that had woken in his grandmother's kit-shadow and spread since, a branching dark thing like frost or lightning or a river delta seen from very high up. She was looking at it as though she could see it through the cloth.

"Don't," she said quietly. "Whatever you're thinking of doing—don't. You don't know what it costs yet."

"I know what it costs to do *nothing*," Juniper said.

The first priest raised his voice, and it was the raising that broke something in Juniper's careful, practiced calm. Because the voice that came out of the bone mask was not one voice. It was layered, choral, many throats speaking in unison through one mouth-hole, and the words rolled across the wheat like a benediction:

"*Hearer. Beloved of the silenced. The King Below the Stone has waited a century in the dark for the sound of your blood. Come home. Come kneeling. No harm needs to touch this field.*"

And beneath the words, faintly, horribly, Juniper heard the other thing—the thing he suspected only he could hear. Under each mask, each priest carried bones that were not their own. Finger-bones strung at the belt, knuckle rosaries, a rib slung at the hip like a scythe. And those bones were *whispering*. Praying. Begging, in tiny desiccated voices, to be put down, to be buried, to be anywhere but swinging at the hip of men who used prayer as a leash.

The dead did not serve these men. The dead were *captured* by them.

Something in Juniper went very hot and very cold at the same time.

"Hearer," the chorus called again, closer now, wheat flattening before the horses though the horses' hooves barely seemed to touch it. "Your grandmother chose silence. Your mother chose silence. Generations of cowards chose silence, and the world grew deaf and starved of miracle. You can end the famine. One drop given freely opens every door that cowardice has shut—"

"My grandmother," Juniper said, and his voice came out low and even and strange to him, "was not a coward."

He hadn't meant to speak. He certainly hadn't meant to do what happened next, because he didn't *do* it—it rose through him the way a sneeze rises, involuntary and enormous, from the place under his ribs where the sigil burned. His hands came up. Not fists. Open palms, the way he held them over a carving when listening for the grain of the wood, the true line of the bone. Listening hands. Giving-back hands.

And the sigil answered.

Heat lanced along his ribs—not pain, exactly, worse than pain: *permission*. As if some vast patience inside him had been waiting a hundred years for a reason and had finally been handed one. Light seeped through his shirt at the chest, amber-white, the color of the memorial candles he'd lit for the drowned child a lifetime ago in the valley. The air pressure changed. The wind died all at once, everywhere, so completely that the wheat stopped moving, thousands of stalks frozen mid-sway like a painting of a field—

—and then everything in a circle around them began to die.

It moved outward from Juniper's feet in a perfect ring, smooth and swift and utterly silent. The wheat didn't burn. Burning would have been a mercy, a violence you could name. Instead the gold drained upward out of the stalks like water running the wrong way through a reed—green to gold to gray to white, heads bowing, stems collapsing into husks that crumbled at a breath. In the space of ten heartbeats the three of them stood at the center of a circle of dead wheat thirty yards across, bleached bone-pale, every stalk fallen outward in a pattern too regular to be accidental—a pattern, Sera would say later, staring at it, that was *radial*, that was *drawn*, that was the spitting image of the sigil on Juniper's ribs rendered in ruined grain across sixty yards of living earth.

The horses stopped. Would not come closer. The lead priest's mount screamed and wheeled, and the chorus-voice faltered mid-word, and for one moment the masks turned toward each other, eight identical skulls conferring, and Juniper heard—faintly, from the belts and the rosaries and the slung ribs—a sound he had never heard from relic-bones before.

Cheering.

Tiny, dry, whispering triumph. *Put us down. Put us down. He can put us down.*

Then the moment passed, and the heat in Juniper's chest guttered, and the price arrived.

It came as cold. All at once, the warmth that had filled him poured out through his palms into the ruined circle and kept going, draining, and he felt it leave the way you feel blood leave a wound: a widening chill, a hollowness, a wrongness in the shape of himself. His knees hit the dead wheat. The stalks crumbled under him like ash. Somewhere above him Sera was shouting his name, and Nim was shouting something else—*what did you DO, what did it TAKE*—and the sky tilted, and the last thing he registered clearly was the taste in his mouth.

Not blood. Not ash.

Sweetness. Faint, cloying, like honey stirred into milk, like an offering left on an altar, and he understood, in the final clear second before the darkness took him, that something in the world had just tasted what he'd spent. Had found it good.

Was going to want more.

* * *

He woke to the smell of crushed grain and Sera's hand hard on his shoulder, shaking him, and the light gone long and orange with evening.

"Up," she was saying. "Up, you absolute idiot, up—we have maybe an hour before they decide we're worth the risk of touching."

He sat up. The circle was still there, of course. It would always be there. The dead wheat lay in its perfect radial sweep, silver-gray in the sunset, and walking through it had left a trail like a scar through a drawn heart. Nim stood at the circle's edge, outside it deliberately, arms wrapped around herself, and she wouldn't look at him.

"Nim—" he started.

"Don't." Her voice cracked and steadied. "Don't apologize yet. First tell me what it felt like."

"What it—" He searched for it, and found, to his horror, that the honest answer was waiting right there. "It felt *good*. While it was happening. It felt like—" Like the moment a carving comes free of the block and you see you've guessed the grain right. Like the dead saying *yes, this one, this is the one who listens.* "It felt like being agreed with."

Nim nodded slowly, miserably. "That's how it starts," she said. "My father's whole church started with one person feeling agreed with."

Sera hauled him upright, checked him over with brisk, rough-handed efficiency—ribs, pulse, pupils—and Juniper saw her pause when her fingers brushed his chest, through the shirt, where the sigil lived. He saw her feel the new branch of it. It had spread again. He'd known it would; he could feel it, a fine cold thread reaching up toward his collarbone like ivy finding a wall.

"How many years does it take off?" Sera asked quietly. "Or is that the wrong question?"

"It's the wrong question," Juniper said. "The right one is what it fed. Because it wasn't taking from me. Not mostly. It was—" He looked out over the ruined circle, at the perfect geometry of death he had drawn without meaning to, with the same hands that carved memorials for drowned children. "It was taking from *everything else*. The field. Maybe the field next year. Maybe the well water. I don't think power like this comes from a person. I think a person is just... where it puts its foot down."

Sera was quiet a long moment. Then, because she was Sera, she got out the atlas, unwrapped it right there in the dead circle, and began to sketch the ring from above—from memory, from angles, from the precise cartographer's hunger that Juniper had long since stopped resenting and started relying on. "Sixty yards," she murmured. "Radial symmetry. This will matter. This will matter enormously, someday, to someone." Then she looked up at him, and her eyes were not hungry at all. They were afraid, and kind about it, which was the worst combination he knew. "You killed a field to save our lives. I'll never pretend otherwise, and I'll never thank you for having to."

From the edge of the circle, Nim spoke without turning around.

"There's more," she said. "You need to hear it before you sleep, because you won't want to hear it tomorrow." She still wouldn't look at him. Her shadow stretched long across the dead wheat, all the way to his knees. "When the wheat died—I heard something. Through the ground. The way you hear the dead, I think. And it wasn't the wheat. Wheat doesn't pray. But something under this field does, something old, and it woke up when your light touched the dirt, and it said one word before it went back down."

Juniper's chest went cold, colder than the draining had left it.

"What word?"

Nim turned at last. In the dying light her face was grim and young and certain.

"*More,*" she said.

Far off across the darkening fields, a bell began to ring—not an alarm, not a warning. A calling bell, deep and patient, ringing in the rhythm of a heartbeat, and under it, rising from the west like weather, came the faint layered sound of many voices singing in unison, coming closer.

They ran.
