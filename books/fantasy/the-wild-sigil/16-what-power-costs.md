## 16. What Power Costs

The farmhouse smelled of bread gone stale and lavender gone bitter, and Juniper lay in the narrow bed beneath the window and watched the sigil crawl.

He had been awake since before dawn, when the light came gray through the shutters and laid itself across his forearm like a blade. There, below the inside of his elbow where no mark had been three days ago, the lines ran thin and silver-pale, fine as spider silk dipped in milk. He turned his arm slowly in the half-light. The original sigil on his ribs—the one he'd found glowing beneath his grandmother's old carving kit—was still there. He could feel it now without touching it, a warmth like a coin left too long in the sun. But this new growth was different. It was quieter. It was *patient*.

It looked like something he would have carved himself.

That was the worst of it. Not that the mark spread, but that it spread in his own hand—in the flowing cuts he'd learned at seven years old with a bone awl held wrong until his grandmother corrected him, in the little hooked terminals she'd called "courtesies to the dead." Whoever had marked his line, a century or ten centuries ago, had carved in the same language he spoke. He was not wearing someone else's seal. He was wearing his own signature, written on him by blood.

He pressed his thumb against the newest line. It did not glow as the rib-sigil had glown. Instead it went cold, cold as riverbed stone, and somewhere far down in the silence where the valley's dead used to live, he felt—not a voice, nothing so kind—a turning. Like a page. Like something enormous shifting its weight in sleep.

Juniper took his thumb away and did not touch it again.

* * *

The farmhouse belonged to a woman named Odile Ferran and her husband, who grew wheat in a shallow fold of land between two ridges where the roads still behaved themselves—or had, until the night the wheat died in a perfect circle around four strangers and a god's runaway daughter. They had not asked questions. That was the strange mercy of the rural dead lands: people who lived too near old things learned not to look at them directly. Odile had simply come out with a lantern, seen Sera bleeding and Nim white-faced and Juniper on his knees in the ruined grain, and said, "Well. You'll be wanting the bed by the chimney, then."

They had been there five days. Sera's wound was closing—angry red but honest, the kind of wound that meant to heal. Nim spent her days silent in the loft, turning the key-shard over and over in her fingers like a worry-stone, and her nights pretending to sleep while clearly listening for hoofbeats. And Juniper lay in the chimney-bed and watched the silver lines grow along his arm like frost forming in reverse, spreading inward toward his heart instead of outward toward the world.

On the fifth morning, Odile's mother-in-law died.

She did not die dramatically. She was ninety-one years old, had been dying for a year in the way that very old people die, by degrees, like a tide going out further each day and coming back less. Her name was Maren. She had sat in the chair by the window every afternoon Juniper had been in the house, hands folded, watching him with eyes gone pale as skimmed milk, and she had never once asked what he was. On the fourth evening she had said only, "You have carver's hands. My father had carver's hands. They always smell faintly of bone, you know, no matter how you wash them."

Juniper had looked down at his own hands then, and yes—they did. They always had. He'd stopped noticing years ago.

She died an hour past dawn on the sixth day, between one breath and the next, so quietly that the rooster outside announced it more loudly than anything in the house. And the house came apart around that quietness the way houses do: Odile weeping into her apron in the doorway, the husband standing helpless with his hat in his hands, Nim coming down from the loft and retreating again immediately because grief among the living was a country she had no papers for. Sera, propped against the doorframe with her bandaged shoulder, caught Juniper's eye and said nothing, but her mouth made the shape of a warning: *Don't.*

Because everyone in the house knew what he was by now. Or thought they did. Word had traveled ahead of them from the ford, garbled and growing: a hearer, a bone-man, one who could carry messages across the divide. And Odile Ferran, kneeling by her mother-in-law's chair with tears cutting clean tracks down a face otherwise stunned numb, lifted her head and looked at Juniper with the most terrible expression he had ever seen on a living face.

Hope.

"Don't," Sera said again, aloud this time, barely a breath.

But Odile was already crossing the room. She took Juniper's hands in hers—her grip shockingly strong, flour still in the creases of her knuckles—and pulled them toward her own face as if to warm them at a fire. "They say you hear them," she whispered. "The ford people say it, and the tinker from Marrow's Cross said it, that there's one walking the roads who hears the dead speak through his hands. I don't care if it's true. I don't care if it's sin. My boy—" Her voice cracked and reformed. "My son Tam. Drowned in the millrace four years ago this spring. Sixteen years old. I have prayed every night for four years and gotten nothing back, nothing, not even silence that felt like an answer, just—that hole. And your grandmother's mother was a Ferran, they say, back two generations, which makes you kin if it makes you anything, and kin don't refuse kin."

"Odile." The husband stepped forward. "Love. He's hurt. He's—"

"He's *kin*."

Juniper stood in the grip of her hands and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he was going to say yes. He had known it since she crossed the room. He had perhaps known it since the wheat died in a circle around him and something vast and cold had poured through the sigil on his ribs and out through his palms into the soil, killing everything green within reach—not because he had willed it, but because the power had needed somewhere to go, and he had been nearest. Kindness weaponized. That was what Sera had called it that first night, sitting pale against the barn wall while he stared at the dead wheat. *You didn't choose it. That's what frightens me. It chose you.*

"Sit her up," Juniper heard himself say. "In the chair, by the window. The light should come from behind me, not in front. And everyone else—please. Leave us."

* * *

Maren Ferran's body weighed almost nothing. Four years of dying had winnowed her down to bird-bones and papery skin, and when Juniper arranged her hands in her lap and knelt before her, he felt the familiar terrible tenderness rise in him—the love that came easier than any love for the living, because the dead never lied to make you feel better and never lied to make you feel worse. They only told you what was.

Except.

Except that when he closed his hands around hers, expecting the usual opening—the way a door unlatches, the way a voice comes up through the bones like water through chalk—he touched instead the sigil.

The lines on his forearm flared cold. And the silence where the valley's dead had lived, the silence that had followed him out of the valley and haunted every mile since, suddenly acquired a texture. A pressure. Something was *between* him and Maren's ghost. Something had inserted itself into the oldest channel he knew the way a stone inserts itself into a millrace, diverting everything through its own dark gears.

*Not yet,* said nothing, in no voice, and the nothing was unmistakably amused.

Juniper's breath went shallow. He could pull back. He could open his eyes, tell Odile the gift had failed, tell her the dead were beyond reach now, and she would grieve either way but she would stop hoping, and hope was the thing that broke people. He had seen it at the ford. He had seen it his whole life. Hope was the knife; the answer was only the handle.

But her hands were under his, and they were warm, and she had said *kin*, and he had spent thirty years loving the dead precisely because the living asked things like this of him and the dead never did.

He pushed through.

It was like pushing his hand into freezing water up to the shoulder, then the neck. The cold climbed his arms and seated itself in his chest, and for one vertiginous moment he was not in the farmhouse at all but in the space between, the gray country he had glimpsed only twice before, where the dead waited like travelers at a station with no timetable. And there she was: Maren, but young—young as her granddaughter, hair black, sleeves rolled, flour to the elbows—standing beside a boy of sixteen who dripped river water that never fell, who looked at his mother's kneeling body with an expression Juniper had seen a hundred times on the newly dead and never gotten used to.

Relief. And shame.

"She asks about the race," Maren-young said, and her voice came up through Juniper's hands and out of his own mouth in a register that made the hairs on his arms stand up, a voice like wind through a keyhole. "Every visit to the grave. Every year. She asks why he went in. Tell her the truth, carver. She's earned the truth. It's the only inheritance we leave."

"I can't—" Juniper managed, inside the gray. "If it breaks her—"

"It already broke her. Four years ago. Truth is only the shape of the break. Lies are the splinters left in."

And the boy—Tam—looked up. Looked at Juniper with the dead's terrible directness, and opened his mouth, and what came out was not words but memory, pouring through the connection the way the power had poured through him into the wheat: the millrace in spring flood, brown and muscular; Tam on the sluice gate because the miller paid a penny a day to whoever kept the debris cleared and the family needed the penny; the branch that looked solid and wasn't; the water taking him sideways with the indifference of a thing that has all the time in the world; and underneath, under the drowning, under the panic, one thought repeating like a heartbeat: *don't tell Mother I was doing it for the money, she'll blame herself, don't tell her, don't tell her, don't—*

Juniper surfaced gasping, hands still locked around Maren Ferran's cooling ones, and Odile was leaning forward in her chair with her whole soul in her face.

"What did he say?" she breathed. "What did my boy say?"

And here was the fork in the road, and Juniper saw both paths with perfect clarity, and hated himself in advance for the one his feet took. Because the truth was a mercy shaped like a knife. Because Tam had drowned working for pennies his mother never knew the family needed, and the last thing he'd wanted was for her to know, and the sigil on Juniper's arm pulsed cold and patient, and somewhere in the back of his skull something that was not quite a voice observed: *This is what it costs. This is what it has always cost. We only let you see the bill now.*

"He wasn't swimming," Juniper said. His voice sounded borrowed. "He was clearing the sluice. For the miller. A penny a day."

Odile blinked. "No. No, he told me he was—there was a swimming hole, the boys all—"

"He lied so you wouldn't know the money mattered. He lied because he thought you'd blame yourself for needing it." Juniper's throat worked. The cold in his chest was receding, leaving frost-burn behind, leaving the taste of river water in his mouth that would not rinse away. "His last thought—for how long it lasted, for minutes, maybe—was that you mustn't blame yourself. That was the whole of it. Don't tell Mother. Don't tell Mother. He loved you so much it was the last thing he did with his mind."

For a moment Odile was utterly still.

Then she broke.

Not weeping—Juniper had seen weeping, had comforted weeping, weeping was survivable. This was the sound a person makes when a wound four years old is finally cleaned, and it turns out the cleaning hurts worse than the hurt did. She folded over her mother-in-law's body and made sounds that had no letters in them, and her husband came running, and Sera came limping, and Nim appeared on the stairs and went whiter than the dead and fled upward again, and Juniper knelt alone in the middle of it holding a dead woman's hands, knowing exactly what he had done.

He had told the truth. And the truth had done what truths do.

*Kindness weaponized.* He hadn't chosen to kill the wheat. He hadn't chosen to break Odile Ferran's heart. Both times, something older and colder than his own will had moved through him and used his goodness as the handle and his gift as the blade. And the sigil on his arm had grown a finger's length since morning. He could see it without looking: the silver lines had reached the bend of his elbow now, curling toward the soft inside of it, courtesies to the dead, his own handwriting.

* * *

They buried Maren at dusk, on the low hill behind the barn where the family plot was, and Juniper dug the grave himself because his hands needed work or they would start listening again. The earth came up dark and sweet-smelling. Somewhere in it, he knew, the sigil drank. Everything he did now fed it somehow—even grief. Especially grief.

Sera found him after, sitting on the hillside in the last light, arms wrapped around his shins. She lowered herself down beside him with a grunt, favoring the shoulder, and for a while neither of them spoke. Below them the farmhouse windows glowed yellow, and inside, faintly, Odile was singing—some work song, wavering, broken and mended and broken again. Grief with a floor under it, now. Maybe that counted as kindness. Maybe every kindness was a knife that someone eventually had to hold by the blade.

"You could have lied to her," Sera said at last.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Juniper turned his forearm over in the fading light so the silver lines caught the sun. "Because the dead don't lie," he said. "It's the one rule of the work. You can refuse to carry a message. You can soften it. But you don't change what was said. The moment I do that, I'm not a hearer anymore. I'm just another god deciding what people get to know."

Sera was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, careful. "There were priests on the north road today. Two of them, masked, walking toward Marrow's Cross. I saw them from the loft window while you were—" she nodded at the fresh-turned earth. "They weren't looking for us. They were asking after the farmhouse. Asking after the woman who died."

Juniper went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the sigil.

"They know I'm here," he said. "They know what happened here today."

"No," Sera said. "Worse. I think they know what you *did*. And I think, whatever that mark on your arm is becoming—it's starting to announce itself." She looked at the lines on his skin, then at his face, and her eyes were steady and frightened in equal measure. "We leave tomorrow. Before dawn. Because Juniper—if they can find you by the deaths you attend, then every kindness you do from here to the border is a signal fire."

Down in the house, Odile's song rose once, cracked, and held.
