## 17. The God-King's Offer

Aurel had not dreamed in a hundred years.

Sleep, for one such as him, was a courtesy he paid to the shape of his body—a concession to the meat and marrow that remembered being mortal even when the rest of him did not. In the century of his stone sleep beneath the ruined temple, there had been no dreams at all. Only the long dark, and the slow drip of water through cracked vaults, counting out the years like a miser counting coins he could not spend.

But now, awake, he found the old habits returning. The body wanted its rhythms. So he sat each night in the sanctum where his bone-priests had cleared away the rubble and the moss, on a throne they had carved from the temple's fallen altar-stone because no throne remained from before, and he closed his eyes and let the dark come.

And in the dark, for the first time in a hundred years, he dreamed.

He dreamed of the valley.

It was a small thing to find it. He had felt the sigil flare like a struck bell three nights past—a thin thread of his own blood, singing somewhere in the western hills, in a place so minor it had never earned a name worth remembering. He had sent his pilgrims out along the roads with their masks and their gentle questions, and he had waited, patient as stone, because patience was the oldest thing about him. Empires were built by men who could not wait. Gods were made by waiting itself.

But tonight he did not merely wait. Tonight he reached.

The dream unfolded around him like a map drawn by an eager hand: the valley rendered in perfect miniature, every fence post and ford and wheat field sharp as memory. He stood upon its rim in the dream-space, where nothing was solid and everything was true, and looked down at the scattered lights of the farms below. Smoke rose straight from the chimneys in the still evening air. Somewhere a dog barked. Somewhere a woman was singing a lullaby off-key, the way mothers did when they had learned it from their own mothers and never corrected what they loved.

Small. Humble. Forgettable. That was how they had survived—his descendants, his clever, cowardly descendants. They had buried their crowns and burned their genealogies and taught themselves to be farmers, and the world had obliged them by forgetting.

Aurel smiled in the way that mountains might smile, if mountains could hate.

He turned his attention inward, along the thread, and followed it down into the valley, down to a farmhouse near the ford where a boy slept badly in a borrowed bed. Even here, in the space between sleeping and waking, Aurel could feel him—the sigil banked low under his skin like coals raked over, the gift humming in his hands. A hearer of the dead. Of course. Of course. His blood had always been the blood of doors, and doors needed keys, and keys needed ears.

*Juniper Villalobos,* the bone-priests' reports named him. A carver of memorial charms. A boy who kept himself small as his ancestors had, who mended fences and asked no favors and spoke to corpses more easily than to neighbors.

Aurel almost pitied him. It was, after all, exactly the life Aurel would have chosen for a favorite grandson.

* * *

The boy's dream was poor fare. Aurel moved through it the way a man moves through a market he intends to buy whole: noting what was there, what was missing, what was broken.

There was a river in it, running backward. There was a shed full of smoke that would not clear—guilt, then; the burning of the carving-shed, recent. There was an old woman at a loom whose face kept sliding away whenever the boy tried to look at her directly, which told Aurel something tender and useful: the grandmother. Dead, most likely. Loved beyond all sense.

And threaded through everything, faint but unmistakable, there were voices. The boy heard them even in sleep—murmurs at the edge of the dream like wind through wheat. The dead of his valley, whispering their comfort, their complaints, their small eternal gossip. A hundred years of silence had fallen on this land when the last door sealed, and yet here was a child of the sealed line who had never once been alone in the dark.

That gift alone was worth a kingdom. Aurel intended to say so.

He gathered the dream gently in both hands—it came willingly, dreams always did—and reshaped it. The river righted itself. The smoke thinned. He built a version of the valley as it should have been, as it had been, before his clever descendants chose erasure: terraced orchards climbing the slopes, white roads, the great gate standing open and golden in the mountain's flank. He raised the towers of Ossuary Vale out of the earth of the boy's own memory, and set lamps in every window, and filled the streets with people whose faces the boy had never seen but whose blood he carried, and let them smile at him.

Then Aurel stepped into the dream himself, wearing a body he had worn ten thousand times: tall, gray-robed, crowned with iron that looked like laurel, his face kind. Kindness was the mask that fit him best. Wrath drove men to fight; kindness brought them to their knees.

The boy stood among the ghost-lit streets, turning slowly, his hands half-raised as if to touch things he knew could not be real.

"You feel it," Aurel said. "Don't you. That this is yours."

The boy whirled. He was younger than the reports suggested—young enough that fear sat openly on his face—but his eyes went first to Aurel's crown, then to his hands, assessing, the way a craftsman assessed another craftsman's work. Good. A boy who flinched was useless. A boy who measured was teachable.

"Who are you?" the boy said.

"Come," Aurel said, ignoring the question, spreading his arms to encompass the shining city. "Walk with me. This is Ossuary Vale. You have never seen it, and yet you know it. Look—" he gestured, and the streets filled, the lamps brightened, "—this was your inheritance. Not the farmhouse. Not the valley. *This.* Your grandmothers threw it away, do you understand? They had everything, and they were afraid, so they buried it and pretended to be no one, and they called their cowardice a sacrifice."

"It wasn't cowardice." The boy's voice shook, but held. "They starved him of worship. That's what I've been told."

"Told by whom? The dead?" Aurel laughed, warm as a hearthfire. "The dead are liars, child. They have nothing left but stories, and stories are how they keep you. Ask yourself—who benefits from your smallness? Who has fed on your humility for six generations? Your ancestors took a kingdom and gave you a fence-mending chore, and called it virtue."

The boy said nothing, but Aurel watched the words go in, watched them find purchase. Truth needs no purchase—it only needs a crack.

"I am Aurel," he said then, simply. "Once king of this place. Once god of it. Your ancestor in the old tongue—*grandfather*, if we are being precise, though the genealogy has grown tangled in the dark. I slept a hundred years while my people unmade me. And now I am awake, and I am gathering what is mine, and Juniper—" he let the name fall softly, a stone dropped in still water, "—I would rather gather you than gather around you. Do you understand the difference?"

"No," said the boy, honestly.

"Good. Honesty. Another virtue they failed to breed out of you." Aurel walked, and the dream walked with them, and the city rebuilt itself street by street ahead of their feet. "Here is my offer, then, plainly, because I despise riddles and my priests love them too much. Kneel to me—not now, not in chains, but when you choose, freely, understanding what you kneel to—and I will give you back everything your family surrendered."

He stopped and turned, and let the boy see the city whole behind him, glowing.

"Your gift stays. No—your gift *grows*. You will hear the dead of every nation, not just your little valley's whisperers. You will be the hearer at the right hand of the god who answers prayers. Your valley keeps its name and gains mine—Villalobos, hearer-royal, a house again instead of a hiding place. Your neighbors will want for nothing. Their sick will wake healed. Their dead fields will bear twice. Every prayer whispered in that valley will be answered by morning, and they will know whom to thank, and they will thank *you*, Juniper, because you brought them to me."

He leaned closer, and gentled his voice to the register he had once used to soothe dying kings.

"And I know what you truly fear. Not death. Silence. You cannot bear a world without their voices—I saw it in your dream, in the way you cling to those murmurs like a drowning man. Kneel, and you will never lose them. Never. The dead will speak to you until the sun burns out, and I will make certain of it myself. That is my promise, sworn on my crown."

The boy's breath had gone ragged. His hands—his beautiful, cursed hands—were trembling at his sides. Aurel watched him fight it, watched him reach for reasons to refuse, and loved him a little for the struggle, the way a sculptor loves resistant marble.

"And if I don't?" the boy whispered.

"Ah." Aurel sighed, and let the city dim around them, street by street, lamp by lamp, until only the two of them stood in the dark together, which was, in the end, the honest architecture of every throne. "Then I will take what is mine by other means. Your blood opens the door whether it flows willing or spilled—that is the unfortunate physics of it. But hear me, Juniper, because this is the part the priests won't tell you: I do not need your *love*. I need only your blood, or your knee. If you run, I will follow, and I will answer every prayer in your valley until your neighbors are mine, bound to me by gratitude, which binds tighter than any chain ever forged. And the ones who lie for you—the ones who already paid, at the ford, with their lives for your name—there will be more of them, and their deaths will be on your ledger, not mine."

He reached out and took the boy's hand—could not resist it, the sigil flaring warm between their palms like two halves of a coal pressed together—and turned it over, and traced one line across the palm with a fingertip. The mark he left there glowed faintly gold.

"A token," Aurel said. "So you can find me in your dreams, and I can find you. Think of it as a door left open. Doors, after all, are the family trade."

The boy pulled his hand back and cradled it against his chest like something wounded.

"This isn't real," Juniper said. "None of this is real."

"No," Aurel agreed pleasantly, beginning already to withdraw, letting the dream fray at its edges into the ordinary dark of a farm boy's sleep. "But the mark is. And the offer is. And tomorrow, when you wake and look at your palm, you will know I was here—and you will spend the whole day telling yourself you'll refuse, and the whole night wondering what it would cost. That is how it begins, child. That is how it began with better men than you."

The valley-dream dissolved. The last thing Aurel showed him, deliberately, cruelly, tenderly, was the farmhouse kitchen of his childhood, warm and lamplit, and an old woman at the table whose face slid away when looked at directly—and the sound of her voice saying, clear as memory, *you were always meant for more than fences.*

Then he was gone, and the boy was alone with his ghosts.

* * *

Aurel opened his eyes in the sanctum, and the candles had burned down to pools, and three of his bone-priests knelt at the foot of the throne, waiting, as they had waited all night without moving.

"Well?" asked the eldest, Vesh, her voice dry as page-scraps.

"He is soft," Aurel said. "Soft, and stubborn, and afraid of silence above all things—which means he is already halfway mine. Fear does the kneeling long before the knee does." He flexed his hand, feeling the echo of the boy's pulse in his palm, the twin warmth of the shared mark. "He will run. Let him run. Running men exhaust themselves against the shape of the world, and the world is shaped like me."

"The army grows," Vesh said. "Four thousand answered at the last count. They come from the river towns now, unsent. Farmers. Fishermen. A widow who prayed for rain for nine years." Her voice held the careful neutrality of a servant reporting a harvest. "They ask what you would have them do."

"Feed them," Aurel said. "House them. Answer their small prayers until their gratitude has no floor. An army that marches because it loves you cannot be deserting, cannot be bought, cannot be beaten by any general alive." He rose from the throne, and the candlelight bent toward him the way plants bend toward the sun. "And send riders west. Quiet ones. Wherever the hearer sleeps tonight, I want to know the road he takes by dawn. Do not touch him. Do not frighten him."

He paused at the window, where the first gray of morning was finding the broken hills, and thought of the boy cradling his marked hand, and permitted himself the luxury of certainty.

"He will come to the door on his own feet," Aurel said softly. "They always do, in the end. Blood remembers what pride forgets."

Behind him, the priests bowed. Ahead of him, far to the west, a marked palm woke its owner with a start in a dark farmhouse, and a boy cried out a name into the empty room—an old woman's name—and heard, for the first time since the valley fell silent around him, nothing answer.

Aurel smiled at the sunrise, and began, patiently, to count.
