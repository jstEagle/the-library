## 21. The Door in the Mountain

The shard woke at dawn.

Juniper had been carrying it for six days now—Nim had pressed it into his hands on the morning they left the refugees of the old kingdom, saying it answered to blood, not to her—and all that time it had lain quiet against his chest like a chip of winter, cold and indifferent. But as the first light came slantwise through the pines and touched the eastern face of the mountain the locals called the Sleeping Grandmother, the shard warmed against his sternum with a slow, insistent pulse, like a second heartbeat learning its rhythm.

He stopped mid-stride on the trail and put his hand over his shirt.

"It's awake," he said.

Sera looked back from a dozen paces ahead, her pack already dark with sweat where the straps crossed her shoulders. "Define awake."

"Warm." He worked the shard out from beneath his collar and held it up. It was no bigger than a thumbnail, a splinter of something that might have been glass or might have been bone, and when the light struck it just so, there were lines inside it—hair-fine threads of gold that hadn't been visible yesterday, branching like frost on a windowpane or veins in a leaf. "It's pulling."

"Pulling how?"

Juniper considered the question honestly, because Sera deserved honesty even when he had none of the answers she wanted. "Like a hand around my wrist," he said finally. "Gentle. But certain."

Nim crouched beside a stream at the trail's edge, filling their waterskins, and did not look up. She had been quieter since the ambush in the wheat field—not frightened-quiet, which Juniper knew well enough from his own mirror, but decided-quiet, the stillness of someone who had done arithmetic with her own life and gotten an answer she could live with. Now she capped the last waterskin and said, without rising, "It will pull harder the closer we get. My father made it that way. He wanted his priests to be able to find the door again if the kingdom ever tried to stay lost."

"And you?" Sera asked. "What do *you* want?"

Nim stood and slung the skins over her shoulder. For a moment the light caught her face—the god-king's daughter, though nothing about her suggested divinity except perhaps her patience—and Juniper saw her weigh the question the way a person weighs a stone before throwing it into deep water.

"I want to see what my father is afraid of," she said. "That's what this is, whatever else it is. He didn't march an army across three countries for a door. Doors are cheap. He marched because somewhere behind that door is the one thing in the world that can starve him." She started up the trail, past Sera, past Juniper, not waiting. "Let's go look at it."

* * *

The mountain did not want them there.

Juniper felt it the way he had once felt the moods of graveyards—a pressure in the air, a wrongness in the way sound carried, as though the whole massif were holding its breath. The trail thinned as they climbed, then gave up being a trail at all and became a suggestion: a deer path here, a seam of exposed rock there, a stretch of scree that Sera swore bore the regular spacing of steps before the weather ate them. Twice the shard pulled them toward a wall of deadfall so dense it looked grown deliberately, and twice they found the gap in it only by trusting the pull completely, squeezing through spaces that should not have admitted a child, let alone three adults with packs.

"The land remembers how to hide this place," Sera murmured, the third time it happened. She had her journal out despite the climbing, sketching as she walked in the shorthand of her trade, and her voice had the bright strained quality it got whenever she was excited and furious about it at once. "Look at this drainage—it should run east. Every watershed on this slope runs east, except here, where it bends south for exactly the length of a valley and then bends back. That isn't geology. That's *editing*."

"You're enjoying yourself," Juniper said.

"I'm having the most important day of my life," Sera said, "and I hate that I'm enjoying it, and I'll examine both of those feelings later, preferably over brandy." She tucked the journal away. "Keep walking."

By late afternoon the trees changed. The pines gave way to birch, pale and slender, and then the birch gave way to something Juniper had no name for—trees with bark like grey linen, leaves like small silver coins that made no sound when the wind moved through them. No birds sang here. No insects whined. The silence was total but not empty; it was the silence of a room where people are listening.

And the dead—Juniper reached for them without meaning to, the old reflex, the way a man touches his knife when he hears a noise downstairs—and found nothing. Not the mute terror of the valley after the first miracle, not the deliberate blankness the bones had shown him since his inheritance began waking. Just absence, clean and vast, like reaching into water and finding no water.

"They're not here," he said aloud, and his voice sounded obscene in that hush. "There aren't any dead on this mountain."

"There wouldn't be," Nim said quietly. She had stopped at the head of the line, looking up. "A kingdom that erased itself would have erased its graves first. You don't leave your names in the ground where anyone with your gift—or any gift—could read them back out." She turned to look at him, and something almost gentle moved through her expression. "Your ancestors didn't just hide their city, Juniper. They hid their *deaths*. They took even those away from people like you."

He didn't know why that undid him the way it did. He had spent his whole life among the dead; they were easier than the living, kinder in their needs, honest in their wants. A country that had taken even its dead out of reach—that had denied him even the company of strangers' ghosts on its soil—felt less like a place than like a held breath, a sentence unfinished on purpose.

But the shard burned steady against his chest, patient as a promise, and the pull had become a rope, and the rope led up and around the shoulder of the mountain to a place where the silver-leaved trees simply stopped, cut off in a clean arc as if drawn by a compass.

Beyond the arc lay meadow. Wildflowers he didn't recognize grew waist-high, blue-black blossoms nodding on stems that bent toward the party as they passed, the way sunflowers bend toward light. And beyond the meadow, set into the living face of the cliff itself—

"There," Sera breathed.

It was not hidden. That was the first impossible thing. After a week of trails that unmade themselves and watersheds that lied, Juniper had expected another deception, another door disguised as nothing at all. Instead the gate stood plain as a cathedral front, forty feet of dark stone framed in the mountainside, carved with such depth that the shadows inside the reliefs seemed less like shadow than like actual darkness, preserved and stored. It was sealed—no hinge, no handle, no seam wide enough to slide a knife into—but it was not disguised. It had been left visible the way a letter is left visible inside a locked drawer: meant to be found, only by the right hands.

They crossed the meadow slowly. The flowers turned to follow them, all of them, a silent congregation of blue-black faces.

"Ossuary Vale," Sera whispered. Her hand had gone to the master atlas inside her coat, the stolen thing, the ruin of her, the work of her life. "I've chased ink for eleven years. Eleven years of forged provenance and bribed archivists and one very bad decision I'll answer for someday, all chasing a rumor that appeared on exactly one map in the world, in a hand nobody could date." She laughed, a small cracked sound. "And it's been sitting under a mountain the whole time, growing flowers that watch you walk."

"Don't touch the door yet," Nim said sharply, as Sera quickened. "Not until we understand what it wants."

"It doesn't want anything. It's a door."

"My father built doors too," Nim said. "Trust me. In our family, doors are arguments."

Juniper barely heard them. Because he had stopped, ten paces from the gate, and was looking up at the lintel—at the carving that ran across the top of the great stone frame like a crown laid on a brow.

He knew it.

He knew it the way you know your own hands in the dark. A circle of seven stars, and threaded through them, a river rendered as a single unbroken line that began and ended in the same point, and at the center, where the stars converged, a small stylized object that every Villalobos for four generations had assumed was a decorative knot: the mark his grandmother had burned into the handles of her carving tools, the mark stamped on the family's oldest memorial plaques in the valley churchyard, the mark Juniper himself had carved, unthinking, into the first charm he ever made at eight years old.

His grandmother's crest. His family's crest. Cut forty feet high into the gate of a lost kingdom, in stone older than any record of the name Villalobos.

The vertigo came up through the ground like water through a well shaft. Juniper sat down. He didn't decide to; his legs simply declined further service, and he found himself kneeling in the watching flowers with his palms flat against the cool earth, breathing, while above him the seven stars held their positions in the eternal dark of the carved shadows.

"Juniper." Sera's boots appeared at the edge of his vision. "Hey. Talk to me."

"That's ours." His voice came out strange, flattened, as if heard through a wall. "That's my grandmother's mark. That's on her sewing scissors. That's—" He stopped, restarted. "We're carvers. We've always been carvers. Memorial charms, grave-goods, little things for little griefs. My mother used to say the Villalobos hands were blessed for consoling work. That's all. Consoling work."

"And the royal seal of a nation that erased itself from history is on your grandmother's sewing scissors," Sera said gently.

"Yes."

Sera lowered herself down beside him, cross-legged, her journal forgotten in her lap. Above them both the gate waited. Nim stood apart, arms folded, watching Juniper with an expression he couldn't read—pity, maybe, or recognition, or the particular sorrow of someone watching another person arrive where she herself had arrived years ago and never recovered.

"Say it," Nim said. "Out loud. It helps. Trust me—I've had to say worse things out loud about my family than you're going to say about yours."

Juniper looked up at the crest. The evening light had begun to fail, and in the dusk the carved shadows deepened, and the seven stars seemed less like decoration now and more like a map of something—a constellation, a sigil, a sigil shaped like the one burning under his own skin on his ribs, the birthmark that glowed when touched, the inheritance that had woken whether he willed it or not.

"We weren't farmers who took up carving," he said slowly. Testing each word like rotten floorboards. "We were—what? Court carvers? Bone-priests of the other kind? The kind that *made* things instead of taking them apart?" He shook his head. "My grandmother hummed while she worked. Little songs with no words. I used to think she'd forgotten the lyrics. What if they weren't songs? What if they were—"

*Rites.* He didn't say it. He didn't have to. The word hung in the air between the three of them anyway, and the flowers nodded, and somewhere high above, wind moved through silver leaves that made no sound at all.

* * *

They made camp in the meadow's edge, none of them willing to sleep within arm's reach of the gate but none willing to leave it either. Sera built a fire from fallen silver-leaf branches that burned green and smelled, faintly and impossibly, like bread baking. Nim took first watch without being asked.

Juniper sat closest to the gate, because the shard wanted him close, and because some stubborn farmer's-bones part of him refused to be afraid of his own family's doorstep.

In the firelight he studied the reliefs flanking the crest. Processions of figures climbed the stone on either side—carvers, he realized, watching them properly for the first time. Figures with knives and mallets, figures bending over altars and cradles and coffins alike, and every figure's face was smooth and featureless except for the eyes, which were rendered with terrible precision, wide open, seeing. At the bottom of the left-hand panel, half worn by centuries of weather, a line of script wound like a river. Sera had spent two hours on it with her lantern and pronounced it a dialect of Old Valean that existed in exactly one grammar fragment in a private collection in Vhal Maren—which she had seen once, briefly, before being thrown out of the building.

"But the numbers repeat," she'd said, tapping her notes. "Seven, seven, seven. Seven stars, seven figures in each procession row, seven syllables per line. Whatever this door is, it's built on sevens. And your sigil—" she'd glanced at his ribs, delicately, the way one glances at a wound, "—how many points does it have?"

Seven. Of course it had seven.

Now, in the green firelight, Juniper drew the shard from his shirt and held it up. The golden threads inside had branched further overnight, and in the dark they glowed with their own faint light, and as he watched, the threads shifted—slowly, like roots growing in fast-forward—until they matched, thread for thread, branch for branch, the pattern of the seven stars above the gate.

"It knows the way in," he said softly.

"Of course it does," Nim said from her watch post, not turning around. "It's a piece of the key. My father shattered the key when the Vale closed itself—he kept one piece, the Vale hid the rest, and the pieces have been trying to find each other for a hundred years. Everything in this world that was once whole is still trying." A pause. "Most things shouldn't succeed."

Juniper turned the shard in his fingers. Warm. Patient. Certain as a hand around his wrist.

"Sera," he said. "When we open it—if we open it—what do you actually expect to find?"

Sera looked up from her journal. Firelight moved on her face, and for once there was no ambition in her expression, no hunger, just a tired honesty that looked almost new on her. "Records," she said. "The truth. Why they did it. How a king decides to make his own country stop existing, and convinces an entire people to go along—to scatter, to forget, to plant themselves in valleys and pretend for a hundred years to be simple folk with simple names." She hugged her knees. "Because I've been asking the wrong question my whole career, Juniper. I kept asking *where*. Everyone asks where. Where is Ossuary Vale, where is it on the map, where did it go." She looked past him, at the gate, at the seven stars. "The real question—the question this whole country has been hiding—is *why*. Why does a nation choose to be forgotten? What were they so afraid of being remembered *as*?"

Juniper didn't answer. He didn't have to. They all knew what the Vale had feared. It wore a crown of stone sleep in a ruined temple across three countries and was marching toward them with an army of the grateful devout.

He lay back against the earth and looked up at the gate one last time before sleep took him. The crest caught the last of the green firelight, and for a moment—one long breath of a moment—he would have sworn the carved river-line at the center of the seven stars was moving. Flowing. A hundred years of stillness ending, a current remembering its course, running toward the single point where it began.

The shard pulsed once against his palm, hard, like a heart clearing its throat.

And from behind the sealed door—from the deep carved shadows that were not quite shadow—came a sound so faint he would doubt it by morning, and spend the rest of his life failing to forget it:

Knocking.
