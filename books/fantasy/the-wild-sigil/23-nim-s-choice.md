## 23. Nim's Choice

The temple of her childhood had smelled of cedar smoke and cold stone, of tallow candles and the sweet rot of offerings left too long. The place where the priests cornered her smelled of none of those things. It smelled of salt and iron and old blood soaked into wheat-stubble earth, and Nim thought, absurdly, that her father would have hated it. Aurel loved his incense. He had built a religion out of fragrance, out of the slow curling of smoke toward heaven, as if divinity were something you could breathe.

She crouched in the drainage ditch at the edge of the harvested field, mud to her elbows, the key shard pressed against her sternum where it hung on its cord. Above her, the sky was the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges — evening coming on, the last light pooling in the west like something spilled.

They had been following her for three days. She had known it the way she knew weather: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper coins at the back of the throat. Her father's priests did not track the way mortal hunters tracked. They listened. Every answered prayer was an ear planted in the world, and Nim had spent her whole life walking through fields of those ears, learning which silences to keep.

But tonight they had stopped listening and started walking, and now there were five of them on the ridge above the ditch, their white robes gone grey with road dust, their bone masks catching the dying light.

And at their center, tall and unhurried, stood Vesh.

* * *

Nim had been eight years old when Vesh first taught her to hold a prayer in her hands.

Not speak it — hold it. Any child of the temple could speak prayers; the words were cheap as water. But to hold one, to cup it in your palms like a captured moth and feel its wings beating against your fingers while you decided whether to let it fly — that was craft. That was priesthood.

"Prayer is a knife," Vesh had told her, kneeling so that their masked face was level with her small unmasked one. "Most people use it to cut fruit. Your father uses it to cut throats. Learn the difference, little star, or you will spend your life cutting fruit for a butcher."

They had been kind to her, in the way of the temple — which was to say, precisely, deliberately, with an agenda folded inside every kindness like a letter inside an envelope. Vesh had taught her the old tongues, the grammar of miracles, the hundred small disciplines by which a god's household ran. When Nim wept after her first failed rite, Vesh had sat with her through the whole night without speaking, which was more than her father had ever done. Aurel did not sit with grief. Aurel commissioned monuments to it.

So when Nim fled the temple with the door-key shard burning against her skin, it was not her father's face she saw when she closed her eyes and felt ashamed. It was Vesh's mask, tilted slightly, the way it always tilted when they were about to say something devastating and gentle.

Now that mask looked down at her from the ridge, and the voice that came from behind it was the same voice, warm as banked coals, and that warmth frightened her more than any threat could have.

"Nimaya." Her full name. Her temple name, the one she had shed like a skin at the gates. "Come up out of the ditch, little star. You'll catch your death down there."

"I already caught it," Nim said. "It's called being my father's daughter."

* * *

The priests came down the slope in single file, unhurried, robes whispering against the stubble. Nim stayed low in the ditch and did not run, because running was what prey did and she refused to be prey, and also because she had counted them — five, plus Vesh, six — and calculated the odds of escape with the cold part of her mind that Vesh had trained and that she had never managed to untrain.

The odds were poor. The shard could open doors, but only doors that existed, and the nearest one was a mile back along the road where Juniper and Sera waited in the ruined mill, and if she led six priests there—

No. Not there. Never there.

So she stood up out of the mud, slowly, arms away from her body, and let them surround her in their practiced crescent, and she lifted her chin the way she used to lift it in the temple courts when the bone-priests whispered that the god-king's daughter walked too freely among mortals.

"You've gotten mud in your hair," Vesh observed. They reached out — Nim flinched before she could stop herself — and picked a clod of dried earth from the ends of her braid with two careful fingers, the gesture so familiar it made her chest ache. "You always did get mud in your hair. Your father said you were part river."

"My father says a lot of things." Her voice held. She was proud of that. "He says he loves his children. He has eleven of us, Vesh. Can you name them? Because I can't. I can't even name them anymore. Some of them I haven't seen since I was small. Do you know why?"

Vesh's mask did not move. Behind the five lesser priests, their own masks were blank white ovals with eye-holes like knotholes in a coffin lid.

"He keeps them apart," Nim went on, because the words were coming now and she had learned long ago that once they started you might as well finish. "He keeps all of us apart, because together we're a family, and families have loyalties, and loyalty is the one thing he won't share. Even with himself. Especially with himself."

"The Lord of Answered Prayer keeps his house in order," one of the lesser priests intoned. Nim didn't bother looking at him. The rank-and-file all spoke in that same flattened recitation; they'd had their individual voices sanded off years ago, polished into instruments.

"His name is not for your mouth," Vesh said quietly, and the lesser priest bowed his head.

"There it is," Nim said. "There's the correction. You always were quick with it."

"Nimaya." Vesh stepped closer, close enough that she could smell the temple on them — cedar, yes, faintly, clinging to the robe like memory clinging to a name. "I am not here to correct you. I'm here to offer you back everything."

The word *everything* hung in the cold air between them.

"Reinstatement," Vesh continued, and their voice took on the cadence of liturgy, the rising-falling rhythm Nim had heard ten thousand times in the great hall beneath the mountain. "Your seat at your father's right hand. Your name restored — not Nim the runaway, not the thief who stole from the holy of holies, but Nimaya, first-flame, heir of the between-space. Your sisters and brothers gathered around you again, all eleven, under one roof. And forgiveness, little star. Forgiveness without price. You know your father. You know how rarely that coin is minted."

Nim's hands had gone cold inside her sleeves. Not with fear. With the terrible treacherous ache of homesickness, which is grief's most dishonest cousin.

"And in exchange?" she asked. Her voice cracked on the second word. She hated it. She kept going. "In exchange for what?"

Vesh tilted their head. "You know."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear it in your voice. I want to remember it exactly."

A pause. Somewhere across the darkening field a nightbird started up, three notes, then silence, as though it too were waiting.

"The carter boy," Vesh said. "Juniper Villalobos. The hearer. The last drop of the erased line. Bring him to the mountain, Nimaya. Walk him through the outer gate yourself, with the shard in your hand and your father's blessing on your head, and everything you left behind will be returned to you washed and folded."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about temptation: it does not arrive wearing a monster's face. It arrives wearing your mother's voice, your childhood kitchen, the particular quality of light in a room you thought you'd forgotten. It arrives carrying everything you lost, arranged neatly, priced fairly, and it waits with perfect patience while you do the arithmetic of your own soul.

Nim did the arithmetic.

Eleven siblings. A roof. A name. Forgiveness — actual forgiveness, from a god who forgave the way glaciers moved. She let herself feel the full weight of it, let it press down on her chest, because she had learned that the only honest way to refuse a thing was to first admit how much you wanted it. Anything else was just lying with better posture.

And underneath it all, the other ledger. Juniper, who carved charms for dead children because no one else would listen to them. Sera, who carried guilt the way other people carried knives, always half-drawn. The farmer's widow whose answer had broken her, and who had still pressed bread into their hands at the gate. Small people. Unimportant people, by any measure her father's court would use. People who would not survive one hour of her family's attention.

"People," Nim said slowly, "keep telling me my father needs Juniper alive. Alive, willing, walking through the gate on his own feet. Why? Why does a god need consent?"

Something shifted behind Vesh's mask. It was very slight — a fractional turn of the head, a recalibration. In twenty years of studying that mask, Nim had seen it maybe four times.

"That is above your station," Vesh said.

"No," Nim said. "That's exactly my station. That's the whole point of me. I'm not a priest, Vesh, I'm a *key*. I was born to fit a lock. So tell me what's behind it, or tell me I'm right: the door doesn't open for power. It opens for willingness. My father can move mountains and raise the dead and answer every prayer on a thousand miles of road, but he cannot make one man walk willingly through a doorway. He needs Juniper to *choose* him. And he needs me—" her hand rose, unbidden, to the cord at her throat, to the shard beneath her shirt, hot as a coal even through the cloth, "—because I'm proof it can be done. A daughter who chose. A blood relative who walked back through the door with her own feet. Juniper just has to see me do it."

The field was silent. The five lesser priests had gone very still, the way men go still when they realize the conversation has moved somewhere their training doesn't cover.

Vesh exhaled. Nim heard it — the small human sound through the mask's lower vents, the sound that meant the person underneath was tired, or moved, or afraid.

"You were always too clever for your own good," they said softly. "Yes. That is the shape of it. Your father does not conquer hearts, little star. He *collects* them. Gratitude freely given, worship freely offered, heirs who return with their own consent — these are the only currencies the old door accepts. Armies are ash to it. But a willing heir..." Their voice gentled into something almost tender. Almost. "A willing heir is the master key. And you, Nimaya, were always meant to be turned first."

"So find another heir."

"We found this one. We followed you to him." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat on silk. "And now we are asking you, daughter of the god-king, beloved of your teacher, who taught you every prayer you ever held in your hands — come home. Turn the key. Or stand aside, and we will take what we came for, and you may keep your exile and your mud and your mortal friends' graves to visit."

* * *

Later — in the years after, in whatever life grew out of this night — Nim would return to this moment again and again, turning it over like a stone in her pocket, looking for the seam where the decision actually happened. She never found one. There was no lightning-struck instant, no grand interior speech. There was just Vesh saying *graves to visit*, plural, casual, already counting Juniper and Sera among the dead because in Vesh's arithmetic they had always been dead, merely not yet buried.

And there was the shard against her sternum, warmer than her skin, warmer than blood.

Her father had made eleven children. He had made them the way he made everything — as instruments, as keys, as answers to questions no one else had asked. He had never once made anything just to love it.

Juniper carved charms out of bones for drowned children who couldn't pay him. That was the whole of it. That was the entire man. And standing in the freezing mud with six servants of a living god closing around her like a fist, Nim understood that she had already chosen, weeks ago, on the night she climbed out her window with a stolen shard and no plan. Everything since had been the choosing, drawn out stroke by stroke, the way Juniper drew a sigil — patiently, in order, until the cut was deep enough to be permanent.

"I have a counteroffer," Nim said.

Vesh waited.

"You leave here tonight. All of you. You go back to my father and you tell him his daughter said—" and here her voice finally broke, and she let it, because some things should be said broken, "—said *no*. Again. Louder. And you tell him I'll say it every day for the rest of my life, and that every day he sends someone to change my mind, I will bury whoever he sent, and I am very good at burying things, Vesh. I learned from watching my family."

The nightbird sang its three notes again. One of the lesser priests began to lift his hands, fingers already folding into the shapes of a holding-prayer, and Vesh raised one finger and he froze.

"You cannot kill us, little star," Vesh said, and there was real sorrow in it now, sorrow and something older. "We are answered men. Every one of us prayed at the edge of death and was pulled back by your father's hand. Our lives are his loans. You know what that means. You grew up knowing what that means."

"I know," Nim said. "I know exactly what that means."

She pulled the cord over her head.

The shard came out into the moonlight and it blazed — not reflected light but its own, a thin white line like a crack in the world, like the first split of an egg. The lesser priests recoiled as one, robes snapping in a wind that wasn't blowing. Even Vesh stepped back a half-pace, and Nim watched her teacher's composure crack down the middle, and felt no triumph at all, only a vast and terrible clarity.

"It means your lives aren't yours," she said. "Which means you can't risk them. Which means when I point a piece of the door itself at your hearts, you will flinch, because dying means going back to him unfinished, and none of you — not one — trusts him with your unfinished selves. I grew up watching you flinch, Vesh. I learned that too."

She turned the shard in her hand. It hummed. The stubble around her whitened with frost that had nothing to do with cold.

"But you're right about one thing. I can't kill you. He'd just re-answer you. He'd pull you back a third time, a fourth, until you stopped flinching, and then I'd have made monsters instead of enemies." Her grip tightened. The hum deepened. "So I won't kill you."

Then she looked at Vesh — only Vesh — and took one step forward, and drove the edge of the shard, that sliver of the space-between-worlds, across the joining of her teacher's mask.

Not the flesh. The *answer*.

The sound Vesh made was not a scream. It was quieter than a scream and worse: a single note of pure relief, as though a debt decades overdue had suddenly been cancelled, as though a hand that had gripped their heart for twenty years had opened. The white mask fell into the stubble and lay there, blank side up, filling with frost. And Vesh — Vesh folded down onto their knees in the frozen wheat like a puppet with its strings cut, and breathed, and breathed again, each breath their own, and looked up at Nim with bare human eyes gone wide and wet and astonished.

"Oh," they whispered. "Oh, little star. What did you—"

"I freed you," Nim said. Her voice shook. She let it. "From him. Permanently. The door cuts both ways, Vesh. It always did. You just never taught me that part."

Behind them, the five lesser priests stood motionless in their coffin-lid masks, and Nim understood with perfect certainty that they were screaming inside, every one, screaming and unable to move, because their god's attention had snapped toward this field like a hooked fish-line going taut, and they were waiting — all of them, all at once — to see whether their faith or their terror was stronger.

Vesh got slowly, painfully to their feet. Bare-faced. Old, she saw, truly old, the age the mask had hidden, the age her father's borrowed years had papered over. They looked at her the way she imagined a man looks at the shore after drowning.

"Go," Vesh rasped. "Take your friends and go, and don't stop, and don't sleep twice in one bed, because when he gathers himself he will not send five." They bent, stiffly, and picked up their fallen mask, and turned it over in their hands like a relic of a stranger. "He will send the Answered. Thousands. He will come himself."

"I know," Nim said.

"He will offer them everything, and they will take it, and you cannot save people from being given things."

"I know," Nim said again, and put the shard back against her heart, where it burned like a promise. "But I know some people who can carve."

She ran — across the frosted field, through the stubble, toward the ruined mill and the lamplight in its broken window and the two impossible, ordinary people waiting inside — and behind her she heard Vesh begin, in a cracked and newly-owned voice, to pray.

For the first time in a century, it was not addressed to her father.

It was addressed to her.
