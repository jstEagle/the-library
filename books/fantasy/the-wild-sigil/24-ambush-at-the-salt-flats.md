## 24. Ambush at the Salt Flats

The salt flats began where the wheat country ended, as if some ancient flood had drowned the land and then changed its mind about the water but kept the drowning. White stretched to every horizon, cracked into hexagonal plates like the shell of an enormous sleeping thing, and the sun came off it in a glare that made Sera Quill's eyes water even through smoked-glass spectacles. There was nothing to triangulate from. Nothing to fix a position against. For a cartographer, it was a kind of blasphemy—a place that refused to be measured.

For someone being hunted, she thought, shading her eyes and scanning the flat white distance, it might be perfect. Or it might be a killing floor with nowhere to hide.

"You keep looking at the horizon like it owes you money," Nim said, walking at her left with the hood of her traveling cloak thrown back. Her silver-white hair—her father's hair, though she'd stopped apologizing for it somewhere around the Ash Market—caught the light and threw it back doubled.

"It does," Sera said. "It always does."

Behind them, Juniper trudged in silence, his pack riding high on his shoulders to keep it off the burn scar on his palm. He had not spoken much since the farmhouse. Since the dying mother. Sera understood why. She'd spent three years after the refugee column learning how silence could be a kind of penance, and she recognized the posture of a person carrying something too heavy to set down and too shameful to show.

She adjusted the strap of her satchel—the master atlas inside, wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than any book had a right to be—and looked west again.

The key shard rode in a leather case against Juniper's chest. That was the whole arithmetic of their journey now: get the shard to the mountain, get Juniper to the door, get there before Aurel's army finished eating its way across the countryside like a fire given a taste for worship. Three people. One shard. A thousand miles of country that rearranged itself when it felt like it.

And behind them—Sera turned, squinting back along the way they'd come—a smudge of dark on the white flats, far off, moving.

Her stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

"Nim."

"I see them." All the levity had gone out of the girl's voice. She was standing very still, head tilted, the way she did when she was listening to something no one else could hear. "Bone-priests. Six—no." Her jaw tightened. "Nine. And they're not following us anymore. They're *angling*. They know where we're going."

"The pass," Sera said.

The only way across the flats' western rim was a gap in the uplifted salt-and-stone ridge the locals called the Needle's Eye—a defile barely wide enough for two carts, the sole breach in forty miles of cliffs. Sera had mapped it yesterday from a knoll with her heart hammering with something dangerously close to joy, because finding a pass still gave her the old clean thrill no exile or disgrace had ever managed to dull. Every map was full of doors. You just had to be the one who knew where they stood.

Now the door was going to matter very much indeed.

"They'll beat us there if we keep this pace," she said, already doing the geometry: their position, the priests', the curve of the ridge, the angle of the sun. "We cut northwest. Straight line. The crust will hold if we spread our weight—there's brine under the plates in the low spots, you can go through up to your knee, but it'll hold if—"

"Seraphina." Juniper had caught up. His voice was quiet, the valley voice, the one that made people tell him true things whether they meant to or not. "You're doing the thing with your hands again. The measuring thing."

"Because measuring is what keeps us alive right now."

"And what are the numbers telling you?"

Sera hated him, briefly and completely, for asking the honest question.

"That we arrive second," she said. "By perhaps ten minutes. Perhaps fifteen."

Nim swore in a language Sera didn't know, old and guttural, the liturgical tongue of a temple Nim had run from rather than inherited. Then she stopped swearing and went pale in a new way. "They won't fight us at the pass," she said. "They'll wait for us there. My father's priests don't chase. They *receive*. It's more humiliating for the quarry, he used to say—" She shut her mouth as if she'd bitten down on something.

"Then we don't go to the pass," Juniper said.

"There is no other way through the ridge," Sera said. "I told you. Forty miles of cliff. I checked twice."

"Show me."

* * *

She knelt on the salt and unrolled her field chart, weighting the corners with chunks of crust. The wind coming off the flats was dry and mineral-sharp; it kept trying to lift the paper's edges like fingers testing a seal. Sera traced the ridge line with a fingertip, and Juniper crouched beside her, close enough that she could smell road dust and bone-meal and, faintly, the resin he still rubbed on his hands out of habit, though his hands had been empty of carvings since the wheat field.

"The Eye here," she said. "Cliffs north of it, cliffs south. But look—" her finger moved south along the ridge, to a place where her own hachured lines grew hesitant, uncertain, drawn from a distance in bad light. "Here the rock shows broken on my sketch. Talus. Fallen stone, piled against the face. If it's climbable, it's slow, but it's *unwatched*, because no one climbs it. The priests will be watching the Eye because the Eye is where maps say people go."

"And if your sketch is wrong?"

"Then we die on a cliff face, and I die embarrassed, which I have always assumed would be my end anyway." She heard her own flippancy and regretted it—not because it was untrue but because Juniper flinched. Since the farmhouse, since the mother whose answer had broken her, he took every mention of death like a debt coming due.

"We split," Nim said suddenly.

They both looked at her.

"One group goes to the Eye, loud, visible, obvious. The other goes to the talus. They can't cover both. They're nine—they'll split maybe four and five, and five men defending a scree slope is harder than four men holding a pass." She was talking fast, her hands moving, and Sera saw the temple-trained tactician underneath the runaway, the daughter who had sat at a god-king's war table before she was tall enough to see over it. "But someone has to be the loud one. Someone has to make them believe."

Silence, except wind.

"No," Juniper said.

"Someone has to—"

"I said no." It wasn't loud. It was worse than loud—it was final, the tone of a man who had buried a child's charm and watched a corpse crumble and burned a wheat field black, a man who knew exactly what things cost because everything he touched seemed to pay the bill. "Nobody volunteers to be bait. Not after—" He stopped. Looked at his scarred palm. "Not after everything."

"Juniper." Sera kept her voice level, cartographer-calm, the voice she used for arguing with generals about borders. "He's right that nobody *volunteers*." She rolled up the chart. "So let's not volunteer. Let's decide, properly, with all the information. Nim's plan works only if the decoy force can survive contact long enough for us to clear the ridge. Which means the decoy needs to be fast, needs to be able to read ground, and needs to be someone the priests want alive or won't bother killing." She met Nim's eyes. "You're the god's daughter. They need you alive. You're the decoy."

"No," Juniper said again, sharper.

"She's the *only one it works for*—"

"I'm the one they want," Juniper said quietly. "Me and the shard. That's the whole hunt. Everything else—everything—is noise to them." He looked between the two of them, and Sera watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done, arriving at the sum she'd been circling since she first saw the dark smudge on the white. "Which means the decoy should carry something they want more than catching me. Something that makes them stop."

"Don't," Nim whispered.

"The shard stays with me. But the *case*—" He pulled the worn leather case from inside his shirt, and for a moment Sera saw the shape of it, the sigil-pressed leather, the thing every bone-priest within fifty miles could probably smell. "The case goes with whoever draws them away. Empty."

"That's insane," Nim said. "If they catch the decoy and open it—"

"Then the decoy had better not get caught." Juniper's eyes found Sera. And there it was, in his quiet face, the terrible gentle certainty: *you're the fastest reader of ground we have. You drew the route. You know every fold of this white hell better than any of us.* 

She had known, from the moment she'd seen the smudge, whose job this was going to be. Cartographers are the ones who walk ahead. It was practically doctrine.

"All right," Sera said.

"*No*—" both of them said, and she held up a hand.

"All right, I'll take the case and the Eye, and I'll be *loud*—but we do it my way, which means we do it now, while the light's still high enough to hide movement in the glare, and it means you two climb that talus like your lives depend on being boring." She stood, brushing salt from her knees, and found that her hands were steady. Interesting, she thought. After the column, after the atlas, after all these miles—you'd think they'd shake by now.

Juniper stepped close. Close enough that only she could hear.

"Sera Quill," he said, "if you die out there I will carve your name into every boundary stone between here and the sea, and I will *haunt myself* doing it, so don't."

"That may be the least romantic proposal I've ever received."

"It wasn't—" Color climbed his neck. "That's not—I meant—"

"I know what you meant." She took the empty case from him. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. She tucked it inside her coat where the real one usually rode, against her heartbeat, and allowed herself—one indulgence, brief as a blink—to press her forehead to his. Salt wind. Bone-meal. Warmth. "Go climb your cliff, heir of nothing. I have a pass to make famous."

* * *

The flats rang under her boots like the inside of a bell.

Sera ran the way she'd learned to run in the royal survey corps, before the fall, before the exile: weight forward, breath metered, eyes reading ground three strides ahead. The salt crust held, mostly. Twice it didn't, and brine flooded cold around her shins, soaking her trousers to the knee, and she hauled herself out and kept running because stopping was the same as dying out here.

Behind her, half a mile back and closing, the nine dark figures resolved slowly into individuals. Bone-priests moved strangely—smooth, gliding, their masks of polished femur-plate throwing back the sun in little flashes of dead white. They weren't running. They were *flowing*, the way water flows downhill, patient as gravity, certain of the outcome.

She crested a low swell of crust and saw the Eye ahead: the notch in the ridge, narrow and shadowed, and in front of it—four priests, arranged in a loose crescent, waiting. Receiving. Exactly as Nim had promised.

Sera slowed. Walked. Made herself visible, unmistakable, striding toward the pass with the false case prominent against her chest, and raised one arm in the old survey-corps signal for *parley*, which no priest alive had any reason to honor and every reason to find interesting.

"Hearers!" she called, and her voice cracked flat across the salt and came back at her doubled off the cliff walls. "Hearers of the Risen King! I bring you the vessel! I bring you the case of the key!"

Four bone masks turned toward her in unison. Behind them, far off across the white, she saw the other five pause, tilt, and begin to flow toward the Eye as well—all nine converging, which was either disaster or the best news of her life depending entirely on what happened in the next ninety seconds.

She needed ninety seconds. She'd timed it. From the base of the Eye, the talus slope was invisible behind the shoulder of the ridge; Juniper and Nim needed ninety seconds to reach it and start climbing, out of every line of sight.

Eighty-five seconds, then, accounting for sloth.

The priests came to meet her. Up close they were worse—robes of layered parchment inscribed with prayers gone brown, masks featureless except for two drilled eye-holes, and beneath the hems, feet that didn't quite seem to touch the salt. The lead priest stopped ten paces away and inclined its head with horrible courtesy.

"Little mapmaker." The voice was many voices, a choir compressed into one throat. "We smelled the king's blood on this road for three days. And here you come carrying it to us like a gift." A gauntleted hand extended. "The case."

"First," Sera said, "the toll."

The mask tilted.

"Every crossing has a toll," she said, and her heart was slamming but her voice came out with the bored authority of a customs officer, because she had once argued down a border lord with nothing but a straight face and a forged seal, and fear was just another border. "Information for passage. Ask your master—he loves bargains. The heir you seek isn't on this road. He went north, three days ago, toward the fen country, paying pilgrims to carry the shard by river while he walked false trails. You've been chasing his shadow." She tapped the case. "This holds proof. Names of the paid pilgrims. Their routes. Your master will want it. Your master will want to know who wasted his priests' time."

Lies. Beautiful lies, built on a foundation of truth—because the priests *had* been chasing shadows, hadn't they, chasing a man who'd spent his whole life learning to be no one. The best forgeries were always mostly true. Sera had staked her life on that principle more than once.

The choir-voice was silent a long moment. Wind moved the parchment robes with a sound like turning pages.

"The case," the priest said again, softer. "Open it. Show us."

And there it was—the flaw in her architecture, the crack she'd known was there from the beginning and built the whole lie across anyway. Because she couldn't open the case. The case was empty. And an empty case carried openly was worthless as a decoy, which meant the entire plan rested on one final, simple transaction:

They had to take it from her.

"Naturally," Sera said, and smiled, and stepped backward onto the salt, and ran.

The shriek that rose behind her wasn't human. It was the sound of nine throats discovering fury, and the salt rang with the impact of pursuing feet, and Sera Quill ran west along the base of the ridge with her lungs full of fire and her mind cool as well-water, counting. *Sixty. Fifty-nine.* She knew this ground. She'd read it from the knoll yesterday, every fold and fault, and now she spent that knowledge like coin: cutting behind a fin of uplifted rock to break line of sight, splashing through a brine pool to kill her scent-trail, doubling along a seam where the crust sang hollow and wouldn't hold a heavy footfall. *Forty.* The priests were faster in the open but she was smarter in the broken ground, and broken ground was the only kind she'd ever loved. *Thirty.* The shoulder of the ridge loomed ahead, the talus hidden beyond it, and she dared, for one blazing instant, to believe—

A priest stepped out from behind the rock fin.

Not flowing. Standing. Waiting. One of the four from the crescent, dispatched early, patient as gravity, and Sera understood in a single lurching heartbeat that they had never believed her. That gods' servants did not bargain with customs officers. That the whole parley had been theater on both sides, and hers had simply run out of stage first.

She turned. Two more priests blocked the way she'd come, having cut the seam while she circled. The fourth came over the rock fin behind the first, unhurried, and the crescent closed.

"Well," Sera said, breathing hard, backing until the cliff wall touched her spine. "Worth a try."

The lead priest extended its hand again. "The case, little mapmaker. And then the name of the man who sent you. And then—" the mask leaned close, and through the drilled eyeholes she saw darkness, and in the darkness, something vast turning over in its sleep, "—perhaps your master the king will let you live to draw maps of his new world. He values useful hands."

Useful hands. She thought of her atlas, wrapped in oilcloth in Juniper's pack now—no, wait, she'd kept it, she'd never surrendered it, it was against her back under her coat, the stolen master atlas with Ossuary Vale inked in a dead king's hand, the work of her ruined life. Useful hands. She thought of the refugee column, of the sixty-three names she'd traded for funding, of every entry in every ledger of her guilt.

"I'll tell you what I told the last man who wanted to buy me," Sera said.

The priest waited.

"Nothing," she said, "because I sold *him* instead."

And she threw the empty case high into the air, over their heads, into the blinding white glare of the noon sun—where no bone mask could track it—and while four skulls tilted upward in perfect stupid unison, she pulled the survey knife from her boot and drove it into the thigh of the nearest priest and *ran*.

The shriek behind her was different this time. Real. Angry. The salt rang. Something whistled past her ear—a thrown blade of yellowed bone—and she felt it score her scalp hot as a brand before she registered the throw at all. She didn't stop. *Twenty seconds.* The shoulder of the ridge. The talus beyond. If they'd made it up, if Juniper had listened, if—

She rounded the shoulder and the talus slope rose before her, tumbled grey stone against the cliff, and it was empty. No Juniper. No Nim. Either they were above, hidden among the boulders—or they'd never come.

Movement flickered high on the slope. A hand. Then another figure, hauling itself over a lip of stone, and a flash of silver-white hair.

*Nim.*

Sera laughed, a wild cracked sound, and turned to follow them up—and the bone blade took her in the side.

There was no pain at first. That was the strange mercy of it. Just a massive blow, like falling against a door, and then her legs were wrong, refusing, and the salt came up and met her face with the taste of blood and mineral. She rolled onto her back because she would not—she absolutely *refused*—to die looking at dirt. Above her the sky was enormous and white-rimmed and utterly indifferent, and against it stood three bone-priests, and one of them bent to pick up the empty case from where it had fallen short, thirty paces off across the salt.

Empty. They'd find it empty. They'd know. And they'd tear this ridge apart looking for the shard, and Juniper and Nim were forty feet up a scree slope in plain—

"No," Sera said aloud. It came out as blood.

The priests turned back toward her. Toward the woman bleeding out at the base of the only other route through the ridge. And Sera Quill, disgraced cartographer, seller of columns, keeper of guilty ledgers, did the last piece of mapping she would ever do: she reached under her coat, drew out the master atlas—the true one, the irreplaceable one, Ossuary Vale inked in a dead king's hand—and held it up in both shaking hands where every mask on the slope could see it.

"This," she called, and her voice broke in the middle and she made it a laugh instead, "is what you actually want! The location of the Vale! The true chart! Your master's been hunting it for a century!" Blood filled her mouth; she spat it gloriously down her chin. "Come and take it!"

They came. Of course they came. Greed was greed, even in the devout.

And Sera, alone at the foot of the ridge with her life pouring warm into the salt, began to crawl—slowly, agonizingly, away from the talus, away from Juniper and Nim, dragging her bright red trail across the white flats in a line any child could follow, leading them, leading them, leading them away from the door.

*Every map,* she thought, with the strange floating clarity that was either blood loss or grace, *is a record of what it cost to cross.* 

The sky tilted. The priests' footsteps crunched closer, patient as gravity.

High on the slope, small as a grain of salt, she saw Juniper's face appear over the rocks—saw him see her—saw his mouth open on a scream that never reached her, because the world had begun, gently, to fold shut like an atlas closing, and the last thing Sera Quill thought was that she hoped, she *hoped*, someone would remember to write her down.
