## 26. Bargaining with a Minor God

The river came to them before they found it.

Juniper had heard it first—no, that was wrong now. He had *felt* it first: a low thrumming in the ground beneath the farmhouse floorboards, like a pulse too slow for any living heart. The water was somewhere down the slope beyond the wheat field where the priests had died in their circle of blackened stalks, and it did not sound like water. It sounded like something waiting.

Sera lay in the bed they had carried her to, in the farmer's own room, and her breathing had gone thin. Not labored—that would have been better, almost. Labored breathing fought. This was the breathing of someone who had decided, somewhere below thought, that fighting was no longer worth the trouble.

The wound ran from her hip to her ribs, and though Nim had stitched it with the farmwife's curved needle and Juniper had packed it with yarrow and salt and every herb the valley dead had ever taught him through his hands, the blood kept coming back. It came back patient. It came back like something with all the time in the world.

"She's cold," Nim said. She sat on the far side of the bed, holding Sera's wrist the way one holds a bird—not gripping, just witnessing its weight. "She won't say she is, but she is."

"She'd make a joke about that," Juniper said. His voice came out strange, flattened, as if someone else were using his mouth.

"She already did." Nim looked up at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and young. "Something about how at least this way she wouldn't have to finish drawing the coast of Vell. She said it twice. She only says things twice when—"

"I know."

He knew what things meant now without being told. That was another gift he could have done without.

* * *

The farmer, whose name was Aldous and whose hands smelled permanently of pig and pipe smoke, met him in the yard as the sun went down behind the western ridge. The old man had buried his mother two days after the ambush—Juniper had sat with her at the end, held her hand, told her nothing because there was nothing left in him that the dead could reach—and now he stood looking at the slope toward the river with the expression of a man watching weather he didn't trust.

"You're thinking about going down there," Aldous said.

"Yes."

"Then you've heard what I heard growing up." The farmer spat, but without conviction. "My grandfather called it the Grey Wife. Said it lived in the bend below the falls. Said it took a tithe once a generation—a child, sometimes, or a bride on her way to be married, or just a man who wandered too close in flood season. Said you could trade with it if you were desperate enough. But my grandfather also said," and here his voice dropped, not out of reverence but out of the particular shame country people feel when repeating superstition aloud, "that everything it gives you, it takes back double. In something."

"In memory," Juniper said.

Aldous studied him. "You've dealt with gods before."

"No." Juniper looked at his own hands. They were steady, which seemed obscene. "I've carried the dead for twenty years. I've never once asked anything of the living powers. I always thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I always thought staying small would keep me safe from having to choose."

"And now?"

"Now she's dying upstairs, and I'm done choosing safety."

The old man nodded slowly, and then, in a gesture that undid Juniper more than any prayer ever had, he pressed his own worn knife into Juniper's belt—the knife he'd used to castrate pigs and cut hay twine and carve his late wife's name into the kitchen table. "For whatever you need it for," Aldous said. "Gods respect iron. My grandfather swore by it."

It wasn't iron. It was steel with a bone handle, and Juniper recognized the bone—it was sheep, old sheep, and faintly, so faintly he might have imagined it, the handle whispered a word of greeting in a voice like dry grass.

Even now. Even here. Even with everything going silent inside him, some small stubborn remnant still listened.

He thanked Aldous, and walked down toward the river.

* * *

The bend below the falls was exactly the kind of place a minor god would live: beautiful in an unremarkable way, overlooked, half-forgotten. Willows leaned over dark green water. The current moved slow and deep around the curve, folding over itself in long silver muscles. Dragonflies stitched the air above the reeds. If you didn't know better, you'd think it was peaceful.

Juniper knelt at the bank. He took out Aldous's knife, and then, because he had spent his whole life learning the etiquette of the dead and suspected the living powers kept similar customs, he did not speak first. He waited.

The river waited longer.

The sun sank. The dragonflies went to bed. Somewhere upstream a nightbird started its one-note argument with the dark, and the water turned from green to grey to the color of old pewter, and still Juniper knelt, and still the river did not speak, and he understood that this too was part of the price—that the god wanted him to feel time passing, wanted him to spend an hour of his kneeling on top of whatever else he'd pay.

When the voice finally came, it did not come from the water. It came from the willow roots, from the mud, from the wet earth under his knees—from everywhere at once, in a toneless murmur like many women speaking slightly out of sync with themselves.

*Little carver.*

The words arrived not in his ears but in his bones, which should have felt familiar and instead felt violated. He had spent his life letting the dead speak through him. This thing simply spoke *into* him, uninvited, the way cold comes through a wall.

"You know who I am," he said.

*I know your line.* A ripple moved across the whole surface of the bend at once, though no wind touched it. *I remember when your people built the first door. I remember when they sealed it. I drank the flood-year your great-great-grandmother drowned the lower fields to starve the priests of supplies. I am older than your grief, heir-of-nothing, and I will outlast it. What do you want?*

"The woman upstairs. She's dying of a wound no medicine can close."

*And you want me to close it.*

"I want her to live."

*Mm.* The river made a sound like laughter heard underwater. *You are the first mortal to kneel at my bank in forty-one years, and you do not ask for gold, or victory, or vengeance against the god-king who hunts you. You ask for one cartographer with bad manners and a stolen book.*

"Yes."

*Why?*

The question hung there, and Juniper found, to his surprise, that he had an answer ready—not a noble one, not one polished for telling, but true.

"Because she drew maps of places that chose to disappear, and she never gave up on them even when everyone said she was mad. Because she sold something terrible once and has hated herself cleanly ever since, which is harder than hating yourself dirty. Because when the wheat died around us she grabbed my hand and she didn't pray and she didn't blame me. She just held on." He swallowed. "Because I love her, and I haven't told her yet, and I would like the chance to tell her badly."

The silence that followed was long enough that he wondered if the god had left. Then:

*Love. Hm. That is the most expensive currency there is, little carver, and you have offered it freely, which means you don't understand what you've done.* The water darkened further. *I do not take coin. I do not take blood—I have enough blood in my banks to fill a hundred springs. I take memory. Three years. Chosen by me, not you. And you will not know which three until they are gone.*

Juniper's stomach dropped, but he made himself think it through the way Sera would—methodically, coldly, mapping the terrain. "Three years out of thirty-two. I can survive losing any three."

*Can you?* The murmur turned almost gentle, which was worse. *Your years are not evenly weighted, heir-of-nothing. Some of them hold more than others. And I am a greedy little god, as all minor gods must be, and I will take the ones that hurt the most to lose. That is my nature. The river does not carry the light stones downstream. It carries the heavy ones.*

"And if I refuse?"

*Then the cartographer dies, and you go on carrying everything.* A pause. *I find mortals rarely refuse. Memory is the one thing they think they can spare, because they cannot see it leaving.*

Juniper closed his eyes. He thought of his grandmother—Mamá Rosa, who had taught him the first knife-stroke, who had hummed while she worked, whose hands had been so spotted and strong. He thought of the smell of her kitchen. He thought of the day she died, and the last thing she'd said to him, and whether those things lived in the same year, and whether he could bear—

No. He couldn't calculate it. That was the trap, and the god was right: he could not see what memory weighed until it was gone.

"Do we shake on it?" he asked. "Is there a ritual?"

*There is the river,* said the Grey Wife, and the bend rose up and took him.

* * *

The water was warm.

That was the worst part, Juniper thought later—or thought in the moment, or perhaps had thought once and no longer remembered thinking. The water was warm as bathwater, warm as a body, and it poured into his open mouth and his open eyes and it did not drown him, it *read* him. It moved through his skull like fingers through a drawer of letters, unhurried, thorough, occasionally pausing on a page.

He saw flashes—his father's funeral, rain on the coffin; the first charm he ever carved, a lopsided bird for a neighbor's dead dog; Sera asleep in the cart with her atlas open on her chest; Nim laughing for the first time on the road, a startled bark of a laugh; his grandmother's hands over his small ones guiding the blade—

The drawer opened. The drawer emptied. He felt pages lift out of him one by one, and each departure left a hole shaped exactly like itself, and the holes did not hurt, which frightened him more than pain would have. Pain would have meant the memories were still partly his. These went clean, surgical, like teeth pulled under good anesthetic.

*Three years,* said the god, satisfied, somewhere in the water that was inside his head. *Paid in full.*

Then the river set him gently on the bank, face-up among the willow roots, and withdrew, and the night sky was enormous and indifferent above him, and Juniper Villalobos lay dripping and whole and missing, and tried to work out what was gone.

* * *

He climbed back to the farmhouse on legs that shook, and Nim met him at the door with a lamp held high and terror written across her face.

"It stopped," she said before he could speak. "The bleeding. Ten minutes ago it just—stopped. The wound's closed. It's closed like it's been healing for weeks. What did you *do*, Juniper?"

"Traded," he said. His voice sounded distant to himself, a voice heard through a wall. "Is she awake?"

"Asleep. Real sleep. Her color's come back." Nim searched his face with the sharp, frightened attention of someone who has learned that adults lie about costs. "What did it take?"

"I don't know yet."

"That's horrible."

"Yes."

"Juniper—" She hesitated, and he watched her decide to say it anyway, because Nim always decided to say it anyway. "Was it worth it?"

He thought about the answer honestly, turning it over in the new hollow spaces of his mind. "Ask me again in ten years," he said, "if I still know why I went down to the river."

* * *

Sera woke near dawn.

Juniper was sitting beside the bed—he hadn't slept, couldn't have, was afraid to dream and find out what was missing—and when her eyes opened, gray and clear and furious with life, something in his chest cracked open so wide he nearly made a sound.

"You look terrible," she rasped.

"You died yesterday. Roughly."

"Did I?" She flexed her fingers under the blanket, testing, marveling. "Huh. Feels like I just overslept." Then her gaze sharpened, the mapmaker reading unfamiliar terrain. "Juniper. What did you pay?"

"A fair price."

"To a river god? There's no such thing." She struggled up onto one elbow, wincing, refusing help. "What did it take?"

"Three years. I don't know which."

Her face did something complicated—horror and tenderness and guilt all trying to occupy the same features at once. "You absolute fool," she said softly, which in Sera's language was very nearly a declaration of love, and he held onto that.

"There's something else," he admitted. "There's a hole. I can feel it. Right here—" he touched his sternum "—like a tooth gone. Something important was in that year and I can't even miss it properly, because missing it would require remembering it."

"What year?"

"I don't—"

And then, without warning, he did.

Not the memory. Just the shape of its absence, rising up suddenly like a fish surfacing in dark water: a year, his nineteenth, and in it a kitchen that smelled of cedar oil and bread, and a woman humming off-key, and hands spotted with age closing over his, teaching him the first stroke—

He knew the year contained her. He knew the kitchen had been hers. He knew, with the terrible secondhand certainty of a man reading his own diary, that Mamá Rosa's face had lived there.

He could not summon it. He reached for it the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and found only smooth blankness, a face-shaped window with fogged glass, and beyond it nothing—not darkness, not emptiness, just *absence*, the specific absence of something that had once been vivid and beloved and daily.

His grandmother had raised him. He knew that fact the way he knew his own name. He loved her—he could feel the love itself still lodged in him, intact, orphaned, pointing at nothing.

"Grief," he said aloud, wondering. "That's what this is. Grieving someone I can't remember grieving for." He laughed, and it came out broken. "That clever little god. She said she'd take the years that hurt the most to lose. She wasn't lying."

Sera's hand found his. Her grip was weak but certain. "Tell me about her," she said. "Everything you still have. All of it, right now, while you can say it. We'll keep it between us. If the river wants your memory, it'll have to get past two of us."

So he talked. He talked about the cedar oil and the humming and the spotted hands, about the lopsided bird charm, about the way she'd said his name—with the soft J, the rolling R, the way nobody else in the valley bothered to learn—and Sera wrote it all down in the margins of her atlas, in the blank spaces past the edges of the known world, and dawn came up gray through the window, and outside, very far away, the land began to tremble.

Nim appeared in the doorway, white-faced, still dressed, travel-bag over her shoulder.

"The roads," she said. "They've stopped moving—but something else is coming. I can feel my father in my teeth. Juniper—he's crossed the borderlands. He knows where we are now. He's felt what you paid, and he thinks—" her voice caught "—he thinks a man who'd trade his own past for love is either the easiest mark in the world, or the most dangerous. He's coming to find out which."

Juniper stood. His hands were steady. Inside him, in the hollow where his grandmother's face had been, something cold and clear had begun, quietly, to harden into resolve.

"Then we'd better be gone before he arrives," he said, "and this time, we run toward him."

Sera looked up from her atlas. "Toward?"

"He expects us running. Let's disappoint him."
