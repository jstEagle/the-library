## 28. Refugees of the Old Kingdom

Nim smelled the village before she saw it—woodsmoke and wet wool and something older underneath, a scent like stone that had been wept on for a hundred years.

They had been walking since dawn through a country that did not want to be walked through. The road kept forgetting itself, doubling back into alder thickets, dissolving into sheep tracks, then reforming again as if embarrassed by its own hesitation. Sera would have hated it. Sera was three days behind them now, laid in the back of a salt-wagon with a wound Nim refused to describe even to herself, and Juniper had not spoken more than twenty words since they'd left her there.

"You're limping," Juniper said. It was the first thing he'd said in an hour.

"I'm walking *efficiently*."

"You're favoring your left leg."

"And you're favoring your grief," Nim said, before she could stop herself. She heard the cruelty of it land between them, and she didn't take it back. That was the thing about being Aurel's daughter: she knew exactly where the soft places were in people, because she'd been raised in a house whose whole architecture was soft places, worn smooth by ten thousand answered prayers. Her father never struck anyone. He simply found what they loved and offered it back to them at interest.

Juniper said nothing. He just shifted his pack higher on his shoulder and kept walking, and after a while his hand found hers—not holding, exactly. Resting. Like a bird deciding whether to land.

That was when she smelled the woodsmoke.

It came out of a fold in the hills so deep that from twenty paces away the valley looked empty—a shallow green dish with a stream running through it, a few goats, some terraced gardens cut into the slope like old scars. But there were houses down there, low houses built into the hillside itself, their roofs turfed over so thoroughly that from above they must have looked like nothing at all. Like meadow. Like absence.

"Someone lives here," Juniper murmured, "and doesn't want to be seen."

"Then we should knock very politely," Nim said.

The first door opened before either of them reached it. An old woman stood in the frame, small and square as a loaf, with hands that told a long story of dirt and wool and work. She looked at them both—at Juniper's road-worn coat, at Nim's face, which she had learned long ago to expect reactions to—and Nim braced herself for the usual flinch, the usual *your eyes*, the usual fear dressed up as wonder.

The woman only said: "You'll be hungry. Come in before the light sees you."

*Before the light sees you.* Not before dark. Before the light.

Nim's skin prickled. She knew that grammar. Her father's priests spoke that way when they talked about the years of silence—the century when the gods went quiet and the faithful learned to hide their faith like smugglers, moving it at night, keeping it under floorboards, teaching it to children in whispers so quiet that within three generations the children believed they had invented it themselves.

Inside, the house was warm and dim and full of people who pretended not to be watching. A man stirred a pot. Two children sat by the fire carving—actually carving, little knives and scraps of bone, the way other children whittled sticks. On the mantelpiece stood a row of small figures, each one different, each one worn smooth by handling: a dancer, a fisherman, a woman with her arms raised, a child holding a circle.

Nim counted eleven. Eleven little bone figures on a mantel, and every single one of them posed with its arms open or its head bowed or its hands lifted toward something above the frame.

Prayer-figures. Old ones. Older than any rite her father permitted, because her father permitted nothing—he simply answered, and answering became law.

"They're beautiful," she said carefully.

"They're nothing," the old woman said, too quickly. Then, seeing Nim's face: "They're family keepsakes. Grandmother's work."

One of the children looked up from her carving and said, in the bright carrying voice of the very young: "Grandmother says the tall god ate all our prayers but someday someone will come who can hear them anyway, and then—"

"*Maren.*" The old woman's voice cracked across the room like a whip, and the child shrank, and the pot-stirrer stopped stirring, and for one moment the whole house held its breath like a held secret.

Then the old woman turned and really looked at Nim—looked, this time, past the famous face, past the gold-and-shadow eyes, down to whatever it was she was actually looking for. Whatever she found, it made her go still.

"You're the runaway," she said quietly. "The daughter. We heard you crossed the Salt Ford with two others. One of them a hearer." Her gaze moved to Juniper, and Nim watched the man go pale and still in a way she had come to know: he always thought his gift was hidden, and it never was. People who lived with secrets recognized the shape of one in another person's shoulders. "We heard the hearer was marked."

"We're not here to bring trouble," Nim said. "We're trying to get ahead of it."

The old woman smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful smile—the smile of someone who has been waiting a long time for trouble and finds it almost restful. "Child. We have been *trouble* for six generations. You don't bring it. You've finally brought it home."

* * *

They fed them barley soup and dark bread and a honey-cake that made Nim's throat close up because it tasted like the temple kitchens of her childhood, before she understood what those kitchens fed. The villagers asked no questions through the meal, which was its own kind of question. Afterward the old woman—who gave her name only as Tansy, though Nim suspected it was a working name, a door name—led them down a passage cut into the hill behind the hearth, deeper than any root cellar, to a chamber where the walls were carved.

Not decorated. Carved. Every inch of stone held figures in relief, thousands of them, generations deep, and many of the oldest had been deliberately defaced—faces smoothed blank, names chiseled away, crowns ground off heads leaving only the ghost of a circlet in the grain of the rock.

"The Night of Smooth Faces," Tansy said, following Nim's eyes. "When our ancestors decided that if the kingdom could not be defended, it would be *forgotten*. They erased their own kings from the stones. They burned the genealogies. They changed their names and scattered, and taught their children one thing above all others: *never sing where the tall god can listen.*"

"But you kept singing," Nim said softly.

Tansy's jaw tightened. "We kept *humming*. Little rites. A charm for a birth, a figure for a death, a blessing whispered into bread dough. Enough to remember that we were once somebody. Not enough for anything to hear us." She looked at Nim with those flat practical eyes. "Until last spring. When the prayers started getting answered again. Do you know what that was like, girl? A hundred years of safe silence, and then one night old Hesta prays for rain for her beans, and it rains, and every humming grandmother in seven valleys goes cold to the bone. Because an answered prayer isn't a gift." Her voice dropped. "It's a hook."

Nim thought of her father's temple, the thousands kneeling in rows, faces shining with gratitude, and how none of them—not one—could tell you anymore what they had wanted before he started giving it to them.

"I know," Nim said. "I grew up on the hook."

* * *

Juniper had gone silent in the way he went silent around bones—which meant, Nim realized, that he had been silent since they entered the chamber. He stood at the far wall with his palm hovering an inch from the defaced carvings, not touching, his lips slightly parted, listening.

"What do you hear?" Tansy asked him. There was no skepticism in her voice. That was new for him, Nim knew; most living people wanted proof, demonstrations, a performance. These people wanted only confirmation.

"Names," Juniper said hoarsely. "Hundreds of names, being said over and over, generation after generation, like—like people passing a bucket of water hand to hand so it won't spill. And under the names—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Under the names there's a song. It's been sung so quietly for so long that it's worn down to almost nothing. But I know it." His voice cracked. "My grandmother used to hum it when she worked. I thought it was just a lullaby."

Tansy closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet and fierce. "The Lament of the Vale. Every child here learns it wrong on purpose—we change a note each generation so it can't be recognized. But it's the same song." She touched the smoothed blank face of a carved king. "Your grandmother was one of ours, hearer. Whether she told you or not."

"She never told me anything," Juniper said. "She just... left things where I'd find them."

"That's how we teach," Tansy said. "Leaving things where they'll be found. It's safer than telling."

Nim watched Juniper absorb this—the idea that his whole solitary, careful, invisible life had been a thread in a weaving six generations wide, that every strange kindness of his grandmother's had been a stitch in a pattern he'd never been allowed to see. She watched him grieve it and be grateful for it at the same time, and she understood that feeling completely. It was the exact shape of loving a father who had given you everything except the right to be yourself.

"There are more of us," Tansy said. "Scattered families, twelve valleys, maybe four hundred souls. We marry among ourselves. We keep the hums. And every year on the longest night we meet somewhere new and we say the erased names aloud into a fire, once each, fast, so they blur together and can't be caught." She smiled thinly. "It sounds mad when I say it out loud."

"It sounds like survival," Nim said.

"It sounds like hope wearing a disguise," Tansy corrected. "Which is the only way hope survives." Then she turned to Juniper, and everything in her manner changed—became formal, weighted, almost frightened. "And now I have to tell you the thing we've kept for six generations, hearer, because you need to hear it before you reach the mountain, and because my grandmother made me swear I'd tell whoever came marked. Even if it broke them."

"Tansy," Nim said sharply, "he's exhausted—"

"He needs to know." Tansy took Juniper's hand—his scarred, clever, bone-carving hand—and turned it palm-up, the way you hold a bird or a blade. "The door in the mountain isn't locked, child. Locks can be broken, and ours was built to survive breaking. It's *sealed*. Sealed means it stays shut unless the seal consents to open. Our ancestors bound the seal to their own bodies—to the bloodline itself. The door opens to the heir's consent, freely given. Nothing else will ever move it. Not armies. Not keys alone. Not gods."

"Then Aurel can't open it," Juniper said slowly. "Even with me."

"With you? No." Tansy's grip tightened. "He doesn't need your consent, boy. Consent is only the polite way. Your blood is the impolite way. Spilled on the threshold by another hand, it unbinds the seal just as surely—that's why he hunts you alive instead of dead. Alive, you can be persuaded. Alive, you can be *bargained with*. Alive, you might walk through that door willingly, and a willing heir opens more than a doorway. A willing heir opens the *right* to rule."

The fire in the little chamber popped. Somewhere overhead a child laughed, and the sound fell down the stairwell into the silence like a stone into a well.

"So the elders' council will tell you what my grandmother told me to tell you," Tansy went on. "There is a final rite. The last one. The one our ancestors wrote in case the sealing ever failed—in case a marked heir came home pursued. It's called the Unmaking, and it is exactly what it sounds like. The heir carves the sigil from his own bone—willingly, consciously, stroke by stroke—and with each stroke he gives up a piece of the inheritance. The voices. The hearing. The claim. The name itself, in the end. What's left is a free man standing in front of a sealed door that will never open for anyone again. Not for Aurel. Not for a king. Not for anyone, ever."

"And the cost?" Juniper asked, though Nim could see in his face that he already knew, the way you know the ending of a story you've been living inside.

"The cost is everything the sigil carries," Tansy said gently. "Including the dead. You'd carve away your own gift along with your own crown. You'd spend the rest of your life in silence, hearer. Ordinary silence. The kind everyone else gets."

Nim expected him to hesitate. Instead he laughed—one short, startled breath, almost like a sob—and pressed his hand flat against the defaced wall, against the thousand smoothed faces of the ancestors who had chosen emptiness over empire.

"Ordinary silence," he repeated. "Do you know, that's the first thing anyone's offered me since this began that sounded like mercy?"

* * *

They slept that night in the hill-house, in a room with a turf roof and a window no bigger than a bread oven, and Nim lay awake listening to the village breathe. Near midnight she heard footsteps on the stair and found Juniper sitting in the main room, awake, turning a scrap of bone over in his fingers. One of the children's practice pieces, she saw—a rough little figure, arms raised.

"Can't sleep?" she asked.

"I keep thinking about the children downstairs." He nodded toward the mantel room. "Carving their little figures. Learning their wrong-note lullabies. Six generations of parents committing the same quiet crime: teaching their kids to be small enough to survive." His thumb moved over the crude little face. "I used to think my grandmother was ashamed of something. Some failure. Now I think she was one soldier in a war fought entirely in whispers, and she won. She got me out. She got me *here*."

"To give you up," Nim said. "To the knife."

"To give me the choice." He looked up at her, and in the banked firelight his eyes were steady in a way they hadn't been since the salt flats. "That's the difference between her inheritance and yours, Nim. Aurel gives his children thrones and takes their choices. My people spent six generations buying back one choice, for one heir, at the end. It cost them everything, and they'd say it was cheap."

Nim sat down beside him. Outside, far off in the hills, something howled—or sang; at this distance, in this country, she genuinely could not tell anymore, and she wondered if that was the point of the whole place.

"My father will offer them miracles," she said. "When he comes. He'll heal their sick and fill their granaries and ask for one thing in return, and half of them will want to give it, because gratitude is the most obedient thing there is."

"I know."

"Some of them will waver, Juniper. Maybe most of them. Good people. People who hummed lullabies in the dark for a hundred years."

"I know." He set the little bone figure down on the table between them, arms raised toward nothing, toward everything. "But they kept the names," he said. "Six generations, they kept the names. That's what I keep coming back to. Not the crown. Not the blood. The *names*, passed hand to hand like a bucket of water." He was quiet a moment. "If I do this—if I carve it out—I want it done the way they do everything. Together. With witnesses. With the lullaby sung wrong on purpose. I don't want to erase myself alone in a vault. If I'm going to become no one, I want to become no one *among friends*."

Nim felt something crack open in her chest—not pain, exactly. More like the ice on a river in early spring, shifting, letting the water move underneath.

"That," she said, "is the first kingly thing you've ever said."

"Don't ruin it."

"Too late. I'm putting it in my memoirs. *The heir of Ossuary Vale chose community over crown, and also snored.*"

"I do not snore."

"Sera can confirm—"

The word landed between them like a dropped glass. They both went quiet. Three days ago. The wagon. The wound neither of them named. The river-god's price already paid out of Juniper's memory like coins from a purse, and neither of them knowing yet the full inventory of what he'd lost—only that sometimes now he paused mid-sentence, reaching for a word that had been there yesterday, and his face went carefully blank while he groped for it.

"Do you think she's—" Nim started.

A horn sounded.

Not from the village. From the ridge above it—a long, wavering note, human-made, repeated twice. Below them, doors opened all along the hillside, and the villagers came out into the starlight without lamps, without panic, moving with the terrible efficiency of people who had rehearsed this night for six generations. Tansy appeared in the doorway with a hooded cloak over her shoulders and her face gray as ash.

"That's the watch from the high pass," she said. "There's a column coming down the valley road. Torches. Hundreds of them." She swallowed. "And they're not burning anything. Hearers—they're not burning anything. They're stopping at every farmstead, and they're *healing the livestock*, and leaving grain."

Juniper stood slowly. Through the tiny window, far down the valley, Nim could see them now: a river of torchlight winding toward the village, patient as water, and riding at its head a palanquin of white bone whose glow made the stars around it look dirty.

Her father had found them. Of course he had. He had simply followed the oldest trail of all—the one made of answered prayers.

"They'll offer you anything," Nim whispered. "Whatever you look at, he'll give it to them. That's how he fights. He doesn't break gates. He makes people *carry him through*."

Below, the torches kept coming. And somewhere in that glittering column, Nim knew, her former teachers were already choosing which words would open this village's doors from the inside.

Tansy squared her small shoulders. "Get the boy to the mountain," she said. "The elders are summoned. Whatever happens tonight—" she looked once at the little bone figure on the table, arms raised, waiting— "*no one kneels.*"

The horn sounded a third time, closer now, and the whole hidden valley began, very quietly, to sing.
