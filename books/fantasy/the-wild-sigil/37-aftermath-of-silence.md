## 37. Aftermath of Silence

Juniper woke to a silence so complete it felt like drowning.

Not the silence he had known all his life—the living hush beneath the dead's murmuring, the way a river's surface lies over its current. This was different. This was the silence of a room after the last voice in it has stopped. He lay on the cot in the elders' house with his eyes open and the morning light crawling across the ceiling beams, and he listened, the way he had listened every day since he was four years old, to the bones of the valley.

Nothing answered.

He had expected grief. What he had not expected was the shape of it—not a wound but an absence with edges, like the socket where a tooth had been. For thirty years his hands had been full of voices. His grandmother's kit still sat on the shelf across the room, the little chisels worn smooth by two generations of thumbs, and even from here he could feel—no. He could not feel anything at all. The box was just wood and bone tools now. It looked smaller than it ever had.

He raised his right hand into the light. Along the inside of his forearm, where he had braced the ritual knife against his own ribcage and cut, the skin had healed into a pale seam, thin as a hair. Beneath it, the rib was whole again—or nearly; there was a ridge he could find with his fingertips if he pressed, a place where bone remembered being carved. But the sigil was gone. He had felt each stroke take something with it as he cut: his grandmother's voice first, then her mother's, then the long descending line of Villalobos kings who had chosen to be no one, their names going out like lamps one by one down a corridor, until the corridor was dark and he was standing alone in it, alive, ordinary, free.

The door opened without a knock. Sera stood in it with a tray of bread and goat cheese and the particular expression she wore when she had been standing outside a door for a long time deciding whether to enter.

"You're awake," she said. "You've been awake for three days."

"I've been asleep for three days?"

"You've been *lying* there for three days. Nim says that's different." She came in and set the tray on the stool beside the cot, then did not sit, which was unlike her. She hovered. Sera Quill, who had crossed the Salt Flats on a shattered leg and complained about the quality of the scenery, hovered in a doorway like a girl afraid of a dog.

"How is your leg?" he asked.

"Ugly. Functional. The river-god does good work, apparently, even when paid in stolen memories." Her voice caught slightly on the word *stolen*, and he knew what she was thinking: that he had traded away pieces of himself to buy back her heartbeat, and that among those pieces had been his grandmother's face. She had asked him about it once, gently, on the road down from the mountain, and he had told her the truth—that he could remember the hands, the smell of tallow and bone dust, the exact weight of the smallest chisel, but that when he reached for her face he found only a warm blankness, like a page someone had carefully scraped clean for reuse.

"It's all right," he said, before she could say it. "It was a fair price."

"It wasn't a price at all. You were dying of grief and you paid it anyway."

"That's usually when prices are highest." He sat up. The room tilted and settled. "Tell me what happened while I was lying here being dramatic."

So she told him, sitting at last on the edge of the cot, her bad leg stretched out stiff in front of her like something she was still learning to negotiate with. The valley was healing. That was the phrase everyone used, as though the valley had been ill rather than enchanted, but Juniper understood what they meant. With Aurel unmade and the door closed from the inside—with Nim on whichever side of it she had chosen, gods keep her—the land was forgetting how to be afraid. The wheat that had died in a perfect circle around them during the ambush had come back green at the edges, a ring of new growth advancing inward day by day toward the dead center, like frost melting in reverse. The ford ran clear. Children were swimming in it again, which they had not done in Juniper's lifetime, because for a hundred years the valley's dead had stood in that water like reeds and the living had learned to look elsewhere.

"The elders want to see you," Sera said. "When you're ready. There's talk of—" She stopped herself, and he watched her decide not to say the word *coronation*, watched the word die between her teeth, and loved her a little for killing it.

"There won't be any talk of that," Juniper said. "I'm no one's heir anymore. I made sure of it."

"I know. They know. They'd still like to thank you formally. There will probably be bread involved. Possibly a goat."

"A goat would be excessive."

"They're very moved, Juniper. You closed a door a god couldn't close."

"Nim closed the door. From inside." His voice went flat on the words, and Sera heard the flatness and did not push. They sat together in the silence, which should have been companionable and was instead enormous. He realized he could not hear her heartbeat. On the salt flats, holding vigil while her blood soaked into the white ground, he had heard it—a living pulse, faint and stubborn, the first voice of the living he had ever perceived—and he had thought, absurdly, *so this is what everyone else has been hearing all along*. Now even that was gone. Sera sat six inches from him, breathing, warm, gloriously alive, and to his ears she might have been a chair.

"Sera," he said. "I can't hear you."

She turned. Her eyes, gray-green and mapmaker-precise, searched his face. "I'm right here."

"No—I mean. I can't hear anyone. Not the dead, not the living. Not your heart. Everything's just..." He gestured at the air, at all that empty bright morning. "Quiet."

She was quiet for a moment too. Then she said, "Is it terrible?"

He considered the question honestly, the way he considered everything now, having learned on a mountain that honesty was the only currency that didn't devalue. "It's like losing a limb," he said. "And then finding out the limb was also carrying something heavy. I miss them. I'll miss them until I die, probably. My grandmother. The drowned children. Even the bitter ones, the ones who muttered complaints through my hands while I carved their names. But—" He flexed his fingers. Ten ordinary fingers, attached to an ordinary man. "There's room in me now that there never was before. I keep discovering it. Like rooms in a house you inherited but never entered."

Sera nodded slowly. Then, because she was Sera, she pulled her satchel onto the cot and began unpacking it, and he saw that she had already begun.

* * *

The map covered most of the floor of the elders' meeting hall, weighted down at the corners with river stones, and it was the strangest thing Sera Quill had ever drawn, which was saying a great deal for a woman whose master atlas contained a country that erased itself.

Juniper stood at its edge on the fourth morning after he rose from his bed, leaning on a walking stick the elders had pressed on him and which he had decided to accept out of politeness rather than need, and studied it. It was drawn in her fine surveyor's hand, but the hand had changed—looser, less defensive. And the map itself was mostly empty.

"This is the valley," she said, tapping the center, where the river, the ford, the rim of hills, and the burnt footprint of the old carving shed were rendered in loving detail. "This much I can swear to. Every stream, every field, the exact bend of the road up to the mountain gate." Her finger traced outward from the valley, and the ink thinned, faded, became suggestion. "Beyond this, nothing. Blank."

"You're the finest cartographer in three kingdoms. You crossed half a continent to get here. You could fill that space in your sleep."

"I could fill it with guesses." She looked up at him, and there was something raw under the wry set of her mouth. "Do you know what my whole life was? Filling in blanks. Other people's blanks. The crown wanted the wild spaces charted so they could tax them, garrison them, name them. Every blank on a map, to them, was money or territory not yet owned. I told myself I was doing it for knowledge. For beauty." She touched the faded margin, where her pen had wandered off into nothing. "But I sold a refugee column for this atlas, Juniper. I filled in a blank with forty-one graves. So." She straightened. "New rules. Nothing goes on this map unless it consents to be there."

"*Consents.*"

"The City of Second Graves sent a delegation while you were sleeping. Did they tell you? They want to be on it. First entry. A city that buried its own names twice, asking to be remembered—it took me a whole evening to understand why, and then I understood. Being forgotten by force isn't the same as choosing to be seen on your own terms. Ossuary Vale hid itself. These places—" she swept her hand across the blank spaces, "—these places are going to come forward one at a time, now that Aurel's shadow is off them, and ask. And I'm going to wait for the asking."

Juniper looked at the great pale emptiness of the map and felt something loosen in his chest. All his life the dead had shown him the world whether he wanted it or not—their memories, their grudges, their geography of grief. Knowledge had always arrived in him uninvited. He had never once been asked.

"And me?" he said. "Am I on it?"

Sera's pen paused. "There's a mark where the carving shed burned," she said carefully. "Unlabeled."

"Good. Leave it unlabeled. Let people wonder." He smiled, and found the smile came easily, which surprised him. Grief was still there—he could feel it seated in him permanently now, the way the scarred rib was there—but it sat differently than it had a month ago. Lighter. Carried, not carrying him.

Outside the hall, the valley was loud with living noise: hammering from the bridge, children shouting at the ford, someone arguing cheerfully about fence posts. He could hear all of it with his ears, plainly, the way everyone else did, and it struck him—as it had struck him every morning since waking—that this was the first time in his life he had ever heard the present tense. The dead spoke only of before. The god-king had spoken only of forever. But the argument about fence posts was happening *now*, and belonged to no one's ghost and no one's empire, and Juniper stood in the doorway of the meeting hall listening to it with both ordinary ears and felt, slowly, like thawing ground, the beginnings of peace.

Sera came to stand beside him, her shoulder touching his. Below, on the road, a figure was coming up from the ford—a small figure, quick-footed, with a satchel of carving tools slung across her back that looked familiar, because Juniper recognized the satchel. It was his spare.

"Maret," he said, naming the child before he could see her clearly, and then blinked at himself, because he had named her not from the dead's whispering but simply from knowing her, from months of her hanging around the shed asking questions, from the ordinary accumulation of a neighbor's acquaintance. Knowing someone the slow way. The living way.

"She's been asking for you every day," Sera said. "Nim told her, before—" Sera did not finish. Nobody in the valley finished sentences about Nim yet. They spoke of her in fragments, the way you speak of the departed who might not actually be departed, and Juniper held the same fragile hope the rest of them carried: that a daughter clever enough to steal a shard of a god's key was clever enough to find a way back out of a locked between-space. Or else that she had found something better on the other side. Either way, the door was shut, and shut was what she had chosen, and Juniper honored choices. It was, he supposed, the whole of what he had learned.

Maret reached the foot of the steps and looked up at him, breathless, gap-toothed, nine years old and impatient as spring.

"Master Juniper. The miller's wife wants a memorial for her husband. And—" she hesitated, suddenly shy, suddenly young, "—and I finished the practice piece. The one you left me. Can you look at it? Is the grain right? I couldn't tell. Bones don't say anything to me."

"No," Juniper said, and the words came out gentler than he expected, warmer. "They won't. Not to either of us, ever again. That's the gift we've been given, Maret—we get to learn the slow way. Come up. Show me what you carved."

The girl climbed the steps two at a time, and behind her, far off past the valley rim, the morning haze was lifting off hills that no map had ever troubled to name—hills that belonged, for now, to themselves.

Juniper put his hand briefly against his scarred rib, feeling the ridge of old bone, the place where he had written himself out of every story but his own.

Then he turned, and followed the sound of living voices inside.
