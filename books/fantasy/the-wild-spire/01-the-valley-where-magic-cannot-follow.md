## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The bread was proofing badly, and Freya Delacroix decided — as she had decided every morning for ten years — that this was the worst thing that would happen to her today.

She pressed the heel of her hand into the dough and watched it resist. Too cold. The kitchen of the little stone cottage at the edge of Hollowmere held its chill like a grudge, no matter how much wood she fed the stove. She should have been able to warm it with a thought once. A flick of intention, a thread of heat drawn from the air itself. Now there was only flour on her knuckles and the honest ache of work in her shoulders, and she had learned — slowly, imperfectly, the way you learn anything without magic — to call that enough.

Most days.

She glanced down at her hands, out of habit rather than fear, and turned them over in the grey window light.

The scars ran across both palms like river deltas seen from very high up. Silver-white, faintly luminous when the light hit them wrong, branching from the heels of her hands to the tips of her fingers. In Hollowmere they were called *work-worn* by the polite and *witch-marks* by everyone else, and everyone else said it quietly, the way you'd say a name you weren't supposed to know. Ten years in the valley had taught Freya which neighbors used which word, and she kept her gloves on at market accordingly.

Here is what the valley knew, or believed it knew: ten years ago, a girl from beyond the veil called down a storm that cracked the sky over two worlds, and the covenant between them shattered like dropped glass, and the girl was exiled to the one place in creation where magic could not follow her. Here is what the valley did not know: the girl had been trying to catch a lantern that fell off a windowsill. Here is what almost no one knew at all: the storm had not been hers alone.

Freya punched the dough down harder than it deserved.

"Sorry," she murmured to it. "Bad morning."

The bread forgave her. Bread always did. It was one of perhaps four things in Hollowmere that did.

She shaped the loaves and set them near the stove, then wiped her hands on her apron and went to stand in the doorway that looked out over the valley proper. Morning fog lay in the hollows like spilled milk. Beyond the rooftops of the town — three hundred souls, give or take, all of them descended from exiles and failures and people who had simply wanted to disappear — the mountains rose in their collar of grey, and somewhere past those peaks, though no map showed it and no road reached it, the Wild Spire stood wrapped in its endless storms.

Her mother's kingdom. Her mother's prison. Her mother's lie, if half the things that surfaced in Freya's nightmares at three in the morning were true.

But that was not today's problem.

Today's problem was that she had nothing to wear.

* * *

"You're going to wear the green," Marta announced, before Freya had even finished climbing the steps to the shop.

Marta Osgood ran the secondhand clothing exchange out of the front room of her house, priced everything in barter or gossip, and had appointed herself, without consultation, the supervisor of Freya Delacroix's love life. She was seventy-one, built like a rain barrel, and had survived two husbands and one exile of her own, though nobody remembered anymore what Marta had done to earn hers. She suspected Marta didn't either. That was the way of the valley: the crimes faded, the sentences didn't.

"I was thinking the blue," Freya said.

"The blue makes you look like you're attending your own funeral."

"It's a nice blue."

"It's a *funeral* blue." Marta was already moving through the racks with the certainty of a woman who had laid out this exact argument in her head the night before. She emerged holding a dress the color of new leaves, wool-blend, mended at one cuff with thread that almost matched. "Green. With your hair. Trust me, I've thought about this more than you have."

"That's genuinely alarming, Marta."

"He's a cartographer, you said. Comes up from the lowlands to survey the pass roads. Stays at the inn." Marta held the dress against Freya's chest and squinted at her like an appraiser. "A man who draws maps for a living. You could do worse. You have done worse. Do you remember that tanner's boy—"

"We don't speak of the tanner's boy."

"Three dates. He talked about hides the entire time."

"*We don't speak of him.*"

Marta cackled and folded the dress into brown paper with the tenderness she otherwise reserved only for bread and grudges. "When's he coming?"

"Seven. He said seven. He asked about the bakery first, actually, whether I'd mind meeting at my place because he'd heard I baked, and I panicked and said yes, so now he's going to sit in my kitchen while my whole life happens around him like a play I forgot to rehearse."

"So you'll feed him. Good. Men fall in love through their stomachs, it's well documented." Marta tied the paper with string and pushed it into Freya's arms, then caught her wrist — the bare wrist, above the glove line, where the first silver branch of scarring showed pale against her skin.

Freya went still. It was an old reflex, older than the exile. *Hide it.*

Marta looked at the mark for a long moment. Her thumb moved over it once, gently, the way you'd touch a scar on a child.

"My sister had marks like that," she said. "Before we came here. From the old country." She let go. "You bake good bread, Freya Delacroix. Whatever you were before, that counts for something in this valley. More than most things."

Then, because sentiment in Hollowmere was like weather — brief, violent, and never acknowledged afterward — she flapped her hands at the door. "Go home. Iron something. And if he talks about hides, you come straight back here and I'll find you someone else."

Freya laughed, and meant it, and walked home through the falling afternoon light carrying a paper-wrapped dress and a feeling she hadn't let herself carry in years.

Hope. Fragile as spun sugar. Terrifying as any storm.

* * *

The cottage smelled of bread and rosemary by six, and Freya had cleaned a kitchen that was already clean, twice, and now stood in front of her narrow mirror in the green dress with her hair half-pinned and her hands — bare, deliberately, defiantly bare — hanging at her sides.

She had made herself look at the scars. That was the rule she'd set an hour ago and already regretted. *If he can't look at them, he can't look at me.* Fine words. She'd written similar fine words in the journal she kept under a floorboard, in the margins of her mother's old letters, in the dust of a hundred bad nights. The truth was simpler and worse: she had not been touched, hand to hand, skin to skin, in nine years. Not since the night before the trial. There was a whole geography of her that existed now only in memory, and she was about to sit across a table from a stranger who might, if the evening went impossibly well, reach across and take her hand, and feel the ridges of the storm under his fingers, and ask.

What would she say?

*These are where the lightning went. These are where the sky broke. These are the reason three hundred people live in a hole in the world where nothing magical will ever grow.*

Or the true thing, the thing she'd never said aloud even to the journal:

*I didn't mean it. I've never meant anything as hard as I didn't mean that.*

A knock at the door.

Freya jumped so hard she knocked a pin from her hair, swore at it, left it, crossed the kitchen in four strides, stopped, made herself walk the last two slowly. She checked the fire — banked, safe. Checked the bread — cooled, sliced, waiting under cloth beside butter and honey and the small jar of blackcurrant preserves she'd traded three loaves and a secret recipe for. Checked her hands one last time.

Open. Visible. Hers.

She opened the door.

Elias Vane was taller than she remembered from the inn, and younger than his voice had sounded, with ink stains on his fingers that matched — she nearly laughed — the ones on hers. He had a coat too thin for the valley evenings and the particular posture of a man who spent his life leaning over tables, and he was holding a book against his chest the way you hold something you've carried a long way and aren't sure why.

"Freya?" He smiled, nervous, and it undid some knot in her chest she hadn't known was tied. "I'm Elias. I'm — early? Late? I couldn't tell if the fog counted as distance."

"On time," she said. "Come in. Mind the cat, he'll pretend he hates you but he's lying."

There was no cat. There had been no cat for six years. But Elias stepped carefully over the empty threshold spot anyway, gravely, as though honoring a grief, and something in Freya's chest went loose and warm and dangerous.

He saw the table. He saw the bread, the butter, the preserves, the two chairs angled toward the fire. He saw the green dress, and his ears went faintly pink.

"This looks like a lot more trouble than I deserve," he said.

"It's just bread."

"It's never just bread." He set the book down on the table to free his hand — and she would think about this later, how the evening turned on that small motion, a book finding its place on a scarred oak table like a key finding its lock. "I brought something, actually. I wasn't sure if it was strange. It's probably strange. My grandfather died last spring, back in the lowlands, and I've been clearing out his things, and he collected — well, hoarded, honestly — old regional texts. Survey records, folk histories. This one I couldn't put down. It's a chronicle of the valley. Of *this* valley, Hollowmere. A hundred years old, maybe more."

Freya's breath went quiet in her throat.

"There are maybe five books in this valley older than me," she said. "Paper doesn't survive the damp. What is it?"

"A prophecy, if you believe the title page." He laughed, self-conscious. "Which I don't, obviously, but the marginalia is fascinating, someone annotated it obsessively, whole generations of some family writing in the edges—" He opened the cover. Turned past the frontispiece. His voice changed. "Actually, there's a page I wanted to show you specifically, because it's odd, it's dated entries, like a calendar of — Freya?"

She was staring at the open book. At the page of old brown ink. At the column of entries in a spidery antique hand, each one dated, each one naming a person and a place and a hour.

At the last entry. Dated tonight. Reading, in ink a century dry:

*The mapmaker comes to the storm-caller's door. The veil remembers what the valley forgot.*

And beneath the words, in the same ancient hand, a name:

*Elias Vane.*

The fire popped. Outside, far up past the mountain collar, where no cloud should have been able to form in the magic-dead air of Hollowmere, thunder rolled — soft, single, unmistakable.

Neither of them moved. Neither of them breathed.

"Freya," Elias said again, very carefully, the way you speak to someone standing on ice. "How does a hundred-year-old book know my name?"
