## 3. The Veil Opens

**Freya**

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Elias set the book on the table between them.

Freya hadn't touched her tea in an hour. It had gone cold and bitter, a skin forming on the surface, and she couldn't bring herself to care. All her attention was on the object sitting three inches from her plate of untouched bread — a book bound in cracked grey leather, its corners worn round as river stones, its spine stitched with thread that had once been silver.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly."

Elias turned it so the open page faced her. His hands were steady, but she'd noticed earlier how he rubbed his thumb along his forefinger when he was nervous, an old habit, and he was doing it now without seeming to know.

"The page is dated the ninth of Deepfrost," he said. "That's tonight. That's *this* year. And next to the date—" He tapped the ink. "—it says my name. *Elias Vane comes to the table.* Written in a hand that paper-people at the university archive swear is a hundred years old. Maybe older."

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

Freya looked at the name. The ink was brown-black, oxidized the way old iron does, sunk deep into fibers that had long since turned amber. She knew ink like that. She'd spent ten years trying to forget everything that looked like it.

"That's not just old," she said quietly. "That's valley paper."

Elias frowned. "Hollowmere made books?"

"Hollowmere used to make *everything*." She reached out before she could stop herself — then caught her own hands and folded them under the table, out of sight. Old reflex. Her scarred palms stayed hidden against her knees. "Before the severance, this valley traded with the Spire. Paper, glass, song-glass, storm-silk. Then the covenant broke and all of it died overnight. The looms stopped mid-thread. The kilns went cold with pots still inside them." She nodded at the book. "That binding isn't a hundred years old, Elias. It's ten. It's from the last year the valley could hold a needle to thread."

"But the archive dated the ink—"

"The archive dates things by chemistry. Magic doesn't care about chemistry." She heard her own voice sharpening and made herself stop. Made herself breathe. "I'm not saying you're wrong. I'm saying you're asking the wrong question."

"What's the right one?"

She looked at him across the candle. He was handsome in a rumpled, unguarded way — ink on his cuff, a mapmaker's squint, hair pushed back like he'd been running his hands through it all evening. Three hours ago she'd been terrified he'd be boring. Two hours ago she'd been terrified she'd be. Now there was a century-old prophecy on the table with his name in it, and the terror had changed shape entirely.

"The right question is who wrote it," she said. "And what they wanted you to do when you read it."

* * *

**Elias**

He watched her look at the book the way other people looked at snakes.

Not fear exactly. Recognition. Freya Delacroix had gone very still in the way of someone listening for something behind a wall, and Elias — who had spent his life reading terrain, who could tell where a river used to run by the way willows leaned — recognized the posture immediately.

"You know something," he said. "About this book."

"I know what I know." She unfolded her hands, hesitated, then stood. "Wait here."

She crossed the small cottage — two rooms, low beams, dried herbs hanging from every rafter, more rosemary than any one person could reasonably use — and knelt at a chest beneath the window. When she opened it he saw letters. Dozens of them, bundled with twine, packed in careful layers like something both precious and dangerous.

"My mother's correspondence," Freya said, her back to him. "From before. From after. Everything they let me keep when they sent me here, which was nothing, which is why these are smuggled." She lifted one bundle and held it without opening it, the way you'd hold a bird with a broken wing. "There's a seal she used. A spire-and-circle. If your book has it anywhere, we should know."

"Why?"

"Because if it does, then whoever wrote your name in it knew my mother." She finally turned, and the candlelight did something complicated to her face. "And everyone who knew my mother is either dead or lying."

He took the bundle when she offered it. The topmost letter was addressed in a tall, severe hand: *To the Warden of Hollowmere, regarding the girl.* No first name. No affection. He glanced up; Freya was watching him read with an expression he'd seen on people looking at old battlefields.

"You don't have to," he said.

"I've read them all. A thousand times." She sat back down across from him. "Read the third one."

The third letter's wax seal was broken, stamped with a circle bisected by a single vertical line — no spire, just a pillar of light rising out of a ring. But beneath the signature, in the lower corner of the final page, was a small drawn device Elias knew intimately, because it was pressed into the leather of the book on the table.

A spire wrapped in a spiral of wind.

His mouth went dry. He set the letter down and opened the prophecy book to its inside cover, and there it was again, embossed faintly into the pasteboard, hidden under layers of newer glue.

"It matches," he said stupidly.

"I can see that it matches." Freya had gone pale. Not frightened-pale. Angrier than that. "Ten years ago my mother sat in a court full of people wearing that mark and told them I had called a storm that killed four wardens. Ten years ago they put me on a cart and brought me here, to the one place in the world where nothing like this—" she gestured at the book, at the letters, at the air itself "—could ever reach me again."

"And now it has."

"Now it has."

The candle guttered. Steadied. Guttered again.

Neither of them moved.

* * *

**Freya**

Later — much later, in the bad hours, in the years of nights afterward — Freya would try to remember the exact moment the magic came back, and she never quite could. There was no thunderclap. No warning bell. Just a change in the weight of the air, subtle as the first degree of fever, and then the rosemary hanging from her rafters began, very softly, to sing.

She noticed because she had spent ten years learning every silence in Hollowmere. This valley had a silence with texture to it, a felt thickness, the hush of a world holding its breath. And now the silence was fraying at the edges. The herbs were humming a note below hearing. The cold tea in her cup was rippling in rings, though nothing had touched it.

"Elias," she said. "Don't move."

He'd been reaching for the book. He froze, hand hovering. "What is it? What's happening?"

"I don't know." Which was a lie, or half of one, because some animal part of her knew perfectly well and was screaming. Dead magic didn't stir. That was the whole point of exile, the whole mercy and cruelty of Hollowmere — magic had bled out of this valley like warmth from a corpse, and it did not come back. Things did not hum here. Things did not ripple. Things certainly did not—

The book slid an inch toward the table's edge.

On its own.

Elias snatched it back and held it against his chest, and Freya saw the ink on the open page begin to move — the old brown-black letters swimming, rearranging, the date dissolving and re-forming, and beneath his name new words surfacing through the paper like drowned bodies rising:

*the veil remembers what the valley forgot*

"No," Freya said. Out loud, absurdly, to a book. "No, no — it's sealed, it was *sealed*, I checked, I've checked every year—"

"Freya." Elias's voice was very calm, the way voices go calm right before panic. "What veil?"

She was already on her feet, knocking her chair over, crossing to the window. Outside, the night lay flat and starless and ordinary. The fields were dark. The hills were darker. Nothing moved except—

Except the air above the eastern ridge, where for one heartbeat — one — the darkness bent. Folded. Like cloth pinched by an invisible finger.

Her mother's letters were rustling in the chest. Every page of them, fluttering without wind, and from somewhere inside the paper came a sound like a voice played backward, like a word being unspoken.

"Get away from the letters," she said. "Elias. Get away from the—"

The candle went out.

* * *

**Elias**

Darkness — total, thick, the kind that presses on the eyes — and in it, light. Not lamplight. Something colder, blue-white, bleeding up through the floorboards of the cottage as though the ground itself had become a window onto somewhere else.

Elias couldn't see Freya anymore. He could hear her — cursing, moving, her voice cracking out words in a language he didn't know, sharp and rhythmic, and he understood dimly that she was doing something, working some craft he'd assumed this valley forbade, and that it wasn't working.

The floor under his feet grew cold. Then cold stopped being the right word. The floorboards were still wood, still solid — but *through* them, rising like cold through ice on a pond, came a different kind of cold, an ancient one, a cold with intent. The hair on his arms stood up. His breath plumed white.

The book was shaking in his hands. He thought about dropping it. He found he couldn't. Whether that was fear or something else, he couldn't say — only that his fingers had closed around the grey leather and would not open, as if the book had been waiting a hundred years for someone to hold it and did not intend to be put down now.

Light bloomed.

It tore upward out of the floor between him and the door — a seam, a split, a vertical crack in the world six feet tall, its edges peeling back like burning paper revealing not fire but sky. Through the seam he saw wind. Actual visible wind, spiraling towers of cloud lit from within by lightning the color of dawn, and a structure rising out of the storm's heart — impossibly tall, impossibly far, a spire like a needle stitching heaven to earth.

He knew it. God help him, he knew it — he'd drawn it a dozen times from his grandfather's descriptions, had mapped its impossible geometry in pencil margins, had always assumed it was a story.

The Wild Spire.

"Elias!" Freya's voice, raw, close. Hands seized his arm — he could feel her now, see her silhouette against the rift-light, her sleeves shoved up, and in the blue-white glare he saw her hands for the first time all evening: scarred, both palms, old burn-patterns like branching lightning frozen in the skin.

Beautiful, some detached part of him thought. Terrible and beautiful.

"Let go of the book," she shouted over a sound that was building underneath everything now, a pressure, a bass note, the valley's dead magic waking all at once and screaming.

"I can't—"

"*Let go!*"

He pulled. His fingers wouldn't obey. The seam in the floor widened, and wind poured out of it into the little cottage, whipping the herb bundles, scattering his grandfather's letters, tearing pages from the prophecy book — and the pages didn't fall. They hung in the air around him, turning slowly, hundreds of them, every page he'd never read, blank ones and written ones, orbiting him like the beginning of a sentence the world had been waiting ten years to finish.

Freya grabbed him around the chest with both scarred arms and hauled him backward, and for one moment — one whole, precious moment — he moved. He felt her strength, felt himself sliding free of the light.

Then the rift pulled.

It wasn't violent. That was the horror of it. It was gentle, inexorable, like a tide taking a swimmer, and the book in his hands became an anchor dragging him forward, and the orbiting pages collapsed inward into the seam, and Freya's grip — her strong, desperate, mortal grip — began to slide along his ribs.

"Don't you dare," she was saying, and it wasn't commanding anymore, it was begging, and he'd known her one evening and already knew she was not a person who begged. "Don't you *dare*, I am not losing anyone else, do you hear me—"

"Freya—" He twisted, got one hand locked around her wrist, felt the lightning-scars rough under his thumb. "Listen to me. Listen! If there's a way through — if that's the Spire — I'll find the other side of this. Mapmakers always find the other side."

"That's not how it works, that's not how any of this works—"

The light swallowed his legs. His hips. Cold climbed his spine like water rising in a flooded hold. The book was singing now, actually singing, a thin triumphant note, and the last thing he saw clearly was her face — tear-streaked, furious, lit blue-white, more alive than anything he had ever drawn.

"Midwinter's eve," he shouted, because it was in the book, because it was the only thing in the book that sounded like a promise instead of a threat. "If there's a threshold — I'll be at it!"

Then her fingers slipped from his wrist.

* * *

**Freya**

The rift snapped shut like a wound closing.

The candle relit itself. The herbs hung silent. The tea lay flat and cold in its cup, and the cottage was exactly, precisely, perfectly ordinary — except for the scorch-mark on her floorboards in the shape of a standing man, and the single loose page drifting down through the still air to land at her feet.

Freya stood in the middle of her quiet life and shook.

She picked up the page with hands that would not stop trembling. It was from the prophecy book — she knew the paper, knew the ink. Most of the page was water-damaged past reading. But near the bottom, in that century-old hand, one line survived intact:

*and the storm-caller follows, though the valley takes her memories for toll*

She read it twice. Three times. Then she walked to the window, threw it open, and screamed her mother's name at the dead, silent, magicless hills of Hollowmere until her voice gave out.

The valley, of course, said nothing. It had never answered her once in ten years.

But far away, past the eastern ridge, for the space of a single heartbeat, the darkness folded again — and this time, it did not close.
