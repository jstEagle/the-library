## 4. The Long Winter Falling

The first thing Elias Vane understood about the Wild Spire was that it was falling on him.

Not metaphorically. Clouds — actual clouds, grey and muscular and low enough to touch — were descending in slow spirals around a tower of black stone that went up and up until his eyes watered trying to find its top. Snow came off those clouds in fat, unhurried flakes, the kind that meant business. And the wind carried voices. Not words exactly, but the memory of them, like standing outside a house where people are arguing in a language you almost know.

He was lying on something cold and slightly damp. Moss, maybe. Or lichen pretending to be moss.

*You're dreaming,* he told himself. *You fell asleep at dinner because you got nervous and drank two glasses of wine on an empty stomach, which is a classic move, Elias, truly a masterclass.*

Except he hadn't eaten yet. They'd only just sat down. She'd been laughing at something he'd said about contour lines, and then the book had—

The book.

He sat up too fast and the world tilted, all that vertical space reorganizing itself around him like a deck of cards being reshuffled by someone showing off.

"Easy." The voice came from his left, gravel-deep, unimpressed. "Sit up slow or you'll empty your stomach on my boots, and I've had a long decade."

Elias turned his head. A man crouched beside him, wrapped in a coat made of overlapping leather scales that clicked faintly when he moved, like armor designed by someone who'd only had armor described to them secondhand. He was broad, weathered, somewhere between fifty and geological time, with a beard shot through with frost that didn't seem to bother him and a scar running from his eyebrow down into it like a lightning strike that had given up halfway.

"You're not real," Elias said. "I'm having some kind of episode."

"Everyone says that." The man stood, joints cracking in a way that suggested he agreed with the sentiment. "Name's Brannoc. I'm a warden of the middle reaches. You're lying in a drift-field three leagues below the Spire's root, which is where things fall out of the veil when they fall out hard. Now." He looked down at Elias with pale grey eyes that had clearly seen every stupid thing a person could do and several new ones. "Who pulled you through?"

"I don't—" Elias's hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the frozen ground to make them stop, a trick he'd learned during exams. "Nobody pulled me anywhere. I was at dinner. There's a woman — Freya. We were looking at a book, and the valley did something, and there was light, and—"

He stopped. Because saying it aloud, here, under this enormous falling sky, made it worse rather than better.

Brannoc's expression changed. It wasn't much of a change — a tightening around the eyes, a stillness coming into him like water freezing — but Elias had spent years reading maps and the people who drew them, and he knew what a landmark shifting looked like.

"Say her name again," Brannoc said quietly.

"...Freya."

"And what is she to you?"

"We've been writing letters for eight months. Tonight was our first date. I brought a book I found in my grandfather's estate, and her name was in it, and my name was in it, and the date — tonight's date — written a hundred years ago, and I thought she was playing some kind of joke, except she went white as paper and said her mother used ink like that, and then the ground opened up and I—"

Fell. That was the word his mind kept flinching away from. He had fallen through the world, and now he was here, wherever here was, and Freya was there, alone at their table, probably thinking she had hallucinated the whole thing or worse, thinking she had caused it.

"She's Delacroix," Brannoc said. It wasn't a question.

"You know her?"

"I knew her mother." Brannoc turned away, scanning the sky with the weary attention of a shepherd counting sheep he already knew would be short. "Get up slowly. We need to be under stone before full dark. The storms come down at night, and they're hungry lately. Hungrier than they've been in ten years."

"Hungry," Elias repeated. "Storms aren't hungry. Storms are weather."

Brannoc looked back at him, and there was something in the look — pity, maybe, or the patience of a man who had explained this before and hated doing it — that made Elias's stomach drop further than the fall had.

"Up," Brannoc said. "Walk and ask. It keeps the blood moving and the questions shorter."

*

They walked.

The landscape defied every instinct Elias had spent his life honing. He was a cartographer; he understood terrain the way musicians understand keys, and this place was playing in no key he knew. Rivers ran uphill in slow silver ropes, pausing mid-slope as if reconsidering. Mountains drifted — actually drifted, a few feet per hour, grinding against each other with sounds like distant doors. And everywhere, everywhere, the snow fell without accumulating more than a few inches, as though the ground were drinking it.

Above them all rose the Spire: a single black column miles wide at its base, narrowing as it climbed, wreathed in storm-clouds that turned around it like moths around a dying lamp. Except the lamp wasn't dying, Elias corrected himself. The lamp was frozen. The clouds moved, but sluggishly, half-heartedly, a dance performed at quarter speed by dancers who'd forgotten why.

"What is this place?" he asked.

"The Wild Spire." Brannoc said it the way you'd say the name of a deceased friend — with weight, and care, and a refusal to elaborate. "It's the engine-room of two worlds. Every storm that ever broke over your valley, every river that swelled, every season that arrived on time — it started here. This realm makes weather the way yours makes bread. There were storms living in those clouds older than your grandfather's grandfather, and each one bound by oath to the realms below, and each one keeping its oath, because the covenant held."

"And the covenant doesn't hold anymore."

"No." Brannoc's breath plumed white. "Ten years ago it broke. One night. Everything stopped working all at once, like a clock with the mainspring snapped. The seasons down here froze where they stood. We've been in winter ever since — not a natural winter, mind. A held breath. The storms can't die properly and can't live properly, so they hang there, and every year they hang a little lower, a little colder, a little hungrier." He gestured up at the great slow spiral of cloud. "When the last of them freezes through, the cold comes down the mountain and through the veil, and your valley learns what we already know. There's no wall between your world and ours, mapmaker. There's just a door, and the door is rotting."

"How do you know I'm a mapmaker?"

"You said it. At dinner, apparently, according to you." Brannoc snorted. "Also you've been navigating since the moment you woke up. You oriented north within thirty seconds of opening your eyes. You looked at the drifting peaks and your face said *wrong, wrong, wrong* like a man reading a forged signature. Only two kinds of people look at broken country that way. Wardens and mapmakers. And you're not wearing boots worth a warden's salary."

Elias looked down at his shoes. They were, admittedly, terrible.

"So the covenant broke ten years ago," he said. "And Freya was exiled ten years ago."

"Now you're asking the right question in the wrong tone of voice," Brannoc said. "Don't build a gallows out of coincidences, boy. Build a map first. Then decide where the gallows goes."

"That's genuinely disturbing advice."

"I'm a genuinely disturbing man." But there was something almost warm underneath it, buried deep, like a coal under ash. "Here. We're close."

The "here" was a cliff face that looked identical to every other cliff face, except that Brannoc walked straight at it and the rock opened like a mouth. Inside was a wardens' post: a cavern hollowed and buttressed, lit by lanterns burning something blue and slow, furnished with the aggressive minimalism of people who expected to abandon everything at any moment. Racks of gear. A cold hearth. Maps — real maps, drawn in ink on vellum, pinned across one entire wall.

Elias forgot how to breathe.

He crossed to them without deciding to. His fingers hovered over the nearest one, not touching, the way you approach a painting behind glass. The linework was exquisite — confident, economical, annotated in a tight hand. But it was the subject matter that stopped him. These weren't maps of terrain. They were maps of *oaths*. Rivers of binding-words flowing from the Spire's crown down into labeled territories: VALLEY OF HOLLOWMERE. THE LOWLANDS BEYOND. THE DEEP THAW. Each line traced a promise from a named storm to a named place, and each line had been struck through in red.

Every single one. Struck through.

"When did you draw these?" he asked. His voice came out strange.

"I didn't. My predecessor did, the night it broke, trying to chart what we'd lost." Brannoc hung his scaled coat on a peg. "We keep them up so nobody forgets what a covenant looks like on paper. On paper it looked simple. In practice it took eleven bloodlines and nine hundred years to weave." He poured something dark from a dented kettle into two tin cups and shoved one at Elias. "Drink. It tastes like punishment and works like medicine."

Elias drank. It tasted like punishment. He drank more.

"Tell me about the night it broke," he said.

Brannoc lowered himself onto a bench that groaned in protest, and for a long moment he just looked at the wall of dead oaths.

"There was a daughter born to the Storm-Caller," he said finally. "Seraphine's girl. Firstborn of the first bloodline — the keystone line, the ones whose magic anchored the whole weaving. When the child came of age, she'd have taken up the keystone bond, same as her mother, same as her grandmother, back to the founding. That was the design. Blood holds the covenant; covenant holds the weather; weather feeds both worlds."

"But?"

"But the girl didn't want it. Who would? It's not a crown, it's a chain. You give the bond your whole self — your sleep, your seasons, your children's names. Seraphine pushed. The girl refused. And on the very night the court gathered to force the matter—" Brannoc's jaw worked. "There was a storm. The worst anyone alive had seen. Came off the Spire wild and screaming, no oath riding it, and tore through the valley below before it could be caught. Killed the old Warden of Hollowmere. Nearly killed a village."

"Freya called it."

"It's what everyone believes." Brannoc's voice was carefully, deliberately flat, and Elias recognized the flatness now for what it was: a man stepping around a hole in the ice. "A child of the keystone line, unbound, unsworn, calling a wild storm — the court needed someone to blame, and she was convenient. Seraphine exiled her own daughter into the valley, stripped her magic, sealed the rift behind her. Said it was justice. Said it was mercy." He shook his head slowly. "But here's what I know, mapmaker, and I've had ten years to turn it over. Exiling the keystone heir didn't punish the breaking of the covenant. It *was* the breaking. You don't snap a chain and then wonder why the engine stops."

The blue lanterns hissed. Somewhere far above, something vast shifted in the clouds, and a sound rolled down the mountain — not thunder, quite. Thunder's grief-stricken cousin.

Elias set down the cup. His hands were steady now, which surprised him. Fear had passed through him and come out the other side as something harder.

"Freya told me once," he said, "that she never meant to call any storm. She said it the way you say something you've said ten thousand times, like a rosary. Like a wound she'd stopped expecting to heal." He met Brannoc's eyes. "She also told me her exile was supposed to protect the valley *from* her. Nobody ever told me it might have been protecting her *from* the valley. From the court. From whatever happened that night that nobody wants to draw on a map."

Brannoc stared at him for a long moment.

"You do think like a mapmaker," he said. "Finding the blank spaces."

"They're the important parts. Everything else is just decoration around what nobody will say."

The warden huffed — the closest thing to laughter Elias had gotten from him. Then he stood, crossed to the wall of maps, and pulled down one Elias hadn't noticed, small and old, hidden behind the others. He held it out.

"Then here's a blank space for you." The vellum was soft with handling. On it was drawn the Spire itself, in section — the whole impossible tower cut open like an anatomical diagram, its floors and chambers labeled in the same tight hand. And at its heart, circled twice, a chamber marked with a symbol Elias knew from the prophecy book sitting in his satchel: the sign of the Threshold.

"The old texts say the covenant can be rewoven at the Threshold," Brannoc said. "By the keystone line, on midwinter's eve, when the veil wears thin. Ten years of midwinter eves have come and gone, and nobody's been able to reach it — the roads are paved with broken oaths, and they strip a traveler down to nothing. But you—" he tapped the vellum, right on the little circled room, "—fell through the veil from the valley side, alive, unburned, the first soul to manage it since the breaking. And you fell through carrying a book of prophecy with your own name inked in it a century before you were born."

"I'm not a hero," Elias said. "I draw maps. I'm awkward at parties. I rehearse conversations in the shower."

"Aye, and the prophecy doesn't call for a hero." Brannoc's voice dropped, and the gruffness in it softened into something older and wearier and almost hopeful. "It calls for a mapmaker who mends. Somebody who can see the blank spaces and write them true. The court will want you to be a weapon. Don't let them. Be what you are."

Outside, the light was failing, if light that grey could be said to fail. The snow thickened against the cliff mouth. And down in the valley, on the other side of a rotting door, a woman was sitting alone at a table for two, holding a book that had predicted all of this, and Elias found that the fear in his chest had rearranged itself entirely. It wasn't fear for himself anymore. He knew where he was. He even, terribly, knew why.

It was fear that she would blame herself. That she was blaming herself right now, alone, with ten years of practice at it.

"Midwinter's eve," he said. "How far off?"

"Nine days." Brannoc began banking the lanterns for the night. "And the court will have questions for you long before then. Word travels fast that a stranger fell from the veil with prophecy in his pocket. Rest while you can, mapmaker. Tomorrow you meet the Frozen Court."

He paused at the cavern's inner door, silhouetted against the blue light.

"One more thing," Brannoc said, not turning around. "The girl. Freya. If she's anything like her mother was, she won't sit in that valley grieving. She'll tear the law apart looking for a way through to you. So when you stand in front of the court tomorrow and they ask what you are to her — think carefully what you say. Because everything you tell them, they'll use. Either to open the door for her." He glanced back, frost in his beard and something fierce in his eyes. "Or to seal it forever."

* * *

That night Elias lay on a warden's cot under blankets that smelled of smoke and iron, listening to the Spire breathe above him — that vast, slow, struggling rhythm, a giant's chest half-frozen mid-breath.

He should have been terrified. He was, a little. But mostly he was angry, and the anger was clean and useful, the way a good blade is useful. Someone had broken a covenant and blamed a child for it. Someone had let nine hundred years of promises be struck through in red ink and called it justice. And that child had grown into a woman who laughed at his jokes about contour lines and hid her scarred hands under the table because she thought they made her monstrous.

He'd seen her hands at dinner. She'd thought he hadn't noticed.

*Nine days,* he thought. *Hold on, Freya. Whatever they did to you, whoever wrote your name into a book a hundred years ago and mine beside it — hold on. I'm coming to the part of the map where you are.*

Above him, through the mountain's stone, the long winter fell.
