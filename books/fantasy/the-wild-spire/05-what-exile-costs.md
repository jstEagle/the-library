## 5. What Exile Costs

The valley had rules, and Freya had spent ten years learning them the way you learn a lover's moods: patiently, at cost, in the dark.

Rule one: nothing burned hotter than a hearth-fire. Rule two: no one ran before a storm. Rule three — written nowhere, spoken never, but understood by every soul in Hollowmere down to the children who fed the goats — *she does not go near the veil*.

She broke rule three on a Tuesday, an hour past midnight, wearing Elias Vane's coat.

It was too big for her. He'd left it draped over the chair in his workshop when she'd gone there looking for him and found instead a shattered window, a scorched circle of floorboards, and the smell of ozone that she knew better than her own name. The rift had taken him from inside the safest room in the magic-dead valley. Which meant the dead magic wasn't dead anymore. Which meant it wanted something.

Which meant, if she was very honest with herself in the way she hated being honest, it might have wanted *him because of her*.

She climbed the eastern path with the coat buttoned to her throat and her scarred hands buried in its pockets, where no one could see the pale branching marks that crawled from her knuckles to her wrists like frost given flesh. The valley slept around her. Lamplight in the lower windows. A dog barking once, far off, then thinking better of it. Hollowmere had always been good at pretending not to notice things. It was practically the valley's trade.

The veil lived at the top of the Merrow Falls, where the water fell into mist and the mist fell into nothing. Ten years ago it had been a wall of grey shimmer, humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound is technically over. Tonight, as she crested the last ridge, she saw it standing open.

Not torn. Not broken. *Open*, like a door someone had stepped through and forgotten to close, edges fraying into the dark.

Freya stopped at the boundary stone. Old habit. The wardens had set those stones herself, ten years ago, and she had helped carry them, and every day since they had been the edge of her world: here ends the place where you are allowed to exist.

Elias was on the other side of that edge.

She thought about the date. The stupid, wonderful date they'd planned — the tavern with the crooked sign, the table by the window, how she'd practiced saying something charming in the mirror like a girl half her age. She thought about the way he'd looked at her storm-scarred hands the first time he saw them and hadn't flinched, hadn't asked, just turned his own palm up beside hers as if to say *we all have weather we carry*. She thought about the book with his name inked in it a hundred years ago, and the way his voice had shaken when he showed her: not afraid, exactly. *Chosen.* And how she'd said, before she could stop herself, "That's what I was afraid of."

She had been right to be afraid. She was always right to be afraid. That was the whole tragedy of Freya Delacroix, told in one line.

"Stop," said a voice behind her.

She didn't turn. "Warden Maren." Of course it was Maren. The other wardens were younger; they patrolled with torches and noise. Maren came silent as snowfall and twice as cold.

"You know the law," Maren said. "You wrote half of it."

"I wrote none of it," Freya said. "I was exiled. Exiles don't legislate. They endure."

"They endure," Maren agreed, "and they live longer for it. Step back from the stone, Freya."

"The veil's open."

"I can see that."

"Then you know stepping back won't matter. If it can reach into Elias's workshop, it can reach into my kitchen, your chapel, the cradle in the Aldern house. Whatever's coming through doesn't care about our stones." She finally turned. Maren stood twenty paces off, silver-haired, wrapped in the grey wool of her office, her face lit blue-white by the shimmer. In the veil-light she looked older than Freya had ever let herself notice. Afraid, maybe. Wardens weren't supposed to be afraid. That was practically their trade too.

"Then we seal the town," Maren said. "We do what we've always done. We wait it out."

"We waited out ten years of winters getting longer. You've seen the orchards, Maren. You've seen the river freeze in *May*. The long winter isn't staying politely on its side of the veil. It's coming here. It may already be here." Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the crack, hated that her hands were shaking inside the stolen coat. "A man came to my valley because of me. Because of a book with my mother's covenant in it. And your answer is to pull the blankets up and wait?"

"Our answer has kept three hundred people alive—"

"Our answer has kept three hundred people *small*!"

The words came out of her with force she hadn't meant to spend, and the veil answered.

It shivered. Along its whole height, the grey shimmer rippled like the surface of a pond disturbed by a thrown stone, and the hum — the post-bell hum she remembered from ten years ago, the sound she'd heard in her sleep every night since — rose to something almost melodic. Almost eager. Frost bloomed across the boundary stones, racing toward her feet, and stopped a hand-span short, quivering, like a dog held on a leash.

Maren went very still. "It knows you."

"It's always known me."

"That's not possible. The exile severed you. Seraphine's severance was absolute—"

"You *knew* my mother did the severing?" Freya took a step forward, off the safe side of the stone, frost curling away from her boots now, making room. "You stood at my trial. You voted. And you never once said her name in connection with the binding — you let this whole valley believe the covenant itself cast me out, that some impersonal law—"

"The covenant was dying," Maren said quietly. "Your mother came to us beforehand. She told us what would have to happen, and what it would look like, and she asked—" The old woman stopped. Closed her mouth. Opened it again, and what came out now was quieter still, and worse. "She asked us to let you hate the law instead of her. She said hatred keeps a person warm. Love would have made you come home."

The wind came off the falls and cut through Elias's coat, and Freya stood in it and felt ten years rearrange themselves behind her ribs like furniture in a flooded house.

"She said that," Freya repeated. "She said, *let them hate me, it'll keep her out of the storm*."

"She said the keystone bond would try to call you back the moment you grieved properly. So yes. She built your exile to be unbearable. She built it so you'd be angry enough to stay alive and alive enough to stay angry." Maren's voice didn't waver, but her eyes did, just slightly, catching the veil-light wrong. "I have carried that instruction for ten years, child, and I am tired, and the veil is open, and I think your mother's arithmetic has finally run out."

Freya laughed. It came out wrong, jagged, nothing like laughter. "Protection. You're calling it protection. She took my name, my magic, my *home* — she let a valley full of good people learn to cross the street when they saw me coming — and it was *protection*."

"Yes."

"And the price of that protection was that everyone I loved had to believe I was dangerous."

"Yes."

"And she never once—"

"No," Maren said. "She never once."

The frost at Freya's feet crept another inch closer, patient as tide. Beyond the veil, through the fraying door of it, she could see the Spire now — really see it, for the first time in ten years — a black column stitched with lightning, wrapped in clouds that moved with the slow, exhausted motion of something dying. Snow fell upward around its crown. Even from here, even severed, some part of her chest ached toward it like a phantom limb remembering a hand.

Elias was in there. Somewhere in that dying sky-country, a man who owned one good coat and a grandfather's impossible book was waking up alone in a world that ate strangers.

Freya looked at the boundary stone a long moment. Then she stepped over it.

The frost didn't recoil. It rose — up her boots, along her shins, cool and familiar as water, and where it touched her the old scars lit pale blue, and the hum in her blood that ten years of silence had nearly starved to death woke up and *sang*.

"Freya." Maren's voice, sharp now. "If you cross, the exile breaks. Everything your mother paid—"

"Then bill me."

"If you cross, the valley loses the thing the exile was actually hiding. Not you." Maren's face had gone grey. "Her. What she bound *into* you. Child, do you know why the storm came when you called it? Do you know what your mother put in the keystone before she gave it to a sixteen-year-old girl?"

The question landed like ice water. Freya's hand, halfway to the veil, stopped.

Because no. She didn't know. She knew the storm had answered her — had *obeyed* her, terribly, joyfully, the night everything ended. She had always assumed the power was hers, inherited, her birthright and her crime in equal measure. But Maren was looking at her the way you look at a sealed letter, and suddenly ten years of exile reorganized again, darker: not punishment, not even protection from the Spire.

*Protection from her.*

"What did she put in me?" Freya whispered.

Maren opened her mouth —

— and the veil screamed.

Not hummed, not sang: screamed, a tearing chord of thunder, and the fraying door blew wide, and out of it poured cold so total it silenced the falls mid-fall. Through the opening, vast and distant and unmistakably aware, something looked out. Something that had been waiting a hundred years for a mapmaker, and ten years for a storm-caller, and was done waiting for either.

Maren grabbed her arm. For one heartbeat the old warden's grip was the grip of the woman who'd taught her to fish the Merrow shallows, long ago, before any of it. "Listen to me," Maren said, fast, low. "Midwinter's eve. The Threshold. Your mother will be there — she rules the court now, God help us all. And Freya — whatever she tells you, whatever she *begs* — remember that she put something inside you the night of the storm. Remember it costs truth to get truth from that woman. Every word she gives you, ask what it's hiding."

And she shoved.

Freya went through the veil like a diver through the skin of a lake.

Cold took her — but not the valley's dead cold, the absence of anything. This cold was *alive*, crowded, full of pressure and current and voices layered under voices, and somewhere beneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, the covenant's old rhythm beat on, wounded, limping, refusing to die.

The last thing she heard, from the far side, was Maren's voice, thinning fast:

"—and when you find out what she hid in you, don't let her make you sorry you came."

Then the light changed, and the air changed, and Freya Delacroix stepped out of exile for the first time in ten years — into snow that fell upward, beneath a spire of living storm, with her scars burning like brand-new names.
