## 6. The Storm-Caller's Daughter

The Warden of Hollowmere did not keep her waiting long. That was the first mercy of the night; she suspected it would be the last.

"Sit," said Warden Ilsa Thorne, and gestured to the chair across her desk — a plain oak thing, older than the valley itself, worn smooth by a hundred years of desperate hands gripping its arms. Freya sat. Her palms were sweating. She kept them flat against her knees so no one would see the scars.

The Warden's office sat at the base of the Hollowmere chapel, which was not really a chapel anymore, or had never been one — a stone building with no god in it, only ledgers. The valley had no magic, but it had records. Records were its religion. Somewhere on those shelves was the page that said *Freya Delacroix: exiled. Term: life. Condition: no passage east of the millstone bridge.*

Somewhere on those shelves was the page that said what happened to people who broke that line.

"You want to cross," Ilsa said. Not a question. She was a broad woman gone grey at the temples, with the patient voice of someone who had spent thirty years listening to liars and could now hear truth coming from a mile off, like weather.

"I want my friend back."

"The cartographer." The Warden turned a ledger toward her. Elias's name sat there in fresh ink — he'd been registered at the valley gate like everyone who entered, occupation noted, purpose noted. *Purpose: personal.* Something about seeing it written down made Freya's throat tighten. He had come into a dead valley for a date. For her.

"He didn't choose any of this," Freya said. "The veil took him. It wasn't supposed to be able to do that. Nothing is supposed to be able to do that here."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "And yet." She folded her hands. "You know why the valley is dead, Freya. You've always known, whatever story you tell yourself at night. Hollowmere isn't cursed. Hollowmere is a wound that was cauterized. And you are the brand that did it."

Freya's jaw set. "I've come to bargain, not to be sermonized at."

"Good. Bargaining requires honesty, and honesty is going to hurt you tonight." Ilsa rose, crossed to the shelves, and ran a finger along the spines until she found a slim volume bound in storm-grey leather. She set it on the desk between them but did not open it. "Before we talk price, you should understand what you're asking to pay. You think exile means living here. Growing onions. Going on dates." A flicker of something almost gentle crossed her face. "Exile is a door held shut. And doors held shut are held by someone's hand. Do you understand me?"

"No," Freya said honestly.

"Then let me show you whose."

Ilsa opened the book. And the past opened with it.

* * *

Ten years ago, the night of the storm, the sky over the Spire had been the color of a bruise going green.

Freya remembered that color because she had made it. Sixteen years old, standing on the balcony of her mother's tower with rain in her hair and power in her blood like a second heartbeat, and she had reached up — not meaning to, meaning nothing, meaning only *more* — and the sky had answered.

Her mother's voice behind her: *"Freya. Let go."*

"I'm not holding anything!"

"You are. You always are. Let go."

She hadn't. That was the truth of it, the truth she'd spent ten years sanding down into something more forgivable: *it was an accident, I didn't mean it, the storm came through me.* All true. And none of it changed the fact that when her mother said *let go*, Freya had gripped tighter. Because for the first time in her life, the storm felt like hers. Because Seraphine Delacroix had spent sixteen years teaching her daughter restraint — hold it in, hold it down, hold it close, never show them, never show anyone — and in that moment on the balcony, with lightning walking the clouds like a creature waking, Freya had understood that restraint was just another word for *smaller*. And she had refused to be smaller.

So the storm grew. It walked out of the Spire and down the mountainside, and it found the covenant-stones — the ancient ringed pillars where the two realms shook hands, where the Wild Spire's living storms fed the lowland valley's quiet seasons — and it broke them. Not all at once. One pillar, then another, like teeth knocked from a jaw. The covenant screamed. Freya heard it scream; she could still hear it sometimes, in the space between sleeping and waking, a sound like a choir changing its mind.

Then her mother had done the thing mothers should never have to do.

Seraphine came out onto the balcony with the keystone bond already kindling around her hands — silver light woven through with stormlight, the inheritance of every Delacroix firstborn for nine generations, the living thread that anchored the covenant to flesh. She had been saving it. Hoarding it, maybe. Waiting until Freya was ready.

There was no time left for ready.

"Hold out your hands," Seraphine said.

"What is it?"

"It's yours. It was always going to be yours. Hold out your hands and it will hold the realms together even while the stones heal, even while—" She stopped. Even through the thunder, Freya heard what her mother didn't say. *Even while I find a way to fix what you broke.*

Freya looked at the light in her mother's hands, and she saw — this was the detail she could never explain to anyone, the detail that had cost her everything — she saw a cage. Silver bars. A lifetime of being the thing that holds, the thing that anchors, the thing that cannot move without everything falling. Her mother wearing that cage so gracefully Freya had never once noticed it until now, until it was offered to her, sized to her wrists.

"I don't want it," she said.

*"Freya."*

"I don't WANT it!" And the storm inside her surged at the word, and the nearest covenant-stone went dark, and somewhere below them a valley began, very slowly, to die.

What happened next happened fast. Seraphine caught the keystone bond as it guttered, unclaimed, and did the only thing left: she drove it into herself, alone, unfinished, a bond meant for two bodies borne by one. Freya saw her mother's face when it took her. She would see that face for the rest of her life. Not pain. Worse. *Relief* — the terrible relief of a woman who has finally been given permission to sacrifice, who has spent years dreading the moment her daughter would have to carry this, and who now gets to carry it instead, gloriously, martyrdom arriving like a gift.

Then the court arrived. Wardens in grey, storm-caller elders with their hoods back, faces lit by the dying stones. And Seraphine, half-burned by her own salvation, stood up straight and gave the judgment that ended Freya's childhood:

"She called the storm. She broke the stones. Exile to Hollowmere. The valley will be sealed behind her — no magic in, none out — until the covenant is mended."

Not a lie, exactly. Freya had called the storm. Freya had broken the stones.

But her mother had looked at her when she said it, and Freya had spent ten years telling herself she'd imagined what was in that look. Pity. Regret. Love, even, twisted strange.

She was starting, tonight, in the Warden's office, to suspect it had been something else entirely.

* * *

In the present, the lamp hissed. Ilsa Thorne turned the grey book around so Freya could see the page.

It was a warden's record, dated the morning after the storm. The official account. *Storm-caller's daughter breaches covenant-stones. Keystone bond refused by heir. Realm-mother assumes bond alone. Valley sealed.*

"Refused," Freya read aloud. Her voice sounded far away.

"That word has bothered me for ten years," Ilsa said. "Officially, refusing the keystone bond is treason — it's the whole reason your exile stands. But look at the phrasing. Not *rejected*. Not *fled from*. *Refused*, past tense, complete. As though it were offered and declined in full knowledge." She tapped the page. "Wardens write precisely, girl. Someone chose that word. And I've spent a decade wondering why a mother would put the sentence in writing that damns her own daughter — unless the damning was the point."

Freya's hands had gone cold. "Say what you mean."

"I mean that I was there that morning. I took the testimony. And I watched Seraphine Delacroix dictate this record with her burned hands wrapped in silk, steady as stone, and I watched her choose every word." Ilsa leaned forward. "Here is what I know about the night of the storm that isn't in any ledger. There were three factions in the Spire then, and one of them wanted the covenant broken. Wanted the winter court ascendant, wanted the lowlands bled dry. Your mother broke them — publicly, brutally — six months before the storm. But breaking a faction isn't killing it. And on the night your storm came down the mountain, someone else's hands were on those stones too."

The room seemed to tilt. "That's not possible. The stones—"

"Broke under two forces, not one. I've read the residue reports from the wardens who examined them before the seal closed. Your storm, yes. And beneath it, threaded through it, something deliberate. Something that used your storm as cover." Ilsa sat back. "Your mother knew. I believe your mother has known for ten years exactly what happened to those stones, and who did it, and she let the world believe it was you."

"Why?" The word tore out of her. "Why would she—"

"Why does any ruler make a hard choice badly? Because the good version of the choice was gone. If she named the true saboteurs, faction war — open war, with the covenant already broken and both realms bleeding. If she named you, she got a scapegoat everyone would believe, because you *had* called the storm. And she got you out of the Spire, away from the knives, into the one place in creation where nothing magical could ever touch you again." Ilsa's voice dropped. "Exile wasn't your punishment, Freya. It was your hiding place. She built it around you and locked it from the outside, and she has paid for it every day since — alone, holding a bond meant for two, growing older faster than any storm-caller should."

Freya couldn't breathe. Ten years of hatred rearranged itself in her chest, and there was no room for it anymore, and nothing to put where it had been. Grief, maybe. Or the beginning of understanding, which hurts worse.

"She let me hate her," Freya whispered.

"Yes."

"She let the whole world hate me."

"Yes. And she'll let you go on hating her if it keeps you safe. Which brings us," Ilsa said, with brutal gentleness, "to the bargain. Because I can open the veil for you — there are ways, old ways, roads the seal doesn't watch. But the seal is powered by your exile. Every step you take out of this valley will take something from you. The road east is paved with oaths, and it collects."

"Take what?"

"Memories. Of this place. Of the life you built here. The valley can't hold magic, but it can hold *you*, and it will not give you up politely." Ilsa met her eyes. "You may walk out of Hollowmere remembering how to survive the Spire and forgetting the name of the man at the bakery who saves you the end pieces. That is the price. I won't soften it, and I won't lie about it, because you've had enough lying done on your behalf for one lifetime."

Freya sat very still. Outside the window, the valley lay dark and ordinary — the mill, the bridge, the crooked lane to her cottage where a prophecy book and a half-written letter were waiting, where a cartographer had laughed at her terrible joke about contour lines and meant it.

Elias was on the other side of the veil. In the realm where her mother was burning herself alive to hold two worlds together with her bare hands.

"Open the road," Freya said.

Isla studied her for a long moment. Then she reached beneath the desk and produced a small iron key, old as the chapel, and set it beside the grey book.

"One thing more," she said. "If you reach the Spire — and you will, you're your mother's daughter, stubbornness runs in the blood like weather — you'll find the court is not what you remember. The winter has changed people. And the woman on the throne is not the woman who condemned you. Ask her about the stones, Freya. Make her say it aloud. Whatever else happens — whatever you decide to forgive or refuse — make her say it where the whole frozen court can hear. Truth is the one thing the Spire is starving for more than warmth."

Freya picked up the key. It was warm, somehow, in her cold hand — the first warm thing the valley had given her in ten years.

"And if she won't say it?"

Ilsa Thorne smiled without any kindness in it at all.

"Then burn the court down, girl. Some inheritances are worth taking whether they're offered or not."
