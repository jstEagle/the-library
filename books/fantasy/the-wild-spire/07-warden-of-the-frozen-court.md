## 7. Warden of the Frozen Court

They dressed him in someone else's clothes.

Brannoc's people — if they were Brannoc's people; Elias still wasn't sure where the warden ended and the court began — arrived at the waystation before dawn with a bundle wrapped in frost-stiff silk. Inside: a long coat the color of wet slate, a collar worked with silver thread that spelled out something in an alphabet Elias didn't recognize, and boots lined with fur from an animal he chose not to think about.

"Court dress," Brannoc said, arms crossed, watching Elias struggle with the coat. "You'll be presented to the Frozen Court within the hour. Wash, eat, and for the love of the old covenant, don't speak unless spoken to."

"I thought you were taking me somewhere safe."

"This *is* safe. Relatively. The court has heard the prophecy same as everyone." Brannoc's jaw worked, like he was chewing on words he didn't want to swallow. "A century ago, the book said a mapmaker would come across the veil when the winter grew deepest. It said this mapmaker would mend what was broken."

Elias's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going.

"And you found me," he said carefully, "holding a map."

"You were holding the book."

"The book is a *prophecy*, not a—"

"It's both. Everything in the Spire is both, eventually." Brannoc turned away, barking orders at the others, and the conversation was over.

Elias stood there in his borrowed coat, hands shaking, doing the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since he woke in this world. The prophecy named a mapmaker who mends. He made maps. He could not mend so much as a torn sleeve without needle and thread, and he had certainly never done anything that anyone in their right mind would call magic. Freya had called down a storm that got her exiled from an entire valley. He had once gotten lost in Sheffield for six hours because he trusted a footpath that wasn't on the ordinance survey.

And now he was being dressed for court.

*Tell them,* said a small, honest voice in his head. *Tell them it's a mistake. Tell them the book must mean someone else.*

But then he thought about the ink. The brown-black ink, faded a century, spelling out *Elias Vane* beside tonight's date — beside a date that had already passed by the time he'd read it, already passed before he'd ever opened the cover. His grandfather had salvaged that book from somewhere. From *somewhere*. Which meant someone had written his name a hundred years before he was born, and either the universe had made a spectacular clerical error, or—

Or he was exactly where the book said he'd be.

That wasn't the same as being who the book said he'd be. But nobody here seemed to know the difference, and Elias had spent his whole career learning one lesson above all others: the difference between a true map and a false one is only visible to someone who already knows the territory.

He did not know the territory. Neither did they.

*Play along,* he told himself, and hated how easily the thought came. *Figure it out later.*

The coat fit better than it should have. That bothered him too.

* * *

The Frozen Court convened in a hall that had once, clearly, been magnificent.

Elias saw the bones of it as they walked him in — the soaring arches, the windows of colored ice instead of glass, the great vaulted ceiling carved with constellations he half-recognized and half-didn't, stars rearranged into patterns no astronomer on Earth had ever charted. This had been a place built to hold wonder.

Now everything was sheathed in frost. The arches wore rimes of white like old lace. The colored windows had gone milky, their light diffused into a pale wash that made the whole hall feel underwater. Ice furred the edges of every surface, thickening year over year, the way dust gathers in a house no one can quite bring themselves to clean. At the far end, beneath a dais of black stone, the frost lay deepest — a glacier's worth of it, swallowing the lower steps.

The court itself sat along both walls in tiers: perhaps forty figures, robed in whites and greys and blues, faces weathered or ageless depending on the light. Wardens, mostly, he gathered — the ones Brannoc had called the faction of the old oath. On the dais itself sat three chairs, and only one was occupied: a tall woman with iron-grey hair braided with bands of silver, her eyes the flat pale blue of deep ice. She did not rise. She did not smile. She looked at Elias the way he imagined a geologist looks at a rock sample that might contain something valuable or nothing at all.

"That's Warden-Regent Ilsa," Brannoc murmured. "She holds the court together since—" He stopped himself.

"Since the covenant broke," Elias said.

"Since the Storm-Caller fell." Brannoc's voice had an odd weight on the word. "Careful."

A herald — a thin young man whose breath fogged even though the hall wasn't cold, exactly; it was something older than cold — struck a staff against the floor three times. The sound rang strangely, hanging in the air a beat too long.

"The court receives the traveler," the herald intoned. "Come you across the veil, by storm and rift, as the old text foretold?"

Here it was, then. The first question, and already a trap, because the honest answer was *I came across a rift, yes, but not on purpose, and I was holding a woman's hand at the time and screaming.*

"I came across the veil," Elias said.

True. Every word. He was starting to understand the shape of this game.

The herald consulted a scroll. Around the hall, the wardens leaned forward in their tiers, and Elias felt their attention like weather pressure — the same pre-storm weight he'd felt in Hollowmere's air, except here it came off *people* instead of sky.

"Say your name and craft."

"Elias Vane. Cartographer."

A ripple went through the tiers. Not large. But real. Somewhere to his left, a warden whispered a word that sounded like *vane* and meant something else in whatever language lived under this one. The word for weathervane, maybe. Or wind-reader.

"And the book?" the herald pressed. "The text recovered from the outer world, bearing the mark of the mending?"

Elias reached into the coat — his grandfather's book, which had crossed the veil in his satchel, which was possibly the single most important object in two worlds right now, and which he had definitely, absolutely, not read closely enough. He held it up.

The ripple became a murmur.

Warden-Regent Ilsa rose for the first time. She descended the dais steps slowly, frost cracking under her heels like thin ice on a pond, and stopped an arm's length away. Up close, her face was a map of its own — lines drawn deep by years of holding things together that wanted to fall apart.

"Show me the page," she said.

Elias opened the book to the marked page. His own name. Tonight's date — yesterday's date now. And beneath it, in the same ancient ink, the line he hadn't let himself read aloud yet:

*The mapmaker who mends shall stand where the veil wears thinnest, and the winter shall know his hand.*

The regent read it in silence. Then she looked up at him, and her eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse than if they'd been cruel.

"A hundred years the factions have fought over what the winter means," she said, her voice carrying to every tier without effort. "Some say it is punishment. Some say it is decay. Some say it is the slow death of a realm abandoned by its keystone." She touched the page, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound. "And the text says a mapmaker comes, and stands, and the winter knows his hand."

She closed the distance another step. Close enough that Elias could see the frost on her eyelashes.

"Tell me, cartographer. What does your craft teach you about mending?"

It was, he would think later, the most dangerous question anyone had ever asked him. Because it wasn't *can you do magic*. It wasn't even *are you the one*. It was a craftsman's question, a professional question, the kind of question you ask a fellow worker to see if they know their trade.

And his trade, at least, was real.

"A map," Elias said, "doesn't mend anything. It shows what's broken."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"Go on."

"A good mapmaker doesn't flatter the territory. Doesn't leave out the marsh because the marsh is inconvenient, doesn't round the mountain because rounding it makes the road look shorter. If your maps have been lying to you — about where the veil is thin, about what the winter is actually doing, about—" he gestured vaguely at the frosted arches, at the glacier swallowing the dais steps, "*all of this* — then the first act of mending isn't repair. It's honest measurement. You can't fix what you won't look at."

He heard his own voice steadying as he went, the way it always did when he talked shop. This was the one part of him that wasn't borrowed.

Ilsa studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, the corner of her mouth moved. Not a smile. An acknowledgment.

"The court has not heard honesty from a stranger in a hundred years," she said. "Mostly it hears hope, and hope lies worse than fear does." She turned back toward the dais. "The court will consider the traveler's standing. In the meantime, he will be lodged in the east tower, under wardens' eyes, and treated as—" she paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—a guest of consequence."

*Guest of consequence.* Elias had been around academics and government survey offices long enough to translate: *prisoner we're being polite to while we decide if you're useful or dangerous.*

He bowed, because bowing seemed structural here, and let them lead him out.

Behind him, he heard the tiers begin to argue in low voices. He caught fragments — *the text says*, *but the keystone line*, *a hundred years and now this*, *does the court truly believe a soft-skinned outsider* — and understood that he had just become the most contested object in the Spire.

Not a person. A claim.

*Good,* said the part of him that was terrified. *Terrified is fine. Terrified means you're paying attention.*

* * *

The east tower room had a window, a bed, a brazier that burned without heat — light without warmth, which felt like a thesis statement for the entire realm — and a door that locked from the outside with a sound like a tomb closing.

Elias gave himself ten minutes to panic. He set the terms deliberately, the way you schedule a rest stop on a long survey day: ten minutes, sitting on the edge of the bed, hands between knees, breathing. He let the terror have all of him — the impostor's cold sweat, the certainty that tomorrow someone would ask him to demonstrate power he didn't have, the image of Freya somewhere on the other side of a veil he couldn't find, alone, blaming herself for a rift that had swallowed him mid-sentence.

Then the ten minutes ended, and he stood up, and he got to work.

Because here was the thing about impostors: the good ones aren't liars. The good ones are people who know exactly what they lack and arrange the world so the lack never gets tested. And Elias lacked magic the way a fish lacks a bicycle — completely, structurally, irreversibly. So the work was simple, if not easy: build a version of the mapmaker who mends that required no magic whatsoever, and make it so useful, so *correct*, that no one wanted to test the parts that weren't there.

He started with the window.

The view looked south, over the frozen terraces of the Spire's city — and it was a city, he realized, or had been. Spires within spires, bridges between towers, gardens gone to white sculpture. Beyond it all, the land climbed toward the great storm-walls he'd glimpsed from the ridge with Brannoc: the living storms, the heart of the realm, and even from here he could see they burned low. Dimmed. Like coals banked for a night that had lasted a decade.

He pulled out his field notebook — real paper, real pencil, the tools that had crossed the veil with him and suddenly mattered more than anything he owned — and began to sketch. Not art. Measurement. Sightlines, distances estimated by parallax, the angle of the dimmed storms against the horizon. Notes on where frost lay thickest, where paths had been cleared recently versus abandoned. A city's behavior written in its maintenance patterns; he'd learned that mapping mining towns. Where people still shoveled, people still hoped.

By the time the light through the milky window shifted — afternoon, maybe; the Spire's days ran long and grey — he had three pages of observations and a working theory he didn't like:

The winter wasn't spreading evenly. It radiated outward from the dais end of the court hall. From the empty seats.

Two other chairs stood on that dais. Two other rulers' places, vacant, buried deepest in frost. Whatever the covenant had bound together, it had bound it in threes, and two-thirds of the binding was missing, and everything cold in this world poured from those empty chairs like cold pouring from an open door.

He wrote that down too, and underlined it twice.

Someone knocked — a coded knock, three-and-two, and Brannoc let himself in carrying a tray and a scowl.

"You did well today," the warden said, which from Brannoc sounded like an apology. "Too well. The regent's faction wants to formally name you Mapmaker Who Mends at the next convening. The keystone faction wants you examined by their readers, to test whether the prophecy's blood runs in you." He set the tray down. "You want to know what the readers do to men who fail the testing?"

"No," Elias said honestly.

"Neither does anyone. No one's failed in a hundred years, because no one's been fool enough to volunteer." Brannoc leaned against the wall, arms folded. "So. Tomorrow, you'll be asked to choose: naming or testing. Choose wrong, cartographer, and this ends badly."

Elias looked at the tray — dark bread, pale cheese, tea that steamed *cold*, steam rising off it without warmth, which was going to take some getting used to — and then at his notebook, open to the sketch of the empty chairs.

"Brannoc. The dais in the court hall. There are three seats."

Something flickered across the warden's face. Grief, maybe. Or guilt.

"The Storm-Caller's seat," Elias said. "And one more. Whose?"

Brannoc was quiet long enough that the cold steam finished rising and faded.

"The third seat belonged to the Warden-General of the Threshold," he said finally. "Keeper of the veil itself. The last one to hold it was—" He stopped. Started again. "The last one was Seraphine Delacroix. Before she crossed to the valley side. Before her daughter—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

Freya's mother. Freya's mother had been keeper of the veil, and Freya had never said a word of it, and now Elias was sitting in her mother's empty chair's shadow wearing a borrowed coat, pretending to be a prophet, while the woman herself was presumably on the other side of the veil deciding whether to tear her exile open to come find him.

He picked up the pencil. His hand, he noticed, had almost stopped shaking.

"When is the next convening?" he asked.

"Dawn."

"Then I need ink," Elias said, "and access to every map this court owns of the veil. Because if I'm going to stand in front of both factions tomorrow and claim to be the man from the book—" he tapped the notebook, on the sketch of the three chairs, the theory he didn't like but couldn't shake, "—then I'm going to walk in knowing exactly where the veil wears thinnest. And Brannoc? I'm going to need you to tell me everything you know about Seraphine Delacroix."

Outside the tower window, far to the south, the dimmed storms flickered — once, briefly, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Like something answering.
