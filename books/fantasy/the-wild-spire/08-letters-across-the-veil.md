## 8. Letters Across the Veil

### Freya

The rift opened again at dusk, three days after it took him, and this time it stayed.

Freya had spent those three days doing everything a woman in a magic-dead valley could do, which was almost nothing. She had gone back to the meadow where the veil had torn open and found the grass bent in a spiral, frost-edged even though Hollowmere's autumn had been mild. She had dug her mother's letters out of the floorboards beneath the hearthstone and read them until the candle guttered. She had not slept. Sleep felt like an insult.

On the third evening, as she stood in the doorway of Elias's borrowed room above the map shop — she had no right to be there, and went anyway — the air above his desk shivered like water touched by a fingertip.

Then it split.

Not violently, not the way it had torn him away from her hand. This was quieter: a seam of pale light hanging in the air, roughly the size of a window, its edges flickering like a candle in wind. Through it she saw snow. Not valley snow — *storm* snow, falling sideways through a violet-dark sky, lit from below by something enormous and slow-breathing and blue.

"Elias," she said. Her voice cracked on the second syllable.

He was there in seconds, or what passed for seconds across a veil between worlds — a face pressed toward hers out of the cold, older somehow than it had been four days ago, wind-scoured, alive. His breath fogged the light between them.

"Freya." He said her name the way a man says a word he'd stopped believing in. "I've been trying to get to this side for two days. Brannoc says the rift thins when your side is quiet. Whatever you were doing before — stop doing it."

"I was sleeping," she said. Then, honestly: "I was reading your grandfather's book."

"Don't stop reading it." He laughed, a short, disbelieving sound that steamed in the frozen air. "God. You have no idea how strange it is to see you. I woke up in a storm the size of a country, Freya. I thought I'd imagined you."

She reached out and her fingers met the surface of the seam — cool, taut, like the skin of a drum. On the other side, Elias raised his hand and matched it, palm to palm with a whole world between them. She could feel nothing but pressure. It was still the most contact she'd had with anyone in ten years who knew what she was.

"Tell me everything," she said. "Start wherever you woke up. Don't leave anything out because it sounds impossible. I grew up with a mother who turned into weather."

* * *

### Elias

So he told her. All of it, in order, because she asked him not to edit, and because saying it aloud to someone who believed him made it realer than it had any right to be.

He told her about waking in the roots of the Spire — the mountain that wasn't a mountain, the storm that had learned architecture. About Brannoc hauling him out of a snowdrift by his collar, grumbling that the prophecy book had finally sent them someone and they'd better hope it hadn't sent a fool. About the long winter: twenty years of cold creeping down from the Spire's crown, storms dying one by one like lamps going out in a house where nobody lives anymore.

"The wardens think it's grief," Elias said. "That's the word Brannoc used. Grief. The realm is mourning the covenant, and the mourning looks like winter." He rubbed his face; his knuckles were raw with cold that never quite left. "And everyone here thinks I'm the one who fixes it. The mapmaker who mends. There's a court, Freya. A frozen court, with thrones made of ice that never melts, and they put me in front of them and asked me to perform miracles."

"What did you do?"

"I lied," he said. "I'm apparently good at lying now. I told them I needed to study the old texts first. Survey the damage. Make maps." A grim smile. "It turns out making maps is the one thing I actually know how to do, so at least my fraud has production value."

Freya's laugh came through the seam thin and bright, and it undid something in his chest. He realized he'd crossed into another world, been mistaken for a savior, and nearly frozen twice — and the worst part of all of it had been thinking he'd never hear that laugh again.

"Now you," he said. "Explain the covenant. Brannoc talks about it like scripture but won't explain the actual mechanics. And explain why your name opens doors here that shouldn't open."

Her face changed. He watched her decide to tell the truth — watched it happen, the small surrender of it, the way she squared her shoulders like a woman stepping into cold water.

"My family held the covenant," she said. "For eleven generations. The Delacroix line. We're — the word is *keystone*. One person in each generation carries the bond that holds the two realms together. The Spire's storms feed the valley's seasons, and the valley's stillness anchors the Spire so it doesn't tear itself apart. It only works if both sides hold at once. My mother carried it before me. She wanted me to take it when I came of age."

"And you didn't."

"I ran," Freya said simply. "I was nineteen and I wanted an ordinary life and I thought refusing would just... end it. Instead the night I refused, the covenant frayed, and every wild thing my blood had ever called came looking for a keystone that wasn't there. The storm over Hollowmere. The one they exiled me for." Her jaw tightened. "Except now I know it wasn't exile for punishment. The Warden told me. My mother sent me here because a broken covenant needs somewhere dead for its broken pieces to fall. I wasn't thrown away, Elias. I was *stored*."

Elias leaned closer to the seam. Snow melted against his cheek from the warmth of his own breath and refroze instantly. "Then we can fix this," he said. "You're the keystone. Take up the bond, whatever that means, and the winter breaks."

"That's the part I haven't told you." Her voice dropped. "Taking up the bond means binding myself to the Spire permanently. Living keystone. I'd never come home. The valley would forget me inside a year — that's how it works, the anchor can't have loose threads. Everything I built here, everyone I—" She stopped. Started again, steadier. "Everyone would forget I existed. That's the price. That's always been the price, and my mother never told me, because she thought if she told me I'd refuse. Which I would have."

The seam flickered. Both of them flinched.

"It's thinning," Elias said. "Brannoc says midwinter's eve, the veil between the realms goes soft everywhere along the Threshold — the old crossing point in the mountains. It's the one night the rift doesn't need to be forced. If you came through—"

"I can't force passage. The Warden will only help me if—"

"Midwinter's eve," Elias repeated. "Nine days. Find a way. I'll be at the Threshold on the Spire side, and I'll bring the book, and Brannoc, and every fact I've mapped about how bad things are. You bring whatever a keystone needs to make a choice." He hesitated, then said the thing underneath everything: "Freya. If the price is forgetting, don't pay it. I'll find another way. I got dragged into this prophecy by accident. I'm not going to be the reason you erase yourself."

She was quiet for a moment. Through the seam her eyes caught the storm-light of his world and the lamplight of hers at once, and he understood suddenly that she lived in exactly that place all the time — half in each world, fully in neither.

"You're the first person in ten years who's offered to find another way," she said. "Everyone else just tells me what I owe."

"You don't owe anyone your existence."

"No," she agreed softly. "But maybe I can offer something better than sacrifice. I keep thinking about what the Warden said — protection, not punishment. Maybe the covenant broke because it was built on bloodlines and duty and secrets. Maybe what's wrong with it isn't that it's weak. It's that nobody ever *chose* it." A ghost of a smile. "Ask your court if the covenant requires a Delacroix. Ask them what happens if it just requires someone willing."

"I'll ask."

"Carefully. You're an impostor, remember."

"I'm an impostor with excellent production values," Elias said. "Nine days. Midwinter's eve. The Threshold. Don't be late — I have no idea which side of a collapsing world I'll be standing on, and I'd hate to wait alone."

"Nine days," Freya said. And then, quieter, the words almost too small for the seam to carry: "Come back to the table, Elias. The one where we never got dinner. When this is over. I'm owed a first date."

"You're owed several years of them," he said. "Consider this the world's most catastrophic attempt to extend the evening."

The seam began to close, edges curling inward like paper in fire.

"Midwinter's eve," she said.

"Midwinter's eve," he said.

And the light folded shut, leaving only the smell of ozone and snow, and a spiral of frost blooming across the desk where his hand had rested — five fingerprints, precise as a cartographer's marks, drawn in ice on the far side of the world.

### Freya

She sat down among her mother's letters and did not cry, though it was close.

Nine days. She spread the letters out across the floor in chronological order, a decade of her mother's careful script, and read them the way she should have read them years ago — not as evidence of betrayal but as instructions from a woman who had been afraid.

*The bond does not ask for your life,* Seraphine had written, eleven years ago, in the letter Freya had never finished. *It asks for your consent. I forgot the difference. God forgive me, I forgot the difference, and I taught you the covenant as a cage because no one ever told me it could be anything else.*

At the bottom of the last page, newer ink, written after the exile, after everything:

*If you are reading this, you have already done what I could not. You left. Forgive me for calling it treason. Some inheritances should be refused.*

Freya gathered the letters into a neat stack, tied them with the same cord she used for seed packets, and set them beside the prophecy book — his grandfather's book, with both their names in century-old ink, waiting for midwinter's eve to mean something.

Nine days to find a way through a sealed veil, past a Warden who traded in truths, into a realm that ate memories.

She banked the fire, pulled on her coat, and went out into the valley night to do the one thing she had sworn she'd never do again.

She was going to practice calling weather.
