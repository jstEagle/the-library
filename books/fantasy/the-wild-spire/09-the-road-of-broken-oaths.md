## 9. The Road of Broken Oaths

Brannoc came for her at moonset, the way he'd promised through the rift — no torches, no words until the tree line swallowed the last of Hollowmere's lantern light behind them.

He looked wrong in the valley. That was Freya's first thought, watching him step out of the dark between two dead birches: a man built for storms standing in a place where even the wind had been declawed. His warden's coat was grey as slate and moved like it remembered weather that wasn't here anymore. He carried a satchel over one shoulder and an expression like a closed door.

"You're smaller than your letters," he said.

"You're taller than your voice." Her own voice shook. She'd spent three days writing to him through the thinning rift — scraps of parchment pushed against the seam in the air behind her mother's house, ink bleeding sideways into a world she couldn't see. Three days of Elias's handwriting coming back to her, cramped and careful, describing a sky that froze from the top down. *Midwinter's eve,* they'd agreed. But midwinter was nine days off, and the rift was closing faster than either of them had guessed, and Brannoc had come early because there would be no later.

"The road won't wait," Brannoc said. "Neither will I. Last chance to turn around, storm-caller's daughter."

Freya looked back at the valley one time. The cottage with its crooked chimney smoke. The herb beds she'd planted the first spring, when exile still felt like punishment instead of practice. The bakery where she'd bought the bread for the date that never happened, the baker's daughter waving at her through the window, laughing at something Freya couldn't hear across the distance.

She made herself memorize all of it.

She didn't know yet how much memorizing she'd need to do.

"Let's go," she said.

* * *

The veil stood where her mother's old letters said it would — or had stood, once. Now it was less a door than a wound: a vertical seam of grey light hanging in the air above the frozen creek at the valley's edge, shivering like heat-haze in reverse. Cold poured out of it. Not valley cold, which was honest and seasonal, but the other kind — the kind that fell upward out of storm-clouds.

Brannoc worked the seam open with his bare hands, muttering words in a language that sounded like rockfall. "The covenant's dead weight holds it half-open," he grunted. "Lucky for us. Unlucky for everything else." He glanced at her. "Once we're through, there's a road. You'll know it when you see it."

"What road?"

"The only road left into the Spire since the winter deepened. The wardens call it the Oathway." He hesitated, and Freya learned in that hesitation that the gruffness was armor, not nature. "I'd tell you what it costs if I understood it myself. The old texts are vague on purpose. Roads like this one don't like being described."

"That's comforting."

"It's not meant to be." He held the seam wide. "It's meant to make you choose with your eyes open. Go."

She went.

Crossing felt like being read. Not painfully — thoroughly. Like a hand leafing through every page of her at once, checking for something, finding it, and letting go. Then grey light, then pressure, then —

Sky.

The Wild Spire's sky was a cathedral of cloud, tier upon tier of it, lit from within by slow purple pulses that might have been lightning thinking about striking. Storms hung in the air like architecture. And everywhere, threaded through the grandeur, the rot: frost creeping up the flanks of floating islands, snow falling from clouds that should have been raining, a whole realm wearing its own death like a beautiful shroud.

Freya had been born in this sky. She'd forgotten the smell of it — ozone and wet stone and something floral underneath, some flowering thing that bloomed only in thunderheads. Her eyes stung.

"There," Brannoc said.

The road began at their feet.

It ran out across a chasm toward the distant root-mountains of the Spire itself, a bridge of pale stone wide enough for two abreast, and it was not stone. Freya knelt and pressed her palm to its surface and felt — voices. Faint, layered, countless. A murmur under the texture like a shell held to the ear, except the sea inside it was made of promises.

"Oathstone," Brannoc said quietly. "When the covenant broke, everyone who abandoned it — every warden, every courtier, every common soul who swore the old oath and let it lapse rather than fight for it — their sworn words had to go somewhere. Swearing doesn't vanish, girl. It pools. It paved itself."

"How long is it?"

"Three days' walk, if you don't stop." He wouldn't look at her. "And you can't stop."

* * *

The first memory went somewhere past the second mile, and it took her by surprise so completely that she sat down hard on the oathstone without meaning to.

One moment she was walking, cataloguing the frost-flowers growing along the road's edges — actual flowers, crystallized blooms that chimed faintly when the wind touched them — and the next, a hole opened in her head where a Tuesday should have been. A specific Tuesday. She could feel its outline the way you feel the outline of a tooth that isn't there anymore: Mrs. Alder teaching her to proof dough in the bakery kitchen, flour on both their forearms, laughter. She knew such a Tuesday had existed. She knew Mrs. Alder existed. But the shape of that afternoon — the warmth of it, the specific golden light through the kitchen window — was gone, replaced by a smooth blank certainty that *nothing much happened that day.*

"No," she whispered. She gripped the road. "No, no — Brannoc—"

"The road takes payment," he said, and his voice was gentler than she'd ever heard it, which somehow made it worse. "It was paved with people who gave nothing. So it takes from those who cross it. One memory per mile, near enough. Small ones first. It always starts small."

"Nobody told me—"

"I told you the old texts were vague."

"You could have been *less* vague!"

"Yes." He crouched in front of her, and up close she saw the exhaustion carved into him, the frost-scars on his knuckles, a man who had walked this road before and remembered exactly what it cost him, though he clearly could not remember what. "I could have. And then you'd have stayed, and Elias Vane would freeze with the rest of us, and both realms would finish dying politely. So yes, Freya Delacroix. I lied by omission, and I'll carry that. Walk anyway."

She wanted to hate him. She settled for hating the road.

She got up, and she walked.

* * *

By the end of the first day she had lost, by her own frantic count, eleven miles' worth of herself.

Small things, mostly, and that was the cruelty of it — the road was surgical. The name of the baker's dog. The tune her father used to hum while sharpening knives, though she retained the memory of his hands and lost the sound forever. Which window in the cottage stuck in summer. The taste of the valley's blackberries — she knew she had loved them; the loving remained as a fact, stripped of all evidence. Each loss arrived without drama. A blink. A smooth patch. A day going quiet behind her like a house whose lights had been switched off room by room.

What terrified her wasn't what was gone. It was the arithmetic. Elias was somewhere ahead of her, in the Spire's heart, alive because a century-old book said his name and a broken covenant needed a mapmaker who mends. Nine days until midwinter's eve, and the road was taking a mile of her per mile, and Hollowmere was — how far? How many miles of her were there between here and home?

"Brannoc." Night had come, the Spire's version of it — the purple pulses dimming to a bruised glow, frost thickening on the oathstone. They'd made camp in the lee of a shattered waymarker, a pillar carved with the names of wardens dead five hundred years. "How many memories does a person have?"

He looked up from the small fire he'd coaxed from storm-glass shards — fire that burned cold blue and gave light without warmth. "More than the road is long," he said. "If that's what you're asking."

"And if it's not?"

He was quiet a long moment. "Then I'll say what the wardens say among ourselves, and you'll keep it between us. The road takes small things first because small things hold the big ones up. Lose enough beams and the roof comes down. I've seen a walker reach the Spire's gates who couldn't say why his feet kept moving. Couldn't remember who he was walking toward." The blue firelight made his face hollow. "But I've also seen walkers arrive whole enough. The road takes what you haven't defended."

"How do you defend a memory?"

"You don't defend it." He fed a shard of storm-glass to the fire. "You spend it. Tell it. Out loud, to another person, before the road gets there. What's witnessed in words, the stone can't take clean — it leaves an echo, a shape where the memory was. Enough echoes, and you arrive as yourself. Mostly." A pause. "My mother taught me that. I think. Someone taught me that."

Freya stared at him. "You've told yours away."

"All but the important ones." His jaw tightened. "And I no longer recall which those were. Sleep, storm-caller's daughter. Tomorrow we walk, and tomorrow you talk my ears off, and that's an order."

* * *

She talked his ears off.

She spent the second day like a woman scattering coins, pouring out the valley mile by mile ahead of the road's reaching. Mrs. Alder and the bread. The creek that froze in patterns like ferns. The night she'd taught herself to braid her hair one-handed while stirring stew. Elias — she spent Elias lavishly, hoarding nothing: the way he'd arrived at their ruined first date clutching a prophecy book like a shield, the ink-smudge on his left ring finger, the terrible joke he'd told about cartographers and commitment ("I map everything, I just can't find my way anywhere"), the exact angle at which he'd smiled when she'd laughed despite herself.

Brannoc listened the way stone listens, saying almost nothing, and she suspected that was the point — that her words landing on someone, anyone, was what made them stick.

But the road was patient, and the road was long, and near dusk on the second day it stopped being polite.

She was midway through a sentence — *the winter I turned nineteen, Seraphine came down to the valley unannounced, and we stood in the market square and she almost smiled, and I thought maybe—* — and then it was gone.

Not the small version. Not a detail. The whole of it, the entire afternoon, her mother's face at nineteen-years-distant, the almost-smile, gone as if it had never been quarried out of time at all. Freya stopped walking. The frost-flowers chimed around her boots. Somewhere below the road, in the chasm's fathomless dark, something enormous turned over in its sleep.

"That one was big," she said. Her voice sounded foreign. "That one wasn't supposed to be big. I was spending them. I was doing it right."

Brannoc caught her arm before her knees finished folding. "The road takes what it needs, not what it's owed. Near the Spire, it gets greedy." He lowered his voice. "We're close to the middle now, girl. The middle is where the road remembers what it's made of."

As if summoned, the murmur under the oathstone rose to audibility.

Freya had grown used to the shell-hush of the paving, the layered whisper of ten thousand lapsed vows. This was different. This was distinct. Voices, individual voices, rising out of the stone beneath her feet like bubbles through honey:

*"—swear by root and crown, by the covenant's keystone—"*

*"—my children's children will keep it, I swear it, I swear—"*

*"—until the last storm settles, I will stand watch—"*

And under all of them, woven through them, the same ending, repeated ten thousand ways in ten thousand registers of shame: silence. Every oath here ended the same way. Not with betrayal spoken aloud, but with the vow simply stopping, mid-word sometimes, mid-heartbeat, the way a clock stops — and the silence after was the pavement they walked on.

"They didn't all choose it," Freya whispered. She hadn't meant to speak. "Some of them must have wanted to keep their word. Some of them were afraid, or far away, or—" 

"Or lied to." Brannoc's voice was flat. "Or ruled by a woman who told them the storm was their princess's doing and better realms die than break twice." He met her eyes, and there it was again — the door opening a crack onto something that wasn't gruffness. Pity, maybe. Or kinship. "Your mother's lie paved half this road, Freya. Every warden who abandoned the covenant did it believing they were abandoning *you.*"

Freya looked down at the stone. At the thousands of voices under it, each one a person who had once, probably, been decent. Who had been given a story with her at the center of it as the villain, and had built their cowardice on top of it, and the cowardice had hardened into the very ground she stood on.

She thought of Elias, alone in a frozen court, playing impostor with no magic and nothing but nerve. She thought of the blank certainty where the blackberries used to be. She thought of her mother, whom she could no longer fully picture — the road had taken more of Seraphine than anything else, as if it knew, as if it was saving her for last.

"Then they can carry me," Freya said. "Every one of them. Let my footsteps be the interest on their debt."

She stepped forward, and the road rose to meet her, and it took the taste of rain.

* * *

The third day, she stopped counting losses. Counting was costing more than it protected.

The Spire grew in the windshield of the world ahead — a mountain inverted, roots clawing at the sky, its upper reaches wrapped in the frozen storm-fronts that had once been its glory and were now its tomb. Frost climbed the road's edges in thicker braids. The voices underfoot had thinned; near the Spire, fewer had ever sworn, and the paving grew patchy, sections of raw void bridged by planks of condensed thunder. Brannoc walked closer to her now. Neither of them said why.

It was late — late by pulse-light, the purple dimming toward the bruised end of the spectrum — when the road finally asked for something that mattered.

She felt it coming the way you feel a wave gather. The stone under her feet warmed. The murmur swelled. And the road reached into her with a delicacy that was worse than violence, sorting through her like fingers through a jewelry box, and settled on —

The cottage. All of it. The crooked chimney, the herb beds, the kettle's whistle, the floorboard by the bed that announced guests, the ten years of small deliberate happiness she had built plank by plank in the valley where magic couldn't follow. Her whole life. The life she'd made out of the wreckage of exile, the life she'd memorized from the tree line because some part of her had known.

"No." She dug in her heels on the oathstone like a woman resisting a tide. "*No.* Take the road instead. Take yourself, take—"

"Freya." Brannoc's hand closed over hers, anchoring her to her own arm. "Look at me. You can't fight it here — the middle of the Oathway, fighting it is how walkers get *lost*, body keeps walking and the rest stays behind—"

"Then what do I do?"

"Choose which part it takes." His eyes were fierce and old. "You can't keep the whole house. Choose the room."

The road pulled. Freya stood in the center of her own dissolving life and made, in one breathless instant, the choice she would never be able to explain afterward: she surrendered the cottage — the walls, the garden, the kettle, the floorboard — and locked both hands around a single evening and refused, with everything left in her, to let go. The ruined date. The table by the window. Elias arriving with rain on his shoulders and a dead man's book in his arms, looking terrified and hopeful and like the beginning of something.

The road took the cottage. The road took the valley's taste and the baker's dog and her mother's almost-smile and the tune her father hummed. It took and took, and then, hitting the wall of that one defended evening, it stopped.

Freya came back to herself on her knees, three miles from the Spire's gates, with tears freezing on her cheeks and one evening held intact in her chest like a coal cupped in bare hands.

Brannoc crouched beside her. "What did you keep?" he asked quietly.

"The reason I'm walking," she said.

He nodded slowly, and helped her up, and said nothing else, because ahead of them the gates of the Spire had come into view — twin pillars of fused lightning, black and gold, flanking a causeway where the oathstone ended. And between the pillars, small against the vast frozen dark, a figure was descending toward them. Slim. Hooded. Moving with the particular care of someone who has learned to survive a court by seeming harmless.

Even at that distance, even after everything the road had taken, Freya knew the way he tilted his head when he was pretending not to be afraid.

"Elias," she breathed.

Beside him, half-hidden in the gate's shadow, stood a tall figure in robes the color of deep ice, crowned in rime, waiting with the stillness of someone who has already decided how this meeting will go.

"Brannoc," Freya whispered, "who is that with him?"

The old warden's face went grey as his coat. "That," he said, "is the Queen of the Winter Court. And if she's come down to the gates herself—" he started to run "—then she already knows who you are."
