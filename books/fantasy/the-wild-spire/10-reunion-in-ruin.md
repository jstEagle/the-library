## 10. Reunion in Ruin

**ELIAS**

The Spire's heart was not what he expected. He had imagined a throne room — gilded, frozen, terrible — but the center of the Wild Spire was a wound.

It opened beneath the palace like a crater that had decided to become a cathedral. Walls of storm-glass arched overhead, and through them Elias could see clouds turning slowly, a sky that moved like water. At the floor's center lay the Covenant Stone: a slab of black basalt split down the middle, its two halves drifted apart by a hand's width, and from the crack poured a light that wasn't warm. It was the color of winter noon — bright and utterly without mercy.

The cold came off it in waves. Not the ordinary cold of the road, which Brannoc had taught him to breathe through. This cold had intention. It pressed against his chest like a palm laid flat over his heart, patient as a creditor.

"Stay behind me until they call you forward," Brannoc murmured. The warden had traded his road-worn cloak for formal gray, iron clasps at the shoulder, and looked deeply unhappy about all of it. "And Elias. Whatever you see tonight — whatever names they say, whatever they ask of you — remember you are a mapmaker. You record. You do not rule."

"I've been pretending to be a prophet for three days," Elias said. "I think I can manage a witness."

"That's what worries me." Brannoc's jaw worked. "Prophets get worshipped. Witnesses get questioned."

The court assembled around the Stone in tiers, and Elias tried to read them the way he'd read terrain — by what the shape implied. Wardens in gray like Brannoc, weathered faces closed as shutters. Courtiers in white and silver who glittered without warmth, their breath hanging in the air like small ghosts. And everywhere, threaded through every rank, the frost. It grew on shoulders and hems and the edges of faces, delicate as fernwork, and no one brushed it away. They had stopped fighting it. That was the thing Elias understood now, three days into this dying realm: the people here weren't waiting for spring. They were learning to be beautiful in the ice.

A horn sounded — a long, low note that Elias felt in his teeth rather than heard.

"The Threshold opens," a herald cried, voice cracking on the words like thin ice underfoot. "The exile returns."

Elias forgot how to breathe.

She came down the far stair alone, and even at a hundred paces he knew her walk — the careful way she placed her feet, as if the ground were something she'd once broken and never quite forgiven herself for. Freya Delacroix wore travel-stained wool and her scarred hands were bare, and she held herself the way people hold themselves when they have walked a road that eats memories and refused to let it take the ones that mattered.

Her eyes found him across the crater. Everything else in the room went soft and distant, the way a landscape does when you finally reach the summit.

*You came,* her face said.

*Of course I came,* his answered. *You're the only person in either world who knows how I take my coffee.*

Then the herald spoke again, and the word fell into the silence like a stone into a well:

"Her Radiance, Seraphine Storm-Caller, Queen of the Wild Spire, Warden of the Covenant."

The court knelt. Brannoc went down beside him, hauling Elias with him by the sleeve, and so Elias saw her first from below — which felt right, somehow. Seraphine descended the opposite stair with none of her daughter's care. She walked like weather. Silver-haired now, sharp-faced, beautiful in the way glaciers are beautiful, wearing a crown that looked less like jewelry than like a cage someone had learned to call home.

She reached the Stone. She looked at her daughter.

Ten years passed between them in the space of one breath, and Elias, kneeling, watching, thought: *this is not a reunion. This is an autopsy.*

"Mother," Freya said.

Seraphine's chin lifted a fraction. "You look like your father."

It was not a greeting. It was a blade wrapped in velvet, and the whole court heard it, and Elias watched Freya absorb it without flinching — watched her fold the blow away somewhere behind her ribs for later examination.

"You look tired," Freya said. "Is ruling everything you wanted?"

* * *

**FREYA**

She hadn't meant to say it. The words left her before judgment could catch them, and part of her — the part that had spent ten years learning restraint in a valley where restraint was survival — flinched. But another part, the part that had walked the Road of Broken Oaths while it peeled her memories away like bark, stood firm. She had lost the smell of Hollowmere's bakery. She had lost the name of her neighbor's dog. She would not lose her honesty too.

Seraphine didn't answer. She turned instead to the court, arms spreading, and her voice filled the storm-glass cathedral the way it always had — Freya remembered that voice calling storms to heel when she was six years old, remembered thinking her mother could command the sky itself.

Turned out the sky had just been polite.

"People of the Spire. Ten years ago the covenant broke, and the long winter began. Tonight the exile returns — the storm-caller's daughter, whose untrained grief tore the veil." A pause, measured to the heartbeat. "And with her comes the mapmaker foretold. The one who mends."

Every face turned toward Elias. He rose when Brannoc elbowed him, and Freya's chest tightened at the sight of him — thinner, frost-burned at the cheekbones, standing in a court of strangers with a liar's prophecy around his neck like a noose. But his eyes, when they met hers, were steady. *I'm here. We do this together.*

"The mapmaker will approach the Stone," Seraphine said, "and begin the work of mending—"

"No."

Freya said it quietly, and the quiet carried. Frost-quiet. The court stirred; somewhere above, wind pressed against the storm-glass like a fist.

"He doesn't mend anything," she said. "He can't. He has no magic at all, and the book you're all treating as scripture is a forgery. I've read the true text underneath it. So before anyone lays hands on that Stone — before my mother crowns her savior and calls the debt paid — someone in this room should hear what actually broke the covenant. And who."

The silence that followed had texture. Freya felt it settle on her skin like snow.

Seraphine descended one step. Two. Up close, ten years had carved her mother into something Freya barely recognized — or something she recognized too well, the ambition refined down to its skeleton. "Daughter," Seraphine said softly, "you are exhausted and half-memoryless and you understand nothing of what it costs to hold a realm together—"

"I understand exactly what it costs." Freya's voice cracked and she let it. "You made me pay it. You just never told me what I was buying."

"Enough." Seraphine's composure flickered — one heartbeat, two — and Freya saw it, saw the crack in the glacier, and hated how much she wanted it to widen. "You were a child playing with forces beyond you. I judged you gently. Any other warden would have executed you."

"Gently." Freya laughed, and there was nothing kind in it. "You exiled me to a valley where magic cannot follow. Do you know what that means for someone like me? It means I couldn't feel my own gift for ten years. It means I woke up every morning reaching for something that wasn't there, like a tongue searching for a missing tooth. You didn't cut me out of your life, Mother. You cut me out of myself."

"And yet here you stand," Seraphine said, "alive, having crossed the road alone, having survived the very realm you claim to mourn. Gently, Freya. I said gently."

The court watched them like spectators at a fire. Freya became aware, distantly, of Elias moving — not toward her, but toward the Stone, positioning himself beside it, witness-fashion. Smart. Steady. Her anchor.

"You want to know why I really came back?" Freya said. "Not for you. Not for the throne you're so obviously measuring me for. For him." She nodded at Elias. "Your prophecy dragged an innocent man through the veil because it needed a story tidy enough to hide yours inside. Well. Here's mine, and I'll say it in front of every warden in this room: I'm not leaving until the truth is spoken aloud. His truth. Mine. Yours."

Something moved behind Seraphine's eyes — fear, maybe, or its aristocratic cousin, calculation. "Careful," her mother said. "Some truths freeze faster than lies."

"Then we'll thaw them together," Freya said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

**ELIAS**

Later — much later, after the court dispersed in a rustle of frost and unfinished sentences — he found her on a balcony overlooking the deeps.

The palace clung to the Spire's flank like a barnacle, and the balcony hung over open air: miles of falling cloud, lit from below by the covenant-light bleeding up through cracks in the world. Snow drifted upward past the railing. Elias had given up being surprised by anything this place did.

Freya stood with her hands flat on the stone, scarred palms down, as if steadying herself against the whole sky.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey yourself." She didn't turn. "You survived the court."

"Barely. One of the white-robed ones asked if I'd blessed her greenhouse. I panicked and said yes. I think I'm now responsible for a greenhouse."

That got a laugh out of her — short, startled, real. She finally turned, and up close he could see what the road had taken: a certain openness gone from her face, some memories worn smooth as river stones. But her eyes were the same. That was the thing he'd known since the night of the rift, since the book with his name in century-old ink: her eyes were the same eyes.

"You told them," he said. "In there. About the forgery. About wanting the truth spoken."

"I told them a fraction." She looked back out at the rising snow. "There's more coming. There's a moment coming, Elias, when my mother either says it out loud or lets both realms die, and I don't know which she'll choose, and I have spent ten years believing I knew exactly who she was." Her voice frayed. "I don't know anything anymore. The road took—" She stopped. Started again. "Do you know what I remember about Hollowmere? Your face. Your terrible map jokes. The way you looked when you found your name in that book. I lost my neighbor's name and kept yours. What does that make me?"

"Mine," Elias said, before his brain could file an objection.

She stared at him.

"Sorry," he said. "That was — I rehearsed better lines. There was going to be a speech about how I mapped the road you walked so I could find you, and it was much more dignified—"

"Elias."

"—and then you said *what does that make me* in that voice, and the speech died—"

"Elias." She took his face in her scarred hands, and her palms were cool and rough and the most familiar thing in either world. "Shut up."

He shut up.

When they broke apart, her forehead resting against his, she was shaking — not from cold. "I walked a road that ate my life," she whispered, "to stand in a room where my mother rules over a lie, and the only thing that felt true the entire time was the idea of you. That's either love or madness."

"In my professional opinion as a false prophet," Elias said, "it's usually both."

Above them, the storm-glass darkened. Wind moaned along the palace walls, and far below, the covenant-light guttered — actually guttered, like a candle — and every warden in the Spire felt it, Elias knew, the way sailors feel a change in pressure.

Freya straightened. Her expression shifted from woman-on-a-balcony to storm-caller's daughter in the space of a breath.

"It's getting worse," she said. "The Stone. Midwinter's eve is close, and Vess's faction is moving — Brannoc told you?"

"He told me. He also told me the defenses failed at the north gate today." Elias hesitated. "Freya. When you confront her — your mother. If she confesses, the court will demand blood. If she doesn't, the winter wins. Either way—"

"Either way I lose her twice." Freya said it evenly, looking at the light guttering below. "I know. I did the arithmetic on the road." She turned to him, and took his hand, scarred palm to ink-stained fingers. "But I didn't walk all this way to do the arithmetic alone. Tomorrow we read the true text to the full court. All of it. Your testimony, my memory, whatever pieces of each other we've got left."

"And if the truth breaks the court instead of mending it?"

Freya looked out at the dying light of the covenant, and her jaw set in a way he was beginning to recognize as the most dangerous thing about her.

"Then we'll map the wreckage too," she said. "Together. That's the job, isn't it? Recording honestly so it can't be corrupted again." A ghost of a smile. "Even if what we record is us."

Below them, the covenant-light guttered again — and this time, it did not come back.

Somewhere deep in the palace, a bell began to ring, and it was not the hour.
