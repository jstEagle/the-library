## 16. Vess at the Threshold

The Threshold was not a place Freya remembered. It was a place she remembered *being told about* — a ring of standing stones at the crown of the Spire's highest terrace, where the covenant had once been spoken aloud and where, her mother's letters claimed, every warden in living memory had knelt to swear it.

Living memory had not been generous lately. The stones stood knee-deep in snow, their carved oath-script crusted with frost so thick it looked like the mountain itself had tried to gag them. Above, the sky churned with the frozen storm — clouds locked mid-turn like a wheel seized in ice, lightning hanging inside them without falling, a whole cathedral of weather that had forgotten how to end.

Freya climbed the last steps with Elias's map folded against her chest and Brannoc a half-pace behind, his axe slung across his back out of some old courtesy she didn't fully understand. Below them the Spire fell away in terraces of ruined grandeur — halls sheathed in rime, bridges sagging under snowfall that never melted because there had been no true warmth here in ten years. Ten years since the covenant broke. Ten years since her name became a curse whispered in two realms.

"Stones are still listening," Brannoc said quietly. "That's more than I expected."

"Listening isn't the same as answering."

"No," he agreed. "But it's a start. Most things worth having start with listening."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then the light changed.

It came from the north terrace — a pale gold bloom in all that blue-white dark, torchlight but wrong for torchlight, too steady, too *controlled*, the way magic looked when someone spent decades learning to make it obey. Figures moved through it. A dozen, perhaps more, robed in winter-white trimmed with grey, and at their center walked a woman Freya had only seen in murals and in her mother's bitterest stories: tall, silver-haired, moving through the deep snow as if it were a ballroom floor.

Vess.

"She wasn't supposed to reach the Threshold first," Brannoc breathed. "The east stair collapsed three days ago—"

"Then she rebuilt it." Freya watched the woman come. "You said she wanted the winter permanent."

"I said worse than that. I said she wants the valley consumed." His hand found his axe anyway. "Lady Delacroix — if she offers you anything, understand that whatever it is, it is priced in you."

The party stopped twenty paces from the stones. Vess raised one hand, and her escort halted with a discipline that made Brannoc mutter something unflattering about armies. She came on alone, boots silent on the snow, until she stood close enough that Freya could see her eyes — pale as the horizon before a blizzard, and just as promising of nothing good.

"So," Vess said. Her voice was warm the way a hearth is warm when the house around it is burning. "The storm-caller's daughter. You have your mother's walk. You climb toward judgment the same way she does — chin up, hands open, as if the world owes you gentleness."

"And you have exactly the voice she described," Freya said. "I'd wondered if ten years had softened her memory of you. It hadn't."

Something flickered across Vess's face — not anger. Appetite. "Good. Then we can skip the pleasantries, and I can give you the only honest offer anyone in this frozen kingdom has made you." She gestured, and the air between them shimmered — an image forming in it, small and perfect: a valley. Hollowmere. The mill by the stream, the crooked lane, a stone cottage with smoke rising from its chimney, and two figures in the garden, laughing over a table set for dinner.

Freya's breath caught before she could stop it. She knew that cottage. She knew that smoke. She knew the exact angle of evening light on the far hills, because she had lived inside it for ten years and called it exile and called it home and could no longer always tell which word meant which.

"You crossed the veil," Vess said softly. "You walked the Road of Broken Oaths and paid its toll in memory. You did everything wrong, everything backward, everything the hard way — for a cartographer who cannot work a single spell, and for a realm that has given you nothing but frostbite and old grudges." The image rippled; the two figures in the garden turned, and one of them had Elias's easy smile, and Freya hated herself for how much it cost to look away. "And here is what I know that you do not, child of Seraphine: the rift is dying. Even if your little reading works, even if two realms hold hands and sing, the Spire may fall regardless. Some winters do not break. Some simply finish."

"And your offer?"

"The valley forgets you. That's the toll of the keystone — you know it now, everyone knows it, your clever witness has written it down in his tidy hand." Vess spread her palms, magnanimous, monstrous. "So don't take the bond. Walk away tonight. Go back through the collapsing rift while it still opens for you. Return to your cottage, your mill, your cartographer — yes, him too; the valley will keep him, the prophecy forgives its witnesses. You lose nothing but a throne you never wanted and a realm that exiled you. You gain everything back. Every memory. Every ordinary morning. Magic-dead, magic-free, and safe."

The image hung between them, glowing, patient. Smoke from the chimney. Bread on the table. The life she had built stitch by stitch out of the wreckage of the girl she used to be.

Freya found that her hands were shaking. She let them.

"That's not my life anymore," she said.

"It could be by morning—"

"You're not offering me my life." Her voice came out low and level, and she was distantly surprised by it. "You're offering me a *copy*. A museum piece. The version of me that existed before I knew what my mother did, before I knew what the covenant costs, before I walked a road made of other people's broken promises and felt mine come apart with every step." She stepped forward, into the image itself, and it parted around her like fog. "Belonging isn't a place you flee to, Lady Vess. That's called hiding. I did it for ten years, and I was very good at it, and it nearly killed me anyway — slowly, politely, with bread on the table."

Vess's warmth cooled by degrees. "Then you'll die for a realm that cast you out. Sentiment. Your mother's flaw, rest her ambitions."

"My mother's flaw was lying," Freya said. "Mine is hope. They look similar from a distance. They're not."

For a moment neither of them moved. Snow ticked down through the frozen light. And then Vess smiled, and it was the worst thing Freya had seen in either realm, because it was genuinely fond.

"There she is," Vess murmured. "There's the storm-caller. Do you know, I hoped you'd refuse? An offer accepted proves nothing. But an offer refused—" she stepped back, and her escort flowed forward around her like water finding its level, "—refused publicly, at the Threshold, on midwinter's eve — that tells every warden in this Spire exactly what you are. Not a queen. Not Seraphine's heir. Something older. Something we buried with the covenant."

Brannoc's axe came off his back. "Freya. Behind me. Now."

"No." She didn't move. The standing stones rose at her shoulder, frost-choked and patient, and she laid one scarred hand flat against the nearest — and beneath ten years of ice, faint as a heartbeat, something answered. A hum. A recognition. The dead script warming under her palm. "She's right about one thing, Brannoc. This is exactly where I'm supposed to stand."

* * *

The battle, when it came, was less a battle than a weather event wearing soldiers' faces.

Vess raised two fingers — two fingers, the economy of someone who had never needed a third — and the snowfield itself stood up. It came at them in a wall, white and shrieking, packed hard enough to crack stone, and behind it her wardens advanced with blades of black ice that drank the frozen light instead of reflecting it. Brannoc met the wall head-on with a bellow and a swing that split it down the middle, and then the world was noise.

Freya had fought exactly twice in her life, both times by accident, both times apologizing. This was different. This was chosen. She planted her feet at the Threshold, put her scarred palm back against the stone, and reached — not for power, she didn't know how to reach for power, she'd been a girl the last time it came when called — but for the storm. For the thing she'd been exiled for calling.

*Come*, she thought. *Not to hurt. To hold.*

The stone warmed. The hum deepened into something almost musical. And high above, in the cathedral of seized clouds, the first lightning in ten years shivered loose from its prison and struck the northern terrace with a sound like the sky clearing its throat.

Wardens scattered. The snow-wall faltered, confused, suddenly unsure whose side it was on — and Freya understood, with a clarity that felt like standing in open door after years behind walls, that the storm remembered her. That it had been waiting. That exile had cut her off from the valley, but nothing in heaven or under the mountains had ever managed to cut her off from *this*.

"Impossible," Vess said, and for the first time her voice cracked along its warmth. "The bond is broken. The covenant is ash. You're nothing but a girl with scars—"

"A girl with scars who just asked nicely," Freya said, and pulled again.

Lightning walked the terrace in stuttering steps, herding Vess's line apart from their mistress, and Brannoc waded through the gap with his axe trailing frost-blood, roaring orders to warden-factions Freya couldn't see but who apparently could hear him, because somewhere below, horns began to answer. The loyal ones. The ones Elias's testimony had swayed. The ones who had chosen, freely, in the dark hours of a dying realm, to believe a stranger's honest map.

But Vess was not finished. Vess had never once, in any story Freya's mother told, been finished.

She turned from the fight entirely — dismissed it, the way you dismiss weather — and walked to the far edge of the Threshold, to the ancient keel-stone that anchored the Spire's deepest workings, and pressed both palms to it. Her magic poured in like ink into water.

The Spire screamed.

Not the wardens. Not the people. The *Spire* — a groan rolling up through terraces and foundations, through bridge and hall and root, the sound of something vast being bent against its grain. Cracks spidered across the keel-stone. Far below, Freya felt rather than saw the outer defenses fail, one by one, lights going out along the Spire's flanks like windows going dark in a house at night.

"What is she doing?" Elias's voice — he was coming up the stair, she realized, map under his arm, having run the whole way, because of course he had.

"Breaking what's left of the anchor," Brannoc snarled, fighting backward toward them. "If the keel-stone splits before midwinter, there'll be nothing left to reforge. No covenant, no court, no choice — just two realms falling separately forever!"

The cracks widened. Through them Freya could see down — impossibly far down, past halls and roots and the thawing deeps where the covenant's oldest oaths slept — and at the bottom, growing, a cold so total it made the long winter above look like a lover's touch. Vess's true winter. The permanent one. Rising.

And the rift — the thin, ragged seam in the world that led home, that led to Hollowmere, to the valley, to the cottage and the mill and every memory the Road had stripped from her — began to collapse inward. Not slowly. Not gently. Like a wound deciding to close whether or not anything had healed underneath.

Elias reached the top of the stair, took one look, and started running for the stones. "Freya! The rift—"

"I see it."

"We're out of time. Midwinter isn't coming tomorrow. It's coming *now*."

Above them the frozen storm finally, mercifully, began to move — clouds unlocking, lightning gathering, the whole imprisoned cathedral of weather turning its attention downward like an ancient animal hearing its name called after a decade of silence.

Freya kept her hand on the stone. Kept her feet at the Threshold. Kept her eyes on Vess, who stood with her palms bleeding light into the cracking keel-stone and her pale eyes fixed on Freya with something that was no longer appetite at all.

It was recognition.

"Call it, then," Vess said over the screaming of the world. "Call your storm, daughter of liars. Call it tonight, with the anchor breaking and the rift dying and half your witnesses still climbing. Let's see what your hope is worth at its worst hour."

The first true thunder in ten years rolled down out of the moving sky, and Freya Delacroix — exile, storm-caller, keeper of a date she had promised to keep — drew breath to answer her.

Midwinter's eve had arrived early.
