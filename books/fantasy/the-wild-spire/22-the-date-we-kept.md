## 22. The Date We Kept

**Freya**

The table was still crooked.

A year of seasons had passed through Hollowmere, and the Lamplight Inn had been rebuilt twice over — new beams, new thatch, a new sign painted by a woman who'd never seen the Spire but swore she dreamed in storms now — and yet the corner table by the window sat exactly as it always had, one leg half an inch shorter than its brothers, so that every cup placed upon it trembled like a small nervous heart.

Freya set down two cups and watched them both shiver.

"You could fix it," said the innkeeper, Marta, wiping her hands on her apron. "One shim. Ten seconds."

"Leave it," Freya said. "It's tradition now."

Marta gave her the look that everyone in Hollowmere had learned to give her over the past year — half awe, half exasperation, the look you give someone who has been struck by lightning and lived and now refuses to carry an umbrella. "Ambassador Delacroix. The woman who mended the sky. Afraid of a little carpentry."

"The sky I mended," Freya said, arranging the table just so: the candle centered, the book squared to the table's edge, Elias's chair angled toward hers the way it had been on the night everything began. "The table frightens me."

Marta laughed and left her to it.

Outside the window, Hollowmere was doing what Hollowmere did best, which was nothing at all, beautifully. The valley's evening light came slantwise through the pass, gold going to rose, catching the smoke from chimneys, the slow wheel of a cart on the mill road, two children chasing a dog that had clearly won this game many times before. Ordinary. All of it ordinary. A year ago she would have pressed her hand to the glass and ached at how much she wanted it and how certain she'd been that wanting was all she'd ever get.

Now the ache was gone and something quieter sat in its place. Not peace exactly. Peace sounded finished, and nothing here was finished. It was more like — a held breath that had finally decided it was allowed to become a song.

She touched the scarred skin of her left palm without thinking. The storm-marks had changed after the Reforging. They no longer looked like burns; they looked like branches, or river deltas, or the veins of a leaf — silver-pale lines that caught candlelight when she turned her hand. On bad days they ached with weather three valleys away. On good days they simply were, the way freckles were, the way the crooked table was.

Evidence of what she'd survived. Evidence of what she'd chosen.

The bell above the door rang.

She knew his step before she saw him — she'd have known it blind, in a crowd of thousands, in the middle of a hurricane. That particular rhythm, quick but deliberate, a man who mapped everything because he refused to be lost anywhere. She turned, and there he was in the doorway shaking rain off his coat — actual rain, soft valley rain, the first they'd had since midsummer — and he saw her, and his whole face did the thing it always did, the thing she had never once gotten tired of watching.

He lit up like he hadn't expected her. Like she wasn't exactly where he knew she'd be.

"You're early," Elias said.

"You're later than me, which makes you late." She nodded at the chair across from hers. "Sit down, cartographer. Your tea's getting cold and your reputation with it."

* * *

**Elias**

Here is what Elias Vane had learned in one year as witness to the covenant of two realms: that history does not care about your plans, that maps are arguments about the world rather than records of it, and that there is no sight in either realm — not the aurora-veins of the Spire's thawing deeps, not the first green breaking through a century of ice, not even the moment the long winter broke and the sky itself seemed to exhale — that compared to Freya Delacroix looking at you across a crooked table like you were the answer to a question she'd stopped asking out loud.

He sat. His knee bumped hers under the table, deliberately, and she bumped back.

"You're staring at the book," she said.

"I'm staring at *you*, and the book is jealous."

The book lay between them where he'd placed it — he'd carried it across the valley on foot, which was absurd, because he could feel its pull through his satchel from miles off, but some things deserved to arrive on foot. *The Wild Spire: A True and Faithful Account.* His grandfather's salvage. The forgery layered over truth, the prophecy that had named him a century before his birth, the text that had started all of it by sitting innocently on a table very much like this one while two nervous people tried to pretend they weren't already falling for each other.

Its final page was blank. Had been blank since midwinter's eve, when the last line of the old prophecy wrote itself out and faded like breath off glass:

*And the storm-caller shall call, and the mapmaker shall witness, and the covenant shall be remade in consent, and no one shall be severed again.*

They had argued all year about what should go on the blank page.

"It's waiting," Freya said, resting her fingers on the cover. She still did that — touched books like they were sleeping animals that might startle. Given what this one had done to their lives, Elias supposed the instinct was sound. "Marta thinks we should put it in glass. The council wants it in the embassy archive. Brannoc offered to sit on it, which is his solution to most problems."

"And what do you think?"

"I think it's not finished." She turned her hand over under his — the storm-scarred palm, the branching silver lines. "Look at this. It used to be a wound. Now it's a map. You taught me that, do you realize? Everything that marks us can be read. It's only a question of who's holding the pen."

Elias felt the familiar tightness in his throat, the one that arrived whenever she said something like this, casually, between sips of tea, as though she hadn't just handed him a sentence he'd be turning over for weeks. "That's going in my journal verbatim," he managed. "I'll cite you."

"Cite me properly. Ambassador Freya Delacroix, third of her name, first of her nerve."

"That's not how regnal names work."

"I'm not regal, I'm *regional*."

He laughed — the same helpless laugh as a year ago, at this same table, when she'd knocked over the sugar and caught it midair with a flicker of storm-light she thought he hadn't noticed, and he had noticed, and had said nothing, because some secrets belonged to the person keeping them. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming soft against the window, and the candle flame leaned and righted itself.

"So," he said, and took a breath, because they had rehearsed this, both of them, separately, in mirrors and on walks and once — embarrassingly — aloud to a goat. "One year."

"One year," she agreed.

"To the date."

"To the date we didn't get to keep." Her eyes went briefly distant — he knew where she was looking, though the veil was healed now and the Spire stood bright and thawing beyond it. To the moment the rift tore open in this room and swallowed him whole while she screamed his name into the gap. He still dreamed about the falling. She still dreamed about the screaming. They had agreed, without ever discussing it, not to compare notes on those dreams anymore.

"We kept it eventually," he said. "Late, but we kept it. I've had worse deadlines. I once delivered a survey of the Greywater marshes four years overdue."

"And the marshes?"

"Flooded. New coastline entirely. My map is a work of historical fiction now." He tapped the prophecy book. "At least this one got revised in time."

Freya slid her hand across the table and covered his. Her palm was cool, as it always was — storm-blood ran colder than valley blood, another thing the year had taught them, along with the fact that she stole blankets in her sleep and denied it with total conviction — and he laced his fingers through hers.

"I want to say something," he said. "Before the food comes. Before Marta interrupts. Before anything else happens, because something else always happens, that appears to be the fundamental law of our lives—"

"Elias."

"—and I want to say it while the table is merely crooked instead of actively trying to kill us."

Her eyes had gone wide and bright and slightly terrified, which was, he noted with great satisfaction, exactly how his felt.

* * *

**Freya**

She had known this was coming for three weeks.

She'd known because he'd started measuring things. Elias measured things the way other people breathed — doorways, windows, the exact height of her mother's garden fence — but lately he'd been measuring *her* things, her rings, the width of her knuckles, once, memorably, the inside circumference of a thimble while pretending to examine its engraving. She was a storm-caller, not a fool. And last week Brannoc — gruff, enormous, secretly romantic Brannoc, who had crossed a war-torn Spire carrying contraband hope in both fists — had clapped Elias on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him and said, too loudly, "Whatever you're planning, boy, don't drop the ring in the snow again," and then walked away whistling.

So yes. She'd known.

And she had spent three weeks deciding whether to let him do it anyway.

Because here was the thing about being Freya Delacroix, and she had learned it the hard way, in exile, in betrayal, in the terrible white silence of the keystone bond: she did not wait well. She had waited ten years in a magic-dead valley for a life to begin. She had waited at the Threshold while Vess offered her a lie dressed as mercy. She had learned, at cost of blood and memory and her mother's confession, that waiting for permission was how whole realms froze.

But this — she watched Elias take a breath and fumble in his coat pocket, ears going pink, absolutely certain she didn't know — this she could wait for. This she *wanted* to wait for. Some questions deserved to be asked properly, by someone whose hands were shaking, at a crooked table, in a valley that had forgotten how to forget her.

"Freya," Elias said. "A year ago tonight, I walked into this inn carrying a cursed book and a bouquet of flowers I'd bought off a cart, because I liked you and I was terrified, and I got maybe forty minutes of the best evening of my life before reality literally tore open and ate me."

"It was a nice forty minutes," she said, voice wobbling.

"It was. The flowers survived the crossing, incidentally. Brannoc found them frozen in the rift-scree six months later. Perfectly preserved. He keeps them on his mantle and pretends they're tactical intelligence."

She laughed, wet-eyed, both hands wrapped around his one.

"So here's what I've figured out," he went on, faster now, the way he talked when a map was coming together, when the coastlines matched. "I spent my whole life believing my job was to record the world accurately. And then I met you, and the world started changing faster than any map could hold it, and I panicked — and then I learned that witnessing isn't standing still with a notebook. Witnessing is choosing to stay while everything changes. It's saying *I see this, all of it, and I'm not leaving.*"

He drew the ring from his pocket. Simple band, pale metal, and set into it a stone that was not a stone — a single droplet of solidified storm-glass, blue-white, shot through with branching silver. She recognized it instantly. Rift-glass, from the Threshold. From the place where they had almost lost each other and instead chose each other, in front of two courts and a collapsing sky.

"Freya Delacroix," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word and he pushed through it, brave as a man walking an oath-road, "I am not asking you to give up anything, or bind yourself to anything, or be anyone's keystone. I'm asking if you'll build something with me that doesn't exist yet. No precedent. No prophecy. Just — us, writing the blank page. Will you marry me?"

The rain fell. The candle leaned. The crooked table held its trembling little vigil beneath their joined hands.

And Freya — who had broken an exile, unmade a winter, faced down a mother, refused two thrones, and reforged a covenant older than the valley itself — found that the only possible answer was also the smallest word she owned.

"Yes," she said. "Obviously yes. Yes before you finish the sentence. Yes with weather warnings attached — I steal blankets and I call storms when I cry and I will absolutely turn our wedding into a diplomatic incident—"

He kissed her mid-list, which was cheating, and she allowed it.

When they parted, the ring was on her finger and the storm-glass caught the candlelight and threw branching shadows up the wall — silver rivers, silver roads, a map of everywhere they might still go. Outside, the rain was stopping. Through the clearing clouds, far past the pass where the veil shimmered faint and healed, the first stars of the Spire's side of the sky were coming out, bright and strange and home.

Marta appeared with two bowls of stew nobody had ordered and pretended not to be crying. "On the house," she said thickly. "For the anniversary. And the — the other thing. Obviously."

* * *

**Elias**

Later — much later, the inn empty, the candle burned to a stub, Marta long gone to bed with instructions to bill the embassy for the damage to her composure — they sat in the lamplight with the book open between them one final time.

The blank page waited.

"Do you want to?" Freya asked. Her head was on his shoulder, the ring hand curled loosely around her teacup. "Write it. The first line. Everyone's expecting something grand. The council's taking bets."

Elias considered the page. A year ago, ink had written itself here — prophecy, fate, a century-old trap dressed as destiny. Names assigned. Roles assigned. People severed from their own lives by sentences they never got to read until it was too late.

He picked up the pen.

He did not write *once upon a time*. He did not write *and it came to pass*. He wrote the way he mapped: honestly, plainly, leaving room for revision.

*This account is true and faithful as of tonight,* he wrote. *It remains subject to amendment by mutual consent.*

Freya read it, and laughed, and kissed his cheek. "Perfect. Dry as a treaty and twice as binding."

"That's the highest praise you've ever given me."

"Don't get sentimental."

Below it, unprompted, her hand took the pen from his — and in her looping, storm-struck script, she added a line of her own:

*To be continued, by whoever loves it next.*

They closed the book together. And outside the window of the Lamplight Inn, in the valley where magic had once refused to follow, the rain began again — soft, unhurried, falling equally on Hollowmere and the road beyond the veil, on both sides of a sky that had remembered how to be one sky — and somewhere far off, gentle as a promise kept, thunder answered.
