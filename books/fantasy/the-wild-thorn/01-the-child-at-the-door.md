## 1. The Child at the Door

The rain came down on Copperlane like a verdict, and Isandro Pemberton was counting the hours until it stopped.

Not that he minded rain, precisely. Rain kept customers away, and customers were the one thing his shop could not afford — not the ones who came in asking after love-charms with coins bitten between their teeth, and certainly not the other kind, the kind who came in with warrants folded in their coat pockets and questions shaped like knives. No, what he minded was the sound of it: the way water drummed against his shutters and ran down through a crack above the doorframe, dripping — plip, plip, plip — onto the floorboards he had just swept.

He was crouched behind the counter with a rag and a jar of beeswax when he heard the scratching.

At first he took it for a branch. The old rowan outside leaned close enough to the shop on windy nights to drag its fingers along the clapboard, and this was a windy night if ever there was one. He went back to his waxing. The scratching continued. It had a rhythm to it, patient and deliberate, and branches did not have rhythm. Branches did not pause between strokes as though considering their work.

Isandro set down the rag. He did not light a lamp. Twelve years of living one step ahead of the Crown's witch-finders had taught him that the surest way to die was to announce yourself with a candle.

He crossed the dark shop floor, past the shelves of harmless trinkets — luck-knots, salt-roses, pressed flowers blessed by no one in particular — past the false back of the herb cabinet, and stood before his own front door from the inside, listening.

Scratch. Pause. Scratch-scratch. Pause.

Then, faintly beneath the rain, a sound that turned his blood to well-water: humming. A child's hum, tuneless and small, coming from the other side of his door.

Isandro unbarred the door an inch.

Rain hurled itself into the gap. Beyond it, huddled under the eave where his sign — PEMBERTON'S CHARMS & CURIOSITIES, the letters half worn to ghosts — offered the only shelter on the street, stood a girl. Eight years old, perhaps nine. Soaked to the bone, her dark hair plastered flat to her skull, wearing a shift that had once been white and was now the color of dishwater. She looked up at him without fear, which was the first wrong thing. Children who knocked on strange doors at night were supposed to be afraid.

The second wrong thing was her hands. They were still moving. Even now, even looking directly at him, her fingers worked at the air as though writing on invisible glass — quick, certain strokes, over and over, a word or a warning she could not say aloud because, he understood with a slow cold settling in his stomach, she could not say anything aloud at all.

"You'd better come in," Isandro heard himself say, "before you drown standing up."

* * *

She would not sit. She would not take the blanket he offered, nor the bread, though her eyes tracked both with the naked hunger of a creature that had not eaten in days. She stood in the middle of his shop floor, dripping a puddle onto boards he'd waxed not an hour ago, and pointed — imperiously, he thought, for someone so small — at the door.

"What about it?" Isandro asked.

She pointed again. Then at herself. Then she made a shape with her hands, two fingers dragging down through the other palm's cupped hollow — *writing, falling, gone* — and shook her head violently.

"You wrote something." Isandro felt the floor tilt slightly beneath him, the way it always did when the past leaned its weight against the present. "On my door."

She nodded. Then she took his wrist — small fingers, strong grip, cold as river stones — and pulled him toward the door with a strength that should have been impossible in so slight a frame. He let himself be pulled. Outside, under the sagging eave, shielded from the worst of the rain by the overhang, she guided his hand to the lower panel of the door, to the wood just below where the paint had blistered, and pressed his fingertips against grooves cut deep and clean into the grain.

He knew the shapes before his eyes finished confirming them. His fingers knew. His fingers had traced these same lines a thousand times, in tallow smoke and chalk dust, on the underside of a worktable in a cottage that no longer existed, while a voice like dry leaves told him: *These are older than any kingdom, boy. Kingdoms are candles. These are the dark they burn in.*

Sigils of the First Tongue.

Isandro went very still. Rain ran off the eave and down the back of his collar, and he barely noticed. The child watched him with those unafraid eyes, waiting, as if she already knew what lived inside him and had simply been waiting for him to catch up.

There were nine sigils in all, cut in a spiral, and he read them the way a man reads his own name carved somewhere it should never be. *Vessel. Given. Thirteen turnings. Debt. Beneath.* And at the center of the spiral, closing it like a lock closing: the broker's mark.

That was the third wrong thing. That was the thing that stopped his heart mid-beat and left it thudding crooked ever after.

The broker's mark was a crescent pierced by three lines, drawn in a single unbroken stroke. Mother Vespera's mark. The mark she signed on every charm-bill, every contract, every page of the grimoire she had burned — or claimed to have burned — the night she sent him away with a kiss on the forehead and the words *run far, run quiet, never write to me.*

His mentor. His keeper. The woman who had fed him nettle soup and taught him the names of stars and smuggled him out of the capital the week before the witch-finders came for the hedge-craft quarter. She had put this mark on a child. On *this* child. She had sold —

No. He made himself read the spiral again, slower, hating every stroke. *Vessel. Given. Thirteen turnings. Debt. Beneath.*

Sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain. A soul, given as vessel, payment due when the thirteenth moon turned. And by the look of the sky through the rain — by the swollen silver edge of it bleeding through the cloud-scud overhead — moon twelve was nearly fat.

"Who gave you to her?" he whispered, though he knew the child could not answer, and knew, too, in the place where answers live before words do, that the question was misshapen. Parents desperate enough to sell a soul did not need a reason beyond the coin. He had seen it in the ledger-shadows of Vespera's work, glimpsed and never understood: *one soul, purchased, vessel-grade.* He had thought it a figure of speech. He had thought many things, twelve years ago, because thinking otherwise would have meant staying, and staying would have meant dying.

The girl touched his cheek. Gently. As if he were the one who needed comforting.

And that was when the pounding started.

* * *

It came from three doors down — the cobbler's — fists on wood, voices like iron dragged over stone. *"By order of the Crown. Open in the King's name. Witch-finders."*

Isandro had heard those words twice in his life. The first time had cost him everything but his life. He moved without deciding to move, scooping the child up — she weighed almost nothing, all elbows and river-cold bones — and carrying her into the shop as the pounding moved next door, to the widow Anselm's, whose crime was keeping a cat that caught mice too cleverly.

"Listen to me." He set her down behind the counter, in the narrow gap where his contraband lived: the true stock, the stock that would hang him ten times over. Bottles of storm-light. A cage of bottled breath. Grimoire pages sewn into the lining of a winter coat. And, hanging among them like a dead man's shirt, a cloak woven through with hedge-craft — a cloak of quiet, of *unnoticed*, stitched by hands better than his. "I'm going to hide you, and you're going to stay hidden no matter what you hear. Do you understand? Whatever happens, whatever you see — you do not exist."

She looked at him. Then she raised one small hand and drew, in the air between them, with those tireless fingers, a single sigil.

*Hidden*, it said. Or perhaps *safe*. In the First Tongue, the two were closer than any honest language would allow.

"Yes," Isandro breathed. "Exactly that."

He settled the cloak around her shoulders and eased her down into the shadowed gap among the bottles, and she folded herself into it with the ease of long practice — of hiding, of being hunted, of being *small* — until she was only another darkness among darknesses. Her eyes stayed open. They found his in the gloom and held them, and in them he saw something that frightened him more than the fists on doors: recognition. Not of his face. Of something behind his face. The way one stray recognizes another.

The pounding reached his door.

*"Pemberton! Charm-monger! Open!"*

Isandro smoothed his expression into the bland, aggrieved mask he kept for exactly this occasion and unbarred the door. Rain and lamplight and three men in the grey-and-gold of the Crown's Office of Purity pushed into his doorway, and the foremost — a long-faced man with a wet warrant case clutched to his chest like a Bible — thrust a lantern into the shop.

"Charm-monger Pemberton. We seek a runaway child. Small, dark-haired, mute." The lantern swung. "Seen her?"

"A child? At this hour?" Isandro let his voice climb into the register of a man interrupted at his supper. "In this rain? Sir, I've seen nothing tonight but weather."

The witch-finder's gaze crawled across the shelves of harmless trinkets. Luck-knots. Salt-roses. Pressed flowers. Behind the counter, among the shadows, a silence so complete it rang.

"We're searching every shop on the lane," the man said.

"Then search," said Isandro, stepping aside with the weary grace of the innocent, "and mind the wax on the third shelf, it stains."

They searched. They were thorough in the way of men who enjoy thoroughness, tipping jars, prodding floorboards, holding the lantern high so its light swam across the ceiling. One of them paused at the door, crouching, running a gloved thumb over the lower panel — over the grooves cut into the wood — and Isandro felt his heartbeat relocate to his throat.

"Old damage," the finder said, half to himself. "Water rot."

"Rain does that," Isandro agreed pleasantly. "Terrible for the woodwork."

The finder grunted and straightened. And then, as they filed out into the storm, the long-faced one turned in the doorway, lantern raised one last time, and said — not accusingly, not yet, but in the tone of a man filing a fact away where facts fester:

"Odd sort of rot. Almost like writing."

The door closed. Boots splashed away toward the bridge. Isandro stood in his wrecked shop with rain blowing in around him, listening until the last echo of authority faded, and then he barred the door and turned around and slid down against it until he was sitting on the floor like a man whose legs had resigned.

From the shadows behind the counter, the cloak stirred. The child emerged, blinking, unhurt, and looked at him across the wreckage of his careful little life.

"It's you," Isandro said quietly. It wasn't a sentence; it was a landslide. "She marked you. Vespera marked you, and now the finders are at my door and the Court is owed a soul and the moon is nearly full, and I swore I'd buried all of it." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Twelve years I ran, and the past didn't chase me. It just walked up in the rain and scratched on my door."

The child crossed the floor, sat down beside him against the door, small shoulder against his arm, and began, softly, to hum.

Outside, the rain went on hammering the town, and beneath the hammering — faint, rhythmic, wrong — came a new sound from the direction of the mountain: bells. Bells ringing twelve times, and then, after a pause long enough to hold a whole life, beginning again.
