## 2. What the Sigils Say

Dawn came gray and thin through the shutters, and Isandro Pemberton had not slept.

He had spent the night in the back room with a single candle burning down to its brass dish, watching the child sleep among his contraband like a sparrow nested in a thornbush. She lay curled between a crate of luck-knots and a shelf of bottled breath—things that would cost him his hands if the Crown ever found them—and she slept the way children only sleep when they have gone too long without it: completely, defensively, as if unconsciousness were a country she had finally been allowed back into.

The witch-finders had moved on before midnight. He'd heard their boots in the street, heard old Marta across the lane open her door and lie beautifully about her empty lodgings. Then rain again, and quiet, and the small sound of the girl's breathing filling the shop like smoke fills a room.

Now she was awake, sitting cross-legged on the floor with her knees drawn up, watching him watch her. Her eyes were the color of river ice. When he offered her bread and honey she took it without ceremony and ate with the focused gravity of someone who knew exactly how long food could be trusted to last.

"You're safe," he said, which was a lie of the most ordinary kind, the kind everyone told everyone. "For now."

She looked at him the way one looks at a man who has said something obviously foolish but not unkind.

Then her gaze went to the door.

He followed it. In the gray light the sigils she had scratched into the wood stood out pale against the wet grain, dozens of them, climbing from the threshold to the height of her reach and beyond—as if she had begun standing and ended on her toes, desperate to finish before something caught her. Rain had run through the grooves and dried, leaving them faintly silvered.

He had seen them last night by lightning-flash and candle-stub, enough to know they were First Tongue, enough to feel the cold walk up his spine. Now, in honest light, he made himself read them properly.

It was worse than he thought. It was much, much worse.

* * *

Twenty years ago, in a cottage that leaned against the shoulder of the Ellow Fells like an old drunk leaning on a wall, Mother Vespera taught him letters no kingdom permitted.

"Books burn," she used to say, tapping ash off a pipe carved into the shape of a toad. "Stone crumbles. But the Tongue is older than either, boy. The Tongue is what things said to each other before we came along and started naming everything wrong." She wrote sigils in spilled tea on the tabletop, and the tea steamed, and sometimes the table groaned as if waking. "You don't speak it. You don't own it. You ask it. Politely. And you never—" here she'd fixed him with one milky eye, "—never write it where a debt can find it."

"What's a debt?" he'd asked, eleven years old and full of questions she answered in riddles.

She had gone still in a way he remembered now with terrible clarity. The pipe smoke hung in the air between them, unmoving, as if the room itself was listening.

"A debt," she said finally, "is when somebody writes your name in a book you've never read. Down under the mountain there's a court that keeps such books. They don't send soldiers for what's owed them. They send singers. And a song, once started, doesn't stop until it's paid." She wiped the sigils away with her sleeve and grinned at him, quick and bright, the grin that made the whole cottage warm. "But that's a story for children who can't sleep. Eat your porridge."

He had believed, all those years, that it was a story.

* * *

Now he knelt at his own door in the morning light with a stub of charcoal and a scrap of parchment, copying the child's sigils one by one, and the story walked out of his memory and put its cold hand on his neck.

The first cluster was simple—a plea of shelter, the oldest formula in the Tongue, the hedge-witch's *open to me*. Beneath it, a name-sign. Not Wren—that was what he'd taken to calling her in his head, after the little brown birds that picked crumbs outside his shop—but a longer sign, a true-name fragment, half-erased deliberately. Someone had tried to scratch it out and failed; the Tongue does not permit its names to be wholly unwritten. Whoever erased it had known this, and had settled for maiming it instead, breaking the name so it couldn't be sung whole.

Someone had done that to protect her. Or someone had done it so the Court could hold the soul without holding the name—the way you keep a bird in a covered cage.

His charcoal stopped moving.

Below the name, the transaction itself. He read it twice, three times, tracing each sigil with a fingertip without touching it, the way Vespera had taught him—you read dangerous script with your eyes and your breath, never your skin. *Given. Bound. Held.* The grammar of ownership, rendered in characters older than any kingdom's law. And beneath *held*, the destination: a sigil-cluster he recognized from exactly one place in all his life. A spiral drawn inward, coiled like a sleeping serpent around a single vertical stroke.

The mark of the Court Beneath the Mountain.

Vespera had drawn it once, only once, in the margin of her grimoire, and then scorched the page black over it—but fire is a poor eraser and he had been a curious apprentice, holding the charred leaf up to candlelight until the ghost of the ink showed through. *So I remember what to fear,* she'd said when she caught him, and cuffed his ear, and that night she drank more plum brandy than usual and laughed too loudly at nothing.

The child's soul was sold. Written into the deep ledger. Owned by the thing beneath the mountain that kept its accounts in songs.

Isandro sat back on his heels. His mouth tasted of copper.

There was one more element in the sigils, pressed into the wood at the end of the sequence like a seal pressed into wax. A broker's mark—the signature of the one who had made the sale, required by the Tongue's own merciless grammar: every binding must bear its binder. He copied it onto the parchment slowly, because some part of him already knew, and knowing did not make his hand obey any faster.

A crescent crossed by a willow branch, its leaves falling upward.

He had seen that mark ten thousand times. On the cottage door. On the jars of salve. Branded into the bottom of every loaf of bread she baked, because even her bread was charmed, even her bread was hers. He had kissed that mark on her cheek a thousand mornings.

Mother Vespera had brokered the sale of a child's soul.

* * *

The charcoal snapped in his fingers. He didn't notice.

Twelve years since she vanished. Twelve years of assuming the worst and the best in alternating seasons—dead in a ditch, fled from the witch-finders, gone to ground somewhere south where the hedges grew thick and no one asked questions. Twelve years of building this shop, this careful gray life of licensed charms and hidden contraband, of keeping his head down and his craft dull enough to pass inspection—all of it shaped around the absence of her, the way a river shapes itself around a stone.

And the whole time. The whole time.

"No," he said aloud.

The child looked up from her bread.

"It's wrong." He held the parchment up as if distance might change it, as if the sigils might rearrange themselves into innocence. "She raised me. She fed me out of her own pot when my mother died. She taught me—" his voice cracked and he hated it, forty years old and his voice cracking in front of a mute child, "—she taught me that debts were stories for children who can't sleep. She *knew*. She knew what that mark meant and she—" 

He stopped. Because the child had set down her bread and was looking at him with an expression no child should own: patient, weary, ancient at the edges. And she lifted one small hand and pointed—not at him, not at the door—at the space between them, and drew one finger in a slow circle.

*All of this,* the gesture said. *All of this happened. Argue with the wood.*

He laughed. It came out broken. "You're right. Of course you're right. The Tongue doesn't lie—it's the one thing about it that doesn't lie. That was her first lesson too." He dragged both hands down his face. "Gods. Every lesson she ever gave me is a knife today."

The child crawled closer, cautiously, the way one approaches a wounded animal that might bite. She studied the parchment over his knee. Then she touched the broker's mark with one fingertip—casually, fearlessly, the way you touch a scar that is yours—and looked up at him.

"You know her." Not a question; he heard it anyway, in the tilt of her head, the narrowing of those ice-colored eyes. "You knew the woman who sold you."

Her face did something complicated. Grief and anger and something underneath both, something that might have been pity—for him, gods help him, pity for the man whose whole childhood had just been repossessed.

"I'm going to find her," Isandro said. The words came out low and steady, and he was grateful for that, at least. "Whatever she is now. Whatever she was then. I'm going to find her and ask her why, and if the answer isn't good enough, I am going to take apart every binding she ever wove with these two hands."

The child considered him for a long moment. Then she reached out and, with great deliberation, turned the parchment over. Tapped the blank side. Tapped the door, with its ledger of her stolen soul. Tapped his chest, lightly, once.

*And me?* the gesture said. *What about me?*

And that was when the knock came at the shop's front shutter—three slow blows, deliberate, spaced like the notes of a song beginning.

Not the fist of a neighbor. Not the boot of a witch-finder, which hammered. This was patient. Almost polite.

Through the crack in the shutters Isandro saw the street, empty in the gray morning. Empty except for a figure standing across the lane in the doorway of Marta's shuttered bakery, wearing a borrowed face that almost fit—a smile arranged slightly too wide, eyes that reflected the lamplight a moment after the lamp had gone out.

The figure raised one hand, and in it, a tuning fork of black iron.

And somewhere beneath the cobblestones, beneath the cellar, beneath the roots of the city itself, Isandro felt—or imagined he felt, and knew he hadn't—the first low note of a song begin.

The moon above the mountain, he thought. Thirteen turns. How many were already gone?

He blew out the candle, though the morning needed no candles, and reached for the grimoire-shaped bundle wrapped in oilcloth beneath his bed. If he was going to outrun a debt, he would need his teacher's handwriting after all.

Behind him, the child rose silently to her feet, already watching the door, already listening—with her whole body—to the song coming up through the floor.
