## 3. The Witch-Finder's Debt

The summons came at the hour when candles gutter and honest people pretend to sleep.

Ovelia Thorne read it twice by the light of her own hearth, though once would have been enough to understand what it meant: *Captain Thorne is commanded to present herself before the Office of Inquiry, Ashlar Street, at first bell. Bring your brother's effects.*

*Effects.* That was the Crown's word for a dead man's papers, his ink-stained shirts, his half-finished translations—the residue of a life the Office had already decided was criminal. Her brother's effects had sat in a sealed crate in her quarters for three years because she could not bear to open it and could not bear to surrender it, and now the Crown wanted both.

She dressed in her uniform anyway. It still fit. That was the cruelest part of disgrace—how well the old shape held you.

Ashlar Street smelled of wet stone and tallow smoke, as it always did. The Office of Inquiry occupied a former chapel whose windows had been bricked over during the Reformation of Beliefs; inside, where saints had once looked down, clerks now counted confiscated grimoires into ledgers like grain. Ovelia had spent eleven years in rooms like this one. She had been good at it. She tried not to think about how good.

Captain-Commander Rell waited behind a desk that was too large for him, the way small men's desks always are. He did not offer her a chair.

"Thorne." He said her name the way he'd say *moldy bread*. "You know why you're here."

"A child went missing from the Foundling House on Candlerow Lane," she said. "Two nights ago. The report crossed my desk."

"The report crossed your desk because I put it there." Rell slid a folio across the desk. "A mute girl, age perhaps ten. She vanished the same night a licensed charm-seller in Saltrow reported a break-in attempt—sigils scratched into his door. Sigils no child should know."

"Children scratch things on doors. It doesn't make them witches."

"These sigils predate the kingdom, Thorne. Our linguists identified three of them before they stopped volunteering." His mouth thinned. "The girl is a vessel. The Office believes she carries stolen soul-matter bound for the Court Beneath the Mountain, and that someone means to deliver her there before the thirteenth moon. Your orders are to find her. To take her alive. And then—" he paused, savoring it, "—to bring in the man who's sheltering her. Isandro Pemberton. Charm-seller, Saltrow. Unlicensed practices suspected for years. You'll remember the type."

"I arrested his kind for eleven years, Commander. I remember everything about them."

"Do you?" Rell leaned back. "Because you also seem to remember your brother fondly, and your brother wrote heresy by the ream, and the only reason you're wearing that uniform instead of standing trial beside his ghost is that someone upstairs decided your loyalty was worth one more chance." He tapped the folio. "This is the one more chance. Find the child. Deliver Pemberton. Or the tribunal convenes Thursday, and we discuss Aldous Thorne's writings with you in the witness box instead of across this desk."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a clerk turned a page.

"You're asking me to hunt a ten-year-old," she said.

"I'm asking you to hunt a weapon that looks like a ten-year-old. There's a difference, and your career depends on knowing it."

He dismissed her with a flick of two fingers, the way one dismisses a servant or a dog, and Ovelia walked out into the grey morning carrying orders she intended to follow—and a question she could not put down: why did the sigils on a charm-shop door sound, in the linguists' dry summary, like something Aldous might have written?

* * *

Her brother's crate had a lock on it that she'd never broken, because breaking it would have meant admitting she'd kept it. She broke it that afternoon with a pry-bar and three years of dread.

Aldous Thorne had been the better Thorne—gentler, cleverer, cursed with a mind that couldn't leave a locked book alone. He'd joined the witch-finders for the same reason she had: order against chaos, law against the wild things. And then somewhere in the archives he had found fragments of the First Tongue, the speech older than kingdoms, and instead of cataloguing it for destruction he had begun to translate it. Secretly. Obsessively. The Office burned him for it on a Tuesday, and Ovelia had stood in the crowd in full uniform with her face like carved stone while the smoke rose, and had told herself *he was wrong, law is law*, until the telling wore smooth as a river pebble and meant nothing at all.

Now she spread his last notebook on her table under lamplight.

His hand was cramped and hurried toward the end, pages dense with transliteration tables and marginal notes: *the tongue does not command—it names. Naming is not binding. Binding is a corruption.* She remembered reading that line in tribunal records, quoted as evidence of madness. Reading it now, in his own ink, it sounded like grief.

And there—in the last quarter of the book, mixed among the grammar—a sequence of drawn characters, copied from some source Aldous had labeled only *"the old broker's marks."* Nine sigils. Curved, deliberate, older than any alphabet she knew.

They matched the description from Saltrow exactly. Three of them, character for character.

Ovelia sat back, and the lamplight buzzed, and she understood with cold clarity that she was being handed a choice shaped exactly like her life: follow the law into another burning, or follow her brother's handwriting into treason.

She packed a saddlebag instead of reporting to the barracks. She told herself it was reconnaissance. She was almost able to believe it.

* * *

Saltrow at dusk was all crooked gables and gutter-light, a district where the Crown's authority arrived late and left early. She found the shop easily enough—*Pemberton Charms & Curatives*, its painted sign flaking gold, its windows glowing warm against the rain that had started an hour past.

She did not go in. Eleven years of witch-finding had taught her patience as a discipline, and besides, she wanted to see the door first.

The sigils were scratched low on the wood, at a child's height, in lines gone silvery with rain. Ovelia knelt in the wet street like a beggar and copied them onto her cuff with a stub of chalk, comparing each stroke against the memory of Aldous's page. Three matched perfectly. A fourth was a variant—one he'd flagged in the margin with a note that made her breath catch:

*This mark appears only in binding-contracts. If found outside a contract, suspect forgery or coercion.*

Coercion. Her brother's last word on the subject.

She was still kneeling when the shop door opened above her, spilling amber light across the cobbles, and Isandro Pemberton looked down at a witch-finder squatting in the rain outside his shop with chalk on her sleeve and her sword-hilt conspicuously ungrasped.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Rain ticked on the sign overhead.

"You're the disgraced one," he said at last. Not hostile—wary, tired, the voice of a man doing arithmetic on his odds. "Thorne. I've seen your portrait in the Office gazettes. They don't flatter you."

"They don't hang traitors either, but they manage." She rose slowly, hands open. "I'm not here on orders."

"Witch-finders are always here on orders. Sometimes the orders are just quiet ones."

"My orders are to drag a child to the Office of Inquiry so clerks can open her up and find out what's inside her." The words came out flat and ugly because they were flat and ugly. "I'm telling you I won't do that. And I'm telling you why, and then you can decide whether to let me stand in your doorway or put a charm through my chest."

His eyes narrowed. Behind him, deep in the shop, something small shifted—a shadow moving between shelves, quick and silent.

"Why," he said.

So she told him. About Aldous, briefly, without apology or excuse. About the crate and the notebook and the nine marks, and the margin note, and the word *coercion* in her dead brother's hand. About Rell's threat and the tribunal waiting for Thursday like a trap waiting for weather.

"She scratches your door," Ovelia finished, "in a language my brother died translating, using a mark that shouldn't exist unless someone forced it into existence. I need to see the rest of them. All of them. Because if I'm right about what they say, then the Office isn't hunting a weapon. They're hunting evidence. Evidence that somebody powerful forged a contract with the Court Beneath the Mountain—and pinned it on a child who can't even testify against them."

Isandro's hand had drifted, without his seeming to notice, to the doorframe—as if shielding the scratched sigils from the rain. From her. From the whole watching world.

"You read First Tongue?" he asked quietly.

"I read my brother. It comes to nearly the same thing."

Another silence, and then the shadow behind the shelves stepped forward into the light, and Ovelia saw the child for the first time: small, dark-eyed, watchful as a wren indeed, clutching a grimoire nearly half her size against her chest like a shield. The girl looked at Ovelia—at the uniform, at the Office insignia still stitched at her collar—and Ovelia watched fear move through that small face like weather, and recognized it, because she had put that exact expression on a hundred faces over eleven years.

She reached up, slowly, and tore the insignia from her collar. It fell into the puddle between them with a soft, final sound.

"There," she said. "That's what I am now. Same as you."

The child studied her for a long moment. Then Wren—Isandro must have named her, or she had signed it, somehow—lifted one small finger and pointed, deliberately, at the sigils on the door. Then at Ovelia's chalked cuff. Then she pressed her palm flat over her own heart and drew the fourth mark in the air, the binding-mark, the coercion-mark—and shook her head, hard, twice.

*Not mine. Not chosen.*

Ovelia felt something crack in her chest, some load-bearing wall of duty she hadn't known was still standing. Aldous had spent his last year chasing that exact truth through forbidden archives, and here it was, kneeling in a doorway, pointing at itself.

"Let me in," she said to Isandro, and her voice was not steady. "Before Thursday finds me. Before the Office finds me. I can buy you time—I know their routes, their timetables, which inspectors are bribable and which are merely stupid. But I'm not offering to help you out of charity, Pemberton. I'm offering because if that contract was coerced—if someone used this child the way the Court uses everyone—then whoever forged it has my brother's blood on their ledger too. And I intend to collect."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment, rain running off his eaves between them, the child watching from the warmth of the shop.

Then he stepped aside, and held the door.

Inside, the lamp was lit and the tea was already steeping, and on the worktable lay a grimoire opened to its first page—where, in faded brown ink, a broker's mark waited that Ovelia knew from her brother's notes as well as she'd known his handwriting.

Vespera's mark. The old hedge-witch. The broker herself.

Ovelia set her chalk-cuff beside the page, aligned the copied sigils, and felt the last of her old life click shut behind her like a door.

"We have six days," she said, "before the thirteenth moon turns. And I think we're going to need every one of them."
