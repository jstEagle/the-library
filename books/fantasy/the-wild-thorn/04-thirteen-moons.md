## 4. Thirteen Moons

The moon is a coin behind the rain, and Wren counts it the way she counts everything now—by the pull in her chest, the slow tightening of a thread tied somewhere far below the ground.

Twelve turns gone. One remains.

She knows this without knowing how she knows. The knowledge lives in her body the way hunger lives there, older than words, older than the silence that was sewn into her throat before her first memory. When she presses her palm flat against her sternum she can almost feel it: a hook, a weight, a debt with her name written on it in a language made of roots.

The shop smells of cedar and hot copper tonight. Isandro has given her the corner by the stove, wrapped in a blanket that smells of smoke and lavender and something sharper underneath—protections, layered like scales on a fish. He works at his bench with his back to her, but he keeps turning around. Every few minutes. Checking. The way you check on a candle burning too near the curtain.

He cannot know what she knows. That they are coming early.

* * *

It begins as a wrongness in her teeth.

Wren is half-asleep when it starts, a high sweet ache like biting into ice, and she sits up so fast the blanket slides from her shoulders. The sigils she scratched into the door three nights ago—the ones Isandro traced with one finger, going pale, going quiet—are singing. Not aloud. Underneath. A vibration in the wood, in the iron hinges, in the wet street beyond.

*They have crossed the water. They have borrowed faces.*

She does not hear these things. She feels them, the way she feels weather coming in the scar over her ribs. The Court does not send letters. The Court sends pressure, a change in the air, a taste of deep stone and candle-fat at the back of the tongue.

She scrambles off her cot. Her bare feet slap the floorboards. Isandro turns at the sound, and whatever he sees in her face stops his hands above the charm he is wiring.

"What?" he says. "What is it?"

She grabs his sleeve. She drags him toward the door, and he comes, frowning, and puts his eye to the peephole—

—and recoils as if the wood bit him.

Outside, under the guttering lamp across the lane, two people are standing in the rain. A man and a woman, ordinary as bread, ordinary as Tuesday: the woman with a market basket on her arm, the man with his hat pulled low. But they are not moving. Rain falls through them without wetting their shoulders, and their shadows lie wrong—not stretched by the lamp behind them but pooled beneath their feet, black as wellwater, black as the space under a bed where the dark collects.

"Borrowed faces," Isandro breathes. His voice has changed. It sounds younger, and much more afraid. "They're not supposed to come for weeks. The thirteenth moon isn't—"

The man outside lifts his head.

He has no eyes. Where eyes should be there are two candle-flames, small and steady and utterly patient, and Wren's whole body understands what her mind cannot say: *they looked through every door in this street tonight, and only yours burned them back, and now they are done being polite.*

Isandro moves fast after that. Faster than she would have thought his careful, mending-hands capable of. He sweeps charms off shelves into a leather satchel—warding bells, bundles of rowan, a stoppered vial that glows faintly green. He tears up a floorboard behind the counter and pulls out a book.

The book is old. Old the way mountains are old, old the way the ache in her chest is old. Its cover is cracked leather gone soft as skin, and even across the room Wren can feel it humming, a low note that resonates with the hook inside her until her teeth chatter.

"Vespera's grimoire," Isandro says, to himself or to her, and his thumb strokes the cover once, tender and furious at once. "You absolute, impossible, *reckless* old woman."

Then the window explodes.

* * *

Glass goes everywhere. Not outward—inward, hanging in the air for a heartbeat, each shard lit from within by candlelight that has no source, and Wren sees the two collectors standing in the ruined frame of the window, still dry, still patient, the woman's basket now full of something that writhes gently, like eels, like fingers.

The man reaches out and touches the doorframe.

Where his finger passes, the wood forgets how to be solid. It curls back like paper over flame, and through the gap Wren sees the street, the rain, the neighbors' windows lighting up one by one with frightened squares of yellow—and then the wall itself begins to burn with a fire the color of deep amber, honey-colored, slow, deliberate, a fire that does not spread so much as *consume*, eating the shop plank by plank with an appetite that feels personal.

Isandro's hand closes around hers.

His palm is sweating, shaking, but his grip is iron, and his other arm is full of satchel and grimoire, and he is shouting something—her name? No, not her name; she has never told anyone a name; he shouts "*Ovelia!*" at the wall, at the night, at nothing—

And the back wall of the shop opens.

Not a door. A person. Ovelia Thorne stands in the shattered frame of the rear window, rain streaming off her grey coat, a witch-finder's iron rod in one hand and an expression on her face that Wren will remember for the rest of her life: fury and terror braided together so tightly you cannot tell which is holding which.

"Down," Ovelia roars, and swings the rod.

It meets the man-collector mid-stride. There is no impact, no sound of flesh—but the collector *staggers*, its borrowed face sliding sideways like a mask slipping, and beneath it, just for a moment, Wren glimpses something that is not a face at all. Something smooth and patient and old, with a mouth like a coin slot.

"*Sewers,*" Ovelia snarls at Isandro. "Trapdoor, cellar, now, unless you'd rather argue!"

"The floor's warded against—"

"It was warded. Look at your floor, charm-seller."

He looks. The amber fire has reached the boards, and where it burns, the wards curl up off the wood like scorched handwriting. Isandro makes a sound low in his throat—a grief Wren recognizes, the grief of a man watching twelve years of work become light—and then he kicks open the cellar hatch and pushes her toward it.

*Go down,* his eyes say. *Trust me.*

* * *

Here is the thing about Wren, the thing no contract has managed to erase:

She chooses.

She chose, once, at a crossroads in the rain, to follow a smell of cedar and lavender instead of the cold road. She chose the sigils on the door. She chose the blanket that smells of smoke, the man who checks on her every few minutes like a candle near a curtain.

She could run now. In the burning, in the shouting, in the smoke pouring down the cellar stairs like a grey river, no one is watching her. The collectors want her soul, not her feet; the witch-finder wants her found, not followed; and the cellar door stands open to the alley and the free dark beyond it.

She feels the pull of it. Freedom shaped like running.

Instead she takes the hem of Isandro's apron in both fists and holds on.

He glances down at her, smoke-streaked, wild-eyed, halfway down the ladder into the reeking dark—and something crosses his face that she has no word for because she has no words at all, but she thinks it might be the same thing she is feeling: the astonished, terrifying recognition of being *chosen*, of being chosen back.

"Well," he says hoarsely. "Right then."

Ovelia drops through the hatch last, slamming it above them as the amber light swallows the shop. For a moment, through the gaps in the boards, Wren watches the two collectors stand in the heart of the fire, untouched, unhurried, their candle-eyes turning slowly, slowly, searching the ashes for the shape of a debt unpaid.

Then Ovelia lights a stub of tallow candle, and the dark of the sewer tunnel leans in close and warm and wet, and the three of them stand together in the underworld of the city—fugitive, hunted, homeless—with a satchel of contraband charms and a dead woman's book between them.

Wren's chest aches. The hook in her sternum has begun to pull, gentle and insistent, downward and eastward, toward the mountain she has never seen but has always owed.

One moon left.

She looks up at Isandro, at Ovelia, at the candle-flame trembling between them, and she does the only thing she can do: she points. Down the tunnel. Toward the mountain. Toward the thirteenth turn.

Her hand does not shake.

Ovelia follows the line of her finger, then looks at Isandro. "She wants us to go *that* way. Into the deep roads." A pause. "That's not a request, is it."

Isandro hugs the grimoire tighter against his chest and manages something that is almost, almost a laugh. "No," he says. "I don't think it is."

Above them, muffled by earth and stone, the shop finishes dying. And below, in the dark that smells of the mountain's long memory, Wren walks first—because the thread in her chest is pulling, because the moon is nearly full, and because for the first time since the silence was sewn into her, she is not running from something.

She is going toward it.
