## 5. The Road Under the Law

The pilgrim road out of Greyhollow was a ribbon of mud and old prayers, worn smooth by a thousand years of feet that had nothing left to carry but hope. Ovelia Thorne walked it in a widow's grey, her witch-finder's coat folded into the bottom of a bundle, her badge buried beneath a dead woman's shawl. Twelve years she had worn the Crown's silver. Three days ago she had cut it from her own chest with a belt-knife, and the wound where it had sat still ached like a question.

Behind her came Isandro Pemberton, disguised as her brother-in-law—a lean, nervous man in a tinker's cloak who kept touching his pockets as though checking for charms he no longer carried. And beside him walked Wren, small hands wrapped around a bundle of dried apples, her hood up, her eyes moving over everything: the crows on the fence posts, the shrine niches hollowed into the mile-stones, the faces of other pilgrims trudging east toward the Spring Rites at Aldermoor.

A family. That was what they were pretending to be. A widow, her brother, and a mute child bound for the shrines to pray the silence out of her.

It should have been easy. Ovelia had written the manual on pilgrim screening. She knew every checkpoint's rhythm, every sergeant's boredom, every trick of the trade because she had *taught* half the tricks to the trade.

That was what frightened her. The hunters behind them were hunting with her own methods.

* * *

The first checkpoint came at dusk, at the toll-bridge over the Sorrel Water. Two soldiers and a hedge-priest with a censer of cold iron filings—enough to make any carried charm itch and smoke if it wasn't warded deep. Ovelia had warded theirs deep. She'd spent an hour the night before scratching Vespera's nulling-sigils into the linings of their collars while Isandro watched with an expression she couldn't read.

"You're good at this," he'd said finally. Not kind. Not cruel. Just surprised.

"I arrested forty-one hedge-witches," she said. "You learn what they hide before you learn anything else."

He hadn't answered. He'd turned away and begun packing the grimoire into oilcloth, and she had lain awake wondering which of those forty-one had been someone's mother. Someone's Vespera.

Now, at the bridge, the hedge-priest swung his censer toward them and Ovelia did what she'd told the others to do: she prayed aloud. Loudly, badly, the way a grieving widow would—the Rite of Unburdening, mangled in all the right places. The priest's attention snagged on her incompetence and slid past. Bored men look for guilt; they don't look for bad faith performed well.

"Name and business?" the sergeant asked.

"Widow Thorne. My sister's boy—" she put her hand on Wren's hooded head, and Wren, bless whatever instinct moved her, leaned into the touch and let her eyes go wide and vacant, "—was struck dumb by fever. We walk to Aldermoor to beg the Spring Mother's mercy."

The sergeant glanced at Wren. Something flickered across his face—not suspicion. Recognition. Ovelia's stomach dropped.

"My niece's child went silent too," he said. "Two winters back. Never came back." He waved them through. "Go on, then. Pray hard."

They crossed. It wasn't until the bridge was a quarter-mile behind them that Isandro exhaled, and Ovelia realized she'd been holding her own breath since the sergeant spoke.

"He wanted us to be real," she said quietly. "Grief does that. It makes people want company."

Isandro looked at her sideways. "You could have been one of them forever, you know. The ones who want company."

"I know," she said. "That's why I stopped."

* * *

They made camp that night in a shrine-coppice—an old circle of rowan trees where travelers had hung ribbons and carved wishes until the bark was scarred white with wanting. Wren stood among the trees reading the carvings with her fingertips, and something in her posture made Ovelia's chest tighten: the girl moved through the world like someone cataloguing exits.

Ovelia sat down on her bedroll, pulled out a stick, and began drawing in the dirt between them.

Wren came to see. Of course she did. Curiosity was the one thing the child wore openly.

Ovelia drew a circle. Pointed at herself. *I.*

Then a second circle beside it. Pointed at Wren. *You.*

She tapped her own chest again, then drew two lines connecting the circles. Then she waited.

Wren stared at the dirt for a long time. Then she took the stick and drew a third circle, off to the side, and connected it to both of theirs with lines of its own. She pointed at the third circle, then toward Isandro, who was pretending very hard not to watch from the fire.

*Him too,* the drawing said.

"Yes," Ovelia said, and her voice came out rougher than intended. "Him too."

By the light of the fire they built a vocabulary. *Food. Sleep. Danger. Run. Hide.* Wren learned the way water learns a channel—all at once and without visible effort—and then began inventing signs Ovelia hadn't taught her, quick elegant compressions, until Ovelia realized the child was doing to sign-language what poets did to verse.

"She's improving your system," Isandro said, settling onto his bedroll with the grimoire.

"She's improving *everything* she touches," Ovelia said. "Haven't you noticed? The innkeeper's daughter in Greyhollow cried when we left. You've known her three days."

"Some people are easy to love," Isandro said, in the tone of a man describing weather in a country he'd been exiled from. "It's a talent I never acquired."

"You love her already," Ovelia said. "You just do it the way you do everything. Backwards."

He didn't answer. But across the fire, Wren caught Ovelia's eye and made a new sign—one hand flat over her heart, the other hand folding the first into a fist around it. *Keeping.* Ovelia didn't know yet what it meant. She had a suspicion she'd find out.

* * *

Rain came on the fourth day, and with it the thing Ovelia had been dreading: a checkpoint that wasn't on any map, thrown up overnight in the market square of Harrow Fen. Witch-finders' banners. A mobile screening unit, running the new protocols—her protocols, the revised edition, published after her disgrace with her name scraped off the frontispiece.

"We can't go through it," Isandro said, low, as they stood in the shelter of a wool merchant's awning. "And we can't go around it. The fen roads flood this time of year."

"We can't fight it either," Ovelia said. "Six finders, two priests, a leashed hound-breed that smells residual craft."

"So what do we do?"

She looked at him. Rain ran off the awning between them in a silver curtain. "You teach me hedge-craft. Tonight. Enough to pass me as what I'm pretending to be."

He laughed—short, disbelieving. "Absolutely not."

"Isandro." She kept her voice level. "If they screen us properly, they'll check for craft-signatures. A widow with a mute niece and a tinker brother traveling this road in this season? They'll assume she's carrying. If she carries nothing, they'll dig deeper. The only safe lie is a true one—I need to actually have hedge-marks on my hands. Faint ones. Old ones. The kind a village wise-woman would have."

"And if you get caught with them?"

"Then I get tried as a hedge-witch instead of a deserter." She shrugged. "Better odds. I know the trials. I wrote half the questions."

Something moved behind his eyes—she saw him weigh it, saw the twelve years of running press against the three days of trust. "Vespera taught me that the first law of hedge-craft is that you don't give power to someone who held a leash," he said. "She'd skin me."

"Your mentor," Ovelia said carefully, "is currently a signature on a soul-contract under a mountain. I think her standards have some flexibility."

His jaw worked. Then: "One lesson. One. And if you use anything I teach you against my kind—"

"I burned that coat," she said. "Ask the river where I threw it."

* * *

He taught her that night in the coppice behind a hay barn, rain drumming the canvas above them, Wren sitting cross-legged between them like a small solemn adjudicator.

Hedge-craft, Isandro explained, wasn't power so much as *permission*. "The wild magic doesn't obey. It agrees. Every charm is a negotiation—you offer something small, it offers something small back. The Crown thinks magic is a weapon you confiscate. It's actually a courtesy you extend."

He showed her how to warm a stone with her palm until it held heat like a sleeping bird. How to knot a thread with intent so the knot would hold against panic—against fear itself. How to read the age of a ward by how the air tasted near it.

Ovelia was a terrible student and knew it. Her hands wanted procedure; the craft wanted conversation. Twice the stone went cold in her grip and she swore like a soldier, and Isandro grinned for the first time since she'd met him, and the grin changed his whole face, and she hated that she noticed.

"You're fighting it," he said.

"I'm *doing* it."

"No. Doing is the Crown's word for it." He put the stone back in her palm and closed her fingers over it. "Ask it."

"That's absurd."

"Everything true is absurd the first time you say it out loud."

She closed her eyes. Asked. And the stone warmed—hesitantly, grudgingly, like a stray dog accepting a hand—and Ovelia felt something shift in her chest that had nothing to do with magic. Twelve years of certainty, and here was the heresy at the center of it, small and warm and alive in her cupped hands: the things she had hunted were not things at all.

Across the fire, Wren made her heart-and-fist sign at Ovelia. *Keeping.*

"Now you keep it too," Isandro said softly. "Congratulations. You're contraband."

* * *

The screening at Harrow Fen took four hours in the rain, and Ovelia spent all of it being exactly what she now was: a tired woman with faint hedge-marks on her hands and nothing in her heart worth finding. When the young witch-finder—barely nineteen, wet, miserable, thorough—paused over her palms, she said, "My mother taught me knots for the birthing-bed. Nothing more. Take me in if knots are treason now."

The boy hesitated. Then, quietly, so the priests wouldn't hear: "My gran did the same. Go on, mum."

Walking out the far side of the square, Wren slipped her small hand into Ovelia's. Not for the performance. There was no one watching anymore. She just held on.

That night, camped in the lee of a ruined mill, Ovelia woke to firelight and found Isandro teaching Wren letters from the grimoire—the harmless ones, the household sigils—guiding her finger through the strokes while the girl mouthed shapes at the air, silent, rapt. He looked up and caught Ovelia watching.

"She asked," he said, defensive. "In her way."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

"I was going to say," Ovelia said, lying down again, staring at the underside of the mill roof where swallows' nests clung like old secrets, "that this is the first household I've ever been in that chose me back."

Silence. Then Isandro, gruffly: "Don't make it strange, Thorne."

"Too late."

Above the ruined mill, through a tear in the clouds, the moon rose fat and pale—and Ovelia, who had counted moons on campaigns, felt her stomach tighten as she counted now. Twelve nights of light left before it rounded full.

Twelve nights, and somewhere ahead, the debt-collectors of the Court Beneath the Mountain were counting too.
