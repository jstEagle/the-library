## 6. The Market of Small Mercies

The border town was called Hollowmere, though no one had called it that in living memory. On the maps it was Ashenford; on the witch-finders' ledgers it was a checkpoint town, a place where the pilgrim road narrowed between two garrison walls and every soul passing through was weighed against suspicion. But beneath the weigh-station and the garrison and the tax-collector's office, in the flooded cellar of a collapsed tannery, the old market still breathed.

Isandro found it the way Vespera had taught him to find such places: by walking with his eyes half-closed until the world's seams showed. He had Wren on his shoulders and Ovelia at his side wearing her pilgrim's gray, and when they descended the tannery's rotted stair and passed through a door that was not a door, the smell hit him like a hand across the face—beeswax and bloodroot, tallow and dried lavender, river-mud charms drying on strings. Twelve years since he'd stood in a room that smelled like this. Twelve years, and his chest still tightened with something that was not quite grief and not quite homecoming.

*Don't stare at anyone,* he murmured to Wren, tapping her knee twice—*stay close,* in the hand-language Ovelia was teaching her. *Buy nothing. Touch nothing.*

She squeezed his shoulder once: *understood.* Then, because she was eight years old and incorrigible, she turned her head slowly all the way around to look at everything.

The Market of Small Mercies was a cave of candlelight. Stalls crowded the flooded floor on stilts and rafts; vendors poled between them in flat-bottomed skiffs, hawking luck in paper packets, secondhand dreams bottled in green glass, love-charms with their efficacy honestly rated (*mild*, *moderate*, *once only, do not repeat*). Above it all hung a canopy of moth-eaten banners stitched with sigils from a dozen outlawed traditions, and among them Isandro recognized three marks from Vespera's own grimoire.

"Charm-seller," said a voice at his elbow, "you're bleeding money."

He turned. The speaker was an ancient woman poling a skiff stacked with cages of moths, each wing dusted with tiny written characters. She looked him up and down with the frank appraisal of someone who priced souls for a living.

"You have hedge-craft on you like cheap perfume," she said. "And that child has a debt on her I can smell from here. Bad combination in this economy." She held out a moth. It crawled onto Isandro's finger and its wings opened: *TRANSLATION — FAIR RATES — NO QUESTIONS ASKED*.

"We need a translator," Isandro admitted. "Old text. First Tongue marginalia."

The woman's eyebrows rose. "First Tongue? In a grimoire?" She looked at Wren then—really looked—and whatever she saw made her moth-cages rattle as the insects inside went suddenly still. Her expression shuttered. "Not my trade. But there's a man. Quill-and-Ink. Third raft past the luck-broker. Tell him Marisol sent you, and don't tell him what else I saw." She poled away fast, not looking back.

Ovelia touched his arm. "What did she see?"

"I don't know," Isandro said quietly. "That's what worries me."

* * *

Quill-and-Ink turned out to be a gaunt man in a coat made of layered letters—actual pages, cut and sewn, so that he rustled when he moved. His raft was a floating library, books in oilcloth, ink pots lashed against spillage. He listened to Isandro's request with his eyes closed, which Isandro recognized as a professional habit: reading the texture of a voice for lies.

"First Tongue marginalia," he repeated. "In whose hand?"

"A dead woman's," Isandro said. "A hedge-witch. Mother Vespera."

The rustling stopped. When the man opened his eyes, there was something in them Isandro couldn't name—pity, maybe, or fear, or the particular exhaustion of a man who has known bad news is coming for years and is almost relieved it has arrived.

"You're the apprentice," he said. "The one who vanished."

Isandro's mouth went dry. "I'm a charm-seller from—"

"Yes," the man agreed mildly, "you're certainly that." He began unlashing a strongbox. "Vespera did translation work for this market for twenty years. Half the texts in the deep stalls, she authenticated. And twelve years ago she came to me with a book and asked me to hold one page of it—sealed, untranslated, to be given only to her apprentice, should he ever come asking about First Tongue marginalia." He drew out a single sheet wrapped in waxed linen and tied with thread gone gray with age. "She said, *he'll come angry or he'll come broken. Either way, give it to him whole.*"

His hands were not steady. Isandro took the packet and could not make his own hands work either. Wren leaned against his hip, watching, and he felt her small weight like an anchor thrown into deep water.

He broke the seal.

It was a contract page. Not the grimoire's—older, formal, rendered in the high ceremonial script of the Court Beneath the Mountain, the kind of document Isandro had seen exactly once in his life, through a crack in a cellar door he had spent twelve years trying to forget. The language was legal and cold and precise, and he read it standing in the candlelight of a smugglers' market while his entire history rearranged itself around him like furniture in a house being demolished.

*A debt of one soul, freely offered, to discharge the following obligations...*

He read it twice. Three times. The words refused to change.

"It's Vespera's name," he heard himself say. His voice came out flat, foreign. "Her signature. She sold—" He stopped. Started again. "Twelve years ago. The year I left. The year the witch-finders—" 

"The year you were arrested," Quill-and-Ink said gently, "and released. Everyone wondered how. A hedge-witch caught red-handed with contraband craft, walked out of a Crown tribunal without so much as a fine? People said you'd informed on your teacher. That you'd bought your freedom by giving her up."

"I never—"

"No," the man said. "We know now. Because she told us, after. She came back here with this page and she was laughing, actually laughing, and she said: *the boy thinks he escaped because he was clever. Let him think it a while longer.*" He folded his letter-coat closer around himself. "She traded herself for you, charm-seller. Her soul, to the Court Beneath the Mountain, in exchange for yours going free."

The market noise receded. The candles seemed very far away. Isandro became distantly aware that he was sitting down—that Wren had taken his hand and was pressing it, hard, her thumb moving in the sign Ovelia had taught her just last week: *breathe. breathe. breathe.*

"There's more," Quill-and-Ink said, and Isandro wanted to strike him for the kindness in his voice. "The Court doesn't take simple payment. They take collateral. Against default, against flight, against—well. Against everything. When Vespera signed, the Court required a second soul named on the page as security. Held in trust until the primary debt is discharged." He nodded at Wren. "Hers was already spoken for. Her parents' bargain preceded Vespera's by three days. So the Court took Vespera's soul as the debt, and set the girl's soul as the collateral against it—which means, and I am sorry, truly sorry, that as long as Vespera's soul remains unpaid, the child belongs to them. And if anyone attempts to free the girl without discharging the first debt..."

He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

Isandro looked down at the page. At Vespera's signature—*V. Corvane, freely given, witness the thirteenth moon*—in handwriting he knew better than his own. The hand that had corrected his sigil-work. The hand that had fed him nettle soup and taught him which mushrooms lied. The hand that had signed herself into hell so that he could walk free and never know.

*Let him think he escaped because he was clever.*

Twelve years. Twelve years he had carried the shame of believing, some nights, that he *had* bought his freedom with her betrayal. Twelve years of guilt built on a foundation that wasn't even true—and underneath it, worse, the truth: his freedom hadn't cost one soul. It had cost two. Vespera had paid, and because she'd paid, Wren—a child who couldn't speak, whose parents had sold her before Vespera ever lifted a pen—had been chained as the guarantee of a bargain she never made.

"Can it be undone?" Ovelia asked. She had appeared at his other side without sound—witch-finder training, even disgraced—and her hand rested on her knife in a way that suggested she'd already priced the option and found it wanting. "The contract. Can it be broken?"

"Contracts of the Court are sung, not written," Quill-and-Ink said. "This page is only an echo of the song. To break the song you must sing against it—in the Court itself, under the mountain, before the thirteenth moon completes. And the singer must..." He hesitated.

"Must what?"

"Must be named in the song. Debtor or collateral or broker." His eyes flicked to Wren, and his rustling coat shivered. "Or kin to any of the three. Blood-kin, chosen-kin—the Court isn't particular about which. Only true."

Wren tugged Isandro's sleeve. He looked down. She held up both hands and made the sign they'd been practicing on the road, the one Ovelia had invented on the second night around the fire when Wren had cried herself silent and the two adults had flailed helplessly until Ovelia pressed her own fist to her heart and then opened it toward the girl:

*You are ours. We are yours.*

Then Wren pointed at the contract page, at Vespera's signature, and made the sign again.

Isandro's vision blurred. He knelt on the wet raft planks and pulled the child against his chest and felt Ovelia's hand settle on both their heads like a benediction, like a vow, like a soldier taking up a post.

"Thirteenth moon," he said into Wren's hair. His voice cracked and he let it. "How long?"

Quill-and-Ink consulted a tide-table and a moon-chart and his face went grim. "Nine days."

* * *

They bought nothing else at the market. There was nothing else to buy.

But as they climbed back up through the false door into the gray afternoon light of Hollowmere, an old woman with a skiff full of moths caught Isandro's sleeve at the top of the stair. Marisol pressed something into his palm—a moth, wings folded, characters glowing faintly through the scales—and leaned close.

"What I saw," she whispered. "The debt on the child. It's changing shape. Someone above is renegotiating." Her grip tightened. "There were collectors here yesterday. Wearing borrowed faces. Asking after a charm-seller, a witch-finder, and a little girl." She stepped back into the dark. "They weren't asking where you were, charm-seller. They were asking where you're *going*. Somebody's already told the mountain."

Behind them, far off across the rooftops, a garrison bell began to ring.

Nine days, Isandro thought, closing his fist around the moth. And the road to the mountain had just gotten shorter by half.
