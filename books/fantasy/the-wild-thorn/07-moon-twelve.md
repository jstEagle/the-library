## 7. Moon Twelve

The river smelled like iron and old coins.

Wren felt it before she saw it — a cold thread pulling through her chest, the same thread that pulled whenever the moon grew fat. Twelve moons since the men with borrowed faces had come to the shop. Twelve moons of sleeping in ditches and haylofts and once, memorably, inside a hollowed-out shrine to a god whose name Isandro refused to say. The moon overhead was nearly round now, a coin held up to a lamp, and it tugged at her the way the tide tugs at everything: patiently, without mercy.

*Almost full,* said the feeling in her bones. *Almost time.*

She rode pillion behind Isandro on the mule, her cheek against his back, counting his breaths the way she counted everything now. Forty-two breaths to the mile, if the mule was honest. Ovelia rode ahead on the cart horse, her witch-finder's coat turned inside out so the sigils of office faced her own skin. She'd done that the first night out of the sewers and never turned it back. Wren thought about that sometimes. A woman wearing her own shame against her heart like armor worn wrong on purpose.

Ovelia raised a fist. They stopped.

Below them, where the pilgrim road met the water, the bridge waited — old, humped, built of gray stone older than any kingdom, which was why they'd chosen it. Old stone remembered kindness. But there were lanterns at the near end of the bridge, three of them, burning a color that made Wren's teeth ache. Not fire-color. Debt-color. The light of things owed.

Isandro dismounted slowly, as if the air might break. He lifted Wren down and kept hold of her hand, and she felt his pulse hammering in his palm — fast, fast, faster than forty-two breaths to anything.

"Collectors," he breathed. "Here. Early. They shouldn't—"

"They go where the debt goes," Ovelia said. Her voice was flat, the voice she used when flatness was all that stood between her and screaming. "I told you. I've read their warrants. They don't hunt people. They hunt *balances*." She looked down at the lantern-light for a long moment, and then she did something Wren had never seen her do.

She took off her sword belt and handed it to Isandro.

"What are you doing."

"My face is still half-known," Ovelia said. "Yours isn't — you look like a hedge-pedlar because you are one. Take the cart, take the girl, cut east along the towpath. There's a ford two miles down where the water runs shallow over white stones. Cross there."

"And what will you be doing?"

Ovelia smiled. It was not a nice smile, but it was an honest one, and Wren had learned by now that from this woman those were the only kind. "I'm going to walk onto that bridge and remind three debt-collectors that the Crown's law still outranks the Court's ledger. Loudly. At length." She rolled her shoulders. "I spent nine years learning how to be loud at length."

"You'll be taken."

"Probably."

"*Ovelia.*"

"Listen to me." Ovelia crouched so her eyes were level with Wren's, and Wren saw that they were wet but would not spill, held back by sheer professional discipline. "Twelve years ago I burned a hedge-coven out of a mill in Aldersey because a warrant told me to. One of them was a boy your age. I have been paying that debt ever since, in installments, and tonight I get to make a payment that actually counts. That's not sacrifice. That's arithmetic." She touched Wren's chin, gently, one callused finger. "You keep him alive. He's hopeless alone. Talks to his charms. Forgets to eat."

Wren nodded. Then, before she could stop herself, she threw her arms around the witch-finder's neck and held on, hard, the way she'd once held on to a doorpost while floodwater tried to take her. Ovelia went stiff as a plank — she always did — and then her arms came up, awkward and crushing, and stayed.

"Sign-language," Ovelia said into Wren's hair. "Show me the one we made."

Wren pulled back and made it: right fist over heart, then open hand flung outward, fingers spread. *Mine. Given freely. Go.*

Ovelia's breath caught. She copied it, clumsy, wrong-handed. "That one," she said, "I'm keeping."

Then she straightened, put her hat back on, turned her collar right-side out so the sigils of office blazed silver in the moonlight, and walked down toward the bridge with her boots ringing on the road like a bell that had decided, after long consideration, to be a weapon.

* * *

They watched from the alder shadow, because Isandro could not make himself leave and Wren would not make him.

It went the way Ovelia had promised: loudly, at length. Wren couldn't hear all the words at this distance, but she heard enough. Witch-finder's cadence, tribunal cadence, the cadence of a woman who had spent nine years making grown men flinch with nothing but grammar. The three collectors stood at the bridge's foot under their wrong-colored lanterns, wearing faces Wren recognized — one was the ferryman from two towns back, one was a mother who'd sold them bread, one wore a face that wasn't finished yet, features sliding like wax near a flame.

The unfinished one spoke, and its voice came across the water clear as a bell struck inside the ear:

*"The vessel comes with us. The others may go. This is not a negotiation; it is a collection."*

"The vessel," Ovelia called back, "is a child under the protection of the Crown's road-law, and I am still — whatever else I am — a captain of the Crown. So we're going to stand here, you and I, and we're going to discuss jurisdiction until the sun comes up or one of us dies of boredom."

The unfinished face smiled. It did not have enough practice to know how smiles worked.

*"Captain Thorne. Ovelia Maren Thorne. Sister to Tomas Thorne, who wrote heresy in the margins of his own arrest-warrants before he died of it. We have read his writings. We find the marginalia... familiar."*

Wren felt Isandro go rigid beside her. Felt him understand, one heartbeat before she did, what the thing below was saying. *We know what you know. We know why you hesitated. And hesitation, Captain, is also a debt.*

"No," Isandro whispered. "No, no — Ovelia, *move*—"

But Ovelia had heard it too, and Wren watched something happen in the tall woman's spine — watched duty and doubt, those two dogs she'd wrestled all the way down the pilgrim road, finally stop fighting each other and turn together on the real enemy. She drew no sword. She had given it away. Instead she planted her feet on the old stone bridge, in the middle of it, where no one could pass without moving her, and began to recite.

Not scripture. Not law.

Her brother's last writings.

Wren didn't know the words, but the river did. She felt it shiver under the bridge, felt the water press close beneath the stones like a crowd pressing close to hear a street-singer. The First Tongue, or near it — fragments, a dead man's marginalia, heresy written in a hand that shook. Ovelia had memorized it in some sleepless year and carried it the way you carry a coal in your chest, and now she poured it out over the water in a voice that cracked and did not stop cracking.

The collectors advanced. The lanterns swung. The unfinished face stopped pretending to be a face.

And Wren understood, with a child's terrible clarity, that Ovelia had known exactly how this would end when she handed over the sword. That the arithmetic had always come out this way. That *mine, given freely, go* meant *go* most of all.

The collectors took her between them, gentle as men folding a flag, while she shouted law at them in two languages, and the lanterns swallowed her, and the bridge was empty, and the river went very, very quiet.

* * *

After, there was running. Isandro dragging the mule, Wren clutching the grimoire to her chest, the towpath flying past in strips of mud and moonlight. Two miles. White stones. Shallow water. Isandro talking the whole way, low and frantic, not to her — to himself, to Vespera's ghost, to God-or-whomever-hedge-witches-answer-to: *"Shouldn't have let her, should've gone myself, it should've been me, it was always supposed to be me—"*

At the ford he stopped, because the water was wrong.

The river had come up. Not flooding — *gathering*. It stood along the far bank in slow ropes and coils, moonlit, waiting, and every white stone in the shallows glowed faintly like teeth in an opened mouth. Isandro backed up a step. Wren didn't. She stepped past him, down to the waterline, and the pulling in her chest became a roaring.

Because the river knew the words too.

It had heard them for a thousand years, rolling down from the mountain, wearing away the mountain, carrying the mountain's secrets to the sea. And tonight it had heard a dying man's marginalia spoken aloud on old stone by a woman who gave away her sword, and it had *listened*, the way Wren had listened all her silent life — and listening, Wren knew now, was the first half of speaking.

The second half was hers.

She opened her mouth. Nothing had ever come out of it — not cries, not names, not *no*, not *stop*, not *mother* — twelve years of silence packed behind her teeth like snow behind a door. And now the moon stood almost full above the mountain, and the stolen soul in her chest turned its face toward home like a compass needle, and the river waited, and the door of snow groaned.

The first word came out broken. A croak, a crack, more breath than sound.

The second word came out true.

She didn't know what she said. She knew it the way she knew hunger — not its name but its weight. The syllables were old, older than kingdoms, older than the bridge, and they came up through her like water coming up through a well-rope, hand over hand over hand, each one colder and clearer than the last. Her throat hurt. She didn't stop. The moonlight came down the river toward her like something walking, and where it walked the water stood aside, and the white stones sang one thin high note that Wren felt in the roots of her teeth, in the marrow of the soul that was not quite hers.

Isandro had gone down on his knees in the mud. His face was wet. He was looking at her the way he looked at contraband charms worth hanging for — awe and terror holding hands — and he whispered, "*That's the Tongue, Wren, that's actually the Tongue, how do you—*" and then stopped, because obviously. Because of course. Because she was a vessel, and a vessel does not merely carry.

A vessel *pours*.

When the words ran out, the river settled, smooth as hammered tin. In the water Wren saw a reflection that was not quite the moon: a road of standing stones beneath the current, leading upstream, leading east, leading under — the root-road, the way in, shown plain as a map drawn by someone who wanted to be followed.

And beside it, fainter, written on the water in letters of drifting foam, three words in the First Tongue that she somehow, effortlessly, horribly, understood:

*Beneath waits singing.*

Wren turned to Isandro. She made the sign — fist over heart, open hand flung outward — but she changed it, added a third motion, one they'd never made, one that rose out of her the way the words had: both hands cupped, then lifted, offering upward.

*What is mine,* the sign said, *I give.*

Upstream, beneath the mountain, something that had been singing for twelve years faltered — and listened.

And began, softly, to answer.
