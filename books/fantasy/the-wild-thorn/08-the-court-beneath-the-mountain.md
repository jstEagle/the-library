## 8. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The root-roads began where the river forgot its name.

Isandro had read about them in Vespera's grimoire—passages he'd once assumed were metaphor, the way hedge-witches dressed up ordinary things in wonder. They were not metaphor. The mountain's roots were roads in truth: veins of pale stone threading down through the dark like the underside of a leaf held to light, and they breathed. Warm air rose against his face as they descended, carrying the smell of wet iron and candle smoke, and beneath it something sweeter and far worse—the smell of old promises kept too long.

Wren walked ahead of him with her hand pressed flat to the wall, reading it the way she read everything, through her skin. Twice she stopped and scratched a quick sigil in the dust of the ledge, and twice the mountain answered—a groan somewhere below, a shift of air, a door that hadn't been there opening where the path forked.

"She's talking to it," Ovelia said quietly. She had not slept since the bridge. There was a bruise on her throat shaped like a hand, and she wore her disgrace like armor now instead of shame. "The road. She's asking it for directions."

"Then let's hope it likes children better than it likes witches."

"It liked your Vespera enough to keep her."

He had no answer for that. Twelve years he'd believed her dead—or worse, indifferent, gone off into some other life the way strong people did when they grew tired of weak ones. He had built a whole quiet resentment out of that belief, brick by brick, and it had kept him warm. Now every mile downward dismantled another brick, and what was left underneath was colder: she had sold herself for him, and he had spent those years resenting her absence.

The path narrowed. They walked in single file between walls veined with something that glowed faintly amber when Wren touched it, and Isandro understood at last what he was looking at. Not stone. Wax. The whole mountain's underflesh was candle-wax, centuries deep, and buried in it like flies in amber were shapes—hands, faces, the curve of a shoulder, an open mouth mid-word.

Souls. Half-dissolved. Still singing.

He heard them then, or felt them, a vibration in his back teeth. A chord made of thousands of voices so worn down they no longer remembered being voices.

*Don't look at them,* said the sensible part of him, the shopkeeper part, the part that had survived twelve years by not looking at things. *Look at the girl. Look at the road.*

But one shape near the path turned as they passed—not moving, because the dead in wax don't move, but turning all the same—and it had gray hair pinned up the way Vespera used to pin hers, with two crossed pins, always two, because one was vanity and three was showing off.

"Wren." His voice came out cracked. "How much farther?"

She looked back at him. In the amber light her eyes were the color of the mountain itself, and for a moment he saw the soul behind them clearly—the thing the debt-collectors wanted, vast and patient and older than the child's small body it lived in. Then she blinked, and she was just Wren again, eight years old, terrified, holding his sleeve with fingers that smelled of river water and burnt bridge.

She signed: *Close.* Then, after a hesitation, with hands that trembled only slightly: *Not afraid. You came.*

*You came,* she signed again, as though she needed to press the words flat and keep them somewhere safe.

*Always,* he signed back, badly, with the clumsy grammar of a man three weeks into a language meant for hands. It wasn't true yet. But debts were paid by becoming true, weren't they? That was the whole rotten theology of this place.

* * *

They came down into the Court at moonset, though there was no sky to set a moon in, and no moon either except the one the mountain kept: a great round vault of white stone far overhead, carved with thirteen rings, twelve of them dark and one beginning, even now, to glow.

The Court Beneath the Mountain was a cavern the size of a drowned cathedral, floored in black glass that reflected ten thousand candles and none of the people standing among them. That was the first wrongness Isandro noticed: their reflections were missing. The second wrongness was the sound. The Court was full—tiers of seats carved into the living rock, and every seat occupied by a figure in robes of unbleached wool—but no one spoke. They sang. Low, continuous, interlocking lines of melody without words, each voice a thread, and where threads crossed, Isandro saw the candles gutter.

*Debts are sung here,* the grimoire had said. *Written contracts are only the score. The singing is the binding.*

At the center of the black-glass floor stood three empty chairs of white wax, and beside them, waiting, a figure who could only be the one who ran this place. It wore the borrowed face of a kindly grandmother—Wren flinched hard against Isandro's side when she saw it, and he understood: the face from the market town, the one that had smiled at her over a stall of apples while her parents counted coins. The collectors wore what was given them. What was owed.

"You brought her," the Grandmother said, and its voice was a dozen voices agreeing with itself. "That saves us the trouble of the thirteenth moon. Though we would have collected regardless. We always collect. It is the one law older than our laws."

"I'm not here to bargain," Isandro said.

"No," the Grandmother agreed pleasantly. "Bargaining is for those who have something. You have nothing but what you owe."

"Then hear the payment." He stepped forward, out from behind Wren, onto the black glass where his reflection should have been and wasn't, and he made his voice steady because a trembling offer is a refused offer. "Twelve years ago a witch named Vespera bought a boy's freedom from this Court. I am that boy. Her soul has sat in your wax since, half-sung, and this child's soul sits in collateral beside it—one debt stacked on another, both of them mine. So take me. One soul, freely offered, in exchange for both. The witch released whole. The child returned to her body, her tongue, her life. And my soul stays here, in whatever chair you like, until the mountains themselves wear down."

Silence. Even the singing paused—a held breath of ten thousand voices—and the candles all leaned toward him like grass in wind.

"You understand," the Grandmother said softly, "what you are offering."

"I understand exactly what I'm offering. I've been keeping accounts my whole life. It's the family trade I never admitted to." He thought of the charm-shop, the rain-lashed door, the sigils older than kingdoms. He thought of Vespera's hands teaching his, and of her voice saying: *A price paid unwillingly buys nothing. Remember that, little Isandro, if you remember nothing else.* "Freely offered. Willingly paid. Sing it however you need to sing it."

The Grandmother studied him with the patient eyes of a thing that had watched empires be born and foreclosed upon. Behind it, the tiers of singers leaned in, and the melody changed key—lower, slower, the key of weighing.

"One soul for two is poor arithmetic," it said at last. "The Court does not make poor arithmetic."

"The Court made this arithmetic," Isandro said. "When it accepted a brokered contract that chained a child's soul for her parents' desperation and a witch's soul for a stranger's freedom. You wrote the terms. I'm only paying them."

Something moved in the borrowed face—something almost like appetite, or perhaps only hunger's memory. The Grandmother raised one hand, and the white wax chairs at the center of the floor softened, reshaped, became a table; and upon the table, rising out of the black glass like smoke given paper, appeared a scroll. The contract. Isandro knew it before he could read it, the way you know your own handwriting. He had seen its twin in memory a thousand times—the night his apprenticeship ended, the night Vespera put him on a cart south and told him never to come back.

The Court accepted.

He felt it happen in his chest, a cold thread drawing tight behind his sternum, and distantly he understood that his soul had already begun to loosen from its moorings. It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. It felt like setting down a heavy bag after a long walk—relief, pure and simple, and underneath the relief, the long fall beginning.

*Say goodbye properly,* he told himself. *You're a shopkeeper. Close up the shop.*

He knelt to Wren's height. She was shaking her head, hard, over and over, her hands flying through signs too fast and too broken for him to follow—*no, no, you promised, you said*—and he caught her wrists gently and pressed them still between his palms.

"Listen to me," he said. "Twelve years ago someone paid for me and never told me, and I wasted every one of those years thinking I was alone. I won't do that to you. You'll walk out of here with your own soul and your own voice, and you'll be angry at me for a very long time, and that's right and proper. Anger means you expected to keep me. Keep expecting it. Expect it so hard that—" His voice went. He started again. "Expect it so hard it becomes a kind of promise."

Her eyes were huge and wet and furious, and she wrenched one hand free and signed against his cheek, slow, so he couldn't miss it:

*I will find the word. The word that breaks this. I will find it.*

And there was something in the way she signed it—not a child's comfort, but a certainty, ancient and banked like coal—that made the cold thread in his chest stutter.

Then the doors of the Court boomed open.

Every singer turned. Every candle bent sideways in a wind that came from nowhere, and down the long stair from the root-roads came a procession of debt-collectors wearing borrowed faces Isandro knew—the innkeeper, the ferryman, the kindly grandmother again, three of her now—and in their midst, in chains of white wax that crawled like living things up her arms and across her throat, walked Captain Ovelia Thorne.

They had taken her at the river's edge, then. Or she had let herself be taken—had traded herself for their escape and then, being Ovelia, had refused to stay traded, had fought her way down through the mountain's throat after them with nothing but her disgrace and her stubbornness.

Her eyes found Isandro across the black glass. Her lip was split. Her witch-finder's brand, the one they'd burned into her collarbone when they struck her name from the rolls, was bleeding fresh—they had reopened it, marked her as theirs again, a trophy reclaimed.

"Isandro!" Her voice cracked across the cavern, ragged and furious and absolutely certain. "Don't sign! Don't sing! Whatever they showed you—look at the contract!"

The Grandmother's borrowed face went smooth and pleasant. "The prisoner is distressed. The Court will—"

"*Look at it!*" Ovelia was fighting the chains now, full weight, a soldier's fury in a body that had already given everything. "I spent six years in the archives of the Order! Six years reading brokered contracts, forged marks, coerced signatures—I know Vespera's hand better than you do, you candle-eating vultures, because I catalogued her seizures! The signature on that scroll is *wrong*—the pressure, the slant—it's a signature written under compulsion, and a contract sealed by coercion is void under your own first law!"

The singing stopped. All of it. Ten thousand voices cut silent at once, and in the silence Isandro heard it: the held breath of a Court bound by rules older than its hunger.

The Grandmother turned its borrowed face slowly toward the scroll.

And above them, impossibly, the thirteenth ring of the white stone moon began—early, ahead of any schedule—to glow.
