## 9. The Thirteenth Turn

The Court Beneath the Mountain kept no windows, and so it did not know—could not know, Isandro thought wildly—that somewhere far above them the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its turn. It knew only by its candles. Ten thousand of them, maybe more, set into niches in the black stone like stars that had fallen and been made to work for their keep. Each candle was a debt. Each flame bent, very slightly, toward the soul it was owed from.

Three flames bent toward the dais where he stood.

Isandro's knees ached against the obsidian floor. He had knelt on worse—his shop's flagstones at dawn, scrubbing sigils off the door—but never with so much listening. The Court had no faces, precisely. Its members wore shapes borrowed from the mountain itself: seams of quartz for eyes, ledges of stone for mouths, voices that came up through the floor rather than out of any throat. When they spoke, his teeth buzzed.

*The bargain stands,* they sang. *One soul freely offered, freely given. Speak the words, charm-maker. Sign yourself into the book.*

The Book of Debts lay open before him on a lectern of bone-white stone, its pages soft as skin, its ink still wet after centuries. Somewhere in those pages, twelve years ago, Vespera's name had been written in exchange for his. Somewhere in those pages, seven years ago, a desperate farmer and his wife had signed away their unborn daughter. Isandro thought of Wren standing behind him—small, silent, her hand fisted in the hem of his coat—and felt something in his chest go very quiet and very certain.

He had rehearsed this. On the root-roads, walking by lantern-light, he had rehearsed the words until Ovelia told him to stop before she threw him down a ravine. But now, with the Court's attention settling over him like cold water, all the rehearsal burned away and left only the truth.

"I offer myself," he said. His voice echoed wrong in the candlelit dark, too small. "Freely. For the girl called Wren, whose soul was sold under contract—" he made himself say the number, the clause, everything the old client in the market town had taught him— "and for the witch called Vespera, held these twelve years against a debt already paid."

A ripple went through the candles. A thousand little flames leaned.

*The Court accepts,* sang the stone mouths. And then, softer, in a register that made Isandro's bones itch: *The Court notes an irregularity.*

Behind him, chains clinked.

Ovelia Thorne had been dragged down into the mountain three days ago—or three hours; time moved strangely here—and they had brought her into the court chamber in manacles, expecting her to be broken. She was not broken. She stood between two collectors wearing borrowed faces, one of them wearing, horribly, her brother's face, and her lip was split and her wrists were raw, and when she spoke her voice carried like a drawn blade across the whole chamber.

"The contract is forged!" she shouted. "I've read your law—I read nothing else for six years in the Archives, I read law the way other people drink! The Brokerage Act of the Second Turning: *no signature binds if it was taken under coercion of blood or kin.* You know it. Your own candles know it." She pointed at the flames. "Ask them."

Isandro's heart stopped, started again, hammering. *Coerced.* He had suspected it—had dreamed it, waking, in the nights since the market—but suspicion was ash without proof.

*There is no proof,* the Court sang, and the song had an edge now, a grind of stone on stone.

"There is," said a voice that was not Ovelia's, and not the Court's.

It came from behind the dais—from the wall itself. From a shape half-set into the living rock of the mountain, a figure that might once have been a woman: hair gone to grey quartz, one arm dissolved entirely into stone, eyes still—still, impossibly, stubbornly—hers.

Vespera.

She had been dissolving for twelve years, poured slow as honey into the mountain, and yet her voice came out clear as a bell rung in an empty church.

"Ask me how I signed," she said.

* * *

Wren had known the wall-woman before anyone told her who she was.

Souls recognized each other the way rivers recognized rain. All her life—her short, stolen, sold life—Wren had lived inside sensation, because words would not come and the world came anyway, enormous and bright and unbearable. She had felt the wall-woman the moment they entered the court chamber: a warmth in the stone, banked low, like a hearth fire someone had tried to smother and failed.

*Mother-Vespera.* The sigils Isandro drew in the dust of their hiding places had shown her the shape of her: hands stained with charm-ink, a laugh like dry leaves. Wren had never met her. But her soul had been brokered by her, marked by her, and souls remembered their marks.

Now the wall-woman spoke, and the Court flinched—the whole chamber flinched, ten thousand candles guttering at once—and Wren understood something with the sudden clarity of a key turning:

The Court was afraid.

Not of Ovelia's law. Not of Isandro's sacrifice. Of *proof*. Because the Court Beneath the Mountain was bound by its own laws the way a river was bound by its banks, and if the banks broke, the water went everywhere, everywhere, and drowned even the things that had built the channel.

Above them—she felt it more than knew it, a pressure behind her breastbone like a second heartbeat—the moon was completing its turn. The thirteenth moon. The hour when contracts could be spoken aloud and unmade, the hour the grimoire had whispered about in its cramped, frightened handwriting.

*When the last moon turns, what was sung may be unsung. What was signed may be unsigned. But the tongue must be true, and the speaker must be free.*

Wren looked down at her own hands. Small. Chapped from the pilgrim road. Free? They had tried to sell her before she was born. They had hunted her across three provinces. And still—still—no contract had ever touched her voice. Her parents had sold her soul's *keeping*, not her soul's *speaking*. The First Tongue lived in her like a bird in a house whose doors were all locked but its cage.

Ovelia had taught her signs on the road. *Are you afraid?* the captain's fingers used to ask each night by the fire. And Wren's answer, every night: *Yes. And ready.*

She was ready now.

She stepped forward, past Isandro—she felt him tense, felt his hand half-rise to stop her and then fall, because he understood too, he had always understood, he had taught her the alphabet of the old sigils by lamplight and never once asked her to be less than she was—and she walked to the center of the court chamber, to the place where the candles' light pooled like spilled honey, and she opened her mouth.

And spoke.

Not fragments now. Not the river-words that had turned back the debt-collectors at the crossing, not the small survival-sounds she had scraped together from terror. The First Tongue came up out of her whole, the way a spring comes up out of a mountain when the winter ice finally lets go, and it did not sound like language at all—it sounded like the world being named correctly for the first time.

*I speak the turn of the thirteenth moon,* she said, and the stones repeated it in a whisper, and the candles straightened like soldiers called to attention. *I speak against the contract written in the Book of Debts, the one bearing the mark of the witch Vespera and the names of the farmer Aldous and his wife Mirren.*

The Court's voices ground together, trying to drown her out, and she raised her hands—Ovelia's sign for *listen*, which was also, she realized now, a sign older than Ovelia, a sign that belonged to the tongue itself—and the chamber went silent.

*That contract was signed under coercion of kin. The broker's signature was taken with a knife at the throat of the child she had already saved. Let the Book show it. Let the candles show it. Let the mountain remember what it watched and did not stop.*

And the mountain *did* remember. That was the terrible, wonderful thing. The Court had built its channel through living stone, and living stone keeps every drop that ever passed through it. Deep in the walls, images woke: Vespera seven years ago, younger, her arm already going grey, kneeling in this same chamber while collectors held a squalling infant's swaddling-cloth in one fist and a blade in the other—*sign, witch, or we collect the child now and take your soul besides*—and Vespera signing, signing with her remaining hand, tears cutting clean lines through the charm-ink on her face.

Ten thousand candles bent at once—not toward debts now, but toward truth.

*The signature was coerced,* sang the Court, and its song cracked like ice on a thawing river. *By our own law... the contract is void.*

*Void,* Wren said, in the First Tongue, in the word that meant *unmade, unwritten, returned to the silence before names.* *Return what was held. All three.*

The candles began to go out. Not all of them—a thousand, two thousand, every flame that had bent toward Vespera and Wren and, in the last moments, toward Isandro himself, his offering swallowed by the collapse of the whole rotten ledger. Their light did not vanish. It *rose*—threads of gold lifting off the wicks like sparks returning to a sky, streaming across the chamber, into the wall.

Into the woman set in the wall.

* * *

Isandro watched the stone leave his mentor.

It went gently, the way frost leaves a window in the morning—one edge at a time, quartz becoming hair, granite becoming skin, the arm that had been mountain re-knitting into wrist and elbow and clever ink-stained fingers. She sagged forward out of the rock and would have fallen, but Isandro caught her, caught her the way he had failed to do twelve years ago, and she was lighter than he remembered and heavier than anything he had ever held.

"Stubborn boy," Vespera rasped. Her voice was smoke and rust and the most beautiful thing he had ever heard. "You were supposed to live a quiet life. Sell love-charms. Stay *out* of mountains."

"You sold your soul to buy my freedom," he said, and his voice broke on the last word. "You don't get to lecture me."

"Mm." Her eyes found Wren, standing in the ring of dying candles with her fists still raised, breathing hard, alive with the aftershock of her own voice. "And there she is. The little bird who out-lawyered the Court Beneath the Mountain." Vespera laughed, wet and wondering. "Twelve years I sat in this stone, listening, learning its laws, looking for the flaw in my own contract. And the flaw wasn't in the writing at all. It was waiting above the mountain, learning to speak."

Wren crossed the chamber and folded herself into them both, small arms around whatever she could reach, and for a long moment none of the three of them managed anything resembling dignity.

It was Ovelia Thorne, still manacled, standing over the Book of Debts as its coerced pages blanked themselves one by one, who ruined the moment in the best possible way.

"Pemberton," she said. "Much as I'm enjoying this—you should look at the Book."

He looked. The contract was void, its clauses unwritten, its ink faded to ghost-grey. But beneath it, rising up through the page as though the Book itself were surfacing for air, new script was forming—older than the Court, older than the kingdoms, written in the flowing hand of the First Tongue itself.

Wren read it aloud, softly, in two tongues.

*"What was bound by lies is freed by truth. And what magic has unbound, no law may bind again."*

Above the mountain, unseen, the thirteenth moon completed its turn—and began, slowly, to shine.
