## 10. A Household of Outlaws

The mountain let them go at dawn.

Not drove them out—*let* them go, the way a hand unclenches. Isandro felt it in his teeth as they climbed the root-roads one last time: a loosening, a long-held breath released through stone. Behind them the candlelit courts dimmed and guttered, and the singing of debts faded until it was only water dripping in the dark, only ordinary rock, only geology. Whatever the Court Beneath the Mountain had been for twelve years—a creditor, a jailer, a god with a ledger—it was quieter now. Not dead. Courts that old did not die. But bound, the way its own law had bound it, to release what truth had claimed.

Vespera walked between them, leaning on Ovelia's arm.

She was smaller than Isandro remembered her being, though he knew the memory lied—the last time he'd seen her he'd been sixteen and furious, and everything she did had seemed enormous, inevitable, like weather. Now she was just an old woman with soot in the seams of her hands and half her shadow missing, dissolved into the mountain during her long captivity and never wholly returned. When she stepped into sunlight at the mountain's mouth, her outline went thin as paper at the edges. She squinted against the morning and said, in a voice rusted from disuse:

"You've let your wards lapse, boy. Anyone could have found this road."

Isandro laughed. It came out cracked, and then it came out real, and then Wren was laughing too—her first laugh in two tongues, bright and broken and hers—and Ovelia shook her head at all three of them like a woman who had traded a career for a madhouse and could not, even now, summon regret.

They came down out of the high country slowly, because Vespera's legs were mortal now and mortal legs had opinions. The world below had not changed. That was the strange part. The Crown's banners still hung over the border towns; the witch-finders' pyres had left their black scars on the hillsides; proclamations still fluttered on every post, promising ruin to hedge-craft and heresy alike. Somewhere behind them, Captain Ovelia Thorne existed only as a name on a warrant, her rank struck, her face inked onto traitors' lists nailed up in market squares. Ovelia herself studied one such list in a village they passed through, read her own crimes aloud in a flat voice—"consorting with outlawed magic, aiding a fugitive child, assault upon His Majesty's witch-finders"—and pronounced it, after consideration, "a fair summary."

"You're not angry," Isandro said.

"I spent eleven years believing the law was a wall," she said, "and that I was the mortar. Turns out it was a curtain." She pulled her hood lower and kept walking. "I'm deciding whether to be furious or relieved. Ask me again in a season."

Wren took her hand without asking, the way she'd learned to do on the pilgrim road, and Ovelia's fingers closed around the small ones, and the question answered itself well enough for walking.

* * *

The shop was exactly as the fire had left it, which is to say: a shell, a wound, a skeleton of char and standing stone. The debt-collectors with their borrowed faces had burned it thoroughly, but they had been creatures of contract, not arson, and contracts are precise things. They had destroyed the stock, the ledgers, the front room's cheerful clutter of bottled luck and stitched blessings. They had left the walls. They had left the cellar door.

And beneath the cellar, sealed under wards Vespera had taught him when he was young enough to think warding was a chore, they had left everything that mattered.

Isandro stood in the ruined doorway on the first evening back, rain coming down soft instead of violent for once, and broke the seal with a word of the First Tongue that Wren breathed along with him, her voice a half-step behind his like an echo learning to lead. The stair unfolded into darkness that smelled of beeswax and dried rue and twelve patient years. Vespera's grimoire lay where they had hidden it before the flight, wrapped in oilcloth, beside jars of preserved charm-stock and a strongbox of coin and, wrapped separately in velvet, the contraband that had made him rich enough to be hunted: the true charms, the old magic, the work no licensed practitioner dared touch.

"Home," Vespera said from the top of the stairs, testing the word.

"It's ashes upstairs."

"Mm. So was I, a month ago." She descended one careful step at a time. "We'll manage."

*We*. He looked at her—at the woman who had sold him without telling him, who had bought his life with her own soul and let him spend twelve years believing himself abandoned, unloved, a burden discarded. He had raged at her in the mountain's candlelight. He had shouted the betrayal out of himself until there was nothing left but the shape of it, and the shape, examined honestly, was this: she had never told him because she hadn't wanted to be thanked. She had wanted him free and ignorant of the price, and when ignorance proved impossible, she had wanted him to hate her less than to grieve her.

He wasn't sure he managed either. But she was here, mortal, diminished, drinking tea he made too strong, correcting his sigil-work with a trembling finger, and hatred had nowhere left to stand.

"I'm going to rebuild the shop," he said one night, three weeks in, while they worked by lamplight cataloguing what the cellar had saved. "Upstairs too. Properly."

"The law hasn't changed."

"No. The law is exactly as stupid as it was."

"And you mean to hang out a shingle anyway. Sell outlawed charms under a witch-finder's window."

"Not sell." He set down the inventory quill. "Shelter. There were others like me, Ves. Others she hid, others she brokered for, others the Court holds collateral on right now with no thirteenth moon to force anyone's hand. And there are children like Wren—there will always be children like Wren—as long as desperate parents exist and brokers are willing to take coin. Someone has to keep the door open. Someone has to know which sigils to scratch on it."

Vespera was quiet a long moment. Lamplight moved in the hollows of her face.

"That," she said finally, "was the shop I meant to build. Before I ran out of soul to pay for it." Her eyes, when they lifted to his, held something he had never seen in them as a boy and would have named, as a boy, as weakness. It was apology. It had probably always been apology. "Build mine, then. Build it better."

* * *

They rebuilt through autumn.

Ovelia turned out to be good with her hands in ways that surprised everyone including her—she framed the new second story alone, muttering soldier's curses, and installed a door with a lock worth the name and a peephole worth more. Wren chose the color of the shutters by pressing her palm against samples and watching which one made her sigils hum, and so the shop reopened under shutters the color of river-clay, and if that shade happened to be the exact tone of the First Tongue's written form, well. Only four people in the city would ever know why.

The sign was Isandro's one indulgence. PEMBERTON & COMPANY, it read, in plain letters, and beneath it, smaller: *Charms mended. Debts unwound.* No license number. The licensing office would have refused him anyway; his name was on lists now too, sharing wall-space with Ovelia's. Magic remained outlawed. Justice remained unwritten. The Crown's proclamations fluttered on their posts, unchanged, and the witch-finders still drilled in the square, and none of it mattered as much as it had, because Isandro had seen the inside of the machine that laws were built to appease, and it was only a court, and courts could be spoken to.

Courts could be *spoken to*. That was the outlawry he carried now, worth more than any warrant: the knowledge that the oldest law of all preferred truth to payment, and that a child's voice could make even a mountain keep its word.

Customers began to come before the paint dried. Not the old trade—not the frightened buyers of bottled luck. These came at dusk, hooded, scratching the old sigils on the door the way Wren had scratched them that first rain-lashed night, and Isandro would open to whatever knocked: a girl whose soul was half-promised to something in the fens, a man marked by a broker's brand, a family carrying a grandmother who spoke in tongues older than kingdoms. He fed them. He warded them. He taught them which roads ran under the law and which roads ran beneath it. Ovelia drilled escape routes into the household with a general's rigor and taught the newcomers to read warning-signs in official proclamations—the Crown announced its purges weeks early, if you knew how to parse the phrasing, and no one parsed like a disgraced witch-finder.

And Wren grew.

It was the quiet miracle of that winter. The stolen soul, returned whole, turned out not to have been stunted by its imprisonment but *seasoned* by it—she carried the First Tongue the way other children carried a lullaby, effortlessly, and she was learning the common tongue around its edges, two languages braiding together in a girl who had been mute for nine years and now would not always stop talking. She narrated everything. She narrated the cooking. She narrated the rats (sympathetically). She sat cross-legged on the rebuilt counter teaching Vespera children's hand-signs for *please* and *again* and *that's funny*, signs Ovelia had invented on the pilgrim road, and Vespera learned them with the fierce diligence of a scholar handed a grammar no book contained.

One evening near midwinter, Isandro came down from rehanging the shutters to find the three of them at the kitchen table—his table, bought used, scarred, perfect—with the grimoire open and Wren copying sigils into her own little book, the ones she wrote coming out cleaner than the ones printed in the text, because she wrote them as a native speaker writes and the grimoire recorded a foreigner's ear.

"She'll surpass me within the year," Vespera said. There was no bitterness in it. There was, Isandro thought, something better: appetite. "I intend to steal everything she knows."

"That's my job," Isandro said. "You stole enough already."

"Twelve years of interest, boy. I'm owed."

They were still arguing—comfortably, ritually, the argument they would apparently have for the rest of their lives—when the knock came.

It was late, past the hour when honest customers knocked, and it was wrong in a specific way: not the scratch-scratch of sigils on wood, not the coded triple-tap Ovelia had established for friends. A single knock. Heavy. Patient.

Ovelia was up first, hand finding the knife she no longer officially carried. Wren's chatter stopped mid-word, and the lamplight leaned toward her as every ward in the house woke at once, humming the note of her voice. Vespera closed the grimoire with one finger marking the page.

Isandro crossed the shop floor, put his eye to the peephole, and went very still.

Outside, snow falling past her shoulders, stood a woman in traveling leathers with frost in her hair and the exhausted posture of someone who had walked a very long way from somewhere worse. She was nobody he knew. But pinned to her cloak, glinting dully, was a broker's brand—and clutched in her bare fist, held up to the peephole as if she knew he was looking, a scrap of parchment covered edge to edge in the First Tongue.

Beneath it, in the common script, five words in a shaking hand:

*They took my son's name.*

Behind him, softly, in two tongues, Wren said, "Papa. Open the door."

Isandro looked back at his family—at the witch-finder with her knife, the diminished witch with her grimoire, the child with the key in her throat—and lifted the bar.

* * *

The door opened, and the snow blew in, and the light stayed lit.
