## 1. Salt on the Pass

There are three rules to running the high roads of Thornfell, and I've broken two of them tonight.

Rule one: never travel the Kestrel Pass after the first snow. Rule two: never carry more than you can abandon. Rule three—the one I've kept, mostly, out of self-respect—never use what's in your blood when a mule and a strong back will do.

The mules are doing their best. That's more than I can say for the weather, which has decided, somewhere above the tree line, that autumn is a suggestion rather than a season. Snow comes at us sideways, dry as ash, hissing against the lantern glass. Behind me, six mules plod in single file with salt, iron nails, and a crate of Greyford brandy that would hang us all if the toll-wardens found it. Ahead, old Tam Vetch leads on foot because he doesn't trust any beast he isn't holding by the bit, and Tam is seventy if he's a day, which means every step up this pass costs him something he won't ask back.

"Road's gone soft," he calls over his shoulder, and his voice is half-swallowed by wind. "Feel it under you. It's listening."

I don't laugh. On the Kestrel Pass, that's not poetry. The whole mountainside above us is scree held together by habit and frost, and when the frost lets go, the mountain remembers it was never a road at all—just a wound it's been politely ignoring for ten thousand years.

I've run this route eleven times. I know where the switchbacks bite, where the drop-off falls away into cloud so deep you could drop a cathedral down it and hear nothing come back. I know how to read the sky over Thornfell like a ledger: what's owed, what's coming due. And right now the sky owes nothing and is collecting instead.

We make the third switchback before it happens.

It starts as a sound like a door opening very slowly—a groan from somewhere upslope, low and mineral and patient. Then the snow stops falling sideways and starts falling upward, which is the mountain's way of clearing its throat.

"Rockslide!" Tam's voice cracks like a whip. "Off the road! Get them off the road!"

There's nowhere to go off to. That's the joke about switchbacks: one side is wall, the other side is sky. The mules scream—that flat, human sound they make—and the lead animal rears, and behind her the whole line tangles, ropes snapping taut, and I feel the vibration come up through my boots like something enormous rolling over in its sleep.

Above us, the slope moves.

Not all of it. Just a shelf of it, maybe forty tons of stone and old ice deciding, all at once, that gravity has waited long enough. It comes down the gully directly above the middle of our line, slow at first, then not slow at all, and there is no time for cleverness or courage. There is only the moment where I look at my people—Tam hauling at a mule's bridle with arms too old for it, young Piet frozen flat against the cliff face with his eyes shut like a child hiding from thunder—and I stop being Lena Whitlock, guild runner, and become something else.

Something I've spent thirty-one years pretending I'm not.

I put my hand against the rock.

That's all it looks like, afterward, to anyone who saw anything at all. A woman pressing her palm to stone, the way you might steady yourself. What leaves me goes out through my fingers and into the mountain's bones, and it isn't words—it's never words—but it is *hold*, and *stay*, and *not yet*, said in the old grammar, the one that runs underneath Thornfell like water under ice. The magic takes something from me going out; it always does. This time it takes the warmth from my hands and the color from the edges of my vision and three full seconds of my heartbeat, which stutters like a bird hitting glass.

And the mountain listens.

The slide hits the shelf of bedrock above our heads and splits around it like a river around a stone, roaring past on either side close enough to powder our shoulders with grit, and then it's below us and gone, thundering down into the cloud, taking the road with it. Where we stood there is now nothing but air and a ribbon of gravel hanging on the cliff face like a torn hem.

Silence. Even the wind holds its breath.

Then Tam says, quietly, "Well," and Piet opens his eyes, and the mule nearest me decides, sensibly, to sit down.

* * *

We camp an hour lower, in the lee of a granite shoulder, because nobody's knees will negotiate another mile. I build the fire small and feed it pine knots so it burns blue and hot and throws no light past arm's reach. Piet hasn't spoken since the pass. He keeps looking up-slope, then at me, then up-slope again, wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Lucky," he finally says. "That bedrock. Right where it needed to be."

"The mountain's been standing there longer than we have," I say. "Sometimes it holds."

"Sure." He nods too many times. "Lucky."

I let it lie. That's rule four, the unwritten one, older than the guild: let everything lie. Let luck take the credit, let weather take the blame, let the gift stay buried so deep that even you start to believe the story. Because here's the truth I've carried since I was nine years old and my mother pressed her hand over mine and told me *never where anyone can see, never where anything can feel*: magic in Thornfell doesn't leave footprints. It leaves traces. A cold spot in the air. A wrongness in the grain of things. Any hedge-witch with a knucklebone and a candle can read yesterday's working out of a room like ink off a page.

And lately—I don't know why, maybe the same way dogs know a storm is coming before the sky turns—the crown's readers have gotten better at reading.

Tam settles beside me at the fire's edge, joints cracking like green wood, and passes me a flask of the brandy we're smuggling, because Tam believes cargo is for drinking in emergencies and this qualifies. For a while we just watch the dark where the road used to be.

"My grandfather," he says at last, "worked the passes before the Veil went up. Said there were folk then who could talk to stone the way you'd talk to a horse. Not commanding. Asking." He takes the flask back. "Crown burned every one of them they found, and wrote in the histories that there'd never been any such folk at all. Neat trick, that. Killing a thing and killing the memory of the thing, so no one even misses it."

My hands are still cold. I keep them toward the fire and say nothing, and Tam, bless him, looks at me exactly as long as a man needs to in order to decide not to know something, and then looks away.

"Eleven times you've brought us through this pass," he says. "Twelve, now. Best runner the guild's had since the old days." He stands, groaning, and pats my shoulder once, briefly, like sealing a letter. "Get some sleep, girl. Greyford by tomorrow night."

He shuffles off to his bedroll, and I sit alone with the fire and the arithmetic Piet couldn't finish.

Because here's what I haven't said aloud, not even to myself: the working cost me more than warmth. When I touched the stone, for half a heartbeat I felt the mountain's memory—slow and vast, patient as geology—and underneath it, threaded through it, faint as a hairline crack, I felt *other* workings. Old ones. Recent ones. Someone else has been asking the stone for favors along the Kestrel Pass. More than one someone. The bloodlines didn't die when the histories said they did. They just learned Tam's rule: let everything lie.

And if I could feel them, then—

The thought arrives with the wind, cold down the back of my collar:

*then someone could feel me.*

* * *

Greyford shows itself at dusk the next evening, a smudge of lamplight in the valley with its crooked bridge and its smell of tanneries and woodsmoke, and I come down the last mile feeling like a woman walking into a story she's read before and didn't like the ending of.

The town is wrong. I can't name it at first—same shuttered shops, same drunk singing outside the Salt Cellar—but wrong the way a portrait is wrong when someone has moved the eyes a half-inch. Then I see it: paper. Fresh paper on every post and door and churchyard gate, pasted up in neat rows, the paste still wet enough to shine.

Tam stops at the edge of town, sniffs, and spits. "Proclamations. Crown's been busy."

I swing down and pull the mule line toward the guild's stable yard, telling myself it's toll notices, new tariffs, the usual small tyrannies. But my eye catches on the big sheet nailed to the well-post, the one written in a clerk's careful hand with the royal seal stamped red at the bottom like a wound, and I read it by lantern light because some part of me already knows I have to.

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN OF THORNFELL:** *Let it be known throughout the realm that the practice of forbidden arts is treason most foul. All persons harboring such gifts within their blood shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who register shall be shown mercy. Those who conceal themselves shall be hunted. This is the beginning of the Veilhunt. There will be no second proclamation.*

A boy of maybe twelve is nailing up the last copies, whistling. An old woman reads the notice twice, crosses herself the old way, forehead to navel, and hurries home. Nobody speaks. That's the worst part—how quietly a town can swallow a thing like this, the way a body swallows poison, without a sound until it's too late.

Registration. Mercy. Hunted.

I stand in the lane with smuggled salt on my boots and a dead woman's secret in my blood, and I think of my mother's hand over mine, and of the stone asking me *who else*, and of thirty years of invisibility that I suddenly understand was never real—invisibility is a story the hidden tell themselves so they can sleep, and tonight, in Greyford, the crown just announced that it has stopped believing stories.

Behind me, in the stable yard, Tam is unsaddling the mules and humming.

Ahead, the guildhall windows glow yellow down Crooked Lane, and I realize I need Aldous. Tonight. Before the thirty days grow any shorter. Because if the crown has started hunting, then the rolls—the membership rolls, every name, every runner, every bloodline the guild has kept in trust since before the Veil—are no longer a ledger.

They're a list.

I turn the mules toward the lights, and the wind comes down off the pass behind me carrying, or so I imagine, a faint mineral voice saying what it always says, slow and patient as stone:

*Hold. Stay. Not yet.*

But not yet is running out.
