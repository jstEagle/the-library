## 2. The Proclamation

Greyford smelled the same as it always had in autumn: woodsmoke, wet wool, and the river-mud the tanners never quite washed out of their aprons. I had run this town's back lanes for eleven years. I knew which innkeeper watered his ale and which one watered his ledger, knew which bridge warden took a cut and which took a bribe of honey-cakes because he was too proud to take coin from a woman. Greyford was a town that minded its own business so thoroughly it had made an art of it.

I told myself that was why I'd come here instead of pushing north through the pass. Not because the guild's lowland routes were safer now. Because Greyford didn't look at anyone too closely.

That was a story, of course. I was good at stories. You have to be, when your whole life is one you're telling to yourself before breakfast.

The caravan had come down off the high roads two days behind schedule, three mules lighter than we'd started—no thanks to me. There had been a moment on the Serrat switchbacks when half the trail let go, stone and scree sliding toward Marta Osten's cart with her two boys asleep inside it, and I had done what I always promised myself I wouldn't do. I had reached into the rock with something that wasn't hands.

It took less than a breath. The slide shivered, hesitated like a dog called back from a rabbit hole, and went around the cart instead of over it. Nobody died. Nobody even woke up, except old Tam, who slept badly anyway, and all he saw was me standing very still with my hand out, looking at the mountainside like it owed me money.

"Wind shear," I said.

Tam looked at me for a long moment. "Aye," he said. "Wind."

He didn't say anything else, not that night, not the next day. But I caught him watching my hands sometimes, the way you watch a door you've noticed is never quite locked. And every hour since the rockslide, I had felt the gift sitting in me like swallowed coals, faintly warm, faintly *known*, as if using it had written my name somewhere in the world's margins where anyone literate enough might read it.

So: Greyford. Lowland. Ordinary. A place where nothing ever happened loudly enough to be remembered.

I was delivering salt and iron filings to Ferro the chandler, which was code for delivering nothing at all, and drinking his wife's terrible plum brandy, which was payment for delivering nothing at all. That was the morning the criers came.

* * *

You always hear them before you see them—the bells first, then boots, then the voice. Greyford hadn't heard criers in years. The last time was when the old king died, and that announcement had been met with a shrug and a cheaper vintage of mourning.

This was different.

"The crown decrees!" the man shouted from the well square, his voice cracking on the second word like he was new to the job or scared of it. "By order of His Majesty and the Office of the Veil: all persons harboring the tainted gift shall present themselves for registration within fourteen days. Registration ensures protection. Failure to register constitutes treason."

*Tainted.* He said the word the way you'd say *rat-bitten*.

"Registration ensures protection," he repeated, and I'll grant him this—he didn't flinch under the silence that came back at him. Three hundred people stood in the market square holding bread and turnips and children's hands, and nobody said anything, and the crier's voice climbed higher to fill the space. "Those who conceal the gifted are guilty alongside them. Rewards are offered for information leading to—" and here he faltered, reading from the parchment now, "—*the identification and recovery* of unregistered practitioners."

Recovery. Like you recover stolen goods. Like you recover from a fever.

I stood at the edge of the square with a crate of nothing in my arms and did the arithmetic I'd been doing my whole life without admitting it was arithmetic. Fourteen days. How many gifted in Greyford? Maybe none. Maybe two. Maybe the widow Brenner whose garden grew things out of season, though everyone blamed her southern seeds. Maybe the ferryman's boy who could calm any animal, though everyone blamed his gentle hands.

Maybe me.

Here's the thing about hiding something for thirty years: you stop noticing you're doing it. The concealment becomes the shape of you, like a corset you wore so long you forgot your ribs were being pressed inward. I had built Lena Whitlock out of careful habits—never letting my shadow move wrong, never letting my temper spike near anything breakable, never, *ever* letting the warmth behind my sternum rise past my throat. I had done it so long I genuinely believed I was just a woman. A smuggler, yes, a criminal of the ordinary kind, but a woman with two hands and no more magic than a fencepost.

Standing in that square, listening to a frightened man read a frightened decree, I understood—with a clarity that felt like falling—that invisibility had always been a story I told myself. The Veilhunt didn't make me hunted. It just said the quiet part where everyone could hear it.

Beside me, Ferro's wife, Odile, crossed herself the old way, thumb to brow to navel. "Filth," she muttered, meaning the crier, meaning the crown. "My grandmother had the sight, you know. Read tea leaves. Never hurt a fly."

"Did she register?" I asked, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved.

Odile snorted. "Register? In *this* town? We don't tell people things in this town." Then she looked at me, and there was a half-second where her eyes did something complicated—something that might have been a question—and then she patted my arm and went back inside, and I decided I hadn't seen what I'd seen.

That's the other thing about stories you tell yourself. They only work if everyone agrees not to check.

* * *

The family on Tanner's Row lasted nine days.

The Vessics—Piotr, his wife Anka, their daughter Mila, maybe ten years old—had lived at the end of the row for as long as I'd known Greyford. Piotr fixed boots. Anka kept bees. Mila had a gap in her teeth and a habit of talking to the bees, which everyone thought was charming, the way everyone thinks everything is charming right up until someone official decides it isn't.

I knew. Let's be honest with ourselves, since apparently no one else in Greyford was going to be: I knew Mila was gifted. You can tell, if you know what to look for—not the girl, but the parents. The particular vigilance. The way Anka watched Mila talk to the hive with love on her face and terror underneath it, like a woman watching her child play near a cliff she couldn't explain to anyone. I recognized it because I'd worn the same expression in mirrors my whole life.

I could have warned them. I told myself afterward that I hadn't known how bad it would get, but that's another story, isn't it? I could have walked down Tanner's Row on day two and said, *Anka, take the girl north, take her anywhere, the rules have changed.* I didn't. I told myself it was because drawing attention to them would draw attention to me. That was even true. But underneath the true thing was a smaller, uglier true thing: I had spent thirty years perfecting the art of not being involved, and old habits don't break just because the world does.

On the ninth night, the house next to theirs burned its lamps late, and in the morning a crown wagon came down Tanner's Row, and the men who got out of it weren't soldiers exactly. They wore grey, no livery, and they moved with the bored efficiency of men doing a job they'd done forty times. One of them carried a list.

They took the Vessics out of their own front door at dawn. Piotr argued, briefly, until one of the grey men broke his arm against the doorframe—not angrily, casually, the way you'd close a gate. Anka didn't argue at all. She held Mila's hand and walked, and the girl looked back once, at her bees, at the white boxes in the garden that would die without her.

And the neighbor—Bram Hessel, the neighbor, the man whose kids had grown up sharing bread and chickenpox with the Vessic girl, the man I'd seen Piotr help re-shingle his roof two summers running, the man who'd wept at his own mother's funeral in a way that seemed to prove he had a heart in there somewhere—Bram Hessel stood in his doorway with his arms folded, and when one of the grey men glanced at him, Bram nodded. Just once. Just a small nod, the nod of a man confirming a delivery.

He had written the letter. Of course he had. Everyone knew by noon—the way everyone knows everything in Greyford, which is to say by not saying anything at all. The reward, it turned out, was twenty crowns and exemption from inspection, and I heard later—I heard it the way you hear things, in fragments, in lowered voices at the chandler's—that Bram's letter hadn't even named a crime. It had just said: *the child talks to the bees, and the bees come when she calls, and I thought you should know.*

*I thought you should know.* Five words, and a family gone.

What hollowed me out wasn't Bram's cowardice. Cowardice is common; I traffic in it professionally. What hollowed me out was that I understood him. Completely. I had written the same letter my entire life, over and over, in every silence I'd kept, in every warning I hadn't given, in every gifted person I'd walked past in a market square while telling myself the story about the fencepost woman. Bram had just gotten paid for it.

That evening I sat in my room above the chandler's shop with the shutters closed, and I took stock the way I take stock of cargo. What I had: a gift I couldn't put down, a face that was probably already described in some registrar's book of guild names—I didn't know that yet, not for certain, but I could feel it coming like weather in my knees. What I owed: nothing, to no one, which suddenly felt less like freedom and more like a debt unpaid.

Mila Vessic had talked to bees. Somewhere east of here, in a waggon with barred windows, she was learning what the crown meant by *recovery*.

I poured myself Odile's terrible plum brandy and drank it down, and it burned all the way to the coals in my chest, and the coals answered—just a flicker, just a pulse of heat against my ribs, like something waking up and asking, *now? Is it now?*

"Not yet," I told it. "Not yet."

But I sat down that night with a map of the northern passes spread across my knees, and I traced the road back up toward the guildhall with one finger, over and over, because there were questions I needed answered and only one man alive who could answer them. Aldous knew the rolls. Aldous knew every name, every bloodline, every hidden thing the Smugglers' Guild had spent a century burying deeper than ore.

And I needed to know, before the grey men found their way to my door, whether my name was already on somebody's list—or worse, whether Aldous had been the one holding the pen.

Outside, Greyford settled into its usual quiet, the quiet of people agreeing not to look. Nine days, I thought. This town had held its shape for two hundred years, and it took nine days and twenty crowns.

I blew out the lamp and started packing.
