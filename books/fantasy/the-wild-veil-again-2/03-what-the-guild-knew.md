## 3. What the Guild Knew

The guildhall door was unlocked.

In thirty years of running contraband over the Thornfell high roads, I had never once seen that door unlocked. Aldous kept it barred with three locks and a superstition, and he'd taught every one of us to knock twice, pause, then once—*like a friend asking, not a soldier demanding.* The ritual mattered. Rituals were the only currency a smuggler could mint herself.

I knocked anyway. Old habits die harder than people do, though as it turned out, both were possible.

No answer. I pushed the door open and stood in the doorway while my eyes did arithmetic they didn't want to finish.

Empty.

Not *tidy* empty, not *we've stepped out to the Broken Keg* empty. Emptied the way a house is emptied when someone knows exactly what they're taking and what they're leaving for whoever comes next. The ledger shelf bare. The map cabinet standing open like a mouth mid-scream. Even the iron stove was cold, and Aldous had kept that stove burning through two coronations and one civil skirmish because, he said, "a guildhall that smells of ash smells of defeat, and defeat is contagious."

The long table still stood in the middle of the common room, scarred with twenty years of knife-points and ring-marks and one memorable brand from the night Tam Vetch set his sleeve on fire proving a point about gunpowder. Someone had left a single cup on it. Upside down.

An upside-down cup means *closed*. Every guild runner learns it before they learn to pick a lock. It's how we tell each other a safehouse is burned, a contact is turned, a road is dead. Aldous had left me a message in the only language we both spoke fluently.

*I should have walked out right then.* I want that on the record, somewhere, in whatever ledger the gods keep for fools. I saw the cup. I read it perfectly. And instead of turning around and putting Greyford and its proclamations behind me at a dead run, I climbed the stairs to Aldous's study, because I have never once in my life taken the first exit out of a burning building. I always have to see the fire.

His study smelled of ink and cold pipe smoke. The strongbox under the floorboard was open and hollow. The wall where the guild charter hung—the original, signed by nine smugglers who'd decided that if the crown wouldn't protect mountain folk, the mountain folk would protect each other—was bare except for a pale rectangle where forty years of dust hadn't gotten the memo.

And on the desk, weighted under a stone, a letter. My name on it in Aldous's careful clerk's hand, the hand he used for contracts and confessions.

*Lena.*

*I'll not insult you by pretending this is anything but what it is. You're the best reader I ever trained; you'll have the shape of it before the second line. So read it fast and be gone before dark.*

*The Veilhunt came to me three nights past. Not hunters—worse. A minister's man, soft-spoken, with a list already half-written and my own name at the top in the crown's fair copy. He offered pardon. Full pardon, lands, a stipend, my old bones allowed to finish rotting in a warm bed instead of a cell. The price was the rolls.*

*I gave them to him.*

*Every name. Every bloodline. Yours, girl, though I marked your entry thin—a cousin's daughter, no gift confirmed, a runner and nothing more. It may hold. It may not. I am not writing this letter to beg forgiveness, because I know you too well to think you'd grant it cheaply enough to be worth the asking.*

*Here is what I will say, since no one else will: I am sixty-eight years old. I buried a wife and two sons in the Fever Winter, and I built this guild out of grief and spite because it was the only thing I knew how to build. And last month I sat across a table from a man with clean fingernails and understood, all at once, that the guild was going to die whether I sold it or not. The roads are closing. The criers are reading lists. There is no version of the coming years in which the Smugglers' Guild survives, because there is no version of Thornfell in which Thornfell survives. All I chose was who would be standing in the wreckage when it fell—and I chose myself. I'd like to tell you I agonized. I poured a drink, and then I signed. That was the whole of the agonizing.*

*One thing more, and then I release you from owing me a single thing. The minister's man said something at the door, offhand, the way men say true things when they think they're saying small ones. He said the hunt needs a Door sealed properly, and sealing wants living hands. Gifted hands. I don't know what it means. But you're clever, and you're stubborn, and you're the only person I ever trained who asks the second question. So ask it.*

*Go north. Go anywhere. Don't come looking for me, because I won't be findable, and because if you stand in front of me I don't trust either of us.*

*Burn this.*

*A.*

I sat down in his chair, which felt like trespassing, and read it twice more. Then I did the thing he asked and the thing he knew I'd do, in reverse order: I went looking for him, and then I burned the letter in the cold stove, lighting it first with a match because I wasn't about to waste magic on kindling.

That's a joke. That's what I told my face so it would stop doing whatever it was doing. My hands, notably, did not laugh.

Outside, Greyford's evening bell rang, and somewhere a crier was still working the square, his voice worn down to a metronome. *Register or be taken. Register or be taken.* I pulled my hood up and slipped into the dusk feeling like a woman whose house had been sold out from under her while she slept in it—except the house was her own skin.

* * *

Aldous Vane, in the sixty-eighth year of a life he had recently begun auditing without mercy, rode east in a crown carriage and told himself a story.

It was a good story. He'd been polishing it for three days and it had developed a fine shine: *an old man makes an impossible choice to save what can be saved.* The carriage smelled of velvet and someone else's money. His pardon papers sat in his breast pocket, heavy as a stolen purse, and every mile they jostled against his heart he reminded himself that hearts were unreliable narrators.

He had not looked at the rolls since handing them over. That was discipline. A man who looks at a thing he has done will either flinch or justify, and Aldous had spent a lifetime teaching runners that flinching gets you killed and justifying gets everyone else killed. He preferred to simply *not*, which was a third option he was discovering had teeth.

Still, names surfaced unbidden, the way water finds cracks. Tam Vetch, who'd run powder since he was twelve and laughed like a landslide. The Marrow sisters, who could warm a frozen spring with a touch and had saved, by Aldous's own count, eleven caravans from ice-death. And the Whitlock girl, who'd come to him at sixteen with nothing but quick hands and a quicker silence, and whom he'd taught to hide a bonfire inside a candleflame.

*Marked her entry thin,* he reminded himself. *A cousin's daughter. No gift confirmed. It may hold.*

It was remarkable, he thought, watching the Thornfell peaks slide past the window like witnesses declining to testify, how many sentences a guilty man can build out of the word *may*.

The minister's man sat opposite him, making small notes. "You'll find the pension generous," the man said, without looking up.

"I find it sufficient," Aldous said.

"And the guild? Truly finished?"

"The guild was finished the moment the crown started burning villages. I merely chose the epitaph." This was true, and he clung to its truth the way a drowning man clings to driftwood that is visibly, structurally, insufficient.

The minister's man made another note. "There will be work, later. Consultation. You know the passes better than any living man."

"Any living man," Aldous agreed, and heard, too late, what he'd said, and watched the other man hear it too, and neither of them smiled.

At the relay station outside Duncarrow he asked for a moment alone, stood in the yard in the cold, and permitted himself precisely sixty seconds of grief—one for each year he'd pretended the guild would outlive him. Then he straightened his coat, because a man who has sold his people should at least carry himself like it cost something, and got back in the carriage.

He did not think about the upside-down cup. He had left it deliberately, one last professional courtesy, and telling himself it was courtesy was the finest polish yet on the whole shining story.

*She'll go north,* he thought, as the horses took up their rhythm. *She always goes north when she's angry. North is where the hard questions live.*

He hoped she'd survive the answers. It was the only prayer he had left that didn't embarrass them both.

* * *

By full dark I was on the north road with a pack, two days of food, and the specific fury that comes from being betrayed by someone who taught you everything you know—including, as it happened, how to spot betrayal.

North, because Aldous was wrong about one thing. I wasn't going north because I was angry. I was going north because of the sentence he couldn't explain: *the hunt needs gifted hands to seal the Door.* Somewhere between his lines I had stopped grieving and started planning, which is the Whitlock family cure for grief and probably why we die alone.

Because there was a rumor moving ahead of the fires, the way rumors do—quiet, shapeless, passed runner to runner in the old code that apparently only I remembered now that I was the guild. About a royal cartographer. A disgraced one. A man who'd spent years drawing maps with lies folded into the borderlines—not lies to flatter the crown, but mercies: false passes, phantom valleys, blank spaces where hunted people could actually *be*. They'd stripped his commission for it. They hadn't found his blanks yet.

If anyone alive knew where the Hollow Door truly lay—if anyone had ever drawn it, before the crown started sanding the country smooth—it would be a man who made his living hiding places inside maps.

So north, then. Through passes I'd run a hundred times, now dotted with new watchfires, past signs whose village names had been freshly painted over in crown gray.

I walked fast. Behind me, the guildhall stood dark and unlocked, and somewhere ahead of me a mapmaker was waiting to be found—or caught—and I intended to get to him first.

I owed Aldous one betrayal-for-a-betrayal: I was going to survive this. Loudly. Inconveniently. With witnesses.
