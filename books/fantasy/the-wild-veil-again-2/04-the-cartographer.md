## 4. The Cartographer

They teach you at the Royal Cartographic Office that a map is a promise. Every line you draw, every name you ink, is a contract with the traveler who follows it. Get the ford wrong and someone drowns. Miss the ridge and a caravan walks off a cliff in the dark. I believed that for nineteen years. I believed it right up until the morning I picked up my pen and drew a village into existence where no village had ever been.

My name is Ivo Marsh, and for eleven of those years I was the finest mapmaker Thornfell ever produced. That's not vanity; that's what the archives say, or said, before the archives were purged along with everything else. I could read terrain the way priests read scripture. I once charted the Sablefen marshes from a single afternoon's observation and a dead man's diary, and my map of them has saved—saved, past tense, when saving was still permitted—forty-one lives by official count.

Unofficial count is higher. Unofficial count includes the children.

* * *

The trouble with being good at your work is that people trust you with things. A junior clerk came to me three winters ago, shaking so hard he could barely hold his satchel, and inside the satchel was a list: names of gifted children in the eastern valleys, ages six to thirteen, marked for "assessment" by an office that didn't officially exist. The clerk's own sister was on it.

I want to be able to say I agonized. I didn't. I looked at the list for perhaps a minute, and then I did what any honest cartographer would do with information that needed to disappear.

I made it geography.

A border here can be moved a half-mile without anyone noticing. A river bends differently on paper than it does in the world, and nobody checks, because nobody expects the maps to lie. So I lied, magnificently, systematically, for three years. Every child on that list became a feature of false terrain: a hamlet that never existed tucked into a valley the crown believed was empty, a shepherd's track drawn through country too rugged to bother surveying, a whole fictional parish called Merrow's Fold with its own church, its own boundaries, its own little ink-drawn immunity. The hunters rode out to assess children who were, according to every document in Thornfell, living somewhere else entirely.

Merrow's Fold. I named it after my mother. She'd have found that funny. She'd have also found the whole enterprise blasphemous and probably reported me herself, but she'd have laughed first.

It held until it didn't. It always holds until it doesn't.

Three weeks ago, an auditor cross-referenced my border surveys against a tithe ledger from 1802 and found that the parish of Merrow's Fold paid taxes to no one, appeared on no census, and had, over sixty years of my family's records, never existed. I was arrested on a Tuesday. I escaped custody on a Wednesday, which I'm still rather proud of, given that my primary physical talent is walking long distances while complaining about the weather.

Since then I've been running. Not well. Maps tell you where everything is; they don't tell you how to survive once everything is looking for you.

* * *

Which brings us to tonight, and the barn, and the very bad position I currently occupy, which is face-down in hay with a knee on my spine and a hunter's blade resting against the back of my neck like a cold, patient finger.

"Master Ivo Marsh," says the voice above me, savoring it. "Formerly of His Majesty's Royal Cartographic Office. Formerly." The word *formerly* gets a lot of work these days. "Do you know what they do to liars at the Citadel?"

"They make them auditors," I say, into the hay. It earns me a knee pressed harder into my back. Worth it.

There are four of them. I counted through the slats as they circled the barn—four hunters in Veil-grey, moving like men who've done this often enough to enjoy it. Their captain, a broad woman with a burn scar down one cheek, crouches now where I can see her, turning my satchel over in her hands. My satchel. Which contains, among other things, the last true map of northern Thornfell, the one thing in this country I am absolutely not prepared to lose.

"Well, look at this," she murmurs, working the buckle. "The famous liar's kit. Pens. Inks. And—" she draws out the map, unrolls it halfway, and goes very still.

I go still too. Because that's not the map I packed.

That's not the map I packed. I've been sleeping with that map under my head for two weeks. What she's holding is a copy—good, but a copy—and I know exactly one person who could have lifted it off me in my sleep and left a duplicate, and that person is currently not in this barn, because if they were, I would already be asking them some very pointed questions about sleight of hand.

The captain frowns at the parchment. "These lines are wrong."

"They're mine," I say. "They're supposed to be wrong."

"No." She looks closer, and something shifts in her face—not doubt exactly, but the particular discomfort of a professional encountering work better than her own. "This valley. There's no valley here. I've ridden that ground." Her eyes come up to mine, and I watch her arrive at the thought I've been dreading for three weeks: *if the mapmaker lies, maybe the crown's maps lie too.* "How many of these are false?"

"Wouldn't you like to know."

"I would, actually. That's rather the point of me." She stands, rolls the map closed, and signals her men. "Take him to the wagon. He'll talk on the road. They always talk on the road."

Here's the thing about being cornered that nobody tells you: it isn't dramatic. It's mostly administrative. You think about the weight of your hands, the distance to the door, whether the hay is dry enough to burn. You think, absurdly, about unfinished work. I thought about Merrow's Fold, about seven children hidden in country only I could find again, about how the knowledge of their hiding place lived in exactly one skull, and that skull was about to be transported to the Citadel at a moderate pace with rest stops.

Then the lanterns went out.

All four, simultaneously, and the temperature dropped like a stone into a well, and one of the hunters said a word I won't repeat, and the captain shouted *flare, flare now,* and somebody struck light and by the time it caught—

By the time it caught, the barn door was open and there was a woman standing in it with frost smoking off her fingers, and she said, in the flat, carrying voice of someone who has practiced sounding calm:

"Gentlemen. The barn roof is about to come down on all of us. I'd run."

The roof beam groaned. Snow that had been sitting peacefully on the loft since morning decided, all at once, to become an avalanche. The hunters are trained men; trained men know when physics has changed sides. Two of them bolted. The captain hesitated—one heartbeat, eyes on me, doing arithmetic—and then the second beam cracked and she went too, barking orders into the whiteout.

And the stranger grabbed my collar, hauled me off the hay, and dragged me out the back into the night at a speed I haven't managed since I was twenty-five.

* * *

We ran for perhaps twenty minutes, which in the foothills above Greyford means we covered the distance a sensible person walks in forty, and then stopped in a hollow of black pine where she finally let go of me and stood breathing hard, hands on knees.

"You," she said, when she could speak, "are the heaviest man I have ever rescued."

"You didn't rescue me. You collapsed a building near me and we both happened to leave." I straightened my coat, which had lost a button somewhere in the last decade. "Also, you stole my map."

She looked up sharply. "What?"

"The satchel. Whatever's in it now, it's a copy. Someone took the original off me while I slept and left a forgery, and since you're the only person who's been within thirty feet of me since Tuesday—" I let that hang.

For a moment she just stared, and I watched her decide something. Then she reached into her coat and produced the map. My map. The real one, edges soft with handling. "You sleep like a corpse and carry contraband in a bag with a torn seam," she said. "I checked it for hunter's marks. I was going to return it."

"Were you?"

"After I'd read it." She handed it back, and here's the part I keep turning over: she handed it back. Everything I know about people in the last three weeks says you don't hand a thing like that back. "Your borders are wrong," she added. "Deliberately."

"Yes."

"How many places on it don't exist?"

"Depends what you mean by exist." I unrolled it between us, angling it toward the moonlight. "Merrow's Fold is here. Sixty households, a chapel, a spring. No such place has ever stood on that ground. But seven children are alive right now because the crown believes it does. So. Does it exist?" I tapped a false river bend. "This watercourse is nine miles west of where I've drawn it. Three families fled along the false route last autumn and the patrols followed the map instead of them. Did that river save them, or did I?"

She was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed—the flat calm gone, something rawer underneath. "I've spent my whole life making sure nobody knew I existed," she said. "No guild record. No registry. I signed nothing, celebrated nothing, told no one. I thought that was survival." She looked at the map, at all its beautiful, merciful lies. "You spent yours inventing people. I spent mine deleting one."

"Lena," she added, after a pause, as though remembering names were also contraband. "Lena Whitlock. Smugglers' Guild, or what's left of it."

"Ivo Marsh. Disgraced, apparently permanently."

"Why did you come to Greyford? Everyone with sense is running the other way."

"Because everyone with sense doesn't know where the children are. I'm the only map of them that's still walking around." I rolled the parchment closed. "And I'm heading north, to the Hollow Door, because rumor says it's real, and rumor is the only institution in Thornfell that hasn't been purged yet."

Something crossed her face at that. *Hollow Door.* Recognition, and fear, and underneath both a kind of hunger I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors.

"You've heard of it," I said.

"I've heard it only opens for people who remember the country as it truly was." Her jaw tightened. "And I've heard what the Veilhunt actually is. It's not a hunt, Ivo. It's an erasure. They're not just taking the gifted. They're striking names off signs. Old women in Greyford can't remember streets they've lived on for fifty years." She looked north, toward the passes. "If they finish, there won't be a Thornfell to escape from. Just a country-shaped hole."

I looked at her—this stranger with frost still melting off her knuckles, who'd pulled me out of a barn for reasons she hadn't explained, who carried my map for hours instead of selling it—and I understood that we had the same wound. Hers was written in absence. Mine was written in ink. But it was the same wound: we had both learned that the surest way to destroy a person is to destroy the memory of them, and we had both, in our different trades, been fighting that destruction alone.

"All right, Lena Whitlock," I said. "Here's my offer. Your gift, my maps. Together we might actually reach this Door before the crown does. And if rumor's right about what it wants—" I hesitated, because the next part mattered, "—then somebody has to remember Thornfell properly enough to open it. Somebody with a head full of the old roads, the old names, the old country."

"And that's you?"

"That's both of us, if we're lucky."

She almost smiled. It wasn't much of one, but it was the first thing resembling warmth either of us had produced all night. "One condition. We stop at the old tollhouse on the north road tomorrow. If the guild sold me out, I need to know what else they knew."

"Tollhouse it is." I shouldered the satchel, original map restored to its proper place against my ribs. "Fair warning: I complain about the weather."

"Noted." She turned north, into the pines, and paused. "Ivo. Those seven children at your Merrow's Fold. How long can they stay hidden?"

"A few more weeks. Maybe less. The auditor's found the thread; he'll pull it."

"Then we'd better walk fast," Lena Whitlock said, and started up the mountain, and for the first time in three weeks I let myself think the dangerous word.

*North.*
