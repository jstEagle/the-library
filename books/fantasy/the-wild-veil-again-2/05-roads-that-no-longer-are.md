## 5. Roads That No Longer Are

*—Lena—*

Ivo's maps were the most beautiful things I had ever seen, and I include in that assessment the sunset over Lake Vess that made me cry when I was nine and didn't know yet that crying was a liability.

They were drawn on oiled linen, inked in three colors of iron-gall, and they were wrong. That was the point. Every border bulged where it shouldn't. Rivers bent politely around villages that officially did not exist. A mountain range called the Gray Teeth on every royal survey appeared instead as a low green spine with a fold of paper hidden inside it—a literal fold, creased so finely you'd miss it unless you knew to look—and tucked in that fold were names. *Kestrel Hollow. The Nine Sisters. Old Maren's ford.* Places that had been struck from the crown's atlases two generations ago, kept alive by a disgraced mapmaker who drew lies for a living and truths in the margins.

"They'll hang me twice for these," Ivo said, watching me turn them over by firelight. "Once for the false lines. Once for the true ones."

"Which set got you disgraced?"

"The true ones." He said it without self-pity, which was somehow worse. "A cartographer who tells the truth is a heretic. A cartographer who lies well is a servant of the realm. I was good at both, and only one of them paid."

We traveled north by night and slept in the folds of his maps, figuratively speaking, though there were nights I'd have happily crawled into the linen itself if it meant being somewhere the Veilhunt hadn't reached yet.

It took four days to learn how wrong we were about that.

The village of Brantley sat in a river valley Ivo marked as *Brant's Lea*, and the difference wasn't a lie or an old name drifting—it was a wound. The sign at the crossroads had been painted over, white slathered across whatever had been written there, and beneath the paint you could just make out the ghost of letters. Someone had burned the old mill. Not looted it, not repurposed it—burned it, and no one had cleared the stones, as if the whole village had agreed without agreeing not to look at them.

An old woman sold us bread at her door. She had flour to the elbows and a face like a folded map herself, all creases going different directions.

"Pretty country," I said, testing. "You been here long?"

"All my life." She smiled. Then the smile did something strange—stalled, like a cart wheel hitting a rut. "All my... yes. It's good land. Good land." She handed me the loaf and looked past me at the burned mill, and her eyes went smooth as pond ice. "Was there something down there? I never go down there. Nothing down there but stones."

Ivo didn't speak until we were a mile out. "She's not lying," he said. "That's what frightens me. Liars have seams."

"I know." My voice came out flat. "I've been one my whole life."

* * *

*—Ivo—*

There is a particular craft to erasing a place. I know because I practiced it from the other side for eleven years. You don't burn the buildings first—that draws attention. You start with the records. Change the tax rolls, and within a decade the clerks forget why the village pays its tithe to a lord whose name matches no deed. Redraw the road as a track, the track as a footpath, the footpath as nothing. Grief needs geography. Take away the geography and the grief has nowhere to stand.

I had assumed the crown was doing this slowly, bureaucratically, the way erasure is always done.

I had not assumed they could do it to a woman's own head.

By the sixth day we had passed through three villages, and in each one the pattern held: fresh paint, dead names, elders who spoke of their own history the way you'd describe weather in a country you'd never visited. In the second village a man told us proudly that his family had "always lived here," and when I asked him the name his grandfather used for the stream behind his house—the stream was right there; you could hear it—he looked at me with genuine, unguarded puzzlement and said, "It's just the stream."

Lena walked faster after that. I noticed she stopped touching things. Doorposts, fence rails, the standing stones at the valley mouth that every traveler touches for luck. When I asked, she said, "My gift reads through my hands sometimes. And everything around here feels like it's holding its breath."

I understood, then, why she'd agreed to come with me so easily, a stranger with a bounty-shaped reputation. It wasn't trust. It was that we were both people who had spent our lives making ourselves smaller than the truth, and now the truth was being shrunk on an industrial scale, and neither of us could bear to watch it done to someone else's life when we'd done it so carefully to our own.

On the eighth night we found the cairn.

It stood above the pass road, waist-high, a marker raised by some guild or clan long forgotten—except it hadn't been forgotten, because I had it on a map. *The Wren Cairn, raised 1213 by the freeholders of the Vale, in memory of the winter they fed each other.* I had copied that inscription onto my private sheets because it was the kind of thing worth copying.

The cairn bore no inscription. The stones were bare, recent-bare, chiseled clean within the last few months. Someone had climbed up here with tools and sanded the memory off the rock.

Lena put her palm against it anyway. I watched her do it, watched her flinch.

"There's still something," she whispered. "Underneath. Like a scar. Stone remembers longer than people." She pulled her hand back and looked at it as if it had betrayed her. "But it's fading. Whatever they're using—it works on stone too, eventually. Just slower."

"That girl you told me about," I said carefully. "In the fold. The one who hears memories in stone. If the stone can remember—"

"Then someone has to ask the stone before the crown finishes sanding." She turned to look south, back down the long dark of the pass we'd climbed. Somewhere behind us were hunters, and somewhere ahead of us, if the old smugglers' songs were half true, was a glacier with a door in its belly. "How far to your Door, cartographer?"

"Four days. Five, with children." I said it plainly because she deserved plainness. "And Lena—if the Door opens for those who remember Thornfell as it truly was—"

"Then we're in a race," she said, "against a country forgetting itself."

She said it lightly. People who say terrible things lightly are either very brave or very close to breaking, and after eight days on these roads I had learned you often can't tell which until too late.

* * *

*—Lena—*

Here is what nobody tells you about watching your homeland get erased: it doesn't feel like tragedy. Tragedy has weight. This felt like static—the slow wrongness of a world slightly out of tune, everywhere, all at once, deniable at every single moment and undeniable in aggregate.

A boy in the last village sang a skipping rhyme with a hole in it. Four lines, and the third line was just humming, a melody-shaped absence where words used to be. His mother hummed along. Neither of them knew anything was missing. That was the worst part—not the loss but the contentment of the lost.

I started writing things down. Names, mostly, in the little wax tablet I carried for tallying cargo. *Wren Cairn. Brant's Lea. The miller's daughter at Greyford was called Tamsin—she had a laugh like a gate hinge and gave extra bread to dogs.* I filled the tablet, then the margins of a spare map Ivo let me deface, which for him was apparently an act of profound intimacy.

"You're doing what I did," he said one night, watching me write. "Becoming a false border. Hiding true things inside yourself."

"Somebody has to be the margin," I said. "Since you taught me they matter."

He almost smiled. On Ivo, an almost-smile was a festival.

We crossed the high shoulder of the pass on the eleventh day, and below us lay the glacier country—white, silent, and utterly unmarked. Even Ivo's maps went vague there. Beyond the ice line, the crown's surveys simply stopped drawing, as if the cartographers themselves had decided some places were better left blank.

Except one sheet. The last one, the oldest, soft as cloth from handling. At its northern edge, in ink gone brown, Ivo had written three words in a hand younger than the rest:

*The Hollow Door.*

Below it, smaller: *Ask the stone. It remembers what we won't.*

"We're close," he said.

I felt it before he said it—a pull, faint and cold, like a hook set gently under my sternum. My gift had never pulled toward anything before. It only ever leaked. But something under that glacier was leaking too, and it recognized the leak in me.

That should have felt like hope. Mostly it felt like being summoned.

We made camp early, in the lee of a ridge, because Ivo's knees had opinions about glaciers and mine had opinions about everything else. I was banking the fire when I saw it: far back down the pass, a thread of torchlight moving with the disciplined spacing of soldiers, not travelers.

I counted the lights. Twelve, maybe fourteen. Too many for a patrol. Too few for an army. Exactly right for a hunting party that knows its quarry.

"Ivo." I didn't whisper. Whispering wastes the seconds you'd spend running. "Company."

He looked, and I watched him do the same arithmetic, and arrive at the same sum.

"They've been behind us since Brantley," he said quietly. "Maybe since Greyford. Herding us."

Toward the Door, or away from everyone who might help us—either way, the conclusion sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone. They weren't following us.

They were escorting us somewhere.

And I remembered, with the sudden awful clarity of a flare lit in a dark room, something Aldous once said over cheap wine in the guildhall, laughing: *Every road in Thornfell ends at a tollhouse, girl. Every one. The crown built them so.*

Ahead of us, where the pass narrowed between two shoulders of black rock, there was a light that wasn't moving. A building hunched across the road like a spider in a web, lanterns burning steady and patient in its windows.

A tollhouse.

Waiting.
