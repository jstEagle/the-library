## 6. The Tollhouse

They took us at the old tollhouse on the Kestrel Road, which was fitting, because the last time I'd passed through it, I'd been the one doing the taking.

The tollhouse sat where the road narrowed between two shoulders of rock, a squat stone building with a roof that had been leaking since before I was born. In the guild's better days, we paid its keeper to look the other way, and he paid his wife's doctor with what we gave him, and everyone pretended this arrangement wasn't bribery so long as no one used the word. That was Thornfell in miniature, if I'm honest. A whole country built on polite arrangements nobody named aloud.

Now there were six horses tied outside it and a fire burning in the hearth, and the smoke rose straight up into a windless grey afternoon like a signal only meant for one reader.

I saw it first. Ivo didn't—he was head-down over his maps, walking with that particular cartographer's hunch, muttering about contour lines as though the road itself might correct him if he got an elevation wrong. I put my hand flat on his chest without breaking stride.

"Smoke," I said. "Straight column."

He looked up. He had the gift of reading country the way other men read faces, and I watched him do it now—the slope of the land, the position of the building, the way the road bent. "Tollhouse," he said quietly. "We could cut east, through the scree—"

"And leave our tracks across open stone for anyone on the ridge to read? No. Scree is honest going but it shouts." I was already thinking, already running the arithmetic I'd run ten thousand times on ten thousand roads: weight, distance, cover, odds. "We walk in calm. We're drovers. We've a delivery for the garrison at Vail and we're behind schedule and annoyed about it. You complain about your feet. I complain about you. Nobody looks twice at people mid-argument."

"You want me to argue with you."

"I want you to be a man who argues with his wife. Can you manage that?"

Something flickered across his face—not quite offense, not quite amusement. "I can manage being irritating. The rest is acting."

* * *

We almost pulled it off.

We were forty feet from the tollhouse door, deep in my invented grievance about how he never once checked the axle grease though I'd asked him eleven times, when the door opened and a man stepped out into the road, and every plan I had built died between one breath and the next.

I knew him. Of course I knew him. I'd taught him to read a trail at fifteen, shown him how to walk a load past a checkpoint with his heartbeat slowed and his eyes bored, drilled him until he could lie to a magistrate without his ears going red. Dain Ferrow. Nineteen years old the last time I'd seen him, all elbows and appetite, laughing at my jokes too loud because he wanted so badly to be good at this.

He stood in the doorway now wearing a hunter's black coat with a captain's braid on the sleeve, and he was twenty-three or twenty-four, and there was nothing left of the boy in the way he held himself except, maybe, the way his eyes went straight to me before anywhere else in the world.

"Lena Whitlock," he said.

"Dain," I said. Because what else was there? *Captain Ferrow* would have been a surrender of a different kind.

Behind him, inside the tollhouse, I counted four more shapes against the lamplight. Hunters. Not a patrol—a hunting party, provisioned for days, moving with purpose. They hadn't stumbled on us. They'd been waiting on this road, and I understood why a moment later, when Ivo shifted beside me and I felt the maps under his coat like a second spine.

"They said you'd come this way," Dain said. "The guildmaster's information was very thorough."

Aldous. Even now, even sold and pardoned and presumably sitting by someone else's fire eating someone else's bread, the man was still spending my name like coin. Something in my chest went cold and clean, the way it always did when grief arrived too efficiently to be felt properly.

"Was it?" I said. "Did he include my preferred brand of tea? He always prided himself on detail."

One of the hunters laughed—a short, startled bark—and Dain turned his head just slightly, and the laugh died. That was new. The boy I remembered couldn't have quieted a room with a glance.

"You'll come inside," Dain said. "Both of you. Hands empty."

"We're drovers," Ivo began, and Dain looked at him with something almost like pity.

"The mapmaker," he said. "Yes. We know."

* * *

They took the maps first. That was the worst of it, watching a soldier who couldn't have told a contour line from a crack in a wall peel Ivo's life's work out from under his coat page by page, laying them on the tollhouse table among the bread and the tin cups. Ivo stood very still while they did it, stiller than I'd ever seen him, and I understood that they weren't taking paper. They were taking the only remaining proof that certain valleys, certain villages, certain names had ever existed at all.

Burn those, and somewhere in Thornfell a grandmother's memory of her own wedding would simply be wrong forever.

Dain sat across the table from me. The other hunters had drifted to the corners with the practiced boredom of men who'd done this many times, and Ivo was bound to a chair by the window—not cruelly, Dain had given the order himself, I noticed—but bound.

"So," I said. "The Veilhunt suits you."

"It pays," he said.

"That's not what I asked."

His jaw worked. Outside, the light was failing, and the fire threw his shadow huge against the far wall, bigger than any boy I'd trained. "You want me to say I'm ashamed. Fine. I'm ashamed. Does that help you? Does that change anything?" He leaned forward. "You know what I learned in the guild, Lena? You taught it to me. *Survival has a price.* You said it like it was wisdom. Well, I survived. I paid."

"You paid with other people."

"And Aldous didn't?" His voice cracked on the name, and I saw it then—the wound under the braid. He'd worshipped Aldous. We all had. "He sold every one of us and they gave him a house in Greyford. So don't stand there and tell me the difference between us is principle."

"The difference," I said, "is that I haven't handed anyone over yet."

"No," Dain said softly. "You haven't. But you will." He tapped the table, once, and something in his face shifted—not triumph, exactly. Relief, maybe. The look of a man about to set down something heavy. "Because here's what you don't know, and what Aldous either didn't know or didn't sell. Why do you think the crown wants the gifted taken alive? All of them, Lena. Registration, pardon offers, the works. Dead gifted are worthless to them."

I kept my face still. It was the oldest trick I knew. "Enlighten me."

"There's a place in the north. Under the ice. The old stories call it the Hollow Door." He said it flatly, like a man reciting orders, but his eyes stayed on mine, and I gave him nothing. "It leads out of Thornfell. Out entirely. And the crown wants it sealed—permanently, irreversibly, so that nothing gifted ever leaves again. But it won't take force. They've tried force; the mountain doesn't care about force. It takes a sealing. A binding done from inside, by a living gift, freely offered." He paused. "Or not so freely. They aren't particular anymore."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere in the walls the fire ticked and settled.

"They need one of us," I said. "Alive. To close it."

"One of you. Willing or otherwise. And every hunter in Thornfell has orders to bring one in." He spread his hands. "So you see the problem. I found you. My report goes out tonight regardless of what happens in this room. If you're not on that report, I'm the one who answers for it."

* * *

Here is what I should have done: I should have waited for dark, and I should have used the gift.

Not the small flare I'd spent on the pass—that was candlework, a whisper. What I'd never shown anyone, not even Ivo, was what lived underneath. The Whitlock gift ran toward memory, toward the spaces in a mind where things are kept. I could open a person the way you'd open a locked box, gently, if they let me—or not so gently, if they didn't—and I could take out whatever was inside and hold it up to the light between us.

And Dain Ferrow had five years of memories he'd rather die than look at directly. I'd have wagered my life on it. In fact I was.

Instead I did something braver and stupider. I leaned across the table and said, "Do you remember the winter we got caught in the whiteout above Marrow Gap?"

His whole body flinched. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Because I do," I went on, conversationally, while the hunters by the wall exchanged glances they thought I couldn't read. "You were sixteen. You cried—I tell you this with love—and you were so ashamed of crying that you tried to bury yourself in the snowdrift rather than face me. And I dug you out, and I told you that shame was heavier than any pack we'd ever carry, and that the day you stopped being ashamed of being human was the day you became a real runner." I tilted my head. "Did I lie to you, Dain?"

"No," he said. Barely a sound.

"Then here's the rest of the lesson." I kept my voice low and level, and I did not touch the gift, and that restraint cost me more than any working ever had. "You're sitting across from the woman who dug you out of a snowdrift, and you're deciding whether to feed her to the same machine that bought Aldous. And I am telling you—I am *asking* you—to remember what it felt like when someone chose you over the sensible thing. Because that's all mercy is. Choosing someone when the ledger says otherwise."

"Lena—" Ivo started, warning in his voice. He thought I'd lost my mind. Bargaining with sentiment, with a hunter, with a *boy*. Maybe I had.

"I can't lose you," I said to Dain. "But you can lose me. That's the whole shape of it. One word from you and I'm in chains and Ivo's dead in a ditch, because they don't need him alive. Or—" and here I leaned closer, and dropped my voice to the register we'd used on the roads, the one meant for secrets that could hang us both— "you write your report. Tonight. Full and honest: *contact made, subjects resisted, pursued south toward Vail.* And tomorrow morning you find cold ash where a campfire was and tracks leading the wrong direction, because I know these mountains better than the men who drew your orders, and so does the man whose maps you're stealing."

"They'll know I let you go."

"They'll suspect. There's a difference, and you of all people know the price of the gap between them." I sat back. "I'm not asking you to be innocent, Dain. Those days are gone for both of us. I'm asking you to be slow. Just once. Just slow enough."

He looked at me for a long time. The fire had burned down to a red heart, and in its light he looked, finally, his age—young, and tired, and carrying something enormous behind his ribs that no coat with a captain's braid could disguise.

"If I do this," he said at last, "and they trace you anyway—if anyone dies because I was *slow*—"

"Then that's mine to carry, not yours. That's what mercy costs. Somebody pays it." I stood, slowly, showing my empty hands to the room. "You were the best runner I ever trained. Better than me, maybe. Don't waste it on their road."

* * *

They released Ivo's bonds at midnight, which was Dain's compromise with his conscience—evidence of resistance, he said, in case anyone asked. The maps they kept. I watched Ivo hand them over one page at a time, and I watched him keep his face empty, and I knew he was memorizing every line as it left him, burning each valley into the back of his skull where no quartermaster could reach.

At the door, Dain caught my sleeve. Just for a moment.

"The Door," he said, low. "If you're fool enough to go looking—it's under the Serel glacier. North. And Lena?" His grip tightened. "The sealing doesn't have to be willing, but it has to be *given*. Understand the difference. They'll take everything else from you first."

"I understand," I lied, or half-lied. I didn't understand yet. I wouldn't, not fully, for some time.

He let go of my sleeve and stepped back into the firelight, and he was already the hunter again, the boy folded away somewhere beneath the braid where no one would find him. But as Ivo and I walked out into the freezing dark and took the south fork of the road, exactly as my false report required, I heard—one last time, faint, carried on the wind off the ice fields—

"North, Lena. Before they send more than me."

* * *

We walked until the tollhouse fires were a memory behind us, and then we walked more, because Ivo was right about one thing: a head start is only worth what you spend it on.

Near dawn, on a ridge with the whole grey country laid out below us, he finally spoke. "You bargained with a hunter. With memory. You could have *taken* him—"

"He wasn't mine to take."

"He was armed, Lena. He had us both—"

"He was mine," I said, "in the way that matters. I trained him. Whatever he is now, some of it I built." I looked north, where the glaciers stood white and patient under the fading stars, hiding whatever it was the crown feared enough to hunt children for. "And if the Door needs a gift freely given, Ivo, then somebody in this story has to remember what giving looks like."

Ivo followed my gaze. He was silent a long moment. Then, quietly, in the voice of a man reading terrain he hoped he'd misread:

"The girl at the fold. The one who hears stone. She said the Door was hungry." He swallowed. "She didn't say for what. But she looked at you when she said it."

The wind came down off the ice, smelling of iron and distance, and somewhere north beneath the Serel glacier something ancient waited with its mouth half-open, patient as a tollhouse, ready to weigh what we carried.

We turned north.
