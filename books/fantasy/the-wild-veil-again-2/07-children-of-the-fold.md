## 7. Children of the Fold

I had told Lena the fold was two days' walk. It was three, because I lied, because I always lied about distances to places that mattered—padding them so that if she turned out to be another hunter's clever lure, she'd have wasted her effort on a road going nowhere. Old habits. A cartographer learns early that a map is just a lie you've agreed to believe together.

She didn't ask why we took the long way through the scree fields below Mount Carnessa. She just watched me with those guild-runner eyes of hers, the ones that counted everything and accused nothing, and after a while she said, "Three days, then."

"Two and a half," I said, wounded.

"You walk like a man who wants to be late." She adjusted the strap of her pack and kept climbing. "I'm not going to sell your children, Ivo. If I wanted the bounty, I'd have taken it at the Tollhouse and slept warm last night."

That was the trouble with her. She kept saying true things in a flat voice, like reading out a ledger, and there was nowhere for me to hide from them except forward.

The fold, when we finally reached it, was a crack in the mountain's shoulder where an old shepherd's bothy had been swallowed by rockfall and then half-digested back out again—a hollow behind a curtain of stone, invisible unless you knew the line of the ridge as it used to be. Which is to say: invisible unless you were me, or unless you were one of the six children hiding inside it who had learned my false maps by heart.

I whistled the three-note signal—the one shaped like the old bend in the Vessa River, before the crown straightened it on paper and made the river itself feel vaguely ashamed—and waited through the long silence that always followed, the silence in which I imagined all the ways this could go wrong.

Then a stone rattled, and a small face appeared in the gap above us, and a voice said, "You're late," with the flat authority of a customs inspector, and something in my chest unclenched for the first time since Greyford.

"Hallo, Wren."

"You're *late*," she repeated, because at nine years old you learn quickly that repetition is the only lever adults respect, "and Marta ate the wrong mushrooms again, and Sef won't stop listening to the walls, and we're down to eating the bark porridge, which tastes like bark."

"That's because it is bark."

"I know what it is. That's not the same as liking it."

* * *

Inside, the fold smelled of wet wool, pine smoke, and the particular sourness of fear that has been living somewhere too long. Six children. I'll give them to you properly, because they deserve to be more than a count:

Wren, nine, who could find water under any ground and had once, memorably, found a hunter's buried cache instead. Marta, eleven, whose hands heated iron without touching it and who therefore wore bandages on principle. Sef, seven, who heard things in stone—I'll come back to Sef, because everyone does eventually. The twins, Odile and Odette, twelve, who could stand very still and become very difficult to look at directly; they'd been smuggled out of a Veilburn village wrapped in a carpet and had developed opinions about enclosed spaces. And Tam, thirteen, the eldest, no gift anyone had identified yet except the gift of having appointed himself responsible for everyone else, which in Thornfell was arguably the most dangerous magic of all.

They were starving. Not dramatically—not the theatrical starvation of ballads—but in the slow, sanded-down way of children who had learned to eat slowly to make it last and to say they weren't hungry when they were. I watched Lena take inventory of them the way I once took inventory of borders, and I saw her do the arithmetic behind her eyes: six mouths, dwindling supplies, winter coming down the passes like a tax collector.

"They can't stay here," she said quietly, when the children had gone to their corner to pretend not to listen. They listened anyway. Gifted children are the worst eavesdroppers in the world; they've had practice.

"No," I agreed.

"The Door—"

"Is real. Probably. I hid children in the margins of maps for four years, Lena. I know exactly how much of my life has rested on 'probably.'"

Sef had come up beside us while we talked—he moved like smoke, that boy—and he was looking at Lena with his head tilted, the way you'd listen to a distant bell. Then he said, "She remembers wrong."

Lena went still. "Excuse me?"

"He does that," I said. "Fair warning. He hears what stones remember. Buildings, roads, cairns—it all soaks into them, apparently, every foot that ever crossed. He's never wrong, which makes him unbearable at games."

"She remembers *wrong*," Sef said again, patient with our slowness. He touched the cave wall, laid his palm flat against it like a physician taking a pulse. "This stone remembers the shepherd's fire, and goats, and a wedding once, right here, with dancing. But she—" he pointed at Lena without looking at her, "—she carries a country inside her, and parts of it are already gone. There are holes. Places she should remember and doesn't anymore, because somebody reached in and took them while she wasn't watching. She keeps walking around the holes without noticing."

The silence in the fold got very wide.

"What kind of places?" Lena asked. Her voice hadn't changed at all. That was the thing about her—she kept her voice level the way other people kept knives.

"Your mother's kitchen," Sef said. "There's a shape where it was. You know the shape of the room but the smell is gone. Someone took the smell."

I had seen Lena face down a rockslide with nothing but stubbornness and contraband rope. I had seen her bargain with a hunter captain using nothing but shared memory and nerve. But I had never seen her look the way she looked now—like a woman opening a drawer she'd trusted her whole life and finding it empty, and worse, finding she couldn't prove anything had ever been in it.

"My mother's kitchen smelled of—" She stopped. Tried again. "It smelled of—"

Nothing came. I watched her reach and reach, and the hole was right where Sef said it was, smooth-sided, surgical. Somewhere in Greyford or beyond, some clerk of the Veilhunt had erased a kitchen, and the woman who'd grown up in it had walked around the absence for weeks without knowing there should have been bread in it.

"They're doing it to everyone," Sef said, almost gently, the way children deliver horrors when no adult has yet taught them to package them. "The stones near the lowland towns don't remember their own names anymore. There's a bridge that used to be called the Kettlebridge and now the stone only knows it as Bridge Four. The stones get confused. Stones hate being confused."

"And the Door?" I asked, because someone had to.

Sef looked at me for a long moment. Then he pressed both hands to the cave wall and closed his eyes, and the whole fold seemed to lean in—the children, the fire, even the draft at the entrance—to hear what the stone would say through him.

"The Door is older than the confusion," he said, and his voice had gone strange, layered, like several speakers at slightly different speeds. "The Door was cut by people who knew that countries die twice. Once when the land changes. Once when the memory does. So they built it to answer the second death. It opens for whoever carries the country whole. Every pass. Every name. Every kitchen smell. It weighs you, and if you're missing pieces, it stays shut, because a half-remembered country is how they win."

He opened his eyes. They were wet, and he wiped them furiously with his sleeve, furious at himself for it.

"It's getting heavier," he added. "Every day. As they take more. Whoever goes to it has to be carrying more than anyone alive is carrying. Has to remember *more* than anyone alive remembers."

Nobody spoke. Outside, wind worried the scree field, making its slow grinding sound, mountains eating themselves at their own pace, indifferent to our schedules.

* * *

That night, after the children slept in their pile of wool like puppies, Lena sat across the fire from me sharpening a knife she had no practical use for, which I understood to mean she needed her hands busy while her mind did something terrible.

"They can't all fit through," she said finally. "If it weighs remembrance—if it demands a whole country—then it isn't a door you walk through. It's a toll gate, and the coin is memory, and none of us is rich enough."

"Maybe it takes less from some than others."

"Maybe." She tested the edge against her thumb, didn't draw blood, looked faintly disappointed. "Ivo. When you drew your false borders. When you moved villages onto paper so hunters would search the wrong valleys. Did it cost you? The real places, I mean. Did drawing over them make them fade?"

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to her had stopped working sometime around the second day.

"A little," I admitted. "For years afterward I'd try to picture the Vessa bend—the real one, the wild one—and I'd get my own forgery instead. The lie drawn over the truth until the truth stopped developing. Cartographers call it ghosting. The old line bleeding through the new one, and then, one day, not bleeding through at all." I fed a stick to the fire. "So yes. I erased places to save people, and the places noticed. Why do you ask?"

"Because I've spent my whole life erasing myself," she said. "Hiding the gift, keeping quiet, being nobody so hard that nobody became a habit. And now there's a girl-shaped hole where a country should be, and a Door that only opens for people who kept everything." She looked up at me across the flames, and her expression was the flattest I'd ever seen it, which from Lena meant it was holding something enormous down by main force. "These children aren't going to remember Thornfell either, Ivo. Not whole. They're children. They remember hunger and hiding and the taste of bark porridge. Somebody has to carry the rest for them. Somebody has to be the archive, or the Door stays shut, or worse—it opens halfway and lets them through into a world where they can't even grieve properly, because grief needs the thing you lost."

"You're talking like you've already decided something."

"I'm talking like a woman doing sums," she said. "Don't interrupt the sums."

I didn't. I sat there watching the fire eat the stick down to a glowing wire, and I thought about how I had spent four years forging maps to move children along lines that didn't exist, and how it had never once occurred to me that the final line might run through a person—that the last honest map of Thornfell might not be ink on vellum at all, but a smuggler named Lena Whitlock walking toward a glacier with a country folded up inside her chest.

Tam stirred in the pile of sleeping children and murmured names in his sleep—Marta, Wren, Sef, roll-call even unconscious, the eldest keeping his ledger—and Lena watched him with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it for years in front of royal ministers while lying beautifully: tenderness disguised as assessment.

"They're yours now too," I said. "I should warn you. That's how it happens. You think you're escorting them and then one morning you realize you'd burn down a province for them and you never decided anything, it just happened, the way weather happens."

"I know how it happens," she said. "I trained runners for Aldous. Same disease." She paused, and something moved under the flatness. "Aldous remembered enough. That's the joke, isn't it? He remembered every pass, every runner, every name on the rolls. He carried the whole guild in his head, and he sold it anyway. Remembrance isn't virtue. It's just capacity. What matters is what you spend it on."

"Sef says the Door will weigh you."

"Then it can weigh me," Lena said. "I've been carrying contraband my whole life. Might as well carry the country."

She said it lightly. She meant it completely. Those were always the two layers of her, and I was starting to understand that the second layer ran all the way down.

In the corner, unnoticed—or pretending to be—Sef lay with his palm flat against the floor stone, listening. And I saw his face change, saw him sit bolt upright with his eyes huge in the firelight, and when he spoke, his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's voice does, and like a warning does:

"The Door felt her just now. From here. Through the deep stone. It felt her say that." He swallowed. "It started opening."
