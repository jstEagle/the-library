## 8. The Hollow Door

The glacier had a voice. I'd been hearing it for two days before we reached it—a long, grinding groan under the wind, like an old man turning over in bed, complaining about the weight of the centuries. Ivo said he'd mapped this ice once, in his first year with the crown, back when the maps were still honest.

"There," he said, and pointed with his chin because his hands were full of children.

The girl—Wren, the one who heard stone—had gone rigid half a mile back. Now she pulled free of my hand and walked toward the ice wall as if she'd been summoned, which, I suppose, in every way that mattered, she had. The other five children trailed her like ducklings who'd learned that ducklings who strayed got eaten.

The glacier's face wasn't blue or white but a strange bruised grey, shot through with veins of something darker. And set into it, low, where the ice met the scree—

A door.

Not carved. Not built. It looked less like something made than like something *acknowledged*, as if the stone had always contained the shape of a doorway and someone, long ago, had simply agreed to see it. It was half-sealed—the lower third choked with a growth of pale stone like candle wax, like the door was being slowly swallowed from its own threshold up.

"It's dying," Wren said. Her voice came out flat and certain, the way children say terrible things. "It's forgetting."

I crouched beside her. "Doors don't forget."

"This one does." She pressed her small palm flat against the sealed part. "It's hungry. It wants to be told what it's for."

Ivo shifted the sleeping boy on his hip. "The old stories say it opens for those who remember Thornfell as it truly was." He laughed, short and bitter, breath steaming. "Which would have been simple a year ago. Now?" He nodded back down the valley we'd climbed, toward the burned villages and scraped signposts and elders who'd stared at us with pleasant blank faces when we asked about their own grandparents' names. "I'm not sure anyone remembers it truly anymore. Including me. I spent twenty years redrawing it wrong."

"Then we're all standing in front of a door none of us can open," I said, "with hunters behind us and a glacier above us. Wonderful. I've missed this feeling. It's very familiar. It's called Tuesday."

But even as I joked, I put my own hand against the stone—and felt it.

Not cold. That was the first shock. The door was warm, faintly, like a hearthstone hours after the fire dies. And under the warmth, something listening. Something vast and patient and starving.

It didn't speak in words. It spoke in *absence*. I thought of my mother's kitchen and the image came to me strangely, with holes in it—I could see the table, the chipped blue crockery, but her face at the window was fog, and I couldn't recall whether the curtains were yellow or whether I'd only ever decided they must have been yellow because yellow is what kitchens are supposed to be. The memory had been sanded. Gently. The way you sand a thing you mean to keep, not destroy.

"Oh," I said quietly. "Oh, it's been eating already. From a distance."

"What?" Ivo's head snapped around.

"The Veilhunt. The burning, the name-striking—it feeds this. Or feeds *on* this. Every village they unmake, every name they strike—" I looked down at Wren, whose eyes were too old for her face. "Every memory that dies out there, the crown takes here, and seals the Door a little more. It's not a hunt. It's a harvest."

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the ice, no fire, huddled together like spoons. The children slept in a pile, the way starved animals do, conserving everything. Wren slept closest to the Door, one hand still touching it, and I noticed that where her palm rested, the pale sealing-stone had softened, just slightly, like butter near a warm knife.

I sat awake and took inventory, the way I always did before a run. Contraband: six children, one disgraced mapmaker, myself. Route: one, through a door that might open if I could remember my country hard enough. Pursuit: certain.

I'd left markers all down the mountain—small things, a twisted branch here, a cairn toppled there—that would tell any guild-trained tracker exactly how fast we moved and how tired we were. Which meant I'd also told them exactly how long they had. Half a day. Less, if their captain was good.

Their captain *was* good. I'd trained her myself.

"Lena." Ivo's voice, low. "If it doesn't open—"

"Then we fight on a glacier with six children and no weapons worth the name, and we lose slowly instead of quickly. I've done the arithmetic. I'm trying not to show my work."

He was quiet a moment. "There's another option. You could go through alone. If it opens for you, it opens for *you*. Leave the children with me, draw them off—"

"No."

"You haven't let me finish—"

"I don't need you to finish. The answer's no." I watched the ice groan overhead. "You know what I did for twenty years, Ivo? I made myself small. I buried the gift so deep I half-believed the grave. I told myself invisibility was survival, and then Greyford burned, and I learned that invisible people burn just fine—they just aren't counted after." I turned to look at him. "I am done being small. Whatever happens at that door, nobody gets left behind to make my escape cheaper. Not again. Never again."

He studied me for a long moment. "You sound like someone rehearsing a eulogy."

"I sound like a smuggler pricing a route. There's always a toll. I just intend to know what it is before I pay it."

* * *

They came at dusk.

I heard them before I saw them—boots on scree, the particular disciplined rhythm of crown hunters, not the scattered clatter of conscripts. Six of them descending the glacier's tongue in formation, torches hooded, and at their center, walking easily, unhurried, a figure I knew by his silhouette before the light touched him.

Aldous.

My guildmaster. My betrayer. The man whose rolls had bought his pardon with our names.

He stopped twenty paces out and spread empty hands, and behind him the hunters fanned wide, cutting the slope. I put the children behind me and felt Ivo come up on my left, and I thought, absurdly, of all the times Aldous had stood in the guildhall doorway just like this, arms open, about to talk some desperate runner down from a bad decision. He'd been good at it. It was how he'd kept us alive so long.

It was, apparently, also how he'd sold us.

"Lena Whitlock." My name in his mouth sounded like a coin being bitten. "Girl, look at yourself. On a glacier, at the edge of the map, with a herd of hunted children and a man the crown has wanted for a decade. Come down. Register. They'll pardon you too—I've argued your case personally. Your gift, used in service, licensed. A life, Lena. A whole life."

"A whole life," I repeated. "Whose?"

"Yours. Isn't that what matters? Survival first. Everything else is philosophy, and philosophy doesn't keep you warm." He took a step closer, and his face did the thing it had always done—folded into such honest sorrow that you believed him against your better judgment. I'd watched that face save runners from the gallows. I'd watched it lie to widows. "I know what I did looks like betrayal. But the rolls were already lost, girl. The crown had them or would have them within the month. All I did was choose who got to live through it. Someone has to make that choice. It might as well be someone who loves the people on the list."

"And you loved yourself best on the list."

"Yes." No flinch. That was the worst of it—he didn't flinch. "Yes. And I'd do it again. Because I'm still here, Lena, and so are you, and I intend for both of us to stay here. That door doesn't open. The stories are stories. What's real is the captain behind me, and the rope, and the pardon with your name on it if you walk down this mountain alive." He extended his hand. "Come home."

And there it was. Twenty years of guild oaths, of him teaching me the high roads, of soup in the guildhall kitchen when I'd failed my first run and cried where no one could see. The man had been a father to me in every way except the one that cost him anything.

Behind me, Wren whispered, "It's listening. It's listening to *you*."

And I understood, finally, with the clarity that comes only at the worst moment of your life, what the Door wanted, and why Aldous couldn't feel it, and why the crown needed a living gifted person to seal it forever.

It didn't want courage. It didn't want blood.

It wanted remembrance. True remembrance. The country as it actually was—not the sanded, fogged versions, not the yellow-curtain guesses, but the whole, heavy, specific truth of a place, held by someone who'd carried it honestly. And giving that away meant giving away the having of it. You couldn't pour out your memory of home and keep the cup.

That was the toll. I'd asked to price the route, and now I had the number.

I thought of my mother's kitchen. Of the chipped blue crockery. Of the smell of bread and tallow and the rain coming up the valley, and I held it—all of it, the whole room, every corner—in my mind like contraband cradled against my chest, the most valuable cargo I had ever run.

Then I walked past Aldous without taking his hand, knelt at the threshold of the Hollow Door, and laid my palm against the stone.

It opened beneath my touch like a throat.

Warmth poured up my arm, and I gave it the kitchen—gave it whole, the crockery and the curtain-yellow I suddenly *knew*, knew beyond doubting, the exact grey of the rain, my mother humming off-key, the burn scar on the table's leg from the pan of '09—and I felt the memory leave me the way water leaves a cup, and the pale sealing-stone shuddered and cracked, and the crack filled with light.

Behind me, Aldous made a sound I'd never heard him make. Behind him, a hunter shouted. Above the glacier, the wind stopped, as if the whole mountain had leaned in to listen.

I reached for the next memory.
