## 9. Everything I Kept

*Lena*

The Door was not what the songs promised.

No arch of silver, no singing stone. It was a seam in the glacier's heart, a crack in the world the width of a coffin lid, and it was closing. I could feel it the way you feel a door swinging shut on a warm room—pressure against my palms, cold and patient and indifferent.

"Remember," Ivo had said on the road. "That's all it wants."

Fine. Remembering was the one thing I'd never let myself do properly.

Behind us, boots on ice. Torchlight crawling down the cavern walls like something spilled. And Aldous's voice, carrying the way it always had in the guildhall, warm as a hearth you didn't notice was burning your house down.

"Lena. Girl. Come away from that thing. It's killed people before—the Door, the old ones who tried it. Don't make me watch another one die for a fairy story."

I put my hands flat against the stone and thought of my mother's kitchen.

Not the fact of it—the *thing* of it. The way the morning light came through the east window and turned her flour-dusted forearms gold. The chipped blue bowl she stirred batter in, the one with the hairline crack she refused to replace because "a bowl that's survived this long has earned its keep." The smell of cardamom bread on a winter morning so thick you could lean against it. Her humming off-key. Her hands, warm when they pressed the top of my head, always floury, always leaving little white prints in my hair like blessings.

The stone drank it.

Not greedily—*thirstily*, the way a man dying in the desert drinks, and I felt the seam widen under my fingers by the breadth of a knife blade. Light came through. Not torchlight. Something older, green-gold, smelling absurdly of rain on summer roads.

"It's opening," the girl said behind me—Wren, the stone-hearer, her voice cracked with wonder. "It's listening to her."

"Then listen harder," Ivo snapped, already moving, gathering children like a shepherd counts sheep—with a hand on each head, a word in each ear, no sentiment because sentiment takes time. "Through the light. Now. You first, Tam. Go."

Boots closer now. Shouting. A crossbow bolt skipped off the ice near my knee, and I flinched, and the memory nearly slipped—

So I grabbed another. Every pass.

The Widow's Stair at dawn, frost furring the guide-ropes. The high road above Greyford where you learned to read weather in the color of the clouds over the western peaks. The switchbacks below Ravenmere where the mules hated life and so did you, and old Doss used to sing filthy songs to keep everyone's spirits up, songs I would never repeat in company but remembered note for note. The smell of pine resin and cold stone and mule sweat. My own boots, worn through at the left heel every single season, because I always leaned left going downhill, always had, probably always would.

The Door drank the passes and groaned wider. Wide enough for a child to walk through sideways, then square.

"Lena." Aldous again, closer, his voice losing its polish, fraying at the edges. "Lena, stop. Whatever you're doing to it, stop. They'll kill you. They'll—"

"They're going to kill us either way," I called back, not turning around. "You taught me that, Aldous. Lesson one: never run from a hunter. Outlast him."

"That's not—" His voice broke off into something smaller. "That's not how it was supposed to go."

*Ivo*

I had driven children through a false border line once, ink and paper and nerve, and I had thought nothing would ever be harder than that. I was wrong. This was harder, because this time the way out was real and narrow and lit by a woman emptying herself into stone, and every second I spent counting heads was a second the hunters spent closing.

"Go, go—hold Wren's hand, don't let go—Tam, take the little ones, count them, seven, there are seven—"

They went. One by one into that green-gold light, blinking, terrified, some crying, one—the smallest, Pip, who'd lost his words somewhere between his village and the fold—turning back to look at Lena with an expression I knew too well. The look of a child watching an adult become a story.

"No time for goodbyes," I told him, and pushed him gently through.

Then the hunters were in the cavern proper, six of them, torches high, crossbows leveled, and their captain shouting about sealing orders and crown writs, and none of it mattered, because none of them understood what they were looking at. You can't arrest a doorway. You can't burn a memory out of stone when the woman feeding it is standing right there, laughing—actually laughing, low and fierce, the laugh of someone who has finally stopped being afraid of the wrong thing.

And Aldous.

Aldous stood between the hunters and her, and he wasn't moving. He'd been walking toward her and then he simply stopped, mid-stride, like a clock running down, and I watched his face do something complicated and terrible. I've spent my life reading faces—I had to, to know which mapmakers were lying—and I read his plainly enough. It was guilt arriving forty years late with all its luggage.

"Lena," he said, and his voice was nothing now, just a man's voice, old and scared. "What are you giving it?"

"Everything I kept," she said.

*Lena*

Here is the thing nobody tells you about giving away your memories: you don't lose them. Not exactly. You feel them leave, feel them pass through your hands into the stone, but they don't go dark—they go *shared*. Somewhere beyond that light, a country is being rebuilt out of my mother's kitchen, one chipped blue bowl at a time.

I gave it the passes until my legs shook.

I gave it Greyford before the criers—the market days, the argument between the fishmongers that lasted eleven years, the way the whole town smelled of river and coal smoke and someone's laundry soap.

And then, because the Door seemed to lean toward it, hungry for the hard things, I gave it Aldous.

Not this Aldous. The one who found me at fourteen, half-starved, caught using a flare to light my way down a black road, and instead of selling me or sermonizing me, sat me down in the guildhall kitchen and said, "Well. You're one of mine now. Rule one: never run from a hunter." The Aldous who taught me rope-work and route-craft and how to sleep sitting up against a pack. Who laughed like gravel in a barrel. Who once carried a sick runner nine miles down the Widow's Stair on his back and complained the entire way about the runner's weight, which was how we all knew he was frightened for him.

The stone took that Aldous and I felt him settle into it like a coin into a well, and something in my chest tore along a seam I hadn't known was there.

"I gave you everything," Aldous whispered. He'd crossed the ice without seeming to decide to. The hunters' captain was shouting his name—*Veyran! Veyran, the writ says—*and Aldous ignored him the way you ignore rain. "I sold the rolls to keep the guild alive. To keep *you* alive. I did the arithmetic, Lena. Somebody had to do the arithmetic."

"You did the arithmetic," I agreed. My voice sounded strange, thin, stretched across the distance between the girl I was and whatever I was becoming. "You counted the guild and got a number you could live with. I'm counting differently."

The hunters moved. Of course they moved. Captains don't stand in caverns having revelations; they have schedules.

The first bolt flew true—for Ivo, who shoved a boy aside and took the graze along his arm without a sound, which was brave and stupid and exactly like him. The second aimed for me.

Aldous caught the hunter's wrist.

I heard the bones of the moment more than saw it—a shout, a scuffle, Aldous roaring something I couldn't parse, the captain snarling *"Traitor"* with real surprise, as if loyalty were supposed to be permanent even after you'd fed it to the crown. Crossbows swung toward Aldous, and I had a heartbeat—one clean, clear heartbeat—to understand that he was buying me seconds with the only currency he had left.

"Aldous!" I screamed, and poured the memory of his laughter into the stone, gravel in a barrel, and the Door *surged* wide.

*Ivo*

Wide enough. It was wide enough.

"GO!" Lena's voice, cracking down the middle. "Ivo, take them through, NOW, don't you dare look back—"

I never obey anyone's instructions, least of all instructions containing the phrase *don't look back*, so naturally I looked. She stood at the seam with her hands flat on the stone and her eyes closed and tears freezing on her cheeks, and she was smiling, which frightened me more than the crossbows. Beyond her, Aldous had two hunters down and a third's arm twisted behind his back, screaming at the captain—not begging anymore, *accusing*, a lifetime of ledgers read aloud at last.

"Seven children," I told her. "All accounted for. I'm coming back for you."

"No," she said, gentle as my mother had been, gentle as nobody gets to be with me. "You're keeping them alive. That's the job now. That was always the job."

The light pulled. I felt it at my back like a tide, and the children beyond it were crying and calling names—her name, mostly—and the hunters were regrouping, and there was no version of this where I won an argument with a woman who was busy becoming a country.

"Wren," I said hoarsely. "Tell her—"

"She hears you," the girl said. Her stone-grey eyes were streaming. "She hears everything. That's the whole point of her."

So I said it anyway, loud, over the clash and the cold: "I will plant flowers over this mountain if it's the last thing I do, Lena Whitlock. Wild ones. The veil-flowers. You'll be able to smell them from in there."

Her smile widened. "Promise?"

"On my maps."

And then the tide took me, and the last thing I saw of Thornfell was a woman holding a door open with everything she'd ever loved.

*Lena*

The silence after was enormous.

The hunters stood in the cavern, breathing hard, staring at a seam of green-gold light that was now, slowly, gently, beginning to narrow again—and I understood, with the calm of someone standing outside herself, why the crown needed a living gifted person to seal it forever. The Door wanted a keeper. It had always wanted a keeper. Someone on the threshold, remembering, so that what lay beyond stayed real.

Aldous knelt on the ice, bleeding from the mouth, three hunters wary around him, and looked up at me with the face of the man from the kitchen, the man I had just given away.

"Rule one," he rasped.

"Never run from a hunter," I finished. "I'm not running, old man. I'm home."

The captain raised her crossbow at me. Behind her, the Door hummed against my palms, waiting, patient, thirsty—and I reached for the next memory, and the next, and the next.

There were so many still. There were enough.

I began with the wild veil-flowers on the high slopes, blue as dusk, and the sound of Ivo promising, and the taste of the future arriving.
