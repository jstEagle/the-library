## 10. The Wild Veil

Ivo

The border was not what any of us expected, because there was no border at all—only a change in the light, the way the air went from Thornfell's thin iron cold to something softer, greener, like stepping from a cellar into a kitchen where bread has just come out.

The children came through in ones and twos, and every one of them stopped on the far side and turned around, the way you do when you leave a house for the last time. Except they had nothing to turn toward. The Hollow Door stood open behind them—a seam of pale gold in the glacier's blue—and beyond it, nothing they could name. Not fog exactly. Not dark. Just an absence shaped like a country.

"That's it?" said Bren, the oldest, who had crossed a mountain range on stolen boots and never once complained. He stared into the Door's glow with his jaw set the way soldiers set theirs before bad news. "That's home?"

None of them could picture it. That was the whole cruelty of the thing—the Veilhunt hadn't only burned villages and struck names from signs; it had sanded the memory out of people too, gently, thoroughly, until a child born in Thornfell could stand at its threshold and see only white. I'd spent ten years drawing false borders to smuggle those children past hunters' eyes, and I'd told myself I understood erasure. I understood cartography. I did not understand this.

"Home is coming," I said, which was a lie so bald that even the littlest girl, Sefa, gave me the flat look children give liars they've decided to forgive anyway.

Lena didn't come through.

I should have known she wouldn't. She'd been feeding the Door since the moment she touched it—memory after memory, her mother's kitchen, the high passes in thaw, Aldous young and laughing with flour on his hands—and each gift had widened the passage, but each one had also taken something out of her face. By the time the last child stumbled through, Lena Whitlock looked like a map someone had erased half of: still herself, still sharp-eyed, still wry, but with great blank spaces where the country of her used to be.

She stood on the threshold with one hand flat against the stone.

"Ivo." Her voice was steady. Of course it was steady. She'd run contraband through collapsing passes with steadier hands than mine ever managed. "Someone has to keep it. Remember it all, or it closes, and everything we fed it goes to rot."

"You could remember out here," I said. "We all could. Together."

"Together we'd forget by spring." She smiled, and it was the smile she'd given me the first day, on the road past Greyford, when I asked if she was afraid. "The crown spent years unmaking a country with an army and a bureaucracy. It takes one woman and a door to put it back. Seems like a fair trade, honestly. I've always been good at carrying things other people couldn't."

I argued. I want it noted somewhere, even if no one reads it, that I argued—for an hour, maybe two, while the children watched from the rocks with their hollow faces, and the glacier groaned like something turning over in sleep. I offered to stay instead. She pointed out, with brutal fairness, that my memory was already compromised—that a man who drew false maps for a living had trained himself to hold places loosely, and the Door needed someone who held tight.

Then she said the thing that ended the argument, the way she always ended arguments: quietly.

"They're yours now, Ivo. Get them somewhere green."

I took the children down off the glacier. I will not pretend I walked away clean; I looked back eleven times, and the twelfth time the Door was already narrowing, a gold thread stitching itself closed around the shape of her, and the last thing I saw was Lena raising her hand—not goodbye, more like a salute between colleagues, one professional acknowledging another job well done.

Then stone. Then snow.

* * *

We wintered in the low valleys beyond the pass, in a country whose name I learned to say correctly and never quite believed. The children grieved the way children do—sideways, in fragments. Bren hoarded salt. Sefa cried at night for a grandmother she could no longer picture, sobbing not from loss but from the horror of losing the loss itself, the grief with no face attached. I caught them trying to reconstruct Thornfell from scraps: the shape of a loaf Lena had described, a song half-remembered, the way mountain water tastes of iron. They built it wrong, of course. Every version was wrong. And every wrong version broke them a little more.

I did what I could. I drew maps from what I remembered of the true ones—but my true ones were already half fiction, borders I'd invented to save lives, and the lines came out of my pen trembling like something ashamed.

In spring I went back up to the glacier alone.

The Door was sealed. Stone, seamless, indifferent. But the ground below it, where the meltwater ran, was bare earth scoured clean by centuries of ice—and it occurred to me, standing there like a fool talking to rock, that a sealed door is still a door, and a keeper is still a keeper, and some things are planted over graves not because the dead need flowers but because the living need to know where to kneel.

I had carried seeds since Greyford. Wild veil-flowers—they grew along every high road in Thornfell, silver-petaled, stubborn, blooming in scree where nothing else bothered. Smugglers used to mark safe routes with them. Guild runners called them traitor-blossoms because they thrived on neglect. I'd gathered a handful in a fold of oilcloth months ago without knowing why, the way you carry a coin from a country that stops existing.

I planted them below the Door, thirty of them, in a rough line like a border nobody would ever survey.

They bloomed by midsummer. Silver, everywhere, absurd against the ice—so bright under the midnight sun that from the valley below you could see the slope shining, a small wild flag over a sealed stone door. The children came up with me that autumn and saw them, and Sefa knelt among the petals and said, wonderingly, "It smells like—" and stopped, because she didn't have the word, because the word had been taken, but her face said the scent had reached something the crown's sanding hadn't gotten to yet.

Not memory. Deeper than memory. The body remembers what the mind loses.

Bren stood beside me looking at the flowers, and finally he said, "Will she really remember all of it? Everything?"

"Yes," I said.

"Alone?"

"For now."

He nodded, jaw set, soldier's jaw, and I understood that I'd just made him a promise I hadn't said aloud. Someday. Someday someone would find a way back through—or the world outside would grow strong enough to go get her, or the Door would open on its own terms, for its own reasons. Countries don't die while one person keeps them. I'm a cartographer. I know how long a line can survive on paper, waiting to be walked again.

On the walk down, Sefa took my hand and asked me to tell her about the high roads, about the passes, about the woman who kept a door. So I did. Badly, at first, missing details, inventing where I had to—and then better, because telling it made me hold it tighter, and holding it made it truer.

And behind us, above us, small and silver and stubborn as anything Thornfell ever grew, the veil-flowers bloomed over the stone, marking the route home for anyone brave enough to come find it.

Lena

Time works strangely inside a country that exists only in one head.

I don't hunger. I don't sleep, though I dream, or something adjacent to dreaming—I walk the passes at night, all of them, every switchback and tollhouse and smuggler's stair, and they hold their shape because I walk them. The kitchen first. Always the kitchen first. My mother's hands in flour, the window over the basin where frost ferned in winter, the smell of yeast and woodsmoke. If I lose the kitchen, I lose the keystone. So I keep the kitchen.

The Door takes what I give and gives back what it is: a passage, patient as stone. I have felt it listening. I have felt, once or twice, a tremor from the far side—footsteps? seedlings? a child pressing her palm to the rock the way I once pressed mine?—and I have chosen to believe. Belief is a kind of cartography too. You draw the shore before the ship returns.

Aldous comes sometimes, in the remembering. Not the man who sold us—the other one, the young one, laughing with flour on his hands, teaching me to read the high roads like sheet music. I keep both versions. That's the job. A keeper doesn't get to curate. She keeps the betrayal and the kindness, the burnings and the blossoms, the neighbor who denounced a family overnight and the same neighbor, years earlier, leaving bread on our step when my mother was sick. All of it. Especially the parts that hurt. Especially those.

Sometimes I think I hear Ivo arguing with me still, on the other side of the stone, his pen scratching, insisting that borders are fictions and therefore anything can be redrawn. He's probably right. He usually was, annoyingly, right at the worst possible moments.

Today—it is a today; I've decided it's a today—the Door shivered.

Not the deep settling groan it makes when glaciers move above it. Something else. Something like a key turning very slowly in a very old lock, or like a word almost remembered, sitting on the tongue.

And through the stone, faint as scent, faint as a rumor of silver petals drifting down a scree slope, came a smell I knew.

Wild veil-flowers. Blooming. Over me. For me.

Someone had marked the route.

Well then, I thought, and I straightened my shoulders the way I used to before a hard pass, and I put my hand flat against the stone that is my country's last wall and its only gate.

Hold on, I told them, whoever they were, however many winters it took them. Hold on. I remember everything.

And somewhere far above, I am nearly certain, a flower opened.
