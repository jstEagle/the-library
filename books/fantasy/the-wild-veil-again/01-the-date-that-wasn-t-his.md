## 1. The Date That Wasn't His

The oath-house library smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular staleness of books nobody had wanted to read in a hundred years. Dominic Reyes stood in the doorway with his boots still muddy from the morning patrol and decided, before Wren Halloway even turned around, that he was going to hate whatever came next.

"You're late," she said, without looking up.

"I'm not. You said noon. It's noon." He crossed the room anyway, because she'd sent a boy with a note that said *come at once* in handwriting sharp enough to draw blood, and because in eleven years of knowing Wren Halloway he had learned that *at once* from her meant something. Usually something inconvenient.

She sat at the reading table beneath the one window, surrounded by an archipelago of open volumes. Her exile had softened nothing about her — the ink stains on her fingers, the hair escaping its braid, the way she held a book like it might lie to her if she wasn't watching. The valley had taken her in four years ago when the capital threw her out, and the valley had never quite known what to do with her. Neither had Dominic, honestly. But she catalogued the oath-house records better than anyone since his grandmother, and she made good tea, and those two things had carried a strange friendship further than either of them would admit.

"Look at this," she said, and pushed a book across the table toward him.

It was old. That was the first wrong thing. The binding was cracked leather gone soft as cloth, the kind of age you couldn't fake cheaply, and the pages were that deep cream color of paper made before the valley sealed itself away from the world. The title on the spine had been worn to ghosts: only *...VEILS...* survived.

"I don't read prophecy," Dominic said. "Prophecy reads people. It's a trap with nice margins."

"It names you."

He pulled out the chair opposite her and sat down slowly, the way you sit when someone says something you've decided not to react to yet. "Wren."

"Dominic." She matched his tone exactly, which was infuriating. "Page two hundred and six. Third stanza. I've checked the script against three other documents from the same period. The hand is consistent. The ink oxidation is consistent. This is not a forgery, and I know forgeries — I was exiled partly for producing them."

"That's supposed to reassure me?"

"It's supposed to tell you I'm competent." She turned the book around to face him and set her finger under a line of cramped brown script.

He read it. Then he read it again, because the first time his eyes had done something untrustworthy.

*When the veil thins and the crowned thing hungers whole,* the stanza ran, *the last son of the oath shall stand at the boundary on the eve of the false feast, and the silence shall speak through him or be silent forever.*

Below it, in a different, later hand — a marginal note, faded almost to nothing:

*D.R. — the night of the harvest moon, the year the king's reign turns thirty.*

Dominic looked at the window. The harvest moon was tonight. His father's father's father had signed the oath that bound their family to the Silent God; every male heir since had renewed it at nineteen, and Dominic had renewed his own eleven autumns ago in this very building, kneeling on cold stone while the priest pressed a thumb of ash to his forehead. D.R. His initials. His name.

"Coincidence," he said.

"There are no coincidences in marginalia," Wren said. "Someone wrote this specifically. A century ago. About you."

"About *a* D.R. There were Reyeses before me. My grandfather was Domingo. My father—"

"—was Rafael. R.R. And your great-grandfather was Emilio. E.R." She'd clearly done the genealogy already. Of course she had. "Domingo Reyes was born eighty years after this book was written. Whoever annotated this page could not have known him. Which leaves you."

"The year, then. Reigns turn thirty all the time."

"Not this reign." Wren's voice dropped, and that was the second wrong thing, because Wren did not drop her voice unless she thought the walls were listening. She glanced at the door — closed, he'd shut it — and leaned in. "The current king has ruled for twenty-nine years. Next spring is his thirtieth. Unless the annotator counted from his coronation as crown prince, which some chroniclers do, in which case—"

"It's now," Dominic said quietly.

"It's now."

He sat back. Outside the window, the valley went on being the valley: terraced fields going gold, the mill wheel turning its slow brown circle, children running along the fence lines where the mist wall shimmered faintly at the far end of the vale, the boundary that had kept magic out for a hundred years and kept the valley's people safe inside it. Safe. That was the word the oath-house used. That was the word his whole life had been built on. Magic cannot follow you here, his father had told him. Here, nothing whispers. Here, you sleep.

He had slept well his entire life. He resented, suddenly and irrationally, the idea that he might stop.

"It's a dead god," he said. "The Silent God doesn't speak. That's the whole point. That's the covenant. We keep the vigil, we stay in the valley, we live plain lives, and the god stays quiet, and nothing from outside gets in. If some prophet a hundred years ago wrote that the silence would *speak through me*, then either they were wrong, or they were writing poetry, or—" he gestured at the book — "someone with too much time and a grudge wanted to rattle an oath-house."

"Or," Wren said, "the prophecy is right, and everything you've been told about why the valley exists is incomplete."

That landed harder than he wanted it to. He stood up, needing his legs under him. "I have patrol reports to file."

"Dominic."

"And you have a catalogue to finish. Thank you for the tea you didn't make." He got as far as the door.

"The boundary moved yesterday," Wren said behind him.

He stopped with his hand on the latch.

"Half a mile inward," she said. "On the north ridge. I walked it myself. The mist markers are new stone — they weren't there last month, and stone doesn't grow. Whatever holds the valley sealed is contracting, Dominic. Slowly. Like something tightening its grip. Or like something pulling."

"The wardens recalibrate the markers every season. It's routine."

"It's not routine for the boundary to move toward the town instead of away from it."

He turned around. She hadn't moved from the table. Lamplight caught the edge of the old book between her hands, and for a moment she looked less like a scholar than like a woman holding a lit fuse and deciding whether to run with it or stand very still.

"Show me," he said.

They climbed the north ridge in the late afternoon, when the light went long and amber and the shadows of the pines lay across the path like fallen spears. Wren talked the whole way — she always talked when she was nervous, a habit Dominic had catalogued years ago and never mentioned — about ink oxidation, about the provenance of the book, about how it had surfaced in a trader's lot from the lowlands three weeks ago and how she'd bought it with money she didn't have.

"How did a prophecy book get out of the capital into a trader's lot?" Dominic asked.

"That," Wren said, "is an excellent question with an answer I don't like. Books don't escape archives by accident. Someone sold it, or lost it, or wanted it found." She paused at the top of the ridge, breathing hard. "Or wanted it *delivered*."

"To whom?"

"To whoever it names." She looked at him. "You."

The boundary lay ahead, maybe two hundred paces down the slope: a wall of pale mist, faintly luminous even in daylight, stretching from the tree line off into the haze in both directions. Dominic had seen it ten thousand times. Every child in the valley had dared each other to touch it and every child had learned the lesson — you press your palm flat against the cool nothing, and it gives like fog, and nothing happens, because nothing ever happens here.

Except.

"Stop," Wren said sharply.

He stopped. She was staring past him, down the slope, and her face had gone the color of old paper.

The mist was moving.

Not drifting — mist drifted, mist breathed with the wind, and there was no wind. This was movement with intent, a slow rolling contraction, the way a fist closes finger by finger. As they watched, the wall of white drew visibly nearer, swallowing a fence post, then a boulder, then the crooked pine that every map of the valley marked as the northernmost point of anything.

"Dominic," Wren whispered. "It's pulling us."

"That's not possible. The boundary keeps things *out*. It doesn't reach in, it doesn't take, it doesn't—" His voice failed on the last clause, because the mist had reached the base of the slope now, and the air had changed. He felt it before he understood it: a pressure behind his eyes, a ringing in his ears like the memory of a bell, a sensation he had no vocabulary for because he had never once felt it in his life.

Magic. The absence of magic, ending.

The mist touched his boots.

There was no pain. That was the terrible part — there should have been pain, there should have been resistance, there should have been *anything* but the gentle, insistent, tidal drag that took hold of him like a current taking a swimmer. He grabbed for Wren's wrist and her fingers closed around his forearm, iron-tight, and then the world folded.

For one heartbeat he saw the valley from above — the gold fields, the mill wheel frozen mid-turn, the oath-house small as a toy — and then it was gone, and the light was wrong, and the air was thick with a hum he felt in his teeth, and they were falling.

They hit stone. Hard. Knees, palms, the breath knocked out of him in a single grunt. Cold air. Smoke somewhere, woodsmoke and tallow and horse. Bells — dozens of bells, ringing from towers he couldn't see, ringing in patterns no valley bell had ever rung.

Wren was coughing beside him, alive, gripping his arm still. Above them rose walls of pale granite and banners the size of sails, and streets climbing away into shadow, and crowds — thousands of people, more people than lived in the entire valley, streaming through torch-lit avenues below.

A boy ran past shouting the news of the day, hoarse with excitement:

"Coronation eve! The feast begins tomorrow — make way for the pilgrims, make way!"

Wren looked at Dominic. Dominic looked at Wren. Somewhere in his chest, beneath the shock and the ringing and the vertigo of a world suddenly, horribly *full*, one thought settled cold and clear as the prophecy's ink:

The eve of the false feast.

They were exactly where the book had said he would be.
