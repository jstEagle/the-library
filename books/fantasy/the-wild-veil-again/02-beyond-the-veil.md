## 2. Beyond the Veil

The falling took longer than it should have.

Wren Halloway had read accounts of the valley's boundary — dry academic treatises describing a "perceptual deadness," a place where magic simply declined to exist. None of them mentioned that crossing it felt like being swallowed. The world folded around them, dark and warm and smelling faintly of old paper, and for one vertiginous moment Wren understood what a bookmark must feel like: held between pages, belonging to neither story.

Then they landed, hard, on cobblestone.

Wren's shoulder took the impact. She rolled, came up on her knees in a narrow street that smelled of woodsmoke and horse dung and roasting meat, and stared at the sky. It was evening here too — but not their evening. Banners hung from every window, deep blue shot with gold thread, and somewhere ahead a crowd was singing.

"Dominic." Her voice came out cracked. "Dominic, look."

He was on his hands and knees beside her, and he wasn't looking at anything. He was making a sound she'd never heard from him before — a thin, involuntary noise, like a man trying to breathe through cloth.

"Dominic—"

"It's *loud*." His voice was raw. "Why is it loud? What is—" He pressed his palms flat against the cobblestones as if checking whether the ground itself were lying to him. "Wren. I can feel everything."

She understood then, with a scholar's terrible clarity, what she had done. Twenty-nine years inside a wound cut out of the world. Twenty-nine years of silence so complete he'd never once heard it *as* silence. And now—

"Magic," she said. "You're feeling magic. All of it. At once."

His eyes found hers and there was something in them she'd only seen in wounded animals at the oath-house: pain without any idea of its source. "It doesn't stop. It's like — like standing in a river after a lifetime of drought. It's in my teeth. It's under my skin. How does anyone *live* like this?"

"They don't notice it," Wren said. "That's the point. You grew up deaf to a sound everyone else stopped hearing centuries ago." She reached for his arm. "Can you stand?"

"No," he said honestly. Then he stood anyway, swaying, one hand braced against a shuttered wall. Above them, the banners snapped in a wind that Wren could feel brushing her face but that seemed, judging by Dominic's flinch, to be screaming.

A lamplighter passed them without a glance — two strangers kneeling in the street meant nothing on coronation eve. Coronation eve. The thought snagged in Wren's chest and refused to let go.

The book had named today's date. It had named Dominic Reyes. And now the valley — the safe, sealed, magic-dead valley that had stood impenetrable for three hundred years — had *swallowed them and spat them out here*, on this night, in this city.

Coincidence was an explanation for people who hadn't spent their lives studying prophecy.

"We need somewhere dark and quiet," she said. "And we need the book."

* * *

They found it in a chapel of some minor saint, unlocked and empty, lit by votive candles no one had tended. Dominic collapsed onto a bench and sat with his head in his hands while Wren spread the prophecy book across her knees and turned to the page she had been avoiding since the oath-house library.

She had translated the passage about Dominic quickly enough — his name, the date, the phrase *the oath-bearer crosses while the veil forgets him*. It had been the next lines she'd skipped, because they made no sense in isolation, and because she had been hoping, in the cowardly way scholars hope, that context would make them kinder.

It didn't.

*"And when the oath-bearer stands beneath the double sky,"* she read aloud, quietly, translating as she went, *"the crowned thing shall finish its meal."*

Dominic lowered his hands. "Its meal."

"The crown eats," Wren said. "Or something wearing the crown eats. The grammar is ambiguous — the old dialect uses 'crowned' as both adjective and agent." She hated herself for the precision even as she offered it. "Either way, someone is being eaten, and tonight appears to be the finishing."

"That's why the book pulled us through." Dominic said it flatly, testing the shape of it. "Not fate. Not the god. The valley itself moved us, or something used the valley, because the oath-bearer has to be present when—"

"When the meal ends. Yes." Wren closed the book gently, as though it might bite. "I've been thinking about the Silent God since we crossed."

"You've been thinking about anything other than how sick you feel. I can see it."

"I'm a Halloway; denial is our native tongue. Listen." She turned to face him fully. In the candlelight his skin looked grey, and sweat stood on his forehead, and still he watched her with that mercenary patience, waiting for her to finish her thought. It was, she reflected, the most unnerving thing about him. "Every account of the Silent God agrees on one detail. It didn't die. Gods don't die — they're *unmade*, and unmaking leaves traces, ruins, echoes. There are none. No ruins, no echoes, no cult fragments, nothing. The Silent God simply... stopped answering. Three hundred years ago. The same century the valley was sealed."

"And?"

"And silence isn't absence, Dominic. Silence can be *imposed*." She let that sit between them. "What if your god didn't abandon you? What if something took its voice?"

Dominic was quiet for a long time. Somewhere outside, the singing swelled — thousands of voices celebrating a coronation — and Wren saw him wince at it, at the sheer crowded weight of sound and magic pressing against senses built for a dead valley.

"If you're right," he said finally, "then my whole life, every prayer, every vigil, every year my family knelt in that oath-house and got nothing back — we weren't being tested. We were praying to a prisoner."

"I know."

"My grandmother died still keeping the vigils. My father too. They called it faith." His jaw worked. "It was a hostage situation and we called it devotion."

Wren reached over and closed her hand around his wrist — the closest either of them ever came to comfort. "We'll grieve properly later. Right now we have a king to save and approximately six hours to learn how."

"How do you know it's six hours?"

She nodded toward the chapel window, where the last light was dying off the rooftops. "Coronations happen at moonrise. It's tradition. Which means we need to get into that palace, and we need a reason, and we need it fast, because I promise you the crowned thing knows we're coming. If the book named you, it may have named us both to anyone who reads the old dialect."

Dominic exhaled slowly. Then he did the thing he always did — the thing that had kept him alive through twenty-nine years of a life that had, unknown to all of them, been a holding pattern. He stopped grieving and started planning.

"Pilgrims," he said. "Coronation eve means pilgrims from every province, and pilgrim guards travel light and get waved through gates because nobody wants to be the officer who insulted a holy order during a crowning. We find a procession, fall in at the rear, keep our heads down until we're inside the walls."

"That's insane."

"That's Tuesday. Come on."

* * *

It worked, which offended Wren's professional opinion of palace security.

They fell in behind a procession of ash-grey pilgrims from the northern provinces — hooded, tired, singing low — and walked through a gate tall enough to admit a siege tower, past guards who looked through them entirely. Inside, the palace grounds blazed with lanterns and the smell of roasting meat and, everywhere, that blue-and-gold, blue-and-gold, until Wren wanted to tear the banners down just to see something else.

Dominic walked beside her with his hood up and his breathing controlled, and she could tell the magic was still grinding against him, wave after wave, a man with a burn standing in sunlight.

They had nearly reached the pilgrims' quarter when Dominic stopped.

"What?" Wren whispered.

He didn't answer. He tilted his head back, slow, like a man following a scent — except she knew it wasn't scent. It was whatever new sense the world had forced open in him. His gaze climbed the palace's eastern facade, up pale stone and lit windows, up to a high arched window near the roofline, where a figure stood alone.

Even from the courtyard, even at distance, Wren could see it was the king. The posture gave it away — that particular stillness of men who have been posed their whole lives, shoulders squared by decades of instruction. He stood looking down at his own capital on the night before his crowning, and it should have been nothing. A tired old man at a window.

Then the lanterns nearest the window guttered.

All of them. Simultaneously. A circle of flame bending away from the glass as if the night itself leaned back from whatever stood there.

And the king turned his head — not toward the courtyard below, not toward the crowds, but *toward them*. Toward Dominic, specifically, with the unerring accuracy of something that had felt an oath-bearer cross three hundred miles of sealed valley and land in its larder.

From this far, Wren couldn't possibly have seen his eyes.

She saw them anyway.

They flickered. Once, twice — like a lamp with something else burning behind the flame — and in the space between flickers the face above them changed. Not aged. Not altered. Just *occupied*, the way a house is occupied, the way a mouth is full.

Beside her, Dominic made a small sound. When Wren glanced at him, he was staring at the window with his hand pressed flat over his chest, over the oath-mark he'd carried unseen his entire life, and his expression was one she would carry to her grave.

Wonder, curdling into horror, curdling into recognition.

"He sees me," Dominic breathed. "Wren — whatever's wearing him — *it knows what I am.*"

Down in the courtyard, the pilgrims' song rose toward moonrise, joyful and oblivious, and high above them the king lifted one hand — slowly, deliberately — and beckoned.
