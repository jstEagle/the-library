## 3. The King Who Blinks Twice

The palace gates took pilgrims at dawn, and so we became pilgrims.

Wren had spent the coin from her exile's purse on two sets of guard livery — plain grey tabards over our own clothes, the kind worn by the hired swords who walked holy processions for pay. She'd sewn a pilgrim's sun onto each shoulder with thread she pulled from the hem of her coat. "Nobody looks at guards," she said. "Guards are furniture that bleeds."

I didn't argue. My hands were still shaking from the crossing, and I needed something to do with them besides notice it.

Magic had not let go of me since the valley spat us out. It sat under my skin like water in a boot, sloshing when I turned too fast, cold where it pooled. In the valley I had never felt it at all. Now I could feel everything: the wards on the city walls humming like struck bells, the blessing-stains in the gutters where priests had poured holy oil, the faint sour tang of a hundred small charms hanging off doorways as we passed through the pilgrims' quarter. The whole capital was soaked in the stuff, and every drop of it noticed me.

"Stop flinching," Wren murmured as we joined the queue at the eastern gate. "You look like a man walking into spiderwebs."

"I am."

"You're a guard. Guards are bored. Be furniture."

So I was furniture. A bored guard with an aching skull, standing behind a fat merchant-pilgrim who smelled of incense and onions, waiting while the gate wardens touched each traveler's brow with an iron rod to check for unlicensed workings. When the rod came to me, the warden paused. Frowned. Touched again.

"Headache?" he said.

"Aye."

He waved us through. Whatever the rod tasted on me, it wasn't worth coronation-eve trouble. But Wren's eyes had gone flat and careful, and she didn't speak until we were three streets inside.

"The mark," she said quietly. "It shows here, doesn't it. To anyone who knows how to look."

"In the valley it never showed at all."

"That was the point of the valley." She adjusted her tabard strap and looked straight ahead. "Dominic. Every mage in this city is going to know how to look. We need to be through the palace before one of them does more than frown."

* * *

Getting inside was insultingly easy, which should have been my first warning.

Coronation eve meant the palace swallowed strangers whole. Pilgrim companies came to sing in the outer chapels; guard-companies came to keep them orderly; provision-wagons came stacked with salt fish and candle wax. We attached ourselves to a procession of Penitent Brothers — actual holy men, gaunt and gray-robed, chanting their way up the processional road — and simply fell in beside them as escort. Nobody challenged us. Twice, actual palace guards nodded to us as colleagues.

The last time someone had trusted me this readily, I'd been eleven years old and buying sweets with a forged copper.

We ended up stationed in a colonnade overlooking the inner courtyard, which was exactly where Wren wanted to be. She stood with her hands clasped like a pious hireling while her eyes did the real work: mapping doors, counting patrols, noting which windows of the royal wing stayed shuttered against the festival lights below.

I watched the people instead. That's what I'm for. Wren reads books; I read rooms.

And the room was wrong.

Not the courtyard itself — garlands, lanterns, musicians tuning, all of it bright enough to blind. Wrong was the servants. They moved through their tasks smoothly, but they moved *quietly*, quieter than servants ever move on a feast day, and every few minutes one or another of them would glance up at the royal wing and then away, fast, like a man checking whether the dog is loose.

"They're afraid of him," I said under my breath.

"Of course they are. He's the king."

"No. This is different fear. I've guarded men everyone fears. They don't look away quick. They look long, to watch for it coming." I tipped my chin toward a scullery boy frozen mid-errand beneath the royal balcony. "That one's praying."

Above us, behind the shuttered lattice of the king's window, a light burned steady and greenish, like sunlight through river ice.

Then the shutters opened.

The king stepped out onto the balcony alone. From below he must have seemed a fine figure still — tall, silver-bearded, robed in white and gold for tomorrow's ceremony. But I was closer than the crowd would be, close enough across the courtyard to see what the lantern-light did to his face.

His face did nothing. That was the horror of it. The body stood in a king's posture, one hand resting on the rail with easy authority, surveying his city on the eve of its greatest celebration — and the face above all that majesty was slack as a mask hung on a hook. Not sad, not angry. Unoccupied.

Then the eyes came around and found me.

Across sixty feet of courtyard, past lanterns and garlands and two hundred oblivious subjects, the king looked directly at me and smiled.

And blinked twice.

Slow. Deliberate. Like a signal.

My hand went to my sword before I knew I'd moved. Beside me, Wren went very still. "*Don't*," she breathed.

The king held the smile another moment. Then the face remembered itself — filled in, animated, warm — and he raised a hand in benediction to the courtyard, and the crowd cheered, and the shutters closed over the greenish light.

"Furniture," Wren whispered. "Be furniture. Someone is watching us watch him."

She was right. At the colonnade's far end, a woman in court grays had stopped pretending to inspect the garlands. She had the stillness of a professional, and her gaze rested on us with the mild patience of a cat at a mousehole.

"Wren," I said, very low. "How much trouble are we in?"

"All of it. All the trouble there is."

* * *

The prince found us before the woman did. Or perhaps the prince was how the woman chose to find us — I never did untangle that.

He came down the colonnade steps unannounced, no herald, only two household guards trailing at a distance that was either protection or surveillance, possibly both. He was younger than I expected — twenty-five, maybe, broad through the shoulders, with his father's height and none of his polish. His festival clothes were immaculate and his knuckles were split, both of them, like a man who hits walls.

"You're not mine," he said, stopping in front of us. "Whose company?"

"We're contracted for the Penitent procession, highness," Wren said, smooth as oiled leather, and offered a bow of exactly the right depth. "Halloway and Reyes."

"Mm." Prince Aldric looked at me. Not at my tabard, not at my bow. At me. "Reyes. And where is your company now, Reyes?"

"Paid off until midnight, highness. We took the liberty of viewing the festivities."

"The festivities." Something flickered across his face — exhaustion, maybe, or hope wearing exhaustion's clothes. "Walk with me. Both of you. If you're going to stand in my father's courtyard staring at his window, you'll do it where I can see you."

The household guards peeled away as we walked, which told me whose idea the distance was. Aldric led us along the gallery above the great hall, where preparations for tomorrow's feast rattled and shouted below us — scaffolding, trestles, a small army of cooks. Noise enough to drown a conversation. He chose the spot deliberately. That, I understood.

"You blinked at my father," he said without preamble, once we were out of earshot even of echoes. "Or rather, he blinked at you. Which is stranger. Who are you?"

"Hired steel, highness—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip, and for a moment the polished prince vanished and something rawer looked out of his face — a son, tired down to the bone. "I've spent a year listening to liars tell me my father is well. One more and I swear by every god I'll have you both thrown in the mire-cells just to hear someone say something true. So. Who are you?"

Wren glanced at me. It was my call. That was the shape of things between us already: she read, I decided.

"Mercenaries," I said. "Real ones. From the valley country, far south. We came because of a book."

"A book."

"A very old book," Wren said carefully, "that says the king will die tomorrow unless something changes. And that says the thing wearing his crown isn't the king at all."

I'll give the prince this: he didn't call for the guards. He went pale, gripped the gallery rail, and laughed — one short, terrible bark that the cooks below probably mistook for festivity.

"A year," he said. "A year I've been telling myself I'm mad. He speaks—" Aldric stopped. Started again, quieter, like a confession dragged out of a wound. "My father speaks in two voices. You understand? Two. The first is him — dry, sharp, complains about my posture and the cost of candles. My whole life that voice has told me I disappoint him, and gods forgive me, I'd give anything to hear it complain again. The other voice—" His knuckles whitened on the rail. "The other voice is *patient*. It's kind. It calls me by my childhood name and tells me how much easier things will be when I stop grieving him and start helping *us*. Us. As if we were the same thing."

"When did it start?" Wren asked.

"Three winters ago. Slowly. Everyone said grief, then age, then the burdens of rule." He turned to look at us fully, and I saw he'd already decided something enormous, and hated himself for it. "Last month the second voice gave an order in council. In front of everyone. And the chancellor obeyed it *gladly*, like a dog hearing its master, and no one else in that room thought anything of it except me."

Below us, the cooks sang a work-song about a cheating baker. Somewhere a bell rang the third hour.

"Why us?" I asked. "Why trust two strangers with livery you can buy by the bolt?"

"Because of what the court mage said about you." Aldric's jaw tightened. "Maerlin tested every soul entering for the coronation. Standard rite. Most pass clean. Yours lit up like a funeral pyre."

Beside me, Wren had gone very quiet.

"He couldn't name what he found," the prince continued. "Said it was old. Older than the registry, older than the kingdom's rites. Said he'd seen its like once before, in records from the founding era — a mark called the Seal of the Silent God." Aldric's eyes fixed on mine, and in them I saw the arithmetic of a desperate man: suspicion and hope balancing on a knife's edge. "The Silent God is dead, Reyes. Declared dead a thousand years. Executed, some histories say, by the first king himself. So either Maerlin is a fool, or you are carrying a dead god's brand into my father's house the day before it eats him. I want to know which."

The truth is, I wanted to know which too.

"It's inherited," I said. "My family has carried it for generations. In exchange for the valley's protection. It never did anything. It never *said* anything. It's a birthmark that aches when it rains."

"Show me."

I almost refused. Then I thought of the greenish window, the empty face, the double blink — *a signal*, aimed at me, from a thing that should not have known I existed — and I rolled up my sleeve.

The mark sat on the inside of my forearm, where it had always been: a spiral of fine gray lines, tight as a closed eye. Except that tonight, for the first time in my life, it wasn't closed. The lines had loosened. Spread. And at the center, dark and unmistakable, was a shape like a mouth.

Aldric stared at it. So did Wren, and her expression frightened me more than the prince's, because Wren understood what she was looking at.

"It's opening," she whispered.

At the far end of the gallery, the woman in court grays stepped out of the shadow of a pillar. Maerlin, then — the court mage himself — tall and gray-robed, with eyes like coins left in a well. He had been there long enough to hear everything.

"The mark of the Silent God," Maerlin said, and his voice was soft and terrible, "has not been seen in the living world since the first king slew it. The penalty for bearing it is death by statute older than this dynasty." He raised one hand, and cold gathered in the air around his fingers. "Unless, of course, the bearer proves useful to the crown. Come with me, mercenary. Both of you. The king will want to look at you himself."

Behind Maerlin, far down the gallery, a door opened onto darkness — and from somewhere deep in the palace, in a voice I recognized with my skin before my ears caught up, patient and kind, something began to laugh.
