## 4. What the Oath Bought

The palace archives smelled of dust and old ambition.

Wren Halloway had spent three days convincing the under-clerk that she was a visiting historiographer from the University of Vell, which did not exist, and that her letters of introduction had been lost in a river, which they had not, because there was no river. The lie worked because lies about institutions always worked. No one questioned a scholar. Scholars were furniture. Scholars were permitted to touch things.

So she touched everything.

She sat now in the records annex beneath the east wing, a lamp burning low beside her, with ledgers stacked around her like siege works. Somewhere above her, Dominic Reyes was playing pilgrim guard in the outer court, standing very still and looking very dangerous, which for him required no acting at all. She'd told him she needed one day among the documents. He'd said he didn't like being separated. He'd said it flatly, without looking at her, the way he said everything, and she had understood it as what passed between them for affection.

*One day,* she'd promised. *Then we know how this ends.*

Now she wished she hadn't promised, because she was beginning to see how it ended, and she wanted more time before knowing.

* * *

Three generations ago, on the last night of autumn, a woman named Sera Reyes walked out of the mountains and into the valley that would one day be named after her family's silence.

Wren knew this because she had grown up on the story — everyone in the valley had. It was the founding tale, told at every hearth-fire: Sera the faithful, Sera who heard the Silent God when no one else could, Sera who knelt in the snow and made a bargain on behalf of all of them.

*Protect us,* the people had begged her, because the wars beyond the ridge were burning their way toward the valley like a tide. *Do whatever must be done.*

And Sera had gone up the mountain alone, and come down at dawn with frost in her hair and an oath in her mouth. The god would seal the valley. Magic would never follow them in. No war, no plague of sorcery, no hungry thing from the wilds would cross the boundary. In exchange, the Reyes line would serve. Would listen. Would keep the faith of a god who asked nothing else.

That was the story. Wren had loved it as a child. She had drawn the boundary line in chalk on the schoolhouse floor and stood inside it, safe, and felt chosen by proxy.

What the story never explained — what no teller ever thought to ask — was what the god got out of it. Or why a god who asked nothing would need servants at all.

* * *

The annex ledger for the reign of King Aldous III was bound in cracked calfskin and smelled faintly of mildew. Wren turned its pages with cotton gloves, moving through the dry machinery of a court: grain tallies, guard rotations, appointments, deaths.

Deaths.

There was a page near the back where the court chronicler recorded unusual fatalities — not the routine toll of plague or age, but deaths that warranted notation. Wren had been skimming these idly, half her mind elsewhere, when a phrase caught her eye.

*Died within sight of the throne.*

She stopped. Read it again. A guardsman, apparently, who collapsed during an audience and died on the steps below the dais. The chronicler had noted it with something like puzzlement: *a man of sound health, taken suddenly, his face turned upward as if listening.*

His face turned upward as if listening.

Wren's mouth went dry. She flipped forward, scanning. Two pages later: a foreign envoy, dead in the throne room itself, mid-audience. Then a priest. Then, years on, a woman listed only as *a pilgrim of the quiet order*, found kneeling below the dais at dawn, cold, her hands folded, "her expression one of profound peace."

Profound peace. Wren had seen that expression once before, recently, described in another hand entirely — in the margins of the prophecy book, in a note about the first of the oath-bearers, Sera Reyes herself.

*Sera returned to the capital in her sixty-first year,* the marginalist had written. *Knelt before the throne. Did not rise.*

Wren sat back. The lamp hissed. Above her, muffled through stone, she could hear the palace waking — boots on flags, doors, the distant bright noise of coronation preparations, the kingdom rehearsing its happiness.

Every oath-bearer died within sight of the throne.

Not eventually. Not coincidentally. Every single one, across four generations, had traveled from the sealed valley to the capital and died in this building, in this room or its predecessors, kneeling or falling or simply stopping, faces turned upward, expressions of peace.

She began pulling the threads faster. Service records. Travel writs. The valley sent its oath-bearer once per generation — sometimes to renew some arrangement, sometimes bearing tribute, always summoned by letter. And each time, the record ended the same way. A death in the throne room. A quiet burial. A new name entered in the valley's rolls a generation later.

Four names. Four deaths. And a fifth name currently walking the palace corridors upstairs in borrowed armor, alive only because the entity wearing the crown hadn't yet decided to finish its meal.

*It wasn't devotion,* Wren thought, and the thought arrived with the cold certainty of a key turning. *It was a leash. The valley wasn't protected. It was penned.*

The bargain had never been between Sera and her god. The bargain had been between the crown and something older, something that ate oaths the way rot eats wood — and the valley, the sealing, the magic-dead sanctuary, all of it was not mercy but storage. A larder. A place to keep the oath-line fat, faithful, and breeding, generation after generation, each heir raised on stories of sacred service, walking willingly into the throne room to be consumed.

Sera hadn't knelt in peace. She had been devoured with her face turned up, grateful to the end.

Wren pressed her gloved hand flat against the ledger page until the paper dimpled. She thought of Dominic as a boy in the oath-house, learning the liturgies. She thought of his mother, who had died quietly in the valley, spared only because the summons hadn't come yet. She thought of the way Dominic spoke of the Silent God — not with love, exactly, but with the settled weight of a man describing gravity. Something always there. Something that held him up.

Had anything ever answered him? Even once? Or had he spent his whole life speaking into a mouth that had been sewn shut decades before his birth, kept as livestock keeps its bell — worn so the owner can be found again?

* * *

She found him at dusk in the guard barracks' shadowed alcove, checking the straps of borrowed armor with unnecessary focus. He saw her face and stopped moving.

"You found something."

"I found your family." She kept her voice level. "All of it. Four generations of it, in the death records."

He didn't flinch. That was the terrible thing about Dominic — he absorbed blows the way stone absorbs rain, showing nothing, taking it all inward somewhere deep. "Say it plainly."

"Every oath-bearer your valley ever sent died in the throne room. Within sight of the crown. They called it service. They called it peace." She swallowed. "Dominic, the valley isn't a sanctuary. It's a pen. Your whole life — the oath-house, the liturgies, the silence you were taught to call holiness — it was husbandry. You weren't raised to serve a god. You were raised to ripen."

For a long moment he said nothing. A guard patrol passed the alcove's mouth; they both went statue-still until the footsteps faded.

"The god is real," he said finally. Quiet. Not arguing — pleading, almost, though he'd have denied the word. "I've felt it. My mother felt it. The binding is real."

"The binding is real," Wren agreed. "A chain is real too. That doesn't make it kindness." She stepped closer. "Something swallowed your god's voice, Dominic. I don't know what yet — but I know where it lives, and I know what it eats, and I'm looking at the last item on its menu."

He looked down at his own hands, the hands that had held the oath since childhood, and for the first time since she'd known him, Dominic Reyes looked less like a wall and more like a man reading his own sentence.

"Tonight," he said at last. "The feast. If it wants me in sight of the throne—"

"Then we stop letting it choose the ground." Wren tucked the copied records inside her coat, next to the prophecy book. "There are catacombs under the old temple. Older than the palace. If your god left anything behind — a voice, a grave, a door — it'll be there."

"And if it's a trap?"

"It's always been a trap," she said. "The difference is that now we're walking in with our eyes open."

Above them, through the high slit windows, the sky over the capital was going the color of a bruise, and somewhere far off across the city, in a tower room hung with velvet, a king who blinked twice lifted his head — and listened.
