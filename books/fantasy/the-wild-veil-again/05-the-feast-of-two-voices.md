## 5. The Feast of Two Voices

They dressed me in borrowed silk, which should have been my first warning.

Wren had argued for plain wool — pilgrims' clothes, humble, forgettable — but the chamberlain's steward took one look at us and declared that anyone invited to the Feast of Two Voices would *not* embarrass the crown by dressing like a beggar. So now I stood in a corridor outside the great hall, tugging at a collar embroidered with silver thread, feeling like a man wearing another man's skin.

"You keep touching it," Wren said. They'd gotten a deep blue doublet that actually suited them, which annoyed me on principle. Their hair was braided back, scholar-neat, and their eyes kept doing that thing where they catalogued everything — the guards' rotation, the number of doors, which nobles wore swords and which wore wands disguised as jewelry.

"It doesn't fit."

"Nothing here fits. That's rather the point of the whole kingdom." They lowered their voice. "The mark. Is it quiet?"

I pressed my palm flat against my chest, over the oath-scar beneath the silk. The Silent God's brand had been silent my whole life — a cold place in me, a door that never opened. But since we'd crossed the valley's edge, it had been *warm*. Not speaking. Never speaking. Just warm, like a banked coal.

"Warm," I said.

"Warm is bad?"

"Warm is new."

Wren chewed their lip and didn't answer, which from Wren was an entire lecture on how much trouble we were in.

Then the horns sounded, and the doors opened, and there was no more time to be afraid in private.

* * *

The great hall of Veymarch Palace was built to make you feel small, and it succeeded. Ceilings arched high enough to lose birds in. Banners of every province hanging like captured things. A thousand candles, and every flame burning perfectly straight, as if the air itself were afraid to move.

And at the head of it all, on a throne of pale wood that predated every banner, sat King Edric the Third.

Except it wasn't quite him.

I'd seen him through the window two nights ago with Wren — the wrong flicker behind the eyes, the blink that came twice when once would do. Up close it was worse. The king smiled and the smile arrived half a heartbeat late, like a messenger repeating words he hadn't understood. His hands rested on the throne's arms with the patience of something that had been sitting a very long time and could sit longer still.

*That is not a man,* said some old mercenary instinct in my gut. *That is a coat someone is wearing.*

We were seated far down the tables, among minor clergy and provincial knights who didn't know each other's faces — good cover, Wren's choice. Wine came. Food came. Wren ate nothing and memorized everything.

"The rite begins at moonrise," they murmured. "Aldric said the entity needs the feast first. It feeds on—"

"On oaths," I finished. "Sworn loyalty. A thousand people swearing themselves to the crown in one room." I looked down the length of the hall, at lords kneeling in sequence before the dais, kissing a ring on the king's finger, each one glowing faintly — so faintly only someone marked by a god would notice — as the vow took hold. "It's not a coronation feast. It's a harvest."

Wren went pale. "Then why are we—"

"I know." I drained my wine. Because Aldric needed proof, and proof meant getting close, and getting close meant standing in front of the thing and letting it look at me. "Stay near the east door. If I signal—"

The line broke off, because the herald called our names.

Not our real names. Our pilgrim names, the ones forged into the invitation Wren had bought in the undercity. But the herald's voice stumbled anyway, just slightly, on *Reyes*, and I felt the whole hall's attention slide toward us like water finding a crack.

We walked. What else was there? We walked up the long aisle between the tables, past nobles whose perfume couldn't mask the smell underneath — old magic, old hunger — and knelt before the dais as we'd rehearsed, heads bowed.

The king leaned forward.

Up close, his eyes were the worst part. Brown, human, tired — and beneath them, way down, something vast turning over in its sleep. When he spoke, both voices came at once: the king's, cracked and old, and beneath it a voice like a landslide given grammar.

"Pilgrims," it said. "From the valley lands."

"We come to swear," I said, keeping my eyes down.

"Yes." The thing inhaled slowly, savoring. "You carry something. Something *old*." And then, softly, almost tenderly, in a voice pitched for me alone while the hall heard only blessing-words: "*There you are. Little door. Little locked door that walked away from its house.*"

My blood went cold. The warmth under my breastbone flared — not pain, but recognition, the way a dog lifts its head when its master's name is spoken by a stranger.

"I don't understand, Your Majesty."

"Oh, I think you do." The ring on the king's hand glittered. Around us, the feast continued, music playing, no one seeing. "You bear the silence. The last crumb of it. The last piece of the meal that got away." The voice dropped lower, richer, honey poured over stone. "I have eaten gods, little oath-bearer. I swallowed your master whole a century ago, and it has rattled in my belly ever since, mute and cold. But you — you carry its *bond*. Its key. Give it to me. Lay it down here, tonight, and I will give you what it never did: *answers*. Power. A voice of your own instead of an empty scar. Every prayer you ever sent into that silence — I heard them all. I can return them *answered*."

And here is the truth I will carry to whatever grave I get: for one moment, I wanted it.

One moment. One breath. A lifetime of kneeling in the dark of the oath-house, speaking to a god that never spoke back, never stirred, never gave so much as a sigh in return — and here was a thing offering to fill that silence with *sound*. With purpose. With a reason for all those wasted years of devotion.

The warmth in my chest trembled like a held breath.

But then I thought about the king's eyes. The tired, buried, drowning eyes above the thing that wore him. I thought about every oath-bearer in my family line, generation after generation, dying within sight of thrones they'd been bred to guard. I thought about Wren, ten feet away, watching me with wide, fierce eyes.

The god had never answered me. Fine. But it had also never *asked* me for anything. That was more than this thing could say.

"No," I said.

The hall's music seemed to skip.

"No?" The entity's amusement was genuinely delighted, which was somehow worse than anger. "You refuse a god's table?"

"You're not a god. You're a *meal* that learned to set one." I rose from my knee. "And I keep what's mine."

For a heartbeat the whole hall went utterly still — candles bending sideways, wine rippling in cups, a thousand conversations dying mid-word — and I knew, with mercenary certainty, that I was about to die.

Then an arrow took the herald through the throat.

* * *

Chaos is a language and I'm fluent.

The second arrow came for me and I was already moving, hauling Wren down behind the high table as it buried itself in the throne's arm. Screaming everywhere. Guards drawing steel — but at *us*, not the archers, and that told me everything. This wasn't an attack on the king. This was the king's own house cleaning up a problem.

"The east door!" Wren shouted, and we ran.

Steel sang behind us. I caught a sword-stroke on my forearm, turned, put my elbow into a guardsman's throat — a boy, barely older than Aldric, sorry, lad — and kept moving. The entity's voice pursued us through the din, no longer honeyed, no longer patient, roaring out of the king's body loud enough to crack the banners:

"*BRING ME THE DOOR.*"

We burst through the east door into a service corridor and slammed the bar across it. Three heartbeats of silence. Then the corridor's side passage opened and Prince Aldric stepped out, sword drawn, cloak thrown over court finery, looking like a man who'd finally stopped pretending he didn't know this day was coming.

"This way," he said. "Quickly. There's a servant stair to the cisterns — the undercity tunnels run beneath the temple district. My father's creatures won't follow past the old wards."

"Your father's *creatures*," I panted.

"My father is currently a dining room." He met my eyes, and there was no flicker in his, no doubling, just grim, exhausted clarity. "You refused it. In front of the whole court. Do you know what that means?"

"That it wants me more now."

"That everyone loyal to the crown wants you dead, and everyone who might help you is too frightened to try." He turned, cloak snapping. "Which leaves me. Come on. We have perhaps four minutes before they cut through that door, and I intend to spend them running."

Wren fell in beside me as we pounded down the narrow stair, torchlight strobing across wet stone. They grabbed my sleeve. "The mark. Dominic — the warmth — what happened when it offered you the bond back?"

I thought about it. The trembling, the held breath, the wanting.

"It flinched," I said. "When I refused. Like it was afraid."

Wren exchanged a look with me that contained an entire conversation: *gods shouldn't be afraid of anything, unless they're prisoners, and prisoners always hope.*

Behind us, the barred door splintered. Above, muffled through stone, the two-voiced roar shook dust from the ceiling.

Below, the dark of the undercity waited — and somewhere in it, I was starting to suspect, the answer to why a silenced god had spent a hundred years warming in my chest like a coal that refused to die.
