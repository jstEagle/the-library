## 6. Where Gods Go Quiet

The old temple of the Silent God had no door.

That was the first thing Wren noticed, standing in the rain-slick alley behind the chandler's shop where Aldric's man had told them to wait. The temple was a wedge of older stone jammed between two newer buildings, its façade blank except for a single carved lintel — and where every other holy house in the capital announced itself with doors of bronze or ironwood, this one had only a doorway-shaped absence, a rectangle of deeper dark that swallowed the lamplight and gave nothing back.

"They bricked it up," Dominic said beside her.

"No." Wren touched the edge of the opening and felt cold air move outward, slow as breath. "They just stopped using doors. Doors are how you leave."

Dominic looked at her. In the three days since they'd fled the feast with assassins' knives in their wake, he had barely slept, and it showed in the hollows under his eyes, in the way his hand kept drifting to the oath-mark on his forearm — touching it like a man checking a wound that wouldn't close. "You're being grim again, Halloway."

"I'm being accurate. It's a different discipline." She pulled her coat tighter and stepped into the dark.

* * *

The catacombs beneath the old temple were older than the kingdom above them. Wren knew this the way she knew most things: from footnotes, from marginalia, from a half-burned survey ledger she'd stolen from the palace archive two nights ago while Dominic stood watch and complained about it. The ledger listed the temple's foundations as pre-dating the first king by two hundred years. Which meant the god had been worshipped here before there was a crown to wear.

She lit her lamp at the top of the stairs and led them down into a corridor of stacked bones and older stone, the air tasting of chalk and still water. Their footsteps echoed wrong — not multiplying the way echoes should, but flattening, as if the corridor were drinking sound.

"It's quiet," Dominic said, and then stopped walking.

Wren turned. He was standing very still, one hand pressed flat against the wall, his face doing something complicated.

"Dominic?"

"I've heard my god my whole life," he said slowly. "Not words. Never words. But — presence. Like standing near a large animal in the dark. A warmth at the back of the oath-mark." His fingers curled against the stone. "It's louder here than it's ever been. And it sounds like—"

He didn't finish. Wren watched him try twice more and give up, and something cold slid down her spine, because she had spent her exile studying exactly one subject with any rigor: what happened to things that gods broke.

"Come on," she said gently. "I think we're about to find out why."

* * *

The prison-shrine was at the bottom of everything.

The stair opened into a chamber that no survey ledger had mentioned, circular, maybe forty feet across, its walls covered floor to ceiling in carved script so dense it looked like fur. At the center stood a low dais of black stone, and on the dais — chained — was a shape Wren took a full minute to understand.

It was a statue. No. It was worse than a statue. It was a god rendered in grey basalt, kneeling, roughly human-sized but wrong in proportion — the shoulders too broad, the head bowed so deep it pressed against its own chest, the hands bound at the wrists with bands of the same black stone as the dais. And threaded through the whole figure, from crown of the skull down through the throat and heart and into the stone beneath, ran veins of something that was not stone at all: a dark, glistening substance like tar given architecture, like a root system grown inside a body.

The roots all converged at the figure's mouth.

"Oh," Wren said. Her voice came out very small in the drinking silence. "Oh, you poor thing."

"Wren." Dominic had gone past her without seeming to walk, drawn forward the way iron is drawn. He stood at the base of the dais looking up at the kneeling god, and tears were running down his face freely, unashamed, the way men cry when they've stopped noticing they're doing it. "It's been here. The whole time. My whole life, my father's life, every Reyes back six generations praying to a thing that was already—"

"Chained," Wren finished quietly. She climbed the dais because someone had to be clinical about this, because if she let herself feel it now she would be useless for an hour. Up close she could read the script covering the walls — not the temple script of the upper levels but something far older, and she recognized the grammatical structure immediately because she'd spent four years of exile translating texts just like it. Binding grammar. Contract language. The same family of script used in the prophecy book, in the crown's inscription, in every rite the royal line had ever used to make power permanent.

"This isn't a shrine," she said, tracing a line of it. "Shrines are built by the faithful. This is a lock. Built around him after." She turned a slow circle, reading. "Listen to this — 'that which speaks shall speak no more; its voice is taken as surety; the debt of the first crown shall be paid in silence.'" Her finger stopped moving. "Dominic. They didn't kill your god. They couldn't. Gods don't die, they just — change custody." She looked down at the tar-roots threading into the kneeling mouth. "The entity ate its voice. Swallowed it whole. Everything your god ever was, every prayer it could answer, every word it could say — it's been held inside that thing wearing the king like wine held in a cask."

Dominic knelt. Not ceremonially — he simply folded, knees hitting the stone, and pressed his oath-mark against the god's bound hand. For a long moment nothing happened. Then the mark began to glow, faintly, a pale silver-grey like moonlight through fog, and Dominic made a sound Wren had never heard from him — not pain exactly, and not relief either, but the sound of a man hearing a voice he'd believed dead call his name from a great distance.

"She's still in there," he whispered. "Wren. She's still *in there.*"

"How do you know it's a she?"

"I don't know anything about my god. That was the point." He laughed, wet and broken. "Six generations of service and we never even got a name. Just rules. Keep the valley. Stay small. Stay silent. I thought the silence was discipline. I thought we were honoring—" He stopped. Pressed his forehead to the cold basalt hand. "We were mimicking a prisoner."

Wren crouched beside him and did the thing she was actually good at: she read. The binding grammar circled the chamber nine times, and each ring repeated the central clause with variations, the way lawyers repeat terms to close loopholes. By the fourth ring she understood the structure. By the seventh she understood the price. By the ninth she sat back on her heels and stared at the tar-rooted god and felt grief arrive in her chest like weather.

"There's an unbinding rite," she said. "It's here. Written into the lock itself — the builders left it, maybe out of arrogance, maybe out of law. Binding grammar always includes its own reversal; otherwise it's not a contract, it's just violence, and even monsters in this kingdom liked to pretend they were lawyers." She exhaled. "But it requires an oath-bearer. A living one, marked, bonded. And it requires that bond — the entire bond, the whole inheritance, everything the Reyes family has carried for six generations — surrendered. Voluntarily. Completely. Not spent, not transferred, not traded to a new master." She looked at Dominic. "*Given up.* Every thread cut. If you do this, you won't be oathless in the way a mercenary is between contracts. You'll be oathless the way an amputated limb is legless. Permanently. The valley's protection dies with it. Whatever your family was to that god — it ends tonight, by choice, or it ends eventually anyway, because the entity will finish digesting her voice within days and then there will be nothing left to inherit from but a hole."

Dominic didn't answer immediately. He stayed kneeling, forehead against the god's hand, and Wren watched the glow of the oath-mark pulse — slow, arrhythmic, like a heartbeat recovering from drowning.

"You should know what you'd be giving up," he finally said, and his voice was steadier than she expected. "Everyone assumes the oath is chains. It wasn't. It was — company. Six generations of never being alone in our own skulls. When my mother died, I was twelve, and the silence held me like—" He stopped. Started again. "And now I find out the company itself was in agony the entire time. Screaming, probably. Into a gag." He lifted his head and looked at the god's bowed face, and Wren saw him do the arithmetic she'd already done: every prayer answered with silence, every silence assumed to be wisdom, and underneath it all a voice swallowed whole, held in a monster's throat, for centuries.

"The worst part," he said, "is that I'm relieved. Isn't that obscene? I find out my god has been tortured since before my family existed, and some traitor part of me is relieved, because it means the silence was never a choice. It means none of it was indifference. She wanted to answer us." His jaw tightened. "She just couldn't."

Wren reached out and put her hand over his oath-mark, over the glowing skin, and the grief in the room was so thick she could have carved her initials in it. She thought of her own exile — books banned, name struck from the university rolls, four years of talking to margins because no living scholar would talk to her — and thought: *we are both people who loved institutions that were lying to us the whole time.*

"Then we do it properly," she said. "If she's going to be freed, she gets freed clean. Full rite. And Dominic—" she made herself say it plainly, because he deserved plainness, "—when it's done, you will have lost the only constant of your entire life. I need you to walk in knowing that. I can't have you hesitating mid-rite. Hesitation in contract grammar creates clauses."

"I know." He smiled, and it was terrible and bright. "I grew up fluent in this silence, remember. I know exactly what clauses are."

* * *

They worked until Wren's lamp burned low, transcribing the ninth ring onto strips of parchment torn from the lining of her coat, checking each glyph three times. It was nearly done — the rite copied, the sequence mapped, the hour chosen (the entity's attention would be fixed on tomorrow's coronation preparations; they had perhaps one night) — when the silence in the chamber changed.

Not deepened. *Changed.* The way a room changes when a third person enters it.

Dominic's head came up sharply. "Someone's on the stairs."

Wren snuffed the lamp. In the sudden dark, the god's veins of tar glowed with a faint sickly light, and by that light she saw Dominic rise, knife already drawn, positioning himself between the stairwell and the dais — and she saw, on the wall beside the ninth ring, a section of binding grammar that she had read past a dozen times without weighing it, suddenly legible in the new light:

*and should the voice be freed, the vessel that holds it shall claim the emptiness left behind.*

The footsteps reached the bottom of the stair. A lantern bloomed. And Prince Aldric stepped into the chamber alone, hood back, sword unbelted, hands raised open — with the court mage's seal burning on his palm like a brand.

"We need to talk," he said, "before my father's mage decides you've found what you found."
