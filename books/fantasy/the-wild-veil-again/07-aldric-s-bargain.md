## 7. Aldric's Bargain

The undercity smelled of wet stone and old incense, and Dominic had spent three days learning its geography by candlelight. Wren called it the Warren — a sprawl of collapsed temple crypts and smugglers' galleries beneath the capital, where the crown's writ ran thin and the lamps burned whatever oil the poor could buy. They had a room above a dye-works, one bed, one window that looked out on nothing but brick. Wren had claimed the bed without apology and was asleep in it now, curled around a stolen ledger like a child with a doll.

Dominic sat by the window because sleep had become a country he couldn't reach.

Three days since the feast. Three days since the thing wearing King Edmun's face had looked at him across forty feet of banquet hall and *smiled* — not with the king's mouth alone but with something behind it, something vast turning over in the dark like a whale surfacing. *I know your mark, oath-bearer,* it had said, in a voice that came from the king's throat but didn't belong to any man. *Your god cannot save you. He cannot even save himself.*

Then the assassins had come, and there hadn't been time to think about it. There still wasn't much time now, but the thinking happened anyway, at night, uninvited.

A knock at the door. Two beats, then one — Aldric's signal.

Dominic unbolted it. The prince slipped in wearing a guard's cloak two sizes too large for him, hood up, and for a moment he stood in the middle of the room looking absurdly young. In daylight, at court, he managed to seem older than his twenty-three years. Down here, in a borrowed cloak with soot on his cheek, he looked like what he was: a boy whose father was being eaten alive and who had run out of people to tell.

"You found him," Dominic said. "The catacombs."

Aldric pulled back his hood. "I found where they were. Not what they found." His jaw worked. "My father's worse. He spoke to me this morning in the garden — my father, really him, I'd swear it on anything you like — and then halfway through a sentence about my mother's roses, the voice changed. Mid-word. Like a man handing off a letter." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "Do you know what it's like, hearing your father say *help me* in the same breath he says *kneel*?"

"I have some idea," Dominic said quietly.

"Right. Your silent one." Aldric dragged a stool to the table and dropped onto it. "Wren showed me the shrine. Showed me what's down there under the old temple. A god in a cage made of its own name." He rubbed his face. "And she told me the rest of it. What the binding needs. An oath-bearer willing to give up everything."

"She shouldn't have—"

"She should have told me *first*," Aldric snapped. Then he caught himself, breathed, and when he spoke again his voice was level and careful, the court voice, the one he'd been practicing his whole life. "I'm not here to fight with you, Reyes. I'm here because I've thought about nothing else for three days, and I keep arriving at the same place, and I need you to hear it before I do something stupid alone."

"Say it."

"Force." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "The rite is a gamble. We don't know if it works, we don't know what it costs, we don't even know if your god can be woken after a century of being swallowed. But steel doesn't need to believe in anything. My father's guard still loves him. Half the household knights saw his eyes at the feast and haven't slept since. If I move fast — before the coronation, before the thing finishes settling into him — I can take the crown off his head. Physically. Put him somewhere safe, put the crown somewhere safer, and hold both until Wren's rite can catch up."

"And the entity?"

"Starves." Aldric's smile had no joy in it. "It's been feeding for a hundred years through that crown. Cut it off from the vessel, cut it off from the rites, and it goes hungry. Hungry things get weak. That's not prophecy. That's just animals."

Dominic turned it over. It wasn't a bad plan. It was, in fact, a better plan than most mercenary work he'd taken, which usually amounted to holding a bridge until someone paid them to stop. And unlike Wren's rite, it didn't require the one cost Dominic had been circling for three days without letting himself look at directly.

"The crown can't just be locked in a chest," he said. "You said yourself it's been fed for a century. It'll want a mouth. It'll find one."

"Then we guard the chest."

"With what? You?" Dominic shook his head. "Prince, I've guarded strongboxes my whole life. Everything wants what's inside them eventually, and the ones that wait longest are the worst."

"Then we destroy it," Aldric said. "Melt it down. Unmake it."

"Wren thinks the entity isn't *in* the crown. She thinks the crown is in the entity — a hook set so deep that pulling it out tears everything with it. The king included." He held the prince's eyes. "You'd be gambling your father's life on force. I'd be gambling mine on surrendering the only bond I've ever had. Neither of us gets a clean option. So let's not pretend yours is clean."

Something flickered across Aldric's face — frustration, then something rawer underneath it. He stood and paced the little room, cloak dragging, and stopped with his back to Dominic.

"My father used to tell me stories about the Silent God," he said. "Before. Before the voices started, years ago, when it was just whispers and the priests said it was grief. He told me our line had a debt to that god. That some ancestor of ours — some first king — had made a bargain that bought us a kingdom and left someone else holding the bill." He turned. "That someone else was your family, wasn't it? The oath-bearers. Every generation, one of you, bound to a god who never answered, keeping a valley sealed, dying near thrones. Wren showed me the ledger. Eleven names. Eleven dead within sight of a crown."

"What's your point?"

"My point is that your family paid for my family's throne with their whole lives, and nobody ever told either of us." Aldric's voice cracked and steadied. "So no, Reyes, I don't have a clean plan. I have a kingdom rotting from the head down, a father with maybe weeks, and exactly one person in the world who carries a god's mark and might carry enough weight to matter. I'm asking you to help me take that crown by force. And I'm offering—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm offering what should have been offered a century ago."

"Which is?"

"A new oath. To me." He said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "Swear fealty to me instead of the god. Not as a vessel, not as a sacrifice — as a knight. Sworn service, honest terms, wages paid. Your family gave everything to powers that never answered. Give your loyalty to a man who will answer. Who *has* to answer, because I'll be the one holding the crown and I intend to be worth answering to."

* * *

The silence afterward went on so long that the dye-works below must have finished a batch, because the smell of mordant rose through the floorboards, sharp and chemical.

Dominic made himself think it through, because the offer was not nothing. That was the trap of it — it was almost everything.

An oath to a man instead of a god. Terms spoken aloud, witnessed, breakable only by betrayal rather than death. Wages. Leave to refuse orders. A lord who could be argued with, shamed, deposed if he failed. Everything the Silent God's covenant had never been: the valley sealed, the family bound, eleven generations dying on schedule with no word of thanks or complaint from the thing they served. His grandfather had gone to his grave still kneeling. His father too. Dominic had spent thirty-one years kneeling to a silence and calling it devotion because the alternative — that he'd inherited a leash, not a faith — was unbearable.

And here was a prince offering to unclip the leash and clip on a different one, gilded, with fair terms printed on the tag.

It would work. That was the horror of it. It would genuinely work. He'd swear to Aldric, they'd take the crown by steel, and Dominic would live out his days as a knight of the realm with a pension and maybe, someday, a small house near the coast. He'd never have to face the shrine in the catacombs. Never have to speak the unbinding rite. Never have to learn whether the Silent God's silence all these years was absence or agony — because he'd have traded the question away along with the bond.

He'd swap one master for another and call it freedom, and the chain would still be real, and someday some heir of Aldric's would find the ledger and discover that oaths given to kings curdle exactly like oaths given to gods. Power was power. It drank what was poured into it. The first king had forged the crown to make rule immortal, and what was an oath of fealty except rule written small, rule of one man over one soul?

"You're doing the thing," Aldric said.

"What thing?"

"Where you stare at the wall like it owes you money. Wren does it too. Is it a scholar thing or a killer thing?"

Despite himself, Dominic huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "It's a weighing thing." He stood. Walked to the window. Brick, brick, more brick, a sliver of grey sky. "When I took my father's oath, I was nineteen. Nobody asked me. It was just assumed — firstborn of the Reyes line, the valley's keeper, the god's man. I knelt on stone in the dark and swore to something that had never once said my name, and I told myself the silence was holiness." He turned. "Last month I learned the silence might be screaming. And my first instinct — my honest instinct, prince, I'll give it to you straight — was to find a way to never have to find out."

Aldric had gone very still. "And now?"

"Now you're offering me a way to make that instinct respectable." Dominic crossed the room and stood over the seated prince, close enough to see the sleeplessness in his eyes. "If I swear to you, I serve a good man. Maybe a great one. But I'd be swearing away the exact thing the rite needs before anyone knows if the rite can work. I'd be choosing comfort over answers, and my family chose comfort over answers for eleven generations and it got them a graveyard."

"So you're choosing the god." Flat. Hurt, though he tried to hide it.

"No." Dominic crouched, bringing himself level. "I'm choosing to stop choosing masters. Listen to me — the entity wins every time someone kneels. It was born from a king who wanted worship without end. Every chain we forge feeds the pattern, mine, yours, your father's. The only way to break a thing built out of inheritance is to stop inheriting it." He let that sit. "I'll help you take the crown. Steel, guards, timing — all of it, gladly, freely, no oath required. But my bond stays mine until I understand what it actually is. Because if there's a chance it can free your father *and* free a god that's been drowning for a century, then spending it on a knighthood would be the smallest thing I ever did."

For a long moment Aldric searched his face. Whatever he found there seemed to settle something, because he exhaled slowly and some of the prince went out of his posture, leaving just the tired young man.

"Freely given," he said at last. "No oath. Just... a man deciding, each morning, to show up."

"That's the only kind worth having."

Aldric snorted and stood, pulling the hood back up. At the door he paused, hand on the bolt, and didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "my father's garden voice — the real one — told me once that the Silent God wasn't silent by nature. He said something was sitting on its mouth." His knuckles whitened on the wood. "I thought it was madness talking. Now I'm not sure my father's madness is better informed than his sanity."

He slipped out. The bolt fell. Dominic stood in the dim room listening to the dye-works churn below, and told himself the cold in his gut was just the hour.

It wasn't. Because Aldric's words had done what three days of dread couldn't: they'd made the shrine real, made the choice real, made the moment — coming soon, coming fast — when he would have to kneel one last time, not to swear but to surrender, and find out what answered when he finally let go.

Behind him, in the bed, Wren stirred and sat up in the dark, the stolen ledger sliding from her arms.

"Dominic," she whispered. "Wake Aldric. Turn around. Wake *everyone*. The book — I hid the prophecy book under the floorboards before the feast, and it's—"

She lifted the loose plank. The cavity beneath was empty, except for a single white feather that had no business existing a hundred feet underground.

"It knows we're coming."
