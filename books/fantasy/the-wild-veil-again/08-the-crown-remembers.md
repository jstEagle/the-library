## 8. The Crown Remembers

They took her in the scriptorium, of all places.

Wren had gone there for ink — a small, stupid errand, the kind of thing you did when your hands needed to feel ordinary. The undercity was three levels below, Dominic was watching the drainage grate they'd come up through, and she had maybe a quarter hour before he started worrying in that silent way of his. Enough time. She'd told herself it was enough time.

The court mage's people didn't grab her. That was the first wrongness. They simply appeared around her, four of them, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no, and one of them held out an open palm as if asking for a dance.

"Lady Halloway," he said. "His Eminence requests the pleasure."

She considered the window. Three floors. She considered the knife at her ankle. Four of them, and the door already closing behind a fifth.

"His Eminence can request," Wren said, and went.

* * *

Veyran Ashgrave kept his audience chamber like a man kept a wound: clean bandages over something that would not stop bleeding. No guards inside. Books everywhere — real ones, older than anything in the royal archive, shelved without system or care, spines cracked, pages dog-eared by hands that loved them badly. A brazier burned green, and the light did something unpleasant to his face, hollowing the cheeks, deepening sockets that were already too deep for a man who couldn't have been past fifty.

"Wren Halloway." He said her name the way scholars say a citation. "Exiled from the Collegium for the crime of being right about the succession crisis eight years early. You wrote a paper. I read it. I disagreed with everything except your central thesis, which was correct, which is why I've had people looking for you since."

"You found me because I let myself be found," she lied.

"Of course." He gestured to a chair. She didn't take it. "You're here about the king."

"I'm here because your men abducted me from a public street."

"And I'm here to show you something no living person outside this room has seen in two hundred years." He crossed to the shelves and drew out a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth, handling it the way other men handled infants or explosives. "You came here carrying a prophecy book. Don't bother denying it; the feast was watched, and the entity felt your friend's mark through forty feet of marble. I know what you know, Halloway. I know what's eating him."

Wren kept her face still. It cost her. "Then you know we're trying to stop it."

"I know you're trying to *save* him." Veyran set the book down on the table between them but did not open it. His fingers rested on the cover like a priest's on a relic. "That's the difference between us. I'm trying to save the *crown*."

"The crown is the problem."

"The crown is the *solution*," Veyran said, and opened the book.

What spilled out was not writing. Not at first. The pages were covered in pressed gold leaf worked into shapes that resolved the longer she looked — diagrams, genealogies, a spiral of names descending page after page, each name struck through in silver. And threaded through the gold, in a hand that shook with either ecstasy or terror:

*We made it to last forever. We made it so the kingdom would never again bury a child-king in a cold spring. We made it hungry because hunger is faithful. Hunger does not forget its purpose.*

"That's Aldric's handwriting," Wren said slowly. "No. No, that's *older*. That's—"

"Aldren the First. Yes." Veyran turned a page. "Founder of the dynasty. And its first architect of survival. Sit down, Lady Halloway. You'll want to be sitting."

She sat. Not because he asked. Because her knees decided independently.

* * *

The book told it plainly, which somehow made it worse. Great horrors preferred embellishment; this was just procedure, annotated in margins by six generations of court mages.

Aldren the First had been a king with enemies and no heirs old enough to defend themselves. So he had done what kings do — reached for power — except he'd reached further than most. There had been a war in heaven once, the margins explained, a war between gods, and one god had lost so completely it was cast down voiceless, nameless, stripped even of silence's dignity. Aldren had found the fragments of that defeat. And instead of destroying them, he had forged them — hammered divine ruin into a circlet of gold, bound it with oaths of permanence, and set it upon his own head.

It could not be stolen, because it knew its wearer. It could not be outlived, because it did not permit itself to be outlived — it sustained the body, fed the mind, kept the king *king* indefinitely. All it asked in return was a meal.

A soul. Slowly. Over years. Each heir crowned, each heir consumed, while the entity wore their shape and ruled in their name, growing more practiced at being human, more patient, more certain that it *was* the kingdom, that the kingdom existed to feed it.

"It doesn't kill them quickly," Veyran said quietly. He had stopped sounding like a scholar somewhere around the third page and started sounding like a man reciting his own confession. "It savors. Your current king has been dying since his coronation twenty-two years ago. Aldric is next. And then whoever follows, and whoever follows *them*, forever, until the world ends or someone ends the arrangement."

"Someone can end it." Wren's voice came out flat. "There's a rite. Unbinding. It requires—"

"An oath-bearer willing to surrender the bond entirely. Yes. I know. I helped write the modern transcription." He closed the book. "And now you understand my position. If you unbind the crown-entity, the god's voice returns — the Silent God, whole and furious and two centuries starved. What do you imagine a god does first, waking inside the capital of the kingdom that imprisoned it? What do you imagine happens to the succession, to the throne, to every institution built on the lie that the crown protects?"

"The alternative is feeding Aldric to it."

"The alternative," Veyran said, "is *continuity*. I have served five kings. I have watched each one hollow and die and be replaced by something wearing his smile, and I have kept the realm stable through every transition, because stability is the only mercy I can offer these people. Break the cycle and you break the realm. Maintain the cycle and you condemn one boy at a time to be eaten. Choose."

He said *choose* like a man laying cards on a table, and Wren realized, with a cold clarity that felt almost like laughter, that he genuinely believed he was offering her a fair bargain. That he had spent decades rationing horror into manageable portions and calling it wisdom.

"You keep saying *the realm*," she said. "I keep hearing *it*. The thing wearing the crown. You've been keeping it comfortable. Well-fed. You transcribed its unbinding rite and then buried it where no oath-bearer would ever find it, and you call that mercy."

"I call it the lesser grief."

"I call it collaboration." She stood. Her legs held. "Every heir. Every one of them walked to that throne believing they were inheriting a kingdom, and it was a dinner plate. And men like you polished the silver."

Something moved behind Veyran's eyes — not anger. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own indictment read aloud in words he'd forbidden himself.

"The prophecy book," he said. "Give it to me, and walk out of here alive. The rite alone won't save your mercenary. An oath-bearer needs the full sequence, and the full sequence exists in exactly one copy now that I've taken yours."

Wren's blood went very quiet.

"You don't have it," she said. "You never had it. You wanted to know if I'd trade it."

"I want to know what kind of scholar you are."

"The kind who memorizes," Wren said.

And she had. Three nights in the undercity with the prophecy book open on her knees, copying the unbinding sequence into memory the way her mother had taught her — rhythm and rhyme, the oldest mnemonic, the one no confiscation could touch. She recited nothing now. She simply smiled at Veyran Ashgrave, and watched him understand, and watched the understanding curdle into something like fear.

"Guards," he said.

* * *

They should have searched her. That was their mistake, and hers to exploit: scholars are dismissed as harmless, and harmless people carry knives nobody checks. She used the ankle blade on the second guard's belt rather than his throat, dropped the brazier's green fire across the oilcloth-wrapped genealogy as she passed — let Veyran choose between pursuing her and saving his confession — and went out the window she'd priced earlier, three floors onto the awning of a chandler's shop, then into the labyrinth of laundry lines and rain gutters that the palace district wore over its bones like a disguise.

She ran until the shouting faded. Then she ran until her lungs objected. Then she walked, fast, through streets that were beginning to notice coronation morning approaching like weather.

Dominic was where she'd left him, and his face when he saw hers told her everything his mouth wouldn't say.

"They took the book," she said. "But I have the rite. All of it. In here." She tapped her temple. "Veyran showed me the rest — the origin. The first king built the thing, Dominic. Deliberately. To make rule immortal. Every heir since has been its meal, and the court mage has been setting the table for two hundred years."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. In the east, above the palace, the sky had begun to pale toward a dawn that would end in a coronation.

"He offered you a deal," Dominic said. It wasn't a question.

"He offered me continuity. One boy at a time, forever, in exchange for a stable kingdom." Wren heard her own voice shake and let it. "I told him where he could file his continuity."

"Good." He said it simply, without heat, and started walking. "Then we do it today. Before the crowning. Where's the shrine from here?"

"Under the old temple. Half a mile. But Dominic—" She caught his sleeve. "Once we start the rite, there's no version where you keep the bond. You understand that. Whatever the Silent God gives back to you at the end — it won't be what you carried into the valley all those years. You'll be unmaking the last piece of your family."

He looked at her then, and she saw that he had already grieved this, sometime in the night, alone, and arrived on the far side of it.

"My family's inheritance was a leash," he said. "Time to see what's on the other end of it."

Above them, bells began to ring — not the coronation peal, too early for that, too fast, too many towers at once — and the crowd noise shifted from festival murmur to something with fear in it.

Wren grabbed his arm. "That's not the schedule. Something's happening at the palace."

Dominic was already running.
