## 9. Unbinding

The doors of the throne room were gold, and they were screaming.

Not the hinges — the metal itself, a high thin keel that only men with god-marks could hear. Dominic felt it in his teeth as he climbed the last stair, Wren's rite rolled tight against his palm like a second skin. Behind him the palace burned in patches; ahead of him, through those gilded doors, something ancient was finishing its meal.

"They'll have sealed the hall," Wren said beside him, breath ragged from the run. Her sleeve was scorched where the court mage's fire had grazed her. She hadn't slowed once.

"Good," Dominic said. "I'd hate to do this in front of an audience."

He put his shoulder to the doors and pushed, and the screaming metal gave way like old ice.

* * *

The throne room had been beautiful once. He knew it from coins and stained glass — the vaulted ceiling painted with the first king's ascent, the banners of nine generations hanging in ranks. Now the banners hung black, every thread of color drunk out of them, and the painted ceiling wept. Not paint. Wax. The whole ceiling was running slow and golden, dripping down the walls like the inside of a candle.

And on the throne sat the king, wearing a crown of light that did not belong to any kingdom Dominic had ever sworn to protect.

The entity had stopped pretending. The old man's body was there — thin, dignified even now — but the thing behind his eyes filled them edge to edge, and when it turned its gaze on Dominic, the air went thick as drowned honey. It wore the king's face the way a hand wears a glove: well-fitted, and not the hand.

"The oath-bearer," it said, and the voice came from everywhere at once, from the wax and the walls and the wet gold of the doors behind them. "You came after all. I did offer you power."

"You offered me slavery with better food." Dominic walked forward. Every step cost him. The thing's attention was a weight on his chest, pressing the breath shallow. "I'm here to make you a different offer."

"There are no offers left for you, little marked one. There is only the feast." The crown flared. "Your god is in my belly. Your valley was my larder door. And you—" the entity smiled with the king's cracked lips "—you are the last crumb of it."

Dominic kept walking. Ten paces from the throne, he stopped, because Wren had told him to stop, because the rite needed distance and stillness and a man who would not flinch. He knelt. Stone cold through his trousers. He set the unrolled page flat before him and pressed his palm over the first sigil, the one shaped like a closed mouth.

"I don't need offers," he said. "I need an opening. And you made one the day you swallowed my god and called it silence."

He began to speak the words.

* * *

They came out of him rough, half-learned, a language he'd heard only twice — once in the catacombs from Wren's careful mouth, once in a dream he refused to describe to her. But the sigils didn't care about accent. Under his palm the ink began to glow, faint as banked coals, and the closed-mouth symbol opened, line by line, into a shape like a throat. Like a door.

The entity screamed, and this time it screamed alone, without the walls.

"Stop." The king's body rose off the throne, joints moving wrong, puppet-smooth. "Oath-bearer. You misunderstand what you unbind. Your god was *weak*. That is why I could take it. Weakness dressed up as virtue, silence dressed up as peace — I am offering you what it never could. Strength. Voice. A name that kingdoms fear."

The words slid against Dominic's mind like oil against stone. He knew this cadence. It was the same voice that had lived in his oath all along, he realized — the quiet promise beneath the quiet: *keep faith, keep silent, keep small, and be safe.* The oath had never asked him to serve. It had asked him to hide. And hiding had a master too.

"No," Dominic said, and kept speaking.

The entity crossed the floor in three strides that covered thirty feet. Its hand — the king's hand, spotted and trembling with age or with rage — closed around the back of Dominic's neck, and cold poured in. Not pain exactly. *Unmaking.* His memories of the valley went soft at their edges. The smell of rain on the oath-house roof. His mother's hands teaching him the knot of the vow-cord. The taste of the bread Wren had baked badly on their first night beyond the veil.

It was eating him already. Starting with the parts that made him *him.*

Through the roar in his skull he heard Wren shout his name, heard her boots on stone, and he wanted to tell her to stay back — but his mouth was busy with the rite, and maybe that was mercy. He poured everything into the words instead. The third sigil. The fourth. The shape of a key turning in a lock no one had oiled in a thousand years.

And somewhere beneath the cold, beneath the theft, something *answered.*

* * *

It was not a voice, not at first. It was a pressure change, like weather. The cold in Dominic's neck met something colder and older and *other*, and the two of them recognized each other across the entity's grip the way two exiles recognize a homeland's accent.

Then, faintly, worn smooth as a river stone:

***Let go.***

Dominic nearly lost the rite. After a lifetime of silence — his whole life, his family's whole history, generations of kneeling to a god who never spoke, never answered, never gave so much as a dream in return — the Silent God was speaking, and its first word was permission.

***You were never mine to keep,*** the voice said, and each word cost it visibly; Dominic felt the effort as heat against his skin. ***The oath was a wall I built around the last of myself. You were the brick that remembered it was a brick. I am sorry. I could never tell you. Telling you would have fed it faster.***

"So all of it," Dominic gasped, still speaking the rite, tears cutting through the sweat on his face. "The valley. The silence. My whole life on my knees—"

***Was a prison I made you guard,*** the god said. ***From the inside. With me.*** A pause, vast and tired. ***You were the best of what I touched. Do not kneel to me again. Kneeling is how it eats.***

The entity's grip tightened. "Lies," it thundered, in a dozen voices at once — kings dead a century, queens, heirs, all the meals it had taken. "*I* am what endures. I am every reign since the first—"

"Yes," Wren said, arriving at Dominic's shoulder, blood on her temple, the stolen binding-rite open in both hands. "That's rather the problem." She slapped her palm down beside his on the glowing page. "Finish it, Dominic. Last four lines. Say them like you mean to survive them."

"I don't mean to survive them," he said honestly.

"Even better."

* * *

He said the last lines.

He offered the bond — not broke it, *offered*, held it out flat between himself and his god like a man returning a borrowed blade. And the Silent God, imprisoned, devoured, diminished to a single word's worth of strength, took it with something that was almost tenderness.

***Released,*** it said. ***Freely given, freely returned. Go be a man, Dominic Reyes. It was always the better shape.***

The bond tore free.

There was no other word for it. Something that had lived behind his sternum since before memory ripped loose, and Dominic screamed, and the scream was grief and relief in equal measure, because he could feel it going — the valley-sense, the god-warmth, the certainty that someone, *something*, stood behind him. All the architecture of his identity, dismantled in a heartbeat. He was suddenly, entirely, only a man. A mercenary with a sword and a scarred neck, kneeling in a burning room.

And the bond, freed, struck the crown-entity like dawn striking a candle.

The entity let go of his neck to claw at the light pouring out of the sigils, out of Dominic's emptied chest, out of every oath ever sworn by every bearer who died within sight of the throne — because that was the trap, Wren had found it, that was the whole ugly inheritance: the god had been feeding its captives' bonds into the lock for a thousand years, waiting for one bearer who would give it *willingly*. Power freely surrendered could not be eaten. It could only be spent.

It spent itself now, all at once, gloriously.

The crown of light cracked down the middle with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat. Inside it the entity thrashed — a shape with too many faces, all of them royal, all of them starving — and then the faces peeled away one by one, each dissolving with what might have been relief, and the last face was the first king's, and it looked almost surprised before it came apart into gold dust and was wind.

The wax ceiling hardened. The banners bled color back into themselves like wounds closing. And the king — the actual man, the father, hollowed and used for decades — folded gently off the throne and would have hit the stone if Dominic hadn't caught him, arms full of a stranger who weighed nothing anymore.

For a moment, neither of them moved. The throne room was very quiet. Somewhere far below, water dripped in the catacombs, ordinary as anything.

"Is he alive?" Wren asked softly, kneeling beside them, fingers at the king's throat.

A pause. Then, under her fingertips: a pulse. Slow, stubborn, human.

The king opened his eyes. They were just eyes now — clouded, frightened, ninety years old and finally only that. He looked up at Dominic, at the man holding him, and his mouth worked.

"Who..." he whispered. "Who won?"

Dominic thought about it. The oath was gone. The god was gone, or scattered, or sleeping wherever freed things go. He had spent his whole life as a servant of a silence, and the silence had turned out to be a scream muffled for a millennium, and he had been its lock, and now he was nothing but free.

"Nobody," he said. "Everybody. Ask me again tomorrow."

The king laughed — a dry rustle, but real — and slept.

Wren sat back on her heels. Soot-striped, singed, prophecy-bookless, she looked at Dominic for a long moment, and then she started to laugh too, helplessly, the wild laugh of a woman who had carried a century-old book into a burning palace and walked out with a king instead.

"You're oathless," she said. "Do you understand what that means? No god, no valley, no chain. The whole world, Dominic Reyes. What will you do with it?"

Outside, through the shattered windows, dawn was coming up over a city that no longer smelled of wax and hunger. Magic moved in the air like weather turning mild. And far away, past the hills, a valley that had been sealed for a thousand years was, at this very hour, quietly beginning to breathe.

Dominic looked at the sunrise, then at her, and found the answer needed no oath at all.

"Ask me again tomorrow," he said.
