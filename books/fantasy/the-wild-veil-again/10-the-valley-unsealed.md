## 10. The Valley, Unsealed

The valley had a smell now.

Dominic noticed it before he crossed the old boundary — green and wet and faintly sweet, like rain on stone that had never been rained on. For thirty years the air past the marker-stones had smelled of nothing. That was the thing about the dead zone no one told you: it wasn't emptiness you felt, it was absence, a held breath, a room with the window painted shut. He had grown up inside that held breath and called it peace.

Now he stood at the tree line where the veil had been, and the air moved.

*You could still turn back,* said some old reflex in him, the voice of every oath-bearer who'd walked this ground before him. *The valley is yours by inheritance. It was bought for you.*

That was exactly why he kept walking.

Wren was ahead of him, boots muddy to the knee, hair escaping its braid in gray-streaked wisps, muttering measurements into a battered slate as she paced out the boundary line. She'd been pacing it for three days. Every few yards she'd stop, tap the slate, frown, and pace back.

"The flow isn't uniform," she said without looking up when he reached her. "It's strongest along the old riverbed. Which makes sense if you think about what we know now — the crown-entity anchored itself through ley-lines, and your god's prison sat on top of the deepest one. When you broke it, everything the entity had been siphoning for nine hundred years came back down the channel all at once." She glanced up, and there was something careful in her face, the way there always was lately when she looked at him. "How's the head?"

"Loud," Dominic said. "But good loud."

It was true, mostly. Where the silence had lived behind his sternum since childhood — the flat, obedient nothing of the oath-bond — there was now a low hum, ambient and undirected. Magic didn't speak to him the way the Silent God once had. It simply existed around him, indifferent as weather. He was learning to be weathered by it instead of commanded.

He hadn't realized, until it was gone, how much of his life had been listening for orders.

* * *

The king recovered slowly, and everyone pretended not to be surprised.

Dominic had seen him once more before leaving the capital — a thin man propped against pillows in a sunlit chamber, blinking at the world like a man waking from a long fever, which was precisely what he'd been having for forty years. He had taken Dominic's hand in both of his own, felt the calluses of a mercenary's grip, and said, "You gave up your god for me. I don't know how to repay that."

"You don't," Dominic had said. "Give it to someone else."

He'd meant it lightly. The king hadn't taken it lightly at all. Neither had Aldric.

The prince — regent now, though he refused the title until his father died naturally, whenever that might finally come — had spent the weeks since the unbinding tearing apart everything the crown had touched. The succession law was first: no longer would rule pass automatically down a bloodline the entity had been farming for generations. Aldric had proposed, to the shock of half the court, that the throne pass to a council drawn by lot from among the guilds, provinces, and temples, with the royal family holding only a ceremonial seat.

"An inherited crown is a meal waiting to happen," Aldric had told them over wine the night before they left, tired-eyed, younger-looking than his years. "My family proved that. Nine hundred years of proving it. Let's try something that can't be eaten."

Wren had argued with him until midnight — not about the principle but about the mechanics, because Wren believed every good idea deserved to be interrogated until it confessed its flaws — and somewhere in the argument Dominic had watched her laugh for the first time since the catacombs, sharp and sudden, and had thought: *oh.* Not a revelation. Just a door he'd been standing beside for weeks, finally noticing the handle.

* * *

They stayed three days at the boundary, then two more, then Wren stopped pretending they were leaving soon and set up camp properly, with a cookfire and a drying line for her ink-stained sleeves.

The valley was changing faster than anyone had predicted. Magic didn't rush back; it seeped, gentle as meltwater. Grass grew in the dead fields in colors the farmers' grandparents had names for. A spring that had run cold for a century now ran warm and tasted faintly of iron. Children born in the valley's last generation — children who had never once seen anything impossible — were doing small, harmless, astonishing things: making moths land on their fingers, finding lost coins, dreaming true dreams.

Some families welcomed it. Some packed their carts. Dominic understood both.

"It'll take a decade before anyone knows what this place is," Wren said one evening, feet toward the fire, slate balanced on her knees. "A decade, maybe two, before the Crown Council figures out what to do with a population that can suddenly do this." She wiggled her fingers and produced, with visible effort, a small blue flame that guttered above her palm. She'd been practicing. She was terrible at it. "There will be panic. There's always panic."

"There will be opportunists," Dominic said. "There are always those too."

"Mm." She let the flame die and looked at him across the fire. "And which will you be?"

He considered the question honestly, because she'd earned honesty. "I don't know yet," he said. "For the first time in my life, nobody's told me what I am. Mercenary. Oath-bearer. Vessel. Key. I keep waiting for the next assignment to arrive." He turned his hands over, studying palms that bore no mark now, no brand, nothing but work. "It doesn't arrive. It's strange how much space that leaves."

"And what will you fill it with?"

"I had an idea," he said. "It involves staying near a stubborn scholar who insists on walking into ley-flows without a ward."

Wren snorted. "That scholar outranks you."

"She does," Dominic agreed. "I've checked."

She looked at him then — really looked, the way she looked at texts, reading for what was underneath — and whatever she found made her go quiet, and go pink, and look back at the fire. "The valley needs documenting," she said, gruff. "Years of documentation. Someone has to record how magic re-establishes in a sealed region, or every idiot with a theory will write their own version, and most of them will be wrong and some of them will be dangerous."

"That sounds like a job for years."

"It is."

"Years," Dominic repeated. "In one place."

"In one place," she confirmed, chin up, daring him to find it funny.

He didn't find it funny. He found it, unexpectedly, like setting down a pack after a long march — the specific ache of weight leaving shoulders that had forgotten they were carrying anything.

"I never chose anything before," he said. "I was born into an oath I didn't understand, spent my life guarding a silence that turned out to be a scream, and broke it because there was no other way. None of it was mine. This—" he gestured at the fire, the darkening valley, her face, "—this would be mine. Chosen. Do you understand the difference?"

"Yes," Wren said softly. "I'm exiled, remember. Everything I have, I chose after losing everything I didn't choose." She fed a branch to the fire. "It's the only kind of loyalty worth having. The kind you could take back and don't."

* * *

On the last morning of autumn, they walked together to the old oath-house.

It stood empty now, doors open, dust sheets over the furniture. The last of the valley's elders had left for the coast a week prior, unable to live in a world where the god they'd served was gone and the silence they'd trusted had been revealed as a wound. Dominic didn't blame them. Grief takes many shapes, and some people had built their whole lives inside the shape of that silence.

In the library, the prophecy book lay open on the table where he'd left it weeks ago — a lifetime ago. *Dominic Reyes,* it said. *On this night the veil breaks.* The ink had faded to ordinary brown. Whatever had animated it, whether the entity's long reach or his god's last flicker, it was inert now. Just paper. Just history.

He closed it gently, like closing a door on the dead, and carried it outside.

Wren waited in the yard. Beyond her, the valley spread out under a clean gray sky — terraced fields going green at the edges, the warm spring catching light, a hawk riding a thermal that shouldn't exist over land this recently alive. Somewhere downslope, children were shouting, and one of their shouts had a shimmer to it that wasn't entirely sound.

"They'll need guards eventually," Wren said. "Not mercenaries. Wardens, maybe. People who know what it costs to serve something, so they can teach others not to."

"People who know the difference between devotion and dinner," Dominic said.

"Your words will look very impressive carved over a door."

He laughed — really laughed, the sound surprising him with its ease — and handed her the book. "First entry in the new archive."

She took it, weighed it, and tucked it under her arm like the ordinary object it had finally become. Then she nodded down the slope, toward the valley floor, toward whatever was growing there.

"Well, warden," she said. "Shall we see what it becomes?"

Dominic Reyes, oathless, unmarked, belonging to no god and no crown and no inheritance but the ones he'd pick himself, followed her down into the living valley — and for the first time in his life, every step was his own.
