## 3. The Thirteenth Notch

Wren had counted the boundary stones eleven times before, and every time there had been twelve notches.

They were certain of this the way they were certain of anything: by having written it down. That was the first heresy of Wren's short life, though they hadn't known to call it that yet — the habit, learned at their grandmother's knee before the Temple took them in, of keeping a little book of things noticed. Grandmother Ashen had called it *paying attention*, as if noticing were a form of currency. The novices called it *Wren's nonsense* and left it alone, which suited Wren fine. A ledger no one else reads is the only truly free book in the valley.

The entry from last spring, in Wren's cramped hand: *Westmarch stone, twelfth notch worn shallow but whole. Counted twice.*

And now, kneeling in the wet grass with their breath fogging in the grey of an autumn dawn, they looked at a thirteenth.

It was fresh. That was the thing that made Wren's stomach turn over — not the number itself, which could be argued with, explained away, attributed to some elder's ceremonial addition or a bored shepherd's knife — but the *freshness*. The twelve original notches were ancient, polished smooth by generations of touching fingers and weather, cut deep into the granite in a style so old the stonemasons' guild had no record of it. They looked like scars. The thirteenth notch looked like a wound. Pale stone showed through where the lichen had been cut away, and the edges still held a fine grit of dust, as if the cutting had happened within days. Within hours, perhaps.

The stone itself was older than the Temple. Everyone knew that. The Quiet Ledger's founding texts said the stones predated the covenant, raised by "those who came before," and that the twelve notches marked the twelve turns of the sealing moon — the great working, three generations past, that had closed the valley against the wild realm and its hungry gods. Twelve moons, twelve notches, twelve oath-bearers who had paid the price of peace. It was the first lesson every acolyte memorized, before even the alphabet of accounts:

*Twelve is complete. Twelve is safe. Anything beyond twelve is debt uncounted, and debt uncounted is the door through which ruin walks.*

So what was a thirteenth notch doing on the Westmarch stone, cut clean and new, while the elders sang the morning office forty paces behind Wren, their voices rising smooth as poured milk?

Wren did not touch it. Some instinct older than training kept their fingers curled back. Instead they took out their little book, licked the tip of their pencil-stub, and drew the notch — carefully, exactly, counting the chisel-marks along its edge (seven, uneven, made by someone or something working fast). Then, because a good observer records conditions, they wrote beneath it:

*Cut since spring. No tool marks consistent with guild work. Lichen regrowth absent. Smell of rain where there has been no rain.*

That last one was the detail that frightened them most. The air around the stone smelled like the first minute of a storm — that green, metallic, waiting smell — though the sky above the valley rim was the flat pewter of a dry morning, and had been for nine days. The wells were low. The farmers muttered about it. And here, at the very edge of everything, the stone smelled of downpour.

Behind them, the office ended. Wren heard the shuffle of vestments, the creak of Elder Coram's bad knee, and made the quick decision of someone who has already crossed a line and might as well walk a little further: they stayed kneeling, head bowed, as if in devotion.

"Wren." Coram's voice, mild as ever. Mildness was the old man's armor; he wore it over whatever else he was the way other men wore mail. "You are meant to be inside the circle for the blessing, not outside it."

"Forgive me, Elder. I stopped to study the stonework."

"The stonework." A pause. Wren kept their eyes down and watched Coram's sandals come into view at the edge of their vision, toes pointing toward the stone. Toward the notch. "There is nothing to study. The stones are accounted for."

"Accounted for," Wren repeated. Not a question — questions invited lessons, and Wren had learned to offer statements instead, small mirrors that made people show you their faces. "Yes, Elder. Twelve notches each."

Something shifted in the silence. Wren felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before weather: the mildness thinning.

"You have been counting," Coram said.

"I count everything, Elder. It's my flaw. The Novice Master says I'll count the hairs on a corpse at my own funeral."

Another silence, longer. When Coram spoke again, his voice had resumed its smoothness, but Wren heard the seam now, the place where the words had been stitched shut. "Then count your blessings instead, child. Come. Breakfast, and then copywork. The Ledger does not keep itself."

He walked on. He had not looked at the stone. Wren stayed kneeling until all the grey robes had filed back through the mist toward the temple road, and then they looked up at the Westmarch stone again, at its twelve old scars and its one new wound, and wrote one more line in the little book before closing it:

*Elder Coram did not look. You do not avoid looking at a thing unless you already know what it looks like.*

* * *

The problem with being a skeptic in a house of faith, Wren reflected that afternoon over a cold supper of barley and beans, is that everyone assumes your doubt is a phase, like measles or piety. Nobody takes it seriously enough to lie to you well. They just smile and pat your head and say *you'll understand when you're older*, which is either a promise or a threat depending on the day.

But today nobody smiled. Today something else was happening, and Wren had been watching it gather since dawn.

First: Brother Aldous, whose job it was to inspect the boundary stones each season — a duty so routine it was practically a joke among the acolytes, *Aldous and his rocks* — had been summoned to the High Scribe's chambers before breakfast and had not emerged. His inspection reports, Wren knew, were due tomorrow.

Second: the copying roster had changed. Wren checked it twice against their memory of yesterday's board, because the change was small and precisely aimed: Wren's assigned section for the week had been moved from the outer ledgers — tithes, harvests, the ordinary arithmetic of the valley — to the deep archive. Cataloguing only. No transcription. The note in Elder Coram's hand added, almost as an afterthought: *Catalogue numbers only. Do not read.*

Do not read. To a scribe-in-training, those three words were not an instruction; they were a lit fuse. Every text in the archive wanted reading. That was what texts were *for*. And the deep archive was where the Temple kept what it did not want read — the pre-covenant records, the sealed annals, the books bound in grey leather with no titles. Wren had catalogued there once before, two years ago, and remembered the particular quality of its silence, a hush like held breath.

Third: Sister Maren, who taught the youngest novices their letters, had told her class — Wren overheard it through the courtyard wall while pretending to sweep, a skill at which they excelled — that the story of the sealing would be postponed this term. "Postponed?" one of the little ones asked. "Why?"

"Because," Maren said, in the bright voice teachers use when they are choosing their words with pliers, "some stories are best told in their proper season."

The proper season. As if the story of how the valley was saved were a winter squash, ripening on some hidden vine.

Wren sat with their cold supper and laid the three facts out in their mind like coins on a table. Individually, explainable. Together, a pattern. And patterns, Wren had noticed, were what people built walls to hide — you didn't build a wall around a single coin; you built it around a hoard.

The question that had been circling them since dawn finally landed, and landed hard: *what did Aldous find?*

Because Aldous inspected the stones. All thirteen of them — no, all *twelve* of them, Wren corrected themselves, and the correction snagged like a burr in wool — walking the valley rim twice a year with his tally-book and his little hammer for testing soundness. If a notch had appeared on the Westmarch stone, Aldous would have found it. Unless he'd found it before Wren did. Unless he'd found it weeks ago and reported it, and the report had gone up the chain like blood through water, and everything Wren was watching today — the closed doors, the shuffled roster, the postponed stories — was the ripple.

Which meant the elders had known before dawn. Which meant the elders had known, and had chosen silence, and silence from the Temple of the Quiet Ledger was never empty. Silence here was an entry in an account. It always balanced against something.

Wren finished the beans without tasting them.

* * *

They went back at dusk, alone, breaking the curfew they'd never once broken in four years as an acolyte. This was the vertigo part — not the notch, not even the lying, but this: discovering that the rules had always had exceptions, and the exceptions had names, and none of the names were theirs. Wren had been a believer in the way a bird is a believer in the branch — total, unthinking, structural. You did not examine the branch. You stood on it. And now, with the ground suddenly visible far below through gaps they'd never noticed, the whole tree swayed.

The evening mist was coming up off the fields as Wren climbed the west path, the little book in their sleeve like a smuggled coal. They told themselves they were going to verify. That was the honest word for it. Verify: to make true by looking again. But underneath the honest word, another truth squirmed — they were going because if the elders were lying, then Wren needed to know what the world actually was, even if it was worse, *especially* if it was worse. Grandmother Ashen's voice, from memory: *"Better a hard truth than a soft floor, little wren. Soft floors are how cellars get you."*

The Westmarch stone rose out of the mist like a broken tooth.

Wren circled it once, slowly, checking the ground for footprints, for chips of granite, for any sign of the cutter. Nothing. The grass grew undisturbed right up to the stone's base — which was impossible, if a human hand had cut that notch. No ladder, no scaffolding, no crushed ring of stems. As if the notch had simply *arrived*, the way frost arrives, the way age arrives.

They put out their hand at last. Stopped a finger's width from the stone. The storm-smell was stronger now, and beneath it — faint, absurd, undeniable — something like ink. Fresh ink. The smell of a page newly written.

Wren touched the notch.

The world tilted.

Not physically — their feet stayed planted, the mist stayed mist — but something behind their eyes swung wide like a door caught by wind, and for half a heartbeat Wren saw the valley *doubled*: the same grey fields, the same dark line of temple roof, and over it, under it, through it, another valley — greener, stranger, moving with a slow light that came from everywhere and nowhere, hills breathing, a river running silver and singing—

Gone.

Wren sat down hard in the wet grass, heart slamming. Their nose was bleeding, a single thread of warm iron over their lip. On the stone, the thirteenth notch seemed to drink the last of the dusk.

"Notch thirteen," they whispered, tasting copper. "Turn thirteen." The lesson-chant surfaced unbidden, the one every acolyte recited at year's end: *twelve moons turned, the seal holds fast.* And the chant had never mentioned what happened after twelve. Because nothing was supposed to happen after twelve. Twelve was complete. Twelve was forever.

Except forever, apparently, had a thirteenth moon.

Wren wiped their nose, opened the little book with shaking hands, and wrote the only sentence that mattered, pressing hard enough to score the page beneath:

*The elders are not hiding a mistake. They are hiding a countdown.*

They were still staring at the words when they heard footsteps on the west path behind them — unhurried, heavy, familiar. Wren closed the book and turned, and their stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Brother Aldous stood at the edge of the mist, lantern in hand. He had been released from the High Scribe's chambers, then. Or let out. He looked at Wren — a long, strange look, grief and warning braided together — and then, deliberately, he looked at the stone.

"Don't count it again, lad," he said quietly. "Whatever you saw today — don't write it, don't say it, don't count it again. There are ears in the temple walls, and the High Scribe has begun burning."

"Burning," Wren repeated. "Burning *what*?"

Aldous lifted the lantern higher. In its light Wren saw, for the first time, that the old man's hands were bandaged — fresh burns wrapped in linen, the kind you got from pages pulled too fast from a fire.

"The margins," Aldous said, and turned back toward the temple road. "Come along, lad. Curfew's curfew. And Wren—" he paused, not looking back, his burned hands trembling faintly at his sides, "—whatever the scribes copied out before the flames took it... find out what they saved. Before the thirteenth turn finds us first."
