## 4. Ink and Interrogation

The inquisitor arrived on a Tuesday, which felt obscene. Disasters should come at night, in storms, with warning bells — not on a gray Tuesday morning while the valley smelled of bread and wet wool and the tithes still needed copying.

Jude heard him before they saw him: the sound of hooves that did not quite match each other, an arrhythmic striking against the temple cobbles, like a heart skipping. Then the horse itself, a tall black thing whose coat seemed to drink the light, and atop it a man dressed not in the temple's somber browns nor the court's gaudy colors but in charcoal-gray so finely cut it looked poured onto him rather than worn.

Halvard Vey dismounted before the Temple of the Quiet Ledger as though he had built it.

He was younger than Jude had imagined. Inquisitors came to the valley perhaps once a decade, and the ones from Jude's childhood had been old men with old men's smells — lamp oil and dried herbs and the sourness of long roads. Vey was perhaps forty, silver threading his dark hair at the temples in a way that looked deliberate, arranged. His face was the kind of face that made you want to confess things just to watch his expression change. And when he smiled at the High Scribe on the temple steps — a smile of perfect warmth — Jude, watching from the scriptorium window, felt the hair rise along their arms.

Because there was nothing behind the smile but arithmetic.

* * *

They were summoned at midday.

"Brother-Scribe Ashgrove." The acolyte who delivered the summons could barely meet Jude's eyes. "The Inquisitor requests your presence in the reading room. He asks for you *by name*, Jude." The honorific dropped away, and that was its own kind of terror. In the temple, names were currency, and no one spent them casually.

The reading room was the oldest chamber in the temple, older than the Ledger itself by local reckoning, though no one could say what had stood here before the sealing. Its shelves held the valley's accumulated memory: land grants going back four generations, marriage records, death records, the great tithe ledgers bound in calfskin and chained to their desks like dogs that might bolt. Light fell through high windows in pale rectangles, laying itself across the floor like pages waiting to be filled.

Vey stood among the shelves with one hand resting on a ledger spine, reading without opening the book. He'd somehow already learned the room's grammar — knew which volumes were chained, which were free, where the light was best for close work.

"Scribe Ashgrove." He turned, and up close his eyes were a color Jude couldn't fix — brown, or amber, or something between, shifting with the angle like oil on water. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. May I offer you tea? Your High Scribe makes an appalling cup, I'm afraid. All reverence and no patience."

"I don't take tea during working hours," Jude said, and sat anyway, because refusing twice would be defiance, and defiance this early would be suicide.

"Discipline." Vey nodded approvingly. "I've read your hand, incidentally. In the tithe ledgers. Exquisite work. There are maybe six scribes in the capital who form their terminals that cleanly, and none of them under thirty." He drew out a chair opposite Jude and sat, folding his hands. "You love the letters themselves. That's rare. Most scribes love what letters can buy them."

"They're all I have," Jude said, before thinking better of it.

Something flickered across Vey's face — not pity, worse, *recognition*. "Yes," he said softly. "I know."

He let the silence sit. It was a technique; Jude recognized it from the interrogation manuals they weren't supposed to have read, shelved in the restricted stacks, copied once out of pure forbidden curiosity. Let the subject speak first. Every word offered is a rope handed over.

Jude kept their hands folded and said nothing.

"You're wondering why I asked for you specifically." Vey produced, from inside his coat, a leather folio — plain, unmarked, the kind that carried documents too important or too damning for official seals. He set it on the table between them but did not open it. "Three weeks ago, a beggar named Odell Marsh prayed at your temple door for rain for his dying fields. Rain came. Not weather-rain — *answered* rain, warm, sweet-smelling, falling on six acres and nowhere else. Dozens witnessed it. Since then: a child's fever broken by a voice in the well. A lame woman dancing at the harvest fair. Lights over the boundary stones that moved like writing."

"The temple has filed its reports," Jude said carefully.

"The temple has filed *nonsense*." Vey's voice didn't rise at all, which made it worse. "Atmospheric anomalies. Mass suggestion. Seepage from the hot springs. Your High Scribe writes reports the way drowning men write wills — quickly, and hoping no one reads them closely." He leaned forward. "But I read closely. That's my entire function. I am a man who reads closely."

"And what do you read, Inquisitor?"

"In those reports?" He almost smiled. "Fear. But elsewhere—" He opened the folio at last, and turned it around so Jude could see.

Pages. Old pages, brittle and foxed, covered in a cramped antique hand. Jude's breath caught, because the handwriting was familiar — not from life but from *work*. They had spent years deciphering exactly this script in the margins of the master ledgers, the marginalia of the predecessor-scribe whose annotations threaded through three generations of records like roots through soil.

"Where did you get these?"

"The capital archive holds copies of everything your temple has ever burned." Vey said it mildly, terribly. "You did know that, didn't you? That burning a page in the Veiled Valley is not destruction but relocation? We keep a fire-archive, Scribe Ashgrove. Every heresy your valley has ever tried to forget, we remember *for* you. It's a kindness, really. You people carry so much."

Jude stared at the pages. Names. The same forgotten god-names Jude had found scratched into the ledger margins and copied out in secret two nights before the burning — *Ash-of-the-Threshold*, and others, dozens of others, each annotated in the old hand with dates and debts and small desperate observations. *Heard at the mill-pond. Asked only for the first loaf.* *Came to my mother in fever. Wept.*

"These were written by—"

"By Maren Ashgrove." Vey watched Jude the way a hawk watches a field mouse that has just realized the sky is empty. "Your grandmother. Final Scribe of the Quiet Ledger before the office passed to your current High Scribe's line. And — you'll know this part better than I do, perhaps — signatory of the original covenant. One of thirteen. The thirteenth, actually. The last to put ink to the sealing."

The room went very quiet. The light through the windows seemed to thin.

"My grandmother," Jude said, and their voice came out steady, which surprised them, "died before I was born."

"Yes. Which means she never told you." Vey tilted his head. "Never told you that she helped seal the gods out of this valley. Never told you that the covenant binding them expires — that it was always designed to expire, thirteen moon-turns of the great cycle, and that the cycle is ending *now*. Never told you why a family of scribes has been indentured to this temple for three generations, paying a debt no ledger actually records."

"There's a debt," Jude said. "It's in the founding records. My family owes—"

"Owes *what*, precisely?" Vey spread his hands. "Show me the entry. Show me the amount, the creditor, the terms. You're a scribe. You know how contracts work. A debt without terms isn't a debt — it's a *chain* someone forged and called accounting." His voice dropped lower, gentler, infinitely more dangerous. "I've read your grandmother's contract, Ashgrove. The true one. I know what she traded. Do you?"

Jude's blood-mark — the faint birthmark at their wrist, shaped like nothing, shaped like everything, the mark every Ashgrove carried — began, very faintly, to warm.

"No," Jude said.

"She signed away her bloodline. Not her goods, not her lands — those went to the other twelve. Her *blood*. As surety. As the hinge the seal would hang upon." Vey recited it like scripture he'd memorized for an exam. "While the covenant held, the Ashgroves served the Ledger and paid their unnamed debt and never questioned it. When it failed—" He paused, delicately. "Well. The seal needs a hinge, and hinges don't get to choose which door they hold shut."

"That's superstition," Jude said. "Old law-talk. No one's blood opens or closes anything."

"Then explain the rain, Scribe." For the first time Vey's composure cracked — not into anger but into something hungrier, and Jude understood suddenly that this man had crossed mountains on a mismatched-gaited horse because he *needed* this to be true. "Explain why miracles cluster around your valley like flies around a wound. Explain why the old names are surfacing in your margins after sixty years of silence. Explain why, three days ago, a moth landed on my window in the middle of a rainstorm and left wing-dust in the shape of a key."

The mark at Jude's wrist burned now, unmistakably, and Jude pressed it flat against the table and prayed Vey hadn't noticed.

He had noticed. Of course he had noticed. His eyes flicked down and back up with the speed of a card sharp.

"Here is what happens next," Vey said, rising, smoothing his coat. "In two days I begin formal interviews with every member of this temple. Those interviews will determine who aided heresy, who merely witnessed it, and who is innocent. Innocent persons return to their lives. Guilty ones come back to the capital with me." He gathered his folio, then paused, and looked down at Jude with that warm, arithmetical smile. "Or — you could spare us both the theater. You've already shown me you can keep your head when you're frightened; most people talk too much when they're frightened, but you go quiet and count exits. Good instincts. Bring me what your grandmother left in the margins. The complete names. The annotations. Everything you copied before the burning."

Jude's stomach dropped through the floor. "I don't know what you mean."

"Don't." Vey held up one finger, gently. "Please. Ink transfers, Ashgrove. Your right index finger has had graphite under the nail for two days. Your desk in the scriptorium faces the window; anyone with a spyglass and patience can watch a person copy pages by lamplight and note which book they hide them in. I've had patience since I was twelve years old." He straightened his cuffs. "You have until the interviews begin. If the pages appear on my desk anonymously, I'll assume a loyal scribe recovered them from a heretic predecessor's effects. If they don't, I'll assume the heresy is *current*, and hereditary."

He walked to the door, then stopped, hand on the frame, and spoke without turning around.

"One more thing, since no one in this valley will tell you the truth. Your grandmother didn't sign that contract because she believed in the sealing. I've read her private correspondence. She signed it because the gods gave her no choice — because one of them came to her in the night and begged her to, weeping, saying the others would devour the world if they weren't caged. She spent the rest of her life trying to find a flaw in her own ink." Now he did turn, and his eyes were steady and terrible. "She failed. Perhaps you'll do better. Or perhaps you'll simply burn like everyone else who plays with old fire."

* * *

The reading room held Jude for a long moment after the door closed, the way deep water holds a stone.

Then Jude walked back through the cloister, past the chapel, past the acolytes rehearsing evening chants that suddenly sounded like lullabies sung to keep children from looking under the bed, and climbed to the scriptorium, and locked the door, and knelt, and pulled the loose floorboard beneath the desk.

The packet was there. Fourteen pages in Jude's own careful, terminal-perfect hand — the names, the annotations, the dates, the debts. Grandmother Maren's secret history of a valley's stolen gods, copied in three nights of candle-burning fear.

Jude had planned to destroy them. Had planned it for days, turning the plan over like a coin: feed the pages to the stove, let the fire-archive of the capital keep its copies, stay small, stay safe, stay a scribe with clean terminals and no history.

But Vey had said *hinge*, and the mark on Jude's wrist was still warm, and somewhere beyond the boundary stones a beggar's prayer had been answered by a voice made of rain.

Jude sat back on their heels and looked at the packet, and understood — with the cold clarity of a person reading the final line of a contract — that there was no version of the coming days in which these pages stayed hidden. Either they went to Vey, and Jude became his instrument against whatever was waking. Or they went elsewhere.

And *elsewhere*, Jude realized, had just knocked at their window.

On the glass, in the gray afternoon light, a moth clung — wings dusted pale gold, folded in a shape Jude had seen drawn once, in margin-ink, beside a name: *Ash-of-the-Threshold, keeper of doors, who asks only to be remembered.*

The moth waited. The packet lay in Jude's hands.

Outside, far off toward the boundary stones, thunder gathered where there had been no clouds at dawn — and it sounded, if you listened the way a scribe listens, less like weather than like a voice clearing its throat to speak.
