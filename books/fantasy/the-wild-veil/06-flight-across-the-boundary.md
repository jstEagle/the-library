## 6. Flight Across the Boundary

The knock came at the third hour past midnight, and Jude had been awake to hear it.

Not that it was meant for their ears. It was a soft knock, almost courteous — three raps on the door of the scriptorium's lower archive, where Vey's men had spent the evening turning Jude's life inside out like a purse shaken for coins. Jude sat in the narrow cell they'd been given, not locked in, merely *left*, which was somehow worse. The temple had no dungeons. The Quiet Ledger did not deal in punishment; it dealt in accounts. And Jude's account, as of sundown, had been declared insolvent.

They heard Halvard Vey's voice through the wall, low and pleasant, the voice of a man discussing the weather over someone's grave. "No, leave the copies. I want them catalogued before dawn. Every name. Cross-referenced against the marginalia in the master ledger." A pause. "And find out who taught the Ashgrove child to write so small. That hand is deliberate. Deliberation is intent."

Jude's copied names were folded against their breastbone, sewn into the lining of their coat — thirty-one god-names in ink that had taken them four nights of stolen candlelight, each letter small enough to hide beneath a thumbnail. Vey had found *other* pages. The ones Jude hadn't gotten to. Pages that still said what Jude's had once said, before the knife scraped them clean.

*So he knows,* Jude thought, pressing their palm flat against their own heartbeat. *He knows there are more.*

The moth-wing key lay in their pocket, weightless as a held breath. All night it had felt warm. All night it had felt like a question being asked politely, over and over: *now? now? now?*

When the guard outside their cell changed — the shuffle of boots, the yawn, the young acolyte's nervous cough — Jude stood, took up their satchel with its inkpot and spare nibs and the ledger-book of their own keeping, and walked out through a door that Ash-of-the-Threshold had, sometime in the last day, quietly unlatched.

The small god was good at doors. It lived at all of them.

* * *

The valley at night was a thing Jude had only ever seen from windows — lamplit, orderly, tucked in like a child under blankets. Walking through it was another country entirely. The cobbles were slick with dew. The tannery reeked. A dog followed them for two streets, decided Jude was insufficiently interesting, and trotted home. Above the rooftops the twelve moons hung in their slow procession, eleven silver and one — the thirteenth — barely a bruise of light at the sky's edge, swelling by fractions no almanac admitted to.

Jude kept to the drainage lane behind the tithe-barns, moving from shadow to shadow with a scribe's economy: no wasted motion, nothing left behind. Their heart was not pounding. That frightened them most, in retrospect — how calm the body could be while the mind screamed *run*.

At the mouth of Shepherd's Cut, where the lane bent toward the boundary road, a figure detached itself from the wall of the wool-house and Jude nearly drove an elbow into its throat before recognizing the sharp silhouette of Wren — hood up, satchel bulging, eyes enormous in the dark.

"You're going the wrong way," Jude whispered.

"So are you," Wren whispered back. "Together we're consistent."

"Wren—"

"Don't." Wren's voice cracked and steadied, cracked again. "Don't do the speech. I've heard it already, you know. In my head, all evening, in your voice. *Go back, Wren, it's dangerous, Wren.* I've rehearsed my answers. You lose."

Judy stared at them. In the thin moonlight Wren looked about nine years old and about ninety, both at once. "Vey will interrogate anyone I spoke to. He'll—"

"He already did. Two hours ago. Politely. He's very polite, have you noticed? He asked me if I'd seen any irregular documents. I said the whole ledger is irregular, that's rather its defining feature." Wren's grin was trembling at the edges. "Then he asked me what I thought happened when prayers got answered, and I said, honestly, that I'd always assumed the Temple handled it administratively, and he smiled like a cat watching a bird do arithmetic. He knows I'm lying. He let me go anyway. Do you understand? He *let me go*. Because he wants to see where I run."

"Then don't run."

"Too late." Wren pulled something from their satchel and held it up: a boundary stone rubbing, charcoal on paper, the thirteen notches stark as a wound. "I made this three days ago. I've been carrying it around like a confession. I keep thinking — everyone I've ever trusted told me the valley ends where it ends because that's where the world ends. And they *knew*. They counted the moons, Jude. They built the whole calendar around a number they pretended didn't exist." The grin died. "I can't pray in that temple tomorrow. I'd choke on it. So either I come with you or I stand in the square at dawn and tell everyone everything and get burned with the marginalia. And I'm a coward, so."

"That's not cowardice."

"It's absolutely cowardice. I'm choosing exile because facing Vey is scarier than facing the unknown, and I resent every part of that sentence." Wren shouldered their satchel. "Also, someone has to take notes. You'll be too busy having feelings."

Despite everything — despite the guards, the hunt, the thirteenth moon fattening in the dark like a secret going septic — Jude laughed. It came out ragged, half sob, but it came. "Fine. But when Marisol scolds us—" They caught themselves. "When *someone* scolds us, I'm telling her it was your idea."

"You know the hedge-witch's name?" Wren blinked. "How?"

"The moth-god talks in its sleep." Jude turned down the Cut, toward the pale line of the boundary road, and did not elaborate, because elaborating would require admitting how much of the last week had been spent listening to a ragged little god mutter through the cracks of the world, and some dignity yet remained to be lost.

They walked. The valley fell away behind them street by street, and Jude found their feet memorizing it without permission — the lean of the well-post on Tanner's Row, the exact note the temple bell struck at compline, the smell of wet ash from the margin-fire that had burned that afternoon and was probably still smoldering in the courtyard grate. *You will never smell that again,* their feet reported, and kept walking.

The boundary road climbed. It had always climbed; every child knew the road went up to the stones and stopped, because beyond the stones was not-road, not-place, a gray shimmer that hurt to look at, like trying to read text written on water. Pilgrims went up once a year to sweep the stones and touch them respectfully and come home. No one had crossed in three generations. No one had *wanted* to, which was the part Jude only now understood was strange — a whole valley of people who had never once, in living memory, been curious about the other side. Curiosity had been sealed out along with everything else. It had leaked back in with the rain.

The stones appeared out of the dark one at a time, tall as doorways, ancient as grief. Twelve of them, arced across the saddle of the pass, and each carved with its dozen notches except the eleventh — Wren's stone — which bore thirteen, the last cut fresh and pale as new bread.

Jude stopped between the fourth and fifth stones and looked back.

The valley lay below in its bowl of darkness, and here and there lights: the temple, the watch-house, a few stubborn candles. Somewhere down there was the cell they'd left, the desk with its half-copied tithe rolls, the debt in the Ledger bearing their family name in their grandmother's hand — *Ashgrove, owed in full, term of payment: perpetual.* Seventy years of scribal servitude purchased by a signature Jude had never seen until this week, when Vey had laid it before them like a fishmonger displaying the day's catch.

"They say you can see the whole valley from here on a clear day," Wren said softly. "All our lives in one look. Isn't that obscene?"

"Yes," Jude said.

"I'm going to miss it so much I think I might die of it."

"Yes," Jude said again, and found Wren had taken their hand, or they had taken Wren's, and neither of them remarked on it, because some things survive only unexamined.

The moth-wing key had gone hot in Jude's pocket. Not burning — *insistent*, the way a word rises to the tip of the tongue. Jude drew it out. In the moonlight it was barely there: a sliver of gray translucence shaped like a key the way a rumor is shaped like a fact, veined with faint gold, trembling with its own existence.

"What is it?" Wren breathed.

"A door-opener," Jude said. "For a door I haven't found yet."

They walked to the gap between the fourth and fifth stones, where the not-road shimmered, and understood — with the sudden terrible clarity of a proof completing itself — that the key wasn't for a lock anywhere. It was for *them*. It had been warming toward this moment since the night it was pressed into their palm. Ash-of-the-Threshold was a god of doorways, and Jude Ashgrove, blood of the signatory, debtor in perpetuity, was the doorway. The covenant ran in their veins like ink runs in a pen, waiting for a hand.

"Tell me if it hurts," Wren said.

"It won't," said a voice like dry leaves, and Ash-of-the-Threshold was simply *there*, sitting cross-legged atop the fourth stone as though it had always been part of the architecture, ragged and gray-eyed and smaller than memory insisted. "Hurting is for bigger gods. I only do thresholds." It looked at Jude with an expression that on a human face would have been apologetic. "Beyond these stones, the wild realm will notice you immediately. Everything notices a hinge. Some things will want to open you. Some will want to weld you shut. Be polite to both and trust neither."

"That's your guidance?" Jude said. "Be polite and paranoid?"

"It's kept me alive since before your valley had a name." The god flicked its gaze to Wren, then back. "The girl-who-counts comes freely. Good. Free choices weigh more. Now — walk through, scribe, and learn what your grandmother paid."

"And what did she pay?" Jude asked. "Really. Not what the Ledger says."

Ash-of-the-Threshold's face did something complicated, the way weather does. "Ask the moon," it said. "It watched. I only watched the door."

Jude turned to the gap. The shimmer hung there, patient, gray as unwritten paper. Behind them the valley, ahead of them nothing guaranteed. Their whole life had been a document written by other hands — the debt, the indenture, the margins scraped clean. This was the first blank space that was truly theirs, and it terrified them exactly as much as it had always, secretly, in the quietest chamber of their heart, been wanted.

"For the record," Jude said, "I would like it noted that I am leaving voluntarily, debts voided by conscience, witnesses one."

"Noted," said Wren, and produced, absurdly, a pencil.

Jude laughed again — that same broken sound — and stepped between the stones.

The crossing was not pain. It was *attention*. For one suspended instant Jude felt themselves perceived by something vast and vegetal and slow, the way a seed must feel the sun deciding about it — and then magic touched them, actually touched them, poured through the seam of their skin like light through a crack in a shuttered room. It tasted of rain and iron and growing things and old, old anger. It knew their grandmother's name. It sang it, once, contemptuously, and let it go.

Jude came out the other side on grass that had never been mowed, under stars arranged in constellations no temple chart had ever permitted, and dropped to their knees in the wet dark, laughing and shaking and alive in a way that felt slightly like dying.

Wren came through a heartbeat later, staggered, sat down hard, and said, with tremendous dignity, "I would like it noted that I vomited a little."

"Noted," Jude managed.

Above them the thirteenth moon cleared the ridge-line, and it was wrong — swollen, honey-colored, veined like the moth-wing key, and it was *looking* at them. Down the unseen slope, miles off, a river said something in a language of water that Jude's bones translated without asking permission: *hinge, hinge, the hinge is loose, the hinge is walking.*

Far behind, back through the stones, a bell began to ring in the valley — not compline, not matins. The alarm. Vey had found the empty cell.

"They'll send riders to the pass," Wren said, scrambling up.

"No," Jude said slowly, getting their feet under them, staring at the impossible stars. "He won't waste riders on a road that doesn't exist anymore. He'll do something worse." They touched the stitched lump of names against their breastbone, thirty-one gods sleeping over their heart, and turned to face the wild country rolling away into the dark — hills that moved when unobserved, a road that was, even now, considering whether to exist for them.

"We need to find Marisol before the moon fills," Jude said. "Before the thirteenth turn. Because I felt it back there, Wren. What's coming through the crack." They swallowed. "It isn't just the old gods. Something else is following them out. Something they were sealing *in with themselves*."

Ash-of-the-Threshold, perched on a boundary stone that was suddenly very far away, raised one gray hand — farewell or warning, impossible to say — and was gone, and somewhere behind them in the valley, faint as a prayer, the alarm bell went on ringing for a home neither of them would ever enter again.

The road, at last, decided to exist. It unrolled downhill into the dark, and they took it.
