## 8. A Court of Crows and Contracts

The crows found them three days out from Marisol's cottage, and they did not behave like birds.

Jude noticed it first in the way they flew — not the scattered, quarrelsome patterns of valley crows, but in formation, in ranks, wheeling above the road with a discipline that made Wren stop chewing mid-bite.

"They've been following us since the river," Wren said, squinting up. "Since the drowned chapel."

"Since before that, maybe." Jude kept walking. Their blood-mark had been quiet since the chapel, but now it stirred beneath their sleeve like a sleeper turning over, a slow warmth that rose toward the sky. "They're not hunting us."

"How do you know?"

"Because things that hunt you don't wait." Jude nodded at the trees ahead. "Look where they're going."

The crows were funneling down into a clearing off the road's shoulder, folding into the branches of an enormous oak — or what Jude had taken for an oak until this moment, when they looked properly at it and realized no oak grew that way, its trunk braided like rope, its roots gripping the earth like fingers around something precious. The birds settled among its branches until the tree seemed to have grown black leaves, and every one of those leaf-eyes was turned toward the road.

Toward Jude.

Marisol came up behind them, staff tapping, and made a sound low in her throat — half laugh, half warning. "Well," she said. "He's finally decided to introduce himself. I wondered how long Kessivar would circle."

"You know him?"

"Everyone out here knows *of* him." She planted her staff and studied the tree with the weary respect of someone regarding an old creditor. "God of crows, of carrion, of bargains struck at dusk. He collects debts the way other gods collect souls — patiently, and with interest. And he has been watching you, scribe, since the day you crossed the boundary stones. Probably longer. Crows were watching your temple long before the miracles started, if you know how to look."

"I don't want to make a bargain with a god," Wren said flatly. "We've seen what gods cost. The river took my—" They stopped, jaw tightening. The memory Wren had paid as toll was gone; only the shape of the absence remained, a hole worn smooth by grief. "I know what they cost."

"Yes," Marisol said gently. "And you'll notice the river didn't lie about the price. That's more than your temple ever did." She turned back to Jude. "But he won't force you. Kessivar never forces. Forcing is for lesser powers. He *invites*. And his invitations are hard to refuse twice."

As if summoned by the name, one crow detached itself from the canopy and dropped through the air in a straight black line, landing on the road ten paces ahead. It didn't hop. It stood very still, head tilted, one eye bright as a wet stone fixed on Jude.

Then it spoke.

Not in words, exactly — the sound was a rasping chorus, many throats layered into one voice, like wind through a burned forest: *"SCRIBE. THE COURT IS OPEN. COME AND BE COUNTED."*

Wren made a small involuntary noise. Marisol just sighed. "Polite, at least. He's always polite. It's worse than if he weren't."

Jude's heart was hammering against their ribs, but underneath the fear was something else, something that had been waking in them since the night Ash-of-the-Threshold left a moth-wing key on their desk: curiosity. The old, dangerous, ledger-shaped hunger to know what was written somewhere they hadn't read yet.

"We're going in there," they said, "aren't we."

"We're going in there," Marisol agreed. "Try not to sign anything until you've read it twice."

* * *

There was no path into the clearing, and then suddenly there was, and the transition between *not* and *was* happened somewhere behind Jude's eyes rather than under their feet. One step they were pushing through bramble; the next, they walked a lane of trampled grass lined with standing stones, each stone carved with marks that hurt to look at directly — contracts, Jude understood dimly, contracts so old and so binding that the language itself had become solid.

The oak opened at its base like a doorway. Inside, it was not a tree at all.

It was a hall.

Ribs of dark wood arched overhead into vaulted darkness, hung with feathers instead of banners — thousands upon thousands of them, black and grey and one startling iridescent blue, drifting slowly in air that had no wind. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, the amber light of late afternoon that exists everywhere and belongs to no place. Rows of rough-hewn benches descended toward a dais built from a wagon wreck, its axle still intact, its wheels still bearing dried mud from some road a hundred years dead.

And the court was full.

Crows covered every surface — perched along the benches, hanging from the rafters, standing in patient rows on the floor like mourners at a funeral that never ends. But they were not all crows. As Jude's eyes adjusted, they saw figures seated among the birds: a woman whose cloak was stitched from forty different coats; a man with a surveyor's chain coiled around his shoulders, his eyes the flat silver of a frozen pond; a child-sized thing made entirely of lost buttons and keys; shapes that might once have been human and had been renegotiated into something else over centuries of careful agreements.

"The Court of Quiet Claims," Marisol murmured. "Every god too small to hold a mountain and too stubborn to die. Every spirit that survived the sealing by being too minor to bother with. This is where they've lived for three generations, Jude. In the cracks. Making do."

At the center of the dais, on a throne assembled from gallows timber and a bishop's broken chair, sat Kessivar.

He wore a man's shape the way a coat is worn — well enough, but you could see the seams. He was tall and thin and dressed in mourning that had gone elegant with age, and his eyes were the exact black of the birds, without any white at all. When he moved his hand, three hundred crows moved theirs in perfect echo. When he spoke, his voice came from his mouth and from every beak in the hall simultaneously, so that the whole court spoke with him:

*"Jude Ashgrove. Scribe of the Temple of the Quiet Ledger. Grandchild of Selen Ashgrove, who signed away the sky. You are late, but you are expected."*

"I wasn't aware I had an appointment."

Kessivar smiled, and it was almost a human expression, which somehow made it worse. *"You had a debt. Debts are appointments. Yours has been accruing interest for sixty years — though not yours to pay, I grant. Yet here you are anyway, walking into my hall of your own free feet. Curious creature, the mortal who volunteers for obligations."*

"I'm not here to volunteer for anything. We need shelter, passage, and information about the oath-bearers. We're willing to trade fairly—"

*"Fairly."* The word rippled through the court like a feather disturbed. Several of the gathered gods leaned forward. *"Do you hear that? Fairly. Oh, little scribe. Do you know why the wild realm loves your kind and hates your temple in equal measure?"* He rose from the throne, unhurried, and descended the dais steps. Up close he smelled of cold iron and rain about to fall. *"Because your temple wrote everything down — tithes, penances, the price of every candle — and called it holiness. And because we cannot write anything down at all. Do you understand what that means, to be a power that cannot keep records? A promise spoken can be denied. A memory can rot. A god who cannot write is a god who can be cheated by anyone with a good lawyer and a bad conscience. Your grandmother knew this. It is half the reason her seal held so long — she wrote it, and we could not unwrite it."*

He stopped before Jude and extended one long finger — and the finger ended not in nail but in the black gleam of a claw, polished like an inkwell.

*"But you," he said, softer now, and the whole hall leaned in. "You are a child of both worlds whether you wish it or not. Blood of the signatory, trained in the Ledger's hand. You can hold a pen. You can read a clause. You can spot a trap hidden in the fourth line of a contract, where we lesser powers have been stung a thousand times. Do you know what you are worth to this court, Jude Ashgrove?"*

"A weapon?"

"*A library.*" The word came out with something dangerously close to reverence. "A living one, that walks and speaks and can be trusted — if trust can be purchased. Which brings me to my offer."

From his sleeve — from somewhere; the sleeve was deeper than sleeves should be — Kessivar drew a scroll. It was written in no script Jude recognized, characters that writhed slightly, that rearranged themselves when observed too directly. Beside it he produced a quill cut from his own wing feather, and an inkhorn filled with something dark that was not quite liquid.

"My protection. Mine and my court's. Vey's inquisitors move against you even now — you've seen their work at the grave, you'll see worse before the thirteenth moon. Under my mark, they cannot touch you without owing me a debt no inquisitor would dare carry. Passage across the wild realm. Counsel. The names and locations of the surviving oath-bearers, which I possess and you desperately need." He laid the scroll in the air, and it hung there. "In exchange: service. Your hand, your craft, your literacy. When my court requires writing — contracts, records, oaths set down so they cannot be cheated — you will write them. Not forever. A term. Until the matter of the covenant is resolved, one way or another."

"That's it?" Wren said suspiciously, from beside Jude. "No soul? No firstborn? No memory?"

Kessivar's eyeless-black gaze slid to Wren, and there was something almost gentle in it. *"Child, I watched the river take your memory at the chapel. I could have taken far more, any time these three days, and you would never have known what was missing. If I wanted souls, I'd take them and skip the paperwork. No — I want something rarer. I want a record that holds. Everything in the wild realm is fluid — roads, rivers, promises. Mortals forget, gods fade, and every bargain we strike dissolves like sugar in rain. I am offering you employment, scribe. Honest work. The kind your temple taught you was holy, and then used to grind your family into the dirt."*

Jude stared at the hanging scroll. "May I read it?"

Something shifted in the hall. A murmur ran through the assembled gods — surprise, Jude realized, actual astonishment. Kessivar tilted his head.

*"Ask,"* he said. *"By all means, ask."*

Jude took the scroll. The script fought them at first — characters sliding like fish — but the Ledger had trained them for difficult hands, for faded marginalia and deliberate obfuscation, and slowly, line by line, the contract yielded.

Most of it was what Kessivar said. Protection, passage, counsel. Service as scribe until the covenant question resolved. But in the seventh clause, tucked into a subordinate phrase, Jude found the first hook: *service rendered shall be at such hours and occasions as the Court may require.* Unbounded. Every hour of every day, if the Court willed it.

In the eleventh clause: *the undersigned consents to such amendments as the Court may find expedient.* Amendments. An open door.

And in the fourteenth clause, buried deepest, the one that made Jude's blood run cold and then hot: *should the undersigned fail in service, all protection granted is retroactively void, and all debts incurred during the term of protection fall due immediately.* Meaning: accept his shelter tonight, and if the contract broke tomorrow, they'd owe for every mile, every meal, every breath of safety — at rates set by the court of crows.

Jude looked up. Kessivar was watching them with an expression of vast, patient pleasure.

"Well?" Marisol whispered. "Is it clean?"

"It's three traps wearing a clean coat." Jude heard their own voice come out steady, and marveled at it. Somewhere between the temple scriptorium and this impossible hall, their hands had learned not to shake. "Clause seven makes me your slave by schedule. Clause eleven lets you rewrite the terms whenever convenient. Clause fourteen converts your protection into a debt-trap the moment I stumble."

Silence. Total silence — three hundred crows and forty gods, utterly still.

Then Kessivar began to laugh. It started in his own throat and spread outward through the hall, beak by beak, until the whole court was laughing in that layered rasp, and the sound was terrible and delighted and, strangely, not unkind.

*"Ohhh,"* the god breathed, wiping at an eye that had no moisture to wipe. *"Oh, magnificent. Sixty years I have waited for someone to walk into my hall and read past the third line. Do you know how many mortals have signed that scroll as it stands? Fourteen. All of them mine now. All of them regretting. And you — you stand there and itemize my hooks like tithes in a ledger."* He swept into a bow that managed to be mocking and sincere at once. *"Amend them, then. Strike what you will, write what you must. Show my court how it is done."*

He offered the quill.

Jude took it. The feather was warm and faintly alive, and the ink — they tipped the horn and understood now — was not ink at all but a suspension of dissolved promises, every word ever spoken carelessly and meant deeply, ground fine. It would write true. It would write *binding*. Whatever they put down in this ink would become law.

Their hands were trembling now, but not from fear.

Jude crossed out clause seven and replaced it: *service rendered at reasonable hours, not exceeding three demands in any seven days, save in cases of immediate mortal peril.* They rewrote clause eleven: *amendments permitted only by mutual consent, recorded in duplicate, one copy held by each party.* And clause fourteen — Jude paused over it, feeling the whole hall leaning in, feeling Kessivar's attention like weather — and rewrote it: *protection, once granted, is not retroactively void. Failure of service forfeits future protection only. Past shelter freely given remains given.*

"You understand," Kessivar said quietly, "that last change costs me. Shelter already rendered becomes a gift. Gifts create obligation — *mine*, not yours."

"I know," Jude said. "That's the point. You asked for a scribe. Scribes don't just copy words. We decide what words mean." They met the god's bottomless eyes. "If you want a record that holds, hire someone honest. Even to you. *Especially* to you."

For a long moment, nothing in the hall moved.

Then Kessivar extended his hand — the clawed one, the inkwell-dark one — and said, in a voice stripped of all its choruses, just one voice, quiet and genuine: *"Sign, Jude Ashgrove. And welcome to the wild realm. You may be the first thing in sixty years worth welcoming."*

Jude signed. The ink sank into the vellum and vanished, and Jude felt the contract close around them like a garment fitted precisely — weight, but not chains. Warmth, but not a cage. Around the hall, the court bowed its heads, and somewhere far off a bell tolled once, though Jude suspected no bell existed anywhere within a hundred miles.

"So," Marisol said, as the hall began to dissolve around them like mist at sunrise, depositing them all back on the road beneath the great tree, evening falling honestly now, stars coming out. "You've made a god smile and rewritten a contract in his own ink. What next, scribe?"

Jude rolled the amended copy — their copy, their duplicate, warm in their pocket like a coal — and looked north, where the boundary hills lay black against a sky already bruising toward moonrise. Toward the second oath-bearer. Toward whatever Vey's people had left in their wake.

"Next," Jude said, "we get the names. All thirteen. Because I've just realized something, and I think Kessivar realized it too, the moment I picked up his pen."

"What's that?"

"Ink doesn't care whose side it's on. Neither does paper, or law, or truth." Jude touched the pocket where their copy of the contract lay. "Vey has relics and soldiers and sixty years of planning. But everything he intends to build — the new seal, the permanent covenant — has to be *written*. And there is only one scribe in either realm that both worlds will trust."

Wren went pale. "Vey will come looking for you."

"He already is." Above them, unseen in the dark, a single crow lifted from the oak and flew north, fast, carrying word. "And now, at least, we'll see him coming."

Behind them, deep in the braided trunk of the tree, Kessivar's laughter echoed softly, like pages turning.
