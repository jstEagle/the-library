## 10. Moonturn Rising

The moon was three nights from full when the wheat in Marisol's field decided it could not wait any longer.

Jude heard it before they saw it — a sound like a thousand small sighs, like paper unfolding — and came out of the cottage with their boots unlaced to find the whole eastern slope shimmering gold under the starlight. The wheat had been green at dusk. Jude was certain of it; they had walked the field's edge that evening with Marisol, listening to her grumble about late frosts, and every stalk had been knee-high and stubbornly unripe.

Now it stood taller than Jude's shoulder, heavy-headed, ripe as harvest-day. And as they watched, it kept going — ripening past ripeness, the gold deepening to bronze, to rust, heads drooping, stems bowing, until in the space of twenty breaths the entire field collapsed into rot. The sweet smell of overripe grain rolled down the hill and into the doorway of the cottage, thick enough to taste.

Marisol stood beside them, arms crossed, her silver-streaked hair loose around her shoulders. She did not look surprised. She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"Third time this week," she said. "Barley on Sunday. Apples yesterday. Now the wheat." She spat into the dirt, an old habit that seemed almost ceremonial. "It can't decide what season it wants. None of it can."

Wren appeared at the door behind them, still holding the cup of willow-bark tea they'd been drinking, their face pale in the light spilling from the window. "Is it supposed to do that? Fields aren't supposed to do that."

"Nothing's *supposed* to do anything anymore," Marisol said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They spent the morning cutting what could be saved, which was less than nothing — the grain crumbled to gray dust between the fingers even as they gathered it. Wren worked in silence, and Jude caught them more than once standing motionless in the ruined field, staring at their own hands as though checking whether they, too, might bloom and rot out of order.

By midday the river began.

It started as a hum — low, tuneless, felt in the teeth more than heard — and by the time the sun touched the ridge it had become words. Not words in any language Jude knew, but words all the same, syllables rising off the water like mist, breaking apart against the stones. Marisol went down to the bank and stood listening for a long while, her expression unreadable.

"It's the Aldwater," she said when she came back. "Or it was the Aldwater. It's remembering being something older." She looked at Jude, then away. "Rivers were gods once, where I come from. Most places, if you go back far enough. When the covenant thins, they start to wake up. They start to remember their names."

"And you understand it?"

"I understand enough. It says the banks are too narrow." Her mouth twisted. "It says it has been asleep so long it dreamed it was only water, and now it isn't sure which is the dream."

That night, Jude sat up with their copied pages spread across the floor of the loft — the god-names rescued from the ledger margins, transcribed in their smallest hand, each one a small act of treason they had committed back in a life that already felt like someone else's story. They read the names aloud, quietly, testing them the way one tests a lock: *Ash-of-the-Threshold. Kessivar-who-counts-crows. Vellum-of-the-Long-Rain.* The moth-wing key hung on a cord at their throat, impossibly light, and when certain names passed their lips it stirred against their skin like a living thing.

Below, through the floorboards, they heard Marisol talking to the river. She had taken to going down at night. Jude didn't ask what she said to it. He didn't ask what it said back.

* * *

On the second night, the mark burned.

Jude woke with their hand pressed hard against their sternum, where beneath the skin the blood-mark — the thin, branching line that had appeared the night they crossed the boundary stones, pale as a vein of ink under parchment — had begun to glow. It hurt. Not the sharp hurt of a burn or a cut but a deeper ache, like a word remembered on the tip of the tongue for thirty years finally forcing its way out.

They stumbled down the ladder and out into the yard, half-blind with pain, and the moon met them — waxing fat, two nights from full, hanging low and yellow over the valley — and the world came apart.

It was not a dream. Jude had dreams; this was not one. This was memory, pressed into their blood before they were born, waiting for a moon strong enough to play it back.

---

A hall of white stone. Cold, though torches burn in iron brackets along the walls. Thirteen chairs arranged in a crescent, and twelve of them filled — mortals, all of them, farmers and knights and priestesses, faces lit uncertainly, the way people look when they have been asked to do a thing larger than their lives were built for. The thirteenth chair stands empty, and before it, a table, and on the table, a document.

And a woman leaning over it with a pen in her hand.

She is old — older than Jude has ever imagined being, her hands spotted, her back bowed — but there is something in the set of her shoulders that Jude knows the way they know their own reflection. They have seen that posture bent over a scriptorium desk their whole life. They have seen it in the mirror every morning.

*Grandmother.*

Not the grandmother of family stories — the gentle ghost invoked over supper, the woman who had supposedly died pious and poor, leaving nothing but debts. This woman wears scribe's robes of deep blue, and her eyes are red-rimmed, and her hand shakes.

Around her, the other twelve wait. One of them speaks — a broad man with a soldier's bearing, his voice heavy with exhaustion. *"Sign it, Sera. For mercy's sake, sign it. You've read the terms a dozen times. We have no better offer."*

*"There are no good offers,"* she says, and her voice cracks on the last word, and it is Jude's own voice, pitched lower, worn older. *"You know what they asked for. You know what the price was."*

*"We know,"* says a young priestess, gently. *"But the alternative is the valley. You've seen what happens to valleys that refuse."*

Silence. The torches crackle. Somewhere beyond the walls, something vast moves in the dark, patient as weather.

Sera Ashgrove looks down at the contract. Jude looks with her, reads with her, the old script swimming but legible — clauses of sealing and silence, thirteen years of thirteen moons, a boundary drawn in blood and ink. And then her eyes stop moving, and Jude feels her see it, the clause near the end, small as a splinter:

*A debt shall be bound to the signer's line, unto the last of their blood, redeemable only by—*

And here the memory blurs, smears, the ink seeming to writhe as though the contract itself resents being read — but Jude catches the shape of what follows, catches it the way you catch a falling cup without knowing how: *redeemable only by* something. Some escape. Some flaw, hidden in the grammar, buried in the wording, placed there by a hand steady enough and clever enough to hide a door inside a wall while everyone watched her build it.

*"Sera,"* the soldier says again, softer now. *"Your grandchildren will carry this debt. You understand that."*

*"I understand,"* she says. *"Better than any of you. That's why I'm the one signing."*

And she weeps as she writes — great silent tears dropping onto the parchment beside her signature, tears that the ink drinks in greedily, that seem to become part of the contract itself — and she signs her name, and the name burns off the page as she finishes it, and the thirteenth chair fills with shadow, and the walls of the valley rise—

---

Jude came back to themselves lying in the wet grass of Marisol's yard, dawn-gray sky overhead, the mark on their chest still warm but quiet now. Wren crouched over them, shaking their shoulder, saying their name with the particular urgency of someone who has said it many times already. Marisol stood a few paces off, watching, her face carefully neutral in the way of someone who has seen people come back from visions before and knows not to crowd them.

"You screamed," Wren said. "For about an hour. Then you stopped, and started talking instead. Names." They hesitated. "One of them was yours. Or — a version of it. *Sera.* Who's Sera?"

"My grandmother." Jude sat up slowly. The grass was soaked with dew and with something else — their own sweat, cold now. "My grandmother sealed the gods away."

They told them. All of it — the white hall, the twelve, the trembling hand, the tears the ink drank. Wren listened with their tea forgotten in their hand, gone cold. Marisol listened without blinking, and when Jude described the clause — *redeemable only by*, and then the blur — something flickered across her face, quick as a fish under ice.

"A hidden clause," she said. "In a binding contract. Signed by your own blood." She was quiet a moment. "Clever woman."

"She was crying."

"People cry when they build traps, Jude. Even kind ones. Even necessary ones." Marisol crouched, took their chin in her rough fingers, and made them meet her eyes. "Listen to me. Your grandmother hid a door in that contract for a reason. Which means she wasn't betraying the gods — or she wasn't betraying *only* the gods. Which means whatever you inherited, it isn't simple, and it isn't finished, and it may yet be *yours to change*." She released them and stood, joints cracking. "Now get up. There's work. The river's been shouting since midnight and I think today it starts making demands."

* * *

The demand came at noon.

The Aldwater had swollen overnight without rain, running high and brown and loud, and at noon it simply stopped — the whole river halting in its bed like a held breath — and spoke. Not in mist-syllables this time. In words. Clear ones, carrying up the bank, in a voice like gravel turning over in a current.

***THE SCRIBE OF THE LEDGER.***

Every hair on Jude's arms rose. Marisol looked at them. Wren looked at them. Nobody moved.

***WE SMELL THE OLD INK ON YOU,*** the river said. ***WE REMEMBER WHOSE BLOOD SEALED US UNDER THE WATERLINE. COME DOWN TO THE BANK, LITTLE DEBT. WE WOULD LOOK AT WHAT SHE LEFT BEHIND.***

"She?" Jude called, before they could stop themselves. Their voice sounded very small against the water's bulk.

***THE ONE WHO WEPT.*** A long gurgle, almost — almost — like laughter. ***OH, SHE PAID US RESPECT, THAT ONE. POURED HONEY IN OUR COURSES EVERY THIRTEENTH MOON, ALL HER LIFE, SO WE WOULD SLEEP KINDLY AND NOT RISE ANGRY WHEN THE COVENANT THINNED. SHE KNEW IT WOULD THIN. SHE TOLD US SO HERSELF, AT THE SIGNING.***

The river resumed — slowly, grudgingly, like a sleeper rolling over — but its voice followed, riding the current downstream and up the bank and into Jude's bones.

***SHE TOLD US: WHEN THE THIRTEENTH MOON COMES ROUND AGAIN, MY BLOOD WILL STAND WHERE I STOOD. AND THEY WILL NOT DO AS I DID. THEY WILL DO WHAT I SHOULD HAVE DONE.***

***SO COME TO THE STANDING STONES, SCRIBE. BRING YOUR INK AND YOUR GRANDMOTHER'S TEARS. THE MOON IS NEARLY FAT, AND THE OTHERS ARE ALREADY GATHERING.***

***AND KNOW THIS: YOU ARE FOLLOWED. THE IRON MAN RIDES WITH RELICS IN HIS WAGONS, AND HE WILL REACH THE STONES BEFORE YOU IF YOU LINGER.***

The river fell silent. The normal sound of water returned, ordinary and indifferent, as though nothing had happened.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then Wren, in a small voice: "The iron man. Does it mean—"

"Vey," Jude said. The name tasted like cold metal. Halvard Vey, who had burned the marginalia, who had hunted them across the boundary, whose elegant hands had catalogued the bones of dead oath-bearers like a collector cataloguing butterflies. "He's coming to the standing stones."

Marisol was already moving, snatching a canvas pack from beside the door, stuffing it with jars and twine and a knife. "Then we don't linger. We ride tonight, ahead of the full moon." She paused at the gate and looked back at Jude, and her expression was grim, but underneath the grimness — unmistakably — was hope, fierce and battered and alive.

"Your grandmother left you a message, child. Through sixty years and a river's memory. Whatever she did at that table, she meant for you to finish it differently." She shouldered the pack. "So let's not keep her waiting."

Behind them, upstream, the Aldwater ran fast and dark toward the standing stones — and Jude could have sworn it was singing.
