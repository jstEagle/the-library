## 11. What Vey Wants

The relics arrived in a lacquered case, packed in wool and ash-salt, and Halvard Vey received them the way other men received newborn children: with both hands, with held breath, with the private terror of being responsible for something that could not be replaced.

He waited until his clerks had gone before opening it. This was ritual, though he would never have called it that. Ritual was for the credulous. He preferred *procedure*.

Inside, on a bed of grey wool, lay the finger bone of Saint Ossen of the Ford — or what the ledgers called Saint Ossen, and what Vey's own research had identified, after eleven years of patient cross-referencing, as an oath-bearer of the old covenant: a woman named Ossane who had knelt at the boundary stones three generations ago and sworn her marrow to a god of crossings. The bone was yellowed, no longer than a man's thumb, and it hummed.

Not audibly. Vey had learned long ago that most people mistook their own anticipation for the world's voice. But when he laid two fingers against it, he felt the tremor travel up through the small bones of his wrist, past the pulse point, into the meat of his forearm, like a plucked string still ringing from a song played decades ago.

*Thirteen moons,* the reports said. *A thirteenth notch cut fresh in the western boundary stone.* And prayers answered in the valley where prayer had been a formality for seventy years — rain falling where rain was asked for, bread multiplying in a beggar's hands, a scribe's voice heard speaking names that should have burned with everything else Vey's predecessors could find.

Vey closed the case and sat for a while in the lamplight of his traveling tent, listening to the wind move across the moorland outside the valley's edge.

He did not hate the gods.

This was the misunderstanding everyone made — the peasants with their charms, the temple clerks with their nervous whispers, even the Inquisitorial Council back in the capital, half of whom believed him a zealot and the other half a cynic exploiting old fears for budget and rank. They all assumed hatred. Hatred was easy to understand, and so they understood him wrongly and slept well.

But Vey had grown up in the border provinces, in the years just after the sealing, when the last of the wild things were dying out of the hedges like foxes driven from a wood. He remembered his grandmother — not Jude Ashgrove's grandmother, but his own, a different woman entirely, though history was full of these mirrored lives — kneeling by his bed with her hands folded, telling him about her brother.

Her brother had been taken by a river.

Not drowned. *Taken*. The river in those days, before the sealing finished its work, had been one of the small hungry gods, the kind that demanded tolls and forgot why, the kind that ate memories the way fish ate flies, indiscriminately, because eating was all they knew. It had reached up out of the water on a market day — she swore this, weeping, sixty years later, and Vey had believed her because she was not a woman given to invention — and it had pulled her brother under, and when it let him go, three days later, downstream, alive, whole, walking — he was not her brother anymore. Something wore him. Something smiled with his mouth and answered to his name and ate at their table for eleven years until the sealing came and the wearing stopped, abruptly, between one supper and the next, leaving behind a hollow man who could not remember how to be a boy.

The god hadn't been evil. That was the horror of it. Vey had spent his career studying the old accounts, the recovered fragments, the testimony wrung out of terrified villages, and nowhere — nowhere — had he found malice in the old gods. Only appetite without conscience. Love without proportion. A crow-god that blessed a farmer's fields and then, delighted by its own generosity, blessed them again, and again, until the grain rotted in the ear and the family starved amid abundance, and the god wept over them, sincerely, magnificently, and could not understand what it had done wrong.

Parasites, then? No. Even parasites had a logic. What the gods were was *children* — vast, beautiful, immortal children, with the strength to reshape rivers and the restraint of toddlers. Mortals had lived among them for millennia the way mice lived among owls: briefly, and by luck.

And then someone had built a cage.

Three generations ago, thirteen mortals — oath-bearers, each carrying a fragment of the binding in their blood — had signed the covenant at the standing stones, and the wild magic had drained out of the valley like water from a breached cistern, and for the first time in living memory children had grown up without checking the shadows under the bed. The valley had flourished. Of course it had. Peace always looked like prosperity when you didn't count its price.

But the covenant was mortal work, and mortal work frayed. Thirteen moon-turns, the old texts hinted — a cycle, a term, a lease running out. And now the lease was expiring, and things were leaking through, and a beggar in the Veiled Valley had been answered by a voice of rain.

Vey opened the lacquered case again and looked at the bone.

He intended to renew the seal. Not restore — *renew*. The distinction mattered, and he had built his entire strategy upon it. The original covenant had been a treaty signed by desperate people with inadequate information, its terms distributed across thirteen bloodlines, its enforcement scattered through thirteen mortal lives that would live and die and leave the binding to rot. Amateur work. Noble, perhaps, but amateur.

What Vey proposed was architecture.

The harvested relics — bones, tokens, grave-goods of the original oath-bearers, gathered over years by agents who were very good and very quiet — contained the covenant's signature, its structural grammar. With all thirteen assembled at the boundary stones under the proper moon, the seal could be rewritten: permanent, self-sustaining, anchored not in mortal bloodlines that faded but in the relics themselves, which did not age, did not die, did not forget. A wall instead of a fence. A wall that would stand until the mountains stood down.

There was only one problem, and it was the problem that kept him awake at night, staring at the tent canvas, and he acknowledged it honestly because he had never once allowed himself the comfort of dishonesty: the rewriting required a hand. Someone had to speak the new terms. Someone had to hold the pen, so to speak, and mean it, and keep meaning it, year after year, decade after decade, because a covenant was not a document filed away but a sentence spoken forever.

The original signatories had shared that burden thirteen ways.

Vey intended to take it alone.

He told himself it was practicality. Diffusion bred weakness; a single arbiter could act with the speed the situation demanded. He told himself it was his right — he had found the flaw, he had gathered the relics, he had spent eleven years and two fortunes and the goodwill of every superior he possessed. But late at night, with the bone humming faintly against his fingers like a cat purring in its sleep, he admitted the truth, because honesty was the one discipline he never broke:

He did not trust anyone else to carry it.

He had seen what conviction diluted became. Committees. Compromises. A council that renewed the seal for a century and then, in some soft generation, voted to loosen it, out of curiosity, out of compassion, out of the perennial human itch to open locked doors just to see what was inside. And the door would open onto a river that took little boys and gave back strangers, and there would be no one left alive who remembered why it had been locked.

So: himself. Sole arbiter. Judge of what passed the boundary and what did not, keeper of the terms, speaker of the sentence. He would be hated. He accepted that. Every warden is hated by the thing warded and envied by the ones who sleep safely because of it. His grandmother had been hated too, probably, by whoever remembered the brother. It changed nothing about whether the work was right.

A clerk scratched at the tent flap. "Inquisitor. The rider from the valley."

"Send them in."

The rider was mud-spattered, young, frightened in the way Vey found useful — frightened people reported everything, having no confidence to filter. She knelt, produced a dispatch tube, and talked while he read: the scribe Ashgrove had fled. Taken with them, copied pages bearing the forbidden names. An acolyte missing, presumed complicit. And the High Scribe herself —

Here the report faltered, and here Vey looked up.

"The High Scribe has left the temple," the rider said. "Gone out the western gate the same night. The council doesn't know what to make of it. They're saying—" the girl swallowed— "they're saying she followed the heretic."

Vey read the rest of the dispatch twice. Then he set it down and pressed his fingertips together and considered.

Serevanne Quill. He had met her once, years ago, at an archival conference in the capital: a dry, precise woman with ink-stained cuffs and a memory like a ledger itself. He had liked her. He had also, even then, noticed the way she spoke about the sealing — not with reverence but with *familiarity*, the tone of someone discussing a relative rather than a doctrine. As if she had known the people involved.

As if she had been one of them.

If the twelfth seat of the old covenant still had a living occupant — and Vey had suspected for years that it did, hidden in plain sight in the valley's most guarded institution — then she had just walked out of her fortress and onto the road, toward the standing stones, toward the child of the thirteenth bloodline, toward the exact convergence Vey needed to prevent.

Or, said a quieter voice, the voice he permitted because it kept him sharp: toward the exact convergence she needed to complete whatever the original covenant had begun.

He rose, went to the campaign map pinned along the tent's inner wall, and studied it as he had studied it a hundred times. The valley behind him, sealed and failing. The road west, winding toward Marisol's country, toward the drowned chapel, toward the standing stones where the ground still remembered thirteen voices. Somewhere on that road: a scribe with stolen names in their pockets, a runaway acolyte, a hedge-witch marked by failure, and an old woman who had spent seventy years keeping a secret she now carried into the open air.

And somewhere beyond all of them, patient as weather, the gods themselves, waking one by one, tasting the thinning of the veil, remembering that they had once been fed.

Vey took the bone from its case and held it up to the lamp. In the light, the humming seemed almost like words. Almost like pleading. That was the trick of the relics, he'd learned — they wanted to be used, all of them, every saintly finger and hallowed stone, straining toward purpose like dogs toward a whistle. The old gods had put pieces of themselves into their oath-bearers, and pieces of a child are still a child: eager, guileless, ruinous.

"I know," he murmured to it, the way one murmurs to a fretful animal. "I know what you want. You want to be whole again. You want the door opened."

He turned the bone slowly in the light.

"But you don't know what's on the other side. None of you do. I'm the only one who remembers."

He wrapped it back in wool, closed the case, and buckled it shut, and outside the tent the wind came down off the moors and carried, very faintly, from the direction of the valley, the smell of rain — falling, he would learn by morning, precisely where a beggar had asked for it, in answer to a name that should have stayed dead.

Vey stood a long time in the dark, listening.

Then he began giving orders. The column would move at dawn — west, along the pilgrim road, toward the chapel country. All thirteen relics must be gathered before the moon filled. And the fugitive scribe, Jude Ashgrove, was to be taken alive.

"Alive?" the captain repeated. "Inquisitor, the warrant says—"

"I wrote the warrant," Vey said. "Amend it." He looked out at the darkening west, where the thirteenth moon was climbing, thin and white and patient as a blade. "Their grandmother signed the seal with her own blood. Whatever runs in that child's veins, I want it *at the stones* — willing or not."

He paused, and something almost like gentleness crossed his face, the gentleness of a man about to do a great kindness that will feel, to its recipient, exactly like a knife.

"After all," he said, "we're the only ones left who remember why the door was shut. It seems fitting that it should be one of us who shuts it properly."

* * *

That night he dreamed of the river.

It was the river from his grandmother's story, or enough like it — brown water moving without wind, banks soft as skin. On the far side stood a boy in a market coat, waving. The boy's face was wrong in the way of dreams, smooth where features should have been, and yet Vey knew him, knew him the way you know people in dreams, absolutely and without evidence. *Come across*, the boy's mouth said, and the voice was the water's voice, layered, many-throated, kind. *We miss you. We remember you. We have missed you since before you were born.*

Vey stood on the bank and did not move.

"You took my grandmother's brother," he said.

*We took a hundred brothers,* the river said, gently, the way you correct a child's arithmetic. *We took ten thousand. We loved them all. Come and see.*

"No," Halvard Vey said, and his voice did not shake, because he had trained it not to, forty years, his whole life, against exactly this. "No. Stay on your side."

The boy smiled with the river's smile, and the dream dissolved into brown water rising, and Vey woke with his hand already closed around the hilt of the knife beneath his pillow, and lay listening to the ordinary sounds of the camp, and did not sleep again.

Because that was the thing no one understood — not the Council, not his clerks, not the trembling riders or the burning-eyed priests. He did not hate the gods. He pitied them, the way you pity anything enormous and innocent and hungry. And pity, Vey knew — pity was the most dangerous emotion there was. Hatred kept its distance. Pity walked straight up to the cage and wanted to open it, just to see the poor creature stretch.

He rolled from his cot before dawn and dressed in the dark, and when the sun came up the column was already moving, west along the pilgrim road, thirteen empty reliquary slots waiting in their padded cases, one vacant seat at the standing stones that only a bloodline could fill — and ahead of them, somewhere in the wet green country, a scribe, an acolyte, a witch, a traitor, and a handful of ragged, desperate gods, all hurrying toward the same door.

Vey rode at the head of the column, upright, calm, certain, and if his eyes went often to the thin white sliver of the rising moon, no one was fool enough to comment on it.
