## 12. The Drowned Chapel

The river found them before they found it.

Wren heard it first as a whisper beneath the birdsong — a low, patient murmur, like someone reading aloud in a room below. By midmorning the sound had grown to a hush that pressed against their ears, and by noon they stood at the lip of a gorge where the water ran green and slow, deep enough to drown a cathedral. Which, Marisol said, was precisely what had happened.

"There." The hedge-witch pointed with her crooked staff. "See how the current bends?"

Wren squinted. At first the river seemed only wide, its surface stitched with light. Then they saw it: a long, straight seam beneath the water, running bank to bank, too regular for stone or root. A roofline. And where the current bent around something vast and square, a bell tower leaned out of the depths at an angle, its bronze bell furred with weed, still hanging in silence.

"The chapel of Vess," Marisol said. Her voice did something Wren hadn't heard from her before — it went soft, almost reverent, and older, as if she were speaking in two times at once. "Before the sealing, this was a place where mortals and gods met on equal footing. Neutral ground. The river-god drowned it rather than let Vey's people take what lies beneath."

"Vey's people haven't taken it yet," Jude said quietly.

"No," Marisol agreed. "But his agents took the tomb at Hollowmere. He knows what he's looking for now. The Covenant Stone won't stay safe down there much longer — and we can't renew anything without it."

The water looked cold even from here. It looked like a mouth.

"And the toll?" Jude asked.

Marisol turned to look at Wren. Not at Jude. At Wren. And Wren understood, with a lurch, why the hedge-witch had been watching them all morning with that careful, measuring kindness.

"River-gods don't take coin," she said. "They take weight. Something you carry that you'd notice the absence of. For most folk, that's a memory. A good one, usually — the river has no use for bitter things. It wants what shines."

* * *

They made camp above the gorge while the light lasted. Wren didn't eat much. They sat at the fire's edge turning a stick over in their fingers and tried to imagine choosing which memory to lose, and discovered that every attempt felt like reaching into their own chest to decide which rib mattered least.

It was absurd. They'd known the temple for eleven years. They'd been brought to its gates at six years old, feverish and orphaned, and the Quiet Ledger's scribes had fed them broth and taught them letters and never once asked for payment beyond obedience. The temple was cold incense and the scratch of pens and Brother Aldous snoring through evening prayers. The temple was the smell of vellum and tallow. The temple was home.

Surely they could give up something smaller. The taste of their mother's — no. They couldn't remember their mother's face anymore, only that she'd been warm, and that memory was already half-dissolved; surely the river wouldn't want something so thin. A festival day? Their first copied verse? The moment they'd first seen the boundary stones and felt the wrongness of the thirteenth notch?

But those were all braided together with the rest. That was the trap Wren kept falling into as they turned it over: memory wasn't a purse you could pick one coin from. It was a cloth, and pulling any thread unraveled more than you meant to give.

"You're doing the thing," Jude said, sitting down beside them. In the firelight their face was all shadow and cheekbone, tired in a way that sleep hadn't touched since Hollowmere.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you suffer in advance instead of letting the suffering arrive on schedule."

Despite everything, Wren huffed something like a laugh. "You've been spending too much time with Marisol."

"I've been spending exactly the right amount of time with Marisol. She says the same about me." Jude poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "I want to tell you not to do it. I want to say we'll find another way down, or bargain harder, or—" They stopped. Shook their head. "I'm a scribe. I know when the terms are written and there's no margin left to negotiate. But I need you to hear that I hate it. Whatever you pay down there, I'll remember it for both of us. If you come up missing a piece, I'll hold it for you until you can take it back."

"You can't hold memories for people, Jude."

"No," Jude said. "But I can write them down. Words are the one magic I've got. Tell me your temple, Wren. All of it. Before tomorrow. So if the river takes it, I can give it back."

And because there was nothing else to do with the fear except pour it out, Wren talked. They talked until the fire burned low and Marisol's snores rose soft from the far side of the camp. They told Jude about the dormitory with its crooked shutters that whistled in winter wind, and how the younger acolytes swore the whistle was the ghost of a scribe who'd died mid-sentence. About Brother Aldous, who caught flies in his cupped hands and released them out the window rather than kill them, muttering that even flies owed debts. About the day Wren had first been allowed to grind ink, and how they'd spilled an entire pot of expensive black across the floor and wept, and old Sister Maren had knelt beside them and said, *Ink cleans. Fear doesn't. Learn which stains are worth keeping.*

About standing at the temple door on the evening the beggar's prayer was answered by rain, and feeling, for the first time, that the world was larger and stranger and more alive than the Ledger had ever admitted — and how terrifying that had been, and how wonderful.

Jude wrote it all. Their pen moved fast and sure in the dying light, and Wren watched the words appear and thought: *there. Now it exists twice. Once in me, once on the page.* It helped. Not enough. But some.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping. Marisol led them along a fisherman's path downstream to a shelf of rock where an ancient mooring ring jutted from the cliff, crusted with barnacles, and a rowboat hung upside down on posts, tarred many times over many years.

"The chapel has a door under the water," she said. "There's a stair inside the tower that climbs up through the flooded nave and breaks the surface in the crypt — the crypt sits high, built into the gorge wall. You swim the last stretch. The river will let us in, because the river is fair. It always takes its toll, but it always opens the door."

"It just... lets us?" Wren asked.

Marisol smiled, and it wasn't entirely comforting. "Child, you're going to meet him. Ask him yourself."

The boat rode low with three of them in it. Marisol poled them out onto the green water, past the leaning bell tower so close Wren could have touched the drowned bell, and then to a place where the current went strangely smooth and still, a circle of glass-calm in the moving river. Beneath them, forty feet down, pale shapes resolved out of the murk: arches. Windows glowing with refracted daylight. A door.

Marisol shipped the pole. "When you go under, don't fight the pull. It isn't drowning. It's invitation. And whatever he offers you — think before you speak. River-gods count every word."

She looked at Wren a moment longer. "Especially you."

Then she tipped a pouch of river-pebbles into the water — an old offering, Wren would learn later, a courtesy between witches and water — and the calm circle opened like an eye.

The world folded. There was no plunge, no shock of cold. One breath Wren sat in the boat; the next they stood on wet stone in a corridor of green light, water arched overhead like a ceiling of liquid glass, fish drifting past the windows of a drowned nave. They could breathe. That was somehow worse than if they couldn't. Their clothes were damp but their lungs worked, and the silence had weight, and every sound they made came back to them slow, like the river was considering each word before returning it.

Jude surfaced beside them, gasping once out of habit, then staring. Beyond them both, the nave opened up — shattered pews drifted in slow orbits, silt lay over everything like snow, and colored light fell through broken windows in shafts of green and gold. At the far end, where an altar should have stood, something enormous waited.

It was shaped mostly like a man, if a man were made of the idea of a river. Its body moved in slow currents; reeds grew from its shoulders; its eyes were two pale stones worn smooth. When it spoke, its voice arrived not through air but through the water itself, vibrating up through Wren's boots, their ribs, their teeth.

**SMALL TRAVELERS.**

"We've come for the Covenant Stone," Jude said, and their scribe-voice held steady, though Wren saw their hands trembling. "We ask passage to the crypt."

**PASSAGE IS GIVEN TO ALL WHO PAY.** The god drifted closer, currents coiling. **THE STONE SLEEPS IN MY KEEPING. THE ENEMY SENT DIVERS LAST MONTH. THEY PAID IN BLOOD AND GOT NOTHING. YOU WILL PAY BETTER.**

"We don't carry much," Jude began.

**NOT YOU, SCRIBE. YOUR DEBTS ARE ALREADY SPEAKING FOR YOU — I HEAR THEM RUSTLING LIKE REEDS. ANOTHER TIME.** The smooth stone eyes turned. Fixed. **YOU, LITTLE ACOLYTE. YOU CARRY A TEMPLE IN YOU. WHOLE AND SHINING. BELLS AND BROTH AND INK-STAINS. I WANT THAT ONE.**

Wren's mouth went dry, though their body was soaked through. "Which part? I get to choose—"

**NO.** The word rolled through them like a wave. **THE RIVER CHOOSES ITS WEIGHT. THAT IS THE FAIRNESS OF IT. YOU WOULD PICK THE CHEAPEST THING AND CALL IT SACRIFICE. I DO NOT TAKE CHEAP THINGS.**

"Then what?" Wren's voice cracked. "What do you want?"

The god considered them for a long moment, and the whole drowned nave seemed to consider them too, the light dimming and brightening with its attention. When it answered, its voice had changed — quieter, almost gentle, which frightened Wren more than the booming had.

**HOME.**

"No," Wren said. It came out small. They made themselves stand straighter, the way Sister Maren had taught them to stand when the elders lied about the boundary stones. "No. Take something else. Take a year of my life, take my sight in one eye, take—"

**A YEAR OF LIFE IS CHEAP. YOU'D NOT MISS IT TILL IT WAS GONE, AND BY THEN YOU'D HAVE FORGOTTEN THE MISSING. BUT HOME —** the currents stirred, restless — **HOME IS WHAT I LOST WHEN THEY DROWNED MY CHURCH. EVERY GOD IN THIS VALLEY'S SHADOW KNOWS WHAT HOME WEIGHS. I ASK ONLY THAT ONE OF YOU UNDERSTAND ME.**

Jude stepped forward. "Take mine instead. My grandmother signed the sealing. My home was built on the price of yours. It's owed—"

**YOUR HOME IS ALREADY UNRAVELING, SCRIBE. THERE'S NO CLEAN THREAD LEFT TO PULL.** The eyes returned to Wren. **ONLY THE YOUNG ONE. ONLY THE TEMPLE.**

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Wren would carry (or not carry) forever after: they understood. Standing in the god's drowned church, breathing borrowed water, they understood exactly what it meant to lose a holy place to other people's war. The elders had lied. The temple had lied. And still — *still* — it was whistling shutters and Brother Aldous's cupped hands and Sister Maren kneeling in spilled ink, saying *learn which stains are worth keeping.*

"Wren." Jude's hand found theirs. Squeezed. "You don't have to."

"Yes, I do," Wren said. "That's the whole point of a covenant, isn't it? Somebody pays." They looked up at the river-god. "If I give it — all of it, the whole temple, everything inside those walls — does it hurt you? Keeping it?"

The currents stilled. **IT WILL HURT ME THE WAY ALL KEPT THINGS HURT. I WILL HOLD IT CAREFULLY. I SWEAR IT ON MY WATER.**

"Then show me what happens after," Wren said. "Show me the shape of myself without it. Before I agree. That's my condition. That's my word-price."

Something like surprise moved through the god — a ripple, head to foot. **A NEGOTIATOR. GOOD. THE OLD COVENANT NEEDED MORE OF THOSE.** It raised one arm of drifting water and reeds, and the nave filled with a vision—

Wren saw themselves walking away from the chapel door into ordinary morning light, and the vision-self paused at some future threshold — a temple, a school, a building that smelled of ink and bread — and felt... nothing sharp. No wound. Just a faint, unplaceable ache, like a word on the tip of the tongue, like hearing a whistle in the wind and not knowing why the chest tightened. The vision-self went on inside. Was welcomed. Was fine.

**YOU WILL NOT BLEED,** the god said. **YOU WILL ONLY WONDER. MOST FOLKS CAN LIVE WITH WONDERING. SOME EVEN BUILD NEW HOMES FROM IT.**

Wren let out a breath they'd been holding since yesterday. Wondering. They could survive wondering.

"All right," they said. "I agree. Take it."

**WAIT—** Jude started.

But the river had already begun to move.

* * *

It didn't feel like drowning and it didn't feel like theft. It felt like tide.

Wren stood in the green-lit nave and the memory rose in them like water rising in a lock — gently, level by level. The whistling shutters lifted away first, and Wren watched them go with the strange clarity of a dreamer who knows they're dreaming. Then Brother Aldous's cupped hands, the fly trembling against his palms, his murmured *even flies owe debts*. Then Sister Maren kneeling in the black spill of ink, her face blurring even as Wren reached for it — *learn which stains are worth* — and the sentence ended mid-word, forever, the way the ghost-scribe had died mid-sentence, and Wren almost laughed at the symmetry of it, almost, through the tears they didn't realize were streaming.

The broth. The vellum smell. The tallow. Eleven years lifting away in layers, each one lighter than the last, until what remained was not emptiness but a clean, bright space — the shape of home with the home gone out of it. Like the negative space around a statue that's been removed. Like a bell tower leaning under water with no bell in it.

At the very last, one image lingered, refusing the tide: the temple door, evening rain, the beggar's prayer being answered. Wren clung to it — *that one, that one's mine, that's the night everything changed* — but the water was patient and thorough and fair, and it took that too, smoothing it away, leaving only a faint shine where it had been.

Then it was done. The tide receded. Wren stood in a drowned church knowing they had lived somewhere once, knowing it had mattered, feeling only a soft unplaceable ache where eleven years used to be — and, strangely, underneath the ache, a kind of lightness. As if something heavy had been set down after a very long carry.

**IT IS DONE,** the river-god rumbled. **AND HELD CAREFULLY. NOW — THE PASSAGE.**

The altar behind it split along a seam Wren hadn't seen, stone grinding aside to reveal stairs climbing out of the water, dry steps winding up into darkness that smelled of cold air and old incense. Real air. The crypt above the floodline.

Jude caught Wren's arm as they moved toward it. Their eyes were wet and furious and full of something fierce. "I wrote it down," they whispered. "All of it. Every detail you told me last night. It's in my pack. When this is over — when we're safe — I'm reading every word back to you, and maybe the river's claim ends where ink begins, and maybe it doesn't, but I promise you, Wren Ashgrove-of-nowhere-now, you'll get the choice of taking it back."

Wren searched their own heart for grief and found only the ache-shaped absence, and the strange gratitude beneath it, and nodded.

"Thank you," they said — and meant it toward the god as much as toward Jude, though they couldn't have said why, only that the words felt right, like an offering dropped into still water.

The river-god inclined its reeded head. **GO WELL, SMALL ACOLYTE. WHEN THE NEW COVENANT COMES — AND IT IS COMING, I CAN FEEL IT IN MY WATER — REMEMBER THAT SOME DOORS ONLY OPEN FROM THE INSIDE OF A SACRIFICE.**

They climbed. The stairs spiraled up through the tower's throat, past the drowned bell hanging silent overhead, and broke at last into a crypt lit by one shaft of gray daylight falling through a crack in the gorge wall. Dust lay thick on the floor. Against the far wall, on a plinth of black stone, something the size of a loaf of bread waited under a shroud of cobweb.

Marisol was already there — she'd taken some witch-path of her own, apparently, and stood with her staff planted, her face grave. She didn't congratulate them. She simply stepped aside.

Jude drew back the cobwebs.

The Covenant Stone was smaller than Wren had imagined — a rough oval of granite shot through with veins of quartz, and carved across its face in lines so old they were nearly worn smooth: thirteen names. Twelve of them crossed through, struck out in long diagonal strokes, deliberate, ceremonial. Only the thirteenth remained unmarked, its grooves still sharp, still waiting.

And fresh, cut into the plinth beneath it, in a hand Wren now recognized from the desecrated tomb at Hollowmere — precise, clinical, unhurried:

*One seat remains. We know who you seek. — V.*

Marisol read it and swore in three languages. Jude went white. Because the thirteenth name — the uncrossed one, the empty seat — the name carved in the stone was *Ashgrove.*
