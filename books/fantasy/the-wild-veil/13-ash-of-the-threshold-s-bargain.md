## 13. Ash-of-the-Threshold's Bargain

The drowned chapel had given up its stone, and now the Covenant Stone lay wrapped in Marisol's oilcloth at the bottom of my pack like a sleeping animal I was afraid to look at too long. We made camp that night in the hollow of a hillside where the wild thyme grew so thick it smelled like the whole slope was breathing. Wren slept first — they'd paid a memory for our passage and the paying had hollowed them; they kept reaching up to touch their own temple as if checking whether something was still there.

I could not sleep. So when the moth came, I was awake to see it.

It came out of the dark the way all of Ash-of-the-Threshold's works did: quietly, apologetically, as if it were sorry to intrude on the night. It was grey and soft-winged, and it settled on the back of my hand with the weight of a thought, and then it wasn't a moth anymore. The god sat cross-legged on the thyme beside me, small and ragged as ever, wearing its doorway like a coat turned inside out. Behind its shoulders I could see, faintly, the suggestion of every threshold it had ever haunted — doorframes, stiles, the gap beneath gates, the line where floorboards met. All of them worn smooth by feet.

"You're not sleeping," it said. Its voice was dry leaves and hinges.

"No one in this camp is sleeping well."

"True." It picked a sprig of thyme and did not eat it, only held it, turning it over in fingers like curled paper. "You have the Stone."

"You know we do."

"I know everything that crosses a boundary," said Ash-of-the-Threshold. "That is most of what I know. Things crossing. Most gods know larger things. Kessivar knows every secret carried on wings. The river-god under the chapel knew every drowned thing. I know doors. It has always seemed to me a respectable portion of the world."

I almost smiled. Almost. "You didn't come here to talk about your portfolio."

"No." The god set down the thyme. When it looked at me, its eyes were the color of candlelight seen through frosted glass — warm and utterly obscured. "You asked me once, in the scriptorium, what my price would be. I told you I asked nothing yet. You have carried my key across half a country. You have gathered oath-bearers. Tomorrow or the day after you will stand among the standing stones and try to do what thirteen people failed to hold together three generations ago. And still I have not named my price, because I hoped — foolishly, the way small things hope — that someone else would tell you first. That someone braver than me would break it gently."

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the slope, Marisol turned in her sleep and muttered a word in a language I didn't know but recognized anyway — an old god-name, one of the ones from the ledger margins.

"Tell me," I said.

"To reforge the covenant," the god said, "an oath-bearer must stand at the hinge of it. Not kneel before it. Not sign beside it. *Be* it. The old covenant was sealed in ink and stone, and ink fades and stone cracks — Vey proved that already, if nothing else. A new covenant must be sealed in something living. Someone living. One who stands between the realms and holds them apart and holds them together, forever, the way a door stands between two rooms. Neither inside nor outside. Never again either one."

"And this person—" My mouth was dry. "This hinge. What happens to them?"

"They surrender their name," said Ash-of-the-Threshold. "Entirely. Not hidden. Not changed. Erased. From every record, every memory, every tongue. Their mother's grief will have no shape to attach itself to. Their debts will dissolve because no debtor ever existed. Even the gods will forget them — I will forget them, and I am telling you this now precisely because afterward I *will* forget it, and I want someone in the world to have heard it while it was still true." The god's paper hands folded together. "They become the hinge. They feel both realms forever. Every prayer arriving. Every answer leaving. Every crossing, every threshold, every footstep over every boundary in the world. That is the price. That is why I have waited centuries."

"Because no one would pay."

"Because no one *could*, mostly. The covenant requires an oath-bearer — someone whose blood is bound into the original signing. There were twelve after your grandmother's generation. Eleven are dead. One is hiding. And of all the bloodlines that signed—" the god's voice gentled into something terrible, "—only yours was the price itself. Your grandmother did not merely sign the sealing, scribe. She signed it *with her line*. Her children's names, and her children's children's, written into the margin of the contract as surety. Collateral. When the seal weakened, the debt would come due, and it comes due through you. You are not only eligible to be the hinge, Jude Ashgrove. You are the only candidate left who carries the ink in the blood."

I looked at my hands. Ordinary hands. Ink-stained at the knuckles always, no matter how I scrubbed, a scribe's stain. I thought about the ledger margins, the scratched names, the thirteenth notch on the boundary stone, the burn of my blood-mark under the waxing moon. I thought about my grandmother weeping as she signed, and understood, suddenly and completely, that she had known. She had known what she was spending. Maybe she'd hoped the seal would outlast the debt. Maybe she'd hoped I'd never exist.

"How long?" I asked. My voice sounded far away, like someone else copying tithes.

"The choice must be made at the stones, under the full moon, with all voices willing. If the circle is broken — if even one seat refuses — there is no covenant, and the realms tear along the old seams. But hear me." The god leaned close, and its doorway-coat rippled, and for a moment I saw through it: a threshold, any threshold, the exact place where one world ended and another began, and how thin it was, how much thinner than anyone guessed. "Hear what I waited centuries to say to whoever finally stood here. You may refuse. Refusal is permitted. The gods will not blame you — most of them will not remember you long enough to blame you. The valley will drown in wild magic within a season, perhaps a year. People will die, and it will not be your doing; it will be the doing of everyone who signed the old bargain and everyone who profited by it. But you may refuse. I need you to know that the door is a door from both sides. That is the whole of my divinity, scribe. Doors open both ways."

"And if I pay? If I say yes?"

"Then you will never be cold, never be hungry, never be alone in the way mortals mean alone — you will hold every threshold in creation, and every crossing will pass through you. And you will also never again be *Jude*. No one will grieve you because no one will recall there was a person to grieve. Wren will wake one morning knowing they traveled with someone, knowing they loved them, knowing they lost them, and there will be a hole in their memory shaped exactly like you, and they will never learn its name." The god's frosted eyes held mine. "Marisol will keep your pack and wonder why she cannot throw it away. The High Scribe will find your cell in the scriptorium empty and feel a sorrow she cannot source. And somewhere in the world there will be a door that opens more kindly than other doors, and travelers will bless it without knowing why, and that blessing will be the only monument you get."

The fire burned down to embers. I became aware that I had been gripping the moth-wing key in my fist hard enough to mark my palm, though I had no memory of taking it out.

"Why are you telling me all of it?" I whispered. "Why not just — let me find out?"

"Because I am a small god," said Ash-of-the-Threshold, "and small gods cannot afford lies. The great ones can promise paradise and collect souls by the thousand, and no one audits them. But a god of thresholds lives or dies by whether the door does what it says. If I deceived you, every door in the world would grow a little heavier. So: I tell you true. The price is your name. The reward is everything, and nothing. And no one will remember you chose it."

*

Wren woke near dawn, as I knew they would — they woke badly now, surfacing from sleep like a diver afraid of what the surface held. They found me still sitting by the dead fire, the Covenant Stone between my knees, the moth-wing key in my open palm.

"You talked to it," Wren said. Not a question. They could read me the way I read ledgers; three hundred miles of road had taught them every entry.

"Yes."

"And?"

I told them. All of it. I owed them that — owed them the truth while I still existed to owe things. Wren listened without moving, and when I finished they were quiet for a long time, and then they said the thing I had been dreading and needing in equal measure:

"It has to be you. Doesn't it."

"It might not come to a choice at all," I said. "Vey is still out there. The circle might fail before—"

"Don't." Wren's voice cracked on it. "Don't give me the escape-hatch version. I gave a *memory* to a river god, Jude. I don't remember my initiation. I don't remember the smell of the scriptorium, I don't remember the High Scribe's voice, I don't remember why I used to flinch at bells. I paid a toll and I'm still standing and I still chose it. So don't hand me a maybe. Tell me what you're going to do."

I looked at them — this acolyte who had followed a heretic over the boundary stones rather than stay silent in a lying temple, who walked around with a hole in their head shaped like home and refused to let it make them cruel. The light was coming up grey behind them. The thyme smelled like forgiveness.

"I'm going to say yes," I said. "Not today. Not until I've seen it through — Vey, the circle, all of it. But when the moment comes, if it comes, I'm going to pay. Because I spent my whole life copying other people's debts into ledgers, Wren. Tithes, penalties, obligations, interest accruing against families who couldn't read the entries. I never once got to settle anything. This is the one account I can close myself." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "And honestly? Of everything the gods could take — my blood, my years, my heart — they chose the part I've been trying to lose since I was a child indentured to a ledger I didn't sign. My name was never mine anyway. It was collateral."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard you say."

"It's the truest."

Wren crawled across the cold ground and put their arms around me, and I let them, and we stayed like that while the sun came up over a world that still, briefly, knew my name.

*

We broke camp mid-morning. Marisol said little — but when I shouldered the pack with the Stone in it, she caught my wrist, and her grip was iron despite her age, and her marked palm pressed against my pulse.

"The god spoke to you," she said. "I felt the thyme go quiet."

"It did."

She studied my face the way she studied weather. Then she nodded once, slowly, and did not ask, and I loved her for it, and hated her a little, because her silence meant she'd already guessed, which meant the erasure had already begun — the world learning, ahead of schedule, how to forget me.

"We reach the standing stones tomorrow at dusk," she said. "The knight and the twins and the child will meet us there. Twelve seats, twelve voices — if the traitor keeps whatever truce brought them this far." Her thumb moved over my pulse, deliberate, memorizing. "Eat something, scribe. Whatever you're carrying, carry it fed."

That evening, as we crested the last ridge and the standing stones rose against a sky gone the color of hammered tin, the moth-wing key in my pocket grew warm — warmer than it had ever been — and I understood that somewhere beyond the ridge, at some threshold I could not yet see, a very small god was waiting at its door, holding it open, keeping its word.

And below us, on the road winding toward the stones, torchlight. Dozens of torches.

Vey had found us first.
