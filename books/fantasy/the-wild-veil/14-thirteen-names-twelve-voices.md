## 14. Thirteen Names, Twelve Voices

Marisol had spent thirty years learning how to find people who did not want to be found, and she had learned, in the end, that the trick was never to look for them at all.

You looked for what they left behind. A knight left a wake of repaired fences and paid debts and taverns where no one remembered his face but everyone remembered his coin. A shepherd left wool caught on hedges in a pattern no ordinary flock could make — braided, deliberate, like a signature. A child left footprints that bloomed.

So Marisol did not ride toward the disgraced knight. She rode toward the stories of good deeds done badly, the charity with a temper attached, and three days out from the drowned chapel she found him mending a bridge nobody had asked him to mend, on a road nobody used anymore, because that was the sort of thing Ser Aldric Vane did now: useless penance, done thoroughly.

He heard the horses coming and did not turn. His sword lay across two stones beside him like something he was babysitting rather than wielding. Grey in his beard, grey in his cloak, hands scarred in the specific way of a man who had gripped a weapon for forty years and hated it for thirty of them.

"Marisol of the Hedge," he said, without looking up. "They told me you were dead."

"They tell everyone I'm dead. It saves time." She swung down from the horse and stood watching him fit a stone into the bridge's broken span. "The covenant is waking up, Aldric. The thirteenth moon is rising. We need our oath-bearers."

"I know."

That stopped her. "You *know*?"

"My knee knows. My knee has been screaming for six days." He finally turned, and she saw it in him — the old mark, the oath-brand that had faded to almost nothing over the decades, now dark and hot as fresh ink. He rolled up his trouser leg without being asked and showed her: the sigil of the standing stones, burned into his flesh, weeping light at its edges. "It's calling us home. All of us. Whether we come or not."

"It called you here instead," Marisol said. "To a bridge. In the middle of nowhere."

"Someone has to hold things together while everything falls apart." He set the stone, pressed it into the mortar with a craftsman's patience that sat strangely on a soldier's body. "I broke an oath once, Marisol. The only way I know to be sorry is one stone at a time."

"The oath you broke was *theirs*. You refused to stand at the sealing. That's why they cast you out — you were the only one of thirteen who said no."

"And then I stood by and let it happen anyway." He wiped his hands on his ragged cloak. "Refusing to sin isn't the same as fighting it. I've had a long time to learn the difference." He looked past her, at Wren and Jude sitting their horses on the road — the scribe with the blood-mark wrapped in bandages, the acolyte with hollowed eyes where a memory of home used to be. "Is that them? The hinge?"

"This is Jude Ashgrove," Marisol said. "And this is going to sound strange, but the gods have been asking after you by name."

Aldric Vane stood very still for a moment. Then he picked up his sword, considered it, and drove it point-first into the earth at the edge of the bridge like a grave-marker, and left it there.

"All right," he said. "All right. I'll need a horse. And someone to finish my bridge."

* * *

The twins were harder.

Not because they were hiding — because they weren't hiding anywhere. One day their flocks grazed the high pastures above the Thornwater valley; the next day, according to every shepherd between there and the coast, they had always been somewhere else. Their names changed with the villages. They were Brann and Sorrel, or Corm and Lily, or nothing at all. They had been walking the boundary lands for so many years that the land had learned their habits and started practicing them.

Marisol solved it the way she solved most things: she stopped chasing and made herself findable. She pitched her camp in the center of a wide moor where four ancient sheep-paths crossed, built a fire, and set out seven cups of tea, which was two more than her company required.

The twins arrived at dusk, out of two different directions, as though they had simply been waiting for the invitation to expire.

They were older than she expected — sixty, perhaps seventy, weathered to the color of the moor itself, with faces so alike they might have been one face worn by two people. Their flocks came with them: forty sheep that walked in perfect silence, and whose eyes, when they caught the firelight, flashed briefly gold.

"You're the hedge-witch," said the one on the left.

"You're the one who failed," said the one on the right.

"I'm the one who's still trying," Marisol said. "Sit. Drink. Then tell me why your sheep have god-light in their eyes."

They sat. They drank. Between them — finishing each other's sentences, or perhaps just sharing one sentence between two mouths — they told her.

They had been children when the sealing happened, shepherds' kids on the boundary farms, and the old gods had liked children best because children still left offerings without being taught. When the covenant broke, the gods had poured what remained of themselves into whatever vessels would hold them. Most people ran. The twins had stood in a doorway with their flock around them and said, in one voice, *you can shelter in us.*

"So we carry them," said the left twin — Brann, maybe, or Corm.

"A little piece each," said the right one. "Twelve small gods, folded up small as seeds. We walk the boundaries so they can see the sky."

"That's why the land moves around you," Marisol breathed. "You're not lost. You're *escorting*."

"We don't use that word. It makes the sheep nervous." The right twin looked at Jude then, really looked, and whatever the twins saw in the scribe's blood-marked skin made them both go quiet. When the left one spoke again, his voice had changed — deeper, layered, several throats agreeing on one sound. "Hinge. You smell like grandmother's contract and burnt marginalia. You smell like the last page of a book we thought would end differently."

"I'm doing my best," Jude said. It was the first thing Marisol had heard them say in two days, and it came out cracked.

"Do better," said both twins gently, in unison. "We buried a world once because everyone did their best too late."

But they came. Of course they came. They whistled up their silent flock, and the twelve small gods folded in the sheep's warm bodies walked with them toward the standing stones, and Marisol watched them go and felt something in her chest she had not felt since her own casting-out: hope, fragile as a moth's wing, beating anyway.

* * *

The god-touched child they found by accident, or the child found them, which amounted to the same thing.

She appeared on the ninth morning, sitting on a milestone at the road's edge, swinging her legs. Eight years old, maybe nine, in a dress that had been mended by at least four different loving hands, with a crown of pale feathers growing directly from her hair — not tied there, *growing*, preening and rustling in the wind. Her shadow, Marisol noticed, did not match her posture. It sat perfectly still while the girl moved, and it was smiling when the girl wasn't.

"Well met," the girl said, with the odd formality of a child repeating adult words. "I'm Ivy. The lady in my head says you're collecting us."

Wren nearly fell off the wagon. Jude went white as paper. But Marisol had grown up among hedge-folk, and she had seen god-touched before — rare, and usually tragic, and never once boring.

"Does she now," Marisol said, crouching to the child's level. "And what lady is that?"

"The river one. The one under the chapel." Ivy frowned, concentrating. "She says she's sorry about the boy's memory. She says tolls are tolls, but she kept it safe, and if the new covenant holds, she'll give it back with interest." She pointed at Wren. "You're the boy. You gave away home. She says that was brave and also stupid, and she's never sure which one matters more."

Wren's mouth worked. No sound came out. Jude put a hand on the acolyte's shoulder, and Wren stared at the child as if staring could open the memory locked inside a river.

"Where are your parents, Ivy?" Marisol asked.

"The village gave me to the temple when the feathers came in. The temple gave me back when the shadows started talking. Now everybody gives me away." The girl shrugged with the enormous resilience of the very young. "The lady says you'll keep me. She says you have room, since you lost yourself years ago and never filled the space."

Marisol laughed despite herself — a short, startled bark. Even the gods' smallest messengers had a hedge-witch's manners.

"She's right," Marisol said. "Climb up. Don't touch anything that hums."

Ivy scrambled onto the wagon between Jude and Wren, and immediately began interrogating the scribe about whether ink tasted different when it was holy, and whether the moon really had a back side, and whether it hurt when your own name didn't fit you anymore. Jude answered each question with increasing bewilderment and, Marisol noticed, increasing warmth. By evening the child was asleep against the scribe's bandaged arm, feather-crown folded flat, shadow curled beneath her like a sleeping dog.

"Thirteen seats," Aldric murmured, riding close. "Count them off."

Marisol counted on her fingers, the old ritual count, the one carved into every boundary stone. "Jude. You. The twins carrying twelve small gods between them. Ivy, who is either one seat or several, depending on how you read her shadow. Me." She hesitated. "My mark counts, even failed. That's eleven — twelve if the twins split."

"And?" Aldric prompted.

"And the twelfth," Marisol said quietly, "is the traitor."

The word dropped into the campfire circle and sat there, ugly and alive.

"You're certain the seat must be filled?" Jude asked. Ivy slept on through the tension, feather-crown twitching with dreams.

"The covenant was written for thirteen voices. Not twelve plus a gap. The seal broke *because* the thirteenth voice went silent mid-word — the grandmother's signing was the twelfth, and the one who betrayed them all was the thirteenth, and the covenant has been limping on a broken foot for three generations." Marisol fed the fire a branch and watched the sparks climb. "Every oath-bearer's line, every relic, every scrap of the old rite assumes thirteen. We gather twelve and try to speak it whole, and the words will slide sideways. I've seen half-rituals before. Half-rituals are how you get rivers that eat memories and moons that turn wrong."

"Then we find them," Aldric said. "Whoever they are. Whatever they did."

"Find them." Marisol laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "Ser Aldric, do you know why no one has ever found the thirteenth? Because the thirteenth didn't run away. The thirteenth went *home*. Went back behind the veil, into the valley, into the temple, and took the holiest office there as a hiding place. Who better to guard the lie than the one who wrote it? Who safer from suspicion than the traitor promoted to keeper of records?"

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, one of the twins' sheep lowed once, softly, a sound like a struck bell.

"The High Scribe," Wren said slowly. The acolyte's voice was thin, uncertain — a person reaching for a shape in fog, grasping at the outline of a life no longer theirs. "I don't— I can't remember her face. I remember *that* there was a High Scribe, the way you remember there's weather. But I remember the elders whispering, near the end. About her leaving. About following someone out of the valley." Wren's eyes found Jude. "About following *you*."

Jude went very still. Ivy stirred, muttered, settled.

"She signed nothing," Marisol said. "That's the thing that's haunted me thirty years. Twelve signatures on the old covenant, and a thirteenth space left blank — deliberately blank, because the thirteenth wanted deniability. Wanted clean hands in the record even while dirtying them everywhere else. A scribe's trick, that. An ink-trick." She looked at Jude. "You'd know the type."

"Don't," Jude said. Just that. Quietly. But their hand had gone to their bandaged forearm as if the blood-mark burned, and Marisol guessed it did.

"I'm not saying it's her," Marisol said. "I'm saying the seat is empty, the moon is waxing toward full, and the only person alive who knows the covenant's true terms from the inside — who helped build the cage — may already be walking toward us. Or away. Traitors get to choose, that's their whole privilege." She stood, brushing off her skirts, and surveyed her strange company: a knight without a sword, twins with a flock of sleeping gods, a child crowned in feathers, a scribe marked in blood, an acolyte missing home. Fragile. Ragged. Impossibly insufficient for what the thirteenth moon would demand.

And yet.

"Tomorrow we make for the standing stones," she announced. "We take our seats. We practice the old words until our throats bleed, and we pray — loudly, rudely, with offerings — that the twelfth voice finds us before Vey does."

* * *

It was Ivy who woke them, an hour before dawn, standing in the cold at the edge of camp with her arms spread and her shadow spread wider, both of them pointing east.

"He's coming," the girl said, and then, in a voice that was not entirely hers — layered, deep, several throats agreeing — "the one who wrote the blank line. He walks with the inquisitor's fire behind him and his own guilt ahead of him, and he carries a book with a moth-wing pressed between its pages."

Marisol was on her feet before the echo faded. Down the eastern slope, where the moor met the first grey suggestion of morning, a single figure climbed toward them — old, bent, robed in travel-stained temple grey, leaning on a staff, moving with the stubborn pace of someone who had walked a very long way to confess.

Behind that figure, far below at the treeline, other lights moved. Lanterns. Too many lanterns, arranged in a pattern Marisol recognized from thirty years ago and had hoped never to see again: a hunting circle, closing slow and patient as a noose.

The figure crested the rise and stopped, breathing hard, and pulled back its hood, and Marisol saw the face of the High Scribe of the Temple of the Quiet Ledger — older than legend, eyes red-rimmed from days of weeping or wind or both — looking past all of them, straight at Jude.

"I have come to fill the empty seat," the old woman said. "God forgive me. There is so much you need to know about what we sealed away — and what Halvard Vey means to unleash instead."
