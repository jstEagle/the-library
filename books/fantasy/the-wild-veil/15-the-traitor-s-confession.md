## 15. The Traitor's Confession

She came at dusk, on the day the river learned to speak.

Jude heard her before they saw her — the particular cadence of temple sandals on wet stone, that measured tread drilled into every novice until walking became a form of prayer. For three days they had been camped in the hollow below the drowned chapel, and for three days Jude had been listening to Marisol argue with Kessivar about who would carry the Covenant Stone, and for three days they had not thought once of home.

Then: sandals. Measured. Familiar.

They turned, and Mother Cassiara stood at the edge of the firelight with rain in her grey hair and her hands folded the way she had always folded them — left over right, the posture of a woman waiting for an account to be settled.

The hollow went silent. Wren made a sound like a struck string. Marisol's knife was out and open before Jude could blink, though she held it low, angled toward the mud rather than the woman, which from Marisol was a kind of courtesy.

"You," Jude said. The word came out broken into pieces. *You burned the marginalia. You ordered it. You stood in the scriptorium and watched the smoke rise off Vey's brazier while I copied names into my sleeve.*

"Me," Mother Cassiara agreed. Her voice was exactly as it had been over fifteen years of morning lessons: dry, patient, unhurried. "And I have walked eleven days behind you, child, through country that rearranges itself out of spite, so that I might say what I should have said in the scriptorium, if I had been braver than I am." She looked at the fire, then at each of them in turn — Wren, hollow-eyed from the memory they'd traded; Marisol, marked and mistrustful; the moth-wing key hanging at Jude's throat where it had hung since the night a small god first came asking. "I am the twelfth oath-bearer. And I am the one who helped seal them."

* * *

No one moved. The fire cracked and spat. Somewhere above them, in the flooded chapel, water dripped its slow count onto stone.

"That's not possible," Wren said finally. It was the same voice they used when reciting doctrine, and they seemed to hear it themselves, because their face flushed. "The oath-bearers were — the texts say they were—"

"The texts say they were thirteen heroes who gave everything to save the valley from the wild gods." Cassiara lowered herself onto a flat rock at the fire's edge, uninvited, the way she had sat down at every lesson bench Jude had ever occupied. "Yes. I wrote those texts. Three of them. I was very careful."

Marisol's knife did not lower. "Thirteen seats. We've found six living or dead. If you're the twelfth—"

"Then the thirteenth is empty, and has been since the beginning. That is the part no text records." Cassiara held her hands to the fire. They were old hands, ink-stained at the knuckle the way all High Scribes' hands were, but Jude noticed now — noticed as if seeing them for the first time — a scar across the right palm, old and white and deliberate. A cut made by a blade in a circle. An oath taken. "Sit down, all of you. What I have to tell will take until moonrise, and you'll want to be sitting when your legs stop trusting you."

Nobody sat. She began anyway.

* * *

"I was twenty-two years old," she said, "and I believed, as you believe, that the gods were the valley's shepherds. Ash-of-the-Threshold kept our doorways. The river-god under the chapel sang our children calm. Kessivar carried our messages and our dead. We paid them in names and songs and small offerings, and they kept us, and it seemed a fair trade because we had never known anything else.

"Then the famine year came, and with it the thing we had no name for. The elders called it *the Hungering*. Whether it was a god gone rotten or something older that even gods fear, I cannot tell you — I have spent fifty years not knowing. But it ate worship the way a fire eats a house. Not devotion — *devotion*, the raw substance of belief, stripped from the faithful like marrow from bone. Whole villages knelt in the fields praying until they forgot why they were kneeling. Until they forgot their own children's faces, because every scrap of love they owned had been poured into that open mouth."

Jude's blood-mark prickled cold beneath their skin. They thought of the visions the waxing moon had shown them — their grandmother weeping at a table of stones — and said nothing.

"Twelve of us were chosen. Young enough to survive the working, old enough to be trusted with it. Your grandmother was the eldest of us at thirty-one, and the only one who read contracts for a living, which is why the others let her draft the terms." Cassiara's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "We believed we were building a wall. A seal between the valley and the wild, so that nothing — nothing — could cross in either direction. And here is the confession you actually want, child, so listen closely: the seal worked. It worked perfectly. The Hungering starved against it and died, and the valley slept safe for three generations, and every child born inside those boundary stones has never once felt a god's hand on their shoulder.

"And the price—" She stopped. Started again, more quietly. "The price was written into the covenant's root, and we knew it when we signed. A seal must be fed. Thirteen lives bound to thirteen moons, renewed each turn of the great cycle, and the bloodline of the principal signatory pledged as the anchor — generation after generation, indentured, in perpetuity, until the covenant either lapsed or was remade." She lifted her eyes to Jude. "Your family did not owe the Temple a debt, Jude Ashgrove. The Temple owed *your family* one. Your grandmother's signature purchased every safe night you have ever slept, and the Ledger recorded it backward, as debts do when the powerful hold the pen. You were never paying for a crime. You were paying for everyone else's salvation."

The fire had burned low. No one fed it.

"I didn't know," Wren whispered. "All those years copying tithes—"

"No one knew. That was the mercy clause of the arrangement, if you like — ignorance as anesthesia." Cassiara spread her scarred palm toward the light. "I took this oath at twenty-two. Then I spent fifty years keeping the ledger of the lie, because the alternative was telling the truth to people who would tear the seal open in grief and rage and let whatever remained of the Hungering's kin come pouring back through. I told myself it was protection. Some nights, I still believe it was."

"And other nights?" Marisol asked.

"Other nights I remember that the seal also locked the kind gods out. That Ash-of-the-Threshold — who once carried a dying child's soul gently as an egg — has been standing outside a door for seventy years, half-starved on scraps of forgotten prayer, while we told ourselves he was a story." Her voice frayed, just slightly, and Jude understood with a jolt that this woman had loved the small god, in the way scribes love the words they are forced to erase. "So when the thirteenth notch appeared on the boundary stone, I did not send word to the capital. I sent word to no one. I packed one bag and followed the only person in the valley whose blood the covenant recognized."

"Why me?" Jude asked. It came out smaller than they intended. "Why follow me?"

"Because you are the hinge, whether you accept it or not. Because your grandmother hid something in her signature — I have suspected it for decades, and Halvard Vey suspects it too, and only the principal line can find it." Cassiara leaned forward, and the firelight caught the deep lines of her face and made them look, suddenly, like tally marks. "And because Vey's answer to all of this is worse than the original sin, and someone had to warn you before his agents found you."

* * *

"He wants to renew the seal," Jude said slowly. The pieces had been assembling themselves since the desecrated tomb, since the harvested bones, since the knight's blood on the standing stones. "But not as it was."

"Not remotely as it was." Cassiara drew a breath. "The old covenant bound thirteen mortals *willingly*. It cost them everything, but they chose it, chose it together, chose it in full knowledge — that was your grandmother's doing, insisting every signer understand every term. Vey means to build a new seal with no oath-bearers at all. Relics instead of people. Bones instead of blood. And at the center of it, instead of thirteen consenting voices — himself. One arbiter. One man holding the pen, forever, deciding what crosses the boundary and what does not, what is prayed to and what is erased." Her eyes went around the circle of them, hard now. "Do you understand? He will not lock the gods out. He will keep them as livestock. Fed on rationed worship, milked for miracles, permitted to exist only at his discretion. He calls it mercy because he genuinely believes the alternative is the Hungering. He is wrong, but he is wrong *sincerely*, which makes him far more dangerous than any villain who knows he sins."

Wren had both arms wrapped around their own knees. "The texts — the ones you wrote—"

"Were true as far as they went. I have never lied cleanly; I lie the way the Ledger lies, by omission, by ordering, by what I leave in the margins." A ghost of the old classroom dryness. "Which, incidentally, is why I ordered the marginalia burned the moment the prayers started landing again. Not because the names were dangerous. Because I recognized the handwriting of the predecessor who wrote them — my own teacher, the eleventh seat — and I knew that if Vey's people found proof the Temple had always known, he would accelerate everything. I was buying time. It appears I bought very little."

Marisol spoke for the first time in a long while. "You're asking us to trust you."

"I'm asking you for nothing. That is the point of confession." Cassiara rose, stiffly, an old woman's rising, and stood with her back to the fire so her shadow fell long across all of them. "I betrayed the gods to protect mortals. Vey will betray mortals to control gods. And the child I taught to write will have to forge something neither of us dared — a covenant with no master, no prisoner, and no clean hands anywhere in it. I cannot tell you it will work. I can only tell you that the alternative futures are all worse, and I have spent fifty years studying the alternatives."

She reached into her robe and drew out a scroll — sealed with plain wax, unmarked, the humblest thing Jude had ever seen a High Scribe carry.

"Your grandmother's original draft," she said. "The terms before the terms. I stole it from the deep archive the night I left. Whatever mercy-clause she hid in the ink of the final contract — the seed of it is here, in her first handwriting, before the others softened it." She held it out to Jude. "I am giving you the weapon I was too frightened to use for fifty years."

Jude took it. The scroll was lighter than it had any right to be, the way the moth-wing key was light, the way true things often weighed less than lies. Their grandmother's hand — the same slanting script Jude had inherited, the same impatient crossing of t's — waited under the wax.

Above the hollow, through a tear in the clouds, the moon showed herself: swollen, a hair past full, climbing toward her thirteenth turn.

And far off to the east, down in the dark where the road should have been, torches. Dozens. Moving in formation. Not searching anymore.

Coming.

"They've found us," Wren breathed.

"Yes," said Mother Cassiara, turning toward the light with fifty years of guilt and one night of resolve written across her face. "So I suggest, children, that we reach the standing stones before Halvard Vey reaches us — and that when he does, we show him a covenant he never imagined possible."

She paused at the edge of the firelight, and added, very quietly, in the voice of a teacher assigning the hardest lesson:

"And this time, no one signs alone."
