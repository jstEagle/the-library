## 1. The Ledger of Quiet Hours

The candle had burned down to its last finger-width when Coral Blackwood noticed she was copying the same name twice.

She stopped, quill hovering over the vellum, and let the mistake sit there a moment like a small rebellion. Down in the valley the festival lanterns would be going up by now — paper and willow-wisp light strung along the millrace, the whole of Vell glowing soft as an ember in a cupped hand — and here she was in the scriptorium of the Temple of Quiet Hours, transcribing the debts of strangers for the eleventh year of her indenture, with eleven more stacked behind it like wet firewood that would never catch.

*Marla Thessaly, widow, three crowns owed against the autumn loom.* She'd written it once at the top of the page. Her hand had wandered back to it out of pure hunger for anything that wasn't the column of figures directly beneath.

Coral scraped the errant line away with her knife, blotted the ghost of it clean, and told herself — as she told herself every night, in the same flat tone, the tone of a woman reading terms aloud — that eleven years was not forever. That time passed whether you counted it or not. That counting was, in fact, her one talent, and so she might as well count.

The scriptorium held six desks, and five of them were empty. The other scribes were asleep in their cells or, if they were Brother Anselm, awake somewhere doing whatever senior scribes did after dark. Coral worked nights because nights were cheaper. The Temple charged its debtors for candles; the rate was posted on the refectory wall in letters so fine you needed a scribe's eye to read them, which Coral supposed was the joke. But the night hours cost less than the day ones, and the silence cost nothing at all, and silence was the only luxury Vell had ever offered her in abundance.

Outside, through the leaded window, she could see the first lanterns of the ward-wall rising along the ridge. Tiny gold points, strung up the ancient stones like beads on a black thread. Once a year the whole valley climbed to the wall and hung its hopes on it — *for good harvests, for safe passage, for the wards to hold another winter* — and once a year Coral watched from below, or from a window, or from nowhere at all.

This year, maybe, would be different. She had been building toward different the way a mason builds an arch: stone by patient stone, each transcription a little faster than the last, every debt ledger finished early, every margin clean. Master Quillon had begun, in his dry way, to notice. Two days ago he had looked at her completed copy of the millers' ledger and said, "Hm," which from Quillon was practically applause.

She intended to trade on it. She wanted permission to go up to the wall on festival night. Not as a debtor, not under escort, not carrying the Temple's ink-stained banner of supervision — just to stand among her own people and touch, for one hour, the world outside the ledger.

Eleven years left. She could price one evening of freedom precisely, because pricing things was what she did.

*Marla Thessaly, widow,* Coral wrote again, correctly this time, and moved down the column.

* * *

The trouble with being a scribe, Coral had learned long ago, was that you saw everything and were permitted to understand none of it.

Debt ledgers came up from the valley offices twice a week, bound in grey leather, stamped with the Temple's seal: a closed door above an open eye. She copied the names, the sums, the interest accrued, the terms of service rendered in lieu of coin. She copied them into the fair books that went into the archive-crypts beneath the temple, where no debtor would ever see them again. What happened to the originals afterward was not her business. Curiosity, Brother Anselm liked to say, was a debt like any other, and it compounded.

But Coral's eyes were fast, faster than her conscience some nights, and tonight the original ledger lying open beside her fair copy was not the usual run of loom-debts and harvest-advances.

It was thin. Thin ledgers were rare, and rare meant important, and important meant someone senior would come collect it soon. It listed no sums. It listed names — perhaps forty of them, in a cramped old hand — and beside each name a date and a single word in the margin. Most of the words she couldn't make out in the candlelight without leaning close enough that anyone walking past the door would see her lean.

She copied two more lines of Marla Thessaly's honest, boring debt and made herself look at nothing but the fair book for the space of ten breaths.

Then she leaned.

The first name on the list was *Aldous Blackwood.*

Her own name. Her father's name, dead these nine years, drowned in the millrace the winter the ice took him. Beside it, a date she didn't recognize — a year before her birth — and the marginal word, written in that cramped antique script: *keeper?* With the question mark struck through and rewritten as something firmer. A confirmation. Beside several of the other names, the same word. Beside others, different words entirely: *extinct. married out. erased.*

Erased. Underlined twice.

Coral sat very still. The candle flame stood straight as a pin. Somewhere far below, the temple bell tolled the third watch, and the sound seemed to pass through her rather than around her, the way cold water passes through a swimmer.

Her family had never owned land, never held office, never owed more than any other weaving family owed. They were nobody. Nobody did not get entries in thin ledgers. Nobody got the word *keeper* written beside their name in a script older than the current High Scribe's grandmother.

Footsteps in the corridor. Measured ones. The particular tread of shoes worn by a man who had walked these halls for thirty years and expected the halls to move aside.

Coral snapped upright, pulled the fair book square before her, and set her quill moving. By the time Brother Anselm filled the doorway — tall, grey-robed, his face the color and texture of old parchment — she was deep in the fourth line of Marla Thessaly's account, the picture of dutiful industry.

"You're late with the millers' fair copy," he said.

"It's done." She turned it toward him without looking up, because looking up invited conversation, and conversation with Anselm was priced by the word and he always collected.

He crossed the room anyway. That was the second unusual thing about the night. Anselm did not cross rooms for completed work; he sent novices. He stood over her desk, and Coral felt his attention slide off her neat columns and onto the original ledger lying at her elbow, and something in the air between them changed temperature.

"You've been reading."

"Copying," Coral said. "As instructed."

Anselm picked up the thin ledger. He did it casually, the way a man picks up a thing he has picked up a hundred times, but his thumb found the page she had been reading as though guided there, and Coral watched his eyes move down the column of names and stop, precisely, where hers had stopped.

For one heartbeat the scriptorium was so quiet she could hear the candle burning.

Then Anselm carried the ledger to the brazier.

There was no drama in it. That was the horror of it, Coral thought later — how little drama there was. He simply opened the grey cover, found the page, and fed it into the coals the way a cook feeds a scrap of fat to a fire. The vellum caught slowly, reluctantly; old vellum always fought. Names curled and blackened. *Blackwood* went up in a breath of flame that smelled, absurdly, faintly of honey.

"What—" Coral started, and heard her own voice crack, and hated it.

Anselm turned. His expression was not anger. It was worse than anger; it was the mild, tired patience of a man closing a ledger himself. "You are indentured to this temple for eleven years, four months, and sixteen days," he said. "You have been a credit to us. Fast hands, clean work, no questions asked until tonight." He dusted ash from his fingers with a small cloth produced from his sleeve. "Questions are not free, Coral. You know our rates."

"I wasn't asking a question. I was working."

"The page you touched is not your work, was never assigned to you, and does not exist." He said it simply, the way one states that water is wet. "Now it exists even less. Consider the matter closed."

"The names," Coral said, and could not stop herself, could not apply the discipline she applied to everything else, the discipline that had kept her alive and solvent and unbroken through eleven years of candle-smoke. "Whose names were they?"

Something flickered behind Anselm's eyes. If she hadn't spent a decade learning to read faces the way she read contracts — quickly, hungrily, for the clause everyone else skimmed — she would have missed it. It wasn't guilt. It was recognition. As if her question had confirmed something he'd been waiting years to confirm.

"Names are just marks on vellum," he said. "And vellum burns. That is the mercy of the craft. Remember it." He set the ruined ledger's cover back on her desk — only the cover, the contents now a skin of black flakes in the brazier — and paused at the door.

"Master Quillon tells me you wish to attend the festival at the ward-wall," he said, not turning around.

Coral kept her breathing even. So the arch had been noticed after all. Noticed, weighed, and now apparently brought out like a coin to be tested between teeth. "I do. I'll have the weavers' ledger finished a week early to pay for it."

"I'm sure you would," Anselm said. "I'll speak to Quillon." And he was gone, his footsteps fading down the corridor, measured as ever, unhurried, a man whose business was all concluded.

Coral looked at the brazier. The last flakes of vellum had gone white and were crumbling into themselves. Forty names. Her father among them. The word *keeper*, confirmed, and the word *erased*, underlined twice, and none of it existing anymore except in the memory of a debtor who had been foolish enough to read.

She thought about the way he'd said *I'll speak to Quillon*. Not yes. Not no. A promissory note, drawn on an account she didn't control.

Coral Blackwood had grown up in the Temple of Quiet Hours, and the Temple taught one lesson above all others, in every prayer and every contract and every candle-rate: *nothing is given; everything is traded; read the terms.* She had lived by those terms for eleven years. She knew exactly what she was worth per hour, per page, per year of her life.

She also knew, sitting alone in the guttering light with the smell of burned vellum sweetening the air, that somewhere in the grey ledgers of the world there was an entry with her name in it that she had never signed. A debt she hadn't incurred. A word beside her father's name that someone had decided, decades ago, should be followed by a question mark — and then, quietly, deliberately, not.

Eleven years, four months, sixteen days. That was the sentence she'd always believed she was serving.

Tonight, watching ash settle in the brazier like grey snow falling upward, Coral understood for the first time that it might not be a sentence at all. It might be a term of payment. And she had never once been shown what she was paying for.

She cleaned her quill. She squared the fair book. She wrote *Marla Thessaly, widow, three crowns owed against the autumn loom* one final time, in her best hand, and reached for the next page.

Above the ridge, unseen through the leaded glass, the festival lanterns climbed the dark line of the ward-wall — and on that wall, on the oldest stone, in characters no living soul in the valley could read, something waited that had her family's name written all over it.
