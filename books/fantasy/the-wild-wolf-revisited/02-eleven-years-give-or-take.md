## 2. Eleven Years, Give or Take

The candle had burned down to its last finger-width when Coral finished the final line of the Aldermoor grain ledger.

She set down the pen and flexed her fingers, one at a time, working the ache out of the joints. Eleven hours at the desk. Her back had long since stopped complaining and settled into a dull, permanent negotiation with the chair, and her eyes had that particular dryness that came from too many small figures in too little light. On the page before her, in a hand so neat it looked typeset rather than written, lay the sum of Aldermoor's harvest debt to the Temple of Quiet Hours: four hundred and six crowns, eleven shillings, payable over nine years, interest compounding annually on the feast of Quiet Hours itself.

Coral had copied that same figure — or ones shaped very much like it — for six years now. She knew the arithmetic of debt the way other people knew prayers. She could look at a village's yield and tell you, before the assessor's report arrived, how many years of their children's labor the Temple would claim. She had once calculated, idly, during a slow week, exactly how much a single hour of her own transcription was worth against her outstanding balance.

It came to less than the candle burning beside her hand.

That was the joke she never told anyone. The Temple charged its indentured scribes for their candles. It was itemized on the quarterly statement pinned inside her cell door: *tallow, twelve sticks, two coppers.* The light by which she worked off her debt was itself a debt. She had spent one entire evening, early in her indenture, trying to find the flaw in that circle — the place where the accounting broke open and let you out. There wasn't one. There never was. Whoever designed the ledgers of Quiet Hours had been very thorough, and very patient, and had understood something Coral was still learning: that a debt didn't need to be large to hold you. It only needed to renew itself faster than you could pay it.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark a moment, listening to the scriptorium settle around her. The great room held forty desks; hers was the last occupied. Through the high windows she could see the first grey suggestion of dawn over the temple roofs, and beyond them, past the bell tower, a thin dark line on the northern horizon where the ward-wall stood.

Eleven years, give or take.

*Give or take* was the Temple's phrase, not hers. It appeared in every contract of indenture, always in the same clause, always in the same careful language: *term of service shall be fifteen years from date of signature, give or take days accrued through negligence, wastage, or fault.* The *give or take* only ever took. In six years, Coral's fifteen had become sixteen and a half. A fever in her third winter had cost her forty days. A dropped inkwell — an accident, though the assessor had recorded it as *wastage through carelessness* — had cost her twenty more. She kept her own ledger of these deductions, hidden in the binding of a psalter she'd repaired herself, because someone had to remember what the official numbers erased.

Four hundred and seventy-three days. That was the *take*, so far.

She rose, gathered the finished ledger under her arm, and went to deliver it.

* * *

Brother Halven was already awake, which was unusual, and already unpleasant, which was not.

He stood at the delivery alcove with his hands folded into his sleeves and his face arranged into the expression he wore for inferiors: a mild, patient disappointment, as if her continued existence were a clerical error he'd simply given up contesting. He was senior scribe for the western collections, second-cantor of the Hour of Ledgers, and he had been Coral's superior since her first day. He was also, though no one said it aloud, the reason the scriptorium ran as fast as it did — fear traveled through those forty desks like ink through wet paper.

"The Aldermoor account," Coral said, setting the ledger on the shelf. "Complete. I checked the interest calculations twice."

"How diligent." Halven did not touch it. "And the Vessery tithes?"

"Tomorrow evening. The original documents arrived late — there was water damage to the third folio, and I had to reconstruct two entries from the fair-copy."

"You reconstructed them." His voice went soft, which was worse than sharp. "From memory."

"From the fair-copy, Brother. It's kept in the west archive. I compared character by character."

"Mm." He looked at her for a long moment, and Coral understood, with the weary clarity of six years' practice, that accuracy itself was the offense. A scribe who reconstructed damaged folios from the archive was a scribe who had access to the archive. A scribe with access to the archive was a scribe who read things. And scribes who read things — that was the root of every trouble the Temple had ever had with its indentured class.

"Your speed is becoming a topic of conversation," Halven said. "Among the brothers. They wonder what a debtor needs with so much time."

"Time is the one thing I'm short of, Brother."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a move made correctly. He inclined his head a fraction. "See that the Vessery account is flawless. Flawless work buys quiet. Quiet is the whole of our order's purpose, after all."

He turned and left, and Coral stood alone in the alcove with her heart beating too fast, doing the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing: *flawless work buys quiet.* Which meant imperfect work bought noise. Which meant noise was currency, if you were brave enough to spend it.

She filed that thought away in the psalter-binding of her mind, next to four hundred and seventy-three days, and went to breakfast.

* * *

Doss found her at the noon meal, wedging himself onto the bench beside her with the unbothered bulk of a man who had long ago stopped apologizing for taking up space. He was a lay brother, kitchen-assigned, broad as a door and nearly as talkative when food was involved, and he had been her friend since her second year, when he'd smuggled her a heel of honey-bread the night her mother's last letter had finally been declared lost in transit and officially ceased to exist.

"You've got your negotiating face on," he said, tearing bread in half. "I can see it from across the refectory. That little line, here." He tapped the space between his own eyebrows.

"I don't have a negotiating face."

"Everyone has a negotiating face. Yours just looks like praying." He chewed, watching her. "What is it?"

"Festival night," Coral said. "The Vigil of the Wall. I want to go."

Doss stopped chewing.

Lay folk and debtors attended the Vigil, technically. They stood in the outer yards with the valley crowds and watched the priests process along the inner parapet, far away, tiny with distance, and heard maybe half the liturgy, and saw nothing of the wall itself except its silhouette against the bonfires. But there was a tradition — old, half-forgotten, honored mostly in its breach — that a scribe who finished a full season's quota ahead of schedule could petition for attendance *within*, on the parapet walk itself, among the brothers. Coral had found the reference three months ago, buried in a collection-rule codex nobody opened, and had been quietly building toward it ever since.

"You want to stand on the wall," Doss said slowly, "that holds in everything, on the night everyone prays it keeps holding."

"I want to see it up close. Once."

"Why?" And there it was — not disapproval, exactly. Doss rarely disapproved. It was more like the flinch of a man watching someone reach toward a stove he knew was hot. "Coral. What's at the wall for you?"

She looked down at her bowl. Barley and roots, like every noon. "I touched it once. When I was small, before — before the debt. My father carried me up on his shoulders, right up to the stones at the Millbrook gate. Everyone did it, then. It was a festival thing, touching the wall. For luck." She turned her cup in her hands. "I don't remember what it felt like. That's all. I want to remember."

"That's not all."

"It's enough."

"Coral." Doss set down his bread. When he spoke again his voice had gone low and careful, the voice he used for serious things, which was rare enough that she actually listened. "My grandmother used to say: in this valley, wanting is a debt like any other. You take out a loan of hope, and the Temple collects on it with interest. Best to want nothing. Best to keep your accounts empty. Then they can't take anything from you, because you never had anything to begin with."

"She sounds like a cheerful woman."

"She lived to be ninety-one." He nudged her shoulder. "I'm telling you what she told me. Don't want things. Not here. Wanting is how they find the soft places in you."

"And if I never want anything, what am I? An entry in a ledger?"

"A living entry. Which is better than a corrected one." He picked the bread back up, but his eyes stayed on her. "You're going to do it anyway. I can see it. You're going to finish the season early and walk into Halven's office with your petition like it's a perfectly reasonable request, and he's going to look at you with that face, and you're going to think you've won."

"And if I have?"

"Then you'll have wanted something," Doss said, "and they'll know exactly what you're willing to work for. Think about that. Think about what a person's price tells you, once you know it."

Coral thought about it. She thought about it all through the afternoon, at her desk, transcribing the water-damaged Vessery folio while the thought sat in the corner of her mind like an unpaid creditor. *A person's price.* Halven already knew hers — or thought he did. Eleven years. Freedom, priced daily in candlelight and barley. What else could a woman with nothing want?

But that was the thing, she realized somewhere around the fourth hour, the thing Doss had missed and perhaps his grandmother had missed too. Knowing a person's price cut both ways. If the Temple knew what she wanted, then she knew what they valued her willingness at. And value, in any ledger, was negotiable. Her father had taught her that much, before the debt took him — haggling in the Millbrook market, letting some poor farmer sweat through three rounds of offer and refusal before naming a fair price. *Never show them what you'll pay, Cora. But always know what they will.*

So she would show Halven nothing. She would simply be fast — faster than anyone in the valley had ever been — and let the speed speak, and make the petition sound like protocol instead of desire. A formality. An entitlement claimed, not a wish confessed.

She dipped her pen and wrote until her hand blurred.

* * *

It took her nineteen days.

Nineteen days in which she did not sleep properly, ate standing at her desk, and transcribed more pages than any scribe in the scriptorium's recent memory — she knew this because she checked the output boards each morning, and because Brother Halven began, around day eleven, to appear at odd hours and watch her work without speaking, the way a man watches weather he distrusts. By day fourteen, the junior scribes had started a betting pool. By day seventeen, Cantor Maren — ancient, half-deaf, keeper of the quotas — had personally come down from the upper gallery to squint at Coral's completed stacks and mutter something that might have been a blessing or a warning.

On the nineteenth evening, Coral sealed the last folio of the season's quota — the full season, autumn collections complete, verified, shelved — thirty-one days ahead of schedule. Then she washed, put on her clean collar, and walked to Brother Halven's office carrying the codex with the old rule marked by a thread.

Halven read the petition twice. He read the codex passage once. He looked at the thirty-one-day margin, and then, for a very long time, at Coral.

"Festival night," he said. "Within. On the parapet."

"It's within the rules, Brother."

"It is within the rules," he agreed softly. "Rules are such faithful servants. They hold the door open precisely as wide as they were built to, no wider — and yet people are always surprised by what walks through." He set the codex down. "Very well, Scribe Blackwood. You will attend the Vigil of the Wall. You will stand where you are placed, speak to no brother unless spoken to, and touch nothing."

"Of course."

"And Coral—" He paused at the door, and for one moment the mild disappointment fell away entirely, and what looked out of his face was older and colder and much more tired. "Whatever you imagine you'll feel, up there, at the stones — whatever the valley grandmothers say about luck and listening — the wall is masonry. Old masonry. It does not answer, because there is nothing behind it to answer. Remember that, and the night will cost you nothing."

He left. Coral stood alone in his office, surrounded by the smell of lamp oil and vellum, and heard Doss's grandmother in her head: *don't want things.*

Too late, she thought. She'd already paid for this one. Thirty-one days, priced exactly, receipt attached.

Now she would collect.

Outside the window, far to the north, the first bonfires of the festival were being stacked along the wall-road, small and bright as struck matches against the coming dusk. Three nights hence, they would burn. Three nights hence, she would stand on the parapet with her palm against the stone her father had once lifted her toward, and find out whether the wall remembered her the way she remembered it.

She did not know — could not have known — that the stars above that wall were already beginning to dim.
