## 3. The Wall That Holds Nothing

The permission came to her in writing, which was how all things came to Coral Blackwood now — folded twice, sealed with grey wax, and priced.

*The scribe Blackwood is granted leave to attend the Festival of Held Borders, first bell to last. She will conduct herself with the modesty of her station. Signed under seal, Office of the High Scribe.*

Doss read it over her shoulder in the refectory line and made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling. "You transcribed four hundred folios in nine days for one evening outside."

"Four hundred and eleven."

"Of course you counted." He tore his bread in half and gave her the bigger piece without being asked about it, which was Doss's whole character in a gesture. "I still say wanting things is how they get you."

"You want things."

"I want bread," Doss said. "They have bread. Wanting things they *don't* want you to have is different. That's not wanting, that's — " he searched for the word, chewing — "ambition. And ambition gets people sent to the copying cells where the light is bad and the ink is worse."

Coral put the writ away inside her sleeve, where she kept everything that mattered: a stub of graphite, a scrap of good vellum she'd won in a wager of penmanship, and the memory — carefully unexamined, like a page turned face-down — of that single heartbeat when the stone of the archive wall had answered her touch. *Later*, she told herself, as she always did. *When there's time.*

There was never time. There was only ledger.

But tonight there was the festival, and there was the wall.

* * *

The valley of Vell did its celebrating the way it did everything else: quietly, thoroughly, and with an eye toward the ledger. The Festival of Held Borders was three hundred years old and had never once been cancelled, because cancelling it would mean admitting the borders needed holding, and admitting that would mean asking held from what, and no one in Vell — priest or farmer or scribe — wanted to be the one who asked.

So they lit lamps instead. Hundreds of them, along the low stone wall that ran the valley's rim like a seam of grey stitching, until the whole border looked from a distance like a constellation someone had laid out by hand. Children were lifted up to set their candles on the capstones. Farmers who had spent all year complaining about the Temple's tithes drank the Temple's festival wine and sang the Temple's festival hymns, which thanked the Quiet Hours for the wards that kept the wild country beyond asleep.

Coral walked the wall alone, as far from the lantern-crowds as her leave permitted, which was not very far, but she had learned to make small distances enormous through sheer stubbornness of attention.

Up close, the wards were less impressive than the songs claimed. They were stones. Old ones — older than anything else in the valley except the mountain's bones — furred with lichen on the north faces, worn smooth on the south where generations of festival-goers had rested their palms. Each capstone bore a single carved mark, the same mark repeated down the entire length of the wall: a circle crossed by a horizontal line, like an eye that had been closed and then sealed shut.

The priests called it the Sign of Quiet Hours. Coral had copied it ten thousand times in ledgers and hymnals and deed-books, and she had never once thought about what it was closing its eye *on*.

She stopped at a capstone apart from the others, where the crowd noise fell behind a shoulder of hillside and the wind off the dead country could reach her. It smelled strange, that wind. Not foul. Just — unaccounted for. Every smell in the valley was a smell she could name and price: hay, tallow, river-mud, incense. This one sat outside the ledger entirely. Green, somehow, though nothing green grew past the wall. Like the air before rain in a country where it hadn't rained in three hundred years.

Her hand rose before she decided to raise it.

She noticed herself doing it the way one notices sleepwalking — with a distant, disapproving interest. Her fingers hovered over the capstone, over the sealed eye of the Sign, close enough to feel the day's heat still stored in the stone.

*Superstition,* Brother Anselm would say. He said it every year when some farm child claimed the wall hummed, or warmed, or dreamed. *Stone is stone. The wards are maintained by the Temple's office, not by wishes.* And the children would laugh, and the priests would smile their thin smiles, and everyone would go home reassured that the world was exactly as large as Vell.

Coral touched the stone.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. Stone is stone. She felt the grit of lichen, the cool beneath the warmth, the honest dumb weight of the world —

— and then, for exactly one heartbeat, the stone answered.

She had no better word for it, and she was a professional with words; she owned hundreds and used most of them daily. But *answered* was what it did. Something beneath the lichen and beneath the rock and beneath, it seemed, the bedrock of the valley itself turned its attention toward her fingertips the way a sleeper turns toward a candle carried into the room. A pressure, vast and slow and impossibly gentle, like the ocean noticing a single drop of rain. It did not speak. It had no voice. It simply *knew she was there*, in the way that she suddenly understood she had been known — measured, entered into some record older than any ledger she would ever copy — for precisely as long as her skin touched the stone.

And then her heart completed its beat, and it was gone.

Coral snatched her hand back and stood very still, listening to her own pulse hammer in her ears, watching the capstone with the expression she reserved for ledgers that refused to balance.

The stone sat in the lamplight. Lichen. Grit. Warmth fading into evening. Nothing.

But her palm tingled, and the tingling was not going away, and deep in the marrow of the feeling — she could not have explained this to anyone, would not have tried — was a sensation she recognized from the only other time it had happened. The archive wall. The same attention. The same terrible, wonderful sense of being *counted*.

"Hello," she said to the stone, and immediately felt foolish, and immediately stopped caring that she felt foolish, because the alternative was admitting she had felt nothing, and she knew — with the absolute certainty of a scribe who has checked a figure three times — that she had felt *something*.

The stone said nothing back. Whatever it was, whatever had looked at her through three feet of granite and three centuries of silence, it was either gone or sleeping or satisfied.

"Right," Coral said. Her voice sounded thin out here where the wind came off the dead country. "Right."

* * *

She found the priests at the second lantern-station, where Father Ellery — round, red, beloved of farmers and despised of scribes for reasons neither party examined too closely — was dispensing blessing and wine in alternating measures.

"Scribe Blackwood!" he boomed, delighted, because a scribe at the festival was exotic as a foreign bird. "Come to see the wards hold?"

"They hold," Coral said. "Father, a question. The capstones — the Sign of Quiet Hours. What does it seal?"

Ellery blinked at her the way a man blinks at a sum that has come out wrong. "Seal? It seals nothing, child. It *marks*. It says: here ends the ordered world, here begins the wild. It's a fence-post sign, not a lock."

"Then why do the hymns call them wards? Wards keep things out. Or in."

"Wards, wards," Ellery said comfortably, waving his cup. "Old word. Means boundary, means blessing, means whatever the hymnist needed it to mean. You spend too long among ledgers, girl. Everything looks like a lock when your whole life is keys." He pressed a cup into her hands before she could decline it and turned to bless a farmer's squalling infant.

Brother Anselm, standing a little apart in his neat grey robes, had watched the whole exchange with his head tilted, like a crow considering whether something shiny was also edible.

"You touched a capstone tonight," he said.

It was not a question, and Coral's blood went cold and pleasant all at once, the way it did whenever she was caught at something — which was often, because she was always doing something slightly outside her leave, and rarely caught, and had begun to build her whole interior life in the gap between those two facts.

"The whole valley touches the capstones," she said. "It's tradition."

"The whole valley rests its hand," Anselm agreed. "Tradition. I watched you from the ridge. You didn't rest. You listened." His eyes were pale and patient and entirely without warmth. "What did you hear?"

"Nothing. Stone is stone. Isn't that the doctrine?"

"It is the doctrine." He smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Doctrines exist because the truth is untidy. A scribe should understand that better than anyone. We write the tidy version down so no one has to carry the untidy one around." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice, and for a moment — one heartbeat, again, the universe apparently dealing in heartbeats tonight — she saw something in his face that was not contempt or boredom but a kind of weary, professional vigilance. "If ever you *do* hear something, Scribe Blackwood — from stone, from wall, from anywhere the Temple hasn't put words first — you'll bring it to me. Not to Father Ellery. To me. Before you bring it to anyone else. Before you even finish deciding what you heard. Do you understand?"

"No," said Coral honestly.

"Good," said Anselm, and patted her shoulder once, gently, the way one pats a locked box whose contents one is not yet authorized to inventory, and walked away into the lamplight.

* * *

She did not go back to the wall that night. She wanted to — her feet argued for it the entire walk back down, and lost to the part of her that had heard the word *before* in Anselm's sentence and understood she had just been enrolled in something without signing anything.

Instead she went home to her cell in the scriptorium annex, lit her candle, and pulled out the one object she owned that predated her indenture: her mother's. A small notebook, cheap paper gone soft as cloth with age, filled in a slanting, hurried hand that Coral had been copying since she was six years old, learning letters from it before the Temple taught her theirs.

Her mother had been a scribe too. That was the story. A temple scribe who married below her station, left the order, died of fever when Coral was seven. The Temple had collected the mother's effects, as it collected everything, and Coral had received back exactly one item, wrapped in oilcloth, with a receipt she still had. One notebook out of an entire life, issued like rations. She had never wondered why she got even that. She wondered now.

She turned to the last written page, the one she had read a thousand times and understood perhaps twice:

*They will tell you the wall holds nothing. This is true and it is the worst thing they will ever tell you. Ask instead what the nothing would be if the wall were gone.*

Below it, in fresher ink, in her mother's hand but slower, deliberate, as if written for finding:

*Coral — if you are reading this and you have felt it answer, go to the third capstone east of the Shepherd's Gate on any night the stars are clear, and count them. Count them every festival. If the number is ever wrong, run.*

Which was nonsense, of course. Stars were stars. You couldn't count them wrong; the sky wasn't a ledger.

Except that her mother had been a scribe too, and scribes did not write nonsense. Scribes wrote *tidy versions* — Anselm's own words, arriving inconveniently — of untidy truths, and hid instructions in the margins where only another scribe would think to look.

Coral sat for a long time with the candle burning down between her and the open notebook, listening to the distant festival bells, feeling the ghost-tingle still alive in her palm like a coin held too long in a fist.

Then she climbed the annex stair to the flat roof where the laundry was hung, because the scriptorium faced east and the Shepherd's Gate was east and above the Shepherd's Gate, low over the black shoulder of the wall, hung the sky.

She counted the stars.

She was very good at counting. Four hundred and eleven folios in nine days; she did not miscount.

Above the wall that held nothing, above the sealed eyes of the Sign, the stars of the eastern sky numbered one hundred and forty.

She wrote the number in graphite on the inside of her wrist, where a sleeve would hide it, and went down to her cell, and lay awake listening to the wall of the world breathe.

One hundred and forty. She would count again next festival, and the festival after that, for all eleven remaining years of her debt if she had to.

She never got a second count.
