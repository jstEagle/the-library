## 4. The Night Without Stars

Coral had copied four hundred and twelve lines of debt entries when the candle guttered for the third time that night.

She frowned at it. The scriptorium's air was still — no draft from the shutters, no breath of wind off the mountain — and yet the flame lay down flat as if pressed by a thumb, then stood again, trembling. She watched the light shiver across the ledger page, over names and sums and years owed, eleven of which were hers. The wax pooled unevenly. Somewhere below her, in the valley of Vell, dogs had begun to bark.

Not one dog. All of them.

She set down her pen. The nib left a small black star on the margin, a blot she would normally have scraped away with a knife, but her hands had gone strange and slow, and she found she didn't care about blots.

The barking rose to a kind of chorus, then cut off all at once, like a door closing on it.

"Coral." Brother Aldous stood at the end of the long table, his copy-lamp held high. He was an old man, dry as the vellum he loved, and Coral had never once seen him afraid. She saw it now. It sat on him badly, like borrowed clothing. "Come to the window."

They went together down the row of sleeping desks, past the other scribes stirring and muttering, to the tall east window that looked out over the temple roofs and the terraced fields and, beyond everything, the black shoulder of the ward-wall against the sky.

The sky was wrong.

Coral had spent twenty-three years under the same stars everyone in Vell saw: the Ploughman, the Sisters, the great slow wheel of them turning over the wall every night of her life. She knew their arrangement the way she knew the alphabet. So when she looked up and found the sky smooth and whole and utterly, absolutely dark — not clouded, not veiled, but *empty*, a ceiling of unbroken black from horizon to horizon — her mind simply refused it, the way a reader refuses a page where the words have been printed backward.

"They've gone," she said. Her voice sounded very young.

"Every one." Aldous's lamp-hand was steady. His breathing wasn't. "I counted. I always count. Four hundred and six visible on a clear night from this window. There are none. Not behind cloud — there is no cloud. They are *gone*."

The valley was waking. Lamps bloomed in the farmhouse windows down the slopes; bells began, first the chapel in Millrow, then, confused and overlapping, every bell in Vell at once, none of them ringing any hour that existed. People were coming out into their yards in their nightclothes, holding candles up toward the blank sky as if the stars could be summoned back by offering.

And beneath it all — beneath the bells and the dogs and the rising human noise — Coral heard something else. Something so low she felt it in her teeth rather than heard it with her ears. A hum out of the ground, out of the walls, out of the ancient stones of the temple itself. The wards. Everyone in Vell grew up with the wards the way they grew up with weather: the faint pressure at the border-stones you felt if you touched them, the way birds flew wide of the wall, the sense — never spoken aloud, because it was superstition — that the valley was a held place. A kept place.

The hum was changing key. Rising. Straining.

"It sounds like—" she began.

"Hold your tongue," Aldous said sharply, and then, gentler, because he was not an unkind man, only a frightened one: "Whatever it sounds like, child, do not say it here."

But she had already thought it, and thinking it made it true in some private chamber of herself where no abbot could reach:

It sounded like a breath being drawn. Long and deep and shaking, after three hundred years of holding.

The candle on the nearest desk went out. Then the next. Then all of them, down the whole length of the scriptorium, in order, one after another, as if something were walking the room snuffing flames with its fingers — except nothing walked there, and the air did not stir, and the darkness came on like water filling a hull.

In the last instant before the final candle died, Coral saw the ink on her ledger page begin, very slightly, to glow.

Then the wards fell.

She would spend years trying to describe the sound, and never satisfy herself. It was not thunder. Thunder arrives from outside. This came from inside everything — from the stone underfoot and the bones of the building and, she was certain, from inside her own chest, a single vast exhalation that passed through the world the way a wave passes through a rope. *Hhhhhhh.* A held breath released. Every bell in the valley stopped mid-swing. Every dog, every bird, every human voice dropped silent for the space of one heartbeat, as if the whole of Vell had been struck dumb together.

In that heartbeat of silence, Coral felt the thing she would carry for the rest of her life: not fear. *Relief.* The valley itself, the land under her feet, sighed — and for one impossible moment she felt what it felt, the way you feel a cramp let go of a muscle. Three centuries of clenching, ended. The wards hadn't broken.

They had *stopped.*

Then the screaming started, and the moment was gone, and awe curdled in her stomach into something colder.

* * *

By dawn — a grey, sourceless dawn with no sun-position anyone could swear to — the Temple of Quiet Hours had done what the Temple always did with catastrophe: it had organized it.

Coral stood in the great refectory among forty other scribes, ink-stained and sleepless, while the High Scribe Corvel read the Proclamation of Holy Emergency from the pulpit steps. He was a tall man built like a door, with a voice engineered for exactly this purpose, and he wore the full grey-and-gold vestments that Coral had previously seen only twice, both times at funerals.

"The stars have been withdrawn," Corvel declared. "Withdrawn, not lost. This is a testing. The Quiet Hours — those hours in which the gods count our debts and weigh our faithfulness — have been extended over us without limit. We do not know when the reckoning ends. We know only that we are being audited."

Murmurs ran the benches. Coral kept her eyes on her hands.

"The border wards have failed," Corvel continued, and here his voice dropped into a register of grief so precisely calibrated that Coral, who had spent eleven years learning how tone could be manufactured, felt a chill of professional admiration. "The protections our founders raised — the sacred enclosure of Vell — stand open. What walks in the dead country now has no fence between it and our children. Therefore: curfew at dusk. Assembly rolls in every parish. And the Temple's own expedition—"

He paused. Coral looked up despite herself.

"—will cross the fallen wall within the month, to learn what has become of the world, and to restore, if the gods permit, what has been taken from us."

Applause broke out, ragged and frightened and grateful. That was the genius of the Temple, Coral thought, watching it spread: it never told people what to feel. It told them what their feelings *meant*, and gave each feeling a duty attached. Terror became vigilance. Grief became tithing. Wonder—

Wonder became paperwork.

"Blackwood." Brother Aldous touched her elbow. "The Provost wants the emergency registers begun. Census of souls, stores, livestock. You'll take the eastern parishes."

"Yes, Brother." It should have been an honor; emergency registers were the fastest route to advancement. But as she gathered her pens, Coral caught sight of Corvel descending from the pulpit steps, and saw him lean close to the Provost, and saw the Provost's face go white, and heard — she was not meant to hear, she was nobody, a debtor with ink on her fingers — four words pass between them:

"*It answered someone's touch.*"

She walked out into the starless morning with her heart hammering and her face arranged into perfect dutiful calm, and she thought: *the wardstone. Festival night. My hand on the stone. One heartbeat, something answering.*

They couldn't know. No one had seen; the priests had laughed at her. Superstition, they'd called it.

But the wards had fallen the same night, and the High Scribe was already hunting whatever had woken them, and Coral Blackwood had spent her whole life learning the first law of ledgers:

Everything gets written down eventually. The only question is whose hand holds the pen.

* * *

Veyra Osten had been captain of the Temple garrison for nine years, and in nine years she had learned that the surest sign of trouble was not panic but efficiency.

The valley was panicking properly — looting in Millrow, a run on the granaries, two drowned already in the reservoir because someone swore the water had gone holy or poisoned, accounts differed. That was normal. Fear was weather; you dressed for it.

What interested her was the Temple's response, because it was *too fast*. By noon of the first starless day, supply manifests existed. By dusk, requisitions. Corvel had named the expedition's officers before most parishes had finished counting their congregations — as though the list had been written before the stars went out.

Veyra stood on the barracks roof with the expedition's preliminary orders in her hand and read them three times.

*Objective: assess condition of the border wards. Map the territory beyond. Determine cause of stellar cessation. Secondary objectives to be communicated under seal.*

Under seal. To her — the ranking officer, the woman expected to keep forty soldiers alive in unmagic country — and the actual objectives were sealed even from her.

"Captain?" Sergeant Ives, at the ladder, watching her face.

"Illegal orders look like these," Veyra said. "So do desperate ones. Sometimes they're the same document." She folded the paper along its existing creases — someone had creased it before her; the fold was worn, as if the orders had been folded and unfolded many times, long before tonight. "Double the watch. And Ives — if any priest asks what my men are carrying, the answer is standard kit."

"And if they ask what we're actually carrying?"

Veyra looked south, toward the wall, a black line against a blacker sky, where no stars wheeled and something vast seemed to be waiting with the patience of stone.

"Then we're carrying questions," she said, "that somebody wrote down a long time ago."

* * *

That night, forbidden to be abroad, Coral climbed anyway.

She told herself it was for the census — the eastern parishes' night-watch rosters needed verifying, and verification required walking the terrace road above the dormitories, and the terrace road happened to pass the scriptorium's roof-access stair. Lies, she reflected, were just entries in the wrong column; they balanced only until audit.

From the roof tiles, the valley of Vell lay below her in scattered lamplight, and above her lay nothing at all.

No clouds. No moon — the moon too was gone, which no one in the refectory had dared say aloud, though every prayer composed since had carefully avoided mentioning heaven. Just black, seamless, total. She had never seen true darkness before. Even in the deepest cellar of the temple there was always *some* light, some memory of light in the stone. This was different. This was a page before any ink.

She hugged her knees and felt the terror of it properly now, alone, with no duties to arrange the fear into. The sky was a closed book. Whatever had been written on it had been erased — not smudged, not obscured, *erased*, cleanly, the way a senior scribe burns a page rather than let a junior read it.

Her breath caught.

*Burns pages rather than let me read them.*

The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, glowing like her ink had glowed: Aldous burning that ledger page in her second year, telling her it was mis-copied, his hands shaking worse than hers ever had. Names on that page — she'd glimpsed shapes, columns of them, a register of some kind. She had told herself for eleven years it was nothing. Debt lists. Dead debtors.

On a night when every light in heaven had been put out, Coral found she no longer believed in coincidences. Coincidence was a luxury of ordered skies.

Far to the north, along the ridge of the ward-wall, a light moved.

Not a torch — torches flickered, and this burned steady, a cold pale thread traveling the length of the wall where no patrol had any reason to walk, following the line of the old wardstones one by one, as if something were checking them. Counting them.

Auditing them.

Coral pressed herself flat against the warm tiles and watched until the light reached the far end of the wall, paused — she would swear forever that it paused, that it *looked*, though looking implied eyes and she refused to imagine what kind — and then winked out.

The valley slept badly beneath its lidless sky. Bells rang at wrong hours. Children cried and were told the stars were merely resting.

But Coral Blackwood, lying on the roof of the house of lies she had served for half her life, understood one thing with total clarity, the way she understood a balanced ledger:

The wards hadn't failed.

They had been *answered* — by her hand on the stone, or by whatever walked the wall tonight, or by something older than either — and somewhere in the Temple's sealed archives, in a register of names she had been forbidden to read, her own name was waiting.

Eleven years of debt, give or take.

Give, she thought, staring into the dark. Or take.

She didn't yet know how very much it was about to take.
