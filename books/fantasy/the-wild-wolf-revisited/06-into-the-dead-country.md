## 6. Into the Dead Country

The gate in the wall was smaller than Coral had imagined. After three days of marching along its base — three days of looking up at stones the size of cottages, mortared with something that was not mortar, carved with script so old the Temple's own primers had no letters to match it — she had expected the passage through to be a wound, a breach, something violent. Instead it was a door. A plain, arched door, set into the wall's flank like an afterthought, its frame inlaid with a line of dull metal that no longer gleamed.

"It was never meant to be opened from outside," Captain Veyra Osten said, standing beside her at the head of the column. "That's what the old survey notes say. The gate opens from Vell because Vell is the warden's side. Whatever's on the other side was never supposed to leave."

"You believe the survey notes?"

Veyra's jaw worked. She was a compact woman, weathered to the color of old rope, with the kind of face that gave away nothing except when it gave away everything. "I believe the stars went out," she said. "Everything else is negotiable."

She gave the order, and the door was unbarred — the bar itself a beam of black iron, cold enough in the morning air to burn bare skin — and the expedition of the Temple of Quiet Hours, forty-one souls strong, walked out of the world.

* * *

Coral felt the crossing in her teeth before she saw anything.

It was like the moment a scribe lifts a freshly inked page and the wet characters catch the light — a faint pressure, a shimmer at the edge of perception. Then she was through, and the pressure broke over her like a wave, and she staggered, and someone behind her caught her elbow, and the dead country opened up before them all.

Grass. That was the first absurdity. The land beyond the wall was not dead at all — it was a rolling plain of grass that grew silver, each blade pale as moonlit wire, rippling in a wind that touched everything and made no sound. The color of it was wrong in a way Coral's vocabulary could not fix; it was not grey, not white, but silver the way silver is in a ledger — a color that meant *value*, that meant *something is held here*. It rolled away to a horizon hazed with violet, and above it, where the sky should have held the sun, the sun hung small and cold and perfectly round, like a coin nailed to blue slate.

No stars. Even in daylight, Coral found herself searching for them, the absence aching like a missing tooth.

"Ledger-keeper." Veyra's voice, low. "You're the only one not staring at the grass."

Coral realized this was true. The soldiers were staring. The muleteers were staring. Old Brother Halloway, the expedition's priest, had his lips moving in a prayer that had probably never been written down because no one had ever needed it before. Only Coral was looking — not at the grass, but at the *ground beneath* it, and at the air above it, and at the slow, luminous drift of something that pooled in the hollows of the land like morning fog, except it wasn't fog, and it wasn't quite light either.

"Captain," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, "do you see the pools?"

"Pools."

"In the low ground. Between those two hills. There's — standing water, or something like it. Do your scouts see it?"

Veyra squinted. Raised a hand; a scout trotted up, a lean woman named Ferren with a crossbow slung across her back. "Water in the dips, Captain?" Veyra asked. "Report."

Ferren looked where Coral pointed and frowned. "Dry as an old bone, Captain. All of it. There's been no rain here in — " she glanced at the sky, at the coin-sun — "well. In a long time, I'd guess."

Coral's mouth went dry. She looked back at the hollows, and the silver light pooled there, lapping gently at the slopes like a tide that had forgotten which way to go, and she understood, with a scribe's cold clarity, that she was seeing something the others could not. That the training that had made her valuable enough to conscript — eleven years of copying ledgers, of learning to read not just words but *the shape of what is written beneath words*, the discipline of seeing ink as intention — had done something to her eyes. Or had opened something that was always there.

The magic of the dead country was not invisible. It was legible. And she was the only one in forty-one who could read.

* * *

They made camp that first night in the lee of a fallen wardstone — a fragment the size of a haywain, tumbled from the wall miles behind them, its carved script still faintly warm. Coral knelt beside it while the soldiers pitched tents, and she did what she had been doing all day, which was trying not to be sick with wonder.

Because it was everywhere. That was the thing no one had told her, the thing the Temple's briefing — three hours of Corvel's dry voice describing terrain and supply lines — had somehow never mentioned. Magic, in Vell, was a rumor. A story about the wall, about the old days, told to children. Here it was *weather*. It drifted down the valleys in slow braided rivers. It pooled in hollows. It hung in the branches of a stand of trees to the east like fruit too heavy for the boughs. And every thread of it, every current and eddy, had *texture* — the fine-grained legible texture of handwriting. Some of it flowed in strokes as clean and confident as a master scribe's hand. Some of it was scrawled, hurried, the script of someone writing in the dark. And some of it — this was the part that kept her kneeling by the warm stone long after her knees ached — some of it was *erased*. Palimpsests. Great tracts of the plain where the underlying writing had been scraped away and something else written over it, and beneath the over-writing, ghosted through like old ink under new, she could just make out the pressure of what had been removed.

"Ledger-keeper."

She looked up. Veyra stood over her, and behind the captain the camp had gone quiet in that particular way camps go quiet — the way that means everyone is pretending not to listen.

"You've been staring at empty air for an hour," Veyra said. "Halloway says you're praying. I told him you're a debtor, not a mystic, and debtors pray differently. So. What are you seeing?"

Coral weighed the question. Veyra Osten had not spoken to her once on the march out of Vell except to confirm the supply tally. But the captain had also, Coral had noticed, positioned Coral's tent at the center of the camp rather than the rear, and had assigned her two guards rather than the one the manifest allowed, and had asked her — casually, on the second morning — whether she could read the script on the wardstones. *No*, Coral had said, which was true in the way that a person who can read letters cannot yet read a language. It had been the safest true thing.

"The land is written," Coral said. "That's the nearest I can come to it. Everything here — the grass, the light, the air — it's all carrying script. Old script. I can't read the words yet. But I can see the handwriting. And Captain —" she hesitated, then decided that Veyra Osten, who had asked, deserved the whole of it — "some of it has been erased. Deliberately. The way you'd scrape a page to reuse it. There's writing under the writing, and someone wanted it gone."

Veyra was silent for a long moment. The coin-sun had set — it simply slid below the horizon like a coin into a purse, without color, without ceremony — and the dark that came on was not fully dark. The silver grass held the light a long time, and the pooled magic in the hollows glowed faintly, and overhead, where stars should have been, there was nothing at all, a black so complete it looked painted.

"When I was a girl," Veyra said at last, "my mother worked the dye-vats at Merrow's Ford. She could look at a bolt of cloth and tell you which vat, which mordant, which day, and whether the dyer had cut the madder with anything cheaper. Everyone else saw blue cloth. She saw a history." She looked down at Coral. "The Temple sent a ledger-keeper because the Temple believes this country is an account that's been closed. I'm starting to think they sent you because you're the only one who can read the entries."

"Is that what you believe, Captain?"

"I believe," Veyra said, "that my orders are sealed, and that sealed orders from the Temple are usually orders the Temple doesn't want read. Get some sleep, ledger-keeper. Tomorrow we cross the silver plain, and I want your eyes on the ridgelines."

* * *

The second day, the vertigo came.

It began as a small wrongness at the edges of things — a sense, walking through that silver grass, that the ground was tilted when it was flat, that the horizon had shifted when she hadn't moved. By midmorning it was a full rolling sickness, and Coral had to stop, and Veyra called the halt, and Coral stood swaying in the soundless wind with her hand pressed to her mouth and the whole legible world swimming around her.

It was too much. That was the truth of it, the shameful, overwhelming truth: it was simply too much. Every hollow held its pool of light, and every pool was a paragraph, and every paragraph was in a hand she could almost read, and the almost was torture — like being handed a ledger in a language one word short of fluent, page after page after page, forever. The grass itself was written. She looked down and the blades near her boots carried script so fine it lived along their edges like frost, and she could see where the writing *ended* — a clean margin running across the plain in a vast, ruler-straight line, and beyond the margin, nothing, blank silver, unwritten grass.

"Someone ruled a line here," she whispered.

"What?" Ferren had dismounted and was watching her with the wary expression soldiers give to things they may shortly have to shoot.

"The grass. The — the writing on it stops. There." She pointed. "A straight line, miles long, as far as I can see both ways. On this side, everything's inscribed. On that side, it's blank. Like a page someone ruled off and never used."

The soldiers exchanged looks. Brother Halloway murmured something about the vanity of scribes, seeing letters in grass. But Veyra walked to the line — Coral walked with her, because no one else could see it and someone had to mark it — and stood on the unwritten side, and Coral watched the captain's face as the captain felt, for the first time, whatever it was that the blank country did to a person. Veyra's jaw tightened. Her hand went to her sword hilt, not drawing, just holding, the way a man holds a rail in a high wind.

"It's quiet over here," Veyra said. "Not silent. *Quiet.* Like a room after the argument's over." She stepped back across the line, and her shoulders came down half an inch. "We keep to the written side. Whatever was written here, I want to know who held the pen. That's your task now, ledger-keeper. Not the supply tallies. The land. Every day you'll report what you read. In writing. Signed."

"And if the Temple asks why the expedition's scribe is doing anything but scribing?"

Veyra looked at her then, and for a moment the rope-weathered face was open all the way down, and Coral saw that underneath the professionalism was a fear as large as the blank country, held the way a dam holds water.

"Then I'll tell them their scribe is documenting the expedition for the historical record," Veyra said, "which is true, and which they ordered me to facilitate, in writing, before we left. The Temple loves paper, ledger-keeper. It's the one place they never look for knives." She turned away. "Column forms up in ten minutes."

* * *

They found the first pool deep enough to touch on the third day, in the bowl of a valley where the silver grass grew taller than a horse.

It lay at the bottom of the bowl like a mirror laid on the earth — a wide, still sheet of light, utterly without current, and Coral, when she knelt at its edge, saw that it was not light at all. It was writing. Compressed, layered, centuries of it, all the loose script of the plain run downhill and gathered here the way silt gathers in a pond, and where it lay still it was legible — legible in a way nothing on the surface had been, because here the layers had settled into strata, oldest at the bottom, and the bottom layer was written in a hand so fine and so controlled it made Coral's own penmanship look like a child's scrawl.

She reached out. She did not entirely decide to; her hand went the way a hand goes toward a page in a locked archive when the lock is open and no one is watching.

The moment her fingers broke the surface, the pool *answered*.

Light ran up her arm — not burning, not cold, but *reading* her, the way a reader's eye runs down a page, and Coral gasped, and the pool flared, and every soldier in the column saw the valley light up like dawn. And in the flare, for one heartbeat, the strata opened for her, and she was looking down through three hundred years of settled script to the bottom layer, and the bottom layer was not words.

It was a name. Written over and over and over, in that impossibly fine hand, thousands of times, the way a prisoner scratches marks on a wall. And beneath the names, in the very deepest layer, pressed so hard the script had nearly torn through the world itself:

*WE ARE STILL HERE.*

Coral snatched her hand back. The light died. The pool went still. The soldiers stood in a wide, frightened ring, and Brother Halloway was making signs against evil, and Veyra Osten had her sword half-drawn and her eyes fixed on Coral with an expression that had passed through fear and come out the other side into something harder.

"What did it say?" the captain demanded. "You read something. What did it say?"

Coral knelt there with her hand cradled against her chest, her fingers still tingling, the name still ringing in her — though she could not, now, remember what the name had been, only that it had been a name, and that it had been repeated the way you repeat a thing so it won't be forgotten — and she understood that she had just done something that could not be un-done. That the pool knew her now. That she had put her hand into the country's memory and the country's memory had put its hand into hers.

"It said," Coral heard herself reply, "that someone is still here."

And far off, on the ridgeline where the blank country met the written, where the ruled line of the unwritten page ran against the inscribed grass — a line no one in the column was watching, because no one could see it — something that had been standing very still for a very long time turned, and began to walk down toward them.
