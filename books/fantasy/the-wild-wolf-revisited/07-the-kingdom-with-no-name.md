## 7. The Kingdom With No Name

Veyra Osten had commanded soldiers for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned that orders came in three flavors.

There were the honest ones: *Hold this bridge. Burn that granary.* You could argue with them, you could die from them, but at least they told you what they wanted.

There were the cowardly ones: *Act as judgment dictates,* which meant the sender wanted a victory without owning the cost of it.

And then there were the sealed ones — written in cipher, bound with wax stamped by hands she was not permitted to imagine, carried against her ribs in an oilcloth case since the night the stars went out. She had been ordered to open it only when she crossed the border wall, and only alone, and she had obeyed, because obedience to the first sealed order is what buys you the right to refuse the second.

She had read it three times now, each time in a different place: once on her knees in the wet grass just past the fallen wards, once in her tent by lamplight with the flap tied shut, and once at dawn, standing on a rise of grey stone while the expedition broke camp below her like ants dismantling something they'd built too well.

The orders said two things.

*Map the country beyond the wards. Establish its extent, its inhabitants if any, and its relationship to the valley of Vell.*

And then, in a hand different from the rest — smaller, drier, pressed so hard into the paper that the letters had nearly cut through it:

*Find what killed the stars.*

Not *what fell*. Not *what changed*. Killed. As though the stars were livestock, or men, or a fire left unattended in a house full of sleeping people. As though someone, somewhere above Vell's little roofed world, already knew there had been a death.

Veyra folded the orders along their old creases and put them back against her ribs, where they sat like a second heart beating slightly out of time with the first.

Below her, Sergeant Ilex was arguing with the scribe-girl about the pack oxen, which meant the column would move within the hour. Veyra watched them for a moment — the girl's ink-stained fingers moving as she counted, quick and precise, and Ilex's big hands spread wide in exasperation — and felt again the small, cold discomfort that had been sitting behind her sternum since they'd crossed the wall.

The High Scribe had given her forty soldiers, six scribes, two engineers, and a cartographer who wept at altitude. He had given her supply for sixty days and authority over everything inside the border except the scribes' records. He had given her Coral Blackwood, debtor, property of the crown, eyes like a woman reading something just over your shoulder.

He had not given her the reason for any of it.

*Find what killed the stars.*

You did not send a mapping expedition to find a killer. You sent soldiers to find a killer. You sent an army. Unless — and here was the thought Veyra kept circling back to the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth — unless whatever killed the stars was not something you could fight. Unless what you actually needed was someone to see it clearly enough to write down what had happened afterward.

Forty soldiers. Six scribes. A cartographer who wept.

A record-keeping expedition, dressed as a military one. And every soldier in it carrying a sword against a country that, so far, had offered them nothing but silver grass and silence and magic lying in the hollows like rainwater.

Veyra turned from the rise and went down to move her army of witnesses another six miles into the dead country.

* * *

By midmorning they had reached the first river, if it could be called a river. It ran backward.

Not uphill — Veyra had seen hill-country streams run uphill where the rock tilted wrong, and it meant nothing. This was worse. The water moved in slow, deliberate pulses, gathering itself in wide pools and then releasing all at once downstream, like breath. Like swallowing. The engineer, a narrow man named Corb who had spent thirty years making bridges stand, stood at the bank for a long time before saying anything at all.

"Captain," he finally said, "that water is deciding."

"I can see that."

"No, Captain. I mean—" he rubbed his face, leaving a streak of chalk dust across one cheekbone, "—I mean it's *deciding*. There's intent in the flow pattern. I've never—" He stopped, started again. "I've built aqueducts my whole life. Water does four things. It falls, it pools, it runs, it freezes. That's the whole vocabulary." He pointed at the pulsing current with a hand that was not quite steady. "That water is doing a fifth thing."

"Can we cross it?"

"Oh, yes. Yes, absolutely. It doesn't mind us." Corb said this with the particular horror of a man who has just realized the most frightening sentence in his professional life is *it doesn't mind us*. "It went around the survey stakes this morning. Around them. It waited until they were set and then it made room."

Veyra looked at the water for a long moment. It pulsed. Gathered. Released.

Three centuries ago, the founders of Vell had built the border wards, and every child in the valley learned the catechism along with their letters: *Beyond the wall, the wildness. Beyond the wildness, nothing. The wards keep nothing out; they keep us safe in.* A careful sentence. She'd recited it ten thousand times before she was old enough to hear how carefully it avoided saying what the wildness *was*, or why safety required a wall forty feet high and older than any kingdom on the map.

Nothing beyond the wall. She was standing in it now, and it had a vocabulary five words longer than water should have.

"Sergeant Ilex," she called. "Fording detail. Ropes at chest height, no one crosses alone, and nobody drinks from it. We carry our own water."

"Yes, Captain."

"And Ilex." She lowered her voice. "If anyone starts telling you the water spoke to them, I want to hear it before anyone else does. Not because I'll flog them. Because I want to know what it says."

Ilex looked at her the way good sergeants look at captains who say things like that — a long, flat, measuring look that asked whether the rot had reached the officer class yet — and then nodded once and went to shout at men.

They crossed by noon. The water parted around the ropes without being asked. On the far bank, Coral Blackwood stood a moment with her bare palm flat against a boulder, and Veyra saw her do it, saw the girl's mouth go strange and soft, and made a note in the private ledger she kept behind her eyes: *the scribe touches things. The scribe listens to them. Find out why the High Scribe chose a debtor who listens.*

* * *

They made nine miles that day through grass gone silver as coin, through stands of trees whose leaves had kept their shape but lost every other property wood was supposed to have — light passed through them, and when the wind moved them they chimed faintly, a sound like distant table-setting. The mapmaker, Halven, worked with his weeping half-controlled through sheer professionalism, sketching contours that matched nothing in his reference books. Twice he came to Veyra holding his notebook open like a man presenting evidence at trial.

"The elevation's wrong," he said the first time. "Or my instruments are wrong. Or—" he gestured helplessly at the horizon, where hills rose and fell in long smooth waves like a frozen sea, "—or distance out here doesn't mean what it means at home. I paced that ridge this morning. Two thousand paces. My theodolite swears it's four hundred yards away. Both readings are *confident*, Captain. That's what frightens me. Nothing is uncertain. Everything is certain and none of it agrees."

"What did you write down?"

Halven blinked. "Which?"

"When two confident measurements disagree. What does a mapmaker write?"

He looked at his notebook. Looked at the impossible hills. Something in his face steadied — not courage exactly, but the specific relief of being handed a rule in a ruleless place.

"I write down both," he said. "And I mark the disagreement."

"Good," said Veyra. "Do that everywhere. Mark everything that disagrees. If this country has a fault line, that's where it'll show."

He saluted, which mapmakers don't technically do, and went away happier than he'd been all day, and Veyra stood alone in the chiming trees and admitted to herself what the thought under all her other thoughts had been since dawn.

*Mark everything that disagrees.* Because the sealed orders disagreed with everything else. A mapping expedition with no maps to return to — no, that wasn't fair, there were maps, there were the great survey charts of the Temple archive, she'd seen them as a young officer, Vell drawn small and neat at the edge of a page that simply stopped. Blank vellum past the border. Not empty land. Empty *paper*. The cartographers hadn't failed to explore; they'd been given nothing to draw on. Three hundred years, and no king, no company, no ambitious duke had ever funded an expedition past the wards. Not once. She had assumed, her whole life, that this was fear.

Standing in the silver grass, watching her breath fog though the afternoon was warm, Veyra understood for the first time that it might have been policy. That somewhere in the Temple archive there might be a shelf of sealed orders exactly like hers, carried by officers exactly like her, across three centuries — or, more likely, not. More likely the sealed orders had stopped generations ago, and hers was the first in a very long time, issued now only because the wards had fallen and silence had become more dangerous than knowledge.

Which raised the question she could no longer avoid, the one that hardened a degree further every day:

If the Temple had known for three hundred years that there was a country past the wall — had *drawn the edge of the page* around it, had built a catechism around it, had raised forty-foot stones around it — then the Temple had known the stars could die. Had known there was something out here worth keeping separate from the world. Had known enough, three nights ago, to have sealed orders ready within hours of the wards falling.

Orders take time to write. Cipher takes longer. Wax takes a moment, but the seal itself — the High Scribe's personal seal, pressed into the oilcloth case that had been waiting in her quarters before she even knew she'd be crossing the wall—

Veyra stopped walking.

She stood very still among the chiming trees, and let the thought finish itself, because she was a professional and professionals do not flinch from completed arithmetic.

The orders had been written before the stars went out.

Not that morning. Not in panic. The dry second hand, the pressed-too-hard letters — that was a document composed slowly, revised carefully, held ready. The Temple had known the stars would go out the way a physician knows a fever will break. They'd been waiting for it. Perhaps for a very long time.

*Find what killed the stars.* Not because they didn't know. Because they needed to know what everyone else would find when they looked. Because the answer was already in some archive, and what the High Scribe wanted from her was not discovery but confirmation — a soldier's eyewitness account, a captain's sworn testimony, to stand beside the truth they already held, so that when the moment came, whatever the moment was, the Temple's version would be the one with boots and blood and a banner behind it.

Veyra breathed out slowly. Her breath hung in the still air between the trees a moment longer than breath should, then faded.

"All right," she said quietly, to no one, to the country, to the dead air. "Then we'll find it first. And we'll write down what *we* see."

* * *

That evening she inspected the perimeter herself, as she always did, walking the picket line in the blue hour while the camp fires caught. The land around them rolled away in silver waves toward mountains that glowed, very faintly, very far off, with a light that was not sunrise and not sunset and not any color she had a name for. Warmth without heat. Light without source. The men had started calling it the far glow, and had stopped remarking on it, which worried her more than screaming would have. Three days in, and her soldiers were already learning to normalize the impossible. That was what soldiers did. It was also, she thought, looking back toward the south and the invisible line where Vell began, exactly what the founders of the valley must have counted on. Give people a wall and a catechism and a hundred years, and they will stop seeing the wall. They will stop asking what it holds. They will raise children who think the ceiling of the world is the sky their parents described to them.

At the northeast picket, Private Sorrel was on watch, a farm boy with a crossbow he maintained like a religious object. He straightened as she approached, then hesitated — visibly, uncomfortably — the way men hesitate when duty and sense are pulling opposite directions.

"Captain. Probably nothing."

"'Probably nothing' is my favorite kind of report. Give it to me."

Sorrel pointed east, along the ridgeline they'd noted at midday, the one Halven had marked with a double contour and a small annotation: *disagrees.*

"There's been a bird," Sorrel said. "On that saddle. Same bird, I think. Been there maybe an hour. Big — hawk-sized, bigger. Grey." He paused. "It hasn't flown. All evening. It's just... standing."

"Birds perch, Private."

"Not like this. It stands like—" he searched for it, frowning, "—like a sentry stands. Facing us. And Captain, when the relief came up at last watch, it turned its head. Watched the changeover. Turned back." His voice dropped. "Birds don't count watches."

Veyra looked at the saddle. The light was failing; the ridge was a charcoal line against the far glow. And on it, yes — a shape. Small at this distance, motionless, angled toward the camp with the fixed attention of something that had chosen a post.

She watched it for a full minute. It did not move. Then, as she watched, it lifted, unhurried, and slid along the ridgeline — parallel to the camp, matching their position, keeping its distance exact — and settled again on the next high point, facing them once more.

Her skin tightened from nape to wrist.

"Private Sorrel."

"Captain."

"From this hour, double the watch. Nobody walks anywhere alone after dark, including officers — *including* me. And nobody fires at anything on those ridges without my direct order." She was already turning, already running the arithmetic of her dispositions, forty soldiers strung along six miles of march, rear guard thin as parchment. "Whatever that is, I want it observed, not provoked. Understood?"

"Understood, Captain." A pause. "Captain — do you think it's one of them? From the village?" The ash village. They had marched around it that morning, giving it the road's width, and no one had spoken for a mile afterward. Grey figures, mid-flight, frozen for three hundred years. Sorrel was twenty. He'd grown up on the catechism. *Beyond the wall, nothing.*

"I don't know what it is," Veyra said. "That's why I want it alive and I want it *watched*."

She walked the rest of the line faster than she meant to. Behind her, the shape on the saddle stood its post. Ahead, in the deepening dark, the mountains glowed their sourceless warm glow, and somewhere between here and there — she could feel it now the way you feel weather coming, pressure behind the ears, a taste of iron at the back of the throat — somewhere ahead was the thing that had killed the stars, or the thing that knew who had, or the thing that had been waiting three hundred years for someone from Vell to walk far enough to ask.

In her tent, by lamplight, she took out the sealed orders and read the second hand one more time. *Find what killed the stars.* Then she took a blank sheet, and in her own cipher — the one she'd invented at seventeen and never told another living soul — she wrote a single line and dated it:

*The Temple's questions are not the right questions. Begin keeping my own account.*

She sealed it, and hid it, and slept with her sword unsheathed beside her, and dreamed of birds that counted watches.

* * *

Two days later, at the place where the glass road began — a ribbon of fused, mirror-smooth stone running arrow-straight toward the glowing mountain, ancient beyond reckoning and warm underfoot like a living body — the rear guard did not answer the recall horn.

Veyra stood at the head of the column while the horn sounded a third time, a fourth, its note falling flat and unanswered in the vast silent air. Twelve soldiers. Fourteen minutes overdue on a route they had marched twice. She counted the seconds the way she counted everything, and at ninety seconds she stopped counting, because counting had become its own answer.

"Sergeant Ilex. Take six and bring them back."

Ilex was already moving. But as he went, Veyra looked up — up at the ridgelines, at every saddle and high point along their path, and found them, one by one, the way you find stars emerging from dusk. Shapes. Still, patient, spaced with terrible precision along the high ground on both sides of the glass road. Dozens of them. They had been there for days. They had let the column pass beneath them like a stream flowing under a bridge, and now that the stream's tail end lagged, they had simply — closed.

One of them, closest, on the near ridge, stepped forward into view. Tall. Grey-cloaked. Unarmed, as far as she could tell, and utterly still, and watching her — not the camp, not the soldiers, but *her*, with an attention she could feel like a hand laid flat between her shoulder blades.

Beside her, Coral Blackwood had gone very pale, and very quiet, and her ink-stained hand had risen unconsciously to touch the wardstone charm at her throat — and Veyra saw the girl's lips move, shaping a word in no language the valley taught, and saw, impossibly, the figure on the ridge incline its head.

As if it had heard her.

As if it had *answered*.
