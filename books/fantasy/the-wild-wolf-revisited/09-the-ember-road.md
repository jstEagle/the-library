## 9. The Ember Road

The fire left footprints.

Not the kind a person leaves — nothing so blunt as boot-heel or bare toe — but marks of heat in the world itself, and only Coral could read them. Where the others saw ordinary ground she saw the residue of passage: grass blades curled at the tips like paper held too near a candle, dew gone to steam before dawn, a stone with its grey skin blistered into a faint rose glaze. Something living had come through here, recently, moving with purpose. And something had fed it as it went.

"Again," Captain Veyra Osten said behind her.

Coral crouched over the scorched tuft and let her fingers hover an inch above it, the way she'd learned in her first week beyond the wall: never touch until you've read. Scribe-training translated strangely out here. In the scriptorium she had learned to see ink not as blackness but as intent laid down in a medium; here, magic behaved the same way, pooling and drying and smudging, and the land was a page that no one had thought to stop writing on.

"Warm," she reported. "Two hours old, maybe three. It came from the northwest and it slowed here." She traced the air above the grass, where a shimmer lingered that wasn't quite heat-haze. "It stopped. Like it was... listening."

"Listening for what?"

"For us." She stood, brushing grit from her knees. "Or checking whether we were still following."

Veyra's jaw worked. Up close, without the distance of rank, the captain looked like what she was: a woman who had crossed a border that hadn't existed a month ago and now commanded thirty-one soldiers in a country where the grass grew silver and the rivers ran uphill if they felt like it. Her orders were sealed. Her map was blank parchment. And every night since the third day, someone — something — had been pacing them along the ridgelines.

"Corporal Denna says the watchers were on the eastern spur again at first light. Six figures. Maybe seven."

"They're not hiding well," Coral said.

"No. That's what worries me." Veyra turned toward the column, where the soldiers were striking camp with the tight economy of people who no longer trusted silence. "Hiding badly is a message. Either they want us to know we're seen, or they want us to think we've found all of them."

"And which do you believe?"

"I'm paid to believe neither." But Veyra's eyes went, briefly, northward — to the mountain.

It had been visible for two days now, growing out of the horizon the way a ship grows out of the sea: first a smudge, then a shoulder, then a fact. It didn't look right. Mountains in the valley of Vell wore their age openly, craggy and snow-capped and indifferent. This one sat low and broad and smooth, its slopes dark as cooled slag, and at its crown — only visible at dusk, only when the light failed just so — there was a glow. Not firelight. Firelight flickered. This was steady, deep, patient, like a coal banked under ash, like a lamp behind a curtain, like a heartbeat seen through a closed chest.

*Ember Mountain,* the soldiers had taken to calling it, half in jest. No one joked about it anymore.

"It's pulling us," Coral said quietly. "Whatever burns up there. The traces get stronger every mile."

"Good. Stronger traces mean we find it faster, and finding it faster means I can send my report back through the wards and get all of you home before winter." Veyra paused. Then, in the voice she used maybe once a day, when the mask slipped: "Do you feel it too? The pull?"

Coral hesitated. Lying to the captain felt like miscounting a ledger — possible, but it corrupted everything downstream. "It's like standing outside a house where someone is singing. You can't make out the words. But your feet want to go in anyway."

Veyra nodded slowly. "Then we'd better learn the words before our feet decide for us. Break camp. We move in ten."

* * *

By midmorning the silver grass began to fail.

It happened gradually — first in patches, then in swathes, until the ground ahead showed bare and dark and glassy. The soldiers' boots rang on it. That was the wrong sound entirely. Earth should muffle; this rang, high and clear, like walking on the roof of a struck bell.

"Gods below," breathed Sergeant Hallow, at the point.

Coral pushed forward past him and understood, and understanding did not help.

The road was fused glass. Not laid, not poured — *fused*, as though some vast heat had passed over the land once, long ago, and melted the ground into a single seamless pane that had cooled into permanence. It ran arrow-straight toward the mountain, wide enough for six abreast, and beneath its surface things moved: veins of color, amber and gold and a red deeper than blood, threading through the glass like ink dropped in water and frozen mid-bloom.

She knelt and pressed her palm flat against it.

Warmth. Not the warmth of sun-heated stone but a living warmth, rhythmic, slow. She counted without meaning to — scribe's habit, counting everything — and got one beat every nine seconds. Steady as a pendulum. Steady as a pulse.

"Coral." Veyra crouched beside her, keeping her voice low. "What is it?"

"A road." Her own voice sounded strange to her, thin over the ringing glass. "But roads are made by people deciding where they want to go. This—" She spread her hand. Under her palm, the colored veins brightened very slightly, as if acknowledging her touch. She pulled it back quickly. "This was made by something deciding where *it* wanted everyone else to go."

"You're saying the road is alive."

"I'm saying it isn't dead."

Veyra stared at her for a long moment. Then she stood and raised her voice to the column: "Single file! Watch your footing — the surface is slick. Nobody wanders. Nobody touches anything without the scribe clearing it first." A beat. "That includes you, Hallow."

There was a ripple of tired laughter down the line, the kind soldiers make when laughter is the only tool they've been issued. Coral fell in beside Veyra as they started onto the glass road proper, and the whole world changed key. Their footsteps became music — or nearly music, a chiming arrhythmia, thirty-one people playing a broken instrument. Ahead, the road ran straight as a ruled line toward the dark mountain, and the veins of fire beneath the glass pulsed in time with that nine-second heart.

They walked. The morning aged. The mountain grew.

It was past noon when the road began to climb, gently, through country where the glass gave way to black rock studded with crystals that caught the light like trapped weather. And it was early afternoon when Corporal Denna came down off the flank at a run.

"Captain." Denna was young, competent, and had not yet learned to hide fear from her face. "Ridgeline east. They're back. Closer."

"How many?"

"More." Denna swallowed. "A lot more. Twenty? Thirty? They're not even trying to be subtle anymore."

They climbed the shoulder of the road where it crested a ridge, and Coral looked east and saw them.

Figures on the ridgelines. Standing. Dozens of them, strung along the high ground like dark stitches in the landscape, motionless, watching. At this distance she couldn't make out faces or dress — only shapes, human-shaped or near enough, each one distinct against the pale sky.

Nobody spoke. Thirty-one soldiers and one scribe stood on a road of singing glass and were watched by a silent audience arranged along every horizon.

"They've been herding us," Hallow said at last, flatly. "Three days of shadows on the ridges, always ahead, always flanking, never behind. We haven't been following the fire traces, Captain. We've been *driven* along them."

"Like sheep to the mountain," someone said, and regretted it immediately.

Veyra's face didn't change. That was the thing about her that Coral had begun, reluctantly, to admire: fear went through the captain like current through wire and emerged as decisions. "Denna, take four and shadow the east flank — observation only, no contact. Hallow, rear guard stays doubled. If anything crosses the road behind us, you fall back to me, not fight." She scanned the ridgelines once, unhurried, cataloguing. "They've had three days to kill us and haven't. That's data. Whatever they want, they want it more than they want us dead — for now. We keep moving. We keep discipline. And nobody—" her gaze swept the column, "-nobody fires unless fired upon. We are guests in this country until proven otherwise. Understood?"

A ragged chorus of assent.

Coral stayed at the crest a moment longer. The nearest watcher — a figure perhaps half a mile off, on a spur of black rock — seemed to shift its weight. Just slightly. As if it had been waiting for her specifically to look, and was satisfied.

Her hand drifted to the scribe's kit at her belt, to the little iron stylus she'd carried for eleven years, the instrument of her indenture, her craft, her cage. It felt suddenly absurd. What use was a pen against a horizon full of silent watchers?

But then — she remembered the burned page in the temple archive, the one Senior Scribe Maren had snatched from her hands and fed to the candle rather than let her read. She remembered the shape of the letters she'd glimpsed through the flame, angular and strange, nothing like the Temple's round mercantile hand. She had assumed they were decorative. Old ceremonial script, meaningless.

Now, looking at a country that had been erased from every map of Vell, she wondered.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a rock formation shaped, unsettlingly, like cupped hands. No fires. Cold rations. The mountain filled half the sky now, and its glow was unmistakable at dusk — a dull red bloom at the summit, breathing. Slowly. Nine seconds out, nine seconds in.

Coral sat apart, as she had learned to, and opened her field journal by the light of a shuttered lantern. Around her the camp settled into watch-shifts and uneasy sleep, and above them the stars — the real ones, the ones that had returned after the Night Without Stars, dimmer than before, as if the sky itself were recovering from a fever — wheeled slowly over the dead country.

She wrote:

*Day twelve beyond the ward-wall. Road of fused glass discovered, running northeast toward the glowing peak (provisionally Ember Mountain). Surface warm, sub-surface veins luminous, pulsing at regular intervals of approximately nine seconds. Traces of living fire continue along the route, fresher daily. Watchers observed on eastern ridgelines in numbers exceeding thirty. No hostile action. Movement pattern consistent with escort or with drive — unable to distinguish.*

She paused. Then, smaller, in the personal shorthand she permitted herself:

*I touched the road today and it answered. Not like the wardstone at the festival wall — that was a knock at a door. This was more like a hand closing around mine. It knew I was reading it. I think it has been waiting for a reader.*

*I should tell the captain. I will tell the captain. Tomorrow.*

*Eleven years ago I copied debt ledgers by candlelight and wanted nothing but the end of the row. Now I am lying on a road made of someone's ancient decision, being watched by a whole nation I cannot see, walking toward a heartbeat inside a mountain. Doss would say: don't want things. But wanting is the only compass I have out here, and it points north, and it has never once pointed anywhere useful before.*

She closed the journal. And because she couldn't sleep, and because the mountain's slow breath was the loudest thing in the world, she took out a blank page and, from memory, began to draw the letters she'd seen burning in Maren's candle eleven years ago.

Angular. Vertical strokes with crossbars like latches. A script built for doors.

She had gotten perhaps a dozen characters down when the hair rose on her arms.

Someone had whistled. Three notes, soft, from the darkness east of camp — a signal, or a greeting, or a lure. Then silence. Then, closer, from the rocks at the edge of the lantern-glow, a sound she recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water:

Scratching. Deliberate, measured. Someone writing.

She rose without waking anyone, stylus in hand, and stepped to the edge of the firelight. On the flat face of a boulder, in the last fringe of the lantern's reach, fresh characters gleamed faintly — scored into the stone as if by a heated point, still smoking at their edges. Angular. Vertical strokes with crossbars like latches.

The same script. The same hand, almost, as the burned page.

And beneath the unfamiliar words, added separately, in careful, accented, unmistakably readable Temple-trade glyphs — the trade-tongue every ledger in Vell was kept in — five characters:

**WE SEE THE SCRIBE.**

Coral stood very still. Behind her the camp slept; around her the mountains watched; ahead the ember-heart of the hidden kingdom beat its slow nine-second count. She looked down at her journal page, at the dozen borrowed letters she'd drawn from an eleven-year-old memory of fire, and understood that she had just answered a question she hadn't known was being asked.

They had tested her. And she had passed.

Far up the dark slope, at the edge of vision, a single figure detached itself from the ridgeline and stood silhouetted against the mountain's glow — waiting to see what the scribe would do next.
