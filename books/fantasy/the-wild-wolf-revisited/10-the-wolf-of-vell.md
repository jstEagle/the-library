## 10. The Wolf of Vell

Renn had been dead for six years, which was a convenient way to live.

The kingdom with no name kept two lists: the living and the loyal. He had been struck from both in the same season — first by the regent's seal, then by his own mother's silence when the riders came to their door. Exile inside exile. They hadn't cast him beyond the wards; there was nowhere beyond the wards. They had simply made him a ghost among ghosts, free to walk the ash-tunnels and the grey orchards and the long ridgelines where nothing grew but memory, so long as he never spoke his family name aloud.

He had almost gotten used to it. That was the worst part.

Now he lay on his belly in the silver grass above the Ember Road, watching eleven foreigners walk into a country that had killed everyone who ever tried to leave it, and he thought: *there they are.*

The scouts had brought word three days ago — strangers past the wall, armed, organized, carrying instruments of brass and glass that hummed faintly when magic pooled near them. Lady Ashvael's court had split like rotten timber. The Harvest faction wanted them captured and questioned with fire. The Quiet faction, what remained of it, wanted them studied and released. And the regent, as always, had wanted neither to decide until she could see which way the wind of it would blow.

So she had sent for the one man whose death freed him to move without orders anyone could trace.

"Find out whom they serve," she had said, in the cinder-court where the braziers burned low because fuel was counted like blood. "The Flame or the Silence. If they serve the Flame, we welcome them. If they serve the Silence—" She had not finished the sentence. She never did. In the kingdom with no name, unfinished sentences were how you survived politics.

Renn had asked the only question that mattered. "And if they serve themselves?"

Ashvael had looked at him for a long moment with those eyes like banked coals. "Then they're honest," she'd said, "and honesty we can work with."

* * *

He tracked them the old way, the way his grandmother had taught him back when the sky still had stars over the mountain and children were allowed to look at them. Not by footprint — the expedition was too careful for that, and the silver grass healed behind boots in an hour anyway, closing over disturbance like water. He tracked them by what they left *in* the land rather than on it.

Magic here behaved like weather. It pooled in the hollows at dusk, ran off the mountains in slow invisible streams, gathered in the fused glass of the Ember Road until the whole road shimmered like a river of frozen lightning. Three centuries of the wards had pressed it all down flat, held it under the skin of the world. Now the pressure was off, and everything that had been waiting was rising.

A trained soldier walking through that weather left ripples. A mage left wakes. And a scribe — Renn had learned to read the traces the hard way, exiled among the tunnel-keepers who tended the residual spellwork of the dead country — a scribe left writing.

These people were full of writing. Charms stitched into cloak-hems. Wards inked along scabbard-leather. A compass-thing at the head of the column that drank from the pools of magic and ticked like a dying beetle. And threaded through all of it, faint but unmistakable, a signature Renn recognized from documents older than his grandmother: the Temple of Quiet Hours. The lockhouse faith. The jailers' creed.

His stomach tightened. *They serve the Silence, then.* He should have turned back that first night and reported it, let the Harvest faction have its way, been done with the whole miserable business.

But there was a problem, and the problem walked at the rear of the column with ink stains on her fingers and a book chained to her belt.

* * *

Her name, he learned from listening, was Blackwood. Coral Blackwood. She was the expedition's scribe, which meant she did the work nobody else wanted: recording distances, sketching landmarks, transcribing the captain's spoken log each evening in a hand so precise it looked printed. Renn watched her do it from thirty yards away, hidden in the shadow of a leaning wardstone, and felt something he hadn't felt since before his exile — the itch of curiosity, sharp and unwelcome as a splinter working toward the bone.

Because her handwriting was wrong.

Not badly wrong. Beautifully wrong. Every letter landed exactly where the Temple's formal script demanded it land — he'd seen enough confiscated Temple documents in the archive-crypts to know the style — and yet underneath the discipline, something in the letters *leaned*. Toward the old kingdom's script. The one written in the covenant-books, the one carved over the ash-tunnels, the one the dragon's dream-priests said had come down from the sky itself, from before the doors closed.

She couldn't know she was doing it. Nobody raised in Vell knew that script; the Temple had spent three hundred years making sure of it. But her hand leaned toward it anyway, the way a plant leans toward light through a crack in a cellar wall.

*Blood remembers,* his grandmother used to say, sharpening her knife by the peat-fire. *You can burn the books and salt the fields, but blood remembers.* She had said it about the old families, the keeper-lines. Then the riders had come for their own family, and Renn had learned that blood remembering was exactly the kind of thing that got you erased.

He watched Coral Blackwood write, and the splinter went deeper.

* * *

On the fourth day, the column reached the standing stones.

They were old even by the standards of a country where *old* meant *older than the silence between stars*. Wardstones, the great anchor-stakes of the border network, planted in a ragged line across the shoulder of the valley hills — except these were on the far side, the inward face, the side no one in Vell had ever been permitted to think about. From this direction, Renn knew, they didn't look like protection. They looked like teeth.

The soldiers gave the stones a wide berth. Renn saw why: even now, with the wards fallen, the stones drank. Grass grew brown in rings around each one. Air bent slightly near them, like heat-shimmer in winter. Whatever fire had fed the wards for three centuries was gone, but the stones remembered being fed, and they were greedy things. Touch one uninvited and it would take the warmth out of your hand, the strength out of your arm, and if you were unlucky or stubborn, the years out of your life.

Every child of the kingdom learned it before they learned letters. *The grey stones are hungry. Let them starve.*

The expedition's captain — Osten, tall, grey-eyed, moving like someone perpetually listening for the second sound behind every first sound — called a halt well short of the line. Renn approved. Professional. Whoever had trained her had trained her properly.

Then the scribe stepped forward, and Renn's breath stopped in his throat.

"Coral." The captain's voice carried flat across the grass. "What are you doing."

"Recording them." Her voice was quieter; Renn had to strain. "Captain, I've been reading the survey notes for two days. These stones match the boundary markers in the temple ledgers — every measurement, every spacing. But the ledgers describe them facing outward. Defending the valley." She was already pulling her glove off as she talked, finger by finger, in the absent way of someone thinking harder than she was acting. "From this side, the carvings are different. They're not defensive sigils at all. I've seen this notation before. In the burned pages."

"You don't know that notation exists. You've never seen a burned page."

"No, Captain." A pause. "I just can't stop seeing it."

Renn rose halfway out of the grass without meaning to. *Careful,* he told himself. *Careful.* But his body had gone ahead of his judgment, the way it always did, the way that had gotten him named traitor at nineteen — for asking one question too loudly in the cinder-court, one question about where the flame actually came from and what the Silence actually was.

Coral Blackwood reached out her bare hand and laid it flat against the wardstone.

* * *

Later — much later, sitting alone in the dark of an ash-tunnel with his heart still going like a war-drum — Renn would try to reconstruct exactly what he had seen, and fail, and try again, and fail better.

Here is what he saw:

The stone woke.

Not the greedy sip it took from careless hands, not the slow draining pull that aged orchard-thieves into the petrified statues the kingdom used as scarecrows and warnings. Something else. The browned ring of grass around the stone flushed green — actual green, the first green Renn had seen in the dead country since the wards fell, green like a color returning from exile — and spread outward in a circle ten feet wide, twenty, racing through the silver grass like dawn coming up over a hill.

And Coral Blackwood did not flinch. Did not gasp. She stood with her palm flat against the stone and her eyes half-closed and her lips moving — *reading*, he realized, she was reading, tracing the carvings with her fingertips the way she traced letters on paper, translating as she went — and the stone gave her what it had, and took from her only what she offered, and the exchange between them was so gentle and so ordinary, so much like a woman greeting a horse she'd known since foaling, that Renn forgot entirely to be afraid.

The soldiers were not so calm. He heard the captain shout, heard boots running, heard steel clearing leather. By the time Osten reached the stone, the green was already fading, the circle contracting, the grass sighing back to silver. Coral pulled her hand away. She looked at her palm. She looked at the stone.

"I know what it says," she said, and her voice shook, and Renn understood suddenly that the shaking wasn't fear. "It's a census, Captain. This isn't a wall. It's a *list*. There are names in here. Hundreds of names. And every single one is crossed out."

Silence, then — real silence, the kind the dead country specialized in, silence with weight and grain to it.

"The priests said touching the wards was superstition," one of the soldiers said finally. A young one. His voice cracked on the last word. "Back home. They said—"

"I know what they said." Coral flexed her fingers, staring at them as though they belonged to someone else. "It answered me. Why would it answer me? I'm nobody. I'm a debtor. I copy other people's accounts in a room that smells like tallow."

*No,* Renn thought, pressed flat into the grass, his mouth dry. *No, you're not.*

*You're keeper-blood. You're the thing my whole country has been praying for and hiding from for three hundred years, and you don't know it, and the people who sent you might know it, and if the Harvest faction learns it before we do, every grey stone from here to the guttering mountain will drink you down to a statue and put you in an orchard.*

*And you touched a hungry stone and it went green, and you called yourself nobody, and your handwriting leans toward the sky-script, and I am lying in the dirt forty feet away falling in love with a ghost story.*

He caught himself on that last thought with something close to panic, and buried it, and failed to bury it, and buried it again.

* * *

He followed them two more days. It was reckless. Every hour increased the chance a scout would cut his sign, and the expedition's outriders were good — one of them, a wiry woman with a cross-scarred jaw, passed within fifteen feet of his hiding place at dusk and stopped, sniffing the wind like a wolf herself, and Renn spent four minutes not breathing while she stared directly at the hollow where he lay. She moved on. He marked her. If it came to knives, he would want the knife thrown before she finished turning.

But he needed to be sure. Ashvael deserved more than one miracle and a feeling in the gut. So he shadowed them down the long descent toward the Ember Road, watched them camp in the lee of a glass ridge, watched the scribe sit apart from the fire with her book open on her knees, not writing. Just looking at the mountain.

The mountain glowed. Faintly, deep in its roots, like a coal under ash — the dragon's breath, the last fire, the flame that stitched the kingdom together and was running out of thread. Everyone in the country felt it the way you feel a fever in a parent sleeping in the next room: the low constant dread that tonight might be the night it breaks.

She sat looking at it, and her face did something complicated. Longing and grief and fury, all at once, none of them performed. Renn had spent six years learning to read faces — it was how the dead stayed alive — and he had rarely seen one so legible and so honest.

"She feels it," he murmured to himself. "Three hundred years of silence, and she walks in and feels it in an afternoon."

Which raised the question he'd been circling for days and couldn't dodge anymore.

If she served the Silence, why did the Silence's tools sing in her hands? Why did the stones go green for her? Why did the Temple — the Temple that had built the walls and written the lists and crossed out the names — send keeper-blood across the border wearing its own chains?

Unless they didn't know. Unless somewhere in the lockhouse bureaucracy of Vell, a page had been misfiled, a name overlooked, a debt-ledger allowed to keep a keeper-blood alive because nobody upstairs remembered what keeper-blood was *for*.

Or unless somebody downstairs knew exactly, and had sent her deliberately, and was playing a longer game than the regent had imagined possible.

Either answer meant the same thing: Renn could not go back yet. He had to get closer.

* * *

He chose the moment the way his grandmother had taught him to choose everything — by waiting until wanting and needing pointed the same direction.

It came on the sixth day, at the rear of the column. The expedition had grown strung-out and tired; the magic-rich air wore on outsiders like a head-cold that never broke, and the two rearmost soldiers — the young one whose voice cracked, and a heavyset veteran with a Temple brand scarred over on his neck, which was a story Renn very much wanted to hear someday — had dropped a hundred yards behind the rest, arguing quietly about ration weights.

Renn dropped from the rocks in front of them.

He gave them credit: they didn't scream. The young one fumbled for his sword and the veteran actually got his half-drawn, which was faster than most. But Renn had chosen ground where their footing was glass-slick and his wasn't, and he had the hood of his ash-grey cloak back far enough to show his face — young, unscarred, unmistakably one of *them*, one of the watchers, one of the people the scouts had been reporting on the ridgelines for a week.

"Don't," he said. In Vellish — their tongue, the lockhouse tongue, learned from confiscated prayer-books during the worst winter of his exile. Their eyes went wide. "Steel will bring my kin down on you inside a minute, and they won't parley. I'm alone, and I'm here to talk. To your scribe."

"The captain doesn't negotiate with—"

"Not the captain." He sheathed the moment, deliberately, letting them watch him make himself unarmed. "The scribe. Tell her the wolf of Vell wants words with the woman who reads burned pages. She'll understand. If she doesn't—" he shrugged — "then I've guessed wrong about everything, and you may as well run me through, because the country's ending either way."

The veteran's scarred neck moved as he swallowed. The young one looked at his officer-less hundred yards of empty road, and then at the ridgelines where shadows sometimes stood up and weren't there when you blinked.

"And if she asks who sent you?" the veteran managed.

"Tell her the truth," Renn said. "Nobody. That's rather the point of me."

* * *

They came at moonrise — the scribe, and the captain, and four soldiers in a wide crescent that pretended not to be a trap and fooled no one. Renn stood in the open where they could see all of him, hands loose at his sides, hood down, letting the strange double light of the glowing mountain and the starless sky show his face plainly. Behind them, Coral Blackwood held a shuttered lantern, and the thin blade of light it leaked fell across his features, and he watched her study him the way she'd studied the wardstone — thoroughly, hungrily, without fear.

"You speak Vellish," the captain said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's coat.

"I speak six tongues, Captain. Exiles collect languages the way honest men collect debts." He inclined his head. "Veyra Osten. Expeditionary command, Temple charter, crown commission. You map, you sample, you report. Your secret orders are sealed in wax I could smell from the ridge — temple wax, black grade, the kind they use for orders the officers aren't meant to read. I don't need to know what they say. I need to know something simpler."

"Which is?"

"Whom do you serve?" He let the question hang in the cold air, let it reach all five of the crescent soldiers and the woman with the lantern. "The Flame that's dying under that mountain. Or the Silence that buried it."

Osten's expression didn't change. Good, he thought. Professional.

But Coral Blackwood stepped forward — out of the formation, out of the lantern-light's safety, one step onto ground no foreigner had walked in three centuries — and looked at him with those ink-stained fingers curled at her sides, and said:

"You called me the woman who reads burned pages. How do you know that phrase? Who told you I read anything?"

"Your handwriting told me," Renn said.

And he watched the words land, watched her go absolutely still in the way of someone hearing a door open in a house she'd believed empty all her life.

"My handwriting," she repeated.

"It leans," Renn said, "toward the script of the sky. The one from before the doors closed. Only one kind of blood does that, lady scribe. And my people have spent three hundred years crossing out the names of everyone who has it."

Behind her, the captain's hand had gone to her sword. Ahead of him, Coral Blackwood lifted her bare right hand into the lantern light, palm out, the way she must have shown it to the wardstone — showing him the ink stains, the callus of the pen, the hand that had touched a hungry stone and been answered.

"Then tell me," she said quietly, "whose names were on the list."

Renn looked at her — this stranger out of the lockhouse world, this debtor, this nobody, this impossible green-ring miracle — and understood that whatever he reported to Lady Ashvael, whenever he reported it, the war over the last flame had already changed shape tonight, here, on this road, in the space between one question and the next.

"The list is long," he said. "We should start walking. And you should start at the beginning — with the page they burned."
