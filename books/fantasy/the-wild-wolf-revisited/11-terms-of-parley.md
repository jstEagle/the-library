## 11. Terms of Parley

* * *

**Renn**

The rear guard made it easy.

They straggled, the way lowlanders always did when the silver grass confused their eyes and the ground hummed beneath their boots like a plucked string. Four of them, half a mile behind the main column, wrapped in Temple-grey cloaks that did nothing to hide them against the ash-pale hills. One carried a lantern. A lantern. As if light were a courtesy here, as if anything in this country wanted to be seen.

Renn watched from the fold of a ridge with his hood back and his breath shallow, and he hated how much he understood them. He had been a lowlander once — or near enough. Nine years in the outer dark would do that to a person. You forgot which sounds meant warning and which meant weather. You forgot that the land itself was watching.

"Take the lantern one alive," he signed. His three companions of the watch — Sef, Ila, and the boy called Marn — melted into the grass without answering, because answering was for people who hadn't grown up learning silence as a first language.

The capture itself took eleven heartbeats. He counted, out of old habit. Sef rose out of the ground like something the country was dreaming; Ila's weighted cord took the lantern-bearer's ankles; Marn, who was barely sixteen and already better at this than Renn had been at twenty-five, put a sleep-needle into two necks before either man finished turning around. The fourth dropped his sword and knelt, hands laced over his head, whispering what Renn was fairly sure was a prayer to a god who had never heard of this place.

Alive. All four. That mattered. Lady Ashvael's orders had been precise on that point: learn whether they serve the Flame or the Silence, and bring proof, not corpses.

Renn came down the slope slowly, letting them see him come. The kneeling soldier flinched from his face — from the grey ash-marks on his cheekbones, the kingdom's old sigils cut into his leather — and Renn felt the flinch land somewhere under his ribs where he kept things he didn't look at.

"You're from Vell," he said. "The valley beyond the wall."

The soldier nodded so hard his helmet slipped.

"Good," Renn said. "Then you can answer the only question there is." He crouched, forearms on knees, close enough to see the sweat crawling down the man's temple. "Who sent you? The Temple of Quiet Hours. What do they want?"

"I d-don't—"

"You don't know. Of course you don't. They never tell the ones who carry the lanterns." He stood. "Bring them. The scribe especially."

"There is no scribe among us," said the kneeling one, and then, because fear makes honest creatures of soldiers: "There's a woman. Back with the column. She reads things off the stones. The captain keeps her close."

Reads things off the stones.

Renn went very still. Last night, from three hundred yards, he had watched a small figure in expedition grey lay her palm flat against one of the old boundary-stones — a wardstone, dead these three centuries, or supposed to be — and he had seen the stone answer. A thread of pale fire, thin as spider silk, running up between her fingers. She hadn't burned. Keeper-blood didn't burn; that was the whole point of keepers, though no one in the court below the mountain liked hearing it said aloud.

He'd told himself he'd imagined it. He was getting tired of telling himself that.

"The woman," he said. "What's her name?"

"Coral. Coral Blackwood."

The name went through him like cold water through a crack. Not because he knew it. Because it sounded like a name out of the archive-crypts — a ledger-name, a valley-name, the kind of name that got written down by someone who intended to find it again later.

"Bind their wrists in front," he told Marn. "They'll walk easier. And send a runner ahead — tell the regent's watch we're bringing guests, and tell them—" He hesitated, hating the words even as he knew they were true. "Tell them I want the parley held before she reaches the mountain. Before the court can make her a prisoner instead of a guest. There's a difference, and it matters."

Sef raised an eyebrow at him. *Since when do you care about the difference?*

Since a dead stone lit up under a stranger's hand, Renn didn't say. Since the stars went out and everything I was exiled for started coming true.

"Move," he said instead.

* * *

**Coral**

They came for the rear guard at dusk, which in the dead country was a strange violet hour when the silver grass threw shadows in the wrong direction, and Coral — walking at the column's center with her scribe-case strapped to her back — knew it before any horn sounded, because the spellwork told her.

She had been reading the land all day the way she read ledgers: line by line, looking for discrepancies. The magic here pooled in the hollows like rainwater and ran along the old roads in bright threads only she seemed able to see. Captain Osten had stopped asking how Coral knew things and simply started positioning her where she could know more of them. It was, Coral thought, the closest thing to respect the expedition had offered anyone.

So when four knots of light in the distance guttered and went out — not dimmed, *extinguished*, snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers — she turned to Veyra Osten and said, "We've lost the rear guard."

Veyra's hand went to her sword. "How do you know?"

"Their wards went dark." Everyone wore a temple-issue charm now, a little lead seal stamped with the Quiet Hours' closed-eye sigil. Dead weight in the valley; faintly warm out here. Four of those warm points had just gone cold at once, in the same instant, in the same place. "Someone took them. Quietly. Professionally."

"Sound the column. Tighten the—"

The grass around them began to sing.

It wasn't wind. It was the stalks themselves, each silver blade vibrating at a pitch that slid past hearing and lodged behind the eyes, and out of the singing rose figures — dozens of them, maybe more, rising out of a hillside that had been empty a moment before, draped in ash-colored cloth, faces marked with pale glyphs that Coral's eye itched to transcribe. They did not advance. They did not need to. Every arrowhead in sight was aimed at the column, and every archer stood with the unhurried patience of someone defending home ground.

"Stand fast," Veyra breathed. "No one looses. No one moves. Scribe Blackwood—" her voice dropped, "—whatever happens next, stay behind me."

But Coral had stopped listening, because one of the figures had stepped forward — tall, lean, moving with a wariness that looked rehearsed but wasn't — and the glyphs on his face were *writing*. Old script. The same angular, beautiful lettering she had spent three years copying in secret from a single smuggled fragment in the temple archives, the fragment Senior Scribe Halloway had torn from her hands and fed to a candle rather than let her finish it.

She could read it.

Three centuries after the wards rose, three centuries after the kingdom beyond the wall was struck from every map and memory in Vell, she could stand here and read its language off a stranger's face.

The tall one spoke, and his words were the valley tongue — accented, careful, learned late — but beneath them, stitched into his cloak hem, the old script ran in lines like verse:

*We are the watch of the unnumbered country. Speak your purpose or be spoken for.*

"They want our purpose," Coral translated, without meaning to say it aloud.

"I gathered," Veyra said through her teeth. "Tell them we come under treaty—"

"There's no treaty. There hasn't been anything for three hundred years." Coral's heart was slamming against her ribs, and underneath the fear was something worse and brighter, the feeling from the ward-wall multiplied a thousandfold: *the world is larger than the ledger.* "Captain. Let me talk to them."

"Absolutely not."

"They took four of our people. They'll kill us or they won't, but right now they think we're invaders, and I'm the only one here who can prove we aren't." She was already stepping forward, and some distant part of her mind — the part trained by eleven years of indenture to calculate exactly how much trouble a given action would cost — noted that she was going to pay for this. The rest of her didn't care.

"Blackwood—"

"If they take me," she said, "they'll bring me to whoever decides things. Which is where you need someone anyway. Isn't it?"

Veyra looked at her for a long moment — the captain's grey eyes doing their cold arithmetic — and then, quietly: "Come back."

"No promises," Coral said, and walked out of the column into the no-man's-land between two peoples who had forgotten each other existed.

* * *

**Renn**

She came alone.

That alone told him something. Lowlanders sent their priests forward, or their swords, or their trembling designated spokesmen clutching scrolls of authority. They did not send a small woman with ink-stained fingers and a scribe's case and her chin up as if the forty arrows aimed at her chest were a formality.

He met her halfway across the open ground. Up close, the ash-glyphs on his face must have looked fearsome; he'd chosen them years ago for exactly that reason. But her eyes weren't on his face. Her eyes went to his cloak hem, and widened, and her lips moved — silently at first, then aloud, halting, accent terrible, cadence wrong in a way that made the hair rise on his arms:

"*Sel veth mara, sel veth korane.*" *Of the watch, of the keeping.* She was reading his insignia. Translating it. Getting it almost right.

"Where," Renn said, and his own voice came out rough, "did you learn that?"

"A fragment. One page, hidden in the temple archives. I copied it thirty-one times before they caught me, and they burned my copies and the original both." She swallowed. "I memorized it instead. All of it. I've been reciting it to myself for three years like a prayer. I thought—" Her composure cracked, just for a heartbeat, and he saw the girl under the scribe: young, exhausted, burning. "I thought it was all that was left of you. Of your people. I thought I was the only one alive who could read it."

The watch behind him had gone utterly silent. Sef's hand had drifted toward a knife. Ila was staring at Coral with an expression Renn had no translation for.

"You have four of our people," Coral pressed on, steadier now, falling into rhythm. "I'm asking for their return. In exchange — ask me anything. Test me however you need to. I'm not the Temple's creature, whatever they've told you I am. I'm a debtor. I was property until nine days ago. Whatever you think the valley is—" her voice hardened, "—I assure you it's less free than your exile."

*She knows,* Renn thought, dizzy. *Somehow she knows the word exile means something here.* 

He should have taken her prisoner and been done with it. That was protocol. That was survival. Instead he heard himself say: "Recite the fragment."

Her breath caught. Then she straightened, closed her eyes, and spoke.

"*Ardanui sel, ardanui vor. Kemmarath ila, kemmarath dor. Un veneth kalen, un veneth sor—*"

And the whole ridge answered her.

Not the watch — the *ridge*. The grass, the stones, the buried ward-lines that ran under the dead country like veins under skin. Pale fire woke along the ground in slow branching threads, rising toward her voice the way flowers turn toward sun, and the glyph-marks on every watcher's face glimmered in answer, and above them all the dead sky — the starless, sealed sky that had haunted Renn for nine years — flickered with the ghost of constellations, there and gone, an echo remembering itself.

Coral opened her eyes. She was crying without seeming to notice.

"What was that," Renn whispered. Around him, three of his watchers had knelt — actually knelt, in the wet grass, an act that had not happened in the kingdom since before his grandmother's time. Marn was still standing, but the boy's face was wrecked with awe.

"I don't know." Her voice shook. "It's just — it's the fragment. It's the only page I ever—"

"It's the Opening Verse." The words came out of Renn flat with disbelief. "The first words spoken when the kingdom was founded. The words the wards were built to *silence*. No one has spoken them aloud in three hundred years because everyone who remembered them was—" he stopped. Recalculated. "Everyone was supposed to be dead."

Somewhere far away, down under the glowing mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the ground hummed with it, and every watcher on the ridge felt it pass through their bones.

Coral wiped her face with an ink-stained hand and asked, in the small, practical voice of a woman renegotiating terms: "Does this mean I get my four soldiers back?"

Renn laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty from disuse. "No," he said. "It means you get an audience. With the regent herself. Your soldiers come too — unharmed, I swear it on the flame. But if you walk into the Court of Cinders reciting the Opening Verse, scribe, half the court will call you a prophet and the other half will call you a weapon, and both halves will want to use you."

"And the other option?"

"Stay here. Be hunted by the Harvest faction, who will want your blood for exactly the reasons the court will want your voice. Or go back to your captain and march on the mountain and find out what the kingdom does to armed strangers at its gates." He paused. "Those are the terms. I'd take the first one. But I'm biased — the first one's the only one where nobody shoots anybody today."

Behind her, the column waited, Veyra Osten's silhouette rigid at its head. Behind him, the watch knelt in the singing grass. Between them, the last light drained out of a sky with no stars in it.

Coral Blackwood looked up at that dead sky for a long moment — and Renn saw her make the decision the way he'd seen scribes commit ink to an unforgiving page: completely, and all at once.

"All right," she said. "Take me to your regent. But understand the terms from my side, too." Her chin came up again, that ledger-clerk defiance that was starting to seem less absurd and more necessary every minute he knew her. "Whatever your court thinks I am — I'm going in as the valley's witness. Someone has to write down what really happened here. Someone has to finally *keep the account*."

Down beneath the mountain, the great sleeping thing sighed fire into its dreams, and the first true word of a new covenant passed, unrecorded by anyone but the two of them, between the last exile of the hidden kingdom and the first free scribe of Vell.

"Then welcome," Renn said, "to the country with no name. Try not to give it a bad one."

He turned to lead her down — and froze, because far to the south, back along the road they'd traveled, a second light had appeared on the horizon. Not a campfire. Not a lantern.

A beacon. Temple-white, cold, deliberate. Burning where no beacon should be.

Someone else had crossed the wall.
