## 12. Under the Guttering Mountain

They took her horses away at the mouth of the tunnel, and after that they took her boots, and after that Coral stopped keeping track of what was taken, because the mountain had begun to take things of its own.

The passage descended in long spirals, cut not by chisel but by heat — the walls were glass in places, ribbed and flowing, as if the stone had once been liquid and had been caught mid-sentence when the world cooled. Her escort walked barefoot and silent, six figures in ash-grey robes with hoods drawn back, and Coral saw now that the figures who had watched them from the ridgelines were not soldiers at all. They were old. Their hands were stained to the wrist with soot that no washing would lift, and their eyes had the particular shine of people who had spent their whole lives looking at a fire.

They had given her back her satchel. That was the first mercy. Her ink, her pens, her rolls of blank vellum — the tools of her trade, returned without a word, as if the reading of their own dead script aloud on the mountainside had been payment enough for everything else. Coral clutched the strap against her ribs like a child with a blanket and told herself it was practical. Scribes did not surrender their materials. Scribes did not surrender anything.

The air grew warm. Then warm in a way that had a pulse to it.

"Do you feel that?" she asked, and her voice came out smaller than she intended, swallowed at once by the glass throat of the tunnel.

The eldest of her escorts — a woman with a face like cracked river clay, who had not spoken since the parley — glanced back over her shoulder. "Feel what, scribe?"

"The air. It's — breathing."

The woman considered her for a long moment, the way Coral had seen the High Scribe consider a ledger entry that didn't balance. Then she turned forward again and said, in the tone of someone correcting a novice's transcription, "It is not the air that breathes."

* * *

The tunnel opened without warning.

One step, and the glass corridor became a cavern so vast that Coral's mind simply declined to accept it. Her eyes reported a ceiling lost in a red-gold haze, walls that curved away into distance on every side, a floor of dark glass that reflected everything twice — once above, once below — so that she seemed to stand inside a lantern. Heat rose through the soles of her bare feet, steady and rhythmic, and the light did not come from torches or from any lamp she could find.

It came from the center.

She walked — or was walked, her feet moving without her permission — to the edge of a terrace of black stone, and she looked down into the heart of the mountain, and she understood, in the way one understands a fall only after it has already begun, what the woman had meant.

The dragon was the mountain.

Not housed in it. Not sleeping beneath it. The cavern was its chest. The terraces were its ribs, grown over with centuries of fused stone, and the galleries where soot-stained figures moved quietly with buckets and long-handled tools were the spaces between its heartbeats. The warmth in the floor was its blood. The light was coming through its skin, the way a candle flame shows through the wall of a paper shade — a vast, slow, guttering radiance, brighter along the sweep of its flank and dim, terribly dim, along the hollows.

Coral had read descriptions of dragons. Every child in Vell had. They were in the festival plays: monsters of the border stories, wings like storm clouds, fire enough to boil a river. The Temple's own histories mentioned them exactly twice, both times as things that had been driven out of the world by the righteous founders of the valley.

No one had told her they were beautiful.

No one had told her they could be *dying*.

The creature — she could not think of it as an animal, not with that light — lay coiled through the roots of the mountain like a river through a valley it had carved itself. Its scales were the size of temple doors, and they were not the black of the stories but a deep, burnished bronze gone grey at the edges, the way a banked fire goes grey, the way coals go grey in the last hour before dawn when no one has fed them. Along its throat, veins of light pulsed — gold, then amber, then a thin and struggling orange — and with each pulse the whole cavern brightened and dimmed, brightened and dimmed, and Coral realized she had been feeling that rhythm in the floor since the tunnel mouth, and that she had mistaken it for her own frightened heart.

"How old," she whispered. Her lips were cracked; the air was dry as an oven's breath. "How old is it?"

"Older than the wards," the clay-faced woman said. "Older than the valley. Old enough to remember the sky when it was whole." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that was not pride and not quite grief. "We call her the Mother of Embers. She has another name, but it is not for strangers, and it is certainly not for a daughter of Vell."

"I'm not—" Coral began, and stopped, because she did not know how to finish the sentence. *I'm not from Vell* was a lie. *I'm not one of them* — the Temple, the ledgers, the eleven years of her life measured out in copied debts — that was not a lie, but it was not the whole truth either, and she had a scribe's horror of entries that did not balance.

She said instead, "She's dying."

It was not a question. You did not need to be a keeper of anything to see it. The light was wrong. It guttered — that was the word that kept rising in her, the word from a thousand candlelit nights in the scriptorium, watching a flame eat the last of its wick and knowing that the next hour would be dark. The dragon's fire guttered, and the cavern with it, and Coral understood with a cold, sliding certainty that the soot-stained people moving quietly through the ribs of the dying creature were not tending her.

They were keeping her company.

* * *

They led her down. There were stairs, cut into the inner curve of a rib, and each step brought the heat up another degree and the light another shade closer to the color of the ember-road outside. Coral counted the steps because counting was what she did, because counting was the wall she had built between herself and every frightening thing since she was seven years old and her mother's hand had slipped out of hers in a temple courtyard, and the count reached four hundred and eleven before the stairs ended and she made herself stop, because four hundred and eleven was nothing, was a number with no meaning, was the mind's small fist clenched against a truth too large to hold.

At the bottom there was a gallery of black stone, smooth and warm, running along the dragon's flank at the height of her heart. And here the soot-stained people were gathered — dozens of them, maybe a hundred, kneeling or standing in a silence so complete that Coral could hear the fire.

Not metaphorically. She could *hear* it. A sound beneath sound, a vast slow respiration, an in-draw and an out-flow, and with every out-flow the light along the dragon's throat swelled, and with every in-draw it sank, and Coral stood at the rail of the gallery with her bare feet burning gently on the stone and wept without deciding to, the tears running hot down her face and drying before they reached her jaw.

Because she knew what the breath was.

She had known since the ridge, since the glass road, since the silver grass where magic pooled in the hollows like rainwater — she had known it in the way a scribe knows a forgery, not by one detail but by the accumulation of them, by the way every line of the world beyond the wall was *written* in a hand she almost recognized. The wards had not fallen. The wards had been *spent*. She had thought it in the dark of that first night, and the priests had called it superstition, and now she stood inside the proof of it, inside the chest of the proof of it, watching the last dragon-fire breathe.

Three centuries, the expedition's briefings had said. Three centuries since the border closed, since the dead country began, since the stars went strange over Vell and the Temple wrote its histories and burned its pages. Three centuries of this. Three centuries of a fire with nothing feeding it but its own body, spent drop by drop through the stone of the mountain, up through the roots of the world, out along veins no one in Vell had ever been permitted to see — out to the wall, to the wards, to the border that held the hidden kingdom *stitched together*, and beyond it, to the valley, to the fields, to the festival lights of the only country Coral had ever known.

The valley of Vell was warm because this creature was burning.

The valley of Vell was *safe* because this creature had been dying for three hundred years, patiently, magnificently, spending itself to hold together a world that had written it out of every book.

"Stop," Coral said. Her voice cracked. She said it again, louder, and the soot-stained people turned to look at her, a hundred faces in the guttering light, and she realized she had raised her hands as though to push back the whole cavern. "Stop — someone has to *stop* — she's spent, she's been spent, you're letting her—"

"Letting her?" The clay-faced woman stood beside her at the rail, and her voice was very quiet. "Scribe of Vell. There is no *letting*. There is only what she chooses, and what she has always chosen. She was asked, three hundred years ago, at the end of a war your books do not record, whether she would give her fire to hold the kingdom whole until a door could be found. She said yes. She has been saying yes every hour since. We do not let her. We *witness* her."

"Witness," Coral repeated. The word sat strangely in her mouth. In the scriptorium, witnessing was a formal act — two scribes present at a signing, their marks at the bottom of the page, their names bound to the truth of a debt.

"You are a people of records," the woman said. "So record this." She gestured, and the whole cavern seemed to move with the gesture — the ribs, the galleries, the vast coiled dark, the throat of light rising and falling like a tide. "This is the account your valley was built to conceal. Every candle in your temple. Every ledger. Every debt. Warmth has a source, and the source has been paying, and no one in three hundred years has entered the payment in any book."

Coral's hands were shaking. She gripped the rail. The stone was warm as skin.

"I'm a scribe," she said. It came out as an accusation, though of whom she could not have said. "That's all I am. I copy debts. I don't— I wasn't sent to see this. I was sent to *map*."

"Then map," the woman said.

* * *

They gave her a place at the edge of the gallery — a shelf of black stone, smooth as a lectern, and a lamp that burned without oil, fed on something that came up through the mountain itself. Coral knelt and opened her satchel with fingers that would not entirely obey her, and she took out a roll of vellum and her pen, and she did the only thing she had ever been any good at.

She began to take the account.

She wrote the dimensions of the cavern as best she could estimate them, and then crossed the estimate out, because estimates were for grain stores and tithes, and this was not a grain store. She wrote the color of the scales, and the rhythm of the light — she timed it against her own pulse, four beats of the throat-light to one of her heart, a patience built into the very tempo of the dying. She wrote the soot-stained people, their number, their silence, their gentleness, the way one old man moved along the flank with a long-handled brush, clearing ash from a seam between two scales with the tenderness of a man combing his daughter's hair.

She wrote: *The fire is the wards. The wards are the fire. There is no border. There is only a wound, dressed in stone.*

And then, because she was Coral Blackwood, who had spent eleven years learning that every account must balance or the whole book is a lie, she wrote the entry she had no right to write and could not stop herself from writing:

*Received, by the valley of Vell, from an unnamed creditor: warmth, light, safety, three hundred years. Paid: nothing. Owed: everything.*

She stared at the line a long time. The lamp guttered and steadied. Somewhere below her, the vast breath drew in, and the light sank, and for a moment the whole cavern was the color of dusk, and Coral felt the dark reach up toward her like a hand.

Then the breath went out again, and the light returned, and she understood that she was sitting inside the last hour of a candle that had burned for three hundred years, and that no one — not the Temple, not the expedition, not the soot-stained witnesses, not the dying god herself — knew how many minutes remained in it.

* * *

It was near what she guessed was dawn, though dawn meant nothing under a mountain, when the light changed.

Not guttered — *reached*. The pulse along the dragon's throat, which had been sinking all night in a slow tide of grey, suddenly flared gold, and the whole cavern brightened as if a door had opened onto summer, and every soot-stained figure in the galleries went still.

Coral stood up so fast her knees cracked.

The light was moving. It gathered along the dragon's flank, pooled beneath the scales of her breast, and there — where the ribs met, where the great slow heart of the creature must lie — the scales themselves began to shift, sliding apart with a sound like a glacier remembering how to move, and Coral saw beneath them a hollow, and in the hollow a light that was not the tired amber of the rest of the body but something older, whiter, the color the stars must have been before anyone taught them to go out.

The clay-faced woman's hand closed on Coral's arm. "Down," she said, and her voice had lost all its calm. "Scribe — get *down*—"

But Coral could not move. Because the light was not random. It was shaped. It rose from the hollow of the dragon's breast in a slow column, and the column bent, and gathered, and took a form — and the form was looking at her.

Not the dragon. The dragon's eyes, when Coral had dared to glance at them, had been closed for hours, sunk deep beneath ridges of grey scale, sealed like the eyes of a statue. This was something else. A face made of light, hanging in the column above the heart, vast and faint and flickering, and it had no expression that a human face would recognize, and it was looking directly at the gallery, at the shelf of black stone, at the small kneeling figure with the ink-stained fingers.

At her.

The soot-stained people had all knelt. The clay-faced woman was pulling at Coral's sleeve, saying her title like a prayer — *scribe, scribe, scribe* — and Coral stood in the middle of a hundred bowed witnesses with her bare feet on the warm stone and her heart slamming against her ribs, and the face of light hung above the heart of the dying dragon, and it did not speak, because it was not a thing that spoke.

It did something worse.

It *waited*.

The way a creditor waits. The way a ledger waits, open to a blank line, pen laid beside it, patient as stone. Coral knew that silence. She had sat on the other side of it for eleven years. She knew exactly what it meant, in any language, in any world, under any mountain.

The light flickered once — gold, white, gold — and in its flickering Coral saw, or dreamed she saw, or was *shown*, a single image: a sky. A whole sky, wheeling with stars, more stars than the valley had ever had, stars in rivers and stars in clusters, a sky so crowded with light that the dark between it looked like the exception rather than the rule.

Then the image closed like a book, and the face of light began, slowly, to sink back into the hollow of the breast, and the great scales began their glacier-slow slide toward closing, and Coral heard her own voice, thin and cracked and absolutely certain, ringing through the cavern of the dying god:

"Wait. *Wait.* I'm a scribe. I can — I can write it down. Whatever you are, whatever you were — I can write it down. No one else will. I *swear* no one else—"

The scales stopped.

The whole mountain stopped. The breath caught — Coral felt it in the floor, in her teeth, in the lamp flame standing perfectly still — and in that enormous, listening silence, a hundred soot-stained witnesses slowly raised their heads, and the clay-faced woman's grip on her arm went slack, and somewhere far above, through three hundred years of stone, Coral thought she heard the wards of Vell humming like a struck bell.

The face of light rose one last time above the closing scales.

And it asked her — not in words, not in sound, but in the oldest language she knew, the language of every ledger she had ever copied, the language of the blank line and the waiting pen:

*Name yourself.*
