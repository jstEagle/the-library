## 13. The Court of Cinders

The throne room of the kingdom with no name smelled of cold ash and old honey, and Renn hated it the way you only hate a place that once loved you.

Seven years since he'd last stood beneath the vaulted ceiling of the Court of Cinders, and nothing had changed except the depth of the cracks. The ceiling had been painted once — a sky, of course, a painted sky, because the real one had been sealed away before anyone living was born — and now the paint flaked down in slow grey snow onto the floor, where pages swept it into buckets each morning and carried it out to the ash-fields. Even the sky in here was dying.

The throne itself was not a throne. That had always been the kingdom's one honest gesture. Lady Ashvael sat on a block of fused glass, the same glass that paved the Ember Road, the same glass the dragon's dying breath had made of a riverbed three centuries ago. It was translucent at the edges and dark at the heart, and when the light of the gutter-lamps moved behind it, it glowed like a coal remembering fire. A regent's seat made of the thing everyone in the room was arguing about killing.

Renn stood at the edge of the dais steps, where exiles stood. Not at the bottom, among the petitioners — he'd been granted that much, grudgingly, by a herald who had clearly been told to grant it — but not on the steps either. The middle distance. The nowhere place that was his, now and probably forever.

The captives were being brought in.

He watched them come down the long hall between ranks of the Cindersguard, and he found his eye going — against every professional instinct he owned — to the scribe.

The tall one, Osten, walked like a soldier even in chains of grey silk. The kingdom didn't use iron; iron was for things you wanted to hurt, and the court preferred its prisoners comfortable and confused. Osten's eyes were doing what a good officer's eyes do: counting exits, counting guards, counting the weight of the chandeliers and the height of the windows. Renn had served under officers like her. He made a private note that if it ever came to violence in this room, she would be the one to fear.

But the scribe — Coral Blackwood, the watchers had reported the name, passed along the ridgelines in the old hand-signs — Coral Blackwood walked like someone reading a room instead of fighting it. Her head turned slowly, taking in the murals, the banners, the ash-snow falling from the painted sky. Her lips moved once, slightly, and Renn knew — he would have bet his exile on it — that she was sounding out the words on a banner. Reading the old script. The script that half the court's own children couldn't read anymore.

*She read the wardstone aloud,* one of his scouts had told him, still not quite believing it. *The old covenant-script. Her accent was terrible. She read it anyway.*

* * *

"Captain Veyra Osten," the herald called, in the formal cadence, "of the expedition of the Temple of Quiet Hours, out of the valley of Vell. And those who travel under her seal."

The word *Temple* moved through the court like wind through dry grass. Renn heard it hiss and shift. Somewhere to his left, one of the harvest faction's lords — Vael Corrinne, gold chain, hungry eyes — leaned over to murmur to his neighbor, and Renn did not need to hear the words to know them. *The Temple. The Temple has come at last. The Temple that built the cage.*

Lady Ashvael did not rise. That was a statement in itself; regents rose for ambassadors, and she was ruling, in advance, that these were not ambassadors. She sat on her coal-glass seat with her hands folded and her grey braid coiled at her shoulder, and she looked at the captives the way a surgeon looks at a wound she did not make and must now decide how to close.

"You crossed the border wards," she said. Her voice was not loud. It never needed to be. "The wards that fell three nights ago, on the night the sky went dark. You crossed into a country your histories do not name, because your histories were written to ensure it could not be named. Tell me, Captain Osten — what did your Temple send you to find?"

Veyra Osten stopped at the foot of the dais steps and did not kneel. Renn watched the Cindersguard tense, watched Ashvael not react, and revised his estimate of the woman upward again.

"My orders are sealed," Osten said. "I'll tell you what I was told and no more than that: map the country. Learn what killed the stars. Report."

"And do you believe that is the whole of it?"

"I believe," Osten said, "that the Temple of Quiet Hours does not spend the money it spent on this expedition to draw maps."

A sound went through the court — not quite laughter, not quite approval. Ashvael's mouth moved by a degree that in another face would have been a smile.

"Then we understand each other. You are a soldier sent by priests, and you suspect your priests. You will find no argument from me. My own court," and here her gaze swept the hall, and the hall went quiet the way a room goes quiet when a blade comes out, "is currently at war with itself over what to do with the only fire left in the world. You have arrived in the middle of a family argument, Captain. I hope you brought no opinions of your own."

"I brought forty-two soldiers," Osten said. "They're being held somewhere, I assume."

"The eastern barracks. Fed, warm, and bored. Boredom is the gentlest chain we have." Ashvael turned her head slightly. "And the scribe. Step forward."

Renn's heart did something he resented it for doing.

Coral Blackwood stepped forward. Up close — and he had only ever seen her through a spyglass, a grey figure at a grey wardstone, touching it without burning — she was younger than he'd expected, and more tired, and there was ink on her fingers that no amount of scrubbing would ever fully remove. He knew that stain. Every scribe carried it. It got into the whorls of the skin and stayed, so that everything you touched thereafter carried, faintly, the mark of the page.

"You read the wardstone," Ashvael said.

"I read part of it," Coral said. "Enough to know it was a covenant. Not enough to know what was covenanted."

"And yet you came to a parley on the strength of half a reading. That was either courage or recklessness."

"It was the only door offered," Coral said. "I've learned to take doors as they come."

Something flickered across Ashvael's face — there and gone, quick as the ash-snow falling. Renn had served this court long enough, once, to read it. It was the look of a woman hearing an echo of someone she had lost.

"You will be our guests," Ashvael said, which in the Court of Cinders meant what it had always meant: *prisoners, but politely.* "The kingdom with no name extends its hospitality to the expedition of the Temple of Quiet Hours, and its protection, for as long as protection lasts." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Which may not be long."

* * *

The court broke into its factions the moment the formal audience ended, the way water breaks around a stone.

Renn knew the geography of it by heart, because he had grown up in the eddies of it. There were the Loyalists, clustered near the dais — old families, old names, the regent's people, who held the line that had held for three hundred years: *the dragon lives, the flame endures, the kingdom holds.* There were the Quiet, who wanted nothing but to be left alone and had the good sense not to say so loudly. And there were the Harvest.

The Harvest faction had been a fringe thing when Renn was young — fire-priests and armorer-lords muttering at the edges of the court about *waste*. Three centuries of watching the dragon gutter, three centuries of watching the flame that stitched the kingdom together grow thinner every year, and a certain kind of mind arrives at a certain kind of arithmetic. *The dragon is dying anyway. The fire will be spent on nothing, holding together a country no one can leave. Better to take the fire while there is fire to take. Better a weapon than a candle.*

Three years of exile had let Renn watch the fringe become the center. Vael Corrinne's gold chain had more links in it now. His benches in the hall had filled. And tonight, with the wards fallen and strangers walking the Ember Road, the arithmetic had stopped being heresy and started being policy.

He watched Corrinne work the crowd after the audience, moving from cluster to cluster with a wine cup he never drank from, and he watched the scribe being escorted past him toward the guest wing, and the two currents of his attention collided.

Because Corrinne stopped. Corrinne looked at the scribe. And Corrinne smiled.

Renn had been a soldier, a watcher, and an exile. He knew what that smile was. It was the smile of a man looking at a tool he had just realized he needed.

* * *

"You shouldn't be here," said the voice behind him, and Renn didn't turn, because he knew it, and because turning would mean seeing Sera Corrinne's face, and he had seen it last across a courtroom seven years ago when she had testified — truthfully, that was the worst of it, she had testified truthfully — that he had questioned the regent's judgment in open session.

"I'm an exile, not a ghost," he said. "I'm allowed in the halls."

"You're allowed in the halls," Sera said, coming around to his side, "because the regent finds you useful, and the regent finds you useful because you brought her the Temple's expedition wrapped in ribbon. Don't mistake tolerance for welcome. The court remembers what you said about her judgment. The court remembers everything. That's the whole problem with courts."

"The harvest faction remembers too," Renn said. "Your uncle smiled at the scribe."

Sera was quiet a moment. She was Corrinne's niece and had been Renn's friend before she was either, and she had the Corrinne talent for being honest at the worst possible moments. "My uncle smiles at everything he intends to use," she said. "The scribe read the covenant-script. Do you understand what that means? There are perhaps forty people in this kingdom who can read that script fluently, and half of them are archive-keepers over seventy. And an outlander girl reads it off a wardstone with a terrible accent, and touches the stone without burning, and my uncle — who has spent ten years trying to find a way to open the dragon's heart-chamber without killing half the harvest crews — my uncle looks at her and sees a key."

"The heart-chamber is sealed by keeper-blood," Renn said. "Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," Sera agreed. "And everyone also knows the keeper-lines were in the valley. In Vell. Where the Temple has been, for three centuries, doing something to the descendants of the people who built the cage." She looked at him, and her eyes were the grey of the ash-fields. "You saw her touch the wardstone, Renn. The scouts say the stone answered her. You know what the court will do with that."

"The court will do what courts do," Renn said. "It will eat her."

"Yes," Sera said simply. "And my uncle will eat her first, and politely, and call it hospitality, and by the time she understands what she's agreed to, she'll have signed something." She paused. "You liked her. The scouts said you watched her for two days before you reported. That's not procedure. That's fascination."

"That's assessment," Renn said, and Sera laughed, briefly and without much kindness, and left him standing in the ash-falling hall with the painted sky coming down around him in grey snow.

* * *

He went, because he could not help it, to the guest wing.

He told himself it was procedure. He was the expedition's watcher; the captives were his responsibility in the way that a wound is a surgeon's responsibility; he should assess them, catalog them, learn their seams. He told himself this the whole length of the corridor, past gutter-lamps burning the precious rendered tallow that the kingdom could no longer spare, past windows of thick grey glass that showed the mountain's faint glow on the horizon like a coal under a blanket.

The scribe was at the window of her assigned room. Not sleeping, though it was deep in the kingdom's night. Not resting, though she had walked the Ember Road for six days. She was at the window with a scrap of charcoal — they had left her that much, or she had palmed it — writing on the back of her own hand, and when he knocked she did not startle, which told him she had heard his boots for twenty paces and decided they were not worth turning around for.

"You're the one from the ridgelines," she said. "The watcher. Renn." She said his name carefully, the way she'd said the covenant-script, foreign vowels handled like something that might break. "The guards talk. They talk about you the way you'd talk about a dog that bit someone important."

"They're not wrong," Renn said.

"Why are you here?"

"Because my regent wants the dragon saved," he said, "and her court wants the dragon harvested, and the man who leads the harvesters smiled at you tonight in a way that I have seen him smile at exactly three other things in my life. Two of them are dead. The third is the harvest engine."

Coral turned from the window. In the gutter-light her face was sharp and tired and very awake. "The harvest engine," she repeated. "You mean they've built a machine to take the fire."

"They've built four," Renn said. "They've never been allowed to use one. The regent has held them off for six years with procedure and veto and the simple fact that opening the dragon's heart-chamber requires blood no one in this kingdom has." He stopped. He heard his own words arrive in the room and sit there between them, enormous.

Coral looked down at her own hand — at the charcoal marks on the back of it, at the ink-stained whorls of her fingers — and Renn watched her arrive, ahead of him, at the thing he had come to warn her about. He watched it land. She didn't flinch. She just went very still, the way the ash-villages must have gone still, and her voice when it came was flat and scribe-calm and worse than screaming.

"The Temple didn't send an expedition to map a country," she said. "They sent a scribe who could read the lock."

"I don't know what the Temple sent," Renn said. "I know what my court will do. Corrinne will offer you hospitality, and honor, and a chair at every table, and he will not threaten you once. And when you've been here long enough to be tired, and grateful, and far from anyone who loves you, he will ask you — gently, as a friend, as a favor between the last two reasonable people in a mad court — to open a door." He held her gaze. "I came to tell you the shape of the trap before you're standing in it. That's all. That's the whole of why I'm here."

For a long moment the scribe said nothing. Outside the grey glass, the mountain glowed its faint, failing glow, the breath of a dying animal three centuries from the sky it remembered. Somewhere below, in the court, Renn could almost hear the sound of Corrinne's wine cup, never drinking, always offering.

"And you?" Coral said at last. "What do you want, watcher from the ridgelines? The regent's man. The exile. The dog that bit someone important."

Renn found that the honest answer was the only one he had left. Seven years of exile had burned the rest.

"I want the fire to still be burning when this court finishes eating itself," he said. "And I think you're the only person who's walked through that border in three hundred years who wants the same thing for reasons that aren't arithmetic."

Coral Blackwood looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned her hand over and showed him the charcoal marks on her palm — and Renn, who had been raised on the old script, who had been exiled partly *for* the old script, read them, and felt the floor of the world tilt.

They were not notes. They were not a map of the guest wing or an inventory of her guards.

They were the opening lines of the covenant. The wardstone's covenant, copied from memory, in her terrible accent of a hand — and beneath them, in smaller letters, a question she had written and underlined twice:

*What were the wards for?*

"I came to warn you about a trap," Renn said slowly.

"I know," said the scribe. "But I think you've got the trap backwards. I don't think your court is going to use me to open the dragon's heart." She closed her fist over the question, and her eyes were the eyes of someone who had spent eleven years counting other people's debts and had just found her name in the ledger.

"I think the dragon's heart is going to use me. And I think your regent knows it. And I think she's known it since the moment I said *covenant* in her hall — because she looked at me, just for a heartbeat, like she was looking at a ghost." Coral stepped closer to the grey glass, to the mountain's dying glow. "So here is my question for you, Renn of the ridgelines. When Lady Ashvael looked at me tonight — who did she see?"
