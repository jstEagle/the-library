## 15. The Scribe's Burden

The archive-crypts beneath the mountain smelled of cold stone and old ink, and Coral had never felt more at home or more afraid.

She had been given a lamp with a steady flame — one of the kingdom's slow-burning wicks that fed on something other than oil — and left alone among shelves of slate and vellum and stranger things: tablets of pressed ash, ribbons of beaten copper inscribed in scripts she half-recognized from her earliest lessons at the Temple. Three centuries of record-keeping. The hidden kingdom had kept books the way the drowning keep air.

She had come down to find the covenant's provenance. She had found instead the beginning of a list.

It was scratched into the back leaf of the ward-construction ledger, the same ledger she had spent the previous day translating with Renn's help — *the wards are raised, the doors are sealed, let no name of this place survive beyond the wall.* She had read those lines and felt her valley's foundations crack under her. But she had not turned the leaf. Ledgers ended; that was their nature. You did not look past the last entry because there was never anything past it.

There was something past it.

Eleven names, written in a different hand, cramped and hurried, as if the writer had feared interruption. Not debtors' names. Not masons' names. The heading above them, in the old formal script the Temple still used for oaths:

*Keepers of the Blood. To be watched. To be gathered.*

*To be unmade.*

Coral sat down on a shelf-edge built for someone taller than she was, and read the names again. And again. Because the fourth name down was *Blackwood*, and the ninth was *Jossery*, and the eleventh — written last, as if added later, the ink a slightly different age — was *Corvane*.

Her mother's name had been Corvane. Her mother's mother's name had been Blackwood. And the High Scribe of the Temple of Quiet Hours, the man whose signature she had copied ten thousand times until her wrist knew its loops better than her own, was born Corvel Corvane.

She did not cry. Scribes did not cry over documents; that was the first lesson, drilled into children before they learned their letters. A scribe's hands must be steady, because a trembling hand corrupts the record, and a corrupted record is worse than no record at all. So she sat with the lamp between her knees and her hands flat on her thighs, and she made herself into a steady instrument, and she read.

* * *

The list grew. That was the terrible thing — it was not one list but a practice, repeated across decades, across centuries, in hand after hand after hand. In the margin of a grain-tithe roll from two hundred years gone. On the inside cover of a hymnal, where no one would think to look for accounting. Scratched into the underside of a slate tablet whose face held nothing more dangerous than rainfall records.

Each time, the same heading. Each time, new names added, old names struck through — and Coral, who had spent eleven years learning what every kind of mark in a Temple ledger meant, knew exactly what the strikethroughs meant. They were not cancellations. They were completions.

A debtor whose debt is paid is struck through with a single line. A debtor who dies owing is struck through twice, crossed out like an error, so no future clerk wastes time pursuing them. Every struck name on these lists bore the double stroke.

They hadn't cancelled them. They had collected them.

And between the lists, in the margins, in the gaps where a careful hand had thought itself unobserved, the notes. *The Vell line breeds true — the girl touched the wall-stone and did not blister. Remove the mother. Watch the daughter.* *Keeper-blood surfaces in the mill families every third generation. Tithes will be adjusted accordingly.* *Do not burn the records; burn only the readers.*

Coral's own handwriting stared back at her from one of them — no. Not her handwriting. Her *teacher's*. Brother Halven, who had taught her to cut a quill, who had told her she had the finest eye for script he'd seen in forty years of service, who had burned a page in front of her in the scriptorium on a night she had never stopped wondering about. His note, here in the crypts of an enemy kingdom, dated the winter she turned nine:

*The Blackwood child shows the marks. Recommend early indenture. Debt structure attached. If the blood must persist, let it persist where we can count it.*

The page Halven had burned. She had spent years building theories around it — contraband, heresy, some scandal of the senior clergy. It had been a debt structure. *Hers.* Her indenture, her eleven years, the exact arithmetic of her life priced out and filed — and then burned, not to hide it from her, but because once she was bound, the document itself was dangerous. A signed record of why she'd been taken. The Temple burned its own paperwork when the paperwork was evidence.

She thought of Doss saying, festival night, wine-warm and laughing: *You transcribe faster than anyone in the valley, Coral. Faster than anyone I've ever seen. Don't you ever wonder why they wanted you so cheap?*

She had wondered. She had priced the wondering exactly what it cost and put it away, because wanting things was how you got hurt, and dutiful calm was free.

Now the bill had arrived.

* * *

She worked until the lamp guttered low, and she did not stop working, because work was the only shape she could give to the thing clawing through her chest. She copied. That was what she was; whatever else had been done to her, whatever lie she had been raised inside, she was a scribe, and a scribe's answer to horror was transcription. She copied the lists onto fresh vellum in her own clear hand — the keeper-blood lines, four hundred years of them, traced backward through millers and midwives and one famous poet whose suicide the Temple still commemorated annually with a feast of solemn joy.

Four hundred years. The wards had stood three centuries. The lists were older than the prison.

Which meant the hunt had begun before the walls went up. Which meant the founders of Vell had not merely built their cage and erased a kingdom — they had known, from the first night, that the cage would need keys, and that the keys would be people, and that people breed, and that bred-out keys must be found and folded back into the lockhouse before anyone remembered what they opened.

*To be watched. To be gathered. To be unmade.*

Except they hadn't been unmade, all of them. Some had been gathered instead — bound into the Temple's own ledgers, wrapped in debts, kept close enough to count. Coral turned the newest leaf and found the current generation's entries in ink barely faded: a stonemason's son in Vell, a ferryman's daughters, three children of the orchard families. And at the bottom, the freshest entry of all, dated the spring before the stars went out:

*Coral Blackwood. Confirmed. Marks present. Disposition pending expedition authorization.*

Disposition. As if she were surplus grain. As if she were a page awaiting a decision about whether to be copied or burned.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere far above, through a hundred yards of living rock, the dragon breathed — she felt it now, always, a vast slow tide in the stone, the heartbeat of the flame that kept this whole unraveling country stitched together. The flame her valley's wards had been bleeding dry for three centuries. The flame her blood was apparently built to touch.

She understood, suddenly and completely, why the High Scribe had voided her debt with such ceremony. Why he had rebound her within the hour, crown property, expedition scribe, refusal meaning debtor's prison. It had never been about needing someone to copy what the scouts saw. The expedition was a key-hunt. He had brought the last keeper-blood directly to the lock, the way you carry a lamp to a dark room — and he had dressed it as mercy, freedom revealed as a trap, and she had walked into it with her head high because the alternative was chains.

He had known what she was her whole life. Everyone had known except her.

* * *

Footsteps on the stair — the particular uneven tread she knew, boot-heel dragging faintly where an old scar pulled. Renn came down into the crypt carrying a second lamp and a cloth bundle that smelled, absurdly, of bread.

"You've been down here eight hours," he said. "Ashvael's steward offered to send someone after you. I said the scribe would come up when the scribe was finished." He set the bread on a shelf between a rainfall tablet and a book of mourning-songs. "I see I was right."

"Renn." Her voice came out level. Steady hands, steady voice. The training held even now, and she hated how well it held. "What do your people know about the founding families of Vell?"

He crouched beside the open ledger, and she watched him read the heading, and the list, and the strikethroughs. Watched him understand them. He was not a scribe, but he had lived three centuries of his kingdom's erasure, and grief gave him fluency.

"These are valley names," he said quietly.

"Keeper names. Descendants of the people who built the wards. My name is on the current page. My mother's name is on the struck pages — both strokes, Renn. Both strokes." She heard her own voice flatten further, go cold and clerical, and could not stop it. "Brother Halven wrote a note about me when I was nine. *Early indenture recommended.* There's a disposition pending on my file. The High Scribe's family name is Corvane. It's on the original list. Do you understand? The hunters are also the hunted. He's keeper-blood himself, and he's spent his whole life making sure no one else of the blood ever learns it."

Renn was silent for a long moment. Then: "That explains him better than anything I've heard yet. A man who burns the house rather than learn what he was born holding the key to." He looked up at her. "Coral. How long have you known you can touch the wardstones?"

"Since festival night. One heartbeat. Something answered."

"And it didn't burn you."

"It didn't burn me."

He exhaled slowly, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of a man handling something that might go off. "In my grandmother's generation there was a woman called Maren who could do that. Touch the outer stones without blistering. The court called her a threshold-child. There were songs about her. She was going to try to reach the star-doors, before—" He stopped. "Before the Silence took her. That's what we called the disappearances. Keeper-born, always keeper-born, and always just when they began to hear the stones."

"The Silence," Coral said. "Your word for it."

"Our oldest word for it." He met her eyes. "We thought it was a curse. We thought it was the wards themselves, reaching in to snuff anyone who got too close to the truth. But your lists say the Temple reached across the wall for four hundred years. Maybe the curse had administrators."

Coral looked down at her own fair copy — four centuries of hunted bloodlines in her neat, unblotched, prize-winning hand. Every skill she had, every discipline, every steadiness, had been cultivated in her like a crop. Not despite what she was. *Because* of it. They hadn't wanted to erase the keepers. They had wanted to farm them — penned, indebted, illiterate to their own nature, available.

And now the pen had brought her here. Past the wall. Into the crypts. To the very door the lists existed to keep shut.

"They're going to ask me to open something," she said. "Aren't they. That's what 'disposition pending' means. That's why he voided my debt personally. He needs keeper-blood, and I'm the last entry in his ledger."

Renn picked up the bread and put it in her hands, an ordinary kindness against an enormous dark. "Eat," he said. "Then come upstairs. Because if the Temple has spent four centuries hunting the people who can open the way out —" his jaw tightened — "then there *is* a way out. And I think you and I need to talk about what my people believe is behind the stars."

Above them, faint through the stone, the dying dragon breathed out, and somewhere in the dark of the archive-crypts, a copper ribbon on a high shelf — older than the lists, older than the wards — trembled in its case, as if something written on it had just begun, very softly, to wake.

Coral didn't see it move.

But she would.
