## 17. The Harvest Faction

Veyra Osten had commanded soldiers long enough to know the difference between a supply train and a threat approaching. The sound coming off the eastern ridgeline at dawn was neither.

It was bells.

Small ones, high and silver, hung from standards that caught the first grey light — a dozen of them, then dozens more, ringing in a rhythm she recognized from her childhood in the temple squares of Vell. The Hours. The Temple marched to the rhythm of its own liturgy even across dead country, and there was something obscene about it here, where the grass grew silver and the sky held no stars to tell the time by.

"Captain." Serel, her second, crouched beside her on the ridge they'd fortified two nights past. His spyglass was steady; his voice wasn't. "That's not a relief column."

"I can see that."

"It's got *pavilions*."

It did. Behind the ranks of white-cloaked strike troops came wagons under canvas, tall as houses, and folding towers of black lacquered wood that no military engineer would ever design — they were pulpits, or altars, or both. And at the center of it all, riding a pale mule because of course he rode a mule, came a slight figure in ink-dark robes whose hood was thrown back to show a face Veyra knew from a hundred official portraits and one unforgettable audience, eleven years ago, when she'd been a young officer foolish enough to ask why the border garrison rotated its best soldiers out every third season.

The High Scribe Corvel had answered her question with another question: *Why do you suppose a lockhouse needs guards, Captain?* She'd been reassigned within the month. She had never asked again.

Until now.

"How many?" she asked.

Serel counted under his breath. "Three hundred spears. Maybe sixty scribes-in-arms. The rest are... I don't have a word for the rest."

Veyra took the spyglass herself. The unnameable rest were bearers of equipment that made her stomach tighten with professional dread: great brass engines on wheeled frames, each one shaped like an enormous funnel narrowing to a point, their throats lined with mirrors, their bases crowded with kneeling acolytes who fed them coils of copper wire and vials of something that glowed faintly gold even at this distance.

Harvesting engines. She'd seen drawings once, in a sealed annex of the garrison library, filed under instruments of the old wars. She'd assumed they were mythological. The Temple was very good at making its myths look like history and its history look like myth.

"They crossed the wards," Serel said slowly. "Through the fallen border. In force. With *wagons*."

"Yes."

"The same wards that were supposed to be impassable for three centuries. The same wards we spent two weeks finding a way through, losing four men to things in the grass that I still can't describe in a report without laughing or crying."

"I'm aware of the arithmetic, Serel."

"So either the wards fell harder than we thought—"

"Or the Temple always had a key," Veyra said, "and simply never needed to use it until now." She lowered the spyglass. Her hands were calm. She made them be calm; that was most of command, in her experience — the discipline of your own body first, so your soldiers could borrow it. "Sound the camp. Everyone up. No one approaches those pavilions. No one signs anything, swears anything, eats anything, or kneels to anyone without my express order."

"And if the High Scribe himself—"

"Especially then."

* * *

Corvel did not summon her. That was the first move, and she noted it professionally: he made her come to him, and he made it easy, and he made it public. By mid-morning his advance guard had halted exactly one mile from her perimeter — close enough to be a statement, far enough to be a courtesy — and a single herald in white walked the intervening ground alone, unarmed, carrying a folded document sealed with the Quiet Hours' sigil: a candle flame inside an open eye.

The document requested Captain Veyra Osten's presence at the High Scribe's pavilion, "at her earliest convenience, as one servant of the valley to another."

As one servant to another. Not soldier to priest. Not crown authority to church authority. *Servant.* He was telling her he knew what she was — what they all were, out here past the wall where the old titles had begun to feel like costumes left over from a play nobody remembered staging.

She went, of course. Refusal would only sharpen the message. But she went on her own terms: full dress uniform, side sword, six of her soldiers in marching order behind her, and Serel holding her field orders — the secret ones, the ones issued by the Crown's Privy Office before the expedition departed — copied and hidden in three places Corvel's people would not think to search.

If this was to be a negotiation, she intended to arrive knowing which of her debts could be called.

The pavilion was larger inside than any tent had a right to be. The Temple's spatial craft was subtle, deniable, always half-hidden in embroidery and incense smoke, but Veyra had stood in enough consecrated halls to recognize the sensation of walking into a building that was pretending to be a cloth. Candles burned everywhere — real wax, real flame, imported from home at staggering cost, because out here under the starless sky the Temple apparently needed its symbol close at hand.

Corvel received her seated, at a plain writing desk, mid-page, as though she'd interrupted his correspondence rather than arrived at his invitation. He was smaller than his portraits. Grey-bearded, precise, with ink stains worked so deep into his fingers they looked like birthmarks. His eyes were the disappointing part: brown, mild, almost kind. She had expected fanaticism and found instead the thing she feared more — competence.

"Captain Osten." He set down his pen. "Eleven years since our last conversation. You never did answer my question about the lockhouse."

"I was reassigned before I could."

"A flaw in the system I have since corrected." He gestured to a chair. She remained standing. Something flickered behind the mild eyes — approval, she thought, horribly. "You've done remarkable work out here. Mapping the silver country. Establishing contact with the indigenous remnant." A pause, delicately calibrated. "Losing a scribe to them."

"Coral Blackwood is not lost. She is a guest under parley."

"She is a keeper-blood in the custody of people who have wanted one for three hundred years," Corvel said, in the tone of a man correcting a ledger entry. "But we will come to that."

Outside, distantly, the harvest engines ticked and settled in the morning heat, like insects the size of cathedrals.

"You crossed the wards," Veyra said. "In force. With equipment no one outside the Inner Conclave has clearance to know exists. So before anything else, High Scribe, I'll have your authority for this operation. In writing. Sealed."

"Of course." He drew a document from the desk — already prepared, already sealed, already dated — and held it out. She read it standing. It was flawless. Crown countersignature, Privy Office authorization, expedition charter amended and expanded. Every letter correct.

Every letter forged, or extracted under duress, or signed by a minister who had no idea what he was authorizing. She couldn't prove which. That was the artistry of it.

"You'll notice," Corvel said mildly, "that the charter confirms your command of all military elements of the combined operation. Yours, Captain. Field authority, subject only to doctrinal guidance from myself." He smiled, and it was almost warm. "I am told you have kept your people alive in country that kills the careless. I intend to keep them alive too. We want the same things."

"Do we."

"We want the flame secured. We want the kingdom beyond catalogued and contained. We want the valley safe." He rose, finally, and walked to the pavilion's opening, looking east toward the mountain with its faint inner glow. "Do you know what I felt, Captain, the night the stars went out? Everyone else in Vell felt terror. I felt vindication. Because I had spent forty years reading the founding archives of this Temple — the *real* archives, not the ones we teach novices — and I knew the stars going out meant the wards had finished their work. Or failed at it. Either way, the age of pretending was over."

He turned back to her.

"Magic returned to the world, Captain. It is pooling in the grass out there like floodwater. It is in your soldiers' blood right now — you've seen what happens to the sensitive ones. Untrained, uncaged, undirected power, spreading across a continent that has forgotten how to fear it. Three centuries ago our founders built a dam against exactly this. The dam has broken. The question before us is whether we rebuild it—" he tapped the charter in her hand, "—or whether we build something better. A controlled channel. An engine. A *harvest*."

"The dragon," Veyra said quietly. "You mean the dying dragon whose breath holds that kingdom together."

"I mean the largest reservoir of living fire on this earth, currently leaking away into the dark while squabbling factions fight over the dregs. Yes. The dragon." He said the word without reverence and without cruelty, which was somehow worse than either. "It is dying regardless, Captain. Nothing you or I do changes that. The only question is whether its death feeds a weapon that protects ten million people for a thousand years, or whether it feeds nothing at all except sentiment."

* * *

He offered her tea after that. She accepted, because refusing small courtesies was its own kind of information, and because her hands wanted something to hold while her mind ran the numbers.

The temptation, when it came, did not come dressed as temptation. It came dressed as a promotion.

"There will need to be a new order," Corvel said, over the steam. "Once the flame is secured. Something between garrison and clergy — soldiers trained in doctrine, scribes trained in arms. Wardens, we're calling them provisionally. Commanded by someone with field credibility, crown connections, and — forgive me — a documented history of asking inconvenient questions. It makes the appointment credible. Reform-minded officers rally to it. The old families can't call it a purge if the commander is famous for her skepticism." He sipped his tea. "You would answer to me directly. You would take the rank of First Warden. Your soldiers would be guaranteed land grants in the recovered territories. Your dead — the four men the silver country took — would receive Founder's Honors, retroactive, full rites. Their families would never want again."

The names surfaced unbidden. Hask. The twins, Brannet and Brannet's brother — no, Marta and Ilse Brannet. Young Ferrow, nineteen, who'd screamed at things in the grass that left no tracks. Four families in Vell who'd received her letters and nothing else.

Founder's Honors. Full rites. Never want again.

And beneath it, threaded through all of it like wire through a candle, the other offer, the one he hadn't spoken yet: stand aside at the heart-chamber, keep your soldiers out of the final operation, and Coral Blackwood becomes a footnote instead of a casualty. He hadn't said it. He didn't need to. The whole architecture of the conversation pointed at it like a compass needle.

"You've thought about my price carefully," Veyra said.

"I think about everyone's price carefully. It's the only honest way to negotiate. People pretend otherwise and everyone wastes time being offended." He set down his cup. "What I cannot determine, Captain, is whether you have one."

"Everyone has a price."

"Yes. But yours may not be purchasable." He studied her with genuine, unhurried curiosity. "Some people's price is paid *to* them. Others have to be paid *for* them — someone else takes the cost. You strike me as the second kind. Which makes you dangerous in negotiations and invaluable in a war." He folded his hands. "So let me pay for you instead. Consider what happens without us. The Harvest faction of Ashvael's court wants the flame for weapons — crude, immediate, kingdom-scale weapons. The regent wants to preserve a dying god out of guilt. The exile faction wants to burn the whole covenant and flee through doors that may not exist. And somewhere in the middle of it walks your missing scribe, holding a bloodline that opens the dragon's heart like a key opens a lock. If we do nothing, Captain, one of those factions wins, and none of them will care about the valley. The valley that fed you, uniformed you, buried your father with honors—"

"My father was a tanner," Veyra said. "The Temple charged him burial fees he paid off over nine years. I checked the ledger. It was my first lesson in arithmetic."

Something moved behind the mild eyes — not anger. Recalculation.

"Then consider it as a soldier," Corvel said, softer now, and this was his true voice, she realized, the sermon under the paperwork. "Consider the engines. I have sixty of them. Each one, fed properly, could hold a city's worth of caged light for a generation. With the dragon-fire, we don't rebuild the old wards — walls around one valley, starving one kingdom. We build a lattice. Regulated magic, distributed, *owned*, accountable to institutions rather than accidents of birth. No more keeper-bloods deciding the fate of nations by existing. No more children born into power they never chose." His voice dropped. "I watched what the wild return did to your scout Ferrow, Captain. I read your reports. Power without structure is just violence with better lighting. Someone must hold the pen."

And there it was — the coherent creed, laid bare, almost beautiful in its terrible completeness. He wasn't a hypocrite. He wasn't cruel. He believed every word, and belief like that didn't bend, and armies full of good people would follow it gladly because it promised safety with a straight face.

That was what frightened her. Not the engines. The *reasonableness*.

"I'll need time," she said.

"You have until we reach the mountain. Three days, perhaps four." He inclined his head, gracious in apparent victory. "Think of it not as choosing a side, Captain. Think of it as choosing which adults hold the matches."

* * *

She walked back to her lines alone, having dismissed her escort at the pavilion's edge, needing the mile of silver grass to herself.

The sun — the false, dim, ward-shadow sun that had replaced the real one — sat low and wrong on the horizon. Above it, where stars should have been wheeling toward evening, there was nothing. Just black. She had stopped looking up weeks ago; now she forced herself to look, because Corvel had looked at the sky like a man looking at a broken tool he intended to repair, and she wanted to know what *she* saw.

Emptiness. Enormous, patient emptiness, like a page waiting for ink.

*Which adults hold the matches.*

She thought of Coral Blackwood — twenty-two years old, indentured for a debt incurred by a dead father's medical bills, who transcribed faster than any scribe in the valley and touched a wardstone and heard it answer. Who had walked into a parley with armed strangers armed with nothing but their own dead language read aloud. Whose price, Veyra suspected, was the one Corvel genuinely couldn't buy, because it wasn't hers: it was everyone else's freedom, and she kept paying it forward.

She thought of Ferrow's mother receiving a Founder's Honor instead of a son.

At her tent, Serel waited with the day's dispatches and a question in his face he was disciplined enough not to ask.

"He offered me command of a new order," Veyra said. "First Warden of the Harvest. Land grants, honors for our dead, direct line to the Inner Conclave."

Serel whistled softly. "And?"

"And I said I'd need time." She ducked into the tent, pulled her field desk's drawer, and took out the copy of her true orders — the Privy Office charter, find what killed the stars, trust no Temple authority above captain rank. She weighed it in her hand. Then she took out a blank sheet of expedition paper and began, in careful block letters, writing a different document entirely: a private tally. *Item. The Temple possessed passage through the wards and concealed it. Item. The harvest engines predate the emergency. Item. Corvel seeks keeper-blood Blackwood specifically, by name, prior to any confirmed intelligence regarding her role.*

"What are you writing, Captain?"

"My resignation," Veyra said, "from the version of this army that obeys him." She blew on the ink. "Get me a runner who knows the ash-tunnels. Tonight, before the pickets change. There's a parley-camp under the mountain, and a scribe there who needs to know that the man hunting her just brought sixty engines to her doorstep — and that her captain's price, it turns out, isn't for sale."

Serel stared. "You're defecting."

"No." She sealed the letter with her personal signet, pressing hard enough to bite her thumb. "Defection implies I was ever on his side. I'm doing my job. The charter says find what killed the stars." She looked up, and for the first time in eleven years, Theo Joubert's old question — *why does a lockhouse need guards?* — finally had an answer she could carry.

"It didn't fall, Serel. It was spent. And the people who spent it just marched sixty machines across the grave to strip the corpse." She handed him the letter. "Move."
