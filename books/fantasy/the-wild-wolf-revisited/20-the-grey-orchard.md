## 20. The Grey Orchard

**Coral**

The orchard had been a garden once.

She could see it in the geometry — rows planted with a surveyor's love, each tree the same careful distance from its neighbor, the ground between them still scored with irrigation channels that had run silver once and now ran nowhere. But every tree was stone. Not dead: stone. Trunks of grey granite grained like wood, branches spread in attitudes of fruiting, and here and there, caught mid-curve on a bough, the suggestion of apples — round bulges in the rock that her fingers kept wanting to read as flesh.

Renn called it the Grey Orchard. He said it the way other people said the names of saints.

"They were real trees," he said, walking ahead of her with the loose, silent stride he used when he wasn't trying to be quiet and couldn't help it. "My grandmother's grandmother picked fruit from them. Then the wards went up, and everything inside the kingdom started running out of time. The spellwork needed feeding. Trees were the first thing they let go."

"Let go," Coral repeated. "You make it sound gentle."

"It wasn't." He stopped beside one of the stone trees and laid his palm flat against the trunk, an old gesture, worn smooth by repetition. "There's a story that the last man to tend this orchard stood here while his trees turned. Wouldn't leave them. They say you can still find him if you look."

Coral looked. The light through the stone canopy came down pale and grainy, like light through dirty ice, and every shadow between the trunks had the shape of a waiting person. She decided not to look harder.

They had been running for two days. Through the ash-tunnels under the archive-crypts, up through a collapsed cistern, out into country she had no maps for — because there were no maps, because the kingdom had spent three centuries being erased from memory by the very institution that had raised her. Renn knew the way the way water knows downhill. He'd been exiled from these lands, but exile, it turned out, was just another kind of knowing.

Now they were hiding in a petrified garden, eating hard bread and dried fish from his pack, waiting for the search parties to give up or find them.

"You can sleep," Renn said. "I'll watch."

"I know how to keep watch. I did it for eleven years." She bit the bread. It tasted of ash, or maybe everything tasted of ash now. "Sitting still. Copying what I was told to copy. Wanting nothing loudly enough that nobody noticed I wanted anything at all."

He sat down across from her, back against the stone trunk, knees up. In the grey light his face lost its wariness for a moment and became simply young. She kept forgetting how young he was. He carried himself like a man who'd been old for a long time.

"Tell me about the ledgers," he said.

* * *

**Renn**

He asked because he wanted to hear her talk. That was the whole truth of it, and he was surprised at himself for it. Renn had spent six years perfecting the art of wanting nothing loudly enough that nobody noticed — apparently it was contagious.

But she talked.

"There was one ledger for debts under fifty marks and another for debts over," she said. "The small ledger was written in brown ink, the large in black. If your debt was transferred — sold to another creditor, or forgiven as charity, which almost never happened — the entry got a red line through it, but never struck out entirely. A struck-out debt is a finished story. A lined-through debt is a story someone might reopen." She turned her hands over, palms up, and he saw the calluses there, the writer's bumps on her middle fingers, the faint ink stain in the crease of her right thumb that no amount of scrubbing would ever lift. "I wrote ten thousand entries. Maybe more. Names, sums, dates, terms. And every name was a person who owed the Temple something, and most of them didn't even know what the original thing was. Their grandmothers borrowed a roof beam in a bad winter and the family was paying interest on it three generations later."

"That's not debt," Renn said. "That's a leash."

"That's what I thought. Quietly. At night." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "In Vell we had a word for thoughts like that. We pronounced it nothing."

He laughed before he could stop himself — a short, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. She looked pleased. She had a face that showed pleasure honestly, he was learning. No court training in her, none of Ashvael's people's beautiful guardedness. Every feeling arrived at her eyes without stopping at the border.

"And you?" she said. "Your turn. You said you questioned the regent. Nobody gets exiled for one question."

"No," he agreed. "It took me four years of questions."

* * *

**Coral**

He told it plainly, which somehow made it worse.

He had been a warden-trainee of the Third Vigil — the order that maintained the kingdom's inner wards, the lesser spells that kept the hidden country stitched together while the dragon-fire did the great work underneath. His family had served the Flame for nine generations. His father had held a wardstone in his own two hands and been proud of it.

"The first question was small," Renn said. "I asked why the vigil rotations went deeper than they needed to. Why wardens were stationed in the archive-crypts where there was nothing to guard but paper. My instructor told me the regent's business was the regent's business. Fine. I was sixteen. I swallowed it."

"But?"

"But then my sister got sick. The wasting sickness — the one that comes from living too close to spent spellwork. The healers' guild has a treatment. Costs forty marks." He said the number flatly, and Coral felt it land in her chest like a coin dropped down a well, because forty marks was a small-ledger debt, a brown-ink debt, the kind of sum that grew grandmothers' roof beams into lifetimes. "I petitioned the guild. Denied. I petitioned the regent's office. Denied. So I went into the crypts I'd been guarding, and I read what was on the paper."

"What was on the paper?"

"Accounts." His jaw tightened. "Every year, the kingdom pays a tithe to the Flame — goods, labor, bloodlines. That's what everyone's taught. The Flame sustains us; we sustain the Flame. Sacred exchange. Except the accounts showed the tithe going *out* and almost nothing coming back. The dragon was dying, Coral. Had been dying for decades. And the regent's office knew, and the guild knew, and instead of telling people — instead of letting the kingdom prepare, or grieve, or *choose* — they buried it. Because a kingdom that knows its god is dying stops tithing. Stops obeying. And a kingdom that stops obeying unravels faster than a kingdom that loses its fire. That was their arithmetic."

Coral thought of the brown ledger and the black ledger. Of red lines through debts that were never finished. "So you told people."

"I told the Vigil. My own order." He laughed, and there was no humor in it. "Nine generations of my family in the Flame's service, and when I stood up in the vigil-hall and read the accounts aloud, do you know what they did? They didn't argue. They didn't deny it. My own father stood up and said I was sick. Spell-sick. Touch-drunk. They said the crypts had gotten into my head." He looked at his hands. "My sister died in the spring. I was exiled in the summer. And the worst part — the part I've never said out loud — is that for about a year afterward, I believed them. I lay awake in exile camps wondering if maybe I *was* touch-drunk. Wondering if I'd imagined the numbers."

"You didn't imagine them," Coral said quietly. "I've seen the covenant. I've seen what the Temple built on the other side of that wall. You were the only sane man in a room full of accountants."

He looked up at her then, and something passed between them that had no name in either of their languages — the recognition of one bookkeeper of grief reading another's columns and finding them true.

* * *

**Renn**

Night came strangely in the Grey Orchard. No stars — there were no stars anywhere anymore, that was the whole wound of the world — but the stone canopy held the day's pale light a long time, releasing it slowly, so that dusk lasted hours and the darkness, when it finally settled, was soft rather than total.

They made a nest of blankets against the widest trunk. Renn took first watch out of habit, sitting with his back to the stone, knife across his knees, listening to the expedition's distant fires and the nearer silence of the orchard.

"Coral," she said, from the blankets. Not asleep after all.

"Yes."

"Why did you come back? To the kingdom. You were out. Exile is terrible, but it's *out*. You could have gone over the mountains, gone anywhere the Temple's writ doesn't run. Instead you spent six years shadowing the borders, watching for us, waiting for the wards to fall. Why?"

He considered lying, found he didn't want to, and was startled by that too. Two surprises in one night. The orchard was doing something to him.

"Because of the way my father looked at me," he said. "In the vigil-hall. When he called me sick. He wasn't angry. He was *afraid*. Afraid for me, afraid of me, afraid of the room. And I realized he'd probably known about the accounts for twenty years and had done the same arithmetic the regent does — silence now, grief later, obedience always. And I thought: if I ever get free of this place, I'm not going to be the man who runs and stays free. I'm going to be the man who comes back with proof." He turned the knife over. "Then the stars went out, and the wards fell, and a Temple expedition walked through the gap with a scribe who touched a wardstone and didn't burn. And I thought: there's my proof, walking around in a valley girl's boots."

"I'm not proof," Coral said. "I'm a debtor with good handwriting."

"You're the first person in three hundred years the kingdom's stones have answered." He glanced back at her. In the last of the orchard-light her face was half-lit, half-not, and her eyes had the particular steadiness he'd noticed in the parley tent — the steadiness of someone who has counted the cost of things all her life and refuses to flinch from counting this one. "That's not nothing. That might be everything."

She was quiet a moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed — smaller, more careful.

"Can I tell you the worst thing? The thing I haven't said out loud either?"

"Yes."

"When the High Scribe voided my indenture — when he stood there in his grey robes and told me I was free — for about half a heartbeat, I was happy. Before I understood it was a trap. Just half a heartbeat of *free*, like air after being underwater." Her breath caught. "And I've been ashamed of it ever since. That some part of me was grateful to him. That the cage was so familiar that even the man holding the keys frightened me less than the open door."

Renn set the knife down. He crossed the space between them and sat on the edge of her blanket-nest, close enough that she could have pulled away and didn't.

"My sister," he said, "spent her last month asking me to promise she wouldn't be forgotten. Not mourned — *remembered*. There's a difference. Mourning is for the people left. Remembering is for the one who goes." He reached out, slowly, giving her time, and tucked a strand of hair behind her ear — hair that had gone rough with ash and travel, hair that smelled of candle smoke and ink. "You're not grateful to him. You're grateful to the half-second. To finding out what freedom feels like so you'd recognize it when you fight for the real thing. That's not shame. That's intelligence."

Coral made a sound that was half laugh, half something else, and leaned her forehead against his shoulder. Just that. Just weight and warmth and the astonishing simplicity of it. Renn sat very still, the way you sit when a wild thing lands on you and you want it to stay.

"I don't know how to do this," she murmured. "Any of this. Fugitive. Believer. Whatever I am."

"Neither do I. I know ledgers and I know exile, and between the two of us—"

"—we might manage one whole person," she finished.

He smiled into the dark. Above them the stone branches held their stone fruit, patient as patience itself, and somewhere far below the mountain breathed its guttering fire, and somewhere beyond the fallen wall the Temple's hunters moved through the dark with lanterns and lists of names. None of it, for one hour, mattered.

* * *

**Coral**

She woke before dawn to the feel of his hand in hers — when had that happened, sometime in the night, her hand had simply found his the way a reader's finger finds its place — and to a sound she couldn't place.

Not pursuit. Not the crack of a patrol or the whine of spent spellwork. Something softer. A kind of ticking, irregular, coming from everywhere and nowhere among the stone trees.

Renn was already awake, listening. His body had gone taut beside hers, but his brow was furrowed with confusion rather than fear.

"Do you hear that?" she whispered.

"Yes." He stood, drawing her up with him, and together they moved between the granite trunks toward the sound. The pre-dawn greyness was thickening, gathering, and Coral realized with a lurch that it wasn't dawn at all — the light was coming from the trees themselves. Faint veins of warmth were waking in the stone, amber threads glowing along the old wood-grain, pulsing gently, in and out, like breath.

Like a heartbeat.

The ticking resolved itself as they walked: sap. Three hundred years of frozen sap, deep in the petrified hearts of the trees, remembering what it was. One tree near the orchard's center had gone further than the rest — a seam had split along its trunk, and through the crack glowed a color Coral had seen only once before, coiled in the throat of a dying dragon far beneath the mountain.

Ember-orange. Living fire.

"The flame's reaching out," Renn breathed. "It's never done this. The trees have been stone since my great-great-grandmother was a child." He turned to her, and the growing light moved across his face, and she saw in it the same vertigo she felt — hope arriving too fast to be trusted. "Coral. The dragon is dying, and it's spending its last strength on an *orchard*."

"Why?" she whispered. "Why these trees? Why now?"

Renn stared at the cracked trunk, and she watched the answer arrive in his face like cold water.

"Because it's not reaching out," he said slowly. "It's reaching *back*. These were the first trees the wards consumed. The oldest debt the kingdom owes." He looked at her. "Something's calling in its debts, Coral. And I don't think it's the dragon."

Far off across the grey fields, toward the mountain, a bell began to ring — the alarm-bell of the Court of Cinders, deep and urgent, rolling over the sleeping land like a stone dropped into a well.
