## 24. Betrayal at the Glass Bridge

Renn had walked the Ember Road a hundred times before the wards fell, back when the kingdom still had a name worth speaking and the mountain still had light enough to cast his shadow long across the glass. He knew every seam in it — where the road narrowed over the ravine called the Throat, where heat-vents breathed through fissures and made the whole span shimmer like a living thing, where the old watch-post of Kessrin stood hollowed and half-melted at the far end.

He also knew that anyone who wanted to stop them would choose this stretch of it.

That was why he'd argued for the ash-tunnels all the way to the mountain's root. That was why Coral, limping slightly, ink-stained to the elbow, had looked at him with that level scribe's patience and said, "The tunnels add two days. Your wound adds one. The harvest won't wait for our arithmetic."

She was right. She was usually right, which he found both admirable and deeply irritating.

And so they were on the bridge at dusk, four of them: Renn in front with a borrowed spear, Coral behind him with her ledger strapped across her chest like armor, and behind her two of Ashvael's cinder-sworn, Brakk and Ilen, who had volunteered for the crossing with the flat fatalism of soldiers who no longer expected to be thanked.

The glass sang under their boots. It always did — a low harmonic note, the sound of fused sand remembering fire. Renn had forgotten how much he hated that song. It sounded too much like a bell tolling for something.

"Tell me about Kessrin," Coral said quietly. She did this when the silence got heavy. She filled it with questions the way other people filled it with prayer.

"Watch-post. Twelve soldiers, once." He kept his eyes on the ridgeline ahead. "I trained there when I was sixteen. My commander was a woman named Sorrel Vane. She could split a falling leaf with a sling-stone and she taught me that a sentry's first weapon is boredom — you win by refusing to be surprised."

"What happened to her?"

"She questioned the regent's quarantine policy during the last famine year. Politely. In writing." His jaw tightened. "They gave her a choice: exile into the dead country or silence in the archive-crypts. She chose exile. Walked out past the wards knowing what waited out there." He paused. "Or so we were told. Lately I've begun to wonder what *told* is worth."

Coral said nothing to that, but he felt her glance settle on his back like a warm hand.

They were halfway across the Throat when the vents went quiet.

Renn stopped so abruptly that Brakk nearly walked into his spear. The heat-vents along the bridge exhaled on a rhythm, slow as sleeping lungs — he'd been counting it without realizing, the way you count your own heartbeat. Now the rhythm stuttered. Stopped.

The glass beneath them cooled by degrees. He felt it through his boots, the song flattening toward silence.

"Somebody's dammed the vents," Ilen said. Her voice stayed soldier-flat, but her hand found her knife.

"No," Renn said. His mouth had gone dry. "Somebody's *drinking* them."

Harvest-craft. He'd only seen it demonstrated once, years ago, by a court engineer who'd been stripped of rank for even sketching it: sigil-lattices that didn't block flame but fed on it, drawing heat up out of stone the way a leech draws blood. The Temple hadn't invented that. The Temple had *bought* it. Bought it from people inside the walls.

From people he'd grown up with.

"Off the span," he said. "Now. Quietly—"

The first sling-stone came out of the dusk and took Ilen through the throat.

* * *

There was no time for grief. There was barely time for motion. Renn threw himself sideways as the second stone whispered past his ear close enough to part his hair, and then the ridge above the far arch bloomed with movement — six figures, seven, dropping from cover among the melted ruins of Kessrin with the unhurried confidence of people who had chosen this ground weeks ago.

He knew their silhouettes. That was the worst of it. Even at distance, even in dying light, he knew them.

"Drenn," he breathed. "Maret. That's Sorrel's old squad formation — staggered left, stones high, blades low. They trained under her."

"They're not her," Coral said. Her voice was steady, but he heard the effort holding it steady, the way he imagined she held her pen against a trembling hand. "Renn. They are not her."

But they moved like her. They came down onto the bridge in the exact sequence Sorrel Vane had drilled into a generation of teenagers on a windswept parapet, and Renn felt something in his chest tear along an old scar as he set his feet and raised his spear.

Brakk charged. Brakk was twenty-two and grieved loudly and immediately, and Drenn met him in the middle of the span with a blade that drank the vent-light. The two of them went down together, locked, rolling toward the edge, and Renn couldn't help — couldn't split himself, couldn't be two places—

"Coral. *Run.*"

"I'm not leaving—"

"*Run!*"

He didn't wait to see if she obeyed. He couldn't afford to. Maret came at him with the low line, blade rising, and Renn turned it with the spear haft and felt the shock of it climb his arms. Maret had been the gentlest of them, once — the one who'd shared his rations on the winter marches, the one who'd taught him to whistle through cupped hands so the ridge-wolves answered. Now his face above the blade was a stranger's calm.

"You serve the Silence now?" Renn managed, between blows. "Is that what they told you? That the flame has to die so the valley stays asleep?"

"I serve the truth," Maret said, and there was nothing in his voice at all, and somehow that was more frightening than hate. "The dragon is dying anyway, Renn. The court has spent three centuries feeding it our dead and calling it mercy. The Harvest just stops pretending. Corvel's engineers can hold the flame in vessels. We can carry it out. We don't have to burn with it."

"And the door?"

"The door needs a keeper. One life, forever, in a threshold." Maret's blade flickered; Renn caught it a hair late and felt steel kiss his ribs, shallow, burning. "You've seen what the regent asks. You ran from it once. Why do you think we didn't?"

Because I ran from a lie, Renn thought, and parried, and gave ground, and gave more ground, because giving ground was the point — every step backward was a step Coral gained toward the far arch, toward the tunnels, away from the sling-lines. Behind him Brakk and Drenn broke apart, both bleeding, both staggering; Brakk dragged Drenn's blade-hand down and headbutted him, once, twice, brutal and graceless, and Drenn went to one knee and did not rise quickly.

Seven attackers. Two down, maybe three. Four still coming along the span, and the dusk deepening, and somewhere above the ridge a fifth sling-stone sang and shattered on the glass a hand's width from Renn's boot — deliberately wide. A warning shot. Or a herding shot.

Herding. Of course. They weren't trying to kill him. They were trying to *funnel* him, press him and Coral toward the middle of the Throat where the vents ran hottest, where a harvest-lattice could be waiting under the glass like a snare under leaves. Corvel hadn't sent killers. He'd sent accountants with knives. Everything balanced. Everything collected.

"Renn!" Coral's voice, from behind and above — she'd reached the arch, and she'd stopped there, the fool, the brave impossible fool, and she was doing something with her hands, unstrapping the ledger, tearing a page free. "The vents! The rhythm — it's a meter! It counts!"

He understood a half-second too late what she meant, and a half-second was everything, because Maret chose that heartbeat to commit, blade driving for Renn's chest with none of Sorrel's artistry and all of desperation's speed.

Renn didn't parry. Parrying would have held him in place, and holding him in place was exactly what the trap required. Instead he stepped *in* — inside the blade, inside Maret's reach, close enough to smell the harvest-oil on his old friend's collar — and let the steel take him through the meat of his shoulder rather than his heart.

The pain arrived white and total. The world went briefly to a single bright line.

Then his forehead was against Maret's collarbone, his good arm locked around the man's back, and he said, very quietly, into his ear, in the old cadence of the winter marches: "*Whistle low, brother. The wolves are listening.*"

Maret went rigid. For one suspended instant the whole ambush hung on that phrase — a boyhood thing, a nothing thing, a thing with no tactical value whatsoever except that it belonged to a version of them both that Corvel's engineers had never been able to requisition.

Maret's blade withdrew. Not gently. But it withdrew.

"The lattice," Maret said, hoarse, not stepping back, keeping their bodies between the words and the watchers above. "Center span. Under the third vent-seam. When the vents resume, it ignites. Get off the glass."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because Sorrel Vane is alive," Maret said, "and she's been asking the same question about us that you're asking about me. And because someone has to survive this to tell her why you wouldn't shut up about ledgers." His hands pushed, hard, shoving Renn back toward the arch — toward Coral — making it look, to the ridge, like a kill-blow's follow-through. "*Go.*"

Renn went. He went the way the wounded go — badly, lurching, his shoulder streaming, the spear abandoned somewhere behind him on the singing glass. Brakk caught him under the good arm at the arch and hauled, and Coral was there, her hands pressing cloth against his shoulder with a scribe's terrible precision, folding the wound closed like a page being mended.

"Third vent-seam," he gritted. "Center span. It'll fire when the vents—"

Even as he said it, the vents breathed again. The glass resumed its song. And down the length of the Throat, under their feet, a lattice of sigils kindled blue-white through the translucent span — beautiful, Renn thought distantly, even now, even bleeding, it was beautiful, the way all traps built by clever people were beautiful — and began to drink.

The heat went out of the world. The song climbed toward a shriek.

"Move," Coral said, and put her shoulder under his arm without asking permission, and the three of them ran — Renn stumbling, half-carried, his blood spotting the glass in small dark commas — as the center span behind them lit from below like a lamp, like a star, like everything the night sky refused to be anymore.

* * *

The ash-tunnel mouth swallowed them, and the shriek of the lattice faded to a memory, and then to a rumor, and then Renn allowed himself to fall against the tunnel wall and become, for a while, mostly pain.

Coral worked by feel in the dark. She'd learned triage-sigils from a field-medic in the expedition, back when the world was simpler — back when the enemy had merely been the Temple. He felt her fingers tracing the signs along his shoulder, cool and deliberate, and heard her murmuring the words under her breath with the careful diction of someone copying a text she didn't fully trust herself to translate.

"That's keeper-script," he said, through his teeth. "Where did you learn—"

"The medic stole it from an archive she shouldn't have touched. Same as everyone steals everything in this story." Her hands didn't pause. "Hold still. This one closes. The next one hurts."

"The next one always hurts."

It did. He bit down on the strap of his gear and rode it out, and when it passed he found her sitting back on her heels in the dark, breathing hard, her silhouette shaking faintly.

"How bad?" he asked.

"You'll keep the arm." A pause. "Renn — Maret. He had you. Twice. Why did he let you go?"

"He didn't let me go. He made a different calculation than the one Corvel paid for." Renn leaned his head back against the ash-wall. It was warm here, deep in the kingdom's bones, warmer than the surface had been in three centuries. "Sorrel Vane is alive, Coral. In the Harvest camps, or near them. Maret thinks I'm the one who can reach her."

"Can you?"

"I don't know. I haven't seen her since I was sixteen." He laughed, and it hurt, so he stopped. "I used to believe loyalty was a wall. You stand on yours, they stand on theirs, and the wall holds because everyone agrees to hold their own piece. Tonight I watched men I loved hold their piece of Corvel's wall. And I watched one of them leave a gap in it shaped exactly like my body." He turned his head toward her, though he couldn't see her. "Loyalty isn't a wall. It's a door somebody chooses not to close."

Coral was quiet a long moment. Then, in the dark, her hand found his uninjured one and held it — briefly, firmly, the grip of a woman confirming an entry in a ledger.

"Then we'd better find Sorrel Vane," she said, "before the Harvest closes every gap they have."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of ash and stone, the mountain groaned — a long tectonic syllable, the dragon shifting in its sleep, or turning toward its dreams. And far up the tunnel, where the dark thinned toward ember-light, someone was waiting. Renn saw the shape of a lantern before he saw the person carrying it, and the person before he believed them.

A woman, broad-shouldered, grey at the temples, wearing the faded insignia of a watch-post that had burned down three hundred years before she was born.

"Well," said Sorrel Vane. "You always did bleed expensively, Renn. Now get up. We have very little time, and I suspect you're going to want to argue with everything I say."
