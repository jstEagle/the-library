## 25. Triage by Candlelight

The ash-tunnel swallowed sound. That was the first thing Coral noticed after the running stopped — the silence of it, thick as wool, broken only by Renn's breathing.

It was the breathing that frightened her. Not the wound itself, though the wound was bad enough: a harvester's blade had opened him along the ribs, a long diagonal tear that soaked his shirt to a black weight against her hands. She'd pressed cloth to it through three turnings of the tunnel and it kept coming away red, then darker red, then a red so deep it looked like ink. But blood she could understand. Blood was arithmetic. What frightened her was the rhythm underneath — too fast, then catching, then too slow, like a scribe copying a line and losing count halfway through.

"Renn." She said his name the way you tap a ledger to settle the columns. "Stay with me. Talk. Tell me something tedious."

A wet laugh. "You want tedious. I can give you tedious. The regent's court has a ceremony for the changing of the lamp-oil in the underhalls. Four hours. There are verses."

"There are *verses*."

"Hymns to combustion." His voice thinned on the last word. In the grey light of her candle-shield he looked younger than the ridge-lines where she'd first seen him watching, younger than the man who'd taken a blade meant for her spine. "My grandmother sang them. She said fire remembers being worshipped better than being used."

"Save your breath for something useful."

"This *is* useful. If I stop talking—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

Coral got her shoulder under his arm and walked them both deeper into the tunnel. Behind them, faint as a memory of sound, came the clash from the glass bridge — Renn's old comrades, Harvest loyalists now, hunting them through the dark country of their own kingdom. The ambush had been quick and professional; only Renn's reflexes and Renn's body had stood between Coral and the blade, and both were now spent currency.

She needed somewhere to stop. Stopping was death, probably. But moving was also death, more slowly, and slow deaths could be argued with. Scribes learned early that every crisis is negotiable if you get the terms down before panic does the writing.

There — an alcove where the tunnel widened, where old brackets still held stubs of tallow that smelled, when she lit one, of centuries. Someone had sheltered here before. Scratches on the wall: tallies. A counting-house of the desperate.

She lowered Renn against the stone.

* * *

In the scriptorium at Vell, they taught you that healing sigils were above your station. Second-year students copied them into practice books, but always from copies, always with the *authority lines* left blank — the little clause-marks at the base of each glyph that gave the written thing permission to be true. A healing sigil without authority lines was like a debt record without a signature. Ink pretending to be law.

Coral had copied four hundred of them. She had never once written one that worked.

But she had seen things since the wards fell. She had read spellwork pooled in silver grass like standing water. She had read a village of ash-statues mid-flight, and understood the sentence that killed them. And she had stood in front of a dying dragon and felt its breath read *her* — felt the oldest text in the world take her measure and find her legible.

Scribe-craft wasn't the Temple's invention. The Temple had only ever been its censor. Out here, past the wall, in the country where magic pooled and moved, the authority lines weren't granted by rank. They were granted by need. By intent. By — she remembered Renn saying it once, half-mocking, half-reverent — *by what you're willing to pay.*

She knelt in the alcove and pulled off her coat, folded it under Renn's head, and peeled back the ruined shirt with hands that shook until she ordered them not to.

The cut ran from just below his arm down across the ribs. Deep enough that she could see the layered truth of him: muscle, membrane, the pale architecture beneath. Not organ-deep. Not yet. The bleeding had slowed, which was either good or very bad, and his skin had gone the grey-white of paper left too long in rain.

"Coral." His eyes found hers, unfocused but finding her anyway. "You have that look."

"What look?"

"The ledger look. You're about to enter something into an account." A ghost of his crooked smile. "I've watched you do it. Everything gets a column."

"Everything should," she said. "Columns keep people honest."

"And what column am I in?"

She looked at him — really looked, at this exile who had shadowed her out of wary fascination and become the person whose absence she couldn't yet imagine, who had bled out a third of himself onto ash-tunnel stone because his comrades' blades came at *her* — and heard herself say, before any careful part of her could intervene:

"The one I don't know how to balance yet."

His smile faded into something quieter. Then his eyes rolled back and his whole body shuddered, and there was no more time for accounts.

* * *

She laid out her kit on the stone like an altar: candle, knife, the strip of clean linen she had, the small traveling inkhorn she'd carried since the expedition left Vell — temple-issue iron-gall ink, which was wrong, all wrong, she knew it was wrong, but it was what she had. Beside them, from inside her shirt, she took out the page she had never stopped carrying: the scorched fragment rescued from the senior scribe's flame, years ago, the list of keeper-blood names with her own family's line among them. Her inheritance. Her target-mark. Her proof.

Names had power here. She'd learned that much. So did blood.

She pricked her finger on the knife and let one fall into the inkhorn. The ink took it strangely — swirled, then settled, then went glossy, like a pond at dusk. When she dipped the pen it wrote in a color that wasn't quite black anymore. Dark red-brown. Ember-colored.

"Oh," she said softly, to no one. "That's how it works. That's what they burned out of us."

Authority lines weren't blank because of rank. They were blank because the Temple filled them with someone else's blood — consecrated stock, sanctioned donors, generations of it — and called the resulting magic holy. Keeper-blood wrote truer. Which was why they hunted it. Why they erased it. Why her name had been on a page worth burning.

Fine. She was done being censored.

She bent over Renn's side and began to write on his skin.

The first sigil was the simplest she knew: the closure-glyph, the one that ended a line of argument, that brought a runaway sum back to zero. On vellum it meant nothing. On living flesh, drawn in ember-ink, it *meant* — she felt it mean, felt the word leave her pen and land in the world with weight, like a stone dropped into still water. Along the top edge of the wound, the bleeding dimmed. Not stopped. Dimmed, the way a shout dims when a hand closes over the mouth.

Renn gasped. "What—"

"Don't move. Moving smears my handwriting."

"You're writing *on* me."

"I'm editing you. Hold still."

She worked down the cut, glyph by glyph, and with each one the cost arrived immediately, itemized, non-negotiable. It came up her arm from the pen-hand: cold first, then numbness, then a strange hollowing, as though someone were carefully removing her from herself in strips. She understood, distantly, with the part of her mind that was still taking notes, that the sigils were drawing their payment from the nearest available account. The blood in the ink was hers. The authority was hers. The price was hers.

By the tenth glyph her vision had gone grainy, like a bad copy. By the fifteenth, she was writing with her left hand braced against the wall to keep from listing sideways. The wound was closing — actually closing, flesh knitting under her strokes with a sound like a whisper reading itself — but the bottom third of it still seeped, and her hand had begun to wander, and a wandering hand on a healing sigil was worse than no hand at all. A misspelled prayer. An amendment that amended *her*.

She wrote the sixteenth glyph wrong. She felt it go wrong even as the pen moved — a fraction of drift in the terminal stroke — and the hollowing sensation lurched sideways, into something colder, something that tasted like copper and snow, and for one vertiginous heartbeat she was looking at herself from outside her own body, a small figure kneeling in candlelight over a dying man, and the figure was *fading*, going translucent at the edges like a page held to a flame—

"—stop," she said aloud, to herself, in her best scriptorium voice. "Stop. Correct the error."

She crossed out the flawed glyph with a single stroke — deletion, the oldest editorial act — and the world snapped back. She was on her knees. The candle had shrunk to a blue nub. Renn was staring at her with absolute horror.

"You're killing yourself."

"I'm *paying*," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "There's a difference. It's in the terms."

"There is no difference! Coral—" He tried to sit up; the half-healed wound pulled and he went white. "Listen to me. You said it yourself, everything gets a column. Look at yours. Look at what's left in it."

She looked. Honestly looked, the way she'd been trained: assets against liabilities, strength remaining against work required. The arithmetic was ugly. She could maybe finish the closing sequence — the final binding-glyphs that would hold the knit flesh together against infection and motion. Maybe. And then she would be empty. Not sleeping-empty. *Ledger*-empty. Written down to a remainder.

And behind them, somewhere in the tunnels, the Harvest men were still hunting.

Her pen hovered over his skin. Her hand trembled with exhaustion and cold and the particular loneliness of a task that cannot be delegated — and there it was, the thought arriving not as despair but as clarity, plain as a corrected line:

*I can't do this alone.*

She had built eleven years of indenture on never admitting that. Had priced every hope exactly, had wanted nothing that required another person's help, because help was leverage and leverage was debt and debt was the cage she'd been born into. Wanting things was dangerous. Doss had told her that once, on a festival night, with love in his voice: *don't want things, Coral.* And she had obeyed, right up until wanting became the only thing keeping her upright.

"I can't do this alone," she said out loud.

Renn blinked at her.

"It's not rhetoric," she said. "It's an entry. I'm entering it into the account. I need—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish, because unfinished sentences were how lies started. "I need you to talk me through the last glyphs. You know the old script better than I do. Your grandmother sang hymns to combustion; you grew up inside this language. My hand writes it. Your eyes correct it. That's the division of labor. That's the only way this balances."

"That's not how it works," he said. "The reader doesn't pay. Only the writer pays."

"Then the writer pays less, because the errors cost double and you'll halve my errors." She was already dipping the pen again, already steadying it against his sternum. "Four glyphs left. Call them out as I write. Stroke order, terminal marks, everything. Be insufferable about it. Be a *senior scribe*."

Something moved through his face — fear for her, protest, and under it, rising, the fierce reluctant tenderness of a man being conscripted into his own rescue. He exhaled.

"First stroke descends left of center," he said. "Like a wolf-track slanting downhill. Go."

She went.

He was insufferable. He was magnificent. He caught her twice — once a drifting angle, once a terminal mark she'd have flattened — and each correction landed like a hand on a rudder. The seventeenth glyph seated itself into the world with a hum she felt in her teeth. The eighteenth closed the deep layer. The nineteenth drew the last of the bleeding down to a dark seam, thin as a ruled line on a page.

For the twentieth — the seal, the glyph that made the whole repair permanent — her hand stopped. Not from weakness. From knowing.

"This one takes the most," she said. "This is the one that empties the account."

"Then we negotiate terms," Renn said. He caught her pen-hand — gently, the way you handle something already spent — and turned it palm-up. "Your blood opened the credit. Let mine co-sign."

"That's not—"

"You told me the Temple fills authority lines with other people's blood and calls it holy. Fine. We'll fill ours ourselves, and call it what it is." He pressed his own sliced palm — the shallow cut from the ambush, still crusted — against the nib, letting his blood join hers in the well. "Two names on the instrument. Two accounts charged. That's not Temple law. That's ours."

Coral looked at their mingled blood darkening the ink, at the blue nub of candle, at this man who had been a watcher on a ridgeline and was now a co-signatory, and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since a night eleven years ago when she'd signed her first debt.

"Together, then," she said. "On three. One. Two—"

They wrote the seal together, four hands on one pen, and the glyph sank into Renn's skin like light into water, and the cost came — she felt it, a great soft pull, the last of her reserves flowing out through the nib — but it came *halved*, shared, distributed, and instead of fading she merely knelt there, wrung out and present and alive, watching the final seam close to a thin silver line that would scar but hold.

The candle died. Neither of them moved in the dark.

"Coral," Renn said at last, his voice ragged but level, steady, *his*. "What you said. About not being able to do this alone."

"Yes."

"Write that one down too. Somewhere permanent. Because whatever comes next — Corvel, the harvest, the heart-chamber, all of it — we go in as co-signatories. Both names. Every page."

In the darkness, too tired to stand, too full to speak, Coral reached out and found his hand and held it — the first unpriced thing she had ever owned.

Then, far down the tunnel, boots rang on stone. More than two sets. Moving fast, and not trying to hide.

Renn tensed beside her. But the footsteps stopped short, and a voice echoed toward them through the dark — dry, clipped, unmistakably military, and impossibly familiar:

"If you two are finished holding hands down there, Blackwood, I suggest you explain why my scouts just found Harvest banners on the *north* road — and why the High Scribe's sealed orders say the dragon dies at dawn."

Veyra Osten stepped into the guttering light of a fresh-lit torch, and her face was the face of a woman who had finally decided which side of the ledger she stood on.
