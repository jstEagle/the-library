## 26. The Regent's Gambit

The regent's study had not been a study when Lady Ashvael first inherited it, three hundred years of her family's stewardship compressed into the bones of what had once been a watch-post overlooking the dragon's throat. She had kept the arrow-slits. She liked to be reminded that her office began as a place from which one watched for enemies.

Tonight she watched for one who carried a Temple seal.

* * *

She had learned of it the way she learned most things in the Court of Cinders — late, and through someone she did not entirely trust.

Maren of the Deep Archives came at the hour when even the cinder-lamps were banked low, hood drawn, hands shaking so badly that the message tube rattled against her teeth before she could offer it. Ashvael took the tube herself rather than make the archivist fumble with protocol.

"Read it," Maren whispered, as if the walls had ears that mattered. "I can't. I've read it twice and I can't."

Ashvael drew out the slip of membrane-parchment — Temple stock, she noted; the texture was wrong for anything made in the kingdom, too smooth, too white, like skin that had never seen weather. She held it near the coals and read.

It was a requisition order. Dated three days past. Signed in a hand she recognized from captured correspondence: *Corvel, High Scribe of Vell, Keeper of the Ledger of Quiet Hours.*

The requisition called for: four harvest-engines, full crew complement. Sixty pounds of binding-salt. One severance-blade, consecrated. And beneath the inventory, in smaller script, the line that had broken Maren:

*Note: the creature need not survive extraction. A dying flame burns hottest. Proceed to the heart-chamber by any means. Casualties among the native population are acceptable and expected.*

"Native population," Ashvael said quietly.

"That's us," Maren said. "That's you. That's the whole kingdom. He's written us down as casualties *before he's begun*, the way you'd note rain."

Ashvael set the parchment on her desk and weighted it with the stone seal of her house — a wolf couchant, worn nearly smooth by generations of thumbs. For a long moment she said nothing. In the arrow-slit behind her, the mountain glowed its faint and failing rose-color, dimmer than it had been a month ago. Dimmer every week now. Everyone felt it, even those who pretended not to: the lightness in the body, the way old wounds ached, the way the ash-tunnels groaned in their sleep.

The dragon was dying faster. And Corvel meant to hasten the dying and call it harvest.

"You're certain this is genuine?"

"I copied our own requisition codes against his. They match. And the seal—" Maren swallowed. "My lady, the seal is pressed in wax mixed with star-ash. That's a Temple emergency authentication. It hasn't been used since the Warden years. Since the sealing." Her voice dropped further. "Since they built your valley on top of us."

*Our* valley. Ashvael let that pass. Three centuries underground taught patience, if nothing else.

"Who else has seen this?"

"No one. I stole it from the courier drop in the third gallery — the Temple has a drop there, my lady, they've *had* a drop there, someone inside the court is feeding them—"

"I know," Ashvael said. "I have known for some time. You did well to bring this to me before anyone else."

Maren looked at her with the particular expression of an archivist realizing she had just become evidence. "What will you do?"

What would she do? Ashvael turned to the window slit. Below, far below, the great dark shape of the dragon curled through the roots of the mountain, visible only as a slow pulse of ember-light along veins of rock, like a heartbeat seen through a sleeping giant's skin. Her kingdom existed because that heartbeat continued. Every wall, every orchard, every petrified tree and glass road and stubborn, secret life — stitched together by breath older than any nation above or below.

And the man who wanted it wanted it *dead*. Not saved. Not shared. Rendered into weapons and salt and a last hot burst of power, and then sealed away forever behind new wards, a warden's tomb with sixty thousand souls entombed inside it.

"The Court will want to negotiate," Maren said nervously. "They'll say we should bargain with him. Offer terms. Half the flame, maybe. My lady, half of nothing is—"

"Nothing," Ashvael agreed. "Corvel does not want half. Read his note again. *By any means.* He wrote that because he believes no one down here will ever read it. Because he believes this kingdom is already a corpse he's dressing." She turned back. "He is sincere, Maren. That's the worst of it. I have read everything we've taken from his people. There is no cruelty in him, no greed I can find. Only a creed: that magic caged made Vell safe, and safety justifies anything. You cannot bargain with a creed. You can only break it, or be broken upon it."

"And which will we do?"

"That," said Lady Ashvael, "depends entirely on whether I can persuade an enemy to help me."

* * *

The enemy in question was currently hiding in a dead orchard with a wounded exile, and Ashvael had to send for her the way one sends for anyone these days: through runners who knew the ash-tunnels, carrying messages written in the old script so that Temple-trained eyes would see only decoration.

She composed the summons herself. Four drafts. The first was too cold — *Come to me, keeper-blood* — and she burned it, because she remembered what it was to be summoned in that tone, centuries ago, by a regent who thought obedience was owed to a chair. The second flattered, and she burned that too; the Blackwood girl struck her, from every report Renn had given, as someone who could smell flattery the way a wolf smelled fear. The third begged, and she burned it fastest of all.

The fourth said simply:

*Lady Coral Blackwood. I have learned what the High Scribe intends for the dragon, and it is worse than harvest. I have evidence he means to kill it outright and seal the kingdom with its corpse. I am told you know what the wards were for, and what your blood opens. I do not ask you to trust me. I ask you to come and count what I know, as a scribe counts — column against column — and judge me by the arithmetic. Come alone if you must. Bring your knife if you prefer. I will meet you unarmed, in the Grey Orchard, at the hour the cinders are banked. — A.*

She sent it by the boy Tallow, who was twelve, quick, and had never once been caught, and then she sat alone in her study and admitted to the ceiling what she would admit to no one else: that she was afraid.

Not of the girl. Of the arithmetic.

Because here was the ledger as it truly stood, and Ashvael had been a steward long enough to keep honest books even in the privacy of her skull. Assets: a kingdom of perhaps sixty thousand, armed with heirloom pikes and desperation. A captain of the expedition — Osten — whose loyalties were a coin still spinning in the air. An exile named Renn whom her own court had cast out and whose loyalty she therefore had no right to expect. And Coral Blackwood: a debtor scribe from the prison-lockhouse itself, keeper-blood, the only living soul who could stand in the threshold of the door.

Liabilities: a Temple strike force under the walls' notice, growing daily. A faction of her own court — hers! — feeding them intelligence, hungry to harvest the flame into weapons before it guttered out. A dragon too weak to rise, spending its final strength on the stitches that kept the kingdom from unraveling. And above all of it, the simple brutal fact that every plan she had — every single one — ended with a woman standing in a doorway forever.

That was the cost of the door. Renn had told her what the dragon showed the girl. *A keeper holds the threshold, or until relieved by another.* Forever, or until relieved. And there were no other keepers. The Temple had spent three hundred years making sure of that, burning names out of ledgers, hunting bloodlines, and one of those names — Ashvael had read the recovered lists, had read *Blackwood* written in a dead warden's hand — was standing in her kingdom right now, being asked to trade one cage for another.

*We will ask her to die slowly,* Ashvael thought, *and call it freedom.*

No. She would not frame it that way, not to the girl, not to herself. There had to be another reading. Co-keepers, perhaps — the covenant was old and its grammar ambiguous; Renn swore the word *keeper* in the original script carried a plural form that the Temple translations had flattened to singular, deliberately or carelessly. If the plural held, the burden could be shared. Halved. Handed on.

If it didn't hold, she was asking a young woman to stand in fire until the mountains wore flat.

The candle burned down. Somewhere in the deeps, the dragon's pulse flickered — she felt it the way one feels a missed heartbeat in one's own chest — steadied, and continued. Ashvael closed her eyes and gave the dark ceiling the truth she could give no living courtier:

*I built none of this. I inherited all of it. But I will not preside over the ending. Whatever it costs, whatever I have to kneel to — this kingdom walks out of the dark, or I am no daughter of the warders, but their last accomplice, and I will not be that.*

Then she slept two hours in her chair, as regents do, and rose to dress for begging.

* * *

The Grey Orchard at the banked hour was silver-grey and utterly still, the petrified trees holding their fruit the way the village had held its running — mid-gesture, mid-flight, three centuries frozen. Ashvael walked into it unarmed, as promised, wearing no crown and no regalia, just a grey traveling cloak that made her look like what she was underneath: an old woman who had spent her whole life keeping accounts she never opened.

Coral Blackwood was already there.

She stood among the trees with her hood back — bold, or reckless, or simply done with hiding — and beside her, arms crossed and expression suggesting he'd lost several arguments to get this far, stood Renn. Ashvael noted the bandage at his shoulder, fresh, and the way he stood slightly angled toward the girl: guarding her even while glaring at his regent.

"Lady Ashvael." Coral's voice was level. Ledger-level, Ashvael thought. A voice trained to state sums without flinching. "You asked me to count. So count."

"Column one." Ashvael did not approach closer; she had chosen a distance that respected both knives present. "This." She drew out Corvel's requisition and held it where the banked cinder-light could reach it. "Taken from his own courier drop by one of my archivists. Authentication verified against captured seals. Read the footnote."

Coral read. Ashvael watched her face do what faces did when the mind moved faster than composure: a small tightening at the eyes, a stillness in the jaw. When she looked up, her voice was very quiet. "*Casualties among the native population are acceptable and expected.*"

"He wrote you down as weather, Lady Blackwood. All of us."

"He told me he wanted the flame saved." Something moved under the words — the memory, presumably, of a chamber where a dying god breathed and a smooth-voiced priest had offered forgiveness like a purse of coin. "He stood in front of the heart-chamber and spoke about preservation."

"Men speak of preservation while sharpening the blade. It is not hypocrisy. It is sincerity, which is worse. Corvel believes — I have studied his writings, I believe I understand him better than his own acolytes do — that the cage *was* the mercy. That magic unbound killed the stars, burned villages to statues, and that Vell's founders were heroes. To him, killing the dragon and sealing the kingdom isn't murder. It's finishing a holy chore his ancestors left incomplete." She paused. "He will feel grief as he does it. That is the part that should frighten you most."

Renn shifted. "Your grace summoned us to read us a villain's résumé? We've met the villain."

"I summoned you, exile, because of column two." Ashvael turned back to Coral. "Your blood opens the heart-chamber. His orders require the heart-chamber. Which means either he takes you — again — or his entire operation fails at its first lock. You are not a piece on his board, Lady Blackwood. You are the board."

"And your court?" Coral's eyes were sharp as an awl. "Renn tells me half your nobles want the flame harvested themselves. Weapons before salvation. How am I to know your requisition differs from his except in the letterhead?"

For a moment the orchard was silent enough to hear the mountain breathe.

"You aren't," Ashvael said. "Not by my word. Words from a regent are worth exactly the paper they're sealed on, and we both know how easily seals forge. So I won't offer you my word." She reached to her belt, unhurried, and Renn's hand went to his knife — and stopped, because what she drew was not a weapon. It was a key. Old iron, wolf-headed, worn smooth. "This is the Seal-Key of the warders. It opens the archive-crypts where you found the covenant — the *true* crypts, including the vaults beneath, which even my archivists are forbidden. In those vaults is every original record my family kept for three hundred years. Correspondence with the first Temple wardens. The construction logs of the wards. The payment ledgers — oh yes, Lady Blackwood, the Temple *paid* us, generation after generation, for silence and for keeper-hunting, and every payment is recorded in my ancestors' hands. Your grandmother's capture may be written there. Perhaps your mother's flight."

Coral had gone very still. "You're giving me the key."

"I'm giving you the *right of audit*. Take it. Copy everything. Publish it to your expedition, to Captain Osten, to the valley when the door opens — to the world, if the world is listening. Hold my house's three centuries of complicity up to the light alongside the Temple's. I am not asking you to trust me. I am asking you to *audit* me, and to let the records argue my case better than my tongue ever could." She extended the key across the frozen air between them. "A scribe trusts ink, not mouths. So take the ink."

For a long moment Coral did not move. Then she crossed the distance, took the key — their fingers did not touch; Ashvael had timed the pass precisely — and turned it over once in the cinder-light, reading it the way she apparently read everything: as text.

"There's a third column," Coral said. "You're saving it. Say it anyway."

Ashvael inclined her head, acknowledging the hit. "Column three. The door." She drew a breath, and let the regent fall away for exactly as long as it took to say the true thing. "I cannot open it. My armies cannot open it. My court cannot agree on what it even wants. Only you can stand in the threshold, and the price the dragon named is one no one should pay alone. I know what was done to your bloodline. I know what my ancestors helped do. I know I am asking the granddaughter of the hunted to save the house of the hunters." Her voice roughened, and she let it. "So here is my offer, and it is the whole of it. The kingdom's aid — every soldier, every tunnel, every archive, my own person if it comes to that — for your ritual. Full support for relighting the star and opening the door, on your terms, under your authority, not mine. And afterward, when the exodus begins, my abdication. Publicly recorded, in your script and mine, with every sin of my house attached. Let the kingdom walk out of myth with clean books, or not walk at all."

Renn made a sound — half laugh, half disbelief. "You'd step down."

"I'd *step aside*, exile. There's a difference, and the difference is the point." Ashvael kept her eyes on Coral. "Power that requires a prison isn't power. It's a longer sentence with better furniture. I have served mine three hundred years. I find I am... ready to be relieved."

The word hung between them — *relieved* — the covenant's own word, the word that meant the keeper could finally leave the threshold. Coral looked down at the iron key in her palm, and Ashvael saw her thumb move across the wolf's worn muzzle, once, twice, the way one strokes something that might yet bite.

"If I do this," Coral said at last, "and the door opens, and your people pour through into a world that forgot them — there will be reckoning. Not vengeance. Reckoning. Every name your family erased gets restored. Every debt marked paid or unpaid, honestly. No myths. No martyrs. Just the ledger, public, forever."

"My lady," Ashvael said, "you have just described the only prayer my house has ever deserved."

Above them, faint through the mountain's crown where no stars could reach, the dragon's pulse stuttered — a long, terrible interval — then resumed, weaker. All three of them felt it. None of them spoke of it. There was no need.

"One condition more," Coral said. She held up the key. "When we go into the vaults — you walk with me. Every step. You don't send me into your family's sins with a lamp and a map. You carry them yourself."

Lady Ashvael bowed her head, and for the first time in thirty years, it was not ceremony.

"Then let us begin the audit," she said, "tonight. Before Corvel moves. Because I have read his requisitions for forty years, Lady Blackwood, and I know what follows a note like *by any means*."

She turned toward the crypt stair, and behind her heard the two of them follow — the exile and the scribe, the hunter's blood and the hunted's — and thought, with a steward's grim satisfaction, that the columns had finally begun to balance.

What she could not yet know — what waited three galleries below, in the vault her ancestors had sealed with wax and star-ash and lies — was that the payment ledgers contained one entry more than she remembered. A final transaction, dated the night the stars went out. Initialed by her own father.

Addressed, in a hand she knew better than her own, to *the last of the Blackwoods.*
