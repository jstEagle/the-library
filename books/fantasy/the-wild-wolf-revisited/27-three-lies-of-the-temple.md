## 27. Three Lies of the Temple

The war-room had been a chapel once. Coral could tell by the shape of the alcoves along the walls — niches worn smooth where statues must have stood before the kingdom stopped trusting anything it couldn't feed or arm. Now the niches held candles, maps, and jars of ink mixed with ground glass, and the long altar-table at the center was buried under three centuries of stolen paper.

Lady Ashvael sat at the head of that table with her hands folded, the way Coral imagined a judge would sit if judges wore ash-grey silk and had eyes like banked coals. Around her, the court of cinders: Renn at Coral's left shoulder, still pale from his wound but upright out of sheer stubbornness; Veyra standing apart with her arms crossed and her soldiers' loyalty visibly straining against the leash; a scatter of regent's counselors whose names Coral hadn't bothered to learn because she'd spent the last two days not sleeping, reading.

On the table before her lay everything.

The covenant scroll from the archive-crypts, its seal cracked open like an eggshell. The ledger fragments she'd pulled from the Temple's own records, smuggled out in Veyra's supply chests. The burned page — or what remained of it, the charred corner Doss had once pressed into her hand in another life, in another valley, under other stars. And her own pen, capped, waiting.

She had rehearsed this argument forty times. She had rehearsed it in the dark of the ash-tunnels, walking beside Renn's slow breathing. She had rehearsed it while stitching sigils into bandages by candlelight. She had rehearsed it so many times that now, standing at the head of the room with every eye on her, she found the rehearsal falling away and only the truth left underneath — which was, she supposed, the point.

"My lord regents. Lady Ashvael. Captain Osten." She nodded to each. "I am Coral Blackwood. For eleven years I was indentured to the Temple of Quiet Hours as a copying scribe. I copied debt ledgers. I copied land grants. I copied, without knowing what I held, the administrative history of my own prison."

A counselor shifted. Someone whispered. Ashvael lifted one finger and the whispering died.

"The Temple has told you — told all of us — three stories," Coral said. "Three lies. I intend to take them one at a time."

* * *

She put her hand flat on the oldest scroll first, its vellum gone the color of old teeth.

"The first lie: that the valley of Vell was founded as a refuge. A sanctuary built by the faithful after some ancient calamity, warded against the wild magic of the dead country." She let that sit. "Here is what the founders actually wrote, in their own hands, in the founding covenant of Vell."

She unrolled it and turned it so the light caught the first line of the old script. She had read it aloud so often in the past days that the words lived in her mouth now like a second native tongue.

"'Let the wall be raised not against the fire, but around it. Let the valley be built as the lockhouse of the door. Let the wardens dwell within and know themselves wardens, though they be taught to forget.'"

She looked up. "Vell is not a refuge. It never was. It is a lockhouse. The wards your people call the betrayal — the wall that stole the sky and erased a kingdom from memory — were designed, funded, and staffed by the same institution that governs my valley today. The Temple of Quiet Hours did not inherit Vell. It built Vell. It is the prison administration, three centuries running, and every tithe, every festival, every prayer offered at the ward-wall was maintenance performed by prisoners who didn't know they were imprisoned."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

An elderly counselor — Coral thought his name was Serrek, keeper of the kingdom's remnant archives — leaned forward over the table. "We have always known the wall was raised against us," he said slowly. "That is not new. What you are saying—"

"What I am saying is that your enemy and mine is the same office," Coral said. "You knew you were behind a wall. We did not know we were the wall. They made my people the mechanism, lady. Every farmer who paid his tithe, every child who sang the festival songs at the ward-stones — they were turning the key in your lock and calling it devotion." Her voice caught, and she let it. There was no shame in it here. "I grew up inside the hinge of your cage. That is the first lie."

* * *

"The second lie is smaller and worse." She moved down the table, and the crowd of counselors parted to let her pass, the way water parts around something sharp. "It is the lie the Temple told on the night the stars went out. You heard it too, captain — your orders say it plainly. *Find what killed the stars.* As though someone stood in the sky with a snuffer."

She laid out the ledger fragments next, fanning them like a card-sharp laying down a winning hand. "These are the Temple's own energy tallies. Ward-draw records, kept quarterly for three hundred years by clerks who understood them about as well as I understood the ledgers I copied — which is to say, they recorded numbers without ever being permitted to read their meaning." Her finger traced a column. "Look at the draw curve. Every quarter, for three centuries, the wards consumed more than the year before. Slowly. Patiently. The way a loan accrues interest no one intends to pay."

"And the source of the draw?" Veyra asked. Her voice was flat, professional, but her eyes weren't.

"The sky." Coral said it simply, because simplicity was the only mercy the truth allowed. "The wards were built to do two things: cage the dragon-fire below, and blind the world above. But a working that large doesn't run on nothing. It ran on the doors. Each star above Vell was a doorway between worlds — your cosmology says it, and the Temple's own engineers knew it, because they built the wards to drink from them. Starlight wasn't just light. It was the power feeding the wall. The Temple didn't extinguish the stars, ladies and lords. It *spent* them. Three hundred years of withdrawal from an account no one was watching, and on the night of the autumn festival, the balance reached zero, and every star over my valley went dark at once — not murdered, but *emptied*, the way a well goes dry."

Serrek had gone grey beneath his grey. "The night the sky closed," he murmured. "Our oldest songs call it the Silence. We believed—"

"You believed an attack. The valley believes a miracle. Both beliefs serve the same office." Coral gathered the fragments back into order. "And here is the cruelty of it: the lie requires no villain sneaking through the dark. It only requires bookkeeping. The Temple has known for generations that the account was running down — these quarterly reports show panic beginning ninety years ago, contingency plans, harvest proposals shelved and reshelved. They watched the stars die the way a merchant watches a ship sink in slow motion, and they told no one, because the moment anyone asked *why*, they would have to show the ledgers. Well." She spread her hands. "I am showing the ledgers."

Renn spoke from her shoulder, quiet and rough. "The dragon felt it first." Everyone turned. "Under the mountain. She told me once — before I was exiled, when I still had leave to walk the ember road — that the flame had been thinning since her grandmother's grandmother's time. She thought it was her own dying." His jaw worked. "It wasn't. It was theft. Something was drinking the sky she slept beneath."

"Yes," Coral said softly. "And that brings me to the third lie."

* * *

She saved the burned page for last. She had carried it through the dead country folded inside her shirt, against her skin, because it was the smallest document on the table and the heaviest.

"The Temple calls it the Register of Quiet Hours," she said. "Every soul in Vell is entered into it at birth. Debts, tithes, indentures — all of it flows through that register. What the register does not advertise is a second function." She unfolded the charred fragment carefully, handling it like a relic, because that was exactly what it had become. "This is a page from the inner rolls. I saw a senior scribe burn its twin rather than let me read it, six years ago, and I have spent every night since wondering what could be worth destroying over a debtor girl's shoulder. Here is what was worth burning."

She read aloud. Names. Dozens of names, in the cramped clerk-hand she had been trained in, each followed by a notation she had once assumed was routine.

"*Observed. Confirmed. Resolved.*" She repeated the formula three times, letting it rot in the air. "Resolved. Do you know what resolution means, in those rolls? It means a family quietly relocated. A marriage arranged to dilute a bloodline. An indenture extended until the debtor died in service. In four cases—" her voice went very steady, the way it did when steadiness was the only thing holding her together, "—in four cases it means a house fire with no survivors and a Temple relief fund paying for the funeral. Over three hundred years, the inner rolls track one thing and one thing only: descendants of the original ward-keepers. Keeper-blood. The families whose ancestors' hands set the first stones."

"Why?" Ashvael asked. Not rhetorically. The regent leaned forward like a blade coming out of a sheath. "Why hunt them?"

"Because of me." Coral said it and felt the whole room tilt, just slightly, the way the ground had tilted the first time she touched a wardstone and it answered. "The heart-chamber beneath your mountain opens to keeper-blood alone. The founders built it that way — a final lock, in case the wards ever failed. Only the blood of the wardens can reach the dragon's heart. Which means that for three centuries, every person born with that blood has been a living key to the one door the Temple could never afford to see opened." She laid the fragment down. "They haven't been erasing keeper-bloodlines out of superstition or cruelty, though there's plenty of both in these margins. They've been performing security. Systematically, patiently, generation after generation, hunting down every key that might someday turn — until the pool ran so shallow that by my mother's time, there were perhaps a handful left in the entire valley. My mother was one. She ran. I was the one they caught."

She thought of the High Scribe's face in the crypt-light, saying *I knew her*, and made herself keep speaking.

"They didn't burn the page in front of me because it was dangerous knowledge," she finished. "They burned it because it was a *list*, and I was on it, and even then — a debtor girl of nineteen with eleven years left on her indenture — even then, they wanted me never to see my own name written down as a threat to be managed. I was never a scribe to them. I was inventory."

The silence that followed was different from the others. It had heat in it.

Veyra broke it, because Veyra always broke silences eventually, usually with something blunt. "Then Corvel knows you're here. He's known since the expedition roster was drawn."

"He chose me for the expedition himself," Coral said. "Debt forgiven, debt assumed. He didn't conscript a scribe, captain. He delivered a key to the lock, wrapped in chains, and waited for the door to present itself." She laughed, short and joyless. "He probably thanked the Quiet Hours for the convenience."

Ashvael rose. The candles guttered in the draft of her movement, and for a moment Coral saw her as the dragon might see her — small, brief, blazing. "Then hear the resolution of this court," the regent said. "The three lies stand exposed: the sanctuary that was a lockhouse, the murder that was theft, the register that was a manhunt. Let them be copied, sealed, and carried. When the door opens — *if* the door opens — the world beyond will receive this account in the first hour, in your hand, scribe, before Corvel can write a single word of his own version."

"That assumes we get the chance," Renn said.

"It assumes more than that." Ashvael's coal-dark eyes moved to the far end of the table, where a map of the mountain sprawled, the glass bridge inked in red, the heart-chamber marked with a circle like a wound. "Corvel is not a patient man. I have read the intercepted dispatches Captain Osten's people recovered. He believes the flame can be harvested whole — and he believes it is dying faster than his engines can drink. Sooner or later, arithmetic will defeat theology, and he will stop trying to milk the dragon and start trying to gut her."

"Then we move before he does," Veyra said.

"No," Ashvael said. "We move *when* he commits. Once, and only once. We will get one assault, one window, one chance at the threshold — and if we spend it early, we lose everything." She turned back to Coral, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was somehow more unnerving than her anger. "You have assembled the case, scribe. Justice outside the law still requires a battlefield. Rest tonight. Tomorrow we plan the battle."

But Coral barely heard her, because at that moment a runner burst through the chapel doors — one of Renn's ridge-watchers, ash-grey with road dust, breathing hard enough to bend double.

"Lady Regent." He gasped. "Lights on the mountain's south flank. Dozens of them. Camp-fires where no camp was yesterday." He swallowed. "And the glass bridge — someone's rigged engines at the near span. Big ones. They're already hauling cable toward the heart-chamber approach."

Coral looked down at the burned page, at her own name ghosting through the char, and felt the case in her hands become, all at once, a weapon with no time left to sharpen it.

Corvel had done his arithmetic.
