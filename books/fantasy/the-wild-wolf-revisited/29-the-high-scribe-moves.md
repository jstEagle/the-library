## 29. The High Scribe Moves

Corvel had not slept in four days, and he did not intend to sleep again until the flame was in the cisterns.

He stood at the mouth of the Ember Road with the harvest column drawn up behind him — two hundred Temple swords, forty scribes of the Second Veil, and the six harvester engines on their iron-wheeled cradles, each one a cathedral of brass and glass built in secret beneath the Vell scriptorium while the valley slept. The mountain ahead glowed like a banked hearth seen through a cracked door. He could feel it against his face at this distance: a warmth that was not the sun's warmth, older, more patient, the warmth of something that had been burning for three hundred years without being asked whether it wished to.

*Good*, he thought. *It has had its rest.*

"Captain Brann," he said, without turning. "The ridge positions."

"Taken an hour past, Highness." Brann used the old honorific; Corvel had never corrected him. "The kingdom's watchers withdrew when we came through the wards. They know we're here. They're choosing their ground."

"They've been choosing their ground for three centuries." Corvel adjusted his gloves — white kidskin, changed twice daily, a discipline his novices believed was vanity and which was in fact arithmetic. Ink clung to everything in this country. The air itself was ink. Every surface held residue of working, and a man who touched things carelessly would carry the evidence of this place back into the valley on his own hands. "They will have chosen badly. They always do. Pride is a poor surveyor."

He began to walk. The road underfoot was fused glass, smooth as temple tile, and his boots rang on it faintly, a sound like distant bells. Behind him the column followed, two hundred pairs of feet making the same small chime, and Corvel found the sound pleasing. It was the sound of order moving across chaos. It was the sound of the ledger being balanced.

People misunderstood him. He knew that. Even his own scribes feared him in the wrong way — they thought him cold, ambitious, hungry. Let them. Fear kept the lower offices honest better than doctrine did. But the truth was simpler and, he believed, finer: Corvel loved the valley. Not its people particularly — people were variable, and variables were the enemy of every covenant — but the valley itself, the green and ordered length of it, the wheat that came up when it was planted, the children who grew tall because nothing ate them, the nights that passed without one door kicked in, without one field turned to glass, without one village frozen mid-scream into grey statues.

He had seen the statues now. Two days ago, on the march out from the broken wall, his column had passed through the dead country's first village — houses intact, loaves still on tables after three hundred years, and everywhere the people, ash-grey, caught in the attitudes of flight. A woman reaching back for a child. A man shielding his eyes from something above. Corvel had walked the street slowly, alone, as his soldiers waited at the edge in visible dread, and he had read the residual spellwork himself, because he trusted no other eye to tell him true.

The wards had done it. His own valley's founding miracle — the great border working, the Covenant of Quiet Hours, the thing every novice learned to copy before they learned their letters — had done *this*. When the founders raised the wall, they had not merely sealed magic out of Vell. They had wrung it out of the land beyond, and the land beyond had been inhabited, and the inhabitants had been made of the same substance as everything else that magic touched.

He had stood in that street for a long time, and his scribes, watching from a distance, later said he appeared moved. He had not been moved. He had been *confirmed*.

This was what unbound magic did. This was what it had always done, before the founders, before the Covenant, before the Temple existed to hold the line. The world outside the wall was a wound that had scabbed over into legend, and the legends lied about how deep the wound went. The stars going out three months ago had not been a mystery to Corvel. It had been a symptom. The cage was failing, and everything the cage had held was beginning to leak, and if the leak became a flood there would be a thousand villages like the ash one, and Vell would be among them, and there would be no founders left to build a second wall because the knowledge of building walls died with the first generation and had been deliberately, wisely, buried with them.

So: the flame. The dragon-fire that kept this hidden kingdom stitched together — the last coherent working in the dead country, the last proof that raw power could be *held*, could be given shape and purpose and law. If they took it, contained it, brought it home in the harvester engines, they would not merely save Vell. They would rebuild the wall from the inside, with living fire instead of dead stone. A new Covenant, written in flame. An age of Quiet Hours that would not fail in three hundred years or three thousand, because this time the lock would be warm.

That was the creed. He had preached it to his officers on the first night beyond the wall, standing on the wardstone rubble with the starless sky overhead, and he had watched doubt leave their faces one by one as he spoke, and he had thought: *this is what it is to be believed. This is worth anything.*

Safety justified anything. He had never once said otherwise, and he had never once needed to.

* * *

The heart-chamber approach was a tunnel, and the tunnel had a gate, and the gate was the problem.

"The Glass Bridge," Brann reported at midday, spreading a scout's charcoal sketch on a campaign table that two scribes had carried up the road on their backs. "Single span, maybe sixty paces, over a vent shaft that goes down further than our lines do. The defenders have fortified the far end. Stone works, arrow slits cut into the living rock, and—" he tapped the sketch, where a cluster of figures was rendered with unusual care, "—those. Kingdom regulars. Real armor, real drill. Not the ragged watch we were promised."

"We were promised nothing," Corvel said mildly. "We were told stories by a regent who wanted us complacent. Lady Ashvael has been playing for time since the day we arrived, and I have permitted her to play because time served us too." He studied the bridge drawing. Sixty paces of open ground under fire, with a drop at the bottom that no rope in the column could plumb. A frontal assault would cost him a hundred men and might fail anyway, and he did not have a hundred men to spend, because he had done the arithmetic of the whole campaign before he ever left Vell and the numbers did not include waste.

"Then we don't take the bridge," said a voice from the table's edge.

Corvel looked up. Veyra Osten stood there, arms crossed, expedition cloak still travel-stained, and beside her — he registered this with a small cold satisfaction — the girl. Coral Blackwood. Keeper-blood. The one asset in this entire theater that could not be replaced, standing in his camp of her own volition, which meant the leash was holding, which meant the leash had been worth every year of patient bookkeeping.

"Captain Osten," he said. "You have an alternative."

"I have a question first." Her voice was flat, professional, and he respected it the way one respects weather. "You told the council in Vell this was a mapping expedition. You told my officers we were securing relics. Now there are harvester engines on the road and your scribes are cutting stanchion-sockets into the mountain's flank. So before I help you take anything, Highness — tell me plainly what we're doing."

A lesser man would have bristled. Corvel had spent fifty years learning that the truth, properly portioned, was the most efficient tool ever forged.

"What we are doing," he said, "is preventing a second ash village. Sit, Captain. Both of you."

He unrolled his own map then — not the council's map, but his, the one drawn from the founders' sealed annexes, the geography of the prison that Vell had been built to administer — and he showed them. The kingdom's extent. The dragon beneath the mountain, vast and dying, its breath the thread that held ten thousand lives together. The failing of the wards. The extinguished stars. He gave them all of it except the parts that were none of their concern: the keeper-bloodlines, the burned pages, the names.

"You've seen the statues," he finished. "You've walked among them. Now you know what killed them, and you know it wasn't malice. It was architecture. The founders built a machine to make the world safe, and the machine is breaking, and when it breaks fully, everything outside it breaks too. Unless we give it a new heart." He laid one gloved finger on the mountain. "That heart is down there. And I am going to bring it home."

Veyra stared at the map for a long moment. He watched her the way he watched all useful instruments — for signs of strain. She was loyal to Vell; he had verified that years ago, in her file, in her record, in the quiet fates of the officers who had tested her. But loyalty to Vell and obedience to the Temple were not the same thing, and she knew it, and lately she had begun to let him see that she knew it.

"And the dragon?" she said. "Does the new heart require the old one to survive?"

Corvel met her eyes and gave her the only answer he had ever considered honest.

"No."

* * *

The girl flinched. He noted it, filed it, did not look at her again. Coral Blackwood was a ledger entry that had grown legs, and ledgers did not get to flinch.

But she surprised him, as her mother had once surprised him, and he found — walking the perimeter at dusk, checking the engine cradles himself because trust was a currency he minted for others and never spent — that she had fallen into step beside him.

"You knew my mother."

It was not a question. Good. Questions invited evasion; statements demanded response.

"I knew her," Corvel said.

"Then you know she didn't run because she was a criminal. She ran because she read the same page I read. The names. The keeper-lines. You've been—"

"I have been *protecting* bloodlines," Corvel said, and stopped walking, and turned to face her fully, because this conversation deserved his full attention and because he had decided, three days ago, that she deserved the truth in the portion she could bear. "Do you imagine we hunted your family, girl? We *kept* them. Every keeper-born child in Vell for three hundred years has been watched, recorded, guided away from the wall, steered into safe trades, married into safe houses. Do you know why? Because the moment any one of you stands in the wrong place, the whole machine turns on — and the machine, as you have now seen with your own educated eyes, is the only thing standing between Vell and the ash." He let a beat pass. "Your mother understood that. She chose not to accept it. That is not the same as being wronged."

"She's dead," Coral said. Her voice did not shake, and he granted her respect for it. "Isn't she. You never wrote her debt as paid because paid debts come off the ledger, and you wanted her on it forever. Where is she?"

Corvel resumed walking. "Dead," he said. "Before you were old enough to remember her face. Not by my hand — by the country's. She came out here, into the dead lands, looking for answers, and the dead lands answered. I recovered her effects." He heard the girl's breath catch and continued, level, almost gentle, because cruelty was wasteful and precision was not. "I have kept them eleven years. Her journal. Her ring. A letter, unfinished, addressed to a daughter she hoped would be born with her eyes." He did not look at Coral. He did not need to. "Help me open the heart-chamber, Coral Blackwood, and you will have all of it. Help me, and your mother's account closes clean. Refuse, and it remains open — and open accounts, in my experience, are the ones that collect."

She left him then, without another word, and Corvel stood a while among the humming engines and allowed himself the smallest satisfaction. He had not lied to her once. That was the discipline his enemies never credited: Corvel almost never lied. Lies required maintenance. Truth, portioned, maintained itself.

* * *

At moonset — though there was no moon now, only the blind black ceiling where stars had been — he gave the order.

Not the assault. The assault could wait for dawn and the diversion he had arranged through Ashvael's own court, a Harvest loyalist with a grudge and a gate-key. What he ordered at moonset was quieter, and it was the order he had crossed the fallen wall intending to give, and every hour of delay had been purchased only to make certain the moment was right.

"Begin the tap," he told Master of Engines Halloway. "Not the draw. The tap."

Halloway's engineers worked through the dark hours with lanterns hooded, boring the pilot channels into the mountain's flank above the vent shafts, sinking the first hair-thin siphon-lines of the six engines into rock that was warm as a living body. By the fourth hour the gauges trembled. By the fifth, the first needle lifted off zero and held, quivering, at a number Halloway read aloud in a hushed voice like a man in church: *point-three drams per hour, steady, live fire confirmed.*

Corvel watched the needle and felt something he almost never permitted himself, which was hope — not the soft hope of the faithful but the hard hope of the engineer, the kind with tolerances and load limits and a maintenance schedule. Point-three drams. Trivial. A taste. But the tap proved the vein, and the vein proved the plan, and the plan, once proven, became inevitable, and Corvel had built his whole life on the principle that inevitability was simply patience wearing armor.

The dragon would die. He had known that from the first page of the founders' annexes, and he had made his peace with it in the same hour, the way a surgeon makes peace with the limb. The creature was dying regardless — the kingdom's own physicians had said so, the regent's own archives confirmed it, the flame guttered lower every season and had for a century. To leave it burning itself out in the dark, alone, feeding a nation that could not survive its death anyway, was sentiment. To take its fire and set it in a vessel built to hold it forever — to give the flame a *cage worthy of it*, a purpose, a permanence the dying beast could never provide — that was mercy of the only kind Corvel recognized: the kind that worked.

And yes. Yes, he would gut it mid-ritual if the ritual faltered. He had already signed the order; it sat in his dispatch case with the black ribbon on it, the contingency no one else had authority to read. If the transfer destabilized, if the flame surged, if the kingdom's defenders broke the bridge and came screaming down the tunnel with their cinder-forged blades — then Halloway would open the main channels wide and take everything at once, and the mountain would go dark, and whatever died down there in the dark would buy Vell another three hundred years of quiet mornings. He had weighed it. He had weighed it honestly, on both pans, the way the founders taught: one dragon against one valley. One life against a hundred thousand. Any judge in Vell would rule as he ruled. Any *honest* one.

The trouble, he thought, watching the eastern black where dawn would eventually arrive, was that honesty had become scarce in this campaign. Osten hedged. The regent schemed. The girl grieved. Somewhere in the tunnels below, an exile named Renn — he had read the file, of course he had read the file; Renn's exile was Ashvael's doing and Ashvael's mistake, and mistakes like that came home — was teaching keeper-blood to want things.

Wanting things. That was the whole disease, Corvel thought, in the end. The founders had understood it. Wanting was the crack through which every flood came. The Covenant was not stone and it was not spellwork; it was a decision, renewed daily for three hundred years, to stop wanting — to be content inside the wall, under the quiet hours, with the wheat and the ledgers and the long safe nights. Everything that had gone wrong since the stars went out had gone wrong because people had started wanting again. The kingdom wanted out. The regent wanted her throne eternal. The girl wanted her mother. Even Osten, even good solid Osten, wanted — he had seen it in her file, in the marginalia of her psychometry — wanted a command of her own, a new order, a name in the histories.

Well. He could supply wants. That was what a high scribe was *for*.

Dawn came grey and sourceless, the way all dawns came now, and with it came the sound he had been waiting for: far off, at the mountain's shoulder, a horn — the Harvest loyalist's signal, the gate opening, the diversion sprung. Brann was at his elbow within moments, sword already drawn, reporting that the kingdom's defenders were shifting strength toward the false attack, that the Glass Bridge's far works were thinning, that the window was open.

Corvel closed his dispatch case. He drew on fresh gloves, white as ledger paper.

"Sound the advance," he said. "All engines forward. And someone fetch me the Blackwood girl." He smiled, briefly, without warmth, at the mountain that glowed like a promise. "It is time she saw where her blood leads."

The column began to move, two hundred chimes ringing on the glass road, and behind them, in the flank of the mountain, six needles trembled and climbed.
