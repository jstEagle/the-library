## 30. Mustering the Cinders

The hall where Renn had once sworn his first oath had not been swept since he left it.

That was the first thing he noticed — before the regent's throne, before the assembled captains, before the faces that turned toward him with expressions ranging from hunger to hatred. It was the dust. Three years of it, lying grey and undisturbed in the hollows between the flagstones, in the seams of the pillars, on the shoulders of the cinder-courtiers who stood in ranks along the walls like a congregation that had forgotten how to kneel. In a kingdom stitched together by a dying dragon's breath, no one wasted fire on brooms.

"Renn of the Veyline." Lady Ashvael's voice carried down the length of the Court of Cinders without effort. She did not rise from the throne; she never rose for anyone. "You were cast out of this hall as a traitor. You return to it as one."

A ripple through the ranks. Somewhere to his left, someone made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"I was cast out for asking questions," Renn said. His voice sounded strange in here — smaller than the mountain, larger than he remembered himself being. "I'm returning with answers. Whether they make me a traitor still is your judgment to make, Regent. Not mine."

He felt Coral's absence beside him like a missing tooth. She had wanted to come. Ashvael's summons had named her twice over — keeper-blood, witness, the woman who'd read the covenant aloud in the archive-crypts until grown soldiers wept — but Coral had chosen instead to spend this night in the deep archives with the dragon's dream-memory coiling around her, learning the shape of the door she meant to open. *Someone has to know exactly what we're fighting for,* she'd said, *not just what we're fighting against.* So Renn stood alone on the worn flagstones where he'd once knelt, and waited for the court to decide what he was worth.

Ashvael studied him. She was older than the last time he'd seen her — everyone was; time moved strangely under the Guttering Mountain, pooling and thinning like the magic in the silver grass outside — but her eyes had not aged a day. They were the eyes of a woman who had spent three centuries governing a country the size of a held breath, and they weighed him now the way she weighed everything: against survival.

"Captain Osten's report reached me an hour ago," she said. "Two hundred Temple chimes on the glass road. Harvest engines. Six siege-needles climbing the mountain's flank." She let the numbers sit in the silence like stones dropped into a well. "And you bring me a plan authored by a debtor scribe from the prison-lockhouse our founders built. Convince my court it isn't madness."

*There it is,* Renn thought. Not *convince me.* Ashvael had read the plan. Ashvael had already decided. This was the older game, the one he'd been exiled for refusing to play: she needed the court to believe it believed.

He turned to face them.

The Court of Cinders held perhaps two hundred souls — which was to say, nearly everything the hidden kingdom had left. He knew most of their names. That was the cruelty of a small nation: exile didn't free you from knowing people. Third row, the scarred woman with the iron-grey braid: Sefa Karren, who had once shared watch-fires with him on the rim-walks and had not spoken to him since the night he questioned the regent's policy of silence. Second row, arms crossed, jaw set like quarried stone: Davor Ilm, commander of the bridge-guard, who had wanted Renn's post and gotten it when Renn was marched out through the ash-tunnels with nothing but his coat and his questions. Front row, closest to the throne, hands folded neatly: Master Corrin, the court archivist, whose signature appeared beneath Renn's sentence of banishment.

"Three centuries ago," Renn said, "our founders made a bargain. A dragon's fire, spent forever to keep us stitched together, and a valley full of strangers raised above us as the lockhouse lid. We told ourselves we were the ones imprisoned. We weren't. We were the ones being kept." He paused. "Last week a scribe from that lockhouse walked into our crypts and read the original covenant aloud. Every clause. Every erased name. The thing we've whispered about for three hundred years, and a twenty-six-year-old outsider read it fluently while our own archivists stood there pretending the script was lost."

"It is not lost," Master Corrin said mildly. "It is restricted. There is a difference, and the difference kept us alive."

"The difference kept us *small.*" Renn let that land. "Corvel is coming up the glass road with two hundred men and six needles, and his orders — I've seen them, Captain Osten risked her commission to copy them — are to open the dragon's chest and take the flame out of it in pieces. He doesn't need the dragon alive. He doesn't need us alive. The moment his harvest begins, the stitching fails, and every soul in this hall unravels back into the world like a cut seam. And his answer to that problem—" Renn held up the copied page, Corvel's own seal glaring white in the ember-light— "is that there won't be an 'us' left to notice."

"So your scribe proposes," Davor Ilm said, and his voice had the flat patience of a man who had rehearsed this objection all day, "that instead of fortifying the heart-chamber — the only defensible ground under this mountain — we abandon it. We leave the door to the dragon's heart *open*, march half our strength up the glass road in the open, and bet everything on a ritual that requires—" he consulted a paper with theatrical distaste— "*a keeper to stand in the threshold indefinitely*. Indefinitely. While a star relights. Assuming stars can be relit. Assuming the dragon survives long enough to try. Captain, I commanded the bridge for three years. Your plan asks me to hand it away."

"Your plan asks two hundred defenders to hold a corridor against two hundred chimes, six needles, and whatever else Corvel brought through the fallen wards," Renn said. "I've fought beside this kingdom's soldiers. I know what they can do. But holding ground isn't the same as winning, and Corvel doesn't have to beat you. He only has to reach the dragon. One harvester engine at the heart-chamber ends everything — the kingdom, the flame, the valley above, all of it. Defending the corridor means betting our existence on zero mistakes. I won't make that bet with other people's lives."

"And hers?" It was Sefa Karren who spoke, and the hall went quiet, because everyone understood the real question underneath. "The Blackwood girl. Your scribe stands in the threshold and holds the door. For how long, Renn? A year? Ten? Forever?" Her grey eyes found him and did not look away. "We watched them do this to the dragon. We watched them chain a living fire to a doorway and call it a covenant. Now your plan asks us to do it to a girl, and you stand there calling it hope."

Renn had known this question was coming. He had rehearsed answers on the walk down from the Grey Orchard — careful answers, diplomatic answers. He found he didn't want any of them.

"She knows," he said. "She read the mechanism herself, in the dragon's own memory. Nobody lied to her, nobody bound her, nobody wrote her name in a ledger and called it duty. She chose it — the way Doss, her friend, chose to share the burden, so that it's a post and not a prison. And if this court can't tell the difference between a covenant written over someone's life and a choice made freely in front of witnesses—" he looked slowly around the hall— "then we deserve Corvel. Because he also believes chains are safety. He just believes they're *his* chains."

Silence. Dust drifted in the ember-light.

It was Ashvael who broke it, rising from the throne for the second time in Renn's living memory. "Master Corrin. Bring me the roll of the Veyline."

The archivist hesitated. "Regent—"

"You signed his banishment. You will sign its reversal. Bring the roll."

Corrin brought it. The parchment was old and soft as moth-wing, and the ink of Renn's sentence had been written in the formal hand reserved for things meant to be permanent. Ashvael took the court's own pen — a shaft of black glass, dragon-bone tipped, older than the kingdom's memory of sky — and drew a single line through Renn's name.

"Renn of the Veyline," she said formally, "is restored to rank and standing. Commander of the Cinders — all forces under the mountain, bridge-guard included." Her eyes flicked to Davor, and something passed between the regent and the bridge-commander that Renn couldn't read and wasn't sure he wanted to. "Commander Ilm will serve as your second, or he will resign his commission tonight. Those are the terms."

Davor's jaw worked. For a long moment Renn watched three years of ambition and resentment fight something older behind the man's eyes — the memory, maybe, of the night the wards fell, when the whole kingdom had heard the held breath release and every soldier on the rim had looked to every other soldier and asked, silently, *now what?*

"I'll serve," Davor said at last, thickly. "But hear me, Renn of the Veyline. If your scribe's gamble kills this kingdom, I want it recorded that I objected."

"Noted," Renn said. "In your own hand, if you please. I've developed a respect for documents lately."

Someone laughed — actually laughed — and it broke something in the room, some knot of dread that had been tightening since the chimes first rang on the glass road. Even Sefa Karren's mouth twitched, which those who knew her understood to be the equivalent of applause.

* * *

They mustered in the Ember Yard, the great vaulted cavern where the kingdom's army had once drilled ten thousand strong and now drilled two thousand — and even two thousand was a generous count, half of them greybeards and greenboys armed with pikes their grandfathers had burned the rust off.

Renn stood on the reviewing stone with the dragon-glass pen's twin — a captain's baton of fused glass, warm to the touch, always faintly warm, the way everything old in this kingdom was faintly warm — and looked out at the ragged truth of the nation he'd come home to.

They were not an army. They were a rumor of an army, three centuries thinned. Farmers with heirloom blades. Bridge-guard in armor patched with spellwork so old the sigils had faded to suggestions. A company of tunnel-runners whose entire military tradition consisted of knowing which passages collapsed. And scattered through the ranks, glinting here and there like embers in ash, the few true soldiers the kingdom had left — veterans of the rim-walks, keepers of grudges and gate-posts, the stubborn residue of a people who had refused, for three hundred years, to stop existing.

Sefa Karren drew up her company in front of him and saluted — a crisp, ironic, perfectly executed salute.

"Commander," she said. "My people have a question before we march."

"Ask it."

"They want to know what we're fighting for. Not against — for. Corvel's easy. Corvel wants to gut the dragon and everyone understands gutting. But your scribe's plan..." Sefa glanced back at her ranks. "Open the door. Let the kingdom out. Walk into the wide world under real stars, if the stars ever come back. Some of them are afraid of that, Renn. Three hundred years under the mountain, and the outside sounds like dying to them."

Renn looked past her at the faces. Young ones mostly, in the front. Old ones behind, holding the young ones steady the way the old always did.

"Tell them about the orchard," he said.

"The petrified one?"

"The trees died the day the wards rose — flash-turned to stone mid-fruit. Three centuries, and the fruit's still hanging. Grey apples on grey branches." He'd walked it a dozen times with Coral; he knew every tree. "But last month, after the wards fell and the magic came back to the grass outside — one branch, low on the southernmost tree, budded. Living wood, inside all that stone. Nobody watered it. Nobody tended it. It just needed the cage gone." He met Sefa's eyes. "Tell them that. Tell them we are not leaving home. We are the bud. Home is coming with us or it isn't home."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then she turned to her company and repeated it, louder, in the parade-ground voice that made it scripture, and Renn watched it move down the ranks like fire moving down a fuse: *we are the bud.*

Davor appeared at his shoulder. "The glass road scouts just came in. Corvel's column has reached the third milestone. He's not hurrying." The bridge-commander's face was grim in the ember-light. "Confident men don't hurry."

"He thinks he's already won," Renn agreed. "Good. Confident men defend their assumptions instead of their flanks." He turned to the yard, raised the glass baton, and pitched his voice to fill the vault. "Companies of the Cinders! Hear your orders!"

Two thousand faces turned to him. Two thousand faces, and somewhere among them, he knew, were people who had cheered his banishment, and people who had wept at it, and people who had simply endured it, the way this kingdom endured everything — quietly, stubbornly, underground.

"In four hours, a man arrives at our door who believes a cage is the highest form of love," Renn said. "He is wrong. Tonight we prove it. First company, with me up the glass road — we meet him in the open, loud and visible, and we make him commit his needles where he thinks we're weak. Second and third companies hold the bridge and give ground slowly, slowly, until his harvest train stretches thin between the milestones. Fourth company goes under — the ash-tunnels, the old ways, the routes only the exiles ever learned." He allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "Because the exiles came home, and we brought our maps."

A sound went through the yard then — not quite a cheer, deeper than a cheer, a sound like a mountain clearing its throat.

"And when it's done," Renn finished, "when the harvester engines are scrap and Corvel's seals are in our hands — we go to the threshold, and we watch a star come back." He lifted the baton, and the ember-light ran along it like a vein of dawn. "For the bud!"

"*For the bud!*" — two thousand voices, ragged and real, and the sound rolled up through the mountain's bones toward the place where a dying dragon dreamed of sky.

* * *

Later — much later, with the first company formed up and the tunnel-runners already vanishing into the dark — Renn climbed alone to the ledge above the Ember Yard where the watchers kept their signal fires, and looked north through the mountain's high vents.

Far off, down the long curve of the glass road, he could see them: pinpricks of light in the dark country, two hundred chimes ringing their cold bell-note ahead of the column, and behind them, vast and patient, the glow of the harvest engines. And higher, on the mountain's flank, the six siege-needles had stopped climbing.

They hung in the dark like drawn arrows.

Renn counted them twice. Six, yes — but the spacing was wrong, clustered too far east, aimed not at the heart-chamber approach but at the ridge above the Grey Orchard. At the tunnels. At the route Coral would take to the threshold when the hour came.

Corvel knew. Somehow, somewhere, someone had talked — and the High Scribe hadn't sent his needles at the army at all.

He'd sent them at the door.

Renn was running for the stairs before the thought finished forming, shouting for Sefa, for Davor, for anyone with fast legs and a fast lantern, and below him the muster-drums began to change their beat — not the marching rhythm they'd practiced, but the older one, the alarm, the one this mountain hadn't heard in three hundred years:

*The enemy is inside the walls.*
