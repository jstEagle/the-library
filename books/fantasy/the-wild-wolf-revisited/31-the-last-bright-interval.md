## 31. The Last Bright Interval

The war-room of Lady Ashvael had emptied an hour past midnight, and still Coral sat at the long table with a candle burning down between her knuckles and a sheet of the kingdom's grey paper before her.

She had started four letters. The first had been to Doss, three lines of reassurance that she did not believe. The second had been to Veyra, a formal note of thanks that sounded, when she read it back, like something written for a funeral. The third she had addressed to no one — just the words *if I don't come back* and then nothing, because everything after that felt like lying. The fourth was the one in front of her now, addressed to the girl who would inherit the pen. She hadn't met her yet. She only knew that somewhere in Vell there was a keeper-born child who deserved better than a ledger with her family's name struck through it in temple ink.

*When this is over,* she wrote, and then stopped, because she could not honestly write *when this is over* about tomorrow. Tomorrow the dragon's breath might gutter out mid-sentence. Tomorrow Corvel's harvesters might crack open the heart-chamber like an egg. Tomorrow she might stand in a threshold made of starlight and never walk out of it.

She dipped the pen again. Her hand was steady. Eleven years of copying debt ledgers taught you steadiness if they taught you nothing else.

*If I do not come back,* she wrote instead, *then someone has to finish the account. Every name. Every debt marked paid. Don't let them round the numbers down. That is the whole of my instruction: do not let anyone round the numbers down.*

It was not much of a letter. But it was honest, and honesty was the only currency she had ever trusted.

Outside the arrow-slit window, the mountain breathed. She could feel it through the stone floor, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her — a slow tidal pull, weaker than it had been a week ago, like a great animal sinking into sleep it would not wake from. Somewhere below, the last dragon-fire burned down toward its final bright interval, and above, past leagues of rock and ash, the sky waited for one star to remember how to burn.

Coral folded the letter, sealed it with wax from Ashvael's own stores, and set it beneath the ledger she carried everywhere now — the true ledger, the covenant, the founding lie of Vell written out in full.

Then she heard footsteps in the corridor, unhurried and familiar, and did not need to turn to know who it was.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," Renn said from the doorway. "Every commander on this mountain gave the same order. I checked."

"So did you," Coral said. "And here you are."

"Here I am." He came into the room the way he always did, quiet as ash falling, and pulled out the chair across from her without asking. He looked tired in the way that stone looks weathered — worn, but not diminished. His wound had closed; the sigils she'd scrawled by candlelight in the orchard had held, though they'd cost her more than she liked to admit. He still moved carefully on his left side, and every time she saw him favor it, something in her chest pulled tight.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she asked.

"I slept for twenty years in exile," he said. "I can spare one more night awake." He nodded at the letters. "Writing your will?"

"Writing instructions." She turned the ledger so he could see its spine. "Whoever survives tells the whole story. That was the promise. I'm making it official. Signed orders." Her mouth curved. "It worked on Corvel, once. Reading the truth at someone until they break."

"It nearly didn't work on him at all."

"No," Coral agreed. "But nearly is more than anything else we've tried."

Renn was quiet for a moment. The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Down in the deep places, the mountain breathed.

"There's a thing my people do," he said finally, awkward, looking at the table instead of her. "Did. Before the wards. On the night before a battle, or a crossing, or any door you might not walk back through. You write what you owe and what you're owed on a strip of ashwood, and you burn it. Both sides. The debts go into the smoke, and the smoke goes up, and whatever happens after — you start clean. No accounts carried into the fire."

"That sounds like something a scribe would invent," Coral said softly.

"It probably was. We stole most of our good ideas from the people we were hiding from." He produced, from inside his coat, two narrow strips of pale wood and a stub of charcoal, and set them on the table between them like an offering. "I've been carrying these since the glass bridge. Kept meaning to give them to you. Kept finding reasons not to."

"What kind of reasons?"

"The cowardly kind." He almost smiled. "Burning a debt means admitting it exists. And there's one I wasn't sure I wanted to put into smoke."

Coral looked at the strips of wood. At his hands resting flat on the table, scarred and steady. At the low light catching the edge of his jaw, the tired line of his mouth, the way he watched her the way he watched ridgelines — as if she might be shelter or ambush, and he had decided, some time ago, that he would take the risk either way.

"Then don't burn that one," she said. "Burn the others. Keep that one in ink."

Renn exhaled, and it seemed to carry weight out of him, weeks of it, all the way back to the first moment on the ridge when he'd seen a valley girl touch a wardstone and not burn. "You always do this," he said. "Find the loophole. Find the third option nobody wrote down."

"I was trained by liars," Coral said. "You learn where the gaps in the rules are."

* * *

They burned their debts in the brazier by the window, one strip at a time.

Renn went first. He wrote quickly, shielding the charcoal with his palm, and fed the wood to the coals without reading it aloud. "Owed to Marta of the ash-tunnels, my silence about her brother," he murmured as it caught. "Owed to old Fen, a debt of bread. Owed to the regent, my father's service, which she never asked me to repay and I never forgave her for that." The flames took each line and gave back nothing but heat. "Owed to the expedition rear guard," he said, quieter. "Four soldiers taken on the ridge because I was watching you instead of watching the road. If any of them died in the cells, that's mine."

"They didn't," Coral said. "Veyra confirmed it. All four alive. Ashvael released them yesterday — they're eating in her kitchens and complaining about the salt."

Something loosened in Renn's shoulders. "Good. Then that one burns easy." He watched the last strip curl to ember. "Your turn."

Coral thought for a long time. The list was longer than she expected.

She wrote: *owed to Doss, eleven years of friendship underpriced.* She wrote: *owed to the village beyond the wall, whose ash I walked through and grieved too late.* She wrote: *owed to my mother, whose face I don't remember, whose flight bought mine.* Each strip went into the fire and she watched the words blacken and lift, and it should have felt like loss, and instead it felt like setting down loads she had forgotten she was carrying.

Last of all she wrote one more line, hesitated, and held the strip over the coals without letting go.

"What is it?" Renn asked.

"A debt owed to myself," she said. "Eleven years of my life, sold to a temple to pay for a crime I never committed, in a prison I was born inside of. I owe that girl the life she would have had." Her fingers tightened on the wood. "And I can't pay it. No victory tomorrow pays it. Even if we win everything, even if the stars come back — that ledger stays unbalanced forever."

Renn reached across the table and closed his hand over hers, over the burning strip, close enough that she felt the heat through both their skins.

"Then don't balance it," he said. "Carry it. Some debts aren't meant to be paid. They're meant to be remembered. That's what a scribe is for, isn't it? Not erasing the numbers. Keeping them."

Coral looked at their hands, at the fire eating the edges of the ashwood, at the man who had crossed a kingdom's worth of silence to stand beside her in a room that smelled of wax and smoke.

"Yes," she said. "That's exactly what a scribe is for."

She let the strip fall, and watched her grief go up in smoke, and found — strangely — that it rose rather than fell, like something freed.

* * *

Later, when the brazier had burned to orange memory and the corridors had gone silent, they walked the high gallery together. Neither suggested sleep. There would be time enough for sleep, or there wouldn't, and neither of them wanted to spend what might be the last ordinary hours apart.

The gallery ran along the mountainside behind screens of carved stone, and through the gaps Vell was visible far below — the valley, small and lamplit, unaware under a starless sky. From here Coral could trace the line of the fallen ward-wall, a pale seam across the dark land, broken in a dozen places where the expedition and the Temple forces had poured through.

"Do you think they can see us?" she asked. "From down there. Do you think anyone in Vell ever looked up at this mountain and wondered?"

"Your priests knew," Renn said. "They built the lockhouse facing it. They just built the forgetting deeper than the wondering."

"My mother wondered." Coral's breath fogged the cold air. "She got out. Through what — a crack in the wards, a bribed gatekeeper, I'll never know. She ran far enough to have me, and then the Temple found her anyway, or she died running from them, and I grew up in the prison she escaped and called it home." She paused. "I used to lie awake counting the eleven years left on my indenture. Eleven years, and then freedom. I thought freedom was a date on a calendar."

"And now?"

"Now I think freedom is a door," she said, "and doors need people willing to stand in them."

Renn stopped walking. In the dim light filtering through the stone screens his face was half shadow, half ember-glow, and he looked at her with an expression she had seen on him only once before — in the grey orchard, when she'd told him about the ledgers, and he had said *you counted everything except yourself*, and she had not been able to answer.

"Coral." He said her name the way he said few things: plainly, without armor. "Tomorrow you're going to stand in a threshold that wants to keep you. Everyone knows it. Ashvael knows it. Veyra knows it. I know it. You know it better than any of us." His voice roughened. "And I have spent every hour since the regent's gambit trying to find a plan where I go with you, or you don't go at all, or the mountain falls on us all and none of it matters. There isn't one. There's no clever third option. You walk into the door, and I stay outside it, and one of those facts may be permanent."

"Yes," Coral said quietly.

"So before that — before the harvester engines and the glass bridge and whatever Corvel has left in him — I'm done being careful. I've been careful my whole life. Careful is why I was exiled, careful is why I survived, careful is why I followed a stranger through ash-country because she touched a stone and the stone answered." He took both her hands. His were warm. "I love you. I've loved you since the orchard, since before the orchard, since the ridge, maybe since the first time I saw you read a dead language like it was water and I thought — there. There is someone the world hasn't managed to make small."

The mountain breathed beneath them. Far away, a sentry's horn sounded the second watch, thin and clear.

"I love you," Coral said, and the words came easier than any she had ever copied, as if they had been waiting eleven years in some inner ledger for exactly this column, this page, this candle. "Not despite the door. With it. Whatever tomorrow does with us — it doesn't get to undo tonight."

Renn laughed, low and unsteady, and pressed his forehead to hers. "The court would call this treason against good sense."

"The court can add it to my file," Coral said. "I'm told I'm due a very thorough accounting someday."

* * *

Near dawn, they returned to the war-room to find it not empty after all. Veyra Osten stood at the map table with a candle and a stack of dispatches, and Captain Doss — her Doss, her friend, arrived through the broken wards with a caravan of valley folk seeking truth — sat nearby sharpening a knife with the grim calm of a person who had walked out of a prison to walk into a battle and saw nothing contradictory in it.

Doss looked up first. Her eyes went from Coral's face to Renn's hand still brushing Coral's shoulder, and she raised one eyebrow with devastating precision.

"Don't," Coral said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to say something about wanting things."

"I was about to say," Doss replied mildly, "that it's about time, and that I told you so, and that if either of you dies tomorrow I will haunt you personally, and I have references."

Veyra cleared her throat and slid a paper across the table. "Before the sentimentality resumes — this came up from the lower galleries an hour ago. Scout report." Her voice was level, but Coral had learned to hear the tension underneath it, the captain's version of a held breath. "Corvel's harvest crews have moved early. They're not waiting for dawn. They're cutting the approach tunnel to the heart-chamber right now, and the resonance readings say—" she glanced at the report, and for the first time since Coral had known her, Captain Osten's composure flickered, "—they say the flame's gone unstable. The dragon knows. It's fighting, or grieving, or both. We don't have a day. We may not even have the morning."

Silence settled over the room like ash.

Coral looked at Renn. Renn looked at the map, at the thin inked line of tunnels leading down to the chamber where a dying god kept the world stitched together. Outside the stone screens, the false night pressed against the mountain, starless, waiting.

"Well," Coral said, and heard her own voice come out steady, scribe-steady, ledger-steady. "Then I suppose the bright interval is over."

Renn squeezed her hand once, hard, and reached for his sword.
