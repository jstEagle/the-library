## 32. Storming the Heart-Chamber

Veyra Osten had commanded soldiers for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned one law above every other: a battle is won or lost before the first blade is drawn. It is won in the order of march, in the placement of archers, in the lie told well enough to make an enemy commit. The High Scribe believed he had lied to her. He had never understood that she was lying to him back.

She stood now at the head of two hundred men on the glass road, three hundred paces from the harvest camp at the mountain's foot, and she gave the signal they had agreed on — not a horn, not a flag, but the simple act of removing her gauntlet and letting it fall.

Two hundred gauntlets hit the ground together.

The sound carried down the line like the first drop of a storm breaking. Veyra watched her soldiers turn — smoothly, without hesitation, the way men move when they have rehearsed a thing until it lives in their bones — and level their weapons at the Temple force arrayed behind them. The strike company of the Order of Quiet Hours, sixty scribes-in-arms and four hundred hired blades, suddenly found themselves looking down the length of spears they had marched beside all morning.

"Captain Osten." The voice came through the ranks, dry as old vellum. High Scribe Corvel walked forward alone, his white robes unstained by ash, his hands folded in their sleeves as though the world were a library he was merely passing through. "This is insubordination."

"This is testimony," Veyra said. She held up the leather case in her left hand. Inside were his own sealed orders — the true ones, the ones that said *harvest by vivisection*, *the dragon need not survive the extraction*, *expendable personnel may be written off as losses*. "Every word you gave me in secret, Corvel. Every order you thought I'd burn after reading. My quartermaster copied them last night. There are forty copies. By tomorrow there will be four hundred."

Something moved behind the High Scribe's eyes — not fear, Veyra noted. Recalculation. That was worse.

"You misunderstand your position," he said gently, and Veyra felt the air change. The hair on her arms lifted. Along the Temple lines, the scribes-in-arms began to murmur, and the murmuring wasn't words — it was cadence, the rhythm of spellcraft being spoken in unison. "You are standing inside my operation, captain. You always have been."

The first ward-spear struck the ground twenty feet from her and detonated into a cage of white light.

"SHIELDS!" Veyra roared, and the world went to noise.

* * *

Renn felt the assault begin through the soles of his feet before he heard it — the mountain itself flinching, a shudder running up through the fused glass of the bridge like pain through a nerve. Below him, spread across the ember-road, the allied force was moving: Ashvael's defenders in their grey-and-cinder colors, three hundred strong, most of them holding weapons older than their grandfathers, advancing on the harvest camp from the ridge while Veyra's turned column tore into the Temple flank.

He was not with either group. He had a different job, and it was the job nobody wanted, which is how he'd known it would be his.

"Bridge team, with me." He said it quietly, because the six fighters crouched around him in the shadow of the buttress didn't need shouting at. They needed the opposite. "We go under, not over. The harvester engines are anchored in the rock beneath the causeway. If we take the span, they just cut the anchor-lines and float new engines over. We take the *roots*."

"And if the roots are guarded?" asked Sefa, a broad woman with a mason's forearms who had spent forty years carving living stone and had volunteered within the hour of the regent's summons.

"They will be," Renn said. "Guarded well. Guarded by people Sefa trained, probably." He looked around at their faces — young, mostly, faces he half-knew from a kingdom that had forgotten it existed, people who had spent their whole lives under a guttering sky being told the dark was safety. "So we don't kill them if we can help it. We break engines. We break chains. We break *certainty*. Everything else can be repaired."

He drew his knife — the old one, the exile's knife, notched and ugly — and led them down into the throat of the mountain.

* * *

Coral Blackwood ran.

Not toward the battle — away from its center, along the path she and Renn had memorized in the grey orchard's long hiding, the winding route through petrified trees to the secondary entrance of the heart-chamber approach. Behind her the sky over the mountain was strobing white and orange, ward-light against dragon-fire reflected off low cloud, and the thunder of colliding magics rolled across the dead country in waves she could feel in her teeth.

Her job was the door. Their whole plan hung on the door. And the door required her blood, her hand, her name spoken into the threshold — and it required her alive, which was rapidly becoming the difficult part.

A ward-spear screamed overhead and detonated against a petrified apple tree, shearing it into powder. Coral threw herself flat among the grey grass, ears ringing, and lay still until the echo died. When she raised her head, she saw the battlefield laid out below her in terrible clarity: Veyra's line buckling and reforming like a living thing; the Temple's white-robed scribes hurling light that burned holes in the night; and beyond them all, at the mountain's base, the harvest camp — the great brass-and-bone machines, the harvester engines, drinking greedily at the veins of fire that fed up out of the rock. Even from here she could see the dragon's glow dimming where the engines drank, could feel the world going thin at the edges, thread by stolen thread.

And she saw something else. A knot of figures breaking from the harvest camp's center, moving fast and low, not toward the fighting but *around* it — toward the eastern path. Toward her path. Toward the heart-chamber.

They were wearing Temple white. And at their head walked a tall figure whose robes did not stir in the wind, whose stride never varied, whom Coral knew the way you know a scar.

Corvel hadn't stayed to command his army. He had seen the trap closing and simply stepped sideways out of it, taking his best people with him, going for the thing that actually mattered.

"Of course," Coral whispered. "You were never fighting for the flame. You were fighting for the *door*."

She got up and ran harder.

* * *

Under the causeway, the world was red.

Light bled down through the translucent glass of the bridge in long trembling shafts, and beneath it the rock was veined with fire — real fire, dragon-fire, pulsing slow and vast like a heartbeat the size of a country. The harvester engines sat astride the greatest of these veins, six of them, squat brass spiders with limbs of bone-drill sunk deep into the living stone. Each pulse of the vein passed up through the drills, into the engines' hearts, and out along cable-roads toward the camp — siphoned, filtered, *owned*. Three centuries of caged fire, and now someone had built taps.

"By the stars," Sefa breathed. "They've got it *plumbed*."

"Break the plumbing first." Renn pointed to the junction where the cable-roads met the engines — a manifold of glass and gold, humming with contained power. "Sefa, Maret, the manifolds. The rest of you, the drill-limbs. Anything that touches the vein, sever it. And whatever happens—"

He never finished, because the guards came out of the dark exactly as he'd predicted, and they were very good.

They were kingdom-folk, Renn realized even as the first crossbow bolt hummed past his ear. Harvest loyalists — his old comrades, the ones who had chosen Lady Ashvael's court faction or, worse, chosen coin. Eight of them dropping from scaffolds with the silent economy of people who had trained in these tunnels their whole lives. The fight collapsed into a close, brutal, echoing brawl of steel and spellwork, sparks skittering across wet stone, and Renn found himself face to face with a man he had once called brother.

"Dain."

"Exile." Dain's sword came at him in the old pattern, the one they'd drilled together a thousand times under a starless sky. Renn knew every beat of it, and that was the tragedy — he parried without thinking, muscle answering muscle, two halves of the same school trying to kill each other in the red dark. "You bring valley-rats and a *scribe* into our house. You bring the people who built the prison."

"The Temple built the prison," Renn snarled, locking blades. "I'm bringing the people tearing it down. Open your eyes, Dain. Look at what those engines are doing. They're not freeing anything. They're *draining* her. The dragon is dying faster tonight than in three hundred years—"

"She's dying anyway!" For one moment Dain's mask cracked, and underneath it Renn saw not cruelty but grief, vast and shapeless. "Everything dies! The flame, the kingdom, us. At least the harvest means some of us *survive* somewhere else. At least it buys a future—"

"It buys a future for whoever holds the tap," Renn said. "That's all it has ever bought. Ask the Temple. Ask them why their valley ate our sky."

Dain hesitated. It was half a heartbeat, no more — but half a heartbeat is a lifetime in a knife-fight, and Renn used it. Not to kill. He swept Dain's legs, put his knife at the hollow of his old friend's throat, and held him there panting while around them the rest of the guard detail surrendered one by one to Sefa's stone-hard grip and the simple arithmetic of being outnumbered.

"Bind them," Renn said hoarsely. "Gag the loud ones. And somebody start on those manifolds — *now*."

Sefa was already moving. She set her hands against the great glass manifold like a physician taking a pulse, read the flow of it — Renn saw her lips move, counting pressures the way Coral counted words — and then drove both fists, wrapped in stone-hardening sigils glowing dull amber, into the central junction.

The manifold burst. Cable-roads whipped loose like severed arteries, venting plumes of raw fire that scorched the ceiling black. One by one the six engines shuddered, choked, and went dark, their bone-drills slackening in the vein, and Renn felt the change come up through the floor — the heartbeat below strengthening, one great pulse, then another, the dragon's blood flowing free for the first time since the harvest began.

Above them, along the whole length of the glass bridge, the light turned from harvest-gold to living crimson, and the defenders on the span raised a cheer that shook dust from the tunnel roof.

Renn allowed himself three seconds of it. Then he grabbed Sefa's shoulder.

"The heart-chamber approach," he said. "Coral's path. If Corvel split off from the battle—"

Sefa's face told him she'd already done the arithmetic.

* * *

On the glass bridge, Captain Veyra Osten fought in the old style — economically, angrily, with a soldier's contempt for waste. Her shield-line had bent nearly double under the Temple's opening barrage and then, impossibly, held; her people had drilled for this in secret for eleven days, turning and countering, and now the surprise was spending itself and the numbers were beginning to speak. Four hundred hired blades against two hundred professionals with the high ground and a cause for each man to hold in his fist. The hired blades were already looking for the exits.

"Scribes-in-arms on the left! Ward-speakers!" bellowed Sergeant Ilka, spitting blood. "They're building something big!"

"I can see it." Veyra could indeed: at the rear of the Temple line, twelve white-robed figures had linked hands and begun to raise a lattice of light between themselves and the mountain — not an attack. A wall. Or a cage. Corvel's contingency, meant to seal the whole chamber complex against everyone, friend and foe alike, if the day went wrong. A warden's solution. Burn it all down rather than let anyone out.

Veyra looked at her soldiers. Tired, bleeding, triumphant, holding a bridge made of frozen lightning above a dying god's heartbeat. Then she looked at the lattice growing in the distance, patient and white and final.

"Ilka," she said. "How much do you trust me?"

"With my life, captain. Already given it twice tonight."

"Then get me every ward-breaker we have left. We're going to crack that lattice before it closes. And send runners — the regent's people on the far span, and anyone who can run fast enough to find a scribe named Coral Blackwood." She paused, and heard herself say the next words aloud, and found they cost nothing at all, which was perhaps the strangest part of this entire impossible night. "Tell her the High Scribe is heading for the heart-chamber. Tell her she needs to get there *first*."

* * *

Coral reached the eastern entrance as the first of Corvel's escort reached it too, and for one suspended moment the two groups simply stared at each other across fifty feet of smoking corridor — her, gasping, alone, armed with a scribe's pen and a head full of other people's secrets; them, twelve strong, professional, and between her and the threshold of everything.

Then the corridor behind the Temple escort erupted.

Grey-and-cinder defenders poured out of a maintenance shaft Sefa had known about since childhood, and Renn came through the smoke like something the mountain itself had coughed up — bleeding from a gash along his jaw, eyes wild, knife already red. "CORAL! RUN FOR THE DOOR!"

She ran. Behind her the corridor became chaos — shouts, the crack of sigil-fire, Dain's voice screaming something about traitors, Sefa laughing like a landslide. Coral didn't look back. She ran through the heat, through the drumming pulse of the freed vein, through corridors that glowed brighter with every step as the dragon's strength returned, until the passage opened and she stood where no keeper-blood had stood in three hundred years.

The heart-chamber threshold.

It was smaller than she had imagined — a simple arch of black stone set into the living rock, carved with the old script, the covenant-script, the letters she had once been beaten for reading. Beyond it, vast and dim and breathing, the chamber of the dragon opened out like the inside of a cathedral built from ribs and dusk. And the arch itself was warm. It had been waiting. She could feel that, the way she had felt the ward-wall answer her touch a hundred years and eleven chapters ago: something on the other side of the stone, aware of her, patient beyond all human patience.

*Coral Blackwood,* the darkness seemed to say. *Keeper's daughter. You came.*

Behind her, down the corridor, the sounds of battle were changing — thinning, scattering, going wrong. She heard Renn shout her name again, farther away now, and heard another voice answer him, a voice like dry vellum, calm as a ledger line:

"Let them through. Let them all watch. It changes nothing."

Coral turned. At the far end of the corridor, walking unhurried through the smoke with his hands folded in his sleeves, came the High Scribe Corvel — past his own fallen escort, past Renn held struggling between two of his remaining guards, past Veyra's arriving soldiers who hesitated at the sight of him as though unsure whether the war had ended or only changed rooms.

His eyes found hers down the whole length of the burning corridor, and he smiled the smile of a man opening a book to a page he had marked long ago.

"Now then, Mistress Blackwood," he said. "Shall we discuss what your mother and I failed to finish?"
