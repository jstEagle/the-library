## 37. Exodus

Veyra Osten had commanded soldiers through sieges, through blizzards, through the kind of nights where you counted your living by torchlight and stopped counting at forty. She had never once led anyone through a door.

And yet here it was, hanging in the throat of the mountain like a wound in the world: a doorway of standing air, tall as a watchtower and thin as a held breath. Through its frame she could see the valley of Vell — her valley, the one she'd crossed out of under orders — but changed beyond recognition. Real stars burned above it now, a whole sky of them, wheeling slow and silver over the ward-wall's broken crown. The light they cast down through the door fell across the glass road in long pale bars, and every person who stepped into that light paused, just for a moment, as if crossing a threshold made of water.

They had been pausing all day.

"Next family," Veyra said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat. "Names."

The man before her was ash-grey to the elbow, an old farmer from the terraces below the mountain, and he held his daughter's hand so tightly the child was wincing. He gave his name — Hallen Vos, and his daughter Sefa, and his wife behind him, Maret — and Veyra wrote them into the ledger with the stub of pencil she'd taken to carrying everywhere, because ink froze on this side of the door and pencils did not.

"Hallen Vos," she repeated. "Farmer?"

"Orchardist." His voice cracked on the word. "My trees are stone, Captain. My grandfather's trees were stone. Whatever's on the other side—" He looked through the doorway at the stars, and something in his face folded and unfolded. "Whatever's there, there'll be soil."

"There will be soil," Veyra said. "Go."

The three of them stepped through together, and for one heartbeat their outlines shimmered like heat-haze, and then they were simply people walking away down a moonlit hillside in the valley of Vell, small against the enormous sky, and the daughter turned once to wave at nothing at all.

* * *

By the third day, the queue stretched back through the ash-tunnels, past the grey orchard, down the ember road itself — a line of the nameless kingdom's people four miles long, wrapped in every scrap of cloth they owned, carrying what remained of three hundred years of hidden life: seed-sacks and loom-weights, copper pots, the petrified branch of an orchard tree borne like a relic, one entire doorframe pried from a house so someone's grandmother could walk through her own door one last time before leaving it forever.

Veyra had not asked for this command. That was the joke of it, the bitter joke she kept telling herself at night when sleep wouldn't come: she had spent her whole career following orders written by other people, and now eight thousand people were waiting for hers.

It had happened on the second day, in the regent's war-room, when Lady Ashvael had summoned the remnants of her court and the leaders of the crossing and told them, flatly, that she would not be going.

"The kingdom isn't the people," Ashvael had said. She stood at the head of the table in her cinder-grey robes, and she looked older than she had a week ago — older, and lighter, as if some weight had been set down and she was still learning how to stand without it. "The kingdom is the flame, and the flame is dying, and I have spent thirty years pretending otherwise. A regent who leads her people into exile is no longer a regent. She's a refugee with good posture." Someone had started to protest and she'd raised one hand, and the room had gone quiet the way rooms go quiet around old power even after the power has decided to leave. "I will be the last one through, if I go at all. What I will do first is make sure the record survives. Coral Blackwood has my full archive. Every treaty, every lie, every name we erased and every name we saved. It goes with her. The truth travels first; everything else can follow."

Then she'd looked at Veyra. Just looked, the way she did — that flat, weighing stare that had unnerved Veyra since the day they met.

"You crossed the wall with twenty soldiers and secret orders," Ashvael said. "You discovered your Temple's lies and chose, publicly, at gunpoint, to stand against them. And you keep lists." A ghost of dry humor. "Captain Osten, my people need someone who keeps lists. Will you lead them out?"

Veyra had opened her mouth to say that she was a soldier, not a shepherd, and what had come out instead was: "Yes."

She still didn't entirely know why. Except that she did. Because someone had to count the living, and Veyra had spent her whole life being the one who counted.

* * *

The problems were endless, and she catalogued them nightly in her ledger the way she used to catalogue ammunition.

Food. The kingdom's granaries held perhaps six weeks of stores for eight thousand people, and the valley of Vell — emptied of its own harvest by panic and temple requisition — could feed maybe half that number until spring. She had sent runners through the fallen wards on the first day with sealed letters to every garrison commander she trusted, and already two companies from the river forts had declared for her, marching toward Vell with supply wagons and, more importantly, with legitimacy. Soldiers followed Veyra Osten. It was the one thing she'd never doubted, and it turned out to be the thing that mattered most.

Shelter. Three centuries of Vell's border villages stood empty along the wall — built, Coral had explained with terrible clarity, to house the wardens' descendants without knowing it — and now those villages filled with the kingdom's people, who walked through them touching the walls and weeping, because these were the houses their great-grandparents' names had been struck from.

And then there was the Temple.

The Order of Quiet Hours had not surrendered; Orders like that did not surrender, they retreated and reformed. Corvel was dead or broken — the accounts differed, and Veyra believed neither — but the Temple's valley chapters still held grain, gold, and a thousand years of institutional habit. Her scouts reported priests at the ward-wall ruins every dawn, reading from books, watching the star-door burn in the mountain's throat. Waiting. Deciding whether the exodus was heresy or scripture.

Veyra had made a decision about that, too. She'd sent them a copy of Coral's ledger — the founding covenant, the keeper-blood registries, Corvel's own signed orders — with a single line added in her own hand:

*Read it aloud in your chapter-house. If your faith survives the reading, send bread.*

The bread had started arriving on the fourth day. Not much. But it arrived.

* * *

On the fifth evening, Renn found her at the threshold.

He walked up the glass road with a limp he still tried to hide and a sling he'd mostly stopped needing, and he sat down beside her on a block of fused stone without asking, which was how she knew the news was complicated.

"Coral says goodbye tomorrow," he said.

"I know. Doss told me." Veyra wrote one last line, capped her pencil. "Two keepers instead of one. Half a prison each."

"He hates it. The waiting." Renn turned his face toward the door, and the starlight coming through it caught the new scar along his jaw. "But he volunteered anyway. Walked up to Coral and said, *you carried it alone long enough; nobody should hold a door alone.*" He shook his head slowly. "I've known soldiers with less courage and better sense."

"And Coral?"

"Gone quiet. The way she goes quiet when she's copying something important." Renn smiled faintly. "She's writing the last pages tonight. Says the ledger has to be finished before she steps through, or it isn't finished at all."

Veyra nodded. Below them, the line of exiles moved in silence, family by family, and the stars wheeled overhead through the open doorway, and somewhere in the dark behind them a dragon's fire was guttering down to its final embers, feeding itself into the frame of a door that would close when the last of it burned.

"How many left?" Renn asked.

Veyra consulted her tally. "Nine hundred and twelve. We finish tomorrow, if the weather holds and the Temple holds off."

"And if the Temple doesn't hold off?"

"Then we finish anyway," Veyra said, "faster."

Renn huffed something that might have been a laugh. Then he grew serious, and Veyra felt the change in him before he spoke, the way you feel weather turn. "There's a thing I came to say. On behalf of the people." He hesitated — Renn, who never hesitated. "They want you to know that they know what it cost you. Your commission. Your order. Everything you were inside the Temple's machine. You're not one of us, and you led us out anyway, and—" He stopped, jaw working. "They voted. Last night, by firelight. All nine hundred and twelve, plus everyone who's already gone through. They voted that the crossing has a name, and the crossing needs a leader, and the leader should be elected. Not crowned. Elected." He looked at her straight on. "You. Unanimous. Even Ashvael raised her hand, and she's never raised her hand for anything."

Veyra sat very still.

In her chest, something old and rigid — the thing that had stood at attention through twenty years of service, through secret orders and sealed lies, through the night she'd chosen treason with her eyes open — that thing finally, quietly, stood down.

"Elected," she said.

"Elected." Renn's grin broke through at last. "No crown. No regency. Just a captain with a list."

"A list of nine hundred and twelve names," Veyra corrected. "And I intend to check off every single one."

* * *

The last crossing began at dawn.

Veyra stood at the threshold as the morning came through from the other side — real morning, valley morning, gold slanting down the broken hills — and called the families forward by name, one by one, ticking her ledger. The old orchardist Hallen had gone on the first day; now came the glassworkers and the singers, the archive-clerks carrying their crates of rescued memory, the last of the ash-tunnel children born underground who had never seen an unroofed sky until this week and could not stop looking up.

Lady Ashvael came near the end.

She wore no regalia. She had burned the cinder-grey robes two days ago, publicly, in a brazier on the ember road, and stood now in plain travel wool like any grandmother on any road. At the threshold she paused, as everyone paused, in that bar of star-and-morning light.

"History will call this the Exodus," she said to Veyra, without turning. "It will get the details wrong. It always does. Make sure it gets the names right, Captain."

"I keep lists," Veyra said.

"So you do." Ashvael stepped through — and did not look back, and Veyra understood that this, too, was a kind of abdication: the regent refusing even the dignity of a final glance, giving the future her back so it would stop expecting anything of her.

Then only the rear guard remained, and Renn, and the two figures at the doorway itself: Doss, planted in the threshold like a fencepost with opinions, and Coral beside him, a finished ledger under her arm and her scribe's pen tucked behind her ear.

Coral looked at Veyra across the last ten feet of the kingdom, and Veyra saw her own decision reflected there — justice done outside the law, debts written paid — and Coral smiled, small and certain, and mouthed one word: *through*.

The dragon-fire flickered low in the doorframe. The stars above Vell wheeled on. Nine hundred and twelve names stood checked, save the ones still holding the door.

Veyra closed the ledger, squared it under her arm, and stepped through into the morning — into a world where magic must survive without a cage, and where, somewhere ahead, a nation was learning to walk out of myth and into history.

Behind her, faintly, she heard Coral begin to read the closing account aloud — and the first word was a name.
