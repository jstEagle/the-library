## 1. The Valley That Forgets

There are three rules to living in Vaelmoor, and my grandmother taught me all of them before I was old enough to be dangerous.

One: the valley forgets. Whatever happens here—every birth, every quarrel, every sin—stays only as long as the teller does. The ward on the border stones sees to that. Magic doesn't cross into Vaelmoor, and neither, mostly, does news.

Two: never let them see your hands shake.

Three—and this is the one that matters—never, ever speak to an animal where someone might hear you.

I was thinking about rule three the morning I knelt in the bracken behind the shepherd's hut with a wounded wolf's head cradled in my lap, telling it, in a low voice I'd spent twenty-two years perfecting, that it was going to live whether it liked it or not.

"You'll forgive me," I murmured, working the last of the moss poultice under the torn skin at its flank. "But you're not dying in my valley. I have enough trouble."

The wolf watched me with amber eyes that did not blink nearly as often as a wolf's should. It had dragged itself up from the southern pass three nights ago with an arrowhead lodged under its ribs—a hunting arrow, fletched in grey goose-feather, which meant men from beyond the stones. Men who hunted things worth shooting from a distance.

"Drink," I told it, tipping the water gourd. "You're worse than Da when he's ill. At least he thanks me."

It drank. And then—it had done this before, but it still made the small hairs on my arms rise—it pressed its forehead briefly against my wrist. Not a bite. Not a warning. Something closer to what a person does when they mean *thank you* and their mouth can't manage it.

I talked to animals the way other people breathe. I always had. The chickens of Vaelmoor knew my opinions on their laying habits; the mule trains that came down from the high passes knew I disapproved of their drivers' whip-work; once, memorably, a bull named Old Bram had refused to be sold to a drover until I'd had a quiet word with him about the merits of a shorter journey. Nobody in the village ever noticed. In a magic-dead valley, a girl murmuring nonsense at livestock is just a girl murmuring nonsense at livestock. My strangeness wore camouflage. That was the whole genius of Vaelmoor: inside the ward, nothing I said could actually *be* anything. Words were just words. A chicken couldn't understand me any better than it understood the weather.

Except that this wolf understood me perfectly, and wolves were not supposed to be in Vaelmoor at all, because wild things felt the ward like a hand pressing down on the back of the neck and turned away at the border stones.

Which meant this wolf hadn't turned away.

Which meant either the ward was failing, or the wolf was something else entirely, or—I pushed the thought down where I kept all the thoughts I couldn't afford—both.

I bandaged him, fed him the liver I'd smuggled out under my shawl, and told him to stay hidden until moonrise. He made a sound deep in his chest that I chose to interpret as agreement, though honestly, with this one, interpretation felt less like a skill and more like a negotiation.

His name, when he finally gave it to me—which is not how naming works with people, but is exactly how it works with him—was Fen.

* * *

The patrol came at midday, riding up the cart-road through the barley in a line so straight it looked rehearsed, because it was.

Six riders. Grey cloaks, iron-grey horses, and the kind of posture that comes from being paid to sit upright. At their center rode a man in a coat the color of wet slate with a chain of dull silver links across his chest, and I knew what he was before I knew his name, the way you know a storm by the smell of it.

An inquisitor. In Vaelmoor.

My grandmother would have called it impossible. But my grandmother had also said the ward would hold forever, and lately the geese had been leaving the valley in ragged, honking flocks without any reason the villagers could name, and the barley near the southern stones had come up in rows that spelled nothing good—or maybe spelled something, if you knew how to read grain, which I very much hoped no one did.

I was in the dooryard of the shepherd's hut, ostensibly sorting wool, when they reined in. Tam, the shepherd's boy, gawped from the fence. I kept my eyes on the fleece in my hands and my heartbeat somewhere around my boots.

The inquisitor swung down. He had a long face and a voice pitched for large rooms, and he smiled the way a ledger balances: precisely, and without warmth.

"Good folk of Vaelmoor." He said the valley's name like he was tasting it for rot. "I am Brother Cassian, servant of His Majesty King Aldric, blessed in iron and law." Behind him, his men sat their horses with the loose readiness of people hoping for a reason. "We seek a criminal. Perhaps several."

Tam found his voice first. "Criminal, sir? We've no thieves here."

"Not thieves." Cassian drew a folded paper from his coat and unfolded it with ceremony, though I suspected he knew it by heart. "Beast-speakers. Abominations who whisper to animals in the manner of the old gods' covenant—traitors to the crown, enemies of the natural order of man. The law is clear. Such creatures are to be reported and delivered to the king's justice." He paused, letting the words settle over the yard like ash. "Harboring one is death. Speaking to beasts is death. *Hearing* one speak—" another pause, delicate as a knife laid flat— "is also death, though we are merciful about the order of operations."

Somewhere behind me, a chicken clucked, entirely ignorant of how relevant her species had suddenly become.

The villagers murmured. Old Marta crossed herself with the wrong god's sign, caught herself, and did it again properly. The shepherd, Tam's father, shifted from foot to foot. And I stood there with wool in my hands and my face arranged into the pleasant blankness of a woman thinking about wool, while every rule my grandmother ever taught me screamed at once.

They had come *here*. To the valley that forgets. Which meant the valley had stopped forgetting, or someone outside had remembered us, and either possibility was a door I didn't want opened.

"You flatter us, brother," the shepherd managed. "We've hardly beasts enough to speak *to*, let alone folk mad enough to try."

"Oh, I don't think anyone here is mad." Cassian smiled again, and this time it reached his pale eyes, which was worse. "Madness is honest. This is craftier than madness." He began walking along the fence line, unhurried, trailing one gloved finger along the rail. "You'd be surprised how they hide. They look like anyone. They tend sheep, mend roofs, mind their tongues." The finger stopped. He leaned toward a brown hen scratching in the yard and regarded it with scholarly interest. "Though the animals know, you see. That's the tell. Beasts grow calm around them. Too calm. Trusting." He glanced back at the villagers, mild as milk. "Watch your animals, good folk. If a beast seems unnaturally fond of someone—report it."

Every eye in the yard slid, for half a heartbeat, toward me.

Not at me. Toward me. Because everyone in Vaelmoor knew that Noor Kavanagh could gentle anything with four legs, that frightened foals went quiet for her, that even the miller's vicious gander tolerated her presence. They'd laughed about it for years. *The Kavanagh knack.* Harmless. Charming. A party trick at harvest festivals.

A knack. That was all. That was what I needed them to remember it was.

"Horses like me," I said, and heard my own voice come out steady, bored, almost insulted. "Always have. It's the way you stand. Anyone can learn it."

Cassian looked at me then—really looked, the way a man reads a page he intends to correct—and I gave him back the flattest, most provincial stare I owned. Inside, my heart was hammering out something frantic, and I thought, absurdly, *if he asks the hen, she'll tell him everything.*

He didn't ask the hen. He asked the village instead: names, households, who lived alone, who kept odd hours. And then he announced that he and his men would ride the valley's length over the next two days, speaking to each farmstead, inspecting each byre, and that any beast found to be "unnaturally tractable" would be confiscated for examination.

Examination. Of the animals. As if the animals were the suspects.

As we dispersed, Tam fell into step beside me, white-faced. "Noor. Your flock—the ewes practically *kneel* for you—"

"My ewes are well-trained," I said. "Like yours. Go home, Tam."

He went. I stood in the dooryard a long moment, watching the grey riders move off down the road, six neat wounds in the green body of the valley.

Then I went into the hut, put my back against the door, and allowed myself exactly ten seconds of shaking. Grandmother's rule two. Never let them see. She hadn't said anything about doing it privately, behind a door, with your teeth clenched so hard your jaw ached.

Ten seconds. Then I washed my hands, packed a satchel with the efficiency of long habit—bandages, dried meat, flint, the little knife I wasn't supposed to own—and went out the back way into the bracken, toward the hollow where a wolf who shouldn't exist was waiting for me.

Because here was the thing I understood standing in that doorway, the thing that cracked through me colder than fear:

Cassian hadn't come to Vaelmoor on a rumor. Inquisitors don't spend royal coin riding into the middle of nowhere on gossip about a girl and her friendly ewes. Someone, somewhere beyond the stones, had *remembered* us—and the ward that was supposed to keep the world's memory out had let the memory in.

The valley's safety had always been an illusion. I'd just been young enough, lucky enough, to believe the illusion.

Fen lifted his head when I reached the hollow, and his ears came forward, and he looked at me with those amber eyes as if to say, *well? What did I tell you?*

"They're looking for beast-speakers," I whispered. "Here. Now. Looking for *me*, probably, though they don't know it yet."

Fen rose, favoring his bandaged side, and did something he had never done before. He looked past me—south, toward the border stones—and growled, low and continuous, like a storm deciding.

And then, in a voice that was not sound so much as meaning poured directly into my skull, in a place where no such thing should have been possible, he spoke his first word to me.

"*Run.*"
