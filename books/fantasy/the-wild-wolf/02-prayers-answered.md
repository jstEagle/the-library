## 2. Prayers Answered

The morning the barley died in the shape of a wolf's paw, I was milking the goat and pretending, as usual, that my life was ordinary.

I'd like to say I noticed the field right away. I didn't. I noticed the goat first—Bessie, who had the temperament of a tax collector and the udder of a saint—and she was refusing to graze on the east slope. She stood at the fence line with her ears pinned back, staring across the valley like a woman watching a fire she couldn't reach.

"Bessie," I said. "There's grass. Right there. Under your feet. This is not complicated."

She ignored me with the dignity of her kind, which is considerable.

It was only when I carried the pail up past the barley terraces that I saw it. The field was green everywhere except one great patch of yellowed stalks, dead overnight, and the patch was not a patch. It had four lobes and a broad heel. It was a print, vast as a threshing floor, pressed into the living grain by something that had never touched it at all.

I stood there with warm milk sloshing in the pail and felt the particular cold you feel when the world stops pretending to make sense.

Vaelmoor didn't do things like this. That was the whole point of Vaelmoor. The valley sat inside an old ward older than anyone could name, a quiet so complete that magic didn't just fail here—it had never been born. Crops grew because crops grew. Rain fell because rain fell. Nothing whispered in the hedgerows, nothing glowed in the wells, and no one was ever dragged to the square for consorting with powers unseen. My mother used to call it God's blind spot. My grandmother called it mercy. I called it home, the way you call a cage a bed if you've slept in it long enough.

Because here's what the valley's quiet bought me: silence. Inside the ward, my gift was a stone in a riverbed. I could speak to beasts, and they could answer, but the words stayed small and local, a secret folded between me and whatever creature was listening. No resonance. No ripple. In Vaelmoor, a girl who talked to animals was just a girl who was good with animals, and good with animals was a virtue, not a pyre.

Outside the valley, King Aldric's inquisitors burned beast-speakers. I'd heard the stories the way you hear about weather in far-off countries. A shepherd in the Marchlands, a fisherwoman from the coast, each of them taken, each of them questioned, none of them returned. The king called it cleansing. The valley called it distant thunder.

So I did what any sensible person does when their entire survival depends on being unremarkable: I kicked dirt over the wolf-print in the barley and told myself it was blight.

* * *

By midday, half the village had seen it anyway.

They gathered at the terraces in that tight, murmuring huddle people form when they want to look at something terrible without touching it. Old Marta prodded the dead stalks. The reeve scratched his chin and said *blight* with the conviction of a man rehearsing. Someone said the pattern looked like a paw, and someone else laughed, and nobody's laugh reached their eyes.

"It's drainage," I offered. "Water pooling under the roots."

The reeve nodded gratefully. People believe what lets them sleep, and I have always been generous about handing out sleeping draughts of that kind.

But the animals knew. That was the thing I couldn't kick dirt over. All that afternoon, the beasts of the valley moved like water running downhill—away. Bessie wouldn't leave her pen unless I led her, and then she pressed against my leg the whole way. The birds left the elms all at once, a single gray exhalation rising over the ridge and gone. By evening, the dogs were howling at nothing in a chorus that made even the bravest men check their door latches twice.

And Fen—

Fen was pacing.

I found him at dusk in the hollow where I'd splinted his foreleg three weeks before, after finding him limping along the stream with a wound no thorn or trap could explain. He was a big gray wolf, too calm by half, with amber eyes that watched me the way scholars watch difficult texts. I'd named him Fen for the marsh where I'd found him, mostly because calling him "wolf" felt rude once we'd been introduced.

He wasn't speaking. Inside the ward he never truly spoke; our conversations were a language of posture and gaze and low sound, meaning carried without words, which suited me fine. Words were dangerous. Words could be overheard.

But tonight he paced the hollow like a prisoner counting his steps, and when I slid down the bank to sit beside him, he stopped and looked at me with such intensity that my skin prickled.

"What?" I said. "You're making me nervous, and I'm already nervous, so this is a bad economy."

He came closer. He put his head very near mine, close enough that his breath warmed my ear, and he did the thing he should not have been able to do.

"Noor."

My own name. Spoken aloud. Clear as a bell over water, in a voice that was not a growl and not a wind and not anything the valley's quiet should have allowed to exist.

I scrambled backward up the bank until a bramble caught my skirt and held me like a hand. "No," I said. "No, that's not—that doesn't work here. Nothing works here. That's the arrangement. That's the whole point of the arrangement."

Fen followed me, unhurried, and sat. "Noor," he said again, gentler now, and I understood that he had said it gently on purpose, because he could see what it was doing to me. "The ward is thinning. Listen."

I listened. And beneath the crickets and the far-off dog-chorus, I heard it: a hum, faint and rising, like a plucked string that would not stop singing. The sound the valley had never made in my whole life. The sound of the quiet coming apart.

"The covenant is breaking," Fen said. "The old pact. Human law, beast law, god law—one weave, three strands. Your ancestors helped spin it. Aldric cut it." His ears flattened at the name, a motion so human it hurt. "And when a weave breaks, the last thread feels it first."

"I'm not a thread," I said. "I'm a dairymaid. I milk a goat. I am aggressively unimportant."

"You are the heir of the Wolf Covenant, Noor Kavanagh." He said my full name the way a priest says a rite. "The bloodline that spoke for all three realms. You were hidden here because here you couldn't be found. But the hiding is ending, and the finding has begun."

I laughed. It came out wrong, high and thin. "That's absurd. My mother sold eggs. My grandmother cursed at looms. Nobody in my family has ever been anything but tired."

"Your grandmother wove blessings into every thread she cursed at," Fen said. "She thought no one noticed. The gift sleeps in your family the way embers sleep in ash. And yours—" his amber eyes held mine— "yours never slept at all, did it?"

I wrapped my arms around my knees. Above us, the first stars were coming out over a valley that suddenly felt like a stage with the lamps lit. For nineteen years I had been invisible, and invisibility is its own kind of safety, and I had built my whole self inside it like a snail builds a shell. Now I could feel the shell cracking, hairline by hairline, and behind every crack was the same question, arriving with the patience of winter:

What happens to a girl who exists, when existing gets her killed?

"Why tell me now?" I whispered. "If the ward kept us both safe, why break it?"

"Because the gods are waking," Fen said, "and they are frightened. The crops die in patterns because they are writing. The animals flee because they can hear the ward failing. The prayers people forgot to stop saying are starting to be answered, Noor, and answers are coming whether anyone asks for them or not." He rose, and the hum beneath the earth seemed to rise with him. "Someone must speak for the weave before it tears. The gods cannot choose. The beasts will not. It must be a voice of the covenant's own blood."

"And if I say no?"

He was quiet a moment. Somewhere down-valley, another dog began to howl, and another answered, and the sound rolled toward us like a tide.

"Then the tearing comes anyway," he said. "Only without a voice to shape it."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the doorway of my cottage with a blanket around my shoulders, watching the valley I had spent my life disappearing into.

Toward midnight, the hum changed key. I felt it in my teeth. Out past the terraces, in the ruined chapel where the old gods' names had been chiseled off the lintels two generations ago, a light kindled—faint, gold-green, patient. Not firelight. Firelight flickers. This light simply *was*, steady as a held breath.

The prayers were being answered. That was the thing I kept circling back to, numb with awe. People knelt in that ruin sometimes—I'd seen Marta there, lips moving, asking nothing in particular—and for years the gods inside the ward had been deaf stones. Now something was listening. Something was answering. The whole machinery of miracle, grinding awake after generations of silence, and every turn of it scraped against the edge of the king's knives.

Because that was the arithmetic no one in the village understood yet. Wonder was contraband now. A god waking in Vaelmoor wasn't salvation. It was a signal fire, visible from the capital, and somewhere south of here a man whose job was extinguishing such fires was already reaching for his boots.

I thought about the shepherd in the Marchlands. The fisherwoman from the coast. I thought about my hands, which had spoken to beasts since before I could write, and about how many nights I'd thanked whatever governed luck that I'd been born inside the quiet.

The light in the chapel brightened, just slightly. Like an eye opening.

Fen appeared at my side without sound, shoulder brushing mine. "They know where you are now," he said quietly. "The waking draws the hunters. We have little time."

And down the dark road toward the valley's edge, hooves. Many hooves, moving fast, carrying torches whose light I could see even from here—too many torches for travelers, too organized for bandits.

Awe curdled in my chest and turned to something colder.

"Fen," I said, standing, "how much do you know about the road south of the border stone?"

His ears went flat. "Enough to start running."

We ran.
