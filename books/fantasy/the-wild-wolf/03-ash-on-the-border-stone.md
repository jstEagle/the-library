## 3. Ash on the Border Stone

I had been running for eleven days when I finally stopped running, which is the sort of thing a man tells himself to feel better about collapsing.

The horse died on day nine. Good mare, patient eyes, deserved better than a knight who'd spent his last coin on her and his last prayer on himself. I buried my saddle under a hawthorn and kept the blanket, because even disgraced men get cold. After that it was walking—north and west, following the old pilgrim roads that hadn't seen pilgrims since King Aldric decided the old gods were an infestation rather than a faith. My feet had opinions about this. They shared them constantly.

My name is Ser Ivo Marchetti, or it was. Now I'm mostly "you there" and occasionally "the traitor." Aldric's purge of the old orders reached the Knights of the Stone Vigil in the spring. We were sworn guardians of the covenant—that was the pretty version we told ourselves at vigils, over cold bread, while our knees ached from kneeling. In practice we guarded stones and shrines and the small quiet places where the old law still breathed. When Aldric sent his inquisitors east with fire and writs, most of my brothers chose martyrdom. Martyrdom is easy. It requires no planning and no compromise, and you get a nice stone out of it eventually.

I chose the other thing. I dropped my sword at the feet of a nineteen-year-old inquisitor with soft hands and a hard smile, and I walked away, and I have been walking ever since, and every night I dream the same dream: the shrine at Cael Hollow burning, and me standing in the road watching, doing nothing, again.

So understand that when I say what I did at the border of Vaelmoor, I am not claiming virtue. I am claiming arithmetic. A man can only carry so much shame before he starts trying to give some of it away.

* * *

The ward announced itself before I saw anything, the way a hearth announces itself to a frozen man—a warmth in the teeth, a hum behind the eyes. Old magic does that. The priests said it was the covenant's breath; the inquisitors called it contamination. Both agreed you felt it coming, like weather.

Except that evening, the warmth was wrong. It flickered. I crested the ridge above the valley mouth as the sun went down, and there it was: Vaelmoor, green and sheltered and famously dull, ringed by standing stones so old nobody remembered raising them—and the light between those stones was guttering. Not steady, not the deep blue-white I'd felt at vigil-stones all my life. This was a fire burning down to its coals. Streaks of ash-grey ran through the shimmer where there should have been nothing but clean light, and twice, as I watched, the whole curtain of it stuttered like a candle in wind.

Wards don't do that. Wards are stone patience given light. A ward dying is a thing that happens maybe once in five hundred years, and usually it takes a god or a very determined king to manage it.

Aldric had been very determined for six years now.

I stood there in the bracken feeling the hum weaken against my skin, and I thought, with the clarity that exhaustion grants: *here is a door closing.* Behind it, a valley full of farmers who'd never done anything worse than forget their history. Ahead of it, me, and whatever lay beyond Vaelmoor's hills—the free cities, the coast, a boat, a new name, a life spent looking over one shoulder instead of two.

Eleven days of running had taught me the shape of that life. It fit badly.

There were riders on the road behind me. I'd heard them at dusk—hooves, unhurried, the particular unhurried rhythm of men who believe nothing ahead of them can hurt them. Inquisitors travel like that. I knew those hooves the way you know a voice in a nightmare. They were perhaps an hour back, following the same pilgrim road I'd followed, and they would reach this ridge, and they would see exactly what I was seeing: a ward burning down like a lamp running dry, with a fat undefended valley sitting underneath it like fruit.

And they would ride down and ask questions. That's how it always starts. Questions first. Then names. Then lists. Then smoke.

I sat down on a rock and told myself all the reasons to keep walking. Let me tell you, I had excellent reasons. I was unarmed except for a belt knife. I was half-starved. My face was on writs nailed to every church door south of the Greywater, and the reward would feed a family for a year. Nobody pays a man to warn people. There is no coin in warning. Warning is charity, and charity is what knights did before they learned better.

The hoofbeats came closer.

Here is the thing about shame: it doesn't motivate. Shame sits on your chest at night and recites your failures until you can't move. For eleven days it had ridden me like a spur and gotten exactly nowhere—I'd covered ground, sure, but only away from things. What got me up off that rock was something smaller and meaner and better: I was tired of being a man who watched fires. If I walked into that valley and warned them and it bought them one hour, one hour in which grandmothers and children got onto carts and went somewhere the smoke wouldn't find them—then I would be, by the smallest possible margin, a different man than the one at Cael Hollow. Not redeemed. Just slightly less the same.

That's the arithmetic I mentioned. It doesn't balance. Nothing balances anymore. But you do the sum anyway.

* * *

The village was called Harrow's Edge, which struck me as honest naming. Maybe forty houses around a green, a mill, a shrine-house with its alcove boarded over—someone had been careful, someone had read the winds. People were bringing in the evening milking, dogs trotting at their heels, everything smelling of woodsmoke and cut grass and ordinary peace. It hurt to look at. Peace always does, once you've seen the other side of it.

An old woman saw me first. She didn't scream, which I appreciated; she just straightened up from her fence and looked at the state of me—beard like a thornbush, blanket instead of cloak, boots held together with hope and sinew—and said, "You'll be wanting food, then."

"I'll be wanting to speak to whoever leads here," I said. "Quickly. Then food, if there's time."

"There's always time for food," she said, which is the correct philosophy, but I was already moving toward the green.

They fetched me a miller with a strong chin and a headman's chain, and a handful of others gathered the way villagers do, at a distance, arms crossed, deciding whether I was trouble. I didn't blame them. I looked like trouble that had been rained on.

"My name doesn't matter," I began, and a boy near the front said, "It's on your face," pointing at the brand on my cheekbone—the Vigil's stone-and-flame, seared there when I took my vows, and lately useful for identifying traitors. Fair enough.

"It's Ser Ivo Marchetti," I said. "Late of the Knights of the Stone Vigil. Which makes me either a dead loyalist or a live traitor depending on who you ask, and I haven't got time to litigate it." I pointed north, at the ridge. "Your ward is failing. Tonight or within days. And there are inquisitors on the pilgrim road behind me, no more than an hour out, riding slow because they think they're early."

The murmur that went around the green wasn't fear, exactly. It was the sound of people checking whether the stranger was mad.

"The ward's stood three hundred years," the miller said.

"Wards stand until they don't. I've felt a hundred of them, healthy ones. Yours has ash in it. Something is eating it from outside—or something inside it changed." I thought of the reports that had crossed my desk in better years, sealed and quickly burned: a valley where prayers were answered again, where beasts acted strangely, where the old law stirred like a sleeper turning over. I'd assumed rumor. Rumors are usually just wishes wearing costumes. But wards don't gutter for wishes.

"Whatever the cause, the King's men will come through that gap within the day, and they'll ask who here has heard voices, who keeps old shrines, whose cattle behave oddly, whose children say strange things. And everyone will swear to nothing, because that's what people do, and then the questioning starts, and questioning in the King's service has a way of finding answers." I let that sit. "You have tonight. Load carts. Get your children and your old ones out the far side of the valley. Some of you will stay—someone must, fields won't tend themselves—but stay knowing what's coming, not pretending otherwise."

A stocky farmer spat. "We've heard this song. Riders come, shout about beast-speakers and god-touched folk, take a chicken, leave. Vaelmoor's forgotten by everyone, including its gods. That's the bargain."

"Was," I said. "Look at your sky."

They looked. Above the western ridge, faint in the last light, the ward's shimmer pulsed—and this time everyone saw it stumble, the grey streaks crawling wider like frost across a window. A dog began to howl. Then another. All along the green, dogs lifted their heads and sang at nothing, and the horses in the paddock pressed to the far rail, ears flat, staring at the hills as if the hills had said something rude.

The animals know first. They always know first. I remembered that much from my vows, from the verses about the covenant: *beast speaks to god speaks to man, and each hears the others.* We'd been the deafest of the three for centuries. The dogs weren't howling at nothing. They were answering something.

The old woman with the fence crossed herself the old way, thumb to brow then heart, the gesture the inquisitors had outlawed. She caught herself doing it and didn't stop.

"Hour," I reminded them. "Maybe less. Decide fast."

They scattered—not into panic, which would have come later, but into that brisk grim efficiency of people with work to do. Carts came out of barns. Someone started hammering boards off the shrine-house alcove, whether to hide what was inside or carry it, I couldn't tell. The miller stayed, studying me with his strong chin.

"Why?" he asked. "Why warn us? You could be over those hills by dark."

Because I'm tired, I almost said. Because I'm sick of the taste of my own cowardice, I almost said. Instead I heard myself answer honestly, which was a novelty.

"Because eleven days ago I stood in a doorway and watched men I swore to protect burn, and I stepped aside, and I have been stepping aside ever since. I'd like to try the other direction once. See how it fits."

He nodded slowly, the way men nod at prices they don't like but can't argue with. "There's stew on," he said. "Eat fast."

I was halfway to the stew—a miracle of onions and gristle that I intend to remember fondly for the rest of my life, however long that proves—when the dogs all stopped howling at once. Total silence, forty animals, like a hand clapped over the valley's mouth.

Then, from the hills above the eastern pastures, I heard it: a wolf's cry, long and rising, but wrong somehow—too many notes in it, too much meaning. And beneath it, fainter, carried on the failing ward's last warm breath, something I hadn't felt since I was a boy kneeling at my first vigil: the old magic, waking.

Not dying after all. Calling.

The miller set down his bowl. "What in the name of—" He stopped, because he'd realized, as I had, that naming names was the problem now.

"Get them moving," I said. "All of them. Whatever that is, the inquisitors will hear it too—and they won't come asking questions this time. They'll come with fire."
