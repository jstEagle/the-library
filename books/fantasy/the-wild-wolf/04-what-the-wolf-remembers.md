## 4. What the Wolf Remembers

Fen did not speak again that night, and I spent the hours between midnight and dawn lying in the loft with my eyes open, listening to the valley breathe and waiting for the word *Noor* to come back out of the dark like a stone I'd thrown into a well.

It didn't. But it had happened once, and once was enough to ruin everything.

Here is what you need to understand about Vaelmoor: the ward over this valley isn't a wall or a fence or anything so honest. It's more like water over a lamp flame. Magic comes near it, and it goes out. That's what my grandmother told me, anyway, back when I still thought she was just a woman who liked her privacy. The old gods can't reach in. Beasts inside the ward are beasts, plain and dumb, eating grass and dying of ordinary things. And whatever lives in me—the thing that lets me talk to animals—has been muffled my whole life, like shouting through three blankets. I could whisper to a chicken about the fox. I could ask a horse why it limped. But nothing ever spoke *back*, not really, not in words.

Fen spoke my name. Inside the ward. Which meant either the ward was dying, or Fen was something the ward had never been built for, or both—and every possibility on that list ended with me being dragged to an inquisitor's fire before the first frost.

So when morning came gray and cold over the rooftops, I did what any sensible person does with an unbearable truth: I fed the chickens, swept the step, and went to see Tam.

Tam Ashdown has been my mentor since I was nine years old and he caught me telling a snared hare where the loose board in its cage was. He's the village farrier, which means he's got hands like root vegetables and the gentlest voice in Vaelmoor, and he taught me everything I know about keeping my gift quiet. *You don't hide a candle by putting it under a bucket, girl,* he used to say. *The bucket smokes. You hide it by making sure nobody's looking for light at all.* He'd taught me to be boring. Boring as bread. Boring as weather.

I found him shoeing the miller's mare, sleeves rolled, forearms black to the elbow, humming his off-key hum.

"Tam," I said. "I need to tell you something."

He glanced up, and I watched forty years of caution cross his face in the space of a breath. "Not here," he said quietly. "Walk with me to the upper pasture. Bring your dog."

"He's not—" I started, and gave up. There wasn't time to explain what Fen was or wasn't.

We walked. Fen loped behind us at a distance no one would mistake for ownership, because a wolf trotting at a farmer's heel draws eyes, but a wolf crossing the same valley at the same hour is just weather. Tam talked loudly about horseshoes the entire way. When we reached the pasture fence, high and empty above the village, he finally stopped humming.

"Now," he said. "Tell me slow."

So I told him. The wounded wolf. The words. My name spoken aloud in a voice like river stones knocking together. Tam listened without moving except for his jaw, working side to side, the way he does when he's chewing on something worse than tobacco.

"The ward," he said at last. "My grandmother said the ward was paid for. Blood and oath, laid down by the covenant folk before the kings burned them. If it's thinning—"

"Then it's not just me in danger." My throat felt tight. "It's everyone who ever whispered to an animal and thought they were alone. Half the valley does it, Tam. They think it's nothing. A feeling. A way with beasts."

"It *is* nothing," Tam said. "*That's* the mercy. Here, it's nothing. Take it past the border stone and it's a death sentence." He looked at Fen then, straight on, which most people were too smart to do. "What are you, wolf?"

And Fen—who had been staring down the slope at the village like a sentry—turned his great gray head and said, in that voice that made the hairs on my arms stand up:

"I am older than your ward."

Tam sat down on a rock. I don't blame him. My knees did their own negotiating too.

* * *

What follows, I set down as best I can, though words are poor buckets for carrying what Fen gave us. Because that was the other thing he did that morning, up in the empty pasture with the wind combing the grass: he shared memory. Not told—*shared*. He touched his muzzle to my temple, and Tam's, and the world fell open like a door.

I saw the Godstone. Not broken—whole. A spire of pale stone taller than any tower, standing where three valleys met, veined with light that moved like blood under skin. Around it, in rings, stood wolves and people and shapes I can only call gods—not the carved idols Aldric permits in his churches, but presences, vast and patient, wearing bodies the way a queen wears a borrowed coat.

I saw the covenant made. Three voices speaking one law: human hands would till and build; beast fangs would hunt and guard; god will would bind the seasons and hold the deep magic in check. One law, three realms, no master over any. The wolves were the keepers of it—the Wolf Covenant—because a wolf knows both hunger and loyalty, and a law needs both or it becomes tyranny or it becomes soup.

I saw the years of it working. I saw harvests and hunts and children raised in villages where the deer grazed unafraid at the field edges and the old gods walked openly among the barley rows.

And then I saw Aldric.

Not the gray-bearded king from the coins. Young Aldric, broad-shouldered, sworn as Guardian of the Stone—one of the order that kept the covenant's peace. I watched him kneel before the Godstone with his hand flat against it, swearing the guardian's oath, and I heard the stone answer him, and I understood with a sick lurch that the man hunting my kind had once been *bound to it*, closer to the old magic than any living soul.

Then I saw the year he broke it.

There was a famine—or the gods let there be one; Fen's memory was unclear on whether the gods withheld rain or merely refused to spend power they deemed unnecessary. Aldric's home province starved. His brother starved. And Aldric went to the Godstone and demanded the gods spend their hoarded strength, and the gods answered—in the way gods answer—with some cold arithmetic about balance, about what may be given only if something else is taken.

Aldric took the something else himself. He brought soldiers to the stone. He broke the spire with hammers and oath-fire, killed the guardians to a man, and declared mankind free of divine masters. The ward-stones went up across the land after—his great work, sealing magic away from human affairs forever. The beast-speakers, the last living vessels of covenant law, were pronounced abominations. Purged. Every one they could find.

Except the heir. Except the line of Kavanagh, hidden in a valley whose ward was old enough and strong enough to smother even the covenant's bloodline. Hidden until now.

"Your grandmother knew," Fen said, his voice threading through the fading vision as the pasture reassembled itself around us. "She carried the covenant's seed-word north and buried it in a daughter, and the daughter in you. The ward was not only your shield. It was your cradle. While it held, the covenant slept in you, dormant, safe."

"And now it's failing," I said.

"Everything fails. Even wards. Even oaths." His yellow eyes held mine. "The stone is shattered, but a stone remembers its shape. So do I. So do you, now."

Tam was white as ash. "If Aldric learns the heir lives—"

"He already suspects," Fen said. "Why do you think the patrols have come asking about beast-speakers? Not hunting rumors. Hunting *bloodlines*. They've been walking the valley for weeks, counting children, asking grandmothers questions." He turned his head toward the village below, and I followed his gaze, and that was when I saw the smoke.

Not hearth smoke. Hearth smoke rises lazy. This rose fast and straight, from the direction of the forge, and beneath it came bells—the wrong bells, the alarm pattern, rung twice and cut off.

We ran.

* * *

They'd taken Tam's forge apart by the time we reached it, tools scattered in the mud like a spilled purse. Two wagons stood in the lane. Six inquisitors in gray-and-black, faces hooded against the cold, and between them, wrists bound, kneeling in the dirt—

Tam. No. Wait. Let me get this right, because I have to live with getting it right.

The man kneeling in the dirt was Old Wenna, the herbalist, seventy-one years of age, who had once told me that chamomile likes to be sung to. The man being held upright by two soldiers with a boot between his shoulder blades was Tam, and his left arm hung wrong, bent where it shouldn't bend, and his face was the color of tallow.

The inquisitor in charge was reading from a list. "Ashdown, Tamas. Farrier. Reported conversing with livestock in a manner beyond training. Confirmed by three witnesses." He looked up, and his eyes found me over the crowd of neighbors standing frozen in their doorways. Everyone was frozen. Everyone had always been frozen; that was the whole design of the thing. "You're the girl who lodges with him."

"She's my apprentice," Tam rasped. "Girl's simple. Talks to chickens like all farm children do. Waste your rope on me."

"Sir," I said, stepping forward, and Fen pressed hard against my leg—a warning, a wall of fur—but I stepped anyway, because the plan arrived in my head fully formed, the way terrible plans do. "I'm Noor Kavanagh. Whatever he's done, I'll confess it. He taught me the trade, that's all. Take me instead."

Every word a lie except the ones that mattered. Take me instead. Watch their faces. Learn what they wanted, how many they were, where the next patrol would ride.

The inquisitor smiled. It was the worst smile I've ever seen—not cruel, exactly. Patient. The smile of a fisherman watching the line go tight.

"No need," he said. "You're precisely who we hoped would volunteer. Bind her."

The world went very narrow and bright. I remember Fen's growl rolling through the lane low enough to shake my teeth. I remember Tam screaming my name, fighting his bindings, his bad arm swinging dead. I remember thinking, with a strange clarity: *they didn't come for Tam at all—they came for bait, and I walked up and hooked myself.*

But the hook went in deeper than they knew. Because as the soldier's hand closed on my wrist, Fen stopped growling, and the whole lane went silent—dogs, geese, the horses in the wagons, everything—as every animal in Vaelmoor turned its head toward us at once.

And Fen said, loud enough for the entire village to hear, in a voice like the bottom of a lake:

"*Touch the heir, and the covenant answers.*"

The inquisitor's smile flickered—for just a heartbeat, just long enough—into fear.

Which was, I would later understand, the moment the free cities' war truly began. But standing in the mud with a soldier's fist on my wrist and my mentor bleeding in the dirt, I knew only one thing: my secret had cost Wenna her freedom, Tam his arm, and the valley its long sleep.

Time to stop hiding. Time to make them pay for the waking.
