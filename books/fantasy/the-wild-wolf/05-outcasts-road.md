## 5. Outcasts' Road

*Noor*

The valley let go of me the way a mother lets go of a child on the first day of school—one hand at the small of my back, pushing before I'd decided to walk.

Fen trotted ahead through the bracken, grey as morning fog, pausing every hundred paces to taste the wind. Ser Ivo Marchetti walked behind me with his sword slung across his back like an apology he hadn't figured out how to phrase. He had been a knight once. Now he was a big man in a rust-spotted hauberk who flinched whenever I looked at him, which was a strange thing to see in someone that size.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm deciding whether you're worth feeding."

"That's fair." He shifted his pack. "For what it's worth, I was deciding the same thing about you."

"We've known each other two days."

"And I've made worse decisions with better information."

I let that sit. The truth was I didn't trust him yet, and I wasn't sure I ever would. Trust was a thing you spent in Vaelmoor like coins in a town where everyone knew your face. But he had stood between me and the inquisitor's torchlight when he could have run, and Fen—whose judgment I trusted more than any human's—hadn't growled at him once. That counted for something. Not everything. Something.

We climbed out of the valley by the shepherd's path, the one so old it had forgotten it was a path. By midday we were above the tree line, and behind us Vaelmoor lay green and quiet and lying to itself about its safety. Ahead, the world.

"They'll have checkpoints on the north road," Ivo said. "Aldric's men don't patrol the passes much—they're too proud to climb—but the roads are another matter. They'll be looking for a girl traveling alone."

"I'm not alone."

"No," he said, glancing at Fen. "You're a girl, a wolf, and a disgraced knight walking toward the most hunted woman in the kingdom's destination. Alone is the one thing we aren't."

*I'm not the most hunted anything,* I almost said. *I was nobody three days ago.* But nobody was exactly what Aldric's inquisitors were burning at the stake now, if they matched Sela's description, if they smelled of the old gods, if they so much as hummed one of the forbidden hymns while kneading bread. Nobody was the most dangerous thing to be.

Fen stopped. Her ears went up—not alarm, something softer—and then I heard it too: singing. Thin and cracked and unmistakably human, drifting down from the rocks above the trail. A hymn. An old one, the kind the ward used to smother in your throat before you finished the second verse.

Ivo's hand went to his sword. "Ambush."

"It's one voice."

"One voice can be bait."

The singer came around the boulder before we could decide. She was younger than me by a year, maybe, barefoot, her priestess robes cut off at the knee and filthy past recognition, her hair hacked short with what looked like a knife applied in the dark. She stopped dead when she saw us—a wolf, a girl, a knight—and for a moment nobody breathed.

Then she dropped to her knees in the gravel. Not to us. To Fen.

"Blessed be the Wolf that Walks," she whispered. "Blessed be the Covenant. Oh, thank the gods, thank the gods, I thought I was the last—"

"Get up," Ivo said.

She looked up at him, and there was such fierce, exhausted joy in her face that it hurt to witness. "You don't understand. I've been praying since Ashfall. Every shrine, every standing stone, every river—I prayed until my knees bled, and nothing answered, and I thought the gods were dead, I thought we'd killed them—" Her eyes found mine. "But she has the look. You have the look. The heir. You're the heir, aren't you?"

"I'm Noor," I said. "That's all I am."

"She's Noor," the girl repeated, delighted, as if this confirmed everything. "Sela. I was acolyte at the River Shrine at Callow Ford until the inquisitors burned it. I ran. I've been running for eleven days." She got to her feet, swaying. "Where are you going?"

"The Godstone," Ivo said flatly.

"Then I'm coming."

"You're a target," Ivo said. "You sing hymns out loud in open country."

"I pray quietly when required."

"You're barefoot."

"I'll toughen."

"Sela," I said, and she turned to me like I'd handed her a candle in a cellar. I didn't want her. Wanting people was how they ended up in cages because of me. But she had walked eleven days on burned feet toward a prayer that hadn't answered, and there was a stubbornness in that that I recognized from the mirror. "Can you fight?"

"No."

"Heal?"

"A little. Bonesetting, mostly. Fevers."

"Then walk behind Fen and do what she does. If she runs, you run."

Fen gave Sela a long, unimpressed look, then bumped her forehead against the girl's hip, once, the way she did with lambs she'd decided not to eat. Sela burst into tears.

Ivo sighed the sigh of a man watching his odds get worse. "Wonderful. We're four."

* * *

*Ivo*

In the years I wore the king's colors, I learned that armies are honest. An army tells you plainly: I will kill you unless you move. It was the smaller things that lied. A friendly face at a checkpoint. A man offering to share his fire. Aldric himself, most of all, with his gentle voice and his terrible certainty—the only man I ever knew who could ruin a life and make you thank him for the tidiness of it.

So when we saw the smoke of the Waymeet checkpoint curling above the pines, I did not think like a soldier. I thought like a liar.

"Two riders visible, probably six more under canvas," I murmured. "They're checking traffic southbound. Merchants, pilgrims, anyone young enough to be running."

"Then we don't take the road," Sela said.

"There is no other way across the Ferrow. The bridge is there or nowhere."

"Then we swim," said Noor.

"With winter coming? The Ferrow takes grown men in summer." I crouched and scratched a map in the dirt with a stick: road, bridge, checkpoint, the mule yard beside it where drovers watered their animals. "We go through. But not as ourselves."

Noor frowned at the mule yard. Then her face did something I'd seen twice before in two days—a small, inward stillness, like a door opening onto a lit room. "How many mules?"

"A dozen, perhaps."

"How attached are the drovers to order?"

I began, despite myself, to smile. "Not very, on a good day."

* * *

*Noor*

Here is what no one understands about speaking with beasts, and why it isn't a gift anyone should envy: it is mostly negotiation. Animals are not wise. They are not mystical. They are hungry, tired, suspicious creatures with opinions, and convincing them of anything is like arguing with a village council where everyone is naked and afraid of umbrellas.

Mules were the worst. Mules were born offended.

The drover's boy was hauling water bucket by bucket while his master argued freight prices at the checkpoint table, and the mule train stood picketed in the yard, ears swiveling, taking the measure of the world and finding it wanting.

I crouched by the fence rail and did what I had done my whole secret life—opened that quiet place behind my breastbone where words stop being words.

*Small sisters. Small brothers. Who owns you?*

*The rope-ones,* said the nearest mule, a dun mare with a scarred muzzle. *The loud ones. Everything is wrong here.*

*Everything is wrong everywhere,* I told her. *But there is a place where the grass grows sweet and no ropes follow. South, over the water.*

*Liar,* said the mare, without heat. Mules respect honesty about dishonesty.

*I don't lie to animals,* I said. *Only to kings. Come and see.*

And then I did the thing I had never done outside the ward, the thing Fen had been teaching me on the road these two days—reaching down the thread the covenant had tied in my blood before I was born. I didn't command. Command breaks against a mule like rain on stone. I *offered*. I gave her the picture: high meadow, no bridles, the smell of spring grass, freedom like open sky.

The dun mare's ears came forward.

*Show the others,* she said.

What followed was, I will maintain until my dying day, not my fault.

Twelve mules hit the end of their picket lines at once. Two posts tore clean out of the earth; the rest dragged, screaming that particular screaming complaint that only mules can produce, a sound like a hinge being murdered. The drover's boy dropped his buckets. The checkpoint soldiers shouted. Somewhere a man yelled *the beasts have gone mad*, which was unfair, because the beasts had gone precisely sane—they had simply, for the first time in their lives, been given somewhere better to be.

They stampeded south across the bridge, straight through the middle of the checkpoint, scattering soldiers like ninepins, and the entire garrison—every armed man within earshot—did the only reasonable thing, which was to chase twelve mules worth more than their yearly pay.

"Now," I said.

We crossed the bridge in the chaos, four shadows among the dust, Fen flowing low beside me, Ivo walking with the unhurried swagger of a man who belonged wherever he stood, Sela praying silently with her lips moving so fast they blurred. Behind us the shouting faded into the trees. Ahead, the road ran empty and gold in the late light.

It was only when we were a mile clear, hearts slowing, that I started laughing—really laughing, helplessly, the kind I hadn't managed since before the wolf spoke my name. Sela caught it next, then Ivo, deep and rusty, like a gate unused for years.

"You," I gasped at him, "are a terrible influence."

"Me? You incited a mule riot."

"Somebody had to. They were miserable."

"Twelve mules," Sela said reverently, wiping her eyes. "The gods sent twelve mules."

"The gods," said Fen aloud, dry as dust, "sent a girl who owes every mule in Ferrow an apology."

We walked on as the sun went down, and something had changed among us that none of us named. We were still strangers. I still watched Ivo's hands when he reached for anything. Sela still flinched at hoofbeats. But we had done a mad thing together and come out the far side laughing, and that is a stronger mortar than most.

We made camp in a hollow off the road, and I took first watch with Fen warm against my leg. That was when she stiffened, nose lifted to the north, and the laughter died in my chest.

"What is it?"

"Horses," Fen said. "Many. Moving fast." A pause, and her hackles rose like a ridge of mountains. "And on the wind—ozone and iron. The smell of a broken godstone."

I looked north, into the dark, where the sky above the distant hills glowed faintly, faintly, with a light that had no business existing.

"They're not chasing us," Fen said softly. "They're racing us."
