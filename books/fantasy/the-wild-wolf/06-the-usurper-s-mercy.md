## 6. The Usurper's Mercy

I have been called many things in the nineteen years since I broke the covenant—usurper, oathbreaker, godslayer, and, by one particularly creative pamphleteer in Corvane, "the Butcher of Small Mercies." I kept that last one pinned above my writing desk for a season. It made me laugh on the days that needed laughing.

But no one has ever called me a liar, which is why it irritates me so deeply when the truth of my reign is told badly.

Here is how they tell it: A king named Aldric woke one morning hungry for power, murdered the old gods' chosen, shattered the Godstone out of spite, and has spent two decades drowning the world in blood because blood is what he likes to drink with breakfast.

It is a tidy story. Tidy stories are the ones people carry into taverns, and taverns are where rebellions are born, so I do not begrudge the storytellers their work entirely. But since I am the only man alive who remembers all of it—who stood where I stood and saw what I saw—I will set down the truth here, for whoever finds these pages after the last heir is found. Consider it an old man's vanity. Kings are permitted few.

* * *

The Wolf Covenant bound three realms into one law: the law of men, the law of beasts, the law of gods, spoken together at the Godstone each solstice by a voice chosen from among them. The books call this harmony. The priests called it mercy. I was its sworn guardian for eleven years, and I will tell you what I actually did with those years.

I watched a farmer's son be given to the river spirits because his mother had prayed for rain and the rain had not come, and the spirits required a price to forgive her ingratitude. I watched the beast-voices arbitrate a dispute between two villages over grazing rights, and when one village's champion—a woman who could speak to crows—ruled against her own kin because the birds told her the land belonged to the other side, the crows pecked her eyes out as punishment for disloyalty, and the gods declared it justice, and everyone bowed, including me, because I had taken an oath.

I watched the god-wolf itself—the great grey shadow that walked out of the stone at midwinter—demand a tithe of firstborn lambs, then firstborn foals, and once, in the famine year, a firstborn child from the town of Hallowmere. The elders wept. They obeyed anyway. That is what faith does: it makes obedience feel like love.

You must understand something about me before you judge me. I was not born to any of this. I was a border lord's third son, raised on hard bread and harder arithmetic, and when the old voice of the covenant died and the gods chose me as guardian—for my sword arm and my inconvenient habit of asking questions—I thought it an honor. I thought I had been lifted out of the mud and shown the machinery of heaven.

Then I looked inside the machinery.

* * *

Do you know what the gods were, beneath their grandeur? Creditors. That is all. Ancient, patient creditors, and the covenant was the ledger. Every prayer answered was a debt incurred. Every blessing had an invoice attached. The beasts kept their half of the law because the wolf enforced it with teeth, and the men kept theirs because the gods enforced it with worse than teeth, and the whole beautiful structure rested on the principle that some creatures were born to pay and others were born to collect.

And the voices—the beast-speakers, the god-touched, the children who heard things in the dark—they were not honored. They were *used*. Collateral. Leverage. The gods kept a stable of them the way a miser keeps coin, and when a voice grew disobedient or inconvenient or simply worn out, there was a quiet place behind the Godstone where voices went to stop being anything at all.

I know because I carried one of them there myself, in my eleventh year of service. Her name was Maerin. She was seventeen. She could speak to salmon, of all things, and she had told the river spirits that she would no longer deliver messages demanding drowned children. The wolf pronounced her faithless. The gods pronounced her spent. And I—guardian of the covenant, keeper of the peace, a good man with a good sword—walked her to the quiet place because I had sworn to keep the law.

She held my hand the whole way. She said, "You're kind. Why are you doing this?"

Nineteen years, and I still hear it every night. Not as accusation. Worse—as a genuine question. She truly believed I might have an answer that made sense.

That night I stopped believing the covenant was sacred and started seeing it plainly: a cage with very old bars, painted gold, and every soul in Vaelmoor and beyond locked inside it, calling the lock holy. So I did what any honest man does when he discovers the temple is a slaughterhouse. I burned it down.

Yes, I broke my oath. An oath to tyrants is not a vow; it is a hostage situation that lasts a lifetime. Yes, I killed the voices when they rose against the breaking—and I grieve every one of them, though grief is a luxury I ration like everything else. Yes, I shattered the Godstone, and the ward-magic scattered, and the valley of Vaelmoor went silent and magicless, and the world became a plainer, colder, freer place.

I freed mankind from gods. Show me another king who can say that.

* * *

Now. About the purge.

My critics say I hunt beast-speakers out of fear. Fear! As if I feared anything that bled. No—the purge exists for one reason only, and it is the reason I barely sleep: the covenant, broken properly, dies. Broken carelessly, it *lingers*, like a fire banked under ash. The stone was shattered but not silenced. The gods were starved but not starved to death. And somewhere out there, the old blood persists—an heir of the Wolf Covenant, carrying the one thing I failed to destroy: a living voice that could speak the three realms back into their chains.

For nineteen years I searched. My inquisitors turned over every village in the borderlands twice. We found whispers, rumors, a shepherd boy who talked to goats (he talked to everyone; he was merely lonely), and always, always nothing. The heir stayed hidden, and I began to hope—foolishly, I see now—that perhaps the line had died out, that the last voice had gone quiet somewhere unrecorded, and mankind's freedom was finally permanent.

Then, three weeks ago, the prayers started being answered again.

Crops failing in patterns too regular for rot. Game animals fleeing Vaelmoor in a body, as if summoned. Wells singing. My informants in the free cities report that shrines long cold are warm to the touch. The gods are waking, and gods do not wake without a reason, and there is only one reason left in the world.

An heir. Alive. Hidden. Probably in Vaelmoor itself, that damp little valley where the ward ran thickest and my searches ran thinnest, because even I believed the ward would hold forever.

Wards, I have learned, are like oaths. They fail exactly when you need them most.

So I write orders tonight, and I will write them plainly, because my inquisitors sometimes mistake severity for cruelty and I want no confusion. Bring the heir to me alive. Do not harm the girl—or boy, or whatever the blood has produced—until I have explained. That word *explained* matters. Everyone assumes I will torture the heir, break the heir, burn the heir, and I confess the assumption wounds me. I have no interest in cruelty. What I intend is far more merciful than anything the gods ever offered their faithful.

I intend to offer the heir a choice.

Kneel beside me and help me finish the work—help me starve the last embers of the old law until no voice anywhere can ever again sell a child to a river—and live in honor as the person who freed the world a second time, with a name in the histories instead of a grave. Or refuse, and die as Maerin died, holding the hand of someone who wishes it could be otherwise.

There. Is that so monstrous? A tyrant offers annihilation; I offer a partnership and a clean death as the alternative. If that makes me a monster, then the word has lost all meaning, along with most of the others.

* * *

A knock at the door of my study—Captain Verren, snow in his hair, a dispatch in his fist and something odd in his face. Verren is not a man given to oddness. He has watched me sign death warrants with the same expression he uses for signing grain requisitions, which I have always counted among his virtues.

"Word from the Ashen Road, Majesty." He sets the paper down carefully, the way a man handles a snake he suspects is dead but isn't sure. "The ward on Vaelmoor is failing. Our watchers say the border stones are burning away—light coming off them like coals. And there's more."

He hesitates. I let him. Men need their small dramas; it is how they metabolize the large ones.

"A knight. Ser Ivo Marchetti. The disgraced one, from the Corvane purge. He crossed into the valley ahead of our riders and—" Verren clears his throat. "Witnesses say he warned the villagers, sire. Told them we were coming."

Marchetti. Of course. The sentimental fool who threw away his knighthood rather than burn a chapel full of god-touched children—children who, incidentally, were not god-touched, merely imaginative, but try telling Ivo Marchetti that. He has spent six years being noble at my expense, and now he chooses my heir's hiding place to perform his penance.

I should be angry. Instead I find myself almost grateful. A man like Marchetti cannot stay hidden; his conscience is a lantern. Wherever he stands, the righteous gather around him like moths, and moths, whatever else may be said of them, are easy to follow.

"The valley," I say. "Everything we have. Quietly. If the heir runs, I want the roads closed before her feet touch them. And Verren—"

"Majesty?"

"If she comes to me willing, she is to be fed, warmed, and treated with more courtesy than you have ever shown anyone in your life. If she runs—" I dip my pen. "Then run her down gently. I mean that. Gently. The world has had enough of kings who break what they catch."

Verren bows and goes, and I sit alone with the lamp and the map and the memory of a seventeen-year-old girl's hand in mine, trusting me to have an answer.

I do have an answer, Maerin. It took me nineteen years to build, and it is written in ash across half a kingdom, and soon—very soon now—it will stand face to face with the last voice of the old law, and one of us will learn what the world is going to be.

Outside my window, the wind comes down off the mountains smelling of snow and something older underneath it. Something like fur. Something like a growl held just below hearing.

They are waking. Good.

Let them come. I have been waiting nineteen years to finish the argument.
