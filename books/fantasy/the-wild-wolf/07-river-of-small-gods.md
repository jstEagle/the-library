## 7. River of Small Gods

I had been a priestess for eleven years and an apostate for two, and in all that time I had never once considered that the gods might follow *me*.

That was the trouble with abandoning a faith. You assumed it worked both ways.

We came down to the river on the fourth morning out of the checkpoint hills, and I knew before I saw it that something was wrong with the water. Rivers have a sound — a patient, indifferent sound, the sound of something that has been going about its business since before your grandmother's grandmother and will continue long after your name has worn off every stone you ever carved. This river was not making that sound. It was making a sound like a room full of people who have just noticed you standing in the doorway.

"You hear that too," Noor said. Not a question. She'd stopped three paces behind me, Fen a grey shadow at her hip, and she was wearing the expression I'd come to think of as her listening face — head tilted, eyes unfocused, like a woman eavesdropping on a conversation through a wall.

"I don't *hear* anything," I said. "I feel it. Which is worse."

"What does it feel like?"

"Like being watched by something that owes me money." I adjusted my pack straps, which is what I do instead of praying. "Or the other way around."

Ivo said nothing. He'd said very little since the pass, since the checkpoint, since whatever conversation he was having with himself about the shape of his life. He just looked at the river with the flat assessment of a man counting how many ways it could kill him.

Fen walked to the bank, sniffed once, and sat down with his tail wrapped around his feet in the manner of a wolf settling in for something long and instructive.

"Oh good," I said. "The dog agrees with me."

"He's not a dog," Noor murmured.

"He's not *just* a dog. There's a difference, and I suspect we're all going to learn it the hard way."

* * *

Here is what you should know about me, so the rest of this makes sense: I was raised in the cloister of Saint Vessa-of-the-Deep-Well, one of the last orders that kept the old rites after Aldric's purge made them fashionable to burn. The new church — Aldric's church, the church of the Unclouded Flame — taught that the gods were tyrants, that their law was a leash, that mankind had been freed when the Godstone shattered. My order taught something quieter: that the old powers were not gods so much as neighbors, and that you lived beside them the way you lived beside weather. You didn't worship the rain. You built a roof, and you left a bowl of milk out anyway, because it cost you little and the world was mostly made of things you did not understand.

Then the inquisitors came to our well, and I ran, and I told myself I had left the faith behind like a wet cloak.

Standing at that riverbank, I began to suspect the cloak had been following me.

"They're waking," Noor said softly. "The small ones. The little godlings — stream-spirits, field-mothers, the ones too small for Aldric to bother killing. They've been asleep inside the ward for a generation, and now—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened in that way it did when the beasts were saying something she didn't like. "Now they're hungry."

"Hungry for what?"

"Passage costs," she said. "It's old law. Older than the covenant, maybe. You cross a spirit's water, you pay a toll. Everyone used to know this. There were tolling-stones at every ford." She glanced at me, and there was something almost apologetic in it. "You would know this better than me. Your order kept the rites."

"My order kept the *songs*," I said. "Songs are not the same thing as belief. I sang to spirits for eleven years and never once expected an answer. That was rather the appeal."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing here hoping desperately that the water doesn't say anything, which I believe is the theological definition of hypocrisy."

Fen made a sound low in his chest — not quite a growl, not quite a laugh. I've decided wolves laugh. It helps me sleep.

* * *

We followed the river north because north was where we were going and the river was the only path that didn't climb. By midday the feeling of being watched had sharpened into something with opinions. The water ran wrong around our ankles at the shallows — curling toward us, tasting us. Once I saw a fish hold itself motionless against the current for the full length of ten heartbeats, facing me, its eye flat and ancient, before it flicked away like a servant dismissing me.

"They know what you are," Noor said quietly, walking beside me.

"I'm nobody. I'm an apostate with sore feet."

"They know what you *were*. A priestess walks into waking country carrying abandoned vows like old scars, and everything spiritual turns to look." She hesitated. "They're asking about you. The spirits. Fen says—"

"I don't want to know what Fen says."

"Sela."

"*Fine.* What does Fen say."

"He says they remember the well-cloisters. He says they remember being sung to, and then being sung about, and then not at all." Noor's voice was gentle in the way that gentle can be cruel. "He says they think you came back."

"I didn't come back. I never intended—"

"I know."

"I *ran*."

"I know that too." She touched my arm, brief, warm. "But nobody asked them what they thought, did they? Nobody ever does. That's rather the whole problem."

* * *

The ford found us at dusk — a wide, shallow stretch where the river spread thin over pale stones, and in the middle of it stood a tolling-stone, exactly as Noor had described: waist-high, moss-eaten, carved with spirals so old the chisel-marks had weathered to suggestions. Someone had once set offerings in the cup worn into its crown. The cup held rainwater and one dead leaf.

Ivo crouched at the bank and studied the far side the way he studied everything, as if it might contain soldiers. "Good crossing. Hard ground either side. We take it now or we camp wet."

"We don't take it at all," Noor said. "Not without paying."

"It's a river, Noor."

"It's a *neighbor*, Ivo. And the neighbors are awake." She waded out to the tolling-stone, water swirling silver around her calves, and laid her palm flat on the moss. Fen sat at the bank's edge, watchful, ears forward. I stayed where I was, because my legs had decided independently that they were not moving, and honestly I respected their judgment.

Noor went still. Listening. I watched her lips move, faintly, shaping words in no language I knew — and then, horribly, in one I did. Old liturgical Vessan, the tongue of the deep-well songs, the tongue I had sworn I'd never use again except in dreams.

She turned back to us. "They want a price."

"Gold?" Ivo asked.

"No. Gold means nothing to them." She looked at me across the water, and I felt the bottom drop out of the evening. "They want a voice. Someone to speak for the crossing — the old way. A priestess's word, freely given, vouching that we come without malice. They won't take it from me. I'm not theirs. Ivo's oath belongs to a dead king. But you—"

"No."

"Sela—"

"I *left*." My voice cracked on it, which was undignified, and I pressed on regardless because dignity was a luxury for people whose gods stayed dead. "Eleven years I knelt on cold stone and sang to powers that never answered, and when the inquisitors burned the well-house I understood that I'd been talking to empty air my whole life, and I *left*, and I was right to leave, and I am not going to stand in a river and beg the attention of things that ignored me while I bled for them—"

The river rose.

Not much. A handspan, maybe less. But it rose all at once, smooth as a drawn breath, and every sound of it stopped — the whole river silent as held air — and in that silence I heard, clearly, unmistakably, from everywhere and nowhere, a voice like water over gravel:

***We did not ignore you. We were sleeping.***

I sat down. In the shallows, fully clothed, pack and all, because my legs resigned en masse.

"You were *sleeping*," I repeated.

***The ward slept us. The valley slept us. All the small ones, dreaming in the dark under the water, while men above forgot the tolls and the songs and the names.*** The voice was not angry. That was the worst part. It was tired, the way an old woman is tired, the way a dog is tired after waiting by a door. ***You sang anyway. Eleven years. Did you think we could not hear even sleeping? Songs go down deep, little sister of the well. Yours reached us. We dreamed to them.***

"That's—" My throat closed. I started again. "That's monstrously unfair. You let me believe—"

***What could we give you? A sign? In the ward? With the inquisitor-fires on the hills?*** Something moved in the current — shapes, many small shapes, fish and light and things that were neither, circling the tolling-stone where Noor stood with her hand still on the moss, watching me with an expression of unbearable kindness. ***A sign would have killed you. Silence kept you alive. Was that not also love?***

And there it was. The thing I'd been running from for two years, caught in a sentence spoken by a river.

I had believed faith was a refuge. That was why I'd left it — it hadn't sheltered me when the fires came, hadn't saved the well-house, hadn't done a single thing I'd secretly wanted gods to do. But sitting in cold water up to my ribs, listening to a river explain itself, I finally understood the error in the ledger. Faith was never a wall against the storm. It was a relationship — messy, unpayable, sustained across silence and absence and years of nothing — and relationships do not stop existing because one party is asleep. They only wait.

"You could have written," I said, because I am Sela, and I grieve through insolence.

The river laughed. I swear on every dead saint, the river *laughed* — a bubbling, chuckling rush — and the small shapes spun gleefully in the current.

***Cross,*** it said. ***All of you. The toll is paid.***

"Paid by what?" Ivo demanded, hand on his sword, because Ivo trusted nothing, including rivers, especially rivers. "She didn't give anything."

"She gave the word," Noor said quietly. "Freely given. That was the price. It always was." She looked down at me, dripping, wrecked, half-laughing despite myself. "They didn't want a performance, Sela. They wanted to be spoken to. Like everyone else I've ever met."

* * *

We crossed at last light, the water warm around our knees — warmer than it had any right to be in autumn, warm as a held hand. On the far bank I turned back once. The tolling-stone stood black against the sunset, and the cup at its crown, I could have sworn, no longer held rainwater and a dead leaf but something bright, something offered.

Noor fell into step beside me. Fen trotted ahead. Behind us, somewhere up the darkening valley, I heard — or imagined, though I no longer believed in imagining such things — the distant baying of hounds, and beneath the hounds, thinner and colder, the brazen call of an inquisitor's horn.

"They've crossed the ridge," Ivo said grimly, reading the wind. "A day behind us. Maybe less."

Noor's hand found Fen's ruff. "Then we keep the river," she said. "It seems to like us."

I looked back at the water flowing silver in the dusk, carrying its thousand waking neighbors toward the sea, and I thought: eleven years singing to the dark, and the dark was listening the whole time.

God help me. I think I'm a priestess again.

And the river, laughing softly around my ankles, seemed to agree.
