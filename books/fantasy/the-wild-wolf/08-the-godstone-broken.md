## 8. The Godstone Broken

The mountain told us we were close before Fen did. I'd learned by then that stone has a voice too—slower than beasts, slower than rivers, a voice like a sentence that takes a hundred years to finish—and the higher we climbed, the more it groaned. Not in pain, exactly. In *incompleteness*. Like a man missing his teeth trying to say your name.

"The Godstone," Sela said, when we broke through the last stand of black pines and saw it.

I had imagined something grander, if I'm honest. A temple. A spire. Something with steps you could ascend dramatically. What stood at the crown of the pass instead was a wound: a cliff face split down the middle, and set into the split, the shattered remains of a stone taller than any tower in Vaelmoor. Half of it had fallen into the valley below centuries ago—or maybe not so long ago, maybe only since Aldric's men came with their hammers and their certainty. The remaining half jutted from the rock like a broken tooth, veined with lines that still faintly glowed, gold-white, pulsing out of rhythm. Out of breath.

"It's dying," Sela whispered.

"It's been dying for twenty years," Fen said. "It's just stubborn." The wolf sat on his haunches at my left side, and even now, after everything—the checkpoint, the river of small gods, the three weeks of hard road—I still did a small internal stumble every time he talked. A wolf's voice is not a man's voice. It's wind over snow with opinions.

"And me breaking it further?" I said. "That helps how, exactly?"

"You're not breaking anything. You're listening to what's left."

"Listening. Right." I flexed my hands. They were shaking, and I pretended they weren't. "Because my whole life, listening went so well for everyone around me."

"Noor." Sela touched my arm. Her priestess's braid had come half undone somewhere outside Corren Ford, and she'd stopped fixing it days ago, as if she were done performing faithfulness to anything except what she actually believed. "Whatever happens up there—we came this far together. That counts. Whatever the old books say about worthiness, it counts."

"It doesn't count against inquisitors' arrows," Ivo said from behind us.

Ser Ivo Marchetti had a gift for ruining moments, and I'd come to love him for it the way you love a roof in a rainstorm—without sentiment, purely for function. He stood a little apart, checking the straps on his shield, the one with the Marchetti sunburst half scratched away by his own hand back when disgrace seemed like the worst thing that would ever happen to him. His armor was dented, mismatched, and filthy. He looked better in it than he ever had in a lord's hall. He knew it, too. That was the joke neither of us said out loud anymore.

"How many do you think?" he asked Fen.

The wolf's ears swiveled. "Thirty on the ridge above the stone. Twelve in the shrine ruins below. And horses. Maybe forty." A pause. "And one who smells like cold iron and old prayers. Their captain."

"Inquisitor-Captain Veyre," Sela said, and spat, which was very un-priestly of her and which I approved of completely.

"They're waiting for us," I said. "Not chasing. Waiting." My stomach did the slow cold drop it had been practicing since Vaelmoor. "They knew where the covenant heir would have to go. There's nowhere else. It's the only door."

"So we don't use the door," Ivo said. "We go through the window." He pointed—not at the stone, but at the ridgeline east of it, where the mountain's shoulder dropped into a scree chute narrow enough that forty men couldn't stand abreast, and steep enough that forty men would be thinking mostly about their footing rather than their swords. "One man holds that chute, the rest of you reach the stone."

"One man," Sela repeated flatly.

"I've held worse ground with worse men behind me." Ivo buckled the shield strap with a click that sounded terribly final. "Also, I'm the slowest climber here, and I'm the only one of us Aldric's men have no reason to keep alive. No offense meant to the rest of you, but I'm the most expendable person on this mountain."

"That's not—" I started.

"It absolutely is," he said, wry as ever, "and I've made peace with it, which is more than I managed to make with anything else in the last ten years, so let me have it."

* * *

We argued while we climbed. Or rather, I argued and Ivo kept saying things like *we'll discuss it at the top* and *save your breath, Kavanagh, you'll need your lungs for god-bothering.* The scree chute was exactly as bad as it looked. Twice I went down to my elbows in sliding stone, and twice Fen took my sleeve in his teeth and hauled until my feet found purchase again. Above us the sky was the color of an old scar, and the Godstone's broken glow lit the clouds from beneath like a fire under a pot.

At the fork in the trail—one path bending toward the shrine ruins and the waiting twelve, one skirting along the chute's mouth toward the stone—Ivo stopped walking.

"This is where we discuss it at the top," he said.

"Ivo—"

"Noor Kavanagh." He turned and looked at me, and there was nothing wry in his face at all, which frightened me more than the forty men on the ridge. "Ten years ago I held a bridge for a lord who didn't deserve it, and when the order came to fall back, I obeyed, and the people behind me burned. I've been falling back ever since. Running, warning, hiding, apologizing." He smiled, small and crooked. "You gave me something to stand in front of. Let me stand."

"There are twelve of them."

"Twelve men in armor on bad ground against a knight who no longer cares whether he lives." He rolled his shoulders. "Honestly, I like my odds. First time in a decade."

Fen pressed his flank against Ivo's leg—a wolf's blessing, or a wolf's goodbye; I couldn't tell and didn't ask, because my throat had closed.

Sela kissed her own thumb and pressed it to Ivo's forehead, some fragment of the old rite she probably wasn't supposed to remember. "The gods see you," she said.

"Then they can watch me work." He turned down the chute path, drew his sword, and called back without turning around: "Kavanagh. When you talk to whatever's left of that stone—tell it the Marchetti line kept faith after all. Even if it was late."

Then he was gone, and the sound of him—boots on stone, steel clearing leather—faded into wind, and I stood there like an idiot because there was nothing in me that knew how to leave someone on purpose.

Fen nudged me. "*Climb,*" he said. "Grief is a debt. Pay it later. Climb now."

So I climbed.

* * *

We heard the fighting before we saw the stone—a clash and echo, a shout, then another shout cut short. Every one of those sounds went through me like a nail. I counted them. Three clashes, two shouts, one long terrible silence, then steel ringing on steel again and again, stubborn as a heartbeat. *Still holding. Still holding. Don't stop. Don't you dare stop.*

Sela and I came out of the rocks below the Godstone's fallen half, and the twelve men in the shrine ruins never saw us, because they were all looking east, toward the chute, toward the ringing. Toward Ivo.

The stone itself was warm. I felt it before I touched it—warmth like a body, like a fever, like a hand held over a candle too long. Up close the glowing veins weren't veins at all. They were script. Old script, older than any alphabet Sela had taught me on the road, and it moved, slowly, the way frost moves across a window. Words trying to finish themselves and failing.

I put both palms against it.

The world opened.

I don't know how else to say it. I have spent my whole life hearing voices—beasts, birds, once, mortifyingly, a bishop's mule—and I thought I understood what listening was. I did not. The stone didn't speak *to* me. It spoke *everything*: the memory of the pact laid down when the world was younger, human law and beast law and god law braided into one cord, each strand answering for the others. The Wolf Covenant. I felt its shape the way you feel the shape of your own name. And I felt the break, too—a clean severing, twenty years old, done not by hammer but by *oath*, by a guardian of the covenant kneeling at this very stone and swearing himself free of it, and the stone taking the wound into itself rather than letting the break kill everything downstream.

Aldric. Even his betrayal had been careful. Even his treason had been tidy.

*I can be mended,* the stone said, in a voice that was not a voice but a weight behind my ribs. *But a law needs a mouth. Beast cannot speak for man. God cannot speak for either. The cord must run through a willing voice—one who hears all three realms and chooses them.*

"I hear beasts," I said aloud, stupidly, my lips moving against warm stone. "I've never heard a god. I don't want to speak for anyone. I wanted a farm. I wanted—" I laughed, or something near it. "I wanted to be nobody. That was the whole plan. Nobody, with a goat."

*The stone does not ask for your want,* it said, almost gently. *It asks for your yes.*

Below us, the ringing stopped.

Just—stopped. Mid-stroke. And the silence afterward was the worst sound of the entire war, and I have since heard cities burn.

"Noor." Sela's hand found mine. Her voice was steady the way a rope bridge is steady: functional, terrifying, held together by belief. "Noor, look at me. Whatever it asked—it hasn't taken your answer yet. Nothing's taken anything from you yet. But if you say no, everything else gets taken anyway. Everyone. Everywhere. So choose, but choose knowing that 'no' isn't safe. There is no 'safe' left on this mountain."

Fen stepped up beside me, and the air around him shivered. For one moment I saw him doubled—wolf, and something vast behind the wolf, grey as winter, eyes like the space between stars—and then just my wolf again, my impossible, opinionated friend.

"*Say it,*" Fen said softly. "I'll be beside you either way. That part isn't the stone's. That part's mine."

Down the slope, torchlight was moving. Forty men, descending from the ridge, unhurried. They had all the time in the world, because they knew the door was theirs.

And far below, in the chute, I let myself feel it—one breath's worth, no more: Ivo's shield going down, the Marchetti sunburst in the dust, a man keeping faith at last. *Tell it,* he'd said. *Tell it we kept faith.*

"Yes," I said to the stone.

The light under my palms flared white-gold, and the mountain began, at last, to finish its sentence.
