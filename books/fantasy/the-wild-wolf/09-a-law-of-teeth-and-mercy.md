## 9. A Law of Teeth and Mercy

They say you can hear an army before you see it, but that's a lie told by people who've never stood on a mountain with the wind at their back. What you hear first is nothing. A hole in the world's noise. The birds go quiet, then the insects, and then your own heartbeat sounds like a drum in an empty hall.

I heard the hole in the world from the top of the Godstone's shattered crown, three days after we buried what was left of Ser Ivo Marchetti beneath a cairn of grey stones.

"He's coming," Sela said. She didn't need to say who. There was only one man alive with enough soldiers to fill a horizon with dust.

Fen stood at the edge of the ruin with her head raised, ears forward, reading the wind the way Ivo used to read maps. When she turned to me, her eyes were gold as struck coins.

*He is faster than I thought,* she said. *Three days. Perhaps two.*

"Then we'd best be quick."

*Noor.* Fen crossed the broken flags to me, and her voice in my skull went gentle, which frightened me more than shouting would have. *You know what it will cost. Say it aloud. To yourself, at least.*

I looked out over the valley below the stone—the green thread of the river, the dark scatter of the free cities' watchfires, the little lights of people who would burn or kneel if Aldric won here. Somewhere down there were villages like mine. Girls like me, hiding gifts in their teeth because the world had taught them to swallow.

"I know," I said. "The covenant needs a voice. Not a priestess, not a king. Someone who can speak for all three realms, and be bound by all three in turn." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "You know the funny part? All my life I wanted one thing. To be left alone. To tend my goats and talk to my animals where nobody could hear, and die old and unremarkable in Vaelmoor, where the ward kept everything quiet."

*And now?*

"Now the quiet's gone anyway." I spread my hands. "Aldric saw to that. So I get to choose what fills the silence instead. That's not such a bad trade, when you put it that way."

It was a lie, and Fen knew it was a lie, and I knew she knew, and that was its own kind of mercy between us—that neither of us said so.

We spent the last day preparing, though there was almost nothing to prepare. The Godstone rose above us in fragments, a mountain that some god had shattered like dropped crockery: slabs the size of barns tilted against each other, hollows full of old light, channels carved with script in three alphabets—human, beast, and divine—running together like rivers meeting at a delta. Sela walked those channels all day, reading aloud, weeping, laughing, cursing her order for burying this. At dusk she came to me with her prayer beads wrapped around her fist hard enough to leave marks.

"It wants words," she said. "Not mine. Yours. The stone's been waiting centuries for someone it believes will actually speak."

"That makes two of us," I said. "Waiting, I mean. I'm still not sure about speaking."

Sela smiled, and it wobbled. "When I ran away from the temple, my teacher told me faith is a relationship, not a refuge. I didn't understand until the river spirits made me pay for passage. I don't think you understand either, Noor. But you're about to."

* * *

The army arrived at dawn, and they did not come quietly.

They poured up the mountain road like spilled ink running uphill—ten thousand men, maybe more, banners black with Aldric's sigil, a broken circle on a field of iron-grey. The sound of them was worse than any hole in the world: boots, armor, drums, the low animal hum of that many humans wanting one thing.

At their head rode a single figure on a white horse, and even at half a mile I knew him. Not from portraits—I'd seen those, everyone had—but from the way the air bent around him, the way beasts flinched at his passage even through the wind. Aldric. The Usurper's Mercy, they called him in the cities he'd spared. The Butcher of the Covens everywhere else.

He stopped his horse at the foot of the shattered stone and looked up. Even across that distance, I felt his eyes find me, and there was no hate in them. That was the worst part. He looked at me the way a surgeon looks at a wound.

"Noor Kavanagh!" His voice carried impossibly, the way voices carry in holy places. Maybe the stone carried it for him. Maybe that was his last courtesy to the old law, using its own throat. "Daughter of Vaelmoor! Last heir of the Wolf Covenant! I offer you the mercy I offered no coven: walk away. Live unknown. I will even let you keep your wolf."

"And what do you get?" I called back, and my voice cracked like a girl's, because I was a girl, and I was terrified, and there was no shame in that.

"A world with no masters," he said. "I was the covenant's sworn guardian once, girl. I knelt on this very stone and swore to protect the pact between realms. And do you know what I learned, kneeling there?" He dismounted, slowly, an old soldier's movements. "That the gods are tyrants. That the beasts are wolves, whatever pretty names we give them. That men starve while spirits demand tolls. I broke the Godstone to FREE you. Every death since has been the price of freedom—and yours will be too, unless you climb down and live small, the way humans were meant to."

"You freed mankind," Sela muttered beside me, "by making it prey."

But I stepped to the very edge of the highest slab, where the wind pulled at my hair, and I looked down at him for a long moment.

"You're right about one thing," I called. "The old covenant was flawed. The gods demanded. The beasts took. Men suffered. I've read what the stone remembers." I let that land. Let him wonder how much I knew. "So no, King Aldric. I won't restore the old law."

Confusion flickered across his face—the first honest expression I'd seen on it. "Then what—"

"The gods are tyrants? Fine. Then bind them tighter. Beasts are wolves? Then give them law. And men—" I looked down at ten thousand soldiers, and past them, at everything behind them. "Men want a voice. So I'll give all three realms exactly what you wanted to give mankind, Aldric. A law no master owns. Including you."

And I began to speak.

* * *

I don't remember choosing the words. That's the truth nobody asks for—they want drama, a struggle, a mortal wrestling with divine language. But the words were already in me, had been since Fen first spoke my name inside the dead ward. They came up through my feet from the broken stone, through my chest from the wolf beside me, through my mouth from somewhere older than my mouth, and all I had to do was not stop.

*I speak for the realm of flesh*, the covenant said through me, in a voice that was mine and wasn't. *Let no god command what breathes.*

*I speak for the realm of hide and fang. Let no man hunt what runs without cause, and no beast take what is freely given.*

*I speak for the realm of sky and silence. Let no power—crown, claw, or choir—stand above the law that binds all three alike.*

Below me, the world reacted. The soldiers' horses screamed and reared—not in pain, in recognition. Every hound in ten thousand packs sat down as one. In the free cities, I would learn later, every caged bird sang at dawn and every door unlocked itself. And the stone—

The stone woke. Light ran the channels like blood returning to a numb limb, filling the three scripts, climbing the shattered slabs, and the fragments of the Godstone groaned and shifted, drawing toward each other across centuries of ruin, not whole again but *held*—a broken bone, splinted by law.

And Aldric's own oath turned on him.

I saw it happen. Saw the guardian's brand on his forearm—he'd pushed back his sleeve unconsciously, some old habit—begin to glow white-hot. He cried out and gripped it with his other hand, and the horse beneath him bolted, and he stayed standing because the oath held him upright, forced him to face me, forced him to *listen*.

"You swore to guard the covenant," the voice said through my throat, gentle as frost. "You broke your oath and called it mercy. The law remembers, Aldric. The law does not hate you. The law simply holds you to what you promised. Kneel."

He fought it. I'll grant him that—he fought it with everything in him, ten thousand men watching their king bend inch by inch like a bow being drawn. And in that moment I understood him completely, and pitied him completely, because he had done every terrible thing sincerely. Conviction mistaken for justice, Sela had said. He'd broken the world trying to save it, and now the world he'd saved-from-himself was asking him to kneel in front of it.

He knelt.

And the mountain exhaled.

* * *

When I came back to myself, I was on my knees too, and Fen's side was pressed warm against mine, and she was *enormous*.

She stood over me like a small hill, shoulders higher than my head, fur silver-grey shot through with light, eyes like twin dawns. The god-wolf shape. The shape the covenant remembered and returned to her, because a living voice needs a living shield.

*Well spoken,* she said, and her voice filled the whole mountainside, and ten thousand soldiers heard a wolf talk, and no one would ever be able to unhear it. *Now rise, Voice of the Covenant. Your people are waiting to be very confused.*

I got up. My legs shook. My throat felt scoured. Below, Aldric still knelt, bound by his own ancient promise, his army standing in perfect paralyzed silence around him.

"Kill me, then," he said, and there was almost relief in it.

"No," I said, in my own voice this time—hoarse, ordinary, human. "The new law doesn't work that way. You'll stand trial under all three realms. Beast-law judges hunters. God-law judges oaths. Man-law judges kings." I managed something that was almost a smile. "You wanted a world with no masters, Aldric. Congratulations. You're about to meet the first justice none of them control."

His guards surged forward—and stopped, because every wolf, hawk, horse, and hound within a mile had turned to face them, and behind those animals, faint as morning mist, something else had begun to gather. The old gods, waking, bound now, *held* by law rather than hunger. Watching. Waiting to see if mankind meant what its Voice had just spoken.

Sela reached my side, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face. "You did it," she whispered. "You actually—"

"We did it," I corrected, and leaned into Fen's vast warm flank. "All three of us."

But as the sun cleared the eastern ridge and lit the splinted stone gold, Fen went rigid against me, her ears swiveling north, and a growl rolled out of her chest deep enough to shake gravel.

*Voice,* she said. *Something is coming that was not invited to the binding.*

I followed her gaze to the northern horizon, where the sky had begun, very quietly, to bruise.
