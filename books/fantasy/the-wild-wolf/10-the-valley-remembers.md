## 10. The Valley Remembers

The valley of Vaelmoor forgot me for twenty-three years, and now it can't stop remembering.

I knew we were close when the birds started talking about us.

"Two women and a very large dog," a magpie reported from a fencepost as we crested the last hill above the village. "Walking slow. The tall one looks hungry. The short one looks like she's about to cry."

"I'm not going to cry," I told the magpie.

"Nobody said you were," said the magpie, which was true, which is the trouble with talking to birds. They repeat what you don't say back to you until you hear it.

Fen made a sound low in his chest that I've learned to translate as *let her have her dignity*. It's remarkable how much dignity a person can keep when a wolf the size of a pony walks beside them like a bodyguard who's seen too much.

Sela squinted down at the village — at the smoke rising straight from chimneys, at the fields gone green-gold where they should have been gray with blight, at the shrine by the well that someone had repainted without asking anyone's permission, because no one needed permission anymore. "It looks different," she said.

"It looks the same," I said. "That's what's different."

For my whole life, Vaelmoor had been a held breath. A valley under an old god's ward where nothing woke up, nothing spoke, nothing remembered — including, we all pretended, me. Now magic ran through it like meltwater through March soil, quiet and insistent, finding every crack. The wheat grew in patterns nobody planted. The goats had opinions and expressed them. Old Marta's cat had taken up preaching on her roofline, and Marta claimed she found it less annoying than most sermons.

And every soul in the valley knew my name now, which is the one thing I spent twenty-three years making sure would never happen.

Funny thing about getting exactly what you feared most: sometimes you survive it, and then it's just Tuesday.

* * *

We came down through the orchard, because I wanted to arrive on my own terms, which apparently means arriving past the tree where I buried my childhood — or rather, where my mother buried the knife I'd used to talk to a dying fox, because even a knife that has heard beast-speech shouldn't be left lying around in a valley that forgets.

The apple trees had woken up too. They leaned as we passed. I felt it like fingers brushing my hair.

"You're home," the oldest tree said, in the creaking way trees talk, which takes about a minute per word if you're patient and about three seconds per word if you're not, and after everything, I am patient now. Mostly.

"We're home," I said.

"The girl comes back," the tree said, and then, because trees have no sense of drama whatsoever, "the codlins will be poor this year."

Sela laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, the kind I hadn't heard since before the Godstone. "The oracle of Vaelmoor," she said, "and its first prophecy is about apples."

"It's not a prophecy, it's a complaint." I touched the bark. Under my palm, the whole orchard murmured, a low green sound like a crowd settling. "Everything here complains. It's how they say hello."

Fen pressed his shoulder against my leg, nearly knocking me over, which is also how he says hello. Some things don't change, even when everything else does.

* * *

My mother met us at the gate.

She didn't run to me. My mother doesn't run; she considers running and decides against it on principle. She stood there with flour on her hands and her jaw set the way it sets when she's determined not to feel something yet, and she looked at Fen for a long moment, and at Sela, and at me, and then she said:

"You're thin."

"Hello, Mother."

"And you brought a wolf into my yard."

"He prefers—"

"And your hair needs cutting." Her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, just enough, and then she crossed the yard and held onto me with both floury hands gripping my coat like she could anchor me to the ground of this valley by main force, and maybe she could. Maybe that's what mothers do.

Over her shoulder, I saw the door of our house. Above it, nailed into the lintel with four iron nails driven deep, hung a shield.

Ser Ivo Marchetti's shield. Dented, scorched, the painted stag half burned away, carried out of the pass at the Godstone by soldiers who didn't know whose it was and would have thrown it on the midden heap if a runaway priestess hadn't threatened them with excommunication from a faith she'd technically abandoned. Sela had carried it eight hundred miles. I had nailed it up myself two days after we buried him under the rowan on the hill, because Ivo never talked much about his gods, but he did once say that a knight's shield belongs over a door, guarding whatever's behind it.

So now it guards my mother's kitchen. He'd have hated that. He'd have hated it so much he'd have stood under it every night checking that it was straight.

"It's crooked," my mother said into my shoulder.

"It's not crooked."

"It's a finger-width off, Noor. I've looked at it every morning for five months."

I pulled back and looked. It was a finger-width off. Of course it was. I'd nailed it up crying, and crying people are terrible carpenters.

"Leave it," I said.

She studied my face, and whatever she saw there, she nodded slowly. "Leave it," she agreed. And then, briskly, because grief in our family is handled the way bread dough is handled — firmly, briefly, and then put somewhere warm to rise — "There's stew. Both of you. The wolf can eat outside, he knows what he did."

"What did he do?" Sela asked.

"He ate the entire Easter ham in his sleep," my mother said darkly, leading the way inside. "In his sleep, Sela. I watched it happen."

"That was one time," Fen said, "and it was spiritual."

No one asked him to explain. We'd all learned better.

* * *

After supper — real supper, with my mother's bread and Sela telling the story of the river spirits for the fourth time because my mother kept interrupting to ask questions like a woman who'd spent her life under a silence ward and intended to make up for lost wonder — I went out to the well.

The valley at night, these days. That deserves describing, because I want to remember describing it, when I'm old and the burden has worn grooves in me.

Fireflies that weren't fireflies drifting over the barley in slow constellations, spelling nothing, just being beautiful, which minor spirits do when they're feeling generous. The wind carrying actual voices — not words, mostly, just the sense of being spoken to, the way a dog understands a tone. Somewhere on the far hills, the old standing stones humming their one long note that they'd been holding, silent, for centuries, and now couldn't seem to stop. And overhead, stars that seemed closer than they used to be, though Sela insists that's impossible and I insist she's welcome to tell the sky that.

I sat on the well's edge. After a while, Fen came and lay across my feet, heavy as a fallen beam, warm as a hearth.

"They're asking for you tomorrow," he said.

"I know."

"The free cities' envoys. The Harrowgate delegation arrives at midday, and the marsh-folk sent a speaker upriver, and there's a man from the coast cities who talks to fish, apparently, and wants to compare notes."

"A fish-talker," I said. "There are more of us."

"There were always more of us. You were just the only one in a valley that forgot." He rested his chin on my knee. "You could refuse the meeting. Let Sela speak for the covenant's interests. Let the law run itself for a season. The covenant doesn't need you hovering over every negotiation like a—"

"Like a mother wolf over a den?"

"I was going to say 'like a magistrate over a market.' But yes."

I scratched behind his ears, in the spot that makes his hind leg twitch, undignifying a creature who wears the shape of a god-wolf when it suits him. "Do you know what I miss?" I said. "Silence. Not the ward's silence. Real silence. Before all this, when a bird spoke, it was just — a bird sound. Beautiful and meaningless. Now everything means. Every crow has a position on grain tariffs. The river has views."

"You don't mean that."

"No," I admitted. "I don't. But I want it noted that I miss it anyway. The covenant gets my voice. It doesn't get my honesty about how tired I am."

"Noted," Fen said gravely. "The wolf remembers."

And that's the thing, isn't it. That's the whole thing, the thing I understood at the broken Godstone with Ivo's blood on the rocks and Aldric's army coming down the pass like a flood with banners: the covenant was never a spell. It was a memory that three realms agreed to share. Human law, beast law, god law, each keeping the others honest, each remembering what the others forget. Humans forget fast. Gods forget sideways. Beasts — beasts remember perfectly, but only what matters: where the water is, who is kin, what was promised.

Aldric broke it because he thought the gods were tyrants. And maybe they were, some of them, some of the time — I've negotiated with enough river spirits now to know the old law had teeth in places teeth didn't belong. But his answer was to make mankind the only voice at the table, and a table with one voice isn't a council. It's a throne. He traded tyranny for loneliness and called it freedom, and when the balance snapped, the world started screaming in ways only I could hear.

Now the world hears itself. And it needs someone at the table who remembers being nobody. Who remembers hiding. Who knows exactly what it costs to be unheard.

That's me. That's my job description, written in blood and apples and a crooked shield over my mother's door.

* * *

The envoys arrived at midday, as promised. The Harrowgate delegation wore silk and distrust in equal measure. The marsh-speaker smelled of reeds and had eyes like a heron's, flat and patient. The fish-talker turned out to be a girl of perhaps fourteen, sunburned, barefoot, clutching a notebook full of herring negotiations, and when she saw me she stopped dead in my mother's yard and went white.

"You're real," she breathed. "They said — the stories said—"

"I'm real," I said. "And so are you. Come in. There's bread."

That's how it goes now. That's the work. Not thunder and reforged stones — those days are done, thank every god still speaking to me. Just this: sitting down with frightened, gifted, stubborn people and building the memory again, piece by piece, voice by voice. A law of teeth and mercy, and mercy mostly, and teeth only where mercy would be cruelty.

Sela ran the meeting's opening prayers, having decided — in her words — that abandoning the old gods and being abandoned by them were two different things, and she was done confusing them. She's good at it. She cries a little every time, and pretends she doesn't, and everyone lets her have her dignity, which is a lesson she learned from watching a wolf.

Fen lay outside the circle in the sun, appearing to be asleep, missing nothing. When the Harrowgate envoy tried to slip a clause past the marsh-speaker — a clause about "managing" the beast-speechers, registering them, licensing them, the old fear wearing new clothes — Fen opened one eye, and the envoy's horse, tied at the gate, commented loudly and at length on the man's character, and the clause was withdrawn amid much laughter and some blushing.

The covenant protects its own. All of its own. Even the ones who flinch at the word *own*.

By evening it was done — the first accords signed under the new law, witnessed by human ink, beast presence, and three small spirits who lived in my mother's well and demanded, as their fee, that the envoys each compliment her bread. They meant it. The compliments were sincere. Everyone won.

Afterward, I walked the envoys to the road, and the fish-talk girl hung back and tugged my sleeve.

"Do you ever wish you could go back?" she asked. "To before? To being — normal?"

I looked back at the house. At the shield above the door, a finger-width crooked, staying that way. At Sela's candle in the window. At Fen's silhouette on the hill, huge against the stars, keeping watch out of habit and love in equal measure. At the orchard leaning toward the light of my mother's kitchen, murmuring its green complaints, its green hellos.

"Every day," I told her honestly. "And then I come home, and I remember that this is what before was hiding from. Go well, little sister. Talk to your fish. Write everything down."

She grinned and ran to catch the others, notebook clutched to her chest like a covenant of her own.

I stayed in the yard a while longer. The well spirits hummed. The rowan on the hill stirred, where Ivo sleeps under roots that will remember him forever, because beasts keep what matters and I asked them to.

"Come inside," my mother called from the doorway. "Before the wolves eat something."

"One ham," Fen said from the hill, wounded. "One ham, years ago."

I laughed — loud, unhidden, heard by everything in the valley, and everything in the valley, wondrously, mundanely, heard me back.

Then I went inside, closed the door gently on the whole singing world, and sat down at my mother's table, where there was always, always room for one more.
