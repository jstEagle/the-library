## 1. The Charm Seller's Hours

There are three hours in Ashvale when the law sleeps and I don't.

The Wardens call them the charm seller's hours—though they'd deny knowing the phrase, since admitting it would mean admitting the night market exists at all, and admitting that would mean doing paperwork about it, and the magistrates who run this city would rather drown in their own ink than write down a single true thing. So between the last bell of dusk and the first bell of dawn, the Lantern Row stalls unfold like moths from woodwork, and I sell luck to people who should know better.

I'm Emrys Nystrom, and everything I sell is stolen.

Not the charms themselves—I carve those honestly enough, little wooden birds and braided cords and glass beads the color of pond water. It's what's inside them that's contraband. A memory, folded small as a moth's wing and tucked into the hollow of a charm, will hold its shape for a season or two if you're careful. Buy one, wear it against your skin, and for an hour a night you get to be someone else: a woman who danced on the harbor ice before it cracked, a boy who saw his father laugh, anyone whose life was briefly better than yours. The memories are secondhand and slightly worn, like all good lies. Nobody in Ashvale can afford firsthand happiness.

The magic ban has been law for twelve years now. No spellcraft, no soul-work, no memory-craft—the magistrates were very thorough with the list. They burned the grimoires in Founder's Square and hanged three thaumaturges from the customs house and told us we'd all been saved from something terrible. What they actually did was outlaw the competition. You can't hoard what other people are free to sell.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and ahead of myself is where the trouble always starts.

* * *

My stall sits third from the corner of Lantern Row, between Osk's fence goods—he calls them "previously cherished," which is a lie so respectable it's practically a profession—and a woman named Dagny who sells soup that could strip rust. I've had that pitch for six years, which in Ashvale counts as generational wealth.

The trick to selling stolen memory isn't the selling. Anyone can smile and take coins; I have a face for smiling and hands for taking. The trick is reading. Every customer who drifts past my stall wearing that particular hunger—the look of someone missing something they can't name—is telling me exactly what they need, whether they know it or not.

The clerk's wife came at moonrise, as she does every ninth night, wringing her gloves. She never says what she wants. She just stands there until I hold up a charm and watch her eyes go soft.

"Harvest festival," I said tonight, lifting a glass bead on a cord. "A vineyard south of the city, thirty years back. You're seventeen. Somebody's playing a fiddle badly and you don't care."

She bought it without haggling, which is how you know a person is starving rather than shopping.

The lamplighter bought courage—an hour of standing on a cliff edge in wind, from a man who'd apparently done it for a living before the ban made his whole existence illegal retroactively. A dockhand bought sleep, which sounds strange until you understand that what I sold him wasn't rest but the *memory* of having once slept soundly, and his body remembered how, for a little while. Half my trade is people buying back things the city took from them without ever putting it in writing.

And then, near midnight, the sedan chair arrived.

You can tell a magistrate's chair by the way the bearers don't look at anything. Regular men carrying a chair watch the road. Magistrates' men watch nothing, because nothing is allowed to happen near their masters, and reality generally cooperates.

The chair set down outside the Row—not in it, gods forbid—and out climbed Magistrate Aldous Vane, Third Chair of the Revenue Court, seventy-some years old and drunker than the law permits anyone who isn't him. He dismissed his bearers, which magistrates do when they want to do something their own records shouldn't see, and he walked into my market like a man slumming through his own conscience.

I knew Vane by sight. Everyone does; his face is on the tax notices. What I knew better was his walk—that particular stagger of a man whose heart is failing him in real time. My mother died like that, walking. One minute she was crossing the kitchen, the next she was a lesson about mortality I've spent thirty years not thinking about.

Vane made it four stalls down before he sat down hard on Dagny's bench and didn't get up.

Now. Here is where a decent person calls for help. Here is where a careful person walks away. I am neither decent nor careful; I am a professional, and a dying magistrate alone in the night market is less a tragedy than an opportunity wearing nice boots.

Because here's the thing nobody says out loud: a dying man's mind opens. All the doors swing wide. And a memory-thief with quick hands and quicker nerve can walk through those doors and take what's on the shelves before the shop closes forever.

I've robbed the dying maybe a dozen times. It's not my favorite work. There's a moment—you'll have to trust me, or don't, I'm not your conscience—there's a moment when you slip into a stranger's last hour and you feel them feel you, this faint confusion, this sense of being visited. I always try to take only what they wouldn't miss. A childhood summer. A first love's face. The good stuff dies with them anyway, and the alternative is it goes into the ground unspent, which seems wasteful, and waste is the only sin I still believe in.

I knelt by the bench with a cup of Dagny's soup I hadn't paid for, playing the Good Samaritan, and put two fingers against Vane's wrist like I was checking his pulse. What I was checking was the door.

It swung open onto a house so grand it took me a moment to find the rooms that mattered.

Magistrates keep their minds like they keep their offices: front halls full of ceremony, back rooms full of ledgers. I moved past the ceremony—a wife, dead twenty years; a son in the provinces he hadn't written to—and found the ledger room, and there, filed under something dry and official, was a transaction.

I want to say I understood it immediately. I didn't. Memory-theft gives you images, not explanations. What I got was this:

A chamber beneath a mountain, lit red from below, like standing inside a banked coal. Rows and rows of shelves, floor to ceiling, and on every shelf a small light burning in a glass vessel—hundreds of them, thousands, each one pulsing gently, like fireflies kept patient. Clerks moving among the shelves with trolleys. Actual trolleys. Souls, inventoried.

And then a document, signed, with wax seals I half-recognized from tax notices, and a name written in the payment line that hit me like cold water down the back of the neck.

*Bryn.*

Nobody in Ashvale is named Bryn anymore. I made sure of that years ago, the way you make sure a wound stays closed—not by healing it but by never, ever looking at it.

I came out of Vane's dying mind fast, the way you come up from deep water, and nearly dropped the soup cup. His pulse stuttered under my fingers. His lips were gray. Whatever else he'd been, in that moment he was just an old man running out of hours, and I felt the usual brief shame, and I did what I always do with shame, which is file it somewhere I can't find it.

He died on Dagny's bench while I was still crouched beside him pretending to check a pulse I'd already pickpocketed. By the time anyone noticed, I'd melted back into my stall like smoke returning to a chimney.

Here's what I couldn't stop seeing, all the rest of that night, while I sold a widow her wedding day and a beggar a warm kitchen he'd never had: the payment line of that ledger, and the name on it. Not Bryn Nystrom—the seal didn't use surnames, it used something worse, some official designation, a catalog number, as if she were inventory. As if she were one of those small lights on a shelf under the mountain, pulsing patiently in her glass.

Twelve years ago my sister died in a fire. That's the story. That's the story the magistrates certified, the story I agreed to remember because remembering anything truer would have killed me too—or worse, sent me after whoever was truly responsible, and I was nineteen and unarmed and the responsible parties ran the entire city.

I built a life on top of that grave. I built it carefully, stone by stone: the stall, the charms, the quiet competence, the refusal to name what I'd lost even to myself. Grief with no name can't be confiscated. That was the whole architecture of my survival, and it worked for twelve years, and tonight a fat dying magistrate showed me a shelf in a cellar where my sister might be sitting in a jar, catalogued, patient, *kept*—

No. I don't know that. A name in a ledger. A light on a shelf. Could be coincidence. Could be another Bryn. Could be a dozen things that don't require me to tear open the floor I've spent twelve years laying.

I'm very good at not-knowing things. It's my finest craft, better than the theft, better than the charms. Ask me sometime what happened twelve years ago and watch me give you a masterclass.

* * *

Dawn came up over Ashvale the way it always does, gray and apologetic, and the market folded itself away. Osk paused at my stall with his crate of previously cherished silverware.

"You heard?" he said. "Old Vane died on Dagny's bench. Wardens came at first bell, asked questions." He snorted. "Questions. Like they care who dies in the Row."

"Who do they think did it?"

"Nobody. Heart gave out." He shifted the crate. "Though they're asking something odd. About the chair-bearers. Whether anyone spoke to him before he—" He shrugged. "Wardens asking about conversations instead of knives. Strange hours we live in."

Strange hours indeed. Because I *had* spoken to Vane, in the way that leaves no witnesses, and if the Wardens were hunting for a thief in a dead man's head, then whatever was in that ledger was important enough to guard after death. Important enough that a Third Chair of Revenue carried it drunk into a black market, of all the places in the city—unless he'd come looking for someone to take it. Unless he'd known he was dying and wanted the truth somewhere the magistrates' own clerks couldn't burn it.

Or unless I was telling myself stories, which, again, is my finest craft.

Osk lingered, working his jaw. "You alright, Emrys? You've got a look."

"I've always got a look. It's how I sell things."

"Not this one." He set the crate down, which Osk never does; Osk believes in keeping his hands busy so his mouth stays affordable. "That look's older than the stall."

"There's no look, Osk. Go home. Sleep. Cherish something previously."

He went, but slowly, the way friends go when they've decided not to push yet.

I stayed. I sat behind my empty stall in the gray morning with the market struck around me like a stage, and I took out the one thing I'd carried out of Vane's mind that I hadn't meant to take—hadn't consciously taken at all; sometimes the thief's hands grab what the thief's heart wants, and I hate when they coordinate.

A scrap of image. Just one. From the edge of that ledger-room memory, almost an afterthought:

The same underground chamber, the same red-lit shelves—but seen from lower down. Child-height. Small hands pressed against warm glass. And a voice, muffled, young, speaking words in a language I have not heard in twelve years because it exists nowhere in Ashvale except inside my own skull, where I keep it locked next to the name I refuse to say.

The First Tongue. Our secret, Bryn's and mine, invented across a whole summer of bedrolls and candle-stubs, ours and no one else's in all the world.

Except the voice in Vane's memory wasn't mine and wasn't hers. It was smaller. Younger. A child, standing in the court of the dead, speaking our language like it had been born in its mouth.

I closed my hand around nothing and sat in the ruins of my careful life, listening to Ashvale wake up around me—the carts, the gulls, the first bell—and I understood that whatever I decided to do next, the not-knowing was over.

Something had my sister's voice in its mouth. And it knew the way home.
