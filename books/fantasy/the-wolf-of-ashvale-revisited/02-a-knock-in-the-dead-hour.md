## 2. A Knock in the Dead Hour

There are three kinds of knocks in Ashvale, and I've learned to tell them apart the way sailors read weather.

The first is the customer's knock: two raps, apologetic, timed for the hour when honest shops shutter and mine unshutters. The second is the Warden's knock: three heavy blows with a baton's pommel, delivered by someone who wants you to know your door is a courtesy, not an obstacle. And the third—

Well. Before last night I would have told you there were only two.

* * *

I'd come home late even by my standards. The market had run long because a spice merchant from the river districts wanted six charms and kept haggling as though luck were turnips. I'd sold him four, lied about the other two being spoken for, and walked home through streets slick with drizzle, past the ward-lanterns that burn cold blue on every corner since the Ban—little cages of glass that hum if you carry anything the law doesn't like. I've learned to walk the way the lanterns want: flat-handed, empty-pouched, innocent as Sunday.

My rooms sit above a chandler's shop on Tallow Row, which is exactly as glamorous as it sounds. Two rooms, one window that faces a wall, a door with a lock I built myself out of spite and clockwork. I lit no lamp. Habit. I sat in the dark eating bread gone hard enough to qualify as architecture, and I was thinking—not about anything important, just about the magistrate from the night before, about the seal I'd lifted off his cooling hand, the name inked there that I still hadn't let myself look at properly. It sat in my coat pocket like a coal I was afraid to blow on.

That's when someone knocked on my door at the dead hour, and it wasn't the first kind or the second kind.

It was small. Soft. Knuckles, not wood on wood—a child's knock, hesitant, the knock of someone who has been told knocking is how you ask permission to exist.

I didn't move. Rule one of surviving Ashvale: nothing good arrives after the second bell. I finished my bread, chewed slowly, listened. The building settled. Rain ticked the window. The knock came again, patient, terrible in its patience, and then a voice said something in a language that does not exist anymore, and every hair on my arms stood up like soldiers called to a war they'd been told was over.

It wasn't the words themselves. It was the *shape* of them—the vowels rolling like river stones, the consonants clicking soft as beetle shells, the rise and fall of a sentence that sounded less like speech and more like weather over old mountains. A language older than any kingdom in this valley. A language with maybe four living speakers left on the wrong side of the ground.

Two of them were dead. One of them was me.

And I had buried that language so deep under twelve years of careful forgetting that hearing it was like hearing my own heartbeat played back to me from inside a locked room.

"Go away," I said in trade-tongue, because answering in kind would be signing a confession.

The voice answered again—same phrase, repeated, and this time close enough to the door that I could hear the wet rasp of small lungs working hard, like the speaker had run a long way. There was a word in it I knew despite myself. Knew the way you know a scar. *Vessa.* Come in. Or possibly *come home.* In the First Tongue those were nearly the same word, which tells you everything about the people who spoke it.

I should have stayed silent. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them. Every smart bone in my body said *stay quiet, stay dark, let the dead language die on the doorstep.*

Instead I found myself crossing the room, hand on the lock I built out of spite, and whispering, "Who taught you that?"

Silence. Then, in accented but serviceable trade-tongue: "Nobody. It's just what I say." A pause. "Please. You're the only one who answered."

Gods give me strength; they always deliver their ironies gift-wrapped. The only soul in Ashvale who could answer—and did, against all sense—because some part of me had never stopped listening for it.

I opened the door.

* * *

The child standing in my doorway was perhaps seven years old, soaked to the skin, barefoot, wearing a nightshirt roughly two sizes too big with someone else's coat belted around it like a sack. Dark hair plastered flat. A face that might have been any face—Ashvale breeds children who are expertly forgettable, that's half of how they survive—but the eyes were wrong for a child. Not cruel, not old, exactly. *Patient.* Eyes like something waiting in amber.

And hanging at her throat, on a cord of braided hair, a locket.

I want to be clear about what happened when I saw that locket, because it happened faster than thought. My body knew it before I did. My knees went loose. My mouth flooded sour. Some animal ledger deep in my chest slammed open and every entry I'd spent twelve years marking *paid in full* flipped back open to *outstanding.*

It was a locket of black iron, oval, dented along one edge where—where teeth had—

No. Where *something* had dented it. Twelve years ago. In a fire. Don't.

"You're bleeding," the child observed, and I realized I'd bitten my lip hard enough to taste copper.

"What do you want?" My voice came out sanded flat. Good. Flat was professional. Flat was a woman who sold luck charms and had no history.

"To give this back." She lifted the locket in both hands, the way you present something to an altar rather than a person. "It won't stop singing. They said a charm-seller could make it stop."

"They," I said. "Who said?"

"The ones in the walls." She said it the way children say things that are true and insane. "They talk all night under the mountain. They're very tired. They asked me to bring it to someone who remembers."

The rain gusted in past her, and the streetlamp at the corner caught the locket as she raised it—and through the seam of the iron, light leaked out. Not lamplight, not wardlight-blue. Warm gold, the color of the light in my mother's kitchen, the color of a hearth that went cold twelve years ago, and it moved. Not flickered. *Moved,* the way light moves when it's breathing.

Soul-light. Actual, unlicensed, unforgivably real soul-light, hanging around a child's neck in a city where the penalty for a candle flame is the censure block and the penalty for this is a hole in the mountain with your name on it.

"Come inside," I heard myself say, "before you get us both hanged," which was, in fairness, the most affectionate thing I've said aloud in years.

* * *

She sat on my one chair, dripping, wrapped in my spare blanket, eating the rest of my architectural bread like she hadn't seen food in days—which the hollows under her cheekbones suggested was literal fact. She said her name was Vess. She didn't know whose coat she was wearing. She had walked, she thought, four days from somewhere north, following the locket, which "pulled" toward certain houses and went still near others. Mine, apparently, pulled hardest.

"And the ones in the walls," I said, keeping my voice bored the way you keep a hand steady over a fuse. "Under the mountain. They talk to you."

"Not talk, exactly." She frowned, working at it. "It's more like—they remember at you."

Lovely. A seven-year-old receiving psychic correspondence from the magistrates' private ossuary. That's fine. That's a normal Tuesday.

"Let me see the locket."

She hesitated—first real hesitation I'd seen in her, and it made me like her, which was inconvenient—and then she reached behind her neck and worked the knot free and set it in my palm.

Iron, cold as a debt. Dented along one edge. The clasp was broken in a way I recognized the way you recognize handwriting, and I will not tell you why, because telling you why means telling me why, and I wasn't ready to open that drawer yet either. Inside, through a hairline seam, the gold light pulsed slow and steady as a sleeping pulse.

A sliver. A fragment. Somebody's soul, cut into pieces like stolen silverware, and a piece of it sealed in iron and hung around a child who'd walked four days through the rain because voices in the walls told her to.

"Whose is it?" Vess asked. "Do you know? It feels like it knows *me.*"

"I'm going to find out," I said, and discovered I meant it with a violence that surprised us both. The child flinched slightly. I softened my voice into its market-shape, the trustworthy shape. "You can sleep here tonight. Floor's lumpy but it's loyal."

She curled up in the blanket without another word, the way children do when they've been tired longer than they've been safe, and was asleep between one breath and the next. Efficient. I approved.

I did not sleep.

* * *

Here's what I did instead, in the small hours while she breathed at the foot of my bed like a bellows with a secret: I sat at my worktable with the locket in front of me and did not touch it, which is a skill. Memory-work is mostly restraint. Anyone can crack a seal open; the craft is in reading the imprint through the shell, tasting the memory without swallowing it, the way you check a wine's vintage by smell before committing.

Except this wasn't a memory. Memories are grey smoke, secondhand weather. This was the source. This was light with a *name* attached, probably, and names are the most dangerous cargo on any road.

I put my hands flat on the table on either side of it, the way you'd frame a fire you weren't sure was contained, and I leaned close—not touching, just near enough that the imprint brushed the surface of my mind like a hand passing over still water.

And the First Tongue rose up in me like floodwater finding an old channel.

Not words I decided to think. Words that *arrived*, worn smooth, childhood-smooth, words I knew the pronunciation of in someone else's accent—a girl's accent, quick and laughing, correcting my grammar the way she always did because she was eleven months older and treated it as a rank. *Say it softer, Em. The word for sister is a soft word. You'll bruise it.*

I sat back so fast my chair barked against the floor.

The child slept on. The locket glowed its slow gold pulse. Outside, the ward-lanterns hummed their cold blue hymn to a city that had outlawed everything I was currently sitting three feet from.

Twelve years ago I had made a bargain—you'll forgive me if I don't detail it; some invoices you keep folded—and part of the price was agreeing to forget certain things *on purpose*, to fold them up small and put them where even I wouldn't look. It had worked. Gods, it had worked beautifully; I'd built a whole woman on top of that sealed cellar and called her a life. And now a drowned language was seeping up through the floorboards of me, and a dented iron locket sat on my table glowing like the exact color of a kitchen I refuse to describe, and a child was asleep in my blanket wearing somebody's coat, sent by voices in the walls to *someone who remembers.*

I remembered. That was the problem. The bargain had taken the pictures and left the ache, the way a fever breaks and leaves the weakness. I didn't know what I was grieving. I only knew I'd been grieving it for twelve years, professionally, under another name.

I picked up the locket. Held it to the lampless dark. Through the seam, the soul-light turned toward me—I swear this, it *turned*—and pulsed once, twice, and on the third beat the rhythm changed, and I recognized the rhythm, because it wasn't a pulse at all.

It was a knock. Small, soft, patient.

Knuckles, not wood on wood. The way you knock on the inside of a door when you've been shut behind it a long time and you can hear someone in the room.

"Oh," I said, to a locket, in the dead hour, in a dead tongue, in a voice I hadn't used since I was nine years old. "*Vessa.*"

The light flared like a struck match.

On the floor, Vess sat bolt upright out of a dead sleep, eyes wide and amber-bright and not entirely her own, and spoke in a voice layered two voices deep—one a child's, one a woman's, both saying the same word in the First Tongue, a soft word, a word you're supposed to handle gently lest you bruise it.

Then the ward-lantern outside my window began to scream.
