## 3. What the Wardens Burn

The night market had rules the way a knife had edges: you learned them by bleeding.

Rule one: never sell to anyone who asks what's in the charm. Rule two: never sell to anyone who doesn't. Rule three, which was really rule zero wearing a nicer coat: when the Wardens came—and they always came—you were a charm-seller of perfectly ordinary luck-trinkets, dried rowan berries and knucklebones and river stones with holes worn through them by patient water, and if any of them happened to hum at two in the morning that was just the wind through the awning poles, and no, Warden, I've never heard it sing words.

I'd kept those rules for nine years. Nine years of small hours and smaller profits, of smiling at drunk magistrates' clerks and palming their memories like loose coins, of being so unremarkable that the Wardens' lists—actual lists, I once paid good silver for a peek—described me as *Nystrom, E., vendor, charms (genuine), low priority.*

Low priority. In Ashvale, that was the highest honor available to a criminal. I had earned it honestly: by years of silence, by never helping anyone whose trouble might splash, by keeping my head down with such devotion that I suspected my neck had fused to it.

Then Vess arrived, and the child spoke the First Tongue on my doorstep, and my low-priority life ended somewhere between the second word and the third.

* * *

Three nights after the knock, I opened my stall at the usual hour—the dead ones, between midnight and dawn, when the honest city sleeps and the dishonest one conducts its commerce—and found Vess already sitting on my crate like she owned the lease.

"You can't be here," I said.

Vess looked up at me with those eyes that were too old for the face around them, and answered in the First Tongue. Three words. *The walls listen.*

"I know the walls listen. That's why you can't be *here.*"

More First Tongue, softer now, and this time I caught the shape of it even before my mind translated: *Nowhere else to go.* And beneath it, threaded through the syllables like wire through wax, that faint wrongness I'd felt the first night—a resonance, a second voice under the first, the way a bell rings its note and also the memory of every note it's ever rung.

The soul-light in the locket at her throat pulsed gently, like something breathing in its sleep.

I looked down the row of stalls. Old Maren selling luck-threads two spaces over. The twins' cousin running dice near the fountain. And directly across the lane, Osk.

Osk was a fence the way a cathedral is a building—technically accurate, spiritually insufficient. He'd been at the market longer than me, a wide, slow-moving man with a beard he braided into three ropes and hands like slabs of ham that could nonetheless lift a purse off a cardinal without disturbing his prayers. He'd taught me which clerks carried real coin and which carried IOUs from their mistresses. When I'd been new and stupid and had nearly sold a stolen memory back to the man it belonged to, Osk had bought it off my table himself—at full price, though we both knew he couldn't move it—to keep me out of the fire.

He caught me looking and raised two fingers off his tankard in lazy salute. His stall smelled of tallow and old leather, because everything Osk sold was technically secondhand, including most of the excuses he gave the Wardens.

"Fine," I told Vess. "You stay behind the table. You don't speak. You don't hum. You don't do whatever it is you do that makes my charms sit up straighter when you walk past."

She nodded solemnly, the way children nod when they fully intend to ignore you at the first opportunity.

It lasted four hours. It would have lasted longer if Ashvale had any decency at all.

* * *

They came at the hour when the market is thickest and the lanterns are lowest—the hour the Wardens call the Sweep, and everyone else calls nothing at all, because naming things invites them closer.

I heard them before I saw them: boots in formation, too many, too synchronized for drunks. Then the sound I knew from nine years ago and still heard in dreams—the Warden-whistle, a long descending note that wasn't quite a note, pitched to make your teeth ache and your thoughts scatter. Under the magic ban, the only legal magic was the magic of enforcement. The hypocrisy was the point. They wanted us to feel it in our bones: *this power exists, but it isn't for you.*

"Market's closed," barked the sergeant, a woman with a face like a closed ledger. "Wardens' business. Everyone stand by your stalls. Touch nothing."

The lane contracted. Lanterns guttered as bodies pressed together. Somewhere a dice game died mid-throw, the dice abandoned on the cobbles like tiny fleeing animals.

I stood at my table and did what I'd trained myself to do for nine years: I became furniture. Shoulders rounded, eyes down, hands busy sorting rowan berries that didn't need sorting. Behind my knees, hidden by the table skirt and a stack of empty crates, Vess sat very still among my wares, folded up small as a cat.

The sergeant moved down the line with a list and two subordinates. I watched her work the way you watch a surgeon: professionally, impersonally, grateful the knife isn't yours yet.

At Maren's stall: "Luck-threads. Licensed. Move along." At the dice table: confiscation, one cuff to an ear, move along. At Osk's—

Osk was leaning against his counter with the studied boredom of a man who has survived forty of these. "Evening, Sergeant. Buying anything? Got a lovely coat, hardly haunted."

"Open your case."

"Now that's hurtful. Ten years I've stood this spot—"

"*Open your case.*"

Osk sighed the sigh of a man inconvenienced by history itself, and unlatched the case where he kept his inventory: pocket watches, wedding rings, a bishop's snuffbox, three letters sealed with the magistracy's own crest that I chose not to wonder about. The subordinates went through it with the thoroughness of people paid by the hour.

And then one of them—the younger one, the one with the careful hands—went still.

"Sergeant." He held up a scrap of cloth. A swaddling rag, I thought first, then saw the shape of it: a child's hood, wool, sized for someone Vess's age. Where had—Osk's stall backed onto the alley. Vess had come to me through the alleys. She must have passed his door. Or slept there, one night, before she found mine.

"This yours?" the young Warden asked.

"That," said Osk, "is a rag."

"It's a child's hood."

"Ashvale's full of children. Tragic shortage of rags, though, so if you're not going to arrest it—"

"Anomalous speech reported in this market," the sergeant said, flat as slate. "Three nights running. A child, speaking unlicensed language." Her eyes came up from the hood and swept the lane, slow, methodical, a lighthouse beam looking for a hull to crack. "Anyone seen a child?"

Nobody breathed. In the night market, informing on your neighbors was worse than theft; theft was survival, informing was suicide. But Wardens had ways of making silence expensive.

The sergeant's gaze traveled down the row. Past Maren. Past the dice table. Toward me.

I want to be honest about what happened next, since honesty is supposedly the luxury of memoirs nobody reads. My heart did not pound. My palms did not sweat. Nine years of practice had built me into a machine for exactly this moment, and the machine performed flawlessly: I bent to my crates, lifted one, and set it down with the small thud of a merchant doing inventory, and the sergeant's gaze slid past me like water off wax.

That was the invisibility I'd bought. Silence, downcast eyes, genuine dullness—I had paid for it in installments across nearly a decade, and it worked. It worked beautifully.

It worked because the sergeant found what she was looking for two stalls earlier.

"Osk Vellin," she read from the list, and something in my chest turned over. "Fence. Unlicensed goods. Now a child's garment in your inventory during an anomalous-speech inquiry." She tucked the hood away. "You'll come with us."

"Over a *rag?*" Osk spread his enormous hands, all wounded reasonableness. "Sergeant. Be serious. I've never touched a child in my life except to pickpocket their sweets, gods rest their little sticky fingers."

"The child will come forward," the sergeant announced to the lane, louder now, "and this man goes free. Otherwise he comes with us, and the inquiry continues." She let it hang there, the way they did. Making the silence expensive.

Behind my knees, I felt Vess shift. Felt the locket's light pulse warm against my ankle where the crate-shadow pooled. The child was going to stand up. I could feel the intention gathering like weather, and I did the arithmetic I'd spent nine years learning to do faster than conscience: one child, unknown value, versus one fence, known quantity, and if Vess stood up the sergeant wouldn't take one prisoner, she'd take three, because the charm-seller sheltering an anomalous child was not walking away from this either.

The machine ran the numbers clean and fast and cold.

The machine did not tell Vess to stay still. The machine didn't have to. I simply reached back, under the table skirt, and laid my hand on the child's shoulder. One touch. Steady pressure. *Not yet. Not you.*

Vess went still. Thank every god I don't believe in, the child went still.

"Last chance," the sergeant said. "No child? Fine. Vellin, hands out."

"Ah, hell's own—" Osk rolled his eyes at the heavens, offered his wrists like a man donating blood, and delivered his parting shot to the entire lane: "Whoever's kid this is, I hope you choke on your supper. And I want my rag back."

They marched him past my stall. He didn't look at me—he was too good for that—but as he drew level he said, conversational, to no one in particular: "Mind the stock, neighbor. Case sticks on the left latch." And then he was gone into the dark between lanterns, three ropes of beard swaying, hands manacled behind him, complaining about the quality of Warden-issue irons in a voice that carried all the way to the fountain.

The lane exhaled. Business resumed with the terrible elasticity of markets everywhere. Within a quarter hour Maren was selling threads again and someone had scooped up the abandoned dice.

I stood at my table and sorted rowan berries that needed no sorting, and behind my knees Vess watched me with those too-old eyes, and neither of us said anything, in any language, for a very long time.

* * *

Dawn found me doing something I hadn't done in nine years: standing outside a Warden precinct house.

Not going in. Gods, no. Just standing across the street, wrapped in my least memorable shawl, watching the door where they'd taken Osk, performing the ancient ritual of the powerless: bearing witness at a distance, accomplishing nothing, telling yourself the witnessing matters.

Here is the thing about the invisibility I'd bought. I'd always framed the price honestly enough—silence costs, I knew that, I'd chosen it with open eyes. What I'd never let myself count was interest. Every year of silence made the next year's silence cheaper. Every time the machine ran its cold arithmetic and I walked away, the machine got smoother, faster, more certain of itself. Low priority. That's what I'd made myself. And low priority meant that when the knives came out, they fell elsewhere—on loud men like Osk, on visible women like Maren, on children who spoke forbidden languages on doorsteps—while I stood across the street in my least memorable shawl and called it survival.

Vess appeared beside me. I hadn't heard the child approach; I never did. Small feet, big silences.

"He took my place," Vess said—in trade-tongue this time, careful and accented, saving the First Tongue for walls and wounds.

"Yes."

"You made me stay still."

"Yes."

Vess considered this with the terrifying gravity of children deciding how much of you they intend to forgive. "Was it right?"

"No," I said. Because it wasn't, and because lying to a soul-vessel seemed like poor theology. "But it happened anyway, and now we have a debt, and in this city debts are the only thing that grows faster than fear."

"What are you going to do?"

What was I going to do. Nine years of rules sat on my shoulders like wet sand. Never ask questions. Never owe favors. Never go to the people from the old life, because the people from the old life were why I'd needed rules in the first place. Sefa with her lockwitch fingers and her ledger of grudges. Pell and Rook, wherever the twins had buried themselves. All of them half-lives under false names, all of them ghosts I'd sworn to leave haunting each other.

And above all, never—*never*—go to Corvane.

"First," I said, "we're getting that rag back."

Vess blinked. "The hood?"

"Osk's case sticks on the left latch. Confiscated inventory gets logged, tagged, and stored, and storage rooms have locks, and locks—" I felt something in my chest loosen for the first time in three days, something that was almost the old self, the self before the rules, the thief. "Locks are just doors with opinions."

"And after?"

After. After I'd have to decide whether the debt to Osk got repaid with silver or with the one thing I'd promised myself I'd never spend: the silence itself. Going to the crew meant lighting a beacon with my name on it. Going to Corvane meant handing the wolf my address and hoping love counted as a leash.

"After," I said, turning away from the precinct house, from the door that had eaten my friend over a rag, "after, I think the walls listening is about to be the least of anyone's problems. Come on, Vess. We need a lockwitch, a map, and a miracle, in roughly that order."

Behind us, the sun cleared the rooftops and lit the Warden seal over the precinct door—the mountain and crown, the magistrates' mark, official as a gravestone. I'd walked past it ten thousand times without seeing it.

I saw it now. And beneath the crown, small as a maker's stamp, I noticed—for the first time in nine years of careful not-noticing—that the seal bore a date. Twelve years ago. The year the ban was written.

The year Bryn burned.

I stood very still while the arithmetic I'd refused to do came due all at once, and Vess's hand found mine, and the locket at the child's throat glowed like a coal remembering it had once been fire.

"Come on," I said again, and my voice didn't shake, which frightened me more than shaking would have. "We start with the lockwitch."
