## 4. The Locket's Ledger

There is a rule among memory-thieves: never read your own grief twice.

I'd broken it once already, the night I took the locket from Vess's neck while she slept in my spare room with her boots on and one hand curled around a crust of bread she hadn't finished. She'd flinched even asleep when the chain slid over her head, like the thing was warm, like it knew her. It probably did. That was rather the problem.

The locket sat on my worktable for two days while I did everything a sensible criminal does to avoid it. I restocked the stall. I re-soldered three charms whose seams were leaking someone else's wedding day into the shop air — Mrs. Hallen down the lane kept stopping mid-sentence to weep at her onions, which she attributed to grief over the price of fish. I drank tea that had gone bitter because I forgot it existed. I sharpened knives that didn't need sharpening.

A locket with a sliver of soul-light in it doesn't age. Doesn't tarnish. It just waits on the table, glowing faintly gold-green like a firefly that's decided to be patient about dying, and it lets you know — without saying anything at all — that eventually you're going to pick it up.

On the third night I picked it up.

* * *

Reading a memory-imprint is not like reading a book. Books are polite; they tell you what they want, in order, with margins. A memory-imprint is more like pressing your ear to a door and hearing everything behind it at once — every conversation, every silence, every footstep of everyone who ever touched the object — all of it layered, none of it sorted, and your job as the thief is to stand there in the din until the loudest voices introduce themselves.

I've done this hundreds of times. Stolen memories are my trade; I know how to slip into an imprint light-footed, take what pays, and leave before the dead start asking questions.

This time I went in heavy. Boots on, both hands, no subtlety. Because this wasn't merchandise. This was a ledger, and I intended to audit it.

The first layer was Vess. Recent, thin, laid down like breath on glass: fear, mostly. Running. A woman's hands — mine — holding out bread. The word *sister* spoken in the First Tongue, and underneath it a child's bewilderment, the particular loneliness of knowing something enormous and having no one who speaks it.

I set that aside gently. Children shouldn't be handled roughly, even their memories. Especially their memories.

Beneath Vess, the layers got older and heavier, the way sediment does. And beneath them —

Beneath them was my sister.

* * *

You have to understand what that felt like, and I'm going to fail to explain it, but I'll try anyway.

Twelve years ago Bryn died. Everyone said so. There was a fire in the tenement on Cindermaker's Row, an official inquiry, an official cause — faulty lamp-oil, an official condolence letter with an official seal, delivered by an official man who wouldn't meet my eyes and left before the tea was poured. I was twenty-three and half-mad with smoke and loss, and I did what the magistrates' new law required: I buried my grief along with my magic, sealed it under the ban like everyone else, and told myself that Ashvale had always been a city without wonders, that the First Tongue was a game two girls invented, that my sister had simply stopped.

Grief is obedient if you starve it. For twelve years I starved mine into something small enough to carry to market and sell by the pinch.

Now here was its voice again, coming up through the locket like water finding a crack, and it said:

*Emrys. They're going to sell me.*

Not the fire. Not the death. *They're going to sell me.* Present tense. Said in the First Tongue, our language, the secret grammar of two girls playing at being older than kingdoms — and recorded into this metal not by Bryn herself but by whoever held the other end of the transaction.

Because that's what a memory-imprint really is, if you're honest about it: a receipt. Every theft leaves a mark on the stolen thing, and every sale leaves a deeper one. Charms remember their buyers. Lockets remember their makers.

And this locket remembered the men who made it.

* * *

Three signatures. I'll give them that much — they were proud of their work. When you forge a document in Ashvale you bury the forgery deep, but these men signed clean, in the open, because who would ever read it? Who could? You needed a memory-thief to hear an imprint, and memory-thieves were exactly what the ban had been written to extinguish.

First signature: Magistrate Corvel Hask, Keeper of the Sealed Arts. His imprint was all ledgers and lavender water, a mind like a filing cabinet that dreamed of being a vault. I heard him dictate terms to a subordinate in a voice dry as archived paper:

*"Lot forty-one. Female, age nineteen, soul intact, provenance: unlicensed practitioner, deceased by administrative action. The vessel will be prepared separately. Note for the record that the asset was acquired pursuant to the Preservation Act and no irregularities attended its transfer."*

Deceased by administrative action.

I want you to sit with that phrase the way I had to sit with it, alone, at three in the morning, with my sister's voice still ringing in the metal. *Administrative action.* Twelve years I'd believed in a fire, in faulty lamp-oil, in chance — the great neutral god of bad luck — and the whole time it had been a line item. My sister hadn't died. She'd been *processed.*

The second signature belonged to the inquiry officer himself. The man with the condolence letter. His imprint carried the smell of that visit — cheap tea gone cold, my kitchen, my own younger voice cracking on *how did it start* — and beneath it his private satisfaction, small and greasy, at having been promoted for the tidy handling of the case. He had stood in my kitchen knowing exactly how it started, because he'd lit the lamp himself, or signed off on the man who did, and he'd handed me an official lie with an official seal and left before the tea was poured because guilt makes men punctual.

I put the locket down then. Walked to the window. Counted chimneys until my hands stopped shaking.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about fury: grief burns hot and fast and then it's ash, and ash is almost comfortable, you can live in ash, plenty of people build entire lives in it. Fury is different. Fury is cold. It doesn't flare; it *sets*. It gets into the mortar of you. And standing at that window watching the Warden lanterns sweep the street below — the same Wardens who arrest children for speaking, who burned Osk's cart and dragged my neighbor off for the crime of looking foreign — I felt it set.

The law was the crime. It had always been the crime. Twelve years ago they hadn't outlawed magic to protect Ashvale from itself. They'd outlawed magic so that they would be the only ones who could commit it — so they could harvest souls from the unlicensed, seal them away beneath the mountain, and call the whole enterprise public safety. The ban wasn't a wall. It was a fence around a pasture, and we were the livestock.

And the third signature — ah. The third signature I read four times, because I kept hoping I'd heard it wrong.

* * *

*"Payment rendered,"* said the third imprint, and this voice I knew. Older now, weighted, but I knew it the way you know a floorboard that creaks outside your bedroom door. Magistrate Aldous Ren. First Chair of the Sealed Arts. The architect of the ban itself, the silver-tongued terror of my childhood, the reason the word *witch* got children taken from their mothers in the night markets.

*"Payment rendered to the Court Beneath,"* Ren said, in the tone of a man closing a particularly profitable fiscal year. *"One soul, kin-bound, delivered via prepared vessel per contract. The Court confirms receipt. In exchange: continuation of supply. The dragon-fire holds so long as the tithe holds."*

Dragon-fire. Even I, raised after the ban, even I had heard the old stories — that the mountain under Ashvale wasn't stone all the way down, that there was a heart of living flame older than the city, and that the founders hadn't built *on* the mountain, they'd built *around* it, the way you build a lock around something precious or something dangerous. The stories said the fire was going out. The stories said somebody had figured out how to feed it.

Souls feed it. Shelved souls, sold souls, souls bought from grieving families with official condolence letters. The magistrates hadn't hoarded magic despite outlawing it — outlawing it *was* the hoarding. Every raid, every arrest, every "anomalous speech" charge against a child was a harvest. And my sister — bright, reckless, First-Tongue-speaking Bryn, who could talk to locks and argue with weather — my sister was twelve years of feeding.

And Vess.

Vess, the vessel. *Prepared,* they'd said. I thought of her asleep in my spare room, boots on, guarding a crust of bread, carrying a fragment of Bryn's soul the way a courier carries a package — no idea what was inside, only that it was hers, only that it sang to her in the oldest language there is. They'd made a child into a delivery sack. They'd grown her somewhere — bought her, bred her, gods knew — hollowed her out just enough to hold the payment, and set her walking toward the mountain.

Except she'd walked toward my door instead. Out of every door in Ashvale, mine. Because the fragment knew what the child couldn't: where home was.

I laughed. It came out wrong, a sound with no humor in it whatsoever, the kind of laugh that frightens cats. Somewhere below, a Warden patrol paused, listened, moved on. Even my laughter was contraband now.

* * *

Dawn found me still at the table, and I had done what thieves do with ledgers: I'd copied it. Not onto paper — paper can be seized, and Wardens seize beautifully. Into myself. Every phrase of Ren's contract, every date in Hask's filing-cabinet voice, every greasy satisfaction of the inquiry officer, pressed flat and stored deep in the vaults where I keep the memories too dangerous to sell and too important to lose. Let them come take it. It's astonishing how hard it is to confiscate a thing once it's become part of a person.

Then I sat with the last question, the one I'd been circling all night like a moth around a lamp it suspected was lying.

Why give the vessel to *me?*

Not why did Vess come — that I understood, the fragment pulling the child homeward like iron filings to a lodestone. But the magistrates weren't sentimental. A prepared vessel lost in the streets of Ashvale should have triggered a search party, a recovery operation, a quiet administrative action of its own. Instead the patrols were hunting a rumor of strange speech, lazily, the way you hunt something you expect to find on its own.

Unless they weren't lazy. Unless the hunt was theater. Unless somewhere in a marble office, Aldous Ren had opened his ledger, looked at lot forty-one, and noted with dry satisfaction that the vessel had gone to ground with the one person in Ashvale who could hear the imprint in the locket — the kin-bound thief, the loose end from twelve years ago — and decided that the best way to catch a memory-thief was to let her steal her way into the open.

They hadn't lost Vess. They'd *baited a hook with her.* With Bryn. With me.

Cold fury has this advantage over hot: it thinks clearly. So I thought clearly, and here is what I concluded. If it was a trap, it was a trap I was walking into regardless, because there was a child asleep in my back room who spoke the First Tongue and deserved better than being anyone's parcel, and there was a soul shelved beneath the mountain that I had — unknowingly, unforgivably, with a signature I didn't yet understand — helped condemn. Trap or no trap, the road ran the same direction. Down.

What I needed now was things I couldn't buy at my own stall. A map of the undercourt. Locks that answer to witches. Hands that aren't mine. Faces I could trust, or at least faces whose betrayals I could predict.

In short: family. The other kind. The kind you choose in back rooms and betray in back rooms and somehow, impossibly, keep.

I looked in on Vess before I went out. She was awake, sitting cross-legged on the cot, holding the wooden bird I'd won for her at the fair — don't ask, it seemed important to her, and children's importances are the only honest currency left in this city. She watched me in the grey light with those old-young eyes and said, in the First Tongue, the words for *you dreamed loudly.*

"Did I," I said in Ashvalan, noncommittal, a woman with nothing under her floorboards.

She tilted her head. Then, slowly, in the language older than any kingdom — the language I had sworn was a game, the language of a dead girl and a stolen childhood — she said something new. Something I hadn't taught her, that no one had taught her, that came up out of the soul-fragment like heat out of a closed oven.

It meant: *the mountain knows your name now, little thief. It's counting the days.*

I pulled on my coat, checked my knives, and went to find Sefa.
