## 5. Twelve Years of Small Treasons

The trouble with forgetting on purpose is that you have to remember how.

Nobody tells you that. The charm-sellers will take your coin and your grief and hand you back a smooth little stone of nothing, and they'll never mention that the hole it leaves has edges. That you'll run your thumb over those edges for years, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. That some nights you'll wake with the shape of a name in your mouth and no name to fill it, and you'll lie there in the dark doing arithmetic with your own skull—*I had someone, I know I had someone, because I am this sad for a reason.*

So let me tell you how I did it, since I'm apparently in the business of confession now. Let me walk back through the door I paid good money to seal shut.

Her name was Bryn.

There. That's the first treason: saying it out loud, even here, even to myself, where no clerk can hear and write it down.

* * *

We grew up in the Tannery Rows, which is the kind of neighborhood where the river runs brown and the children run faster than the magistrates' clerks can count them. Unregistered births were common enough that we were less citizens than weather—a fact of Ashvale, noted by no one, blamed when convenient.

Bryn was two years older and four inches shorter and infinitely my superior in all matters, a hierarchy she enforced with the gentle tyranny of the beloved. She had a gap in her teeth she refused to have fixed because, she said, whistles came free with it. She could whistle three notes that made dogs sit down. Later I learned those three notes were words.

She taught me the First Tongue the way other children taught each other to skip stones: as a game, as contraband, as ours.

"It's not dead," she told me once, crouched at the edge of the millpond where the reeds grew tall enough to hide two girls from the whole administrative weight of Ashvale. "That's what they want you to think. Dead things don't mind being outlawed."

"Then what is it?"

"Asleep." She picked a reed and stripped it with her thumbnail, the way she did when she was about to say something she'd been carrying a while. "Words go to sleep when nobody says them. You wake them up by using them. Watch."

And she said a word—I won't write it here; some things shouldn't be handled casually—and the pond water stood up. Just for a moment. A column of green water holding its shape like a startled animal, reflecting the sky from underneath, before it remembered gravity and fell back.

I screamed. She laughed so hard she fell in.

That was the summer we built our secret language into a real one—not just the old words she'd pried out of a peddler woman who lived under the tannery bridge and charged us stories instead of coin, but *our* words layered inside them. Bryn invented half of it. Words for the specific silence between sisters mid-argument. A word for the smell of rain on hot slate. A word she never translated for me, which she used only when she looked at me a certain way, usually when I'd done something stupid and brave, which was often, because she was the smart one and I was the one who jumped first.

I asked her once what it meant.

"When you're older," she said.

I was twelve years too slow asking again.

* * *

Here is what I need you to understand about the years before the fire: they were happy. Not the way stories say happy, all trumpets and endings. Happy the way bread is—ordinary, daily, taken for granted until the day there isn't any.

We stole. Of course we stole. You didn't survive the Rows otherwise, and anyway Bryn was brilliant at it in a way that should have frightened me more than it did. Not purses—she said purse-thieving was begging with extra steps—but *memories*. There was a charm-seller off Cooper's Lane, a soft-bellied man named Aldous Vane who kept his stock in glass vials, gray-glowing, humming faintly like trapped bees. He sold forgetfulness to magistrates who needed inconvenient witnesses dimmed, and he bought memories from desperate people by the pound.

Bryn figured out his locks were ceremonial. She figured out that memory, handled right, could be copied without being taken—the original stays, the thief keeps an echo. Nobody knew that then. It's the foundation of everything I've done since, every charm I've ever sold in every night market, and it was hers first.

"We're not stealing," she insisted, hunched over her hoard in the cellar, little vials glowing gray-green among the pickled eggs. "We're *librarians*. Bad ones."

"You're a criminal," I said, delighted.

"I'm an *archivist*," she said, with the particular dignity of a fifteen-year-old, "of things people were careless with."

Our mother worked herself to a nub at the dye-works and noticed nothing, which was the arrangement. Our neighbors noticed everything and said nothing, which was the other arrangement. And above us all sat the magistrates of Ashvale, twelve of them in their gray tower, passing the ordinances that made magic a hanging crime—which meant, in practice, that the only people punished for magic were the poor who stumbled into it, while the men who wrote the law bought it by the vial from men like Aldous Vane.

Bryn saw it early. She had a talent for seeing the machinery behind the curtain, my sister. She used to stand outside the magistrate's court during sessions, listening through the shutters, learning their cadences, and come home furious in that quiet way of hers, folding the fury up small and putting it away for later.

"They're not banning magic," she told me the winter she turned seventeen. "You can't ban a river. They're building a *dam*. Every law they pass moves it a little more uphill, into their pockets. And you know what they need to hold it all?"

I shook my head.

"A container," she said. "Something that can hold a soul without spilling."

I laughed. I actually laughed, because she said it like one of her stories, and because I was young, and because the alternative to laughing was believing her.

* * *

The fire came in autumn, the year I was sixteen and she was eighteen.

I have gone over that night so many times that it's worn smooth in places, like a stair. Here are the pieces I trust:

It started in the dye-works. Officially, it always starts in a place like that—tallow, spirits, carelessness, the poor burning what little they own. Officially, our mother died in it, and our mother's daughters barely escaped, and wasn't it tragic, and didn't the magistrates send a delegation with bread.

Unofficially—and I know this now the way you know a scar is there even when you're not looking at it—unofficially, the fire burned wrong. It burned in a *pattern*. It burned around certain buildings and through others, and the buildings it went through were the homes of everyone in the Rows who could speak a word of the First Tongue. The peddler woman under the bridge died in it. The ferryman who sang old songs to his oars died in it. Eleven families, give or take, and every single one touched by the sleeping language.

They weren't putting out a fire. They were running a search-and-confiscation, and flame was cheaper than clerks.

Bryn got me out. That part I'm sure of, because I remember her hands—burned across both palms, though I never asked how, and she never said, and that failure to ask is one of the great shames of my life. She pushed me through the drainage culvert behind the tannery and the last thing I heard her say wasn't in trade-tongue at all. It was that word. The one she never translated. Said twice, urgent, like a key turned hard in a lock.

Then the culvert, and the cold river, and the morning, and no sister.

* * *

What I did next took eleven days, and I will set it down plainly because it deserves plainness.

For eleven days I searched the ash. I pulled boards. I asked questions of people too frightened to answer. I found, on the fourth day, in the ruins of the magistrate's annex—a building that had conveniently lost its records in the blaze—a scrap of charred paper that hadn't burned all the way. A ledger line. I couldn't read most of it, but I knew the seal pressed beside it: the twelve-fold knot of the Ashvale bench. And I knew my sister's name written in a clerk's careful hand, followed by a figure, followed by the word *delivered*.

Delivered. Like a parcel. Like flour.

On the seventh day I found Aldous Vane's shop abandoned, its cellar empty except for one broken vial and a residue of gray dust arranged—arranged, I swear it—in a circle. A summoning circle, or a shipping manifest. I was sixteen and unlettered in the deep crafts; I understood only that my sister's soul had been *handled*, professionally, and that the men who outlawed magic had signed for it.

On the eleventh day, a man found me. Gray-robed, gray-faced, one of those functionaries whose cruelty is entirely procedural. He didn't threaten me. That was the horrifying thing. He offered me a chair, and tea, and a bargain, laid out like terms of a loan.

"Grief is heavy," he said. "Carried raw, it kills the carrier. We're prepared to help you with the weight."

"What do you want?"

"Your signature. On a document confirming the disposition of certain assets." He watched me with the mild patience of a man who had done this many times and been refused rarely. "In exchange, we lighten you. Selectively. Enough that you can live. Enough that you sleep. You'll keep the fact of her—you'll know you had a sister, you'll grieve like anyone else, clean grief, respectable grief. But the details—" he spread his hands, "—the details are where people drown."

"And if I refuse?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. Refusing meant keeping all of it—every detail, every night, every ledger line—raw and alone, at sixteen, in a city that had just demonstrated what it did to girls who knew sleeping words. Refusing meant becoming a problem, and they had just shown me the price of problems.

I want to be able to tell you I fought him. That I spat in the tea or swore in the First Tongue or went down fighting.

I signed.

That's the treason at the center of me, the one all the smaller ones orbit: not that I forgot, but that I chose it. That when they put the pen in my burned-blistered hand—my hands were blistered too, I'd pulled her out as far as the culvert and no farther—I signed my sister's soul into confirmed disposal, and my own memories into voluntary surrender, and I did it *gladly*, in that moment. Gladly! Because the grief was drowning me, and they offered me a rope, and only later—only much later, standing in night markets selling charms cut from other people's surrendered sorrows—did I understand that the rope was also the leash, and the leash was also the chain, and I had knotted it around my own throat and called it mercy.

They took the details. They left the fact. I woke the next morning knowing I had a sister, knowing she was dead, knowing I was sad—and nothing else. No face. No voice. No gap-toothed whistle. No word left untranslated.

Just the edges of the hole.

* * *

Twelve years I've run my thumb along those edges.

Twelve years, and here is the joke the universe has been saving: the forgetting didn't take. Not all the way. Memory, handled right, can be copied without being taken—that was *her* discovery, and it turns out it applies to the thief's own head. Some apprentice work of mine, some echo I cut of my own life before the magistrates got their hands in it, survived somewhere I couldn't reach, in some vial I sold or hid or lost. Little slivers. A gap in teeth. Three notes that make dogs sit. A pond standing up against gravity.

Enough edges to know there was a shape.

And now—last night, at my door, in the dead hour—a child stood in the dark speaking the First Tongue like it was her mother tongue, which it may literally be, wearing a locket with a sliver of light in it that sang a note I have spent twelve years not remembering.

A note I know. Two notes, if you count the second one. The word she never translated, said twice, urgent, like a key turning.

I told the child to come inside. I lit the lamp with hands that would not stop shaking, and I looked at that locket, and I thought: *I know what you are. I've sold a hundred of you. But I have never seen one burn gold.*

Gray is memory. Silver is soul-shorn, secondhand.

Gold is whole.

Somebody owes this child a life, and I am beginning to suspect—with the cold, rising certainty of a woman reading her own forged signature on old paper—that the somebody is me.
