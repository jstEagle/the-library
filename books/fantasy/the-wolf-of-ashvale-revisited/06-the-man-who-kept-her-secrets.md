## 6. The Man Who Kept Her Secrets

*Emrys*

There are three people in Ashvale who know where I sleep, and I'd only trust two of them not to sell me. Corvane is the third.

His quarters in the Warden garrison were exactly what you'd expect: a bed made with military corners, a shelf of confiscated books he was technically supposed to burn, and a window that faced away from everything worth looking at. He'd chosen the room the way he chose everything—deliberately, defensively, like a man building a fortress out of habits.

"You look terrible," he said, opening the door before I knocked twice. He always knew my knock. That should have frightened me more than it did.

"Thank you. I cultivated it."

He stepped aside to let me in, and I caught the smell of him—leather and ink and that cheap soap the garrison issues by the barrel. Twelve years of that smell. I could have mapped my whole adult life by it.

"I need a favor," I said.

"Of course you do. Sit down. No—stand. You pace when you're about to ask for something enormous."

He knew that too. He knew all of it: how I took my tea, which hand I favored when lying, the exact pitch of silence I went into when a memory of Bryn got its teeth into me. He had spent years assembling me, piece by piece, the way I assembled other people's pasts for sale. The difference was that he never charged, and I never thanked him, because thanking him would have made us something we couldn't afford to be.

"Someone's hunting a child," I said. "Wardens. Yours."

"Not mine." His jaw did the thing it did when he was choosing between truths. "Verrick's order. Anomalous speech, they're calling it. First Tongue, if you ask anyone honest, and nobody does."

"The child is with me."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below us, a recruit was being yelled at about boot polish, and the sound seemed to come from another country.

"With you," Corvane repeated. "Emrys. They're burning stalls over this. Osk's fence shop is ash and Verrick has him in a cell answering questions about you with pliers."

I knew. I'd watched from an alley with a child's fingers laced through mine, doing the arithmetic of survival while a man who'd once watered down my wine to cheat me gently got dragged into the dark because I was too careful to be caught instead. That sum didn't balance. It wasn't going to.

"I need to get her out of the city," I said. "Both of us. Through the gates, past the wards, tonight or tomorrow night. And I need you not to ask why."

Corvane crossed to the window. For a long moment he just stood there, a big man making himself small against the grey light, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd been doing since the knock in the dead hour. Every answer cost something. He was deciding which currency he could spare.

"I can get you through the tanner's gate," he said finally. "Third watch. There's a man named Dulac who owes me his career and hates me for it, which makes him reliable."

"That's it? No lecture?"

"Oh, there's a lecture." He turned. "Just not the one you're expecting. Emrys—if they catch you helping that child, they won't hang you. Hanging's for smugglers and cutthroats. What you do is already a crime they've stopped naming because naming it would admit it exists. They'll take you below. You understand me? Not the undercourt. *Below* below. And they'll take the child first, so you'll tell them everything anyway, so there's no point being brave."

"I'm not brave. I'm annoyed."

"You've been annoyed for twelve years." Something moved behind his eyes—not anger, older than that. "I keep waiting for you to tell me what happened. What actually happened, back then. You were there when the ban passed, weren't you? You knew Bryn."

The name hit like cold water. He'd never said it aloud before. Not once in twelve years, though he must have read it in a hundred reports, seen it on the seal I stole from a dying magistrate, heard it in the gaps of every story I'd ever half-told him after too much gin.

"Don't," I said.

"Why not? Because you'll have to lie to me, and we both hate it?"

"Because I'll have to tell the truth," I said, "and then you'll have to do something about it, and then one of us will be dead. Probably you. Possibly me. Definitely not them."

He laughed—one short, unhappy note—and came toward me, and for a moment I thought he was going to touch my face, and for a longer moment I thought I'd let him, and then he stopped just short, the way he always did, the way we both always did, two people standing on either side of a door neither would open.

"Tonight," he said. "Tanner's gate, third watch. Bring the child. Bring nothing else you can't burn."

* * *

*Corvane*

She left, and I sat on the edge of a bed made with corners sharp enough to draw blood, and I hated myself with the calm efficiency of long practice.

Here is what Captain Corvane Hale knows: that the magic ban is a theft committed by thieves wearing judges' robes. Here is what he has done about it for eleven years: nothing visible. A ledger hidden in a wall. Confiscated charms slipped back to their owners through intermediaries who never learned his name. Small treasons, each one deniable, none of them enough. I told myself I was gathering evidence. I told myself the evidence would matter someday, when the moment came. Mostly I told myself whatever kept my hands from shaking.

And here is what else I know, the thing I've never written down anywhere: that Emrys Nystrom lies to me the way other people breathe, and that I love her anyway, and that these are not separate facts. Her lies are load-bearing. Take one out and the whole structure of her comes down, and I'd be standing in the rubble asking what she was hiding when I always knew.

The child changed things. Children change things; that's their function in stories and in Verrick's orders alike.

I went to my strongbox—the real one, not the one a search party would find—and took out the evidence I'd been hoarding like a miser: ledgers, seals, a magistrate's confession taken off a dying man whose name I still remembered even though no one official ever would. Payment records. Soul-tithe receipts. Twelve years of paperwork proving that the law was the crime.

It wasn't enough. Paper isn't enough in Ashvale; paper burns, and these people own the fire. But paper plus a living witness plus a child who speaks the First Tongue—that might tip something over. That might make the moment I'd been waiting for arrive whether I was ready or not.

So I'd help her. Of course I'd help her. I'd have helped her if she'd asked me to burn the garrison down, which is precisely the problem with loving someone: it converts your judgment into fuel.

Before she left, I'd asked her for one thing she hadn't given me—a charm, one of the luck-charms she sells at her stall, the kind I confiscate by the dozen and return by the dozen through channels she doesn't suspect. She'd laughed and pressed one into my hand without asking what for. She thinks I collect them as keepsakes. She's wrong, but I let her be wrong, because the truth is worse: I've been having them read.

Every charm she sells is a stolen memory folded into glass. I know that. Everyone in the trade knows that. What nobody knows is that for eleven years I've been taking her castoffs to a blind hedge-witch in Lowmarsh who can feel the shape of a memory without seeing inside it, and asking her one question: *whose grief is this?* Because Emrys will never tell me what happened to Bryn. But she sells pieces of other people's nights, and sometimes, in the margins of somebody else's memory, the hedge-witch finds edges of hers.

I put the new charm in my pocket. It was warm. They always were, faintly, like something recently alive.

Tonight I'd get her through the gate. Tomorrow, maybe, I'd finally get her truth. I was a Warden captain planning to smuggle the most wanted memory-thief in Ashvale out of the city while carrying stolen evidence against my own superiors, and my hands were steady, and that steadiness frightened me more than anything Verrick could sign.

* * *

*Emrys*

We met where he said, in the reek of the tanneries, where the stink covers everything including guilt. Vess rode on my hip with her hood up, heavy as a sack of wet sand and twice as likely to squirm, and she looked up at Corvane with the flat golden appraisal children reserve for men who might be either rescuers or kidnappers.

"This is Captain Hale," I told her in trader's cant, which she understood better than she let on. "He owes me."

"He doesn't look like he owes you."

"They never do. That's the trick of owing."

Corvane crouched to her level, which Wardens don't do, and offered her the solemn nod one professional gives another. "Can you be quiet until I say otherwise?"

Vess considered him. Then she said something in the First Tongue—it sounded like water going backward—and I felt Corvane go still in a way that had nothing to do with caution.

"What did she say?" he asked me.

"She said your shadow is tired."

For a heartbeat he looked like a man reading his own death warrant and finding a familiar signature on it. Then he stood, and the mask came back, and he walked us through the tanner's gate past a guard named Dulac who stared at a point six inches to the left of all three of us and waved us through like a man paying off a debt he'd rather forget.

And that should have been it. That should have been the end of the chapter where Corvane saves us and I start believing in him again.

But at the gate, in the torchlight, when he embraced me—the first time in twelve years he'd initiated it—I felt the shape in his coat pocket. Hard, round, the size of a walnut. The size of a charm.

My charm. The one I'd given him at his quarters, except I'd counted my stock that morning out of pure habit, and I knew exactly which one he'd taken. The river one. The drowning boy's last afternoon, the one I'd pulled from a corpse's effects years ago and could never bring myself to sell because some memories deserve to be lost, not resold.

Why would Corvane carry that out of the city? Why would he carry any charm? He confiscated them for a living. Unless—

Unless he wasn't carrying it out. Unless he was carrying it *in*. To someone who could read it. To someone who could tell him what was inside it, or what was around its edges, or what a thief like me left smeared on everything I touched.

He loved me. That was true; I'd stake my life on it, and possibly already had. But love and surveillance had never once, in the history of Ashvale, been mutually exclusive, and I had just watched a good man walk me to freedom with a piece of somebody's soul in his pocket, and I could not make myself believe the two facts were strangers to each other.

"Go," he whispered against my hair. "Don't stop until the ridge. Don't send word. Don't—"

Behind us, the gate bell began to ring. Not the shift-change pattern. The alarm.

Corvane went rigid. Down the wall, torches were multiplying, and a voice I recognized as Verrick's was giving orders in the tone men use when the trap has already closed and they're enjoying saying it anyway.

"Emrys." Corvane's face, close to mine, had gone grey. "Get her out. Whatever happens at this gate in the next five minutes—get her out."

"And you?"

He smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all year, because it was the smile of a man who has just realized the price tag on his loyalty and intends to pay it quietly.

"I have some accounts to settle," he said, and turned back toward the ringing bells, and the gates of Ashvale began to grind shut behind us.
