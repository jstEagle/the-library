## 7. Down Among the Unlicensed

The trouble with living under a false name is that eventually you answer to it.

I've been Marta Kell for six years now — a widowed seamstress with bad eyes and a good excuse for every hour of daylight I spend indoors. Marta Kell pays her tithe on time. Marta Kell attends the Warden-mandated civic readings where a clerk reads out the names of the executed so we can all nod solemnly and be grateful for our lawful, magicless lives. Marta Kell has never once picked a lock, because lockwitches don't exist anymore, do they? Magic's outlawed. The Wardens said so. The magistrates put their seals on it.

Marta Kell was mending a shirt she had no intention of finishing when the knock came at her door. Three taps, pause, two taps. Old crew rhythm. My hands went still before my head caught up, because nobody alive knew that rhythm except three people, and one of them was supposed to be dead in a ditch outside Varrow, and the other two were supposed to be dead in general.

I looked through the peephole out of pure habit, and there she was. Emrys Nystrom, looking like ten years of bad weather, with a child half-hidden behind her skirts like an afterthought.

I didn't open the door. That should tell you everything about how smart I am.

"Go away," I said through the wood. "Wrong house. There's no one here but a seamstress."

"Sefa." Emrys's voice was low and rough, like she'd been gargling with gravel and regret. "Your stitching's showing through the curtain. It always showed. You were a better thief than a seamstress twelve years ago and you're worse now."

"That's slander. I'm excellent at seams."

"You're excellent at excuses. Open the door."

And here's the thing about old loyalty: it doesn't live in your head, where you keep all your sensible reasons for staying dead to people. It lives in your hands. My hand turned the latch while the rest of me was still drafting refusals.

* * *

They sat in my kitchen like ghosts who'd gotten lost on the way to somewhere more haunted. Emrys took tea without sugar because she never took sugar, some point of principle she'd never explain. The child — Vess, small, solemn, maybe seven or eight years old, with eyes too old for the face they lived in — held a cup of milk with both hands and watched me the way you watch a dog that might bite or might be friendly and might, worst of all, be both.

"So," I said. "You're alive."

"I'm alive," Emrys agreed.

"Varrow?"

"Didn't die there. Disappointed a lot of people instead. It's become something of a specialty."

"You could have sent word."

"To where? You burned your letters when we split. You made me watch. You said—" she paused, and I saw her decide not to finish the sentence, which meant she remembered it exactly, "—you had your reasons."

"I did have reasons." I set my cup down harder than necessary. "Reasons named Pell and Rook, who got taken by Wardens because someone — I won't name her, she's sitting right in front of me — decided the Ashvale job needed a fourth pair of hands. They spent fourteen months in the reeducation cells. Do you know what comes out of those cells, Emrys? Not Pell and Rook. Something smaller. Something that flinches at its own name."

Something crossed Emrys's face — not guilt exactly; guilt would've been easier to look at. Recognition, maybe. A woman cataloguing a debt she'd already counted.

"They're here," she said quietly. "In Ashvale. Under new names. I checked."

"Of course you checked. Checking is free. Knocking costs."

The child spoke then, and I nearly dropped my cup.

Not at the words — I didn't know the words. That was the problem. The sounds came out of that small mouth in a rhythm I knew in my bones, a cadence from a room I'd spent six years not thinking about, a voice-shaped hole in the shape of my brother Denny saying *come on, Sef, one more door, one more*, the night before the fever took him at nineteen. The child wasn't speaking to me. She was speaking to Emrys, some low argument in a language older than any kingdom, older than the ban, older than the magistrates' whole rotten ledger. But it landed on me like a hand on the shoulder.

I stood up so fast my chair went over backward.

"What did she say," I demanded. "What language is that."

Emrys looked at me for a long moment. "You recognize it."

"Don't answer questions with questions, that's Corvane's trick and I hate it."

"The First Tongue," she said. "It's called the First Tongue. And what she said was that you're afraid of me, and that being afraid isn't the same as hating me, though she can see why I might confuse them."

The child looked up at me over her milk. Her expression didn't change at all. Somewhere behind her eyes, something looked out that had absolutely no business being in a seven-year-old, and I felt the hairs on my arms stand up like they used to before a job went wrong.

"Get out," I said. "Both of you. Take the weird child and get out of my house, Marta Kell has a reputation—"

"Marta Kell," Emrys said, standing slowly, "is going to want to hear the rest. Because the rest involves the magistrates selling souls beneath the mountain, and a seal with Bryn's name on it, and a locket full of light that doesn't obey any law they've ever written." She stopped at the door. "But sure. Your reputation. Guard it well. It's the only thing you've got left, and look how much use it's been."

They left. I righted my chair. I sat back down. I picked up the shirt.

I sewed four stitches in six hours. Marta Kell's eyesight really is going.

* * *

Finding the twins wasn't hard, which frightened me more than finding them easily would have. Hard-to-find men are careful men. Easy-to-find men are men who've stopped believing anyone's coming for them.

Pell was going by Tam and working the fish stalls off Brackish Row, gutting things with a knife-hand that still moved like it was picking pockets. Rook — Callum now, of all the bland names — swept floors in a counting-house, which struck me as either penance or the world's longest con, depending on which twin you asked. Neither of them asked anything when I showed up. They just looked at each other across the market noise, and then at me, and Rook said, "How bad?" the way you ask a doctor.

"Bad," I said. "It's her."

Rook leaned his broom against the wall with great ceremony, the way he does when he wants you to know he's making a decision. "Then it's a no."

"It hasn't even been explained yet."

"Doesn't need explaining. It's her, therefore it ends with us in a cell or a ditch. Twelve years ago it ended with us in a cell. I've done my time in the arithmetic of Emrys Nystrom, Sefa. The sums don't come out."

"Pell?"

Pell wiped fish blood on his apron, thoughtful. "I want to hear her lie first. She's a good liar. It's almost worth it."

So we gathered that evening in the back room of a tavern called the Drowned Bell, where the owner owed me a favor dating back to a matter involving a magistrate's wife and a memorable quantity of stolen jewelry. Emrys laid it all out: the seal, the locket, the court beneath the mountain, the soul sold by the very men who outlawed the selling of souls. She told it clean and spare, no embellishment, which was itself suspicious — Emrys embellishes when she wants something cheap and goes honest when she wants something dear.

"And you want us to break into the undercourt of the magistrates," Rook said, "guarded by the last dragon-fire in the world, which will kill us, or worse, report us."

"Yes."

"With a child."

"She's not—"

"With a *child*, Emrys."

"A child the clerks are waiting for. Which means she walks in the front door where none of us can." Emrys spread her hands. "I'm not asking you to die. I'm asking you to help me steal back my sister's soul from the men who sold it. If that goes wrong, dying's just the paperwork."

Silence. Pell turned his cup in slow circles. Rook stared at the table like it owed him money.

"No," Rook said again, softer this time, which was worse. "No. I'm sorry. I loved her once too, whatever we were. But I got rebuilt in those cells, Sefa. They take you apart and give you back with fewer pieces. What's left of me sweeps floors and keeps its head down and stays *alive*. Ask me for coin, ask me for alibis, ask me for anything. Don't ask me for that."

And then the child, who had been sitting quietly in the corner eating bread like the fate of nations was somebody else's errand, said something.

Just a few words. Soft. In that tongue.

I watched it hit them like weather. Pell's cup stopped turning. Rook went white around the mouth. Because it wasn't the child's voice, quite — it was layered, the way a bell has layers if you listen long enough, and underneath a seven-year-old's piping was something older, warmer, familiar in the specific terrible way that only dead people are familiar.

"Denny," I heard myself say. My brother's name. I hadn't said it aloud in eleven years; I'd rationed it like water in a drought. The sound the child had made had Denny's lift at the end, his habit of ending statements like questions, *one more door, Sef?*

"What did you say?" Rook whispered. He was leaning forward now, broom-logic forgotten. "Girl. What did you just say?"

Vess looked at him with those old eyes and answered in trade-tongue, plain and clear: "I said what the voices told me to say. They crowd sometimes. I try to be polite about whose turn it is."

"Whose turn," Pell repeated faintly. "Whose turn was it just now?"

The child considered him with grave courtesy. "A man named Aldous. He says he used to hold you upside down over the harbor rail until you confessed to eating the last of the plums. He says you never confessed and he's proud of you for it."

Pell made a sound I'd only heard from him once before, at a funeral. Aldous. Our fence. Dead nine years, drowned, officially an accident, and the twins had carried that loss like a stone in a boot ever since, because Aldous had been theirs the way Denny had been mine — the specific person the ban had taken and filed away.

"He's in there," Rook said. His voice cracked down the middle like cheap ice. "He's *in there*. In the court. On a shelf."

"On a shelf," Emrys said quietly. "Like inventory. Like bolts of cloth. That's what they've built down there, Rook. A warehouse for everyone they told us the fire destroyed. Nobody burned. Nobody was lost. They were *filed*." She let that sit. Emrys always knows when to shut up; it's her single best skill and she wastes it constantly on small lies.

Rook looked at his brother. Pell looked at the table. I looked at the child, and Denny's cadence was still ringing in my skull, *one more door, Sef, one more,* and I thought: I have spent six years being Marta Kell, and Marta Kell is going to die of boredom in a room full of unfinished shirts, and there are worse deaths than one more door.

"Well," I said. "Someone has to keep you lot from getting killed by your own cleverness. History suggests it's a full-time position."

Rook exhaled through his teeth. "This is stupid."

"Enormously," I agreed.

"We'll all die."

"Statistically likely."

Pell grinned, and it was awful and wonderful, the old grin, the pre-cell grin, the one with all its teeth. "One more door," he said, and I flinched, and he saw me flinch, and neither of us apologized, because that's family.

"One condition," Rook said, holding up a finger, recovering his dignity by force. "Nobody tells me what the plan is until after I've eaten. A man shouldn't make peace with his death on an empty stomach."

Emrys laughed — actually laughed, short and startled, like the sound had escaped custody — and reached into her coat, and set a map on the table between the cups and the crumbs. The mountain. The flooded shafts. The way down.

"There's a broker called the Tithe," she said. "He holds the only true map of the undercourt. Tomorrow we buy it."

"Buy with what?" Pell asked. "None of us have two coins to rub together, unless Sefa's still got the magistrate's wife's earrings—"

"I returned those," I said with dignity. "Mostly."

"Buy with what?" Pell repeated.

Emrys's face did something complicated and closed, like a shop pulling down its shutter. "That," she said, "is my problem."

Which is how I learned, later and too late, exactly what kind of coin the Tithe took — and exactly how many times Emrys Nystrom intended to pay it before this was done.

She was already reaching for the map's corner when Vess tugged my sleeve and said, in trade-tongue, in a small voice pitched only for me:

"He says thank you, Sefa. For opening the door."

I didn't ask which *he*. Some doors you open, and some doors open you, and either way, by morning we were thieves again.
