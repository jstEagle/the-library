## 8. The Price of Passage

The Tithe kept shop in a drowned chapel three streets below the tanneries, which was the sort of address that told you everything before you ever met the man. Nobody built a business on drowned-chapel real estate unless what they sold was worse than the smell.

Sefa led us down. Pell and Rook flanked Vess between them, which had been my instruction and which they'd obeyed with the particular enthusiasm of people who'd been handed an excuse to treat a child like contraband. Rook kept up a low patter of commentary—"if anyone asks, she's my daughter, and I'm a respected cobbler"—that fooled no one, least of all Rook, who had been many things in his life and a cobbler in none of them.

Vess walked with her hand in Sefa's and her wooden bird in her other fist, and she looked at the flooded street like it was a story she was being told too fast.

The chapel's bell tower leaned over the waterline like a drunk leaning on a friend. The doors were gone; the doorway hung with oiled canvas that someone had once tried to dye black and gotten a color that could only be described as *apologetic*. Sefa pushed through it without knocking.

Inside, the pews had been sawn into counters, and the counters were stacked with jars.

I have seen a lot of terrible collections in my life. I own several. But there is something specific about seeing memory stored in bulk—hundreds of jars, each one holding a curl of light the color of whatever it held. Grief burns amber. Joy goes pale gold, almost white. Love, I'm told, comes out blue, though I've only seen it twice, and both times I looked away first.

"Emrys Nystrom." His voice came from behind the altar, where he sat in a chair that had clearly been a bishop's once and now served a humbler god. He was smaller than his reputation. Memory-brokers usually are; the work eats you from the inside out, and what's left is mostly appetite. "They said you were dead."

"They say a lot of things about me. Most are flattering lies."

"The dead ones are." He rose, and I watched him take inventory of us the way I'd watched him do to a hundred customers: the twins, Sefa, the child. His gaze snagged on Vess and stayed there a half-second too long, and something moved behind his face that I didn't like, because it looked like arithmetic. "And this is?"

"A customer's daughter. Bored. Expensive to entertain." I stepped sideways, casually, until I was between him and the light under Vess's collar. "We need a map. The mountain. The undercourt. All of it—the approaches, the shafts, the shelving floors, the way out."

The Tithe laughed. It wasn't a cruel laugh, exactly. It was the laugh of a man who has heard every request so many times that cruelty would require effort. "Nobody buys a map of the undercourt, Emrys. People buy maps *out* of the undercourt. Occasionally. Posthumously, through their heirs."

"Then I'll be your first repeat customer in both directions."

He studied me for a long moment. Behind him, the jars did their quiet work, glowing faintly, a congregation that never sang. "You understand what you're asking for. That map exists in one copy, in one head, because paper can be seized and heads can be bargained with. Mine. And I don't sell it. I trade it. Once. Whoever holds it next becomes the only person alive who knows those corridors—and when they die down there, and they will die down there, the knowledge dies with them, and I get to build a new one from whoever survives long enough to sell it back." He smiled. His teeth were very even, which on him looked less like health than like patience. "So. What have you brought me? You're good, but you're not *that* good. Nothing in that satchel covers it."

"I know what it costs."

"Do you?" He came around the altar slowly. "Because everyone says that, and then I name the price, and their faces do this thing. This little collapse. Like a house settling." He stopped in front of me. Up close I could see that his eyes were the flat gray of water over deep places. "Your happiest memory, Emrys Nystrom. Freely given. Fully extracted. Not a copy—I don't deal in copies, copies rot—but the original, root and branch, so that when it's gone you won't even miss it, because missing requires knowing what's absent, and absence without a shape is just weather."

I heard Sefa shift behind me. Heard Pell say, quietly, "Em," and nothing else, because there wasn't anything else to say.

I had known. Of course I had known. In this city you learned early what the Tithe charged, the same way you learned which wells were poisoned: as folklore, as warning, as a story older children told younger ones by candlelight. *He takes your best day.* And I had walked in anyway with my face arranged into the expression of a woman who had already done the arithmetic, because the alternative was letting him see the house settle.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about grief: it makes a kind of sense, after enough years. It gets load-bearing. You build around it. You become a person shaped by the hole, and the hole stops being a wound and starts being architecture.

I had spent twelve years building around Bryn.

But the Tithe wasn't asking for my grief. He knew better. Grief is cheap; the city runs on it. He wanted the counterweight. He wanted the day that made all the other days survivable, the proof I kept against despair, and he wanted it *freely given*, because a stolen memory keeps its edges and a surrendered one dissolves clean, and clean dissolution is what makes a price a price instead of a theft.

"There's a condition," I said.

"You're not in a position for conditions."

"One condition." I took the satchel off my shoulder and set it on the nearest pew-counter. Inside were forty charms, each one a stolen hour, each one worth more than the chapel we stood in. "These go to the crew. Whatever happens down there—they come out, or these find their way back to the people they were taken from. Every jar on your shelves, matched. Consider it a deposit against my return."

"That's not a condition, that's charity."

"It's leverage. A man who holds forty debts to desperate people sleeps badly if those people don't come back." I let him look at the satchel. "You want me walking into your map calm, Tithe. Calm costs extra."

Something flickered across his face—not respect, exactly, but the recognition brokers reserve for customers who've read the same fine print. "Done. Now." He gestured toward the chair the bishop had abandoned. "Sit. And choose."

That was the part they got wrong in the stories. They made it sound like the memory was taken from you, wrestled out, some violent extraction with screaming. It isn't. He sat me down, put two fingers against my temple like a physician checking for fever, and said, *choose*, and the choosing happened inside me, silent, instant, horrible.

Because here's what I found when I went looking: my happiest memory wasn't a wedding or a windfall or any of the days a sensible thief would have paid with. It wasn't even the night Corvane first kissed me in the evidence room of the Warden garrison, reckless and doomed, though God knows that one glowed bright enough to tempt me.

It was a kitchen.

It was a winter kitchen in a house that burned down twelve years ago, and my sister was fourteen and I was eleven, and she had stolen a bag of oranges from a magistrate's private greenhouse—Bryn stole the way other people breathed, without philosophy—and she taught me to peel them in one unbroken spiral while snow came down past the window and she talked about running away to the coast, to a place where magic was still legal and nobody had ever heard of Ashvale, and she made the future sound like a fruit you could open whole, sweet all the way down, no bitter rind anywhere.

*One piece,* she kept saying. *You have to keep it in one piece, Em. Everything worth having stays whole or not at all.*

I remember thinking, in the last moment before the extraction, that it was fitting. That of course it would be her. That I was spending my sister to buy my sister back, paying the same coin twice, and that somewhere beneath the mountain there was probably a shelf with my name already stenciled on it, waiting for the rest of me.

I chose it freely. That was the worst part. No one made me. I opened my hands.

* * *

The extraction itself felt like nothing. That's the trap of it. Two fingers, a pressure like a yawn, a brief coldness behind my eyes—and then the Tithe stepped back, holding a curl of pale gold light in a glass jar the size of a plum, and I watched it swirl there, snow falling past a window, an orange peeling itself in one unbroken spiral, my sister's laugh rendered as pure luminance with no sound at all.

"That's it?" Pell asked, and his voice was too loud in the chapel. "That's all there is to it?"

"That's it," the Tithe said, satisfied, corking the jar. "She'll feel fine. They always feel fine. The mind papers over beautifully—it's the body that files complaints later." He turned to a cabinet behind the altar, and I heard locks turning, three of them, four. "Now. Your map."

What he brought out wasn't parchment. It was a slate, dark and heavy, and when he laid it on the counter and pressed his palm flat against it, lines bloomed up through the stone like veins surfacing under skin. The mountain, seen from below. The mine-shafts, the flooded galleries, and beneath them all—a lattice of rooms drawn in careful clerk's hand, each chamber labeled in a script so small I had to lean close: *Receiving. Assessment. Shelving, tiers one through nine. The Flame Room.*

At the center, marked with a symbol like a closed eye: *The Ledger.*

"Memorize what you need," the Tithe said. "The slate forgets within the hour of leaving this room. It's how I keep my head attached." He looked up at me, and for one moment the broker's mask slipped and I saw the tired old thief underneath, the one who had walked into this chapel thirty years ago to sell a single bad day and never walked out a whole man again. "Take the western shafts. The eastern ones flood on a schedule only the mountain keeps. And Emrys—"

"Yes?"

"When you feel the hole—and you will, tonight, around some ordinary hour, doing some ordinary thing—don't chase it. Chasing an absence is how people go mad. Let it be weather." He tapped the slate. "Learn your map."

So I learned it.

I stood over that stone while the others gathered round, and I committed the undercourt to memory tier by tier: the receiving hall where souls arrived in their lockets and vessels; the assessment chambers where clerks graded light the way grocers grade eggs; the nine shelving floors, row upon row, each cell a jar, each jar a person; and at the bottom, the Flame Room, where dragon-fire guttered in its iron cradle and every contract written in its light waited to come due.

Rook copied it onto his forearm in ink, muttering, in case memory proved less reliable than skin. Sefa watched the door. Vess watched me.

She had climbed onto a pew to see, and she stood there very still with her bird clasped to her chest, studying the glowing slate, and then she pointed one small finger at the Flame Room and said something in the First Tongue—two words, soft, certain—and Sefa went white.

"What did she say?" I asked.

"She said—" Sefa swallowed. "She said, *the fire remembers me.*"

* * *

We left the chapel as the bells rang the hour, and the city took us back the way cities do, folding us into its noise and its smut-smell and its lantern-light, four thieves and a child walking home through streets that had no idea what we carried.

I felt fine. That's the truth of it. I felt fine the whole way up through the tannery district, fine past the market square where the night stalls were lighting their lamps, fine all the way to Sefa's back room where we'd bed down, and I unpacked the map in my head one more time, tier by tier, corridor by corridor, and found it all intact, and thought: *maybe the old man exaggerated. Maybe the body files no complaints at all.*

Then Sefa put water on for tea.

It was nothing. A kettle, a chipped pot, three cups because she'd never owned four. She struck a match and lit the burner and stood waiting for the flame to catch, and outside the window a cart rattled past, and someone in the street laughed at something, a short bright laugh, and the kettle began its low gathering hum—

And I reached for the evening to hold onto it, the way you do, the way I always did, reaching for the small warm weight of *now*—and found nothing there to hold.

Not sadness. Not fear. Just a smooth place, a patch of wall where a window used to be. My mind reached for the best day of my life and touched bare plaster, and worse—worse than the emptiness—was the certainty that something *belonged* there. An absence with a shape I couldn't see. Weather, the Tithe had called it. But weather doesn't make you stand in a stranger's kitchen with your hand pressed flat against your own chest, searching for the wound that isn't bleeding.

"Emrys." Sefa's voice, careful. "You've gone gray."

"Tea's burning," I said, because it was, and because saying true things was suddenly beyond me.

Pell caught my arm as I sat down. "What did you pay him? What did you actually pay?"

"An orange," I said.

He stared at me. So did Rook. So, from her corner, did Vess, her head tilted, her eyes old and young at once, and when she looked at me she did something she hadn't done before: she crossed the room, climbed onto the bench beside me, and put her small hand flat against my chest, right over the smooth place, the way you'd check a horse for fever or a door for drafts.

She frowned. Then she said one word in the First Tongue, very quietly, and Sefa translated it in a voice that barely worked:

"*Empty.* She says you're empty here. And then—" Sefa stopped. Started again. "And then she said, *but not hollow. Something's still holding the shape.*"

I looked down at the child with her hand over my heart, this stranger carrying my sister's soul in fragments, and I understood—with the cold, total clarity that arrives only when it's least welcome—that I had done this before. Twelve years ago. Some bargain, some forgetting, some payment made in the dark that I'd agreed to so thoroughly I couldn't even remember agreeing. I had been spending myself on Bryn since before I knew what I was spending, coin by coin, day by day, and the ledger beneath the mountain had been keeping count the whole time.

The kettle screamed. Nobody moved to pour.

"We leave tomorrow night," I said, and my voice came out steady, which felt obscene. "Western shafts. Everyone sleeps."

Vess took her hand back, slow, and looked up at me with an expression I couldn't read—pity, maybe, or recognition, one keeper of shapes to another. And as she turned away I saw it, just for a second, in the locket at her throat: the soul-light flickering, brightening, straining toward me like a candle flame bending toward an open window.

Toward the shape it remembered.

Toward home.
