## 9. Ashvale Closes Its Gates

There are three kinds of orders a Warden captain dreads receiving.

The first kind comes from above and is stupid — burn the granary to stop the rats, that sort of thing. The second kind comes from below and is worse, because it means someone in your own ranks has been selling information to the very people you're paid to hunt. The third kind is the rarest: an order that is neither stupid nor suspicious but simply true, and true orders are the ones that cost you something.

The order I received on the morning of the ninth of Frostfall was the third kind.

*Seal the gates. Close the market. There are thieves beneath your nose, Captain, and one of them is carrying contraband older than the city. Let no one out.*

I read it twice, standing in my office above the Warden barracks with rain hammering the window like it had a grudge against glass specifically. Then I read the name attached to the seal — Magistrate Aldous Veyne, whose signature I had been authenticating for six years without once wondering what he actually signed — and I understood two things simultaneously.

The first was that Emrys Nystrom was in danger.

The second was that if she was in danger, it was at least half my fault, and possibly all of it, depending on how you counted the charm.

I had taken her charm. That was the thing that kept circling back like a dog that knows it's done wrong. Three nights ago, in her stall between the luck-charms and the bottled courage, while she'd been wrapping up a memory of somebody else's wedding for a widow who wanted to feel loved just once more before winter, I had pocketed a small brass locket off her table like any common cutpurse. I told myself I was investigating. I told myself a lot of things at three in the morning when the barracks were asleep and the rain was loud enough to drown out the smarter parts of me.

Because the locket hadn't held a stolen memory. It had held a *word*. A word in a language I didn't know, spoken by a voice I did, and when I'd held it to my ear alone in the dark, it had said — in Emrys's voice, soft as an apology —

*I know what you're doing, Corvane.*

Which meant either she knew about the locket, or the locket knew about me, and both possibilities were bad in interesting new ways.

So now there was a sealed order on my desk and a city full of gates and one woman with a child somewhere in the Warrens, and every option available to me was some flavor of betrayal. Seal the gates and trap her here for Veyne's men. Open them quietly and commit treason with paperwork. Or the third thing, the thing I'd already decided on before I finished reading the order, because I am apparently the kind of man who decides with his chest instead of his head:

Do both. Seal the gates. And then go tell her to run.

You can love someone and still build a cage around them. Nobody tells you that. They tell you love is open doors and free air, and maybe it is, eventually, for better people than me. But I had spent six years loving Emrys Nystrom from across a legal line so wide you could park a regiment in it, and in all that time the closest I had ever come to keeping her safe was lying to her carefully. Protection, I had learned, is just possession wearing a kinder coat. I told myself this time would be different. I told myself a lot of things.

* * *

Sealing Ashvale takes four hours when it's done properly and forty minutes when it's done scared, and Veyne's order arrived scared. By noon the great gates were barred, the river chains were up, and Wardens stood at every post with instructions that made even the hard ones shift their weight and look at their boots. No one leaves. Not merchants, not pilgrims, not the funeral barges. I watched a ferryman argue himself hoarse at the East Chain and then sit down in his boat and just stare at the water, and I thought: there it is. There's what I've done today. A whole city turned into a jar with a lid on it, and me holding the lid.

"You look like a man at his own funeral," Pell said. He was leaning against the gatehouse wall in a borrowed lamplighter's coat, which fooled absolutely no one, least of all me. The twins had a gift for being exactly where they shouldn't be. "Bad news from upstairs?"

"Routine security measure," I said. "City-wide."

"Right." He nodded slowly. "Routine. That's why there's a Warden on every corner counting noses and why Osk the fence got dragged out of his bed two nights ago for talking funny in his sleep." He pushed off the wall and came close enough that we could speak without performing for the guards. His voice dropped. "Captain. Whatever you think you're doing — she's not going to run. You should know that about her by now."

"She'll run if running is what keeps the child alive."

"And if staying is?" Pell smiled the way he did when he'd already won whatever game we were playing. "You're sealing gates, friend. She's opening doors. Different trades entirely."

He walked off whistling, and I let him, because arresting Pell would have solved nothing and because part of me wanted him to spread it — *the captain sealed the gates but the captain also looked sick about it* — all through the Warrens until it reached her ears. Which was, I admit, exactly what happened. I have never once managed to be subtle where that woman is concerned. Six years of practice and I still telegraph like a boy with his first crush.

* * *

She came to me at dusk, which surprised me. I'd expected to go to her, to spend an evening composing excuses in doorways. Instead there was a knock at my office door — my *office*, in the barracks, where she had no business being and less business surviving — and when I opened it she was standing in the corridor wearing a Warden cloak she had clearly acquired through methods I chose not to inquire into, hood down, arms crossed, looking at me with the expression of a creditor.

"You sealed the gates," she said.

"I received an order."

"You *sealed* the gates, Corvane. Forty minutes. I've seen you slow-walk Veyne's orders for years. You once took eleven days to confiscate a crate of memory-tallow, and I know for a fact you drank half of it." She stepped inside and shut the door behind her. Up close she looked tired in a way that went deeper than sleep — tired like a well that's been drawn from too often. "So don't give me the order. Give me the reason."

And here is the truth, which I will set down plainly since I certainly couldn't say it aloud: the reason was that I had spent six years watching her walk toward fire and calling it patience. Every year she grew more reckless and more brilliant and further from reach, and every year I told myself that as long as she was free — as long as the city stayed porous and her name stayed off the lists — I could keep her alive from the inside. But the lists had found her now. Veyne's seal had found her. The child had found her. The careful architecture of my cowardice was coming down all at once, and the only tool left in my hands was a lid for a jar.

"The reason," I said, "is that they're not hunting smugglers anymore. This isn't a raid. It's a net, and it has your shape in it. Vess — the child — she's what they want. Everything else is noise." I pulled the sealed order from my desk and let her read it, because showing her was faster than arguing and because some stubborn part of me needed her to see I wasn't lying. "When the net closes, everyone connected to her goes in it. You. The lockwitch. The twins. Osk already has. So I'm telling you the one thing I can tell you: there's a drainage culvert under the tannery row that the patrols stopped checking after I reassigned the men. Tonight, while the changeover happens. Take the child and go. Just you. Leave the rest to me — I can lose the paperwork on Sefa and the twins, buy them weeks—"

"No."

"It's not a negotiation, Emrys—"

"You're right," she said. "It's not." She handed the order back with terrible gentleness, which was always the worst sign. "You're going to seal me in a box and call it a way out. You're going to send me alone down a hole you picked, on a night you picked, away from the only people who can do what needs doing, and you're going to stand there with that honest face and believe you're saving me." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was so much worse. "You've been saving me for six years, Corvane. Ask yourself sometime what from."

"From *this*," I said, and I heard my own voice crack like a green branch. "From Veyne. From what happens to people who go under that mountain. From—" I stopped, because the next word was *me*, and I wasn't ready to say it, and she saw me not saying it, which is its own confession.

"From you," she finished for me, softly. "Yes. I know."

"I love you." It came out before I could dress it up. Six years of discipline, undone by one sentence. "That's the whole of it. That's the ugly, small, selfish whole of it. I love you, and I cannot protect you out there, so I am trying to protect you the only way I have left, which is badly, which is with walls. I know what it looks like. I'm asking you to believe it isn't a cage."

And she looked at me — really looked, the way she looked at a lock before picking it, assessing every pin — and she said, "It's a cage, Corvane. It's just one you're standing inside too."

* * *

That's when the door opened.

Not kicked. Not forced. Simply opened, the way a door opens when a child who has no concept of locked things decides to enter, and Vess walked in — small, solemn, wrapped in a coat three sizes too big, with the locket glinting at her throat. How she'd gotten into the barracks, past two checkpoints and a corridor full of armed men, I genuinely could not tell you. I suspect the answer involves Sefa and a great deal of professional embarrassment on my part.

Emrys moved toward her instantly, instinctively, the way water moves downhill. "Vess. Sweetheart, we were just—"

The child ignored her. She looked up at me instead, and her eyes were old in a way no child's eyes should be, and she spoke.

Not in Trade-Tongue. In the other language — the First Tongue, the one that had gotten a fence dragged from his bed, the one that made Wardens write reports with shaking hands. I didn't understand the words. But I understood the *tone*, the way you understand thunder without studying meteorology, and the tone was unmistakable.

She called me a liar.

I know how that sounds — that I, a grown man, an officer of the law, felt flayed by a sentence in a dead language delivered by someone missing several of her front teeth. But there it is. Some words carry their meaning in the bone. She said it twice, quiet and precise, and pointed one small finger at my chest, and then she said something longer, softer, and Emrys went white as paper.

"What did she say?" I asked. My voice came out wrong.

Emrys didn't answer me. She crouched down to the child's level. "Say it again, Vess. Slowly."

Vess said it again. Slowly. Watching me the entire time, the way you watch a man decide something.

Emrys straightened up. When she turned to face me her expression had gone strange — not angry, which I could have survived, but *grieved*, grieved on my behalf, which I could not. "She says you're a liar," she translated, each word laid down like a card turned face-up. "She says you seal the gates with one hand and hold out a key with the other, and the key opens a room inside the cage." She paused. "And then she says—" and here Emrys's voice caught, just slightly, "—she says the cage is the kindest cage ever built, and that's how you know it's still a cage. Because whoever built it built it out of love, and love doesn't make a thing free. It makes a thing *kept*."

The rain filled the silence against the window. Somewhere below, a patrol changed shifts; I heard the muffled exchange of passwords and thought absently that I had chosen the culvert timing well, that the traitor part of my mind was still working efficiently even while the rest of me stood in the wreckage.

"That child," I said finally, "has known me for five days."

"She's known *kinds* of men for longer than that," Emrys said. "Longer than either of us has been alive. She knows what you are, Corvane. She's just the only one of us brave enough to say it in a language nobody can arrest her for." She took Vess's hand. "The culvert. Under tannery row. Tonight?"

I nodded, because what else was there. "Midnight. Changeover runs till quarter past. After that the patrol resumes."

"Thank you," she said, and I knew — I knew with absolute certainty — that she was thanking me the way you thank a guard who's about to be robbed: sincerely, and without any intention of letting gratitude interfere with the robbery. "Keep Sefa and the twins off the lists as long as you can."

"Emrys—"

But they were already at the door, the child and the thief, and the child turned back once to look at me. And this time she didn't speak in the First Tongue. She spoke in Trade-Tongue, plain Ashvale street-words, in a voice like a small bell.

"You can come *with* us," she said. "Instead of closing things."

Then they were gone, down the corridor, past my own Wardens, invisible as smoke, and I sat down at my desk among the sealed orders and the unsealed lies and I did not move for a long time.

Here is what I did next, and I record it without excuse: I opened the drawer where I kept the confiscated things I was supposed to have destroyed, and I took out the brass locket, and I held it to my ear one more time. *I know what you're doing, Corvane.* Then I put it in my coat, over my heart, where a coward keeps the things he can't use and can't discard, and I went downstairs to falsify the patrol logs.

Midnight came. Quarter past midnight came. The culvert under tannery row stood empty, and the patrol log showed nothing unusual, and somewhere beyond the fourth checkpoint a thief, a child, a lockwitch, and two twins were walking into the dark under the mountain with my blessing and without my permission.

They left the cage door open behind them, you see.

The question that kept me awake until dawn, staring at the ceiling of a room I'd lived in for six years — the question I will be carrying down that mountain myself, soon, because of course I am, because a cage is still a cage even from the inside — was a simple one:

If I follow them, am I escaping?

Or am I just bringing the cage along?
