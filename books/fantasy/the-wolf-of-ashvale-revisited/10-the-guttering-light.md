## 10. The Guttering Light

My sister says I count things when I'm nervous. She says it like it's a flaw.

I say it's a talent. Most people, when the world is ending, stand around gaping at it. Me? I count. It keeps the hands busy and the screaming internal, which in my professional opinion is the difference between an amateur criminal and a professional one.

So: as we stood at the mouth of the old Adit Nine, the mine-shaft that the city of Ashvale had spent two hundred years pretending wasn't there, I counted what we had.

Four thieves. One child. One lockwitch with a crowbar she'd named, which I found concerning. Two lanterns, three flares, half a wheel of hard cheese, and a rope that Sefa swore was sound and which I had personally watched her splice in the dark, which meant it was either perfectly fine or a murder weapon depending on her mood. Against all that: one mountain, full of dead men's paperwork and the last dragon-fire under the sky, currently going out.

The fire was the problem. Well. The fire was several problems wearing a trench coat.

"Say it again," Pell said. "Slowly. I want to enjoy it."

Emrys didn't look up from the map — the Tithe's map, bought with a piece of her own head, though none of us had known that at the time and she'd only told us after, in that flat voice she uses when she's decided a thing is already paid for and therefore not worth discussing. "When the dragon-fire dies," she said, "every soul shelved in the undercourt stops being held and starts being *owned*. Permanent transfer. No appeal, no release, no theft possible, because you can't steal something that's been legally absorbed into the hoard."

"And when does it die?"

"The clerks keep it fed on tithed memory. The magistrates have stopped sending tithes." She folded the map along its worn creases. "Sefa's contact says it's guttering now. Days."

"Days," Pell repeated, delighted in the way he gets delighted, which is to say in a way that makes strangers move away from him at parties. "So we're racing an extinction event through flooded tunnels to burgle hell. Before breakfast I was having such a nice ordinary life under a false name."

"You were mending nets in Grayside and complaining about it," I said.

"I was *living*, Rook. There's a difference."

There wasn't, actually. I'd seen his nets. But I kept that to myself, because twins are allowed one kindness per day and I'd already used mine letting him walk in front of me down the switchback trail, where anything falling would hit him first.

* * *

Adit Nine breathed cold air at us, and the air smelled wrong — or right, depending on your childhood. It smelled like struck flint and hot copper and, underneath that, something older and stranger, like the moment before lightning. Dragon-smell. I'd never smelled it before. I knew it instantly anyway, the way you know a word from a language you were never taught.

Vess knew it too. The child stopped at the threshold and lifted her face like a plant finding sun, and said something in that First Tongue of hers — that impossible, ancient language that made my dead grandmother's voice live behind my ribs whenever I heard it — and whatever she said, Emrys went still as a held breath.

"What did she say?" Sefa asked.

"It says the fire is sad," Emrys translated quietly. "She says it's been calling."

"Wonderful. The apocalypse is *lonely*." Pell shouldered his pack. "Lead on. I want this over while I'm still young enough to be traumatized productively."

We went in.

I counted our steps for the first hour. I stopped counting at four thousand because the tunnel stopped being a tunnel and started being a throat, wide enough that our lanterns couldn't find the ceiling, the walls sweating mineral light — veins of blue and green in the rock, some natural phosphorescence the miners must have worshipped once. The water came up to our ankles, then our knees. Cold enough to make your bones file complaints. Somewhere ahead, water fell in long slow drips that echoed like a clock in an empty house.

That was the other reason I'd stopped counting steps. Every drip sounded like one.

"Talk to me, lockwitch," Emrys said. Her voice carried flat along the water. "How far to the dry galleries?"

Sefa held her crowbar-lantern high. She'd named the crowbar *Duchess*, which told you everything about Sefa and nothing you wanted to know. "Map says the shaft forks below. Left fork floods seasonally — we're standing in the seasonal part. Right fork climbs to the ventilation galleries and from there down into the undercourt proper." She paused. "Assuming the map isn't lying, which maps purchased from memory-brokers frequently do."

"The map cost me enough that it shouldn't dare," Emrys said, and there was something underneath the words, some hollow echo, and I thought about what the Tithe had taken from her and decided, professionally, not to think about it. You don't poke at a crewmate's missing pieces mid-heist. You save it for the drinking afterward, if there is an afterward, and lately there's always an afterward.

We took the right fork.

* * *

Here is a thing nobody tells you about dying wonders: they're louder than living ones.

Living magic hums. I've felt it twice in my life — once through a stolen charm gone warm in my palm, once through a door in a magistrate's house that shouldn't have opened — and both times it hummed like a well-fed cat. This did not hum. This *groaned*. As we climbed the ventilation gallery, the groan got into the stone and then into our teeth and then into the spaces between thoughts, a vast slow exhalation, like the mountain itself was sighing out the last of something.

And the light. Saints preserve me, the light.

It came up through cracks in the gallery floor, seams of gold-orange glow widening and narrowing with each groan like embers breathing. Our shadows swung enormous against the walls. Vess walked straight down the center of the gallery with her arms slightly out, the way children do on balance beams, except she wasn't balancing — she was *listening*. Every few steps she'd stop and say a word in the First Tongue, soft, like feeding crumbs to something shy.

"What's she saying?" Pell whispered. Pell never whispers. That he was whispering told me more about the place than the light did.

"She's answering it," Emrys said. "It speaks slow. Old. Like—" She searched. "Like someone very tired trying to remember why they stayed awake."

I looked down through a seam at my feet and the glow looked back, patient and enormous and nearly done, and I am not a man who feels small — ask anyone, it's one of my defining features — but I felt small then. Small and briefly honest. Whatever slept under Ashvale had been alive when my grandmother's grandmother was ash, and we were walking on its deathbed, and we'd come to rob it, or rather to rob the vultures who'd built a filing cabinet around its heart.

"Rook." Sefa's hand on my shoulder. I hadn't noticed I'd stopped walking. "Keep moving. Staring into it is how it gets hooks in you."

"Does it?"

"No idea. Don't find out."

We moved.

* * *

The water found us again in the deep gallery, and this time it wasn't ankle-deep politeness. The map — the expensive, extortionate map — marked a flooded stretch called the Drowned Level, chest-high at low season, and either the seasons had changed or the mountain had opinions, because it came at our waists and rising. Black water, still as glass, warm. Which was worse than cold. Warm water underground means something is heating it, and only one something down here qualified.

"Time check," Emrys said.

Sefa consulted the tide-gauge she'd rigged from a stolen barometer and spite. "Water's rising faster than it should. Fire's cycling harder — big breaths, short rests. When it goes out for good, I'd guess these galleries flood to the roof inside an hour. Maybe less."

"So our real deadline isn't when the fire dies," I said. "It's when the fire *starts dying properly*. The last bad breath."

Pell clapped me on the shoulder. "Look at you, doing optimism."

"Somebody has to balance you out."

"We go fast through the flood," Emrys said. "Rope line, hand on shoulder, no gaps. Vess rides my pack. Nobody drinks the water." A beat. "Nobody *touches* the water longer than you have to."

"Why?" Vess asked — in trade-tongue, her accent still strange, words placed like stones across a stream. "Is it bad?"

"It's been sitting on the fire's doorstep for a hundred years," Emrys said. "It remembers too much."

I decided not to ask how she knew that. Emrys Nystrom knows things about memory the way fish know things about water, and every time she explains it, I sleep worse for a week.

We crossed the Drowned Level in forty minutes that felt like a fiscal quarter. The water pressed warm against my chest like a crowd of hands. Once — just once — I felt something brush past my leg that was not a fish, not a rock, not a rope, and it moved with *intention*, and I did what professionals do: I kept my grip on Duchess — no wait, that's Sefa's; I kept my grip on my own crowbar, unnamed because naming tools is Sefa's whole personality and someone has to be normal — and I counted backward from one hundred and did not scream, and by thirty the touch was gone, and by zero we stood dripping on the far shelf of black stone where the water ended as abruptly as a drawn border.

Beyond the border, the tunnel changed character. Smoother. Worked. And lit — faintly, horribly — by lamps of glass and brass set at intervals, burning without oil, fed by piped threads of dragon-glow. The clerks' lighting. We'd crossed out of the wild dark and into the *premises*.

Pell wrung out his sleeves and grinned at the lamps. "Cozy."

"Don't touch them," Sefa said.

"I wasn't going to touch them."

"He was going to touch them," I said.

"I was *considering*—"

"*Quiet*," Emrys said, and we quieted, because Emrys saying quiet quietly is worth any amount of shouting from anyone else.

Ahead, the gallery opened. We crept to the lip of it and looked down into the approach hall of the undercourt, and even knowing — Sefa had briefed us, Emrys had briefed us, we'd rehearsed it in a net-loft above Grayside with chalk diagrams — knowing did nothing. Seeing is its own crime.

Shelves. Rows and rows of them, descending into gold-lit depth, and on the shelves, lights. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Each one a soul, banked and labeled and tended, glowing softly in their niches like a library of candles where every candle was a person. Clerks moved among the rows with ledgers, trimming, adjusting, *filing*, and the scale of it made my counting habit shrivel and die, because you can't count forever, and this was forever, organized alphabetically.

"There," Vess whispered beside me. She gripped the stone edge with both small hands. Down in the gold, somewhere among those ten thousand flames, was the piece of her that had been sold — the piece that was Bryn, though I didn't yet understand how much Bryn, none of us did. The child's eyes reflected all that light and held it. "She's down there. She's *cold*, Rook. Why is she cold on a shelf with fire?"

I had no answer that wasn't a lie, so I gave her the truth instead: "Because the people keeping it aren't keeping it warm. They're keeping it *counted*. There's a difference, and it turns out it matters."

She looked up at me, this child who spoke the oldest language alive and carried a dead woman's soul in a locket, and she nodded slowly, gravely, like I'd said something wise, which was generous of her, because mostly I'd just said something true and angry.

Then Sefa's gauge ticked. She checked it. Checked it again.

"Problem," she said, in the tone she saves for actual problems, as opposed to Pell, who is a permanent condition. "Fire's rest cycle just shortened by half. It's not guttering on a schedule anymore. It's guttering *fast*." She looked up, and in the lamp-light her face had gone the color of the drowned water. "We don't have days. By my numbers we have hours. Maybe six. Fewer, if it panics."

Below us, a clerk paused mid-row, tilted his head as if hearing something far off, and made a small notation in his ledger.

Emrys watched him do it, and I saw her jaw set in that way I'd learned to read across years of jobs gone sideways: the look of a woman converting fear into schedule.

"Then we stop being careful," she said, "and start being quick. Rook — count us down."

I swallowed. Rolled my shoulders. Found the habit waiting for me like an old friend.

"Six hours," I said. "Give or take a gutter."

And somewhere beneath our feet, vast and tired, the last dragon drew a breath that shook dust from the shelves of the dead — and held it.
