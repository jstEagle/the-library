## 11. Court of Sold Souls

I have picked locks in the dark for thirty years, and I want it on the record that I have never once been afraid of the dark itself. The dark is honest. It doesn't pretend to be anything other than a place where you can't see. What I'm afraid of is light in places where light has no business being—light that's been paid for, light with an owner.

The tunnel opened above us like a throat deciding to swallow, and there it was: light. Not firelight, not lamplight. Something steadier than either, something that didn't flicker, because flickering implies a living hand at the wick and nothing down here was alive in any way the word was built to cover.

Rook went first, because Rook always goes first and pretends it's a coin flip. Pell followed because following your twin into a hole in the world is either love or a failure of imagination, and with Pell it has always been both. Emrys came behind me with Vess between us, one small cold hand in mine, and I'll tell you what nobody tells you about holding a child's hand in a place like this: you grip harder than you need to, not to keep them safe, but because their fingers are the only warm thing within reach and you are stealing warmth now too. Apparently it runs in my family.

Then the tunnel let go of us, and we saw it.

Shelves.

I had imagined a court. I'd spent three days of flooded marching imagining it—pillars, thrones, a magistrate on each one wearing his cruelty like formal dress. Something you could point at. Something with a face you could hate honestly.

What we got was inventory.

Rows of niches cut into the living rock, floor to ceiling, gallery after gallery receding into a haze of pale gold light, and in every niche a small glass vessel, and in every vessel a curl of light no bigger than a sparrow's egg. Thousands. Tens of thousands. They were arranged by some system I couldn't read from where we stood, but it was a *system*, that was the obscenity of it. Alphabetical or numerical or by date of acquisition, it didn't matter. Somebody had decided how these were ordered. Somebody had sat down with a stylus and thought, *yes, this one goes here*, and gone home to supper afterward.

"Gods below," Pell whispered, which was funny, because we were below, and if the gods kept offices down here they'd clearly been outsourced.

"It's a warehouse," Rook said. Rook has the gift of saying the true ugly thing before anyone else has finished hoping it isn't true. "It's not even a cruel warehouse. It's a tidy one."

And moving along the rows, quiet as librarians, were figures in grey robes with slate boards tucked under their arms. Clerks. They went niche to niche, checking vessels against entries, turning each little lamp of soul-light a quarter-turn so its tag faced outward, the way you'd rotate stock. One of them paused at a shelf, frowned at a vessel whose glow had dimmed since the last audit, made a note, and moved on. A note. Somewhere in a ledger down here there was an entry that said, in effect, *item 4471-B, fading, monitor*.

That item was a person. Had been a person. Was still, in whatever grammar souls use, a person—the way a seed is still a tree, the way a name is still a mouth.

Vess made a sound next to me. Small. Not a word, not in any tongue, First or otherwise. Just a child hearing a large room be wrong.

"We knew," Emrys said quietly. She wasn't talking to me exactly; she was talking to the version of herself who'd planned this. "We knew it would be bad."

"I pictured chains," I admitted. "Chains I know how to break. I've got picks for chains. Nobody makes picks for shelving."

"There's always a lock," I told her, because it's the first thing I ever taught her and because I needed to hear it more than she did. "There's always a lock. Sometimes it just takes you a while to find the door it belongs to."

* * *

Getting down among the shelves without becoming part of the collection required the oldest trick in the trade: look like you belong, and belong hard. The clerks wore grey; we had grey, more or less, wrapped over our gear, hoods up, slates improvised from broken tile and chalk stub Rook had thoughtfully looted from a Warden station two years ago and never found a use for until tonight. Rook carries useless things the way other people carry grudges. It paid off.

We split into pairs, the way we used to, back when the worst thing a pair could walk into was a merchant's strongroom. I took Vess. Emrys took Pell. Rook ranged, because Rook ranges, it's not a job, it's a temperament.

And here is what I learned walking those aisles, close enough to touch the glass:

The clerks were not monsters. That was the thing I couldn't shake, the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. They were *tired*. They moved with the particular economy of people who have done a task ten thousand times and stopped seeing it. And when one of them passed us—young, hollow-cheeked, ink-stained fingers—he glanced at Vess's hood, at the shape of a child where no child should be shelved, and something crossed his face. Not alarm. Recognition. He looked at her the way a man looks at a photograph of himself at an age he can't get back to.

He didn't report us. He adjusted his slate, turned a vessel, walked on.

Later, Emrys would tell me the head clerk's history confirmed it, but I knew it already, standing in that aisle, from the ink stains alone. These clerks had come down the same road we came down. Thieves, runners, lockwitches—people who followed a debt or a promise or a sister into the mountain, and were given a choice the way a drowning man is given a choice between two hands. Take a shelf number or take a stylus. Keep your soul in a jar where you can visit it, or keep your hands and file the jars of everyone else.

They chose the stylus. Of course they did. You would. I would. God help me, I might have—I've spent thirty years telling myself I'd burn before I filed another woman's brother, and I believe it the way you believe a rope will hold, which is to say completely right up until you put your weight on it.

Evil down here wasn't wearing robes and snarling. Evil was wearing an apron and squinting at handwriting. Evil had a *filing convention*. Evil took holidays, probably—there must be a feast day when the audits pause—and evil felt bad sometimes, in a small managed way, the way you feel bad about a splinter, and then it made its rounds.

I have robbed magistrates. I have watched Wardens burn a market street with people still in it. I thought I had seen the bottom shelf of human behavior, if you'll forgive the phrase. But there is a special room in hell, and I have now stood in it, reserved for cruelty that keeps minutes.

* * *

We were four galleries in, deep enough that the entrance tunnel was a memory and the light had gone from gold to a paler, thinner thing, when I stopped walking.

I hadn't meant to stop. My feet stopped the way your feet stop when you hear your own name in a crowd—not a decision, a reflex older than decisions.

One aisle over. Third row up. There was a vessel there with a glow like the last coal of a bonfire, banked orange, stubborn, refusing to go out entirely. And the tag on it, the little lead seal stamped with a clerk's mark and a catalog number, hung at an angle I recognized.

You have to understand something about me. I taught myself letters off shipping manifests and smugglers' ledgers because schools weren't for girls like me, and the first full word I ever read, sounding it out letter by letter while Bryn—while my brother—held the page steady for me, was written in a hand I would know anywhere, because I'd seen it on everything he ever touched. On his tools. On the inside of his boot, where he hid his savings. On the note he left me the night he didn't come home.

*Corvin.*

My brother's name on a tag in the court beneath the mountain.

I stood there and the aisle stood there and the light stood there and none of us moved, and somewhere far off a clerk was making his rounds, tapping his slate, keeping the world orderly.

Twelve years. Twelve years I'd told myself a story about Corvin, the way you tell stories about the missing so you can get up in the morning. That he'd run off south with a river-woman. That he'd died quick somewhere honest, a collapse, a knife, a river—something with weather in it, something with a body, something a sister could bury in her head. I'd built him a grave out of guesses and visited it every year with a cup of the cheap arrack he liked, and I'd thought that was grief. That was not grief. That was the brochure. This was grief: standing three feet from your brother's soul in a glass jar with a catalog number, in a place where the air tastes of dust and old decisions, learning that he never ran anywhere. He was *filed*. Some clerk had logged him. Some clerk had rotated him a quarter-turn so his tag faced outward, and gone to supper.

I put my hand on the glass.

It was warm. Warmer than the air, warmer than my hand, warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and I am not a woman who cries, I am a woman whose eyes occasionally require maintenance, but I stood in that aisle with my palm flat against the vessel that held my brother and I shook like a lock giving way.

"Corvin," I said. Out loud. Stupidly. Loudly, by the standards of a place like this. "It's Sefa. It's your pain-in-the-arse little sister. I taught myself letters off your manifests, do you remember that? You held the page. You held the page for me."

Nothing. Or—not nothing. The glow leaned. I swear on every pick I own, the glow *leaned*, a degree, maybe less, toward my voice, the way a plant leans at a window. Maybe it was heat off my hand. Maybe it was a trick of the light. Maybe it was my brother, twelve years shelved, tilting toward the sound of the only person alive who said his name like it was a joke with a punchline coming.

I don't care which it was. I have decided what it was, and down here, deciding is the only magic left that nobody's outlawed.

"Sefa." Emrys, behind me. Her voice very careful, the way you talk to someone standing on a ledge. "Sefa, we have to move."

"He's here." My voice came out wrong, flattened, like it had been run through one of their ledgers. "Emrys. He's *here*. All this time. All this time I lit a candle for him on the anniversary and the candle was burning twelve feet above his actual—" I stopped. Words were failing me, and words have never once failed me; words are the one tool I've maintained better than my picks.

Vess slipped her hand out of mine. I felt it go and I let it, which tells you what state I was in, because I don't let go of that child, that's the arrangement, that's the whole arrangement. She stepped closer to the shelf and looked up at the vessel, and then she said something in the First Tongue, soft, two syllables, and the glow leaned again—harder this time, unmistakable, the whole little flame of my brother bending toward a child's voice like grass bending under a bird.

Whatever language that is, it speaks to souls directly. No ledger between. No filing convention. Just name to name.

And my brother, or what was shelved under my brother's number, heard it.

I got my breathing back. Mostly. Enough. I pressed two fingers to the glass the way you'd press them to a pulse, and I said, in a voice I barely recognized as mine, level and cold and useful, the voice I use when a lock is hard: "Hold on. Do you hear me? Hold on. I came down here with picks and I have never once left a lock unpicked, and I am not starting with you. Whatever number they gave you, it's temporary. It's all temporary. Paper burns."

Paper burns. I said it like a spell. Down here it might as well have been one.

* * *

We regrouped in a junction where two galleries met, in the shadow of a pillar carved—someone had bothered—with figures dancing. Old work, pre-magistrate, pre-ledger. People had danced here once, in this exact spot, before someone looked at a soul and saw a unit. I rested my shoulder against the dancer's stone hip and reported what I'd found, and watched Emrys take it in, and watched her jaw set in that way that means a plan is being born whether anyone asked for one or not.

Pell had counted aisles. Rook had found the audit office—a lit doorway, actual desks, the smell of ink and cold tea—and mapped the guards' circuit, such as it was; the clerks trusted the shelves themselves to be security, the way a farmer trusts a fence. Nobody expected thieves down here because down here was where theft ended. Down here was where everything ended up *kept*.

"They audit," Rook said, still vibrating slightly from having seen the audit office, from having seen, on a desk, unattended, a ledger open to columns of numbers beside columns of names. "Twice a year. There's a schedule posted. There's a *schedule*. For counting souls."

"Of course there is," Emrys said. "Bureaucracy is just violence that's been embalmed. It lasts longer that way."

"And the clerks?" Pell asked. "Do we kill them?"

And there it was, the question I'd been carrying around in my mouth since the ink-stained young one had looked at Vess and flinched. Because I'd seen their faces now. I'd seen the tiredness. I'd seen a man recognize a child and say nothing, and I knew what that silence cost him and what it meant, and I knew—if the head clerk's reputation was half true—that every grey robe down here was a thief who'd taken this road and been offered the stylus instead of the jar.

"No," I said. "Not unless they make us."

"They're collaborators," Pell said. Pell likes clean categories. Pell has never had a brother on a shelf.

"So are we," I said. "Every job we ever ran, somebody downstream got hurt and we called it overhead. We're not clean, Pell. We're just unaudited." I looked down the gallery, at the rows, at the little fires in their jars, at my brother's banked orange coal three rows up and one gallery over, and I heard myself say the thing I'd been assembling since I touched the glass. "But here's the difference, and it's the only difference, and it's going to have to be enough: we can still walk out. They can't. So we open the shelves. All of them. Every jar in this hole gets a crack in it before we're done, and if a clerk stands in the way of a jar, that's his choice, same as it was."

Emrys nodded once, and her nod has the weight of other people's oaths.

Then the plan started building itself, the way plans do when five criminals stand in a warehouse of stolen goods with a deadline burning underneath them. The dragon-fire was guttering—we'd felt it in the rock, a tremor in the deep, a countdown with no clockface—and when it went out, everything shelved became permanent. Property. Forever. Title transfers, deeds drawn up, the whole hideous paperwork of ownership made final. Rook said the audit schedule suddenly made sense: the magistrates wanted a clean count before the fire died. Inventory before closure. Everything down here was about to be *reconciled*.

Which meant the audit was our opening. Twice a year the clerks pull vessels for inspection—out of the niches, onto trolleys, into the light of the counting-room. For one window, the souls leave their shelves. And windows, in my trade, are just doors that haven't been introduced to a crowbar yet.

"We hit the audit," Emrys said. "We're the disruption in the count."

"And Corvin?" I asked, and my voice cracked down the middle like cheap glaze, and I hated it and let it happen, because there are moments when composure is just cowardice wearing a nice coat. "Where does he fall in the order?"

Emrys looked at me for a long moment. Then at Vess, who was watching my brother's gallery the way children watch things they understand better than adults do.

"First," she said. "He goes first."

I should have felt relief. Instead I felt the floor tilt, because I've been in this trade long enough to know that when the plan puts your personal stake first, the plan is about to cost somebody something, and usually the somebody is whoever said *first*.

We were still standing there, shoulders against the old dancing stone, dividing the galleries between us, when the lights changed.

All of them. Every gallery, top to bottom, the pale gold steadiness shifted—brightened, then sharpened, then went white as a Warden's glare. And through the junction came footsteps. Not a clerk's shuffle. Measured boots, several pairs, descending from the direction of the counting-room, and ahead of them, carried on the dead air, a voice. Dry. Amused, in the way a fishhook is amused. A voice that had clearly never once raised itself because it had never once needed to.

"—and the discrepancy, I trust, will be resolved before the reconciliation. I dislike discrepancies. They suggest *hands*."

Rook mouthed it before any of us dared: *head clerk.*

Coming down our aisle. Coming toward the shelves. Coming toward the jar with my brother's name on the tag.

Toward us.
