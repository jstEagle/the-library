## 12. The Clerk Who Remembers Everything

I have been caught by Wardens, by a jealous husband, by the Tithe himself, and once — memorably — by my own reflection in a magistrate's mirror that I had not known was enchanted. I know what it is to be caught. Your body goes to stone and your mind goes to arithmetic: exits, assets, odds.

But when the head clerk came down our aisle, none of that happened. Instead, every hair on my arms stood up and turned toward him, the way iron filings turn, or the way you turn in a crowd when someone says your childhood name.

He was not what the word *clerk* had built in my mind. No stooped ink-stained functionary. He walked like a man descending his own staircase in his own house, which, I suppose, he was. His coat was grey the way old paper is grey, and it moved without rustling, as though even the friction of the world had been filed away somewhere. He wore no badge. He didn't need one. The shelves knew him. As he passed, the soul-lights in their jars leaned toward him — just slightly, just perceptibly — the way flowers lean at a window.

Beside me, Sefa made a sound like a swallowed sob. Rook's hand found her elbow. Pell had gone very still in the manner of someone who has decided that stillness is a hiding place.

And Vess — Vess gripped my hand with both of hers and did not look away from the clerk, and I felt through her small palms a tremor that wasn't fear.

"Emrys Nystrom," the head clerk said.

Not a question. A filing.

"You have me at a disadvantage," I said. "You have everyone at a disadvantage. That seems to be the local industry."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It might have been the ancestor of a smile, three or four generations removed.

"I am Clerk-Principal Ossenvar," he said. "First among the Reconcilers. I remember everything." He said it the way another man might say *I am allergic to shellfish* — a fact of constitution, neither boast nor apology. "It is not a gift. You will understand that eventually, if you live long enough to be reconciled. Memory, kept whole, kept *all* of it, is a weight. Most souls are lighter after we shelve them." His eyes went to Sefa's brother's jar with something that was almost tenderness. Almost. Tenderness run through an abacus. "Your friend is grieving at shelf nine hundred and forty-one. She needn't. He is perfectly preserved. Better than he was."

"You keep him," Sefa said, and her voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "Like a jar of pickles."

"I keep him," Ossenvar agreed, without heat. "Which is more than the flood keeps, or the fire, or the years. Grief is just love with nowhere to put itself. We provide somewhere." He turned back to me. "But you didn't come for philosophy. You came for lot seven-seven-three. Bryn Nystrom. Acquired twelve years past. And you've brought—" his gaze dropped to Vess, and here, for the first time, something flickered behind his face, some faint static of interest, "—ah. There she is. The vessel. Returned unsolicited. How unusual. Vessels don't usually come home."

Vess said something then. In the First Tongue. Four syllables, soft, deliberate.

Ossenvar went still. Not startled — clerks, I would learn, do not startle; they *annotate*. Stillness spread over him the way frost spreads over glass, and beneath it I saw the thought move, vast and slow, like something turning over under ice.

"That," he said quietly, "is not supposed to exist outside this court."

"It exists," Vess said, in Ashvale common now, her voice thin but level. "It lives in a girl who wants her soul back."

"And there," said Ossenvar, "is our problem."

* * *

He invited us to his office. Invited — though two of the reformed thieves who had once walked this same road flanked us as we went, walking with the smooth courtesy of men who remembered exactly how much they'd wanted to refuse. I watched them as we passed. Looking for what they used to be. One of them caught me looking and gave me the smallest shake of his head: *don't ask.* His eyes were the eyes of a man reading a ledger he can never finish. I understood then what Ossenvar meant about weight. These clerks remembered everything, and remembering everything had sanded them down to functions.

The office was at the heart of the undercourt, and it was — this was the horror of it, the domesticated horror Sefa would later describe better than I could — *nice*. A good desk. A lamp burning with dragon-fire light, steady and warm. A window that looked out on nothing, painted with a view of a mountain morning that hadn't existed in twelve years. Someone had chosen the paint color thoughtfully. Someone watered a small plant on the sill.

Evil, I thought, watching Ossenvar pour tea from an actual teapot, is mostly furniture.

"You're wondering why I haven't called the guard," he said, handing cups around. Pell took hers like it might be a trap. To be fair, so did I. "Wondering why I'm negotiating with burglars instead of processing them. I'll tell you. Because you are carrying an anomaly, Mistress Nystrom, and anomalies must be resolved before the reconciliation completes. When the last flame gutters out — and it will gutter soon, days perhaps, hours if the magistrates grow frightened — everything shelved here becomes permanent. Fixed. Signed into the hoard forever. And your sister—" he said the name gently, gently, a knife wrapped in velvet, "—your sister is currently a discrepancy."

"Meaning what?" I said. My tea sat untouched. I trusted this room the way I trusted a smiling moneylender.

"Meaning her record does not balance. Twelve years ago, a payment was rendered to this court. A soul, whole and willing-signed, tendered against certain debts owed by certain magistrates whose names you already suspect. But a whole soul cannot simply be *kept*, Nystrom. It must be *housed*. A housed soul needs a vessel — a living body to anchor its continuity while the fire burns. Vessels are found. Prepared. Bound." His eyes rested on Vess with terrible mildness. "She was prepared beautifully. And then, six years ago, she was lost. Stolen out of the city by parties unknown. The court has carried her as a discrepancy ever since — a payment received, but not fully installed. And now she has walked herself back down my aisle." He set down his cup. It made no sound. "Do you see? You haven't brought me a child. You've brought me the missing line in an entry."

The room tilted. I put my hand flat on his desk and the desk, at least, was honest about being solid.

"You're saying," I said slowly, "that Vess was made for this. Made to carry Bryn. By the magistrates."

"Prepared, bound, and sold alongside the soul, yes. A package transaction. The magistrates were thorough people." Ossenvar folded his hands. "They purchased your sister's soul and its housing in a single instrument. Then the housing escaped, and for twelve years the payment has sat on my shelves, whole and unhoused, accruing nothing. I dislike discrepancies, Nystrom. I told your friend that. They suggest hands — and worse, they suggest *unresolved hands*. So. Here is what I offer, because unlike the magistrates, I prefer clean books to full ones."

He let the silence stretch. Somewhere far above us, muffled by a hundred feet of mountain, the dragon-fire breathed, and I swear the lamp on his desk breathed with it.

"Bryn Nystrom's soul may be installed into Vess. Properly, permanently, tonight — before the reconciliation fixes everything in place. The child lives. Your sister lives, housed and anchored, free in the only way freedom survives down here. Both discrepancies resolved in one motion." He smiled, and it was a real smile, which was the worst thing yet. "All it costs is the vessel's other claims. The body comes to the court as property. The girl walks in carrying one soul and walks out belonging to two masters. She would serve here, as my clerks served. She would remember everything." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Including, eventually, you."

* * *

"No."

I said it before I knew I was going to say it, fast and flat, and Ossenvar inclined his head as though I'd answered a survey question.

"May I ask why? It is, by any ledger, a generous trade. One life restored, one life continued. The child suffers nothing but service, and service here is not cruel. Ask my colleagues."

"She's eight years old."

"She was always going to belong to someone, Nystrom. That is what vessels are. The only question ever on offer was *whom*." He spread his grey hands. "The magistrates bought her before she could walk. I am merely offering her back to the purpose she was built for, with better management."

"And I'm merely telling you no." I stood. My chair scraped, and the sound was enormous in that tidy room. "You want me to hand you a child because paperwork prefers it. I've read your paperwork. I've read the seal off a dying magistrate's correspondence — the one with my sister's name in the ledger lines. I know exactly how your books balance, Clerk-Principal. They balance on other people."

For a moment — one moment — the frost cracked. Something looked out of Ossenvar's face that was old and tired and might once, generations ago, have been a person who also walked down these stairs as a thief. Then it closed over again, smooth as filed paper.

"As you wish," he said. "Though you should know what refusing costs you. Sit down, Mistress Nystrom. This is the part you actually came for, even if you don't know it yet. This is the part I remember *everything* about."

I didn't sit. Neither did Sefa, or Pell, or Rook, who had drifted between me and the door in the old formation, the one we hadn't stood in since before the ban. Ossenvar regarded our little mutiny with something like scholarly appreciation.

"The sale of Bryn Nystrom's soul," he said, "was executed under court law, which predates your magistrates' ban and will outlive it. Court law requires, for any acquisition of a soul, a signature of kinship — blood-kin, willing, witnessed. It is the oldest rule we have, older than the shelves. The dead may not be taken. They may only be *given*, by their own." He opened a drawer. Drew out a folio. Grey, like his coat. Old paper. "Twelve years ago, such a signature was rendered for lot seven-seven-three. Witnessed. Sealed. Filed."

He laid the folio on the desk and turned it toward me, and opened it, and turned the pages until he reached the one, and I looked down at it, and the world went white at the edges and very, very quiet.

There was my handwriting.

Not *like* my handwriting. *My* handwriting — the slanted capitals, the way I cross my sevens, the little hook I put on an R because Bryn teased me about it when we were children learning our letters together, because she said my R's looked like they were running away. *Emrys Nystrom*, signed in brown-black ink gone amber with age, at the bottom of a contract whose language I recognized even where I couldn't parse it — court-law formal, older than kingdoms, the grammar of ledgers and graves.

Willing. Witnessed. Mine.

"I didn't sign this," I heard myself say. My voice came from very far away, from the top of a well. "I have never seen this document. I didn't know this court existed until three days ago. Whatever forgery—"

"We know it's a forgery," Ossenvar said, not unkindly, which somehow made it infinitely worse. "Of course we do. Court law requires a *willing* signature, Nystrom, and yours was not willing — you were not even present. The magistrates' agents copied your hand from letters, from market receipts, from anything bearing your script, and they were very good. Good enough to satisfy the seal. Good enough to open the shelf. But a forged signature is a flaw in the metal, and flaws propagate." He tapped the amber ink with one grey finger. "This flaw is why your sister sits on a shelf instead of standing in a body. It is why the discrepancy has never fully closed. A false giving holds, but it does not *bind*. And now — hear me, thief, because this is the knowledge you bled for, climbing down through flooded shafts, losing your happiest memory to the Tithe to earn the map that led you here — now you stand in the one room in the world where the flaw can be named."

I couldn't speak. Sefa's hand found my shoulder and stayed there, gripping hard enough to bruise, which helped, because pain is honest.

"Only the signer can unmake the signing," Ossenvar said. "Or — since the signer never truly signed — only true kin-blood, acting willingly, in this chamber, under this seal. A release countersigned by the same hand that gave. Yours, Nystrom. Truly yours this time. And a willing signature, as I said, accepts whatever debt follows. Court debts are not gentle things. They are priced in what the signer values most." His eyes moved to Vess, standing pale and stubborn beside me, and then — deliberately, cruelly, precisely — to my own chest, as though he could see through my ribs to the shelves inside me, the stolen memories, the hollows, the hole where my happiest hour used to be. "We will discuss pricing when you decide."

"Decide," I repeated.

"Everything is a decision. Even refusal." He closed the folio gently, reverently, a priest with his relic. "You came down here to steal your sister back. I am offering you the truth instead, which is rarer and worth more: that you cannot steal her. You can only *release* her, and only by signing your own name to your own worst act — claiming it, authorizing it, owning the forgery as if it were your will, because court law does not distinguish between a hand and its copy. To undo the sale, you must become its author."

The lamp breathed. Above us, the mountain breathed. Somewhere in the flooded dark, the last dragon-fire counted down toward nothing, and when it finished counting, everything on those shelves became theirs forever — and my signature, my false-willing, mine-in-every-way-that-mattered signature, would be the lock that held.

Twelve years. Twelve years I had grieved a fire, hated a night, mourned an accident — and all that time the accident had my name at the bottom of it. I had spent my grief like coin, and the coin had been counterfeit, and the debt had been real.

"Get out," I said to Ossenvar, very quietly. "Get out of my way, or I will burn your tidy office and your tidy books and your tidy plant, and I will do it smiling."

"There she is," Ossenvar murmured, almost warmly, gathering his folio. "The willing hand. I wondered when she'd arrive." At the door he paused, grey and patient as dust. "You should hurry, whatever you choose. The reconciliation moves at the pace of the flame, and the flame has begun to cough. And be advised—" the ancestor-of-a-smile again, "—the magistrates have noticed their missing vessel. They sealed the gates for a reason. What comes down those stairs next will not be clerks."

He left us alone with the tea and the painted window and the terrible arithmetic of my own name.

Sefa spoke first, hoarse: "Em. Whatever you're thinking. Think it fast."

Because down the corridor, faint but unmistakable, came the sound I'd been listening for all my life and dreading in equal measure: boots. Many. Warden-issue. Descending.

With them, thinner, threading through the stone like wire — a sound that stopped my heart and restarted it sideways.

Corvane's voice, calling my name.
