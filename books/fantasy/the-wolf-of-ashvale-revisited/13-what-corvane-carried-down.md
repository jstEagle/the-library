## 13. What Corvane Carried Down

I had rehearsed the speech for eleven years without ever saying it aloud.

That's the trouble with secrets you keep for love instead of fear—they don't rot in the dark like other secrets do. They calcify. They become part of your skeleton, and then one day you realize you've been walking around all this time with a confession where a spine ought to be.

Mine went something like this: *Emrys, I am not the man you think I'm lying to.*

* * *

Let me back up. Corvane Vess—no relation to the child, though I would come to think of it as an omen, because the world has a sense of humor and it is a cruel one—joined the Wardens at nineteen for the stupidest reason on record: he believed in them.

Not the way recruits are supposed to believe, with flag-salutes and oath-verse. I mean I believed the ban was mercy. I'd seen what unlicensed magic did to my mother's village when I was small—a hedge-witch's working gone sideways, three houses folded into a shape geometry refuses to describe. So when the magistrates said *magic is outlawed for your protection*, I took it literally, the way children take things, which is to say completely and without checking the fine print.

Twelve years later I was a captain, and I had learned to read fine print.

The night Emrys came to me—wet from the rain, a child's hand in hers, asking me to open a gate that couldn't be opened—I did what she expected. I said yes too easily. I lied about how easily. And while she watched my face for the tell she never found, I palmed one of her charms off her stall table, because I already knew what was in it, and I needed proof she hadn't changed the recipe.

She saw me do it. Of course she did; the woman could spot a lifted coin across a crowded market. She just misread it, the way everyone always had. She thought I was collecting leverage. Insurance against the day she became more useful arrested than free.

The truth was worse, or better, depending on how you price cowardice.

I was collecting evidence.

* * *

Here is what nobody in Ashvale understood about the Wardens' confiscation vaults, mostly because nobody was ever meant to understand it: confiscated memories didn't get destroyed. Destruction would have been wasteful. They got *filed*.

I found out in my third year, doing inventory audit—the least glamorous duty in the corps, assigned to me because I was honest enough not to steal and ambitious enough not to complain. Shelf after shelf after shelf of little glass vials, each tagged with a name and a date and a crime. A baker who'd charmed his bread to stay warm. A midwife who'd sung a fever down. A boy of nine who'd taught a sparrow to say his brother's name.

Nine years old. Filed between a smuggler and a fraudster, alphabetized.

I stood in that cold room for a long time holding a vial that weighed almost nothing, thinking about the word *confiscate*. It sounds so clean. Like tidying. What it actually means is that somewhere in this city there is a person walking around with a hole in their chest shaped like a sparrow, and they will spend their whole life feeling the draft through it without ever learning why.

So I started returning them.

Quietly. One or two at a time, logged as "degraded—unrecoverable," slipped into the dead-letter post, left on doorsteps wrapped in brown paper like nothing more dangerous than bread. It took me nine years to move four hundred memories, and I want to be clear that I am not telling you this to be admired. Four hundred out of forty thousand is not heroism. It's a man bailing the ocean with a thimble and calling himself a sailor because his feet are wet.

But here's the thing about bailing with a thimble: eventually you learn where the leaks come from. Every memory I returned had a paper trail behind it—an intake signature, an authorization seal, a magistrate's mark. And the seals weren't random. Certain names appeared again and again, attached to certain kinds of memory. Memories of the mountain. Memories of the old courts. Memories, in eleven cases, of a woman named Bryn Nystrom.

I didn't know who Bryn Nystrom was. But I knew Emrys Lockridge kept a grief she refused to name, worn smooth as river stone from twelve years of handling, and I knew that when I once asked—gently, over wine, at the stage of a relationship where asking such questions is either brave or fatal—she had looked at me with such total, ancient exhaustion that I never asked again.

I should have asked again. That's the version of me I'd apologize to, if apology traveled backward.

* * *

The morning I sealed the gates, I told myself it was containment. The child was anomalous; the magistrates were frightened; frightened magistrates with hoarded magic are the most dangerous animal in any kingdom. If I closed Ashvale, I could control where the hunt happened. I could steer it away from the undercity, buy the thieves time, funnel the search into corridors I patrolled personally.

And privately—God help me—I told Emrys to run alone, and I meant it as tenderness. *You can't save anyone by walking into the mountain,* I said. *Save yourself. Save the girl.* I thought I was offering her the only mercy left.

Vess called me a liar in the First Tongue, and I didn't understand the words, but I understood the tone perfectly, because children have been calling men liars in every language since language began, and they're right more often than the men are.

Because here's what I actually knew, standing in that doorway with the gates sealed behind me: I knew that the magistrates had moved up the schedule. I knew that the dragon-fire beneath the mountain was scheduled to gutter within days, and that when it died, every soul in the court below would transfer permanently into magistrate custody—forty thousand souls, plus interest, plus everything the fire itself still held. I knew it because I had spent a decade reading the fine print, and the fine print, once you finally assemble all of it, stops being fine print and becomes a confession written in your own city's blood.

I also knew that Emrys would go into the mountain anyway. I'd known it since the night she arrived with the child. You cannot look at a woman carrying a debt like hers—twelve years of unnamed grief, compounding—and believe anything you say will stop her. You can only decide whether to stand aside, stand with her, or stand in front of her with a warrant.

I chose the fourth thing. The thing no one chooses on purpose. I chose to follow her down and find out, at last, whether I was her lover or her executioner, and to make sure that whichever I was, I'd be it honestly.

* * *

They let me bring thirty Wardens. That was the trap I built for myself, wearing the face of authority: Captain Corvane requests a descent detail, anomalous activity in the deep shafts, full authorization. My superior, Magistrate Halloway—dry as ledger dust, with a voice like a drawer closing—signed the order himself, which should have told me everything. Powerful men don't sign orders for descents they haven't already decided the outcome of.

We came down through the flooded galleries, lanterns hissing where the water touched them, and I heard the riot before I understood it—a sound like a library catching fire, ten thousand voices remembering themselves at once. The shelved souls were rising. Emrys had done what Emrys does, which is steal exactly the thing that breaks the room open.

I gave the order I'd been composing for eleven years.

"Stand down," I told my Wardens. "All of you. Weapons on the ground."

Thirty helmets turned toward me. Somewhere behind us, Halloway's honor guard—six men I'd assumed were escort—shifted in a way escorts shouldn't shift.

"Captain," said Sergeant Ilves, young enough to still believe in the oath-verse, "those are direct orders from—"

"I know whose orders those are." I pulled the satchel off my shoulder. "And I'm countermanding them. Not mutiny. Correction."

I walked forward through the chaos—clerks scattering, shelved souls streaming upward like reversed rain, Sefa standing over her brother's empty cradle with a wrench in her fist and tears cutting clean lines through the grime—and I found Emrys at the center of it, at Bryn's shelf, a document in her hands and a look on her face I will carry wherever dead captains carry things.

I held out the satchel.

"Eleven years ago I started stealing from the vault," I said. "Not memories. Paperwork. Intake signatures. Authorization seals. Transfer schedules. Eleven cases referencing a sale dated twelve years past, seller listed as the Office of Internal Accord, buyer listed as the Court Beneath, commodity—" my voice did something I didn't authorize, "—commodity, one soul, partial, vessel-borne. Your sister wasn't lost in a fire, Emrys. She was *inventoried*. And the fire was the receipt."

Her face. I have stolen glances at beautiful things my whole life—sunrise over the ash-fields, the first snow on the mountain—but I have never seen anything like the way her expression came apart and reassembled itself around a new architecture of rage.

"You knew," she said.

"I suspected for nine years and knew for two. I was gathering enough to bring them down properly. Through channels. Legally." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Yes. I know. I know exactly how that sounds coming from me, the lawman, planning to fight paperwork with paperwork while a child carried your sister's soul around like a locket. I told myself patience was rigor. Mostly I think I was just afraid that if I showed you what I had, you'd ask why it took me so long, and the answer is that I loved being the man you trusted more than I wanted to be the man who deserved it."

Around us the riot roared. Behind me, boots on stone—not running toward the fight. Running toward us.

"So arrest me or forgive me," I said, "but take the satchel. Names, dates, seals, the whole ledger. It ends them, Emrys. In daylight, before the whole city, it ends them."

She reached for it. Our fingers touched on the strap, and for one moment—one entire, complete moment—I was the man I'd spent eleven years failing to be.

Then Halloway's guard captain put a blade through my chest from behind, and the moment ended the way moments do, which is to say permanently.

* * *

I want to report, since I apparently have time, that it doesn't hurt the way stories claim. There's a sound like a door. There's the strangeness of looking down and finding your own coat suddenly very interesting. There is Emrys screaming my name in a voice I'd only heard her use once before, twelve years ago, over a grave I now understood I'd been standing beside our entire relationship.

There is the floor, arriving with great conviction.

Halloway stepped over me. I remember that—he actually *stepped over me*, adjusting his cuff, the way you'd step over a dropped glove. "Confiscate the satchel," he said, bored. "Burn it. Burn him with it. Log the captain as killed in the line of duty; it's practically true."

Practically true. The fine print of my whole life, summarized by a man who smelled of ink and rot.

Ilves didn't obey. I saw it from the floor, which is a better view of people than most people ever get: the boy sergeant looking at his captain bleeding out among the shelved souls, looking at the clerks cowering, looking at the satchel in Halloway's guard's hands—and making, in about two seconds, the choice I'd taken eleven years to make. He picked up his weapon. He pointed it at the magistrate.

Others followed. Not all. Enough.

I mention this because I need someone to know it wasn't for nothing. The satchel passed hands like a coal in a bucket line—guard to sergeant, sergeant to a shelved soul who remembered being a courier, courier upward into the smoke and the riot and the rising dark. Halloway's men got some of it. The fire got some. But a copy—there's always a copy; I was an auditor, we live by copies—went up and out, into the current of freed souls flooding toward the surface, toward daylight, toward a city whose wards were already beginning to shiver.

Emrys knelt over me. Her hands pressed the wound the way hands press wounds, which is to say uselessly, lovingly.

"You idiot," she said. "You absolute—eleven years. Eleven years and you tell me *now*, on the *floor*—"

"Had to be sure," I managed. Blood makes wit difficult; consider this the polished draft. "Sure it was you. Sure it was worth—"

"Don't." Her thumb smeared blood along my jaw, marking me like something claimed. "Don't you dare finish that sentence with something noble."

"Worth," I insisted. I have always been stubborn.

Above us the mountain groaned. Water was coming—I could hear it in the stone, a far-off throat-clearing, the magistrates opening the flood-gates to drown rebellion and dragon-fire together. Rook was shouting about high ground. Pell was hauling clerks out of the water's path, because even in a riot, apparently, some people rescue the people who filed them away.

Emrys looked toward the flood, then down at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic no one should ever have to do: the dying man, the drowning court, the child walking toward the flame.

"Go," I told her. "Go. I'm finished either way, and I'd rather be finished knowing you were moving."

"I don't leave people."

"No," I agreed. "You really don't. That's the whole problem with you. It's also the whole point." I got a hand up—it took most of what I had left—and pressed the last thing into her palm, the thing I'd saved out of the satchel, the one document I'd kept back because I hadn't known until tonight who it belonged to. "Bryn's original intake tag. Signed. Witnessed. It's the root of the whole ledger—unmake the signature, you unmake the sale. Kin can unmake a signature. I checked. I checked *years* ago, Emrys, I just never had the courage to wonder why I was checking."

She stared at it. At me. The water was louder now, and the light from below was changing—that deep amber of dragon-fire breathing its last—and somewhere a small voice was speaking the First Tongue directly into the flame.

"I loved you," she said. Present tense. Stubborn, like me.

"I know," I said. "That was the best secret I ever kept. Both directions."

The water came. The fire flared. I felt myself going the way confiscated memories go—out of the filing system, into whatever room has no shelves.

Last thing I saw: Emrys on her feet, tag in her fist, turning toward the light with her jaw set like a woman signing something.

Good. That's the word I'd choose, if words still came to me.

*Signed.*
