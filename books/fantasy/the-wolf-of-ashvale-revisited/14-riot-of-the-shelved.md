## 14. Riot of the Shelved

I want it on record that I said this was a bad idea.

Not out loud—out loud I said "fine" and "sure" and "Emrys, you're the boss," which is what you say when a woman who once picked a magistrate's memory clean through three locked doors tells you she has a plan involving an entire underground court full of angry dead people. But I want it on record somewhere, even if only here, in the privacy of my own skull, which is the one place in Ashvale nobody has managed to rob yet.

The plan was this: Emrys had spent twenty years selling stolen memories as luck-charms, and she still had maybe forty of them left in her coat—the ones too strange or too heavy to move at market. A baker's last morning before his bakery burned. A mother singing to a child who wouldn't remember it. Forty little glass vials of other people's lives, rattling around like a drunk's pockets.

"You're going to throw memories at them," Rook had said in the flooded shaft, water to our knees, dragon-light dying overhead like a candle in a draft.

"I'm going to *give* memories to them," Emrys said. "There's a difference. Ask any fence."

"There's a difference," Sefa agreed grimly, "and it's usually the difference between theft and arson."

Which is how we got here.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain is not what the songs say. The songs say throne room, cathedral, hall of judgment. What it actually is, is a warehouse. Rows and rows of shelves going back farther than lamplight reaches, and on every shelf, little vessels of light—souls, shelved like jars of preserved plums, each with a clerk's tag tied around it in neat bureaucratic script. *Acquired under Statute Nine. Payment rendered. Property of the Magistracy of Ashvale.* Somewhere in all that paperwork was my grandmother, and Sefa's brother, and probably half the people we'd ever cheated, because it turns out the magistrates didn't hate thieves so much as resent the competition.

And the clerks. That's the part I can't stop thinking about, even now, even with everything that came after. The clerks weren't monsters. They were us. Old crew faces gone soft and gray, thieves who'd come down this same road years ago and taken the offered desk instead of the offered shelf. One of them looked at Sefa across the ledgers and I watched him decide, in real time, whether he remembered her or whether remembering would be inconvenient. He chose inconvenient. I broke his nose for it later, which solved nothing but felt extremely correct at the time.

We'd been cornered when it started. That's the honest version. The head clerk—a tall, dry creature called Master Vane, with the voice of a man reading his own eulogy—had us boxed against the ledger-hall doors, Warden crossbows upstairs at every gallery, Corvane bleeding out somewhere behind me where I refused to look because looking would mean admitting it, and Vess clutching Emrys's hand with that locket burning like a small furious star.

And Emrys reached into her coat and pulled out a charm.

"Master Vane," she said pleasantly, "you're standing in a warehouse full of inventory that remembers being people. Would you like to know what happens when they start remembering?"

He opened his mouth to say something with *Statute* in it. She threw the vial.

* * *

Here is what I know about memories, having handled more stolen ones than most priests have handled sins: they are not gentle. A memory doesn't drift into you like smoke. It arrives like a door kicked open. It arrives saying *this happened to you* in a voice you cannot argue with, and your body believes it before your mind can object, because bodies were there first.

The first vial shattered on the flagstones and a memory went up through the floor like frost through a windowpane.

A baker's last morning. It hit the nearest shelf and the soul inside flared—not the polite warehouse glow it had been wearing, but something white and personal—and then it screamed. Not in pain. In recognition. You could hear the name in it, a name it hadn't been allowed to say in however many years the magistrates had kept it pickled in the dark, and the sound of it went down the rows like fire down a fuse.

Then Emrys started throwing the rest.

She threw them two-handed, like a woman feeding birds, except the birds were the dead and the bread was their own lives. A lullaby hit row twelve and thirty souls began singing it, off-key and overlapping, in voices that hadn't agreed on anything in decades. A wedding-day hit the far wall and somewhere back in the dark a soul started laughing, huge and helpless, the laugh of someone remembering being loved. An old man's fury at a landlord's eviction notice caught a whole shelf-block and turned it into a mob.

The clerks did what clerks do. They ran for the ledgers. I will never forgive them for how funny that was—crossbows forgotten, Vane shrieking about proper procedure while his staff scrambled to file an uprising, as if rebellion were just another entry requiring a form in triplicate. One of them actually inked a quill. Rook put an arrow through the ledger stand beside his head—not to kill, Rook never kills if a message will do—and the message was legible enough.

The shelves started coming apart.

You have to understand: these souls had been *inventory*. Tidy, silent, glowing on schedule. And now they remembered hands. Remembered streets. Remembered grudges. They rose out of their vessels like smoke deciding to be people again, ragged-edged and flickering, and the first thing a person does with a body made mostly of grievance is find the nearest representative of the institution that shelved them.

Vane discovered what that felt like from the inside. I'll give him this—he ran faster than a man in clerk's robes should be able to run.

"Row nine!" Sefa shouted over the din. She had her brother's shelf-tag clutched in her fist, torn off its vessel, and her face was doing something complicated that I pretended not to see, because some things you don't watch even in a riot. "Bryn Nystrom, row nine, end case!"

"Row nine's behind the gallery!" Rook called back. "Where the crossbows are!"

"Naturally," I said. "Naturally it's behind the crossbows. Why would my sister's soul be anywhere convenient."

That's the thing about being a twin. People assume you feel what the other one feels. It's not true—it's worse. You feel what they *would* say. Every stupid situation, some corner of my brain supplies Pell, if Bryn were here she'd make that exact joke, and then I remember I'm the one making it alone, and have been for twelve years, and the joke dies in my mouth and comes out as movement instead. So I moved.

* * *

Fighting through a riot of the resurrected dead is, I want to stress, mostly a matter of manners.

They didn't want us. We were small fry, living and breathing, barely worth a second look when there were clerks to tip off balconies and ledgers to burn. The trick was not getting caught in the undertow. Souls move like weather fronts—one minute the aisle is clear, the next there's a wall of remembered grief rolling through it, and if you're standing where a woman died defending her shop you feel exactly what that was like, secondhand and total, and you come out the far side of it crying without knowing whose tears they are.

I cried four people's tears crossing that floor. Maybe five. There was one I'm fairly sure belonged to a dog.

Sefa went ahead of me with her lockwitch's hands doing things to Warden door-locks that would've gotten her hanged twice over on the surface, muttering her brother's name like a prayer with a criminal record. Rook covered the rear with arrows and commentary—"left! no, your other left, Pell, honestly"—and Emrys cut through the middle with Vess on her hip, throwing the last few charms like a woman sowing a field with lightning.

And the crossbows. Right. The crossbows.

Wardens on the gallery, young ones, scared ones, ordered to hold position against a scene out of every nightmare their training manuals politely declined to describe. First volley went wide, mostly, because it is very hard to aim when the floor is full of the weeping dead. Second volley found Rook's shoulder and my thigh, shallow, ugly, ignorable until tomorrow. Third volley never came, because that was when the souls reached the gallery stairs and the Wardens discovered that you cannot shoot a memory, and that a man who spent thirty years shelved in the dark has *opinions* about uniforms.

I took a crossbow bolt meant for Emrys. Not heroically—I tripped. Sometimes the difference between tragedy and comedy is footing. I went down under a tangle of limbs and rising light, and a soul leaned over me, an old woman by the shape of her, and for a moment I thought: well, this is it, trampled by the afterlife, tell Rook he was the handsome one.

Instead she pressed one cold finger to my forehead, and gave me a memory.

Her name was Maret. She'd grown apples. She wanted someone to know she'd grown apples—that the magistrates' ledger had reduced sixty years of orchards to *Asset 4117, acquired under Statute Nine*, and that somewhere above us there might still be trees, and somebody should remember they were hers.

"I'll tell them," I gasped, and she smiled with a face that wasn't quite a face anymore, and dissolved upward into the smoke, and I got up with apples in my chest where fear used to be, and kept moving.

That's the exhilaration part, if you're wondering. That's the beat everyone asks about afterward, the ones who weren't there. Yes, it was terrifying. Yes, it was chaos, real chaos, the kind the law exists supposedly to prevent and actually to monopolize. But it was also the first time in my life I saw justice happen outside of a courtroom, unmediated, unpapered, unpaid—and it looked exactly like what the magistrates always said it would look like. A riot. Fire and screaming and overturned furniture and old women with grievances.

They were right about what it looked like. They were wrong about which side of it they wanted to be standing on.

* * *

Row nine.

The end case stood apart from the others, newer tags on older vessels, and there—third shelf, tagged in the same tidy bureaucratic hand as everything else, *Asset 88, Nystrom, B., payment rendered, property of the Magistracy*—was a soul the size of a broken promise.

It was smaller than the others. That's what stopped me. All around it the risen dead blazed and stormed, whole lives burning at full width, and Bryn's soul sat in its jar like a coin at the bottom of a well, dim, rationed, a fraction of a person. Whatever they'd sold off her, whatever piece they'd kept as change, they'd left her this: a sliver with a sister's laugh folded up inside it somewhere, waiting twelve years for someone to come open the drawer.

Sefa reached it first and had the vessel's ward-lock smoking under her fingers before the rest of us cleared the aisle.

And that's when the head clerk's voice came down the ruined hall, thin and precise and utterly unhurried, the way only truly wicked paperwork can be:

"That asset carries a lien."

We all turned. Vane stood atop the ledger-hall steps, robes scorched, composure intact, surrounded by clerks who had decided that dying at their posts was preferable to being seen running again. Behind him, the great seal of the Magistracy glowed on the wall like a bruise.

"Release requires countersignature," he said. "Blood-kin. Witnessed. Filed." He produced a document from his sleeve—I swear on every god I don't believe in, from his *sleeve*, pre-filed, prepared, as if he'd been waiting twelve years for a Nystrom to walk down those stairs. "Miss Nystrom. Your sister's account has been expecting you."

Emrys stepped forward. Vess's hand in hers. The locket blazing. And I saw my twin's oldest friend read the terms on that page, and I watched her face do the thing faces do when the price finally shows itself—

But that's her chapter to tell, not mine. I only carried the lantern.

What I'll say is this: the riot was still roaring behind us, the dead were still tearing down twelve years of shelving plank by plank, the dragon-fire overhead guttered like a held breath—and in all that thunder, the loudest thing in the mountain was the sound of a quill being uncapped.
