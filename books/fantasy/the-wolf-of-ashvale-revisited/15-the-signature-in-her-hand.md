## 15. The Signature in Her Hand

The ledger was exactly where the head clerk had said it would be, which told me everything I needed to know about how frightened they were.

Frightened people organize. They file things. They put their sins in alphabetical order and lock them behind brass fittings, because a sin you can locate is a sin you can pretend to manage. The great bound volume sat open on the clerk's desk at the end of Row Nine, its pages shivering in the updraft from the dragon-fire far below, and it did not occur to anyone guarding it that a thief might simply *pick it up*, because to the clerks of the undercourt, the ledger wasn't an object. It was scripture. You don't hide scripture. You enshrine it.

I picked it up.

Behind me, Row Seven was burning—not with fire, but with remembering. Pell's handiwork: three vials of stolen memory shattered against the shelving, and now hundreds of soul-fragments were waking mid-scream, mid-laughter, mid-wedding-vow, and the clerks were losing a war they had never once imagined fighting. A woman's soul tore free of its shelf and hovered there, blazing, wearing the shape of a girl who had drowned forty years ago, and a clerk—old, stooped, patient as mildew—raised his stylus to write her back into inventory and found his hands shaking too hard to form the characters.

Good, I thought. Let them shake.

"Emrys!" Sefa's voice, raw from shouting, from somewhere near the flooded stairwell. "We have minutes, not hours! The magistrates know—the water's coming!"

"I know!" I shouted back, and turned to the ledger, and found my sister's name.

*Bryn Nystrom.* Shelf Nine, Tier Four, Lot 311. Not even her full name—the clerks had shortened it, the way you shorten anything you intend to own. Beside the name, in ink so old it had gone the color of dried blood, was the transaction record, and beneath the transaction record was the signature.

My signature.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I had left at that moment. It was my handwriting. The loop of the E too generous, the tail of the y flicking left like a struck match—I'd written that way since I was nine years old, when Bryn teased me that my letters looked like they were trying to escape the word. Twelve years ago, someone had sat down with a sample of my hand and practiced until they could forge not just the shapes but the *hesitation*. There was a tremor in the signature. A small, human wobble in the final stroke, the kind your hand makes when it's writing something it doesn't want to write.

They hadn't just forged me. They'd forged me *reluctant*.

"They made you sign for it," said a voice beside me, and I nearly put a knife in it before I recognized Vess—or rather, recognized the locket at her throat, glowing like a coal wrapped in silk. She stood close enough that her shoulder touched my arm, which was more trust than any child should extend in a riot. Her eyes were on the signature, and her face wore an expression no child's face should know either: recognition without understanding. "Why does it look sad?"

"Because it's mine," I said. "Or it was."

The head clerk had explained it while the corridors shook and Pell's memories ate through the shelves like acid through cloth. He had been almost gentle about it, the way bureaucrats are gentle when the cruelty is procedural and therefore not their fault. A soul-sale requires two signatures, he said. The seller's and the kin's. Consent and corroboration. The magistrates had provided their own consent freely—of course they had; they were buying—and for the corroboration they had needed blood-kin, and Bryn's only blood-kin was a fifteen-year-old girl who had just watched her sister burn and was in no condition to sign anything, which was precisely what made her *perfect* condition.

So they had forged me. And here was the part he delivered with genuine professional admiration, the way a locksmith praises a good lock: the forgery held the sale together, but it could not unmake it. A false signature can create a debt but never discharge one. To release Bryn's soul—to tear lot 311 out of the ledger and let it go home—required a true hand. Kin-blood. Kin-ink. A real signature, given willingly, accepting whatever obligation the release incurred.

"And what obligation," I had asked him, "does it incur?"

He had smiled with the serenity of a man reading terms he'd never expected anyone to reach. "That," he said, "is between you and the fire."

Now I stood over the ledger with a stylus in my hand—a clerk's stylus, bone-handled, worn smooth by decades of other people's ownership—and I understood the trap in its final shape.

It was elegant. I'll give them that. They always are, the people who build cages out of paperwork. If I refused to sign, Bryn stayed shelved forever, and when the dragon-fire guttered out—as the whole mountain seemed to know it soon would—she became permanent. Not sold. *Owned.* The difference matters. Sold property can theoretically change hands. Owned property is a fact of the world, like gravity. And if I signed—

If I signed, I didn't know what I'd owe. That was the whole design. Twelve years ago they had taken my sister and forged my consent because grief made me pliable. Tonight they wanted my actual consent, freely given, because grief had finally made me *brave*, and brave people will agree to almost anything if the alternative is standing still.

"Emrys." Sefa again, closer now, breathless, one arm around Rook, who was bleeding from somewhere above his ear and grinning about it. Behind them the corridor churned—shelved souls rising like sparks off a bonfire, clerks fleeing or worse, joining, because some of those clerks had been thieves once too and apparently remembered it. "Whatever you're going to do, do it. The water's coming down Shaft Three. I can hear it."

"I'm doing it," I said. "I'm reading the fine print."

"There *is* no fine print," Rook said. "I looked. It's just her name and ours and a line for the signature."

"That's what worries me."

But my hand was already moving.

Here is the thing I learned about myself in that moment, standing in a dying court beneath a dying mountain while everything I'd built in twelve careful years burned overhead: I have spent my whole life stealing other people's memories because I couldn't bear to keep my own. Every charm in my stall was somebody else's best day, sold to strangers hungry for a happiness they could pretend was theirs. And underneath all of it, underneath the trade and the aliases and the careful gray life, was a signature I never wrote, committing a crime I never chose, and I had built every wall I ever built to avoid looking at that page.

You can't steal your way out of your own name.

The stylus touched the paper. The ledger—scripture, remember—seemed to lean toward the touch, pages rippling like something breathing.

"What happens," Vess asked quietly, "to whoever signs?"

"I don't know," I said. "That's the price. You pay first and find out after. It's how they've always done business." I looked at her—at the small set of her jaw, so much like another jaw I could barely remember wanting to remember—and said, "Turn around."

She didn't. Of course she didn't. Children obey the people who lie to them gently; she'd already figured out I wasn't going to be one of those. "Is this where you make her whole?"

"This is where I admit it was me," I said. "All along. Even when it wasn't me, it was me. That's what a name is, Vess. It's the thing they can spend without asking. So—" I breathed out, slow, the way Bryn taught me to breathe before picking a lock, back when we were young enough that our crimes were sugar and our getaway route was the kitchen window. "—you claim it. You pick it up. You carry it. And then you put it down somewhere better."

Sefa put her hand on my shoulder. She didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a eulogy or a lie, and she knew both would be wasted on me.

I signed.

Not quickly—that mattered to me, though I couldn't have told you why. I wrote each letter the way I'd written them at nine years old, generous loops and escaping tails, and at the end I let my hand shake, honestly this time, and let the wobble stand in the record. Let them read it in whatever office survives what's coming. Let some future clerk find that trembling stroke and understand that the kin of lot 311 signed wide awake, furious, and afraid, and signed anyway.

*Emrys Nystrom,* said the ledger, in my own true hand.

And the ledger answered.

I felt it go through me like cold water finding every low place. Something reached into the page I'd just signed and pulled a thread, and the thread ran through the ink, through my hand, through my ribs, and anchored itself somewhere below—in the fire, maybe, or in whatever the fire served—and it began, very calmly, to *count* me. Not my coin. Me. My years, my thefts, my loves, my losses, weighing each one against some scale whose other pan I couldn't see.

"Oh," I said. My voice came out very small in all that noise. "Oh, that's what it costs."

"Emrys?" Sefa's grip tightened. "What did it take?"

"Nothing yet." I blinked, checked myself the way you check your pockets in a crowd—memories, quick inventory, childhood, mother's kitchen, Bryn, Bryn, *Bryn*—and everything was still there, all of it, every unbearable piece. "It's holding the debt. Not collecting. Yet."

"Then we move," Sefa said, "before it changes its mind."

But I stood one moment longer, because lot 311 deserved one moment. Shelf Nine, Tier Four: the niche where Bryn's soul sat was glowing now, brighter than the others, brighter than it had any right to be, and inside the glow something shifted—turned—like a sleeper hearing a familiar voice through a wall. The locket at Vess's throat flared in answer, fragment calling to fragment across twelve years of paperwork, and the sound they made together wasn't sound at all but something older, a note in the First Tongue that has no translation because it predates the need for one.

Vess translated it anyway. Children always do.

"She says hurry," Vess whispered. Her eyes were enormous, wet, lit gold from within. "She says—she says she knows your handwriting."

I laughed. It came out cracked down the middle, and I let it.

"Tell her," I said, closing the ledger, taking Vess's cold little hand in mine, turning toward the stairs and the rising water and whatever waited at the bottom of the debt, "that she should. Because I've got one more thing to sign before tonight's over, and I want her to watch me write it."

We ran. Behind us, deep beneath the shelves, the dragon-fire gave a long shudder—like a beast rolling over in its sleep—and far above, muffled through a hundred feet of mountain, I heard the first iron gate of the flood-channels begin to grind open.

The water was coming. And something in the fire was waiting to see what I'd pay.
