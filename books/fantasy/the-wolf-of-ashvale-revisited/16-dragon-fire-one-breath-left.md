## 16. Dragon-Fire, One Breath Left

The first thing you should know about me is that I am very good at doing what I'm told.

I know that doesn't sound impressive. Grown-ups say it about children the way they say a dog is well-behaved—like it's a virtue of the dog and not the leash. But in Ashvale, in the night market, in the ten years since the ban, being obedient was the whole trick of staying alive. Don't speak the old words. Don't look at Wardens. Don't say what you hear when the locket hums against your chest. I had been told all these things by Emrys, and Sefa, and the twins, and even Osk before they took him away, and I had done every single one of them, because that is what you do when you are small and the world is large and made mostly of people who can hurt you.

So understand: when I walked toward the fire instead of away from it, it was the first time in my entire life I disobeyed anyone on purpose.

And I'd like it noted that I did it beautifully.

* * *

It started, as most terrible things do, with water.

We were still in the shelf-halls—the long rows where the souls hung in their glass cases like jars of honey in a merchant's cellar, except each jar was somebody, which is the sort of detail you'd think would bother the clerks more than it clearly did. The rebellion was everywhere by then. Rook had opened the cases, or Pell had, or the souls had learned to open themselves once someone showed them remembering was allowed; after that it got hard to tell who was freeing whom. The air was full of light and screaming and the smell of old paper burning, because apparently even a riot runs on paperwork.

I was holding onto Sefa's hand. That was my job. Sefa had assigned it to me in the flooded mine-shafts, back when the water only came up to our knees, and she had said, "Vess, your one task, the only thing I need from you, is to not let go." Sefa has hands like a lockpick—small, quick, always slightly cold—and she grips the way she picks things: absolutely, and without any intention of ever stopping.

Then the mountain shuddered, and somewhere far above us, doors I could not see began to open.

You have to picture the sound. Not rushing water—that comes later, that comes like an apology after the crime has already been committed. First there was a groan, deep under everything, the sound the mountain makes when men who own it decide to use it as a weapon instead of a home. Then a hiss, far off, getting closer, like a thousand kettles deciding to be rude at once.

Sefa stopped moving. Her grip on my hand went from lockpick to drowning woman.

"No," she said. Just that. One word, flat as a dropped plate.

Pell came skidding around the corner of Shelf Row Nine, soot on his face and somebody else's memory-light caught in his hair like tinsel. "They're flooding the galleries," he shouted. "All of them. Every level. They're going to drown the—" He stopped. He looked at Sefa. He looked at me. Whatever he saw in her face made him finish it anyway, because Pell is cruel in the way honest people sometimes have to be. "They're going to drown the fire."

"The fire," Sefa said.

"The dragon-fire. If the water reaches it while it's this low—"

"Then everyone in these shelves," Sefa said slowly, the way you take apart a trap to see how it kills you, "becomes permanent. No more fragments. No more escape. Property. Forever. Filed."

Nobody said anything, because there wasn't anything to say that wasn't already true.

Except there was something I wasn't saying. Something I hadn't said for hours, actually, because I'd been told—by Emrys, before we came down here, in one of her rare moments of looking directly at me instead of through me—to keep quiet about it until we understood it better.

The locket had been singing.

Not humming. Singing. Since the moment we crossed into the court beneath the mountain, the sliver of soul-light inside it had been doing something I can only describe the way I heard it, which is: a woman's voice, in the First Tongue, saying a name over and over, soft as a lullaby, patient as tide.

The name was mine.

Or—not mine exactly. The name was *Vess*, but the way she said it, it was also a question, and a promise, and the particular tone grown-ups use when they've been waiting somewhere for a very long time and finally see you coming up the road.

I knew whose voice it was. I want to be clear about that, because later—after—I would need to remember that I knew. It was Bryn. It was the sister. It was the soul the magistrates had sold, the life chopped into pieces and shelved in glass, the reason six thieves and one child had come down into the dark to commit the largest act of theft in the history of a city built on it.

She had been calling me since the mines. And I had been told to wait.

* * *

Here is the other thing you should know about me, the thing I figured out somewhere between the third shelf-row and the rising water:

I am not actually a vessel.

That's what the clerks called me, when they thought I couldn't hear. *The vessel.* Like I was a jug. Like the point of me was to hold something better and stay shut. Even the plan—even our plan, the good guys' plan—had a version where I was mostly packaging. Carry the locket. Stay safe. Let the grown-ups negotiate with the universe about who pays what.

But a jug doesn't get sung to. A jug doesn't answer.

For as long as I could remember—which is longer than most people believe, and shorter than is true—I had felt like a house where someone had moved out but left their coat on the hook. There was a shape in me shaped like somebody else. When I spoke the First Tongue, words came out of my mouth that I had never learned, in a cadence I had never practiced, and they fit my mouth the way a hand fits a glove that was made for a bigger person. Everyone thought that was eerie. It was eerie. But it was also, if I'm honest, the least lonely I ever felt—those moments when the glove filled up and I was briefly two sizes larger than myself.

The voice in the locket fit that same shape. Perfectly. The way a key fits the lock it was cut for.

Bryn wasn't calling me toward her. That's the part nobody guessed, and I need you to get it right, because everyone afterward wanted to make it a tragedy, and tragedies require that the small person didn't understand what she was doing. I understood completely.

She was calling me *home*. And home, it turned out, was on fire.

* * *

The water came through the gallery gates at the level of our ankles, then our knees, black and fast and smelling of rust and old stone. The clerks fled first—of course they did; you cannot run a ledger if the ledger dissolves, and fear is just arithmetic for cowards. Some of the freed souls tried to carry the shelved ones who were too weak to move. Some of the shelved ones refused to go, pressing their faces to their glass, because twelve years of being property teaches you that outside is a story told by liars.

Rook was shouting something about the east stairs. Sefa was shouting back that the east stairs were underwater. Pell was shouting that both of them should stop shouting, which is the traditional contribution of twins.

And through all of it, under all of it, the voice kept saying my name, and the light in the locket burned brighter as the dragon-fire ahead of us guttered lower, the way a candle leans toward its own ending.

"Vess." Sefa crouched in front of me, water to her thighs now, her cold hands on either side of my face. She had that look she gets when she's picking a lock that wants to kill her—total attention, total tenderness, no illusions. "Listen to me. We go east. High ground. You don't let go of my hand, and you don't listen to whatever that light is telling you, because lights lie, sweetheart. Lights are just fires that haven't decided yet whether they'll warm you or eat you."

"She isn't lying," I said.

"You don't know who—"

"I know exactly who." And I did, and knowing it felt like holding something hot that I never wanted to put down. "She's been saying my name for three hours. She says it like—like Emrys used to say it, before, when she forgot to be sad. Nobody taught her that. You can't teach somebody how to say a name like it's the best word in the language. Either they mean it or they don't."

Sefa looked at me for a long time. Water shoved past us, impatient. Somewhere behind us, a shelf-case burst and a soul went up through the ceiling like a struck match going home.

"Vess," she said, very quietly, "if you let go of my hand, I will find you. I want you to know that. Whatever happens, however this ends—I am a lockwitch, and there is no door in this mountain, living or dead, that I will not open to come get you. Do you understand? So whatever you're about to do—"

"I'm sorry," I said.

"—you do it knowing someone is coming after you."

"I'm sorry," I said again, because I was, genuinely, and because sorry is the only coin children are permitted to pay adults with, and I had a great deal of it saved up.

Then I let go.

* * *

I want to describe the walk properly, because everyone else will get it wrong. They'll say the child wandered dazed into the flames, hypnotized, a lamb to slaughter, poor little thing. They'll wring their hands and call it a tragedy and use me as an argument for whichever side they were already on.

Here is what actually happened.

I waded. The water was at my waist and rising, and it fought me the whole way, and I fought back, and I lost my left shoe and kept going, because shoes are replaceable and this was not. The gallery sloped downward ahead of me, toward the heart-chamber where the dragon-fire lived—it wasn't really a chamber, it was a wound, a place where the mountain's bones had been cracked open centuries ago and something vast and warm had pooled in the break and stayed, dimming year by year as the magistrates drew it off in thimbles and called it law.

The fire was low. Lower than low. What had once been an ocean of gold was now a lake, then a pond, then a puddle of embers the size of a market stall, breathing in and out so slowly that each breath took a minute and I found myself counting them, the way you count a sick person's breaths to prove they're still alive.

One.

The water reached my ribs. Behind me, faintly, Sefa's voice, and Pell's, and someone screaming my name who sounded like Emrys and also like grief wearing Emrys's coat.

Two.

The locket was blazing now, hot against my chest, and the voice wasn't saying my name anymore. It was saying something else. It had switched to the oldest words I knew—the ones from before, the ones that lived in my mouth without permission—and I understood them the way you understand your own heartbeat: not with your mind, but because they were yours.

*Sister,* she was saying. *Little sister. You came. You actually came.*

Three.

And here is the part I need written down exactly right, so that someday, when Vess-the-adult reads this account—if accounts survive, if anything survives, if the water doesn't take the paper and the fire doesn't take the world—I will remember the reasoning and not just the fear.

I was not walking toward death. I want that on the record. The magistrates thought the fire was fuel, something to be hoarded and measured and spent. The clerks thought it was inventory. The Wardens thought it was contraband. But I had listened to it breathe for three hours, and I knew what it actually was.

It was the first magic. The mother-magic. The thing that was alive before laws existed to forbid it, and which every law since had been a slow, polite, bureaucratic strangling of. And it was dying. Not because it was weak. Because it had been *permitted* to grow weak, by men who profited from its weakness, who needed it frail enough to file.

A thing like that doesn't need saving. Things like that need *witnesses*. Someone has to stand close enough to feel the heat and refuse to look away, so that the last breath is taken in company rather than alone in a locked room full of paperwork.

Also—and this is the smaller, truer reason, the one I'll admit only here—she asked me to.

Not in words. In the way the light leaned toward me. In the way the name sounded when she said it. Somewhere in the pieces of her shelved across twelve years and nine hundred glass cases, Bryn had been alone in the dark, and the last thing she would ever feel, if I turned around and climbed toward safety, was the sound of her little sister's footsteps going the wrong way.

Four.

I stood at the edge of the ember-pool. The water was at my shoulders. My feet found stone, then found the smooth glassy crust near the fire, hot enough that I'd carry the marks forever and I decided, standing there, that I wanted them. Scars are just memories that insist on themselves.

The fire breathed.

Five.

I stepped down into the light.

It didn't burn. That's the secret nobody tells you, the secret the whole city was built to prevent anyone from learning: it doesn't burn what comes to it honestly. It burned the magistrates' greed and the clerks' ledgers and the Warden's chains, but a child who walks in on purpose, carrying a piece of a stolen soul and no agenda whatsoever except *I heard you and I came*—

The fire rose up around me like arms. Like a cloak. Like the biggest hug in the history of the world, given by the oldest living thing, and inside the light the locket flew open at last, and the sliver of soul lifted out of it, and the voice said my name one final time—

—and then said it differently. Said it *whole*. The way you say a word when you've finally got all its letters.

The water hit the edge of the pool and hissed into steam, and the steam went up, and the fire flared—a last breath, the deepest one, the kind a dying thing spends everything on—and for one moment the entire undercourt was lit gold from floor to ceiling, every shelf, every case, every fleeing clerk and rising soul, all of us lit up together like the inside of a lantern.

In the middle of that light, in the middle of the fire that was making me, unmaking me, remaking me—I heard her laugh.

Not a sad laugh. A *Bryn* laugh, apparently, though how I know that I couldn't then have told you. Bright and surprised and a little bit wicked, the laugh of someone who has just realized the trick worked, the con paid off, the impossible thing happened after all.

*Oh,* she said, in the First Tongue, in the old words, in the voice that fit my mouth like a glove made for me. *Oh, you brave, ridiculous, magnificent little—*

The fire went out.

* * *

Darkness. Water. Cold.

I woke on stone, coughing, soaked to the bone, missing one shoe. Someone had their arms around me—Emrys, it was Emrys, holding me so tightly I could feel her heartbeat going like a rabbit's, and she was saying my name over and over, but not in the First Tongue, in ordinary Ashvale speech, ragged and human and terrified.

Behind her, the ember-pool was black. Truly black. Dead for the first time in a thousand years.

And above us—above the drowned galleries, above the shattered shelves, up through the mountain's broken bones—I heard a sound like a held breath finally released. Thousands of souls, no longer shelved, no longer owned, no longer waiting, rising all at once toward a surface none of them had seen in years.

The wards of Ashvale had been fed by the dragon-fire. I knew that the way I knew the First Tongue: without ever being taught.

Which meant that somewhere overhead, right now, the ban was failing. The law was guttering out with the last of the flame it had strangled. Every door in the city that had ever been locked against magic was, at this exact moment, forgetting how.

Emrys pulled back and looked at me—looked *through* me, the way she always did, searching for someone—and I watched her face change as she realized the locket was empty. As she realized the light was gone. As she realized, with the particular horror of a thief discovering the theft complete, that something had happened down here that no one had planned.

"What did you do?" she whispered.

I opened my mouth to answer.

And out of it, in a voice that was mine and also, unmistakably, warmly, permanently someone else's, came words I had never learned in a language I had never been taught:

*"She's coming with us,"* I said—or we said, or she said through me, the grammar still being negotiated. *"Both of us. And Emrys—"*

The voice paused. Even then, even new-risen, she knew. Some things a soul can feel even through glass and water and fire.

*"...Emrys, why are you looking at me like I'm a stranger?"*
