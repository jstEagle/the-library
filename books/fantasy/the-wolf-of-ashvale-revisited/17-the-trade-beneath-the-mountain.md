## 17. The Trade Beneath the Mountain

The water was rising and the fire was dying, and I stood in front of a shelf.

That was the whole of it. Twelve years of theft, betrayal, flooded mine-shafts, a dead man's evidence still warm in Sefa's hands, a child somewhere behind me in the dark—and it came down to a shelf. Pine boards. Iron brackets. A soul in a glass jar the way a grocer might keep pickled eggs.

Bryn's soul did not look like I had imagined. I had imagined fire, because everything important in my life eventually came back to fire. What I got instead was light the color of late afternoon through a window—amber, patient, the kind of light that makes you want to sit down and stay. It turned slowly in its jar. Not restless. Just turning, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice.

"Emrys." Pell's hand on my shoulder, shaking. Water to our knees now, black and cold, carrying that smell mines get when they drown—old iron, old rot. "We have to move. The magistrates opened the sluices. This whole gallery goes under in—"

"I know."

"You don't seem like you know."

I didn't answer, because the clerk was speaking.

He had come out of the stacks when the riot started, this head clerk, the one who remembered everything—which is the occupational hazard of his trade, since what he traded in was remembering. He'd watched us fight down the aisle between the shelves with an expression I can only call professional disappointment, the way a librarian watches people spill wine on the folios. He was dry. Everyone else in the court of sold souls was soaked or burning or screaming, and he was perfectly, insultingly dry.

"The release signature has been countersigned," he said. "Kin's true hand. Accepted by the ledger. You understand what that means."

"It means she's free," I said.

"It means the debt is *live*." He said it the way you'd announce a change in interest rates. "A sale requires payment in kind. The kin who signed must provide consideration equal to the goods. Twelve years of storage, Emrys Nystrom. Twelve years of interest accruing on a child's soul, compounded nightly against the dragon-fire. The ledger does not forgive. It only balances."

"And if it doesn't balance?"

He gestured, gently, at the water climbing the shelves. At the guttering red glow far above us where the last dragon breathed its slow ruin. "Then the goods remain. Permanently. Property does not drown; property is simply relocated. To wherever we keep things when there is no more fire to keep them warm." He smiled. It was a small, tidy smile. "You've seen the clerks. You know what we are. Some debts are paid in labor."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the moment you decide to die: it isn't loud.

I had imagined, over twelve years of imagining, every flavor of reunion. Bryn older than she should be, laughing at me for crying. Bryn furious. Bryn not knowing me at all, which was somehow the one I feared most—not because she wouldn't love me, but because I would have to introduce myself. *Hello. I'm the sister who let them take you. I'm the sister who signed.*

And now here was the actual shape of it, and it wasn't any of those. It was arithmetic.

Equal love for equal love. That was the ledger's rule—I'd spent enough years around memory-thieves to know the old laws of trade, and the court beneath the mountain was nothing if not a trader. The hollow in Bryn's soul, the piece they'd carved out to make her portable, sellable, shelveable—that hollow could only be filled with something of matching weight. Matching *love*. And there was exactly one thing in all the world that weighed as much as a sister.

Me.

Not my body. They'd offered me that trade already, back before the riot—the clerk with his tidy offer, Vess's body for Bryn's jar, and I'd refused so hard I think I frightened him. But there was another currency. There was always another currency. Memory-thieves know better than anyone that a life can be disassembled and sold in pieces, and the pieces hold their value.

Every memory of her. The childhood—all of it, the First Tongue learned in the space under the stairs, the wooden bird she whittled, the games, the secrets, her terrible singing. And the grief too. All twelve years of it. The funeral I'd been drugged through. The bargain I'd made to forget enough to survive. The reason I'd come down here at all, worn smooth as a river stone by now from all the carrying.

They would go into the hollow. All of it. And I would stand up out of this water whole, and healthy, and empty of her, and I would look at my own sister's risen soul and feel what a stranger feels.

The clerk was watching me do the sums. Of course he was. He remembered everything; he probably remembered my face doing this exact arithmetic in other centuries, on other faces.

"You're considering it," he said. Not mocking. Almost gentle, which was worse. "Most kin do, at the end. Love is the only collateral the ledger accepts without haggling. We never have to lower the price."

"How does it work?" My voice came out level. I was proud of that, later. Proud of it in the way you're proud of a stranger.

"A vessel," he said. "The memories pour into the hollow. The soul rises complete. The giver keeps—" he consulted some interior page, some ledger only he could see, "—continuity of self. Body, skills, habits, name. You will function perfectly. You will simply have a hole shaped like her, and no map to it."

"That sounds familiar," I said. "I've done a smaller version. Twelve years ago. To survive."

"Yes," said the clerk. "We kept the receipt."

* * *

Sefa found me then. Came splashing down the aisle with Rook covering the rear and Pell hauling a half-drowned clerk's assistant by the collar, and she saw my face, and Sefa—who can read any lock ever forged but has never once successfully read a room—read it anyway.

"No," she said.

"Sefa—"

"*No.* Whatever you're pricing, whatever deal your eyes are making with that dried-up vulture, the answer is no. We didn't come down here to trade you away. We came down here to steal you both back. That was the job. That was always the job."

"The job changed when the water came in."

"The job doesn't change." She grabbed my arm, and her grip was the grip of a woman who has picked locks with her fingernails and once held a bridge door shut against six Wardens with her shoulder, and none of it moved me, because grief makes you very strong and very weak in the same muscle. "Emrys. Listen to me. I found Tam's shelf tonight. My brother. Forty years I thought he ran off to the coast, and he's been a *jar* on a *shelf*, and when I find him I am going to carry him out of here myself, and if you do this—if you erase yourself out of your own story—then I will spend forty years wondering which version of you I buried. Don't make me bury two siblings."

Behind her, Rook said quietly, "Water's at the third shelf rung. We're out of time for speeches."

Pell said nothing. Pell just looked at me, and Pell had heard Vess speak the First Tongue like everyone else, had heard his dead mother in a child's mouth, and his silence was louder than Sefa's shouting.

And somewhere behind all of them, small and wet and stubborn, Vess.

She'd followed us down. Of course she had. Nobody had given her permission; permission had never once slowed that child. She was standing on the submerged bottom step of the stair, holding the locket open in both hands, and the sliver of soul-light inside it was leaning toward the jar on the shelf like a plant toward sun. Like recognition. Like home.

"Vess," I said. "Come here."

She came. She always came to me; that was the cruelty and the grace of it. She stopped in front of me, chin up, water to her ribs, and she said something in the First Tongue. I understood it the way I understood all of it now that we were this deep in the mountain—in the bones first, the mind limping after.

*I hear her,* she'd said. *In the locket. She's singing.*

Of course she was singing. Bryn sang constantly and terribly and with total conviction. The thought arrived with a physical blow, and I understood that I was already grieving, already standing at the edge of the loss, taking measurements of it. Twelve years of grief, plus all the years before. Equal love for equal love. The scales were going to tip either way, and only I could choose which pan held the weight.

"Emrys," Vess said, in plain Ashvale speech now, the accent of the night market, my market, the one I'd taught her by accident across a winter of charm-selling. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing," I lied, easily, the way I'd lied to her for months. "I need you to do something for me. Can you do something for me?"

She nodded. Children that age will agree to anything; it's one of the reasons selling to them is easy and stealing from them is unforgivable.

"When I tell you—whatever happens—hold the locket against the jar. Both hands. Don't let go until it stops pulling. Do you understand? However strange it gets, however bright, however loud. You don't let go."

"Why?"

"Because you're the door," I said. "You've always been the door. They used you to carry the payment in, and doors work both ways."

She looked at me for a long moment with those too-old eyes, the eyes of a child carrying someone else's soul the way other children carry a stray cat home. Then she nodded once, businesslike, and I felt my heart crack along a seam I hadn't known was there, and thought: good. Better now than never. Everything cracks today.

* * *

"Terms," I said to the clerk.

Sefa made a sound like a kettle dying. Rook put his hand over his eyes.

"The ledger hears you," said the clerk.

"All memories of Bryn Nystrom. Childhood, death, mourning, the forgetting-bargain, the twelve years since. Every dream, every song, every scar that traces back to her. Complete transfer. No residue, no echoes, no little splinters left behind to fester. If I'm doing this, I'm doing it clean—you give me a whole sister, and you leave me *nothing* of her. Those are my terms."

The clerk tilted his head. Somewhere in the stacks, a shelved soul moaned—a fragment of stolen memory I'd released earlier still loose in the aisles, raising the dead the way I raised money. "Complete transfer leaves a hollow in the giver," he said. "Hollows fill with something. Usually within a season. Usually kindness, in cases like yours—it's the nearest material to hand. Are you prepared to become a kind stranger?"

"I've been worse."

"Emrys—" Sefa started.

"Sefa." I turned to her and took her face in my wet, cold hands, and I made myself smile, because if I was going to do the cruelest thing anyone had ever done to her, I was at least going to do it warmly. "Forty years you wondered about Tam. Tonight you get him back. Let me have this. Let me buy my sister with the only coin that clears."

"You're not buying her," Sefa said fiercely. "She was never for sale. That's the whole point, you idiot. That's why we burned down the ledger."

"She'll disagree when she's whole," I said. "Whole people get opinions. It's one of the perks." Then, to the clerk: "Proceed."

The clerk produced, from somewhere in his dry immaculate robes, a shallow bowl of black glass. No ceremony. No chanting. The court beneath the mountain did not deal in theater; it dealt in terms, and terms were met, and that was the whole religion.

"Name the goods," he said.

I looked at the jar. At the slow amber turning. Twelve years I'd carried her, and I realized with a strange, floating clarity that I couldn't actually picture her face anymore—not precisely, not without effort, the way you can't see your own nose without a mirror. Grief blurs. That's its mercy and its crime. So I said her name instead, and let the mountain do the rest.

"Bryn," I said. "My sister. All of her, all of me, settled in full."

The bowl filled with light.

It didn't hurt. That was the obscene part. I'd braced for pain—for tearing, for the ripping-out feeling the Tithe's broker had given me when he took my happiest hour—but this was gentle, this was *draining*, warm water through open fingers. Memories rose out of me like bubbles in amber. Under the stairs, chalk letters, her hand correcting mine. The wooden bird. Firelight. Smoke. A drug-sweet funeral. A magistrate's seal with her name on it, and my hands shaking around it in a night-market stall. A knock in the dead hour. A child on my doorstep saying words older than kingdoms, and my terror curdling into recognition—

Oh, I thought. Oh, that's what that was. That's what she looked like.

I was losing her even as I finally saw her, and the seeing was so bright and so simple that I laughed, or sobbed, or both—one wet sound in a drowning hall while the light poured out of me into a bowl, into a hollow, into the making-whole of a girl who had been broken on purpose, by men with seals, paid for by a signature in my hand.

The last thing to go was not a memory. It was smaller than that. A reflex. Something in me that had turned toward her name for twelve years, the way a compass turns north, without asking permission. I felt it swing, slow, and settle, and stop pointing anywhere at all.

And then it was done, and I was standing in waist-deep water holding a black glass bowl full of everything I'd ever loved, and I felt—

Fine. I felt fine. That was the horror of it. I felt rested. Unburdened. Light as a shoplifter leaving through the window. Somewhere below my sternum there was a room with the furniture gone, swept clean, and I stood in the doorway of it and could not tell you what had been kept there.

"Vess," I said. My voice worked. Of course it worked. Continuity of self, body, habits, name. "Now. The locket. Hold it to the jar."

She was already moving. She'd been moving since the light started pouring, drawn forward through the floodwater with the locket raised in both small hands like an offering, and she pressed it to the glass and the world went white.

The jar cracked. The bowl emptied. The amber light and the locket-light met in the air above the shelf—two streams becoming one, a river rejoining itself—and the sound they made was not thunder. It was a breath. One long inhale, twelve years held, finally released, and every shelved soul in the court turned toward it at once, thousands of jars glowing faintly in answer, the dead remembering what being whole felt like by proxy.

The light gathered. Condensed. Took, for one impossible instant, the shape of a girl of perhaps nineteen, standing on the surface of the floodwater as though it were a stage, with terrible singing still somehow audible in the air around her—

—and then it folded, swift and bright as a swallowed star, down, down into the chest of a child standing knee-deep in a drowned court, and Vess gasped like a swimmer surfacing, and her eyes when they opened were not entirely a child's eyes anymore.

Two lifetimes looked out of that small face. I watched them meet each other. I watched the new soul settle into the old body the way rain settles into ground that's waited years for it.

And I felt nothing.

No grief. No recognition. No crack of joy, no ache of loss—just a stranger's polite awe, the feeling you get watching weather do something remarkable. A child glowed in front of me. Someone I had evidently crossed a kingdom to save. The others were crying—Sefa openly, Rook silently, Pell pretending not to—and I stood among them dry-eyed and clean-handed, holding a bowl of black glass that was only a bowl now, and I searched myself the way you pat your pockets for keys, and found the room swept bare, and could not remember what I was supposed to be missing.

The water shuddered. Above us, far up the drowned shafts, the dragon-fire flared—one vast red pulse, the last deep breath of the last living magic—and the whole mountain groaned around us like an old man standing up.

"Move!" Rook roared. "All of you, MOVE—"

The child with two lifetimes in her eyes turned to me first, before the stairs, before the light, before anything. She looked at me the way you look at a locked door you suddenly, newly remember how to open.

"You," she said—in the First Tongue, in a voice layered like two singers in one throat—and held out her hand.
