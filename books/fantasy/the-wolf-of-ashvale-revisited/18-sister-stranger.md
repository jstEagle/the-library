## 18. Sister, Stranger

The first thing I knew when I woke was that I was taller than I remembered being.

Not in my body. My body was exactly the small, knock-kneed thing it had always been, all elbows and chilblains, with one boot that squeaked on the left foot no matter how much grease Sefa worked into it. But somewhere behind my eyes there was a woman of thirty-four years who remembered being tall — who remembered reaching shelves without a stool, who remembered the particular ache in a grown spine after a night sleeping on stone, who remembered *dying*, which is not an experience I would recommend to anyone, though I suppose I'm biased, having done it twice.

"Vess." Rook's face swam above me, soot-streaked, one eye swollen nearly shut. "Vess, blink if you're still you."

I blinked. Then, because it seemed polite, I blinked again.

"That's her," Pell said from somewhere behind him, voice cracking down the middle. "That's the blink. She's back."

*Back.* What a strange word for where I'd been. I wanted to tell them there wasn't any back to go to anymore, that the girl called Vess and the woman called Bryn had been poured together like two inks into one cup, and what stirred out the other side was neither and both and something new entirely — but my mouth wouldn't work right yet. It felt like talking through wool. It felt like talking through someone else's wool.

So instead I did what Bryn would have done, because apparently that was my job now.

I looked for Emrys.

* * *

She stood at the edge of the doused hearth, where the dragon-fire had sunk to a single thread of gold no thicker than a sewing needle. The floodwater the magistrates had sent down had receded to ankle-deep, steaming faintly where it touched the last warm stones, and the whole undercourt smelled like rain on hot iron — like the end of something very old. Around us the shelved souls were still rising, thousands of them, drifting up through the flooded galleries toward whatever came next, and their light turned the water to spilled starlight.

Emrys stood apart from all of it. She stood the way people stand at funerals when they've come for the free food and don't know the deceased — present, correct, and utterly unmoored.

"Emrys," I said, and my voice came out doubled, a child's pipe underneath and a woman's low note riding on top of it, harmonizing with itself. Everyone within ten feet flinched. "It's done."

She turned. And here is the thing I want you to understand, because everyone else in this story will tell it wrong: she did not look at me with grief. She did not look at me with recognition strangled by forgetting, or love buried alive under the trade she'd made. Books and songs will want it to be like that. They'll give her a trembling lip and a hand pressed to her heart and some line about how the heart remembers what the mind cannot.

What actually happened was this: she looked at me the way you look at a stranger who has just done you a large and confusing favor.

"Oh," she said. "You're awake. Good. Are you — do you need anything? Water?"

Water. Twelve years of grief traded away, every memory of her sister fed into the hollow of a sold soul to make it whole, and her first word to the result was *water*, as if I were a traveler off a long road and she were an innkeeper who'd never seen me before in her life.

"No," I said. "Thank you."

And then, because I couldn't help it, because the woman in my head was leaning against the inside of my skull watching her with an expression I can only call *fond devastation*: "You always say thank you when you mean something bigger. Did you know that?"

Emrys frowned. Not painfully. Just the ordinary frown of a woman doing arithmetic that wouldn't balance. "Do I?"

"You do," I said. "You said it to Sefa when she carried me out of the flood. You said it to Corvane's body." I stopped there, because his body was still lying by the clerk's desk where the Warden commander had left it, covered now with someone's coat, and even two lifetimes between us hadn't taught me how to talk about the dead lightly. "You say thank you the way other people say *I owe you*. You probably don't know you do it."

"I don't know how you know it," Emrys said. There was no suspicion in it. That was the worst part. If she'd been suspicious I could have fought her; suspicion is a door you can pick. But she was only curious, mild, a stranger making conversation while the world ended around them. "Have we met? Before all this?"

*Before all this.* As if the twelve years before all this had been deleted rather than spent. Which, to be fair to her, they had. By her own hand. Sort of. The paperwork on whose hand exactly was, I gathered, complicated.

"No," I said. "We haven't met."

Bryn screamed inside me. Not loudly — she'd never been a screamer, that was Emrys's department, the shouting, the door-slamming, the dramatic exits that Bryn used to narrate in the First Tongue with running commentary like a festival announcer. No, Bryn's scream was quiet, the scream of a woman standing in a crowded market watching her sister walk past without seeing her. I felt it the way you feel weather coming. I put my hand flat against my own chest, over the place where the locket used to hang, and held on.

"You're hurt," Emrys said. She crossed the wet stones toward me — three quick strides, practical, a thief assessing damage — and knelt, and took my chin in her fingers, tilting my face toward the guttering gold light with the brisk competence of someone checking a horse's teeth. "Bruised. Dehydrated, probably. How long were you — " She caught herself, recalculated. Whatever the clerks had told her about vessels, she clearly didn't want to say the word. "How long were you down here?"

"A while," I said. "It doesn't matter."

"It matters."

"Why?" I asked. It came out sharper than I meant. Sharper than *I* meant. Bryn might have aimed it. "Why does it matter to you? You don't know me. You paid — " I stopped. Rephrased. Even with two lifetimes, some things shouldn't be said aloud in front of the person who paid them. "You gave a great deal. Why does what happens to me matter now?"

Emrys sat back on her heels, water soaking through her trousers, and considered the question with the same seriousness she'd given my bruises. Behind her, another shelf emptied itself, souls peeling upward like steam, and the light in the chamber dimmed another shade.

"I don't know," she admitted. "There's a shape where an answer should be. Like a word on the tip of your tongue. I look at you and there's just — nothing. No reason. And I keep almost apologizing for that, which is absurd, because you can't apologize for an empty room." Her mouth twisted. "But I keep thinking someone ought to be looking after you. Whoever they were. Whoever was supposed to. Someone should have been, and I have a strong feeling that somebody dropped the job."

Bryn made a sound inside me that I will carry to whatever grave eventually gets me — a sound like a laugh and a wound sharing one body. *Dropped the job,* she said, in the privacy of my skull, in the First Tongue. *Twelve years, Nystrom. You dropped the job for twelve years and you're worried the paperwork got lost.*

"Hush," I murmured, in the First Tongue, before I could stop myself.

Emrys went very still.

"What was that?" she asked. "What you just said."

"It's a language," I said carefully. "An old one. Do you know it?"

She shook her head slowly. But her lips had parted slightly, the way lips do around a taste, and her eyes had gone somewhere I recognized — somewhere twelve years deep, somewhere she herself could no longer follow. Watching her was like watching a woman stand at the edge of a hole in her own memory and feel the draft coming up out of it without knowing what the hole was for.

"It sounds like..." She stopped. Started again. "Nothing. It sounds like nothing. Like a dream you can't — " She pressed her knuckles briefly to her mouth. "Excuse me. It's been a long night."

"It has," I agreed.

*Tell her,* Bryn whispered. *Tell her everything. Who we were. The river, the stolen plums, the way she cried at the puppet show and blamed the smoke. Tell her she used to sleep with a knife under her pillow even at nine years old because she said trust was a luxury good.*

*I will,* I told her. *Not today.*

*Why not today?*

*Because today she looks at me and sees a stranger,* I said, *and if I tell her she's my sister, she'll spend the rest of her life grieving someone she can't remember, out of obligation, out of guilt, out of duty to a story I told her. That's not love. That's homework.* I paused. *Besides. She deserves to meet you properly. Not be handed you like a ledger entry.*

Bryn was quiet a long moment. Then, wryly, in a tone I was beginning to suspect I'd inherited along with everything else: *You argue like her, you know.*

*I know,* I said. *It's unbearable.*

* * *

They got us out through the mine-shafts while the mountain groaned overhead like an old man changing position in bed. Sefa carried me on her back for the first mile, muttering curses in three languages, until I asked her — in the Fourth Tongue, the merchants' cant, the one her brother used to sing sea shanties in — whether the curse about the magistrate's mother was anatomically accurate or purely aspirational.

Sefa nearly fell down a shaft. When she recovered, she stared at me over her shoulder with wet eyes and said, "Don't do that to a woman on stairs, child," and after that nobody offered to carry me again, which suited me fine. Two lifetimes make you heavy in ways backs can't fix.

Pell walked beside me most of the way. He kept glancing at me sideways, working up to something, and finally he blurted: "When you spoke in the court. To wake the shelves. Was my mother's voice in there? In the language?"

"Yes," I said.

"Was she — did she say anything? About me?"

I reached into the vast new archive behind my ribs, where a thousand borrowed deaths and lives lay shelved in rows, and found her. A broad woman with ink-stained hands who'd once written letters for illiterate dockworkers, who'd named her sons after twin rivers because she liked the idea of them flowing in the same direction forever.

"She said you flow in the right direction," I told him. "Even when the map says otherwise."

Pell cried quietly for half a mile and pretended he didn't, and I pretended I didn't notice, and we were both very dignified about it, and I think he understood that I understood, and that was its own kind of language too.

Rook brought up the rear and didn't ask me anything, which was its own mercy. Some men want their dead delivered gently or not at all.

* * *

We surfaced near dawn into air that tasted wrong — thin, burnt, like the sky itself had been holding its breath and had finally exhaled. Above us Ashvale's famous wards flickered across the peaks like aurora, stuttering, dying strand by strand. The ban was collapsing. I could feel it happening the way you feel a fever break: magic seeping back into the world through a hundred cracks at once, and the city beneath it about to learn what it had been missing.

But none of that was what I watched.

I watched Emrys.

She stood at the shaft mouth with the sunrise turning her gray, looking down at Ashvale spread below like a circuit board going dark, and I stood beside her, and Bryn stood inside me, and the three of us regarded her with more tenderness than any one person should have to receive.

"She doesn't remember me at all," I said softly, in the First Tongue. Not really to Bryn. Maybe to the morning. "Not even the shape of you. She looks right through where you used to live in her and there's just clean wall. Fresh plaster. Like the room was never furnished."

*Then we furnish it again,* Bryn said.

"With what? I can't give her memories back. The trade took them into you. They're yours now. They're — " I faltered. "They're ours."

*No,* Bryn said, and her voice gentled in a way that made me understand, finally, why a woman had once traded twelve years of herself for the chance to fill me. *Memory isn't the only furniture, little one. There are other rooms. She taught me the First Tongue when we were girls, hiding under a market stall, and do you know how she taught me? One word a day. One word, and a game to pin it to. She built a whole secret country in my mouth a syllable at a time.* A pause, and I heard the smile arrive in it. *So that's what we'll do. We'll build her a new one. She won't know why the words feel warm. She won't need to. Warmth doesn't require provenance.*

Below us, the wards failed completely — a soft, city-wide sigh, like a held breath released after twelve years — and somewhere in Ashvale a lamp lit that no one had lit, unbidden, unlicensed, illegal, glorious. Then another. Then a dozen.

Emrys watched the lights bloom and said, quietly, to no one, "Something's ending."

"Something's starting," I said.

She glanced down at me — the stranger's kindness again, that mild, unclaimed warmth, offered without history and without debt. "Will you be all right? Truly? After everything, you'll need — people. Somewhere to go. Food. I have a stall," she added, with the sudden awkwardness of a woman realizing mid-sentence that she was inviting a child to live above her shop and had no idea why the offer felt so much heavier than it sounded. "It's not much. The roof leaks. But it's dry-ish under the leak."

Bryn was laughing inside me, silent and helpless and heartsore. *Oh, Nystrom. Twelve years gone and you're still you. Still feeding strays. Still saying thank you when you mean come home.*

"Yes," I said. "I'd like that."

And I meant it. That's the part I want recorded plainly, whoever finds these words: I meant it. I had her whole sister inside me and she had nothing at all, and we stood on a mountain in the first honest dawn either of us had ever seen, and I chose to start from scratch. Because love that's handed to you complete is a relic. Love you build a word at a time, a day at a time, a leaked roof at a time — that kind is alive. That kind breathes.

"One condition," I said.

Emrys raised an eyebrow. Even stripped of every memory of me, she managed to look deeply suspicious of children on principle. It was, I suspected, a lifelong talent.

"When we get down the mountain," I said, "I'm going to teach you a language. An old one. One word a day."

Her brow creased. The draft from the hole in her memory stirred; I saw her feel it and not know its name. "Why?"

"I don't know yet," I said honestly. "But I think you already promised."

She opened her mouth to object —

— and below us, far beneath our feet, the mountain exhaled.
