## 19. The Mountain Exhales

I want it on the record that I never wanted to be a hero. Heroes get statues, and statues get pigeons, and pigeons get what they deserve, but heroes also tend to be dead by the time the pigeons arrive. I wanted to be a thief. Thieves retire. Thieves grow fat on someone else's coin in some coastal town where nobody asks about your past because everyone's is worse.

And yet here I was, ankle-deep in water that had been underground since before Ashvale had a name, holding a lantern made from a jar and a scrap of burning soul-light, watching a thousand dead people walk out of a ledger.

My name, for anyone keeping score—and I know at least one magistrate who was—is Rook. Pell's my twin, my better half if you ask her, which nobody does twice. We came down this mountain with four other idiots to steal back one soul, and we are leaving with every soul the magistrates ever shelved. That's not heroism. That's inventory shrinkage on a scale that will give an entire government an aneurysm.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

The flooding changed everything, as floods do. One moment the court of sold souls was a cavern of shelves and clerks and paper, all of it lit by the dying ember of the last dragon in the world; the next moment the magistrates upstairs opened their sluices and tried to drown us along with our evidence.

Water came in like it had a grudge. It poured down the old mine-shafts in ropes and sheets, hissing where it hit the hot stone near the fire-chamber, filling the low galleries first, driving everything upward—us, the clerks, the freed souls, the paperwork that had held them for twelve years.

Pell found me in the crush near the great shelf-hall, soaked to the ribs, dragging Sefa by the arm. Sefa's face was wet and I couldn't tell anymore which of it was floodwater. She'd found her brother's shelf. Empty now—Emrys had cracked every lock in the place during the riot—but empty isn't the same as okay, and Sefa knew the difference better than any of us.

"Rook," Pell said, "tell me you have a way out."

"I have a way out," I said.

"Is it a *good* way out?"

"It's a way out. The adjectives are still being negotiated."

Behind her, the souls were rising. I don't mean floating—I mean standing up out of themselves, out of the little glass reliquaries and iron caskets the clerks had kept them in, and becoming people again, or the memory of people, or something better than either. Some of them were bright as lanterns. Some were barely more than outlines, worn thin by years of being owned. All of them were moving toward the exits, and there weren't enough exits, and the water didn't care.

A woman made mostly of light paused beside me. She looked at the jar-lantern in my hand—the scrap of soul-stuff Emrys had torn free during the fighting and pressed into it—and she smiled, and when she spoke it was in a voice like a door opening into summer.

"You're one of the thieves," she said.

"Allegedly."

"They said no one would come for us." Her smile flickered. "They said it every year. It was almost a ritual. 'No one comes for the shelved.' And then—" she gestured at everything: the water, the chaos, the overturned clerk desks, the drifting pages of sale-contracts dissolving into pulp, "—then you did."

"We mostly came for one," I said. "The rest of you were, ah, collateral liberation."

She laughed, and the sound went through me like warmth through cold hands, and then she was gone, moving upstream against the current of souls and water both, because apparently even the dead can't resist a dramatic exit.

"Rook," Pell said again, sharper. "The adjectives. Now."

"Right." I pointed up-slope, toward the flooded gallery we'd come down through three days and one lifetime ago. "The old drainage cut. It runs up under the magistrate levels and out through the ventilation stacks. It'll be half-drowned, but drowned things can't follow us, and neither can clerks."

"And the fire?"

I looked back toward the heart of the mountain, where the last dragon-fire guttered in its cradle of black stone. It had been dying since before we arrived—a red coal the size of a cathedral, breathing once an hour, each breath dimmer than the last. When it died, everything it had ever lit died with it. Every soul bound to it. Every ward built on it. Including, though I doubt the magistrates had thought this part through, every ward over Ashvale itself.

"The fire does what fires do," I said. "We just need to not be underneath it when it finishes."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about walking uphill through a drowning tunnel with several hundred recently-liberated dead:

They're polite.

That's the thing that got me. Not screaming, not trampling—polite. Souls who hadn't been people in a decade stood aside for the living. An old man made of amber light helped Pell boost me onto a ledge I could have reached myself. A child-shaped brightness rode on Sefa's shoulders and hummed something that might have been a lullaby in a language older than kingdoms, and Sefa let it, and I saw her jaw set against whatever the humming was doing to her, and I loved her for letting it anyway.

Vess walked among them like a small queen.

The girl carried two lifetimes now—her own short one, and Bryn's whole restored soul riding inside her—and she moved through the crowd of the freed like she'd known every one of them forever. Maybe she had. Maybe that's what a complete soul knows that the rest of us don't: how much company we're always in.

She caught up to me at the mouth of the drainage cut and fell into step, small boots splashing.

"You're Rook," she said. "The funny one."

"That's the official title, yes. Pell's the clever one. Don't mix us up, it starts fights."

"Bryn says you used to steal apples from the magistrate orchards when you were nine."

"Bryn says correctly. We got caught. It's why I'm so law-abiding now."

Vess laughed, and it was a strange sound, layered—a child's laugh with an adult woman's warmth folded inside it, two people laughing through one throat. I decided not to think too hard about that, on account of the drowning.

The drainage cut was exactly as bad as advertised. Chest-deep water in places, a current trying to pull us back down toward the court, and overhead, the rock groaning in a way that rock should not groan. Behind us, far below, the light changed. I felt it through the soles of my boots before I saw it—a pressure shift, a deep inhale of the whole mountain.

The dragon-fire flared.

Not the slow coal-breath of the past days. A real flare, white-gold, the kind of light that hasn't existed in the world since the last true dragons went into the ground and the magistrates piped what was left of them into their vaults and their wards and their beautiful illegal little empire of hoarded magic. For one moment the entire undercourt blazed like dawn, and every soul in that drainage cut turned around to look, and nobody said anything, because there wasn't anything to say.

It was the mountain exhaling. Everything it had been holding—every stolen year, every shelved name, every drop of magic wrung out of a murdered species—all of it going out in one long final breath.

Then it guttered.

I watched it go. I think we all did. The gold shrank to orange, orange to red, red to a pinpoint, a spark, a thought—and then dark, real dark, the ordinary kind, the kind that just means the lamp's out and nothing else.

And the mountain sighed. Not a collapse. A settling. Like an old dog finally allowed to lie down after years of sitting up on command.

The water went still.

"Rook," Pell whispered in the dark. "What did we just—"

"End of an era," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Keep walking."

* * *

We climbed for hours. Or maybe minutes. Time gets strange when your only clock is the ache in your legs and the glow of several hundred souls lighting the tunnel like a procession of festival lamps.

Somewhere in the middle stretch, the souls began to thin—not fading, but peeling off, finding side passages I couldn't see, going wherever freed souls go when their business in a flooded mine is finished. The amber old man paused beside me one last time.

"Where do you go?" I asked him. Genuinely asking. You spend your whole life thinking of the dead as inventory or ghosts or stories, and then you carry a few hundred of them up a mountain, and you get curious.

"Wherever I'm remembered," he said. "Which, as of tonight, is a great deal wider territory than it was yesterday." He touched two fingers to his brow—an old gesture, pre-ban, something from before Ashvale learned to be afraid—and followed the others into the dark.

By the time we reached the ventilation stack, it was just us six: me, Pell, Sefa, Emrys, Vess, and Corvane's second, a Warden sergeant named Aldric who had pointed his rifle at his own commanding officers during the riot and never quite explained himself. Emrys hadn't spoken since the trade. None of us had asked her what it cost. We all knew what it cost. That was the worst part—we could all do the arithmetic except her.

She climbed in silence, steady as a machine, and when we finally hauled ourselves out of the stack into the night air of the mountainside, she stood at the edge of the rocks looking down at Ashvale, and her face was perfectly calm, and I had to look away from it.

Because Ashvale was changing.

You could see it from miles off. The city sat in its bowl below us the way it always had—but the wards, the great invisible dome of stolen magic that had hung over it for twelve years, the reason spells failed in the streets and Wardens burned charm-sellers' stalls—the wards were *going out*. Not exploding. Failing. Like lights in a house where someone has stopped paying the bill. District by district, block by block, something that had pressed on every soul in that city simply... lifted.

And then the sky over Ashvale did something I had never seen it do.

It filled with light.

Small lights. Rising lights. From chimneys and cellars and attics all across the city, from hiding places the Wardens had spent twelve years hunting—stolen memories waking up, confiscated charms rekindling, every smuggled scrap of magic that had gone dormant when the wards choked it suddenly remembering what it was. The whole city glittering from the inside out, like a chest full of coins catching sun.

"Oh," said Pell, softly. "Oh, they are going to be *so* angry."

"They're going to be worse than angry," Aldric said. He'd taken off his Warden helm somewhere on the climb and looked younger without it, and very tired. "The ban ran on those wards. Every law, every execution, every burned stall—it was all justified by the wards. 'Magic is dangerous, so we must control it.' Except the wards just proved they were never about safety. They were a warehouse lock." He looked down at the glittering city with something between grief and wonder. "There are going to be riots by morning."

"There are going to be riots by *dawn*," Pell corrected. "This is Ashvale. We riot early."

Sefa laughed—a real laugh, cracked and wet and human—and put her arm around Pell's shoulders, and for a moment the six of us just stood on the mountainside in the cold wind, filthy and half-drowned and alive, watching a prison become a city again.

Vess tugged my sleeve.

"Look," she said, pointing.

Below us, on the great gate road, torches were moving. Dozens of them. Hundreds. People coming out of the city gates—which stood open, unwarded, unlocked for the first time in twelve years—and starting up the mountain road toward the magistrates' citadel. Carrying tools, in some cases. In other cases carrying other things.

"Trials," Vess said, in the First Tongue first, then in ours, tasting the word. "Nobody's going to wait for a court."

"No," I agreed. "Courts are how the magistrates hid. Crowns are how they ruled. Paperwork was how they ate people." I watched the torches climb. "Crowds are something else entirely."

Emrys finally spoke. Her voice was flat and quiet and it still landed like a hammer.

"Then we'd better get down there," she said, "before they do something we'd all regret. Or," she added, after a pause, "before they do something we'd all secretly enjoy, which would be worse."

Pell snorted. Sefa shouldered her pack. Somewhere behind us, deep in the mountain that no longer held anything worth stealing, the last echo of dragon-fire faded out of the stone forever.

I took one last look at the glittering city—the city I'd robbed, hidden in, feared, and somehow, without ever meaning to, loved—and I started walking.

The world had changed because a handful of thieves refused a ledger. Tomorrow the world would want to know how. Tonight, all I knew was that my feet hurt, my sister was crying and pretending not to, and somewhere down that mountain, a crowd was about to remember that laws are just words unless somebody's willing to drown you for breaking them.

Time to find out what happens when nobody's willing anymore.

* * *

We reached the gate road as the sun came up, and the crowd parted for us without being asked—souls, I realized. Freed souls who'd made it to the surface ahead of us, standing in the dawn like sentries, nodding us through. The amber old man was there. He tipped two fingers to his brow again.

"Go on, thief," he said. "History's waiting, and you lot are late."

Ahead, at the foot of the citadel steps, the crowd had formed a ring, and in the center of it, pale and cornered, stood the surviving magistrates.

And at the front of the ring, mud-spattered, exhausted, and absolutely terrifying in her calm, stood Sefa—already talking, already holding the crowd's fury in her two hands like something breakable, already choosing, out loud, in front of everyone, between justice and vengeance.

I elbowed Pell. "Ten copper says they listen to her."

"Twenty says she makes them cry."

We shook on it, and pushed forward to hear.
