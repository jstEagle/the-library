## 20. Trials Nobody Presided Over

The magistrates of Ashvale were hauled out of their sanctuary at dawn, which I thought was fitting, because dawn was when they'd always done their worst paperwork.

I know because I used to steal from them before sunrise. There's a window of the morning—call it the hour of gray light, call it the hangover hour—when powerful men are at their most honest. Their masks haven't finished setting for the day. Osk used to say you could pick a magistrate's pocket easier at dawn than a drunk's, and Osk got taken by Wardens for less than that, so let his ghost have the joke.

There were seven of them left. Three had burned with the hoarded magic when the mountain exhaled—the wards they wore like jewelry turned to ash on their throats and took most of them with it. One had thrown himself down the flooded shaft we'd come up, which struck me as cowardly until I remembered what was waiting up here. The other two had simply vanished, doors left open, chairs still warm. Running men leave warmer chairs than you'd think.

Seven. And the crowd that came to see them filled the Grand Causeway from the broken gate to the fountain steps, ten thousand strong, carrying everything from kitchen knives to the shelved soul-fragments some of them had carried up from under the mountain in cupped hands, like embers, like babies.

Nobody presided. That was the thing nobody had planned for and everybody had to live through. There was no court anymore. The court had been the lie, and now there was just the crowd, and the crowd had all the authority in the world and no idea what shape it should take.

* * *

My brother's name was Tam. He went under the mountain eleven years ago, caught lifting bond-notes off a customs barge, sentenced not to prison but to *inventory*. They shelved him between a fishwife who'd sung a forbidden lullaby and a boy of maybe nine whose crime, as far as the ledger showed, was speaking too well.

I found his shelf by touch before I found it by sight. That's how it works, if you're kin. My fingers knew him before my eyes did—a cold little jar of light, dimmed almost to nothing, labeled in a clerk's tidy hand: *NYSTROM-ADJACENT STOCK, T. SOULFRAGMENT 4411, GRADE C.*

Grade C. My brother, who taught me to pick locks with hairpins and patience, who laughed so hard once he fell off a roof he wasn't even stealing from—he was grade C stock. A clerk had graded my brother the way you grade wool.

I held him for an hour down there while Pell kept watch and the dragon-fire guttered and Rook swore at rising water. And when Emrys cracked the shelves open and the souls rose up remembering themselves, Tam's fragment flew past my face so close I felt it, warm as a cheek, and then it was gone up the shaft with all the others, and I stood in the dark with empty hands thinking: *well. That's that. That's all I get.*

Except it wasn't, because here's what nobody tells you about getting your dead back in pieces: you don't get them back. You get the fact of them back. You get the crime of them back. And the crime is heavier than the grief, or at least it sits differently on your shoulders, and I've carried both.

So understand that when I say what I said at the trials—and they were trials, whatever anyone wants to call them, trials nobody presided over but everybody attended—I was not standing on high moral ground. I was standing in the mud like everyone else, holding a knife I'd brought from home, wanting very badly to use it.

* * *

They strung the magistrates up on the fountain steps—not hanging, just bound, made to stand where everyone could see them. Someone had torn the magistrate seals off their coats already. Someone else had thrown rotten fruit, and someone after that had thrown a brick, and the fourth magistrate from the left was bleeding from the brow and nobody had moved to stop any of it, least of all the Warden-captains who'd switched sides sometime in the last three days and were now standing around looking like dogs whose master had died and who couldn't decide whether to guard the house or eat it.

Corvane would have known what to do. Corvane would have hated every minute of this and known exactly what to do. But Corvane was ash on the floor of the undercourt, spent buying us our one clean moment, and so the crowd had to govern itself, and crowds are terrible at self-government. Crowds are excellent at arithmetic, though. Ten thousand minus mercy equals a number that gets shouted louder and louder until it sounds inevitable.

"String them up," someone called, and it wasn't even angry, it was *reasonable*, which frightened me more. "We tried them. We know what they did. The ledgers are right there."

And they were. Rook and Pell had carried the ledgers up themselves—four crates of them, every sale, every signature, every forged consent, every grade-C brother catalogued in a clerk's tidy hand. You could look up your own people in those books. People were doing it all day, walking along the trestle tables we'd set up, running fingers down columns, finding names. Some of them wept. Some of them closed the books gently, like they were sleeping, and walked away and didn't come back.

But others read the ledgers and then looked up at the seven bound men and did different arithmetic entirely.

"The ledgers are right there," the reasonable voice said again. "So do it."

* * *

Here is what I remember about the hours before I spoke: the sun climbing, the crowd thickening, the magistrates' faces going through stages like fruit. First outrage—they still expected law to arrive and save them, these men who had eaten law alive. Then bargaining—one of them, the fat one, Vaskin, started reciting names of other magistrates, dead ones, ones who'd escaped, offering them up like coins. Then the flatness. By noon they mostly just stood there, swaying slightly, watching the crowd the way you watch weather.

Emrys stood near me the whole time. She hadn't spoken since the mountain. She looked at the crowd and at the magistrates and at Vess—who sat on the fountain rim holding Bryn's whole bright life inside her like a lantern she'd been trusted with—and Emrys looked at all of it with the politest blankness I have ever seen on a human face. She'd paid for her sister with herself. She was living in the hole where she used to be. And still she'd come, because showing up was apparently a habit deeper than memory.

Vess kept tugging her sleeve and pointing at things. "That man sold a grandmother," Vess said, in Common Tongue, loud enough for thirty people to hear. "His name is in the book twice. Once as buyer."

The crowd liked Vess. Hard not to. A child reading your sins aloud in a voice that hadn't learned to flatter yet.

It was Vess, actually, who nearly tipped it. Not on purpose. A woman in front of me—big hands, dockworker's forearms, the look of someone who'd found a husband's name in a ledger an hour ago—turned to Vess and asked, gently, terribly, "What would you do with them, little one?"

And Vess considered it with that awful child seriousness and said, "Bryn says—" and stopped, because Bryn was new in there, two lifetimes sorting themselves, and when she continued it came out in the First Tongue first, old and ringing, and then translated itself: "*Bryn says the fire doesn't choose who it warms.*"

Which is beautiful, if you want it to be. But half the crowd heard something else in it, something older and harder, and the shouting started up again, and a man near the front raised a boat-hook, and the nearest Warden-captain—Tallow, his name was, a decent man as those things go—put his hand on his baton and clearly did not know whose side the baton was on.

That's when I climbed onto the fountain steps.

* * *

I'm not a speech-giver. I'm a lockwitch. My whole art is knowing exactly how much force a mechanism can take before it breaks, and applying one hair less. So that's what I did. No speech. Just mechanics.

"Before we kill them," I said, "somebody tell me the procedure."

The shout died down to confusion, which is what I wanted. Confusion buys you a full breath in any room.

"I mean it," I said. "Somebody lay it out. Who counts the votes? What's the standard of proof? Do we weigh twelve years of selling souls against one bad afternoon, or do we just go by volume? And whoever does the weighing—do they wear a badge? Do they write it down?" I pointed at the ledger crates. "Because those books started exactly this way. Not with monsters. With tired people deciding the procedure was obvious and skipping the part where anybody argued."

A voice from the crowd: "It's not the same!"

"It's exactly the same," I said, "right up until the moment it isn't, and none of us can see that moment from inside. That's the trick of it. Nobody who wrote those ledgers thought they were writing evil. They thought they were writing *order*. Grade C. Stock number. It's only paperwork if you're not in the jar."

The dockworker woman was looking at me. The boat-hook man hadn't lowered it yet, but he hadn't swung it either.

"My brother's in those books," I told them. "Tam Nystrom. Fragment 4411, grade C. Eleven years in a jar because he stole from men who stole cities. I found his shelf with my own hands." I let that sit. Let the crowd do its arithmetic—*she has the most right here, she lost the most, listen to her.* "I came up that shaft wanting two things. Him back, and them dead. I got half of that."

Someone laughed, ugly and understanding, and I let that sit too.

"And I've had three days to think about the other half," I said. "And here's what I keep coming back to. Those clerks under the mountain? The ones who tended the shelves, stamped the jars, filed the sales? They weren't born clerks. Every one of them came down the same road we did. Thieves, singers, troublemakers. The mountain didn't make them cruel—it just gave them a procedure and let them hide inside it. And the magistrates built the mountain. So if we put these seven against a wall without a trial, without a record, without somebody standing up to argue the losing side—" I shrugged. "We're not burning the ledger. We're becoming it. New handwriting, same column. And somewhere in forty years there's a kid finding *her* people in *our* books, and the books will be ours, and she'll hate us the way I hate them."

Silence. Ten thousand people quiet is a physical thing—you feel it on your skin, pressure, like diving.

"They should answer for it," I said. "Publicly. On the record. Names read out, crimes read out, ledgers open on the tables so anyone can check the work. And then—" and this was the hard part, this was the part my hands shook through, because Tam's fragment was warm against my memory and the knife was heavy in my belt— "and then they live. In Ashvale. Among us. Walking past the tables where their own crimes are written down, every day, forever, with no magic left and no walls to hide behind. Death lets them off. Dead men don't have to look at what they did. Make them look."

The boat-hook lowered. Slowly, like a flag.

The dockworker woman spoke next, and I'll love her till I die for it. "Who keeps the record, then?"

"You do," I said. "All of you. That's the whole point. Trials nobody presides over are the only kind that can't be bought."

* * *

It wasn't unanimous. Don't let anyone ever tell you it was unanimous—that's another kind of ledger-entry, the flattering kind. It was close, and loud, and it took until well past dark, and two of the seven fainted, and Vaskin wept so long and so theatrically that Pell leaned over to me and whispered, "Ten silver says he's performing," and I said "No bet," because some things are certain.

But when the counting finally happened—done by raised hands, counted by three volunteers who'd never met each other before that morning, checked against each other because we're thieves and we check—it came out mercy. Narrowly. Ugly. Mercy.

They read the crimes out anyway. All night, taking turns, citizens stepping up to the trestle tables and reading from the ledgers in flat voices—names sold, prices paid, signatures forged—while the seven stood and heard every line of themselves read aloud to the city they'd caged. Some in the crowd left early. Some spat. One old man stood through all six hours with his hat over his heart, and when they unbound the magistrates at dawn he was the first to turn his back on them, and ten thousand people followed him doing it, one wave of turning backs, and I have never seen anything so quiet hit so hard.

The magistrates walked out through the broken gate into a city that would not execute them and would not forgive them, and that sentence—*would not*—was the verdict. There's no cell like being known.

Afterward, Emrys found me at the ledger tables. Vess was asleep against her shoulder, worn out from listening to six hours of grown-up horror, and Emrys stood looking at the crates of books with that polite blank face, and I braced myself for nothing, because she had nothing left to give, that was the whole shape of her sacrifice.

But she reached out and straightened one of the ledgers—just squared its corner with the others, a tiny tidying, a stall-keeper's gesture, muscle memory from a thousand nights arranging charms—and said, in a voice like someone reading a stranger's letter:

"Your brother would have liked this. The messy way you won. I don't know why I think that. But I do."

And I had to walk away for a minute, because that's the thing about Emrys's forgetting—we all remember for her now, all of us, carrying Bryn in our mouths, handing her back piece by piece in stories, and some days the weight of remembering for two people is more than grief ever was.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and honest over the causeway. The ward-stones on the broken gate were dead and dull, and for the first time in living memory, magic was neither banned nor hoarded nor owned—it was just gone, except for whatever lived in people, which had always been illegal and now, suddenly, wasn't.

Pell found me packing the last crate. "Word's moving through the market," she said. "The charm-seller's setting up her stall tonight. First time since the mountain."

"She shouldn't," I said. "She should rest. She paid—"

"Yeah, well." Pell shrugged. "You know her. Grief or no grief, the woman's got hours to keep."

And that was true, God help us. Whatever else the mountain had taken out of her, it hadn't touched the part of Emrys Nystrom that opened her stall at dusk and worked the night market like a tide. Some of us were already planning to drift by—not to buy, just to be near her, the way you sit with someone sitting shiva. Because there was a story going around, and I wanted to see the truth of it with my own eyes: that the charm-seller who couldn't remember her sister had hung a child's wooden bird among her luck-charms, in the place of honor, and couldn't say why.
