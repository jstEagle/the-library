## 21. The Charm Seller's Hours, Again

The stall opens at the same hour it always has—the hour when the lamplighters are still drunk from the night before and the honest merchants haven't yet arrived to look down their noses at me. I've always liked this hour best. The market belongs to people like me then: the ones who sell luck that doesn't exist to buyers who need it to.

I set out my wares the way I always do. Little glass vials on velvet, each one labeled in my own careful hand. *Courage for a difficult conversation. Calm for sleepless nights. The memory of summer, for those who've forgotten what warm feels like.* All of them stolen once, all of them worthless as charms and priceless as placebos, and every buyer walks away happier than they came. There's a word for that profession, probably. I prefer *merchant*.

Except tonight—or rather, this morning, the charm seller's hours being what they are—my hands stop at the third row, where a small wooden bird sits among the vials. A child's toy, carved badly, one wing chipped. It doesn't belong there. I know it doesn't belong there the way you know a stone is in your shoe. And yet when I reach to move it, my hand won't close around it. Not won't—*hesitates*. The way your hand hesitates before touching a hot pan, except the bird isn't hot. It's just wood. Cheap pine, someone's whittling knife, an afternoon of somebody's patience.

It's been sitting in that spot for two weeks now. Two weeks of me rearranging my entire stock around it without deciding to.

"New piece?" asks Osk's widow, who runs the fruit stand across the way and has decided, in her grief and her loneliness, that I am a person worth talking to. I let her think so. Grief makes strange friendships.

"Old piece," I say. "Just... repositioning."

She squints at it. "Looks like something a child would've made."

"It does," I agree, and my chest does the thing it's been doing for two weeks, which is ache in a way that has no address. Like a bruise on an organ I don't have a name for.

That's the other thing. The songs. Ashvale's wards failed months ago—everyone still argues about why, some collapse under the mountain, some purge, nobody agrees—but the city kept its habits the way old dogs keep theirs, and one habit is the ballad-sellers who wander the evening rows singing whatever sells. Lately they sing a song about the mountain exhaling, about souls flying free, because tragedy with a happy ending sells better than either alone. It's a pretty tune. Stupid lyrics. Something about a wolf and ash and a sister's name I can't quite catch when the singer slurs it.

Every time it reaches the chorus, I weep. Full, ugly, unearned tears, right there between the courage vials and the calm ones, while customers pretend not to notice because pretending not to notice is also an Ashvale habit.

I've had my memory read twice since the mountain. Once by a hedge-witch who owed me, once by a proper licensed mind-healer, back when those existed again. Both times they found nothing. No trauma locked away, no grief suppressed, no buried anything. "Your past is intact," the healer said, almost apologetic, charging me anyway. "Whatever you're mourning, it isn't hidden from you."

Then what am I mourning? I asked.

She'd looked at me with genuine professional puzzlement and said, "That's the strange part. You're not mourning anything. You're mourning the shape of something."

The shape of something. I paid her fee and went home and held the wooden bird for an hour without knowing why, and finally put it back in its spot in the third row, between *Courage* and *Calm*, where it clearly didn't belong, and slept better than I had all week.

Weary competence. That's what I have left. That and the hours.

* * *

Vess comes every day at dusk.

I don't know how she found me the first time. I came up from the cellar with a fresh crate of vials and she was already sitting on my stool, feet swinging, wooden bird—no, wait, the bird lives here, but she had something similar, a carved fox, turning it over in her fingers—and she said, "You're the charm seller," as if confirming a fact she'd traveled far to check.

"I'm the charm seller," I'd agreed, because arguing with children about their assumptions is a losing trade.

"You sell memories."

"Luck," I corrected. "Charms. Very legal. Ask anyone."

She'd looked at me the way adults look at me when they know exactly what I am and find it quaint. Twelve years old, maybe thirteen. Dark hair cut short in a way that suggested scissors and no supervision. Eyes too old for the face holding them, which should have unsettled me and did, though I couldn't have told you why beyond the obvious—children with old eyes are bad news in any city, and worse in this one.

"You lost something," she said. "In the mountain."

"Everyone lost something in the mountain. Half of Ashvale did." This was true; the stories coming out of the undercourt had emptied half the magistrates' vaults into public outrage, and outrage, like everything else, eventually became merchandise. I sold a *Righteous Indignation* charm now. Best seller.

"Not everyone," Vess said. "You lost something you chose to lose. And you don't remember choosing."

And here is the thing I want recorded somewhere, by whoever keeps records: I was not afraid. I should have been. A child telling me I'd traded away pieces of myself—I knew what memory-theft looked like from the inside; I'd sold enough of it—I should have run her off or checked my own head for tampering or both. Instead I heard myself say:

"Do you know what it was?"

"No," she said. "But I know who gave it back. Or—" she frowned, correcting herself with the precision of someone handling live cargo, "—who it went to. You bought a life with yours. That's what Sefa says. Sefa says a lot of things, most of them rude."

"Sefa," I repeated. The name landed somewhere below my ribs and rang like a struck bell. I didn't know a Sefa. I was certain I didn't know a Sefa. And certain, in the same breath, that I'd have known her anywhere, that she owed me eleven crowns, and that she laughed like a door coming off its hinges. "Lockwitch?"

Vess's whole face changed—surprise and satisfaction and something tender underneath. "You remember her?"

"No," I said honestly. "But apparently I do."

"That's how it is with you," Vess said, nodding like a little scholar confirming a hypothesis. "The names come back before the people. Pell says it's like watching a house get rebuilt room by room, except the rooms were never torn down, just—" she searched, "—locked. Rook says it's worse than that. Rook says most things."

"And you? What do you say?"

She swung her feet. "I say I'm going to come back tomorrow. And the day after. And tell you about a woman named Bryn, until you remember her."

"Bryn," I said.

My mouth made the shape. My voice made the sound. And something in me—something with no name and no location, some organ the mind-healers don't chart—answered the way a tuning fork answers a note sung across a room. Not recognition. Recognition requires a remembered thing to recognize. This was older than that. This was the shape of something, resonating.

"Yes," Vess said quietly, watching my face. "Her."

* * *

So now it's a routine, which is a dangerous thing to build with a stranger, and she is a stranger—I want that on the record too, however it sounds. She tells me about Bryn the way you'd tell stories about a mutual friend who moved overseas. Bryn liked her tea bitter enough to strip paint. Bryn could pick any lock in Ashvale but never learned to cook an egg. Bryn taught her sister a secret language when they were girls, invented it together out of scraps, called it the First Tongue because eight-year-olds don't know the world already has prior claims on grandeur.

"The First Tongue," I repeat each time, tasting it. "What happened to it?"

"You knew it," Vess says. "Before."

"Before what?"

And she always pauses there, always, a hair too long, and says, "Just before."

I've tried to reconstruct the hole. That's the professional in me; you spend years cutting memories out of heads and selling them in bottles, you develop an eye for absence the way a carpenter develops an eye for level. But there's no seam. Whatever was taken wasn't cut—it was spent, spent cleanly, the way coin is spent, leaving no wound because spending isn't violence. Somebody—I, apparently—paid for something enormous and got exact change back in the form of an ordinary woman who sells fake charms and cries at ballads.

The others come sometimes. Sefa, who looks at me like she's biting back eleven crowns' worth of grievance and hugs me anyway, hard, saying nothing. The twins, Pell and Rook, who argue over whether reminding me is kindness or cruelty and have settled on doing it loudly in front of me so I can referee, which I suspect is their real goal. They talk about a man named Corvane sometimes, and go quiet after, and I've learned not to ask, because their quiet has a different texture than mine. Theirs is grief with a face attached. Mine is just weather.

But it's Vess who comes daily. Vess who sits on my stool and eats the plums I pretend I didn't buy specifically for her and tells me about Bryn.

Today she's brought the fox again and a new story.

"She sang," Vess says. "Badly. Sefa says Bryn's singing could curdle milk at ten paces. But she sang anyway, all the time, especially when she was nervous. And she had this song she made up about a wolf—" Vess stops. Looks at me. Looks away. "About a wolf of Ashvale. It's where the ballad comes from, the one the singers do. Except they got it wrong. In Bryn's version, the wolf doesn't die at the end."

"What happens?" I ask.

"The wolf forgets the forest," Vess says, "so the forest comes to find her. Every day. Until she remembers, or until she doesn't, and either way the forest stays."

"That's a terrible ending," I say. "Nothing resolves."

"Yeah," Vess says, and smiles at her fox. "Sefa hates it too."

* * *

Here is what I know, catalogued like stock, third row, between Courage and Calm:

I keep a wooden bird I cannot bear to touch. I weep at a song whose lyrics I've never successfully transcribed. I love a woman named Sefa with the specific exhausted love of someone who has been betrayed by you and chosen you anyway, though I have no memory of earning either the betrayal or the forgiveness. I flinch at the smell of wet stone. I cannot walk past the fountain in Warden's Square without counting exits. And every day at dusk a child arrives who knows me better than I do, and tells me about my own heart in the third person, and I listen the way the devout listen to scripture—not because they understand it, but because understanding it would change everything, and they're not ready, and they know it.

The mind-healer said I'm mourning the shape of something. She was wrong, I think, or incomplete. I'm not mourning the shape. I'm *inhabiting* it. Whatever I paid beneath the mountain, I didn't pay with the memories themselves—I must have paid with the thread that tied them to me. The beads are all still here, scattered through the person I am: the flinch at wet stone, the love of lockwitches, the bird in the third row. Just unstrung. Just loose in the pocket.

Some evenings, after Vess leaves, I sit in the dark behind my stall and try to string them.

*Bryn.* Bitter tea. Bad singing. A secret language. Sister.

The word fits my mouth like a key fits a lock that hasn't been used in years—stiff, reluctant, and then, suddenly, horribly smooth. I say it aloud to the empty market and my eyes flood and I have absolutely no idea why, and that's the joke, isn't it, that's the cosmic punchline: I can cry on command for a woman I cannot remember, but I can't remember her, because remembering her was the price of her freedom, and I—someone, the woman I was—decided that was a fair exchange.

Was it? I'd ask her, if I could find her. Was it fair?

But the woman I was is gone, spent, exact change. And the woman I am just closes up the stall at dawn, tucks the wooden bird safely into the third row where it doesn't belong, and waits for dusk, and the child, and the next bead of a life that used to be strung.

Tonight—this morning, the charm seller's hours—Vess is early. She's standing at the edge of the lamplight holding something behind her back, and she's wearing the expression of someone about to do a thing she's been rehearsing.

"You're up before the birds," I say.

"I couldn't sleep." She comes closer. "I brought you something. For the stall. Sefa says you shouldn't take inventory from strangers, but I'm not a stranger, I'm a regular."

"Regulars get discounts. What have you got?"

She brings her hand forward. On her palm lies a scrap of paper, folded small, soft at the edges like it's been opened and refolded many times.

"It's the First Tongue," she says. "One word. I wrote it out for you. I've been trying to decide which word goes first, and—" her voice does something young, finally, cracks right down the middle, "—and I think you should learn this one first. Before all the others."

I take the paper. Unfold it. The letters are a child's letters, careful and crooked, forming a word in a language I have never seen and cannot read.

"What does it mean?" I ask.

Vess takes a breath, and the lamplight catches her eyes, and somewhere under my ribs that unnamed organ begins to ring before she even answers, ringing like it knows, ringing like it's been waiting years for this note—

"Read it out loud," she says. "And you'll see."
