## 22. First Tongue

The child arrives at my stall on the first evening of the thaw, when Ashvale smells of wet stone and woodsmoke and the particular sweetness of a city learning to breathe again.

She doesn't knock. Children rarely do, and this one never has. She simply appears at the edge of my table among the luck-charms—the ones that are only buttons and bent nails now, since I sold my last true memory months ago to a farmer who wanted his wife's laugh back and couldn't afford it—and she sets down two cups and a paper twist of candied ginger, as if we had an appointment.

"We're starting today," Vess says. "You said you would."

I did not say that. Or rather, I said something adjacent to it, some weeks ago, in the gray hour when she'd found me weeping over a song I couldn't name—a sailor's tune, someone told me later, though no one could tell me why it unmade me so thoroughly. *Teach me why it hurts,* I'd said, or words like them, and she had looked at me with those old, old eyes in a young face and said, "It's not the song. It's what you knew before."

I don't remember agreeing to lessons. But then, there is a great deal I don't remember, and I have made a kind of peace with the shape of the hole where my life used to be. You learn to live around a missing tooth. You keep touching it anyway.

"Starting what?" I ask, because stubbornness is free and everything else costs.

"The language." She pours something steaming from a dented kettle into both cups—chicory, by the smell, bitter enough to strip paint. "The First Tongue. The one you spoke before you spoke anything else."

"I didn't speak any such thing." I arrange my charms with unnecessary care. "I'm a charm seller. I sell charms. My linguistic accomplishments extend to haggling in three markets' worth of dialects and swearing beautifully in two."

"You did," she says, with the maddening patience of a child who has been right about something for longer than the adult has been alive. "And I can prove it. But proof isn't the same as remembering, is it? So we go word by word. Like building a wall. One stone at a time, until one day you look up and there's a house."

I should send her away. I have thought about sending her away every day for weeks, and every day I have not done it, and I have stopped pretending to myself that this is accidental. There's a reason she keeps coming to my stall. There's a reason I keep the wooden bird—carved, painted faded blue—hanging at the corner of my awning where the light catches it. I can't tell anyone why it matters to me. It simply does, the way a scar matters: not a memory, but evidence of one.

"All right," I say, which is how all my worst decisions begin, and some of my best. "One word."

She smiles, and it's a strange smile, layered, carrying something older than her years—as it always does. She sits cross-legged on the crate across from me, cups her hands around her chicory, and becomes very serious, the way children do when they've decided a game has rules.

"*Aeth*," she says.

"Aeth," I repeat, because it seems rude not to.

"It means *light*. Not fire-light. Not lamp-light. Light the way water is wet—it's what light *is*, underneath the burning. Say it again."

"*Aeth.*"

The word goes into me strangely. Not like learning—like *remembering*, except I remember nothing. It's more that the word fits somewhere, a groove worn into wood, and settles there with a small click of belonging. I frown at my own mouth.

"Again," she says, satisfied.

---

We do this every evening.

That's how it begins, anyway. Then it becomes mornings too, because she starts arriving before dawn, when I'm setting out the stall, and stands there in the blue half-light with her hands full of whatever she's brought—bread, wild onions, once a dead crow she insisted was important ("for the lesson," she said, and declined to elaborate)—and I find I cannot teach charms and refuse a child bread in the same breath.

Word by word.

*Aeth*, light. *Mor*, deep water, but also grief—young Vess is very firm about this; the First Tongue apparently refuses to pretend those are different things. *Senn*, to stay. *Senn-vel*, to stay when leaving would be easier. That one takes me three evenings to pronounce properly, and when I finally get it, she looks at me with an expression I can't read, and I feel inexplicably like I've passed a test whose stakes were never explained.

The words come out of her like they're being poured from somewhere deeper than a child should have access to. And they go into me like—they go into me like letters finding their address. I'll say a word wrong, and correct myself before she can, my tongue remembering a shape my mind never knew. I'll wake in the night with a syllable in my ears, and lie in the dark saying it over and over like a prayer, or a key turning in a lock I can't see.

Once, I dream of a kitchen. Two girls, flour to their elbows, laughing in a language I don't understand—except in the dream I do, perfectly, and the laughter means *you missed the bowl entirely*, and *watch this*, and something else, a third phrase, soft, that I lose on waking and grieve for the rest of the day like a coin dropped through ice.

I tell Vess about the dream. She listens with her chin in her hands and her eyes shining, and says only, "Good. That one counts double."

"Counts toward what?"

"Toward you coming back."

"Coming back from where?"

She just sips her chicory. Children are tyrants about rhetorical questions.

---

The market changes around us while we work. It's been half a year since the wards failed and the ban collapsed, half a year since the magistrates faced the crowd instead of a court, and Ashvale is still deciding what to be. Magic comes back slowly, like feeling returning to a numbed limb—the pins-and-needles of small wonders. Sefa's lockwitch work is legal now, and she hates it; legality, she says, ruins the craft. Pell and Rook run a tavern called the Guttering Flame, which Rook insists is a joke and Pell insists is a memorial, and neither will explain it to me. Osk came home from wherever the Wardens took him, thinner and quieter, and took up fencing goods two doors down from my stall as if he'd never left, though he tips his hat to me every morning with a formality that has weight to it. We don't speak of the raid. Some debts are paid in silence.

And sometimes, in the evenings, a woman comes through the market with a child on her shoulders, and I watch them pass, and I feel—nothing. That's the honest truth of it. Nothing, and then a strangeness, a sense of having missed a turn in my own house. She has kind eyes. She always buys nothing and lingers anyway. She watches Vess and me with an expression like watching weather arrive, and once she said to me, "Your student is very patient with you," and I said, "She needs it," and we smiled at each other like strangers, because that's what we are.

Strangers. Though sometimes, when her sleeve brushes mine as we lean over the same charm, something under my ribs turns over like a sleeper, and I think: *careful*. Careful of what? I couldn't say. There's just a place inside me that braces, gently, the way you brace against a wave you've already decided to let hit you.

Vess sees me noticing. Vess notices everything.

"She likes you," Vess says one evening.

"Of course she does. I'm charming."

"That's not—" Vess stops. Starts again. "You'll get there. That's the whole point of lessons."

---

Winter comes, and the lessons move indoors, to my rooms above the stall, because the child refuses to acknowledge seasons and I refuse to let her freeze out of spite for her own stubbornness. She hangs her coat on my hook as if the hook has always been hers. She has opinions about where I keep my tea. Somewhere along the way, without my marking the moment, my solitary life grew a second heartbeat, small and insistent, and now I can't hear over it.

Tonight she brings the wooden bird down off its nail—my bird, the one I can't explain—and sets it on the table between us.

"Today's word needs it," she says.

"It's a bird."

"It's a *hjalli*." She taps it. "*Hjalli* means a thing made to hold a song. A whistle, a cage, a memory. Anything hollow that keeps sound safe." She looks at me. "Say it."

"*Hjalli.*"

My voice cracks on the second syllable. Not emotionally—physically, some catch in my throat, some muscle memory of a word said ten thousand times in another life. I put my hand flat on the table and wait until the floor feels solid again.

Vess watches me with terrible gentleness. "Do you want to stop?"

"No." My voice comes out rough. "What kind of teacher offers that?"

"The kind who knows where the road goes." She turns the little bird in her fingers. "There's a word we haven't done yet. I saved it. It's last, because after it, the others change meaning. All the walls become a house, see? And houses are harder than walls."

"Vess."

"You asked me once why the song hurt," she says. "The sailor's song. Do you still want to know?"

The candle gutters. Outside, Ashvale murmurs its new-old life—carts, laughter, someone practicing cantrips badly two streets over, the city's magic coming in like tide. I am suddenly, intensely aware that I am standing at the edge of something, that every word since *aeth* has been a step down a stair, and that below the last step there is either a floor or a fall.

"Yes," I say.

She takes a breath. And in the candlelight, in my small room above my small stall, in the city that outlawed magic and lost and learned better, she says the word.

"*Swyra.*"

I don't know what it means. That's the truth—I reach for it and find the hole where my memory was traded away, smooth-walled, familiar as my own palm. But my body knows. My body answers before my mind can object: my hand comes up, unbidden, and touches the hollow of my own throat, where the word seems to live, and my eyes fill, and something in my chest opens like a door that has been locked so long the hinges have fused, and behind it—not memories, not faces, not names, just *weather*. A vast warm weather of a person. Sunlight on flour. Laughter meaning *watch this*. A presence so large and loving that my small self was built in its shadow like a city built under a mountain's arm.

"What does it mean?" I whisper. Tears are running down my face and I let them, because wiping them would mean moving, and moving might break whatever is happening.

Vess's eyes are bright too. "It means *the one who shares your blood and your childhood*. It means the person who teaches you the world before the world gets a chance to teach you wrong." She leans forward. "In your old life, you said it every day. To someone who answered to it. Someone who laughed like this—" and she laughs, sudden and ringing and utterly unlike her usual careful child's voice, a laugh with a stranger's music in it, a woman's laugh, and my heart slams against my ribs like something trying to get out—

"—and called you," Vess finishes softly, "*swyra* back."

The room tilts. I grip the table. Inside me, that door stands open, and the warm weather pours through it, and I understand—dimly, terribly, completely—that I am grieving someone I cannot remember, that I gave her up, that the hole in me is shaped exactly like her, that the shape of my entire self, my wryness and my stubbornness and my habit of selling pieces of other people's pasts because I couldn't bear my own—all of it was built by a person whose name I traded away to save her, and saving her cost me the knowing of her, and that is the trade, that was always the trade, and I would—

I would do it again.

That's the part that breaks me open. Not the loss. The certainty. Even now, hollowed out, holding nothing of her but the shape she left, I know that if the choice stood before me again—her wholeness or my memory—I would sign my name a second time in the same steady hand.

"Emrys." Vess puts her hand over mine. Her fingers are small and warm. "Look at me."

I look. And in her face—two lifetimes deep, a child and a woman and something older than both—I see that she knows. She has known since the first morning. Maybe since the mountain. Maybe since before the mountain, since the locket, since the fire.

"She loved you," Vess says. "She loves you. She's here every day buying nothing and lingering, and she's learning to be patient the way you taught her to be patient, and she chose to come back slowly, gently, because she saw what the fast way cost you." Her thumb presses my knuckles. "You built her a whole life out of yours. Now she wants to build you one back. Word by word. Stone by stone. If you'll stay."

"Stay," I repeat. "*Senn.*"

"*Senn-vel*," Vess says. "Stay when leaving would be easier. Leaving would be easier, Emrys. You could sell the stall. Go south. Start clean, with no holes and no ghosts."

"And leave you two?" The words come out before I can weigh them, and they land in the room like coins on a counter—true currency, spent before I could count them. I stare at my own hands. "I mean. Leave my best customer. Who buys nothing."

Vess grins through her tears. "*Senn-vel*," she says again, softer. Teaching it. Asking it.

Outside, footsteps on the stairs—the unhurried tread of a woman who has learned to arrive gently. A knock, courteous, on my door. Through it, faintly, someone is humming the sailor's song, and this time I know why it hurts, and I let it hurt, and it is the finest pain I own.

"One more word tonight," Vess whispers, gathering her things, wiping her face with her sleeve like any child. "Tomorrow. When you're ready. There's a word for *beginning again*, and it's also the word for *bread rising*, and you're going to love it."

The knock comes again. I rise. Whatever is behind this door—stranger, sister, the warm weather of a life I paid away—I find that my feet carry me toward it without consultation, the way they carried me down the mountain, the way they carried me through twelve years of small treasons toward this exact evening.

I put my hand on the latch.

"*Swyra*," I say to the closed door—just to try the shape of it, just to feel the word live in my mouth.

And from the other side, gentle as dawn, someone answers.
