## 1. Charms and Small Lies

The lanterns of Ashvale's night market burned the color of cheap honey, and Beatrix Blackwood had learned to read them the way sailors read weather.

Three lanterns at the fishmonger's row meant the harbor catch was good and the crowd would come hungry for trinkets as well as trout. One lantern guttering outside the chandler's meant a Warden patrol had passed recently, moving slow, which meant they were bored, which meant danger. And when the lantern above her own stall — a stub of tallow in a cracked glass horn that she'd owned longer than some of her customers had been alive — flickered twice without wind, it meant the silver on her table was awake.

It was awake now.

"Evening, mistress," said a woman with flour to her elbows, lifting a bracelet of braided wire. "This one. My mother wore one like it before the sickness took her. Does it do what they say?"

Beatrix smiled the smile she kept oiled for exactly this question. "Depends what they say, mistress. It keeps away headaches. It won't keep away mothers-in-law, no matter what the tinker stalls tell you."

Laughter from two other browsers, the small warm sound of a market doing what markets did. The flour woman laughed too, and bought the bracelet for three coppers, and Beatrix wrapped it in brown paper and whispered into the paper as she folded it — a breath, no more, the shape of a word that wasn't quite a word — and felt the silver inside settle against its new owner like a cat curling up in a lap it had decided to tolerate.

The headache charm would work. That was the terrible joke of Beatrix's life: everything on her stall worked. Not because of any craft in the wire or blessing in the bending, but because twenty years ago she had learned that silver listened, if you spoke to it quietly enough, and that silver could be taught to hold a kindness the way a bell holds a note after the striking is done.

She had also learned, in the same year, what happened to people who were caught teaching silver anything at all. There was a square in the Weaver's Quarter with a scorched scar in the cobbles shaped rather like a person, and every child in Ashvale knew why, and every child in Ashvale had been told never, ever to ask why.

So: charms and small lies. Headache charms. Luck knots. A hanging twist of tin-and-silver that was supposed to keep moths out of wool (it worked; the silver told the moths, in whatever language moths understood, that there were better houses down the street). Nothing grand. Nothing that would make a Warden look twice. She priced everything low and lied about all of it, cheerfully, fluently, until the lying was as natural as breathing and about as memorable.

That was the whole architecture of her survival, and she had built it brick by brick over twenty years, and most nights she didn't even feel tired by it.

Most nights.

* * *

The market thickened as full dark came on. Ashvale kept its night market the way a man keeps a vice — guiltily, warmly, with both hands — because daylight belonged to the crown and its Wardens and its lists and its rules, but night, the city felt, still belonged to itself. Fishwives and foundrymen, students with more curiosity than coin, old soldiers spending pension-coppers on luck they didn't believe in because believing cost nothing extra. Beatrix sold a fever-charm to a grandmother with a grandson coughing in the tenements. She sold a traveler's knot to a carter who swore by them and couldn't say why. She sold nothing at all to a thin young man who stood too long at the edge of her lamplight with his hands in his pockets, watching her hands instead of her wares, and she noted his face the way a fox notes a snare — not with fear yet, just with the location, filed away.

"You've clever fingers," he said at last.

"I've a wife and six children," Beatrix said, "and clever fingers are cheaper than bread."

He smiled, uncertain whether he'd been flattered or dismissed, and moved on. Beatrix exhaled slowly through her nose and rearranged bracelets that needed no rearranging.

Clever fingers. If only. Her fingers were ordinary; it was the silver that was clever, and the less anyone wondered how, the longer everyone stayed unburned.

* * *

She heard the wolf before she saw him, which was itself an absurdity, since wolves made no sound in cities and certainly made no sound in words.

*You're low on the blue thread,* said a voice like gravel rolled in honey. *Third spool from the left. You'll run out before the moon turns.*

Beatrix did not look up. She wound a knot. She counted the knot's turns — nine, always nine, a habit so deep it had worn a groove in her like water wears stone — and she said nothing, and she thought nothing, because she had learned long ago that the worst thing she could do was think back.

*You counted wrong,* the voice observed. *Eight. You always lose one when I talk.*

She sold a charm to a boy buying a gift for his sister. She made change. She let the silence stretch the way she always let it stretch, because engaging was the first step off a very long cliff.

*I brought you news anyway,* the wolf said, patient as winter. *There's a rider coming up from the south road wearing crown colors and carrying a sealed case, and the horses behind him are lathered, and lathered horses mean hurry, and hurry from the crown means proclamation. You'll want to hear it early, little merchant.*

Her hands went still over the spools. Only for half a heartbeat. Then they moved again, winding, counting — eight, damn him, he was right, it was eight — and she lifted her head and smiled at a passing customer as though the night were an ordinary night and her heart weren't tapping at her ribs like a customer impatient for service.

The wolf sat in the alley mouth between the chandler's and the cooper's, where the lantern light gave out. To everyone else in Ashvale, there was nothing there. Dogs wouldn't walk down that alley; cats avoided it; even the rats gave it a wide berth, and Beatrix knew this last because the rats had told her so once, years ago, in their dry rustling way, complaining of *the big grey silence* that had taken up residence and asked too many questions.

Twenty years, the wolf had watched her. Twenty years, and she had never once answered him aloud.

Not because she didn't want to. That was the cruelty of it. Because wanting was the beginning, and the beginning was the square in the Weaver's Quarter with the person-shaped burn in the stones, and because a woman alone in the world survives by keeping her wants small and her lies ready, and because —

Because if she ever answered him, she was afraid she might never stop.

*Stubborn,* the wolf said fondly, the way you'd say it of family. *Twenty years stubborn. Your mother was stubborn too.*

Beatrix dropped a spool.

Blue thread scattered across the table, and she gathered it up with steady hands and a face like a closed shop-door, and if the flour woman saw, the flour woman saw only a merchant being clumsy, and merchants were allowed clumsiness the way they were allowed lies. But under the table, where no one could see, Beatrix's knuckles had gone white around the spool, and the silver in her luck-knots had all gone quiet at once, listening, the way a room goes quiet when someone says a dead person's name.

"Don't," she breathed — barely a sound, less than a breath, aimed down at the tablecloth as if scolding the thread itself.

The wolf said nothing more. But she felt him there, in the dark past the lantern light, feeling enormous and patient and terribly certain, and she felt something else underneath his certainty that frightened her far worse than any Warden:

He sounded glad. He sounded like a man who has waited a long time at a station and finally sees smoke on the horizon.

* * *

By midnight the market thinned to stragglers and pickpockets, and Beatrix began the ritual of closing: charms sorted, unsold silver wrapped in plain cloth, the tallow lantern pinched out with wet fingers. The other stallholders nodded to her in the loose way market-folk acknowledge each other — neighbors by proximity, strangers by design. Ashvale's night market ran on a mutual agreement not to know too much about anyone. It was, Beatrix sometimes thought, the most honest arrangement in the city: everyone concealing something, everyone agreeing to pretend otherwise.

Marek the tinker was packing up two stalls down, his cart half-loaded with kettles awaiting new handles. He caught her eye and raised a hand.

"Blackwood. Got a moment?"

"For you? Always." And it was true, which was dangerous, and she said it anyway, which was more dangerous still.

Marek was one of the few people in Ashvale whose company didn't cost her anything. He was broad and gray-stubbled and moved like a man apologizing to furniture for bumping into it, and he fixed things — pots, hinges, toys, the small broken machinery of poor lives — and he had never once asked Beatrix a question she had to lie about. Tonight he fished in his coat and produced a little brass locket, sprung open, its hinge gone to rust.

"My girl found this in the muck by the river gate. It was her gran's, before — well. Before." He turned it in his blunt fingers. "Can you do anything with it? I know it's not your trade, exactly. Wire and thread and such."

"It's silver underneath the plating," Beatrix said, taking it. It was; she could feel it immediately, a faint sleepy hum against her skin, the metal equivalent of an old dog lifting its head when a familiar hand comes near. "I can reseat the hinge. Won't be pretty."

"Prettiness stopped mattering to my daughter a while ago." Marek's voice did something careful around the word *daughter*, and Beatrix, who noticed everything because noticing was survival, noticed it and elected, as usual, not to have noticed.

"I'll have it by week's end."

"Take your time." He lingered. He shifted his weight. He looked at her hands — she was aware of him looking, aware of it the way you're aware of weather turning — and then he looked away, out at the dying market, at the lanterns going dark one by one along the rows like eyes closing. "Strange nights lately," he said. "Wardens walking in pairs. Criers being paid overtime, someone said. My girl came home talking about men in grey asking questions at the school gates. Questions about children."

"What sort of questions?"

"Fits. Dreams. Whether the little ones talk to their pets strange." He rubbed his jaw. "Probably nothing. Probably always been this way and I never stood still long enough to see it."

"Probably," Beatrix said, and put the locket in her apron pocket, and hated herself a little for how easily the small lie came, and hated the world more for making it necessary.

Marek nodded, unconvinced and trying to be, and trundled his cart off into the dark, wheels squeaking a note that the silver in Beatrix's stock sympathized with, faintly, like a dog whining at a distant siren.

* * *

She walked home the long way, because the long way passed the alley mouth.

This was the part of the night she never admitted to anyone, least of all herself: that she took the detour deliberately, that she slowed her steps at the chandler's wall, that some starved corner of her — the same corner that had gotten a girl nearly killed twenty years ago by answering a wolf's call in the Veylmar woods — leaned toward that darkness like a plant toward light.

The wolf was there. Of course the wolf was there. He was enormous, a grey vastness folded into the shadows between a rain barrel and a broken crate, and his eyes caught the last lantern light and threw it back amber, and he watched her with the unhurried attention of something that had decided, decades ago, exactly what it was waiting for.

Beatrix stopped. Just past him, not facing him, a woman pausing to fix her bootlace in an alley mouth at midnight, perfectly innocent, perfectly deniable.

"The south rider," she said quietly. It was the first time in twenty years she had spoken to him first, and her voice came out rusted. "Lathered horses. Sealed case."

*Criers by morning,* the wolf said. No triumph in it, which somehow made it worse. *I'm sorry, little merchant. I hoped I'd misread the wind.*

"A proclamation." She straightened, bootlace forgotten. "About what?"

*You know about what.* A pause, and gentleness moved through his voice like a shadow through grass. *They've started the counting, Beatrix. Lists. Registrations. Hunts. It begins the way these things always begin — politely, with paperwork — and ends the way they always end. In the Weaver's Quarter.*

Beatrix looked down the alley at him, really looked, for the first time in twenty years. He was older than she remembered allowing — grey shot through the black at his muzzle, a stiffness in his forelegs, scars mapping his flanks like the lines on Ysolt-the-mapmaker's charts. He had been waiting for her since she was nine years old and the world was a different shape. He had waited through the fall of a kingdom and the burning of a name and twenty years of knots and lies, and now here he was, delivering bad news in the dark like the oldest friend she'd never had.

"Why tell me?" she asked. "Why now, after all this time? What do you want?"

And the wolf — Sorrel, though she didn't know his name yet, though she would learn it soon enough, out beyond the walls where names mattered again — the wolf looked at her with those banked amber eyes and said the truest thing anyone had said to her in twenty years:

*Everything. I want everything. But tonight I'll settle for you hearing the crier early, and being gone before the knock comes to your door.*

Somewhere across the sleeping city, a church bell tolled one.

Beatrix Blackwood stood in the dark with a dead woman's locket in her pocket and a legend's patience in front of her, and for the first time in twenty years, she let herself think the thought whole, unbroken, all the way to its end:

*The counting has begun. And I am on the list.*

Behind her, far off toward the south road, a horse screamed — short, sharp, human-adjacent, the sound of a beast driven past its temper — and the wolf's ears swiveled toward it, and his lips peeled back from teeth gone yellow with age.

*Dawn's coming early,* he said. *Go home, pack light, and don't sleep deep.*

Beatrix went home. She did not sleep at all.
