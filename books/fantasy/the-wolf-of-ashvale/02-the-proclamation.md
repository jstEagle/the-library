## 2. The Proclamation

The criers came at dawn, and Beatrix knew before she heard them that the world had changed.

She knew because of the birds. The sparrows that nested in the eaves above her stall had gone quiet all at once, a silence like a held breath, and when she looked up from sorting her stock of copper bells, every bird on the lane was facing east — toward the Wardenhouse — with their heads cocked at the same angle.

Animals always heard bad news first.

Then the human noise arrived: boots on cobbles, a drumbeat, and a voice pitched to carry over rooftops, flat and official as a ledger line.

*"By order of His Majesty's Crown, let it be known throughout the city of Ashvale and all its holdings—"*

Beatrix set down the bell she was holding very carefully, as if it might go off.

*"—that the crime of beast-speaking is hereby declared a matter of state. All persons gifted in the speech of animals shall present themselves for registration at the Wardenhouse within seven days. Those who register will be recorded, marked, and permitted to live under supervision. Those who do not will be hunted."*

A pause, the drum beating twice, and then the crier moved on down Copperlane, his voice thinning with distance. *"Registered speakers will be permitted to live under supervision. Those who do not will be hunted—"*

Around her, the market woke into its ordinary morning. Fishwives arguing over eel prices. A cart of turnips backing into place. Two boys fighting over an apple core. Nobody screamed. Nobody ran. That was the horror of it — the proclamation slid into the city like water into cloth, and the cloth went on being cloth.

"Marked," said a woman at the next stall, spitting apple skin into the gutter. "You hear that? Marked. Like cattle."

"My cousin's wife says they burned three in Harrowmere," the fishwife answered. "Registered first. Then burned."

"That's not true."

"That's what she says."

They went on arguing about whether it was true, which was its own kind of prayer. Beatrix kept her eyes on her charms and her breathing slow and even, and inside her chest something old and cold uncoiled and lay very still, the way she herself had learned to lie still twenty years ago, when she was nine years old and a wolf spoke to her through a fence and her mother put a hand over her mouth and said, *never again, never where anyone can see, never.*

Her mother was dead now, and the rule lived on, and this morning the rule had grown teeth.

*Seven days.* She counted what she had. A stall of harmless charms — knots against headache, bells against nightmares, little woven horses for children who wanted luck. None of it worked, officially. All of it worked, actually, because every piece of silver and copper and bone in her stock had been spoken to, whispered awake, told to be kind to whoever carried it. It was such small magic. A charm that eased a cramp. A bell that rang sweeter. She had spent two decades telling herself that magic this small couldn't possibly matter to anyone, that no crown would ever bother burning a woman over a headache knot.

She had believed it right up until this moment.

The birds were still facing east.

* * *

She sold nothing for the first hour. The market's mood had curdled; people bought bread and herring and walked quickly, and when they passed her stall their eyes did a particular thing, a flick toward her wares and away, quick as a tongue checking a broken tooth. Everyone in Ashvale had bought a charm sometime. Everyone had felt a knot loosen or a nightmare lift and chosen not to ask why. And now everyone was doing the same arithmetic she was doing, the same cold sum: *who else knows? What have I seen? What did I say, once, drunk, careless?*

Dread didn't shout. Dread settled. It settled into the routine of the morning the way flour dust settles into everything, and by midmorning Beatrix had wrapped two knots against headache for a cooper's wife and sold a luck-horse to a child without once looking anyone fully in the face.

Marek came around noon.

He was her neighbor on the lane, four stalls down, a tinker with a broad back going soft and hands like cracked leather, patched at every knuckle. He mended pots and soldered seams and fixed kettles for half the district, and he had the habit of humming while he worked, tunelessly, a sound so constant that when he stopped humming you noticed it the way you notice weather changing. He'd held the stall next to hers since before she could remember holding it herself. He'd taught her to read solder temperatures by color. He called her *girl* though she was thirty-one, and she called him *old man* though he was fifty, and between those two insults lived twenty years of borrowed tools and shared lunches and watching out for each other's stalls on market days when one of them was sick.

He came up her lane slowly today, wiping his hands on his apron, and he didn't hum.

"Blackwood." He stopped at her stall and stood there a moment, working his jaw like a man chewing words to soften them. "You heard."

"I heard."

"You going to register?"

The question landed in the middle of her stall among the bells and knots like something dropped from a height. Beatrix kept arranging a row of woven horses that did not need arranging. "Register what? I sell charms, Marek. Knots and bells. You've watched me make them for twenty years. There's nothing in them but thread and tin."

"Mm," Marek said.

It was not an agreement. It was the sound a man makes when he has decided not to say the thing he thinks. Beatrix's hands went still.

"They're offering coin for names," he said quietly. "Not just hunting. Coin. Fifty crowns a name, my brother-in-law heard it from a guard who drinks at the Tin Crown. Fifty crowns. That's — that's a roof mended, that's a year of coal, that's—" He stopped himself, and Beatrix saw him stop himself, saw the exact place where his mind had gone ahead of his mouth and come back ashamed. "I'm saying it's dangerous times. For everyone. People will do things."

"People will do things," she repeated.

"Forget I said it." He rubbed his face with both hands, dragging the skin down, and for a moment he looked older than fifty, older than the city. "Gods, girl, forget I said any of it. I only meant—"

"I know what you meant."

He bought a charm then, which he never did. He pointed at the headache knots the way a man points when he wants the transaction over with, and she wrapped one in brown paper and took his copper and the whole exchange lasted ten seconds and felt like a funeral.

Except he didn't leave. He lingered at the edge of her stall, turning the paper packet over in his cracked fingers, and his eyes came down to her hands — she was aware of them suddenly, her own hands, ink-stained at the cuticles from marking prices, a burn scar on the left thumb from a soldering lesson he'd given her fifteen years ago — and he looked at her hands the way men look at things they are memorizing.

Or reporting.

The thought arrived whole and terrible and she killed it immediately, drowned it, buried it under twenty years of shared lunches. But it had arrived. It existed now. She would carry it.

"Marek." She made her voice light. "Your daughter. How is she?"

The change in his face was immediate and awful. Not grief exactly — fear wearing grief's clothes. "Better," he said. "The fever broke Tuesday. She's eating again."

"And the cough?"

"Gone." He swallowed. "Gone since she started carrying that bell of yours. Nell swears by it. Swears it's the bell." He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "Silly, isn't it. A bell."

"A bell," Beatrix agreed.

Their eyes met across the stall, and there it was, laid out plain between them without either of them speaking it: he knew. Had maybe always known, in the way neighbors know things — the whispers she thought were private, the way sick people got better after visiting her stall, the small impossible kindnesses that clustered around her like sparrows. He had known and said nothing for twenty years because knowing was safe when it cost nothing.

Now it cost something. Now there was a price list, and his daughter's name might be on it if anyone ever traced a fever-bell back to its maker.

"Take care of yourself, Blackwood," Marek said.

"You too, old man."

He walked back down the lane to his stall and did not start humming, and the absence of it followed Beatrix all afternoon like a draft under a door.

* * *

She closed early. She never closed early; closing early was a flag, a signal, the sort of thing a frightened person does, and she had lectured herself for years on the discipline of ordinary hours. But today she stacked her trays, tied her awning, and packed the most important of her stock — the speaking-silver, the pieces she'd whispered deepest — into a false-bottomed basket she'd built years ago and never once needed, and the fact that she owned such a basket at all told her something about how honest she'd been with herself these twenty years.

At home, in her rooms above a chandler's shop, she fed the fire and sat with her back against the wall and listened.

Rats in the walls, going about their business — she heard them the way other people heard conversation through plaster, fragments and tone. Mice in the chandler's storeroom below, aggrieved about something. Out past the lane, dogs giving their evening reports to each other, and under all of it, far off but unmistakable, the wolves.

There had always been wolves at the edge of Ashvale's hearing. The city sat on the bones of an older country — Veylmar, the name you weren't supposed to say, the kingdom that wasn't anywhere anymore — and the forests beyond the fields had held wolves for as long as anyone remembered, though no hunter ever seemed to catch one, though they never troubled the flocks, though they appeared on ridgelines at dusk like sentries who had forgotten to rotate off duty. People said they were common wolves. People said a lot of things.

One of them had been watching her stall for three nights now. She'd caught the shape of it in the alley shadows, amber-eyed, still as carved wood, and last night, half-asleep, she had heard it speak — not words, wolves didn't use words, but that pressure of meaning against the mind that was the whole of her forbidden gift, the thing no one had ever explained to her, that she'd never asked anyone to explain:

*Soon,* it had said. Or something like soon. Something patient.

She'd pulled the blanket over her head like a child and pretended very hard not to have heard it, and the pretending had taken until dawn.

Tonight, sitting against the wall with the fire ticking, she listened for it again and found nothing — the forests silent, the dogs subdued, even the rats quiet, all of Ashvale's beasts holding their tongues as if they too had heard the criers and understood that listening had become dangerous.

*Seven days.* Register and wear whatever mark they gave you, live supervised, wait for the Harrowmere fires to arrive here. Or run — with what, to where, on whose coin? Or stay hidden and hope, and hope was not a plan, hope was what her mother had died without.

And somewhere underneath all of it, quieter than the rest but growing, a third thought that she kept trying not to think:

*What if hiding was never the point? What if the wolves are waiting for something? What if they've been waiting for me?*

She banked the fire and lay down and did not sleep, and near midnight, in the gray space between waking and not, she heard it again — distant, patient, unmistakable:

*Soon.*

* * *

On the fifth day, the Wardens came to Copperlane.

Not for her — not yet, not openly. They came in ones and twos at first, gray-cloaked, unhurried, walking the market lanes with little books in their hands, stopping at stalls, asking questions in voices too low to overhear. Buying things. Making change. Being customers. By the second day there were more of them, and by the third day every trader on the lane had learned to recognize the cloaks and had developed the same twitch: the flick of eyes toward the gray figures and away, quick as a tongue checking a broken tooth.

Beatrix sold charms. Beatrix smiled. Beatrix made change and complimented children and argued cheerfully about eel prices with the fishwife, and every night she went home and practiced the thing she'd sworn off at age nine — listening — because a voice under the ice of her fear kept insisting that if the wolves were coming, she should at least know before the Wardens did.

Marek's stall stayed open. Marek hummed again, mostly. On the fourth day she brought him a meat pie and they ate standing between their stalls like always, and he talked about his daughter's lessons and the price of tin and never once met her eyes, and afterward she stood in the emptying market and admitted to herself, in the privacy of her own skull, that she did not know what Marek was going to do.

That was the worst of it. Not the Wardens, not the proclamation, not even the fires of Harrowmere. The worst was that the arithmetic of fear had entered the space between her and her oldest neighbor, and neither of them could undo it, and either of them could act on it, and love — twenty years of it, real and worn smooth as his soldering hands — offered no protection at all against what a frightened man might do for a sick child.

On the sixth day, the criers came again.

*"Let it be known that the period of grace is extended by royal mercy. Registration remains open. Also let it be known that the following persons are sought by the Crown for the crime of beast-speaking—"*

Beatrix was stacking her trays. Her hands kept stacking.

*"—and that reward is offered for information leading to their capture. Let all citizens remember: the Crown rewards loyalty, and the Crown remembers betrayal—"*

Two names. That was all, on the sixth day, in all of Ashvale — two names read out in that flat ledger voice, and the crowd in Copperlane went quiet in a new way, not the held-breath quiet of the first proclamation but a hungrier quiet, the quiet of people memorizing.

She didn't know either name. One was a drover from the south markets. One was — she went cold all over, so gradually and then so completely that the tray in her hands might have been ice — one was a name attached to a description she recognized. *A woman, middle years, sells remedies near the Tanner's Row. Speaks to rats, it's said. Feeds them from her hand.*

Old Sefa. Who had fed pigeons outside Beatrix's stall every market day for a decade, who had bought nothing ever, who had once squeezed Beatrix's wrist across the counter and said, too softly for anyone else, *I know what you are, girl, and your secret's safe with me.*

*Sefa,* Beatrix thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *Oh, Sefa. Who told them? Who—*

Across the lane, Marek was watching her. Not the Wardens. Not the crier. Her. His face was gray and still, and his hands — those cracked, capable, beloved hands — were folded on his counter over a half-mended kettle, and they were not moving at all.

Their eyes met for one full second. Neither of them looked away. Neither of them smiled.

The crier's drum rolled on down the lane, and Beatrix turned back to her trays with her heart hammering like the drum, and began, with steady hands, to count what she would take and what she must leave behind — while behind her, four stalls down, a tinker picked up his soldering iron, set it down again, and reached instead for his coat.
