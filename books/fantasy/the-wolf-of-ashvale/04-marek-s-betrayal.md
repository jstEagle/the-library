## 4. Marek's Betrayal

The charm Marek bought on Tuesday was for his daughter's fever, and it worked, and that was the problem.

Beatrix understood this the way she understood weather—dimly, helplessly, from underneath. A charm that only glittered was safe. A charm that actually drew the heat out of a sick child was a confession. She had spent twenty years calibrating her little silver trinkets to hover exactly at the line between *trinket* and *miracle*, close enough to keep customers coming back, far enough to keep her neck out of a noose. And then Marek Tuller had stood at her stall with his big cracked hands cupped around a cooling-charm like it was an egg he was afraid to crush, and said, "Beatrix, this is real."

And she had said, "It's good silver," which was the closest thing to *yes* she had ever let herself say in that market, and she had seen something move behind his eyes—not greed, not suspicion, but relief. The relief of a drowning man who has finally found the bottom of the lake with his foot.

She should have lied better. She should have sold him tin.

Three days later, the criers came back through Ashvale with fresh paper and fresh orders: all beast-speakers to be registered by month's end. All harbors of the same, named alongside them, their families listed as collateral. *Collateral.* Beatrix had read the proclamation twice where it was pasted beside the well, and both times her eye snagged on that word like a splinter.

So when Marek came to her stall on Thursday evening, alone, without his daughter, without his cart of tinkered pots, Beatrix felt the dread arrive ahead of any reason for it—the way you feel rain before the first drop lands.

"Evening," he said. He didn't buy anything. He stood there turning his cap in his hands, and he wouldn't look at the charms, and he wouldn't quite look at her either. "You heard the proclamation."

"Hard to miss it."

"They're saying there's a list already." His voice dropped. "Names written down before the purge even started. Someone in the palace wrote it years ago, they say." He swallowed. "They're saying children go on the list if the parents are named. That they take the children first, so the parents can't run."

The market noise went strange and distant around them, the way sound does underwater. Beatrix kept her hands moving among the charms because her hands needed something stupid to do. "Why are you telling me this?"

"I'm not telling you anything." He looked up then, and his eyes were red-rimmed, and wet, and terrified, and she saw everything in them at once and understood none of it yet. "I'm buying a luck charm. For my daughter. She's been poorly again."

"Marek—"

"Wrap it up." He put coins on the counter, too many, and pushed them across. "Please. Just wrap it up and don't look at me."

She wrapped it up. Her fingers were steady, which surprised her; some part of her had apparently decided that whatever was happening, it would be survived better by hands that behaved. When she handed him the little packet he took it like communion, held it against his chest, and walked away fast, shoulders hunched, not once looking back.

He never once looked back. That was what she remembered afterward, in the sewers, in the dark. Not the words. Not the coins. Just the set of his shoulders walking away from her, carrying something small and silver toward whatever he'd already decided to do.

* * *

The Wardens came at dawn.

Not quietly—that was the thing nobody told you about raids. You expected shadows and whispers, but the Wardens wanted the whole city to see. They came down Copperlane with torches lit even though the sun was rising, six riders and a wagon with iron rings bolted into its bed, and their boots on the cobbles made a sound like the city itself clearing its throat to speak your name to the executioner.

Beatrix was awake. She was always awake before dawn; hiding teaches you that. She heard the boots and lay still in her loft above the stall and did the arithmetic she'd done ten thousand times before: window, roofline, alley, drain. Weight of coin purse. Which neighbors would sell her and which would merely look away.

Then she smelled smoke, and the arithmetic stopped mattering.

They weren't just coming for her. They were burning the stalls—all along Copperlane the canvas awnings caught, and the fire moved faster than the riders, greedy, indiscriminate, eating three years of a tinker's stock in the time it took Beatrix to get her coat on. Through the loft window she saw Marek's cart, or what was left of it, the copper pots slumping in the heat like candles.

And she saw Marek himself, standing in the doorway of his shop with his daughter behind him, and a Warden officer holding a piece of paper, and Marek nodding. Nodding. Pointing—she watched him do it, watched his arm rise and his finger find her loft window like a compass needle finding north—and the officer turned, and looked up, directly at her.

There was a moment, one long suspended moment, where Beatrix stood at the window with firelight on her face and Marek Tuller looked up at her from the street below, and neither of them pretended anymore. He mouthed something. She couldn't hear it over the fire. But she could read it, because she had watched mouths say it over graves and cradles for twenty years:

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. She's seven.*

Beatrix stepped back from the window.

Later—much later, in the cold and the dripping dark—she would understand it fully, turn it over until it stopped cutting: that he had chosen his daughter, that choosing his daughter meant selling her, that he had wept while he did it, that he had bought the luck charm first because he wanted to give her something, anything, one last honest transaction between people who had liked each other. That betrayal and love can wear the same face and hold the same hand and walk away down the same burning street.

But later. First: survival.

The stairs were already glowing at the edges. She went out the window instead, onto the tiles, into air thick with ash, and ran the rooftops south the way she'd rehearsed in her head for two decades without ever believing she'd need it. Behind her someone shouted—a Warden spotting movement—and a crossbow bolt skipped off slate a hand's width from her ankle. She didn't stop. Hiding teaches you other things besides early waking. It teaches you how to run without thinking, because thinking is a luxury you purchase only after distance.

She dropped into the alley behind the tannery, where the smell covered everything, and pressed herself flat against the wall while boots thundered past the alley mouth. Three breaths. Five. Then a sound that wasn't boots.

Scratching. Small, quick, many-footed.

Rats. A dozen of them at least, pouring out of the drainage grate at the alley's end, and every single one of them stopped and turned to look at her.

Beatrix closed her eyes. Twenty years of whispering to silver, of keeping her voice below the hearing of God and crown, and here was the moment all that caution had been building toward, and it felt nothing like she'd imagined. It felt like coming home to a house she'd been locked out of.

"I need passage," she whispered. Her voice sounded enormous in the alley, though it was barely breath. "Underground. Away from the fire and the riders. I'll owe you. I don't know what I have that you'd want, but I'll owe you, and my kind pays debts."

The rats' whiskers twitched. One of them, a big scarred male missing half an ear, came forward and sat on its haunches, regarding her with black bead eyes, and she heard—not words, rats didn't do words, but meaning arriving in her mind wearing the shape of urgency and direction—

*Down. Down and west. The water runs west. Follow the running water and do not step where the floor is soft.*

"Thank you," she breathed, and meant it more than she had meant anything she'd said in years.

She pried up the grate and went down into the dark.

* * *

The sewers of Ashvale were older than the kingdom that outlawed magic, and they smelled like every century at once. Beatrix followed the running water with one hand on the slime-slick wall, the rats flowing around her feet like a dark tide, always ahead, always waiting at each junction where the tunnels forked, sitting up in the gloom like little signposts made of teeth and patience.

*Left,* they told her. *No, not left—left-left. The tunnel that smells of lime. The soldiers are above the grate at the third junction. Stay low. Move now.*

Once, passing beneath a street grate, she heard boots cross overhead and froze in the shadow of a support arch while a voice filtered down through the iron bars, muffled and official: "—saw her go up the roofline, sir, heading east—" and another voice, lower, unhurried: "Then we'll look east."

She knew that second voice. Everyone in Ashvale who'd ever watched a royal procession knew it. Corven Hale, the King's Huntsman, the man whose hounds had never lost a trail. She pressed herself against the cold stone and thought about the officer in the burning lane holding his list, and wondered idly, absurdly, whether her name was written on paper in that man's pocket right now, in ink that had dried years ago, before she was even born. *Someone wrote the list years ago,* Marek had said. The thought slid past her then, one stranger in a crowd of fears.

The rats pulled her west. Hours passed, or what felt like hours; underground there was no way to measure time except by the ache in her legs and the water getting colder. Twice the tunnel branched into places the rats refused to go, and they sat in a chittering mass at the boundary, insistent, and she obeyed them, because they knew their country better than she ever would.

It was in the widest chamber, where an old cistern opened up like a cathedral of drip-stone, that she finally stopped and let herself feel it.

Grief arrived the way the rats had—all at once, from every direction. She sat down on the wet stone edge with her arms around her knees and cried the way you cry when no one living can see you: ugly, gasping, complete. For the stall. For the twenty years of careful small work gone up in awning-fire. For Marek, standing in his doorway, pointing. For his daughter, seven years old, who would grow up knowing what her father had traded and whom he'd traded it for, and who would have to decide someday whether that made him a monster or a saint, and would probably spend her whole life deciding.

Because that was the thing Beatrix kept circling and circling like a rat in a dead-end pipe: he had loved her. That was the worst part, the part that wouldn't smooth over. If he'd hated her it would be simple. But he'd bought the luck charm. He'd overpaid. He'd pointed at her window with tears running down his face, and somewhere in his chest the arithmetic had been simple and unbearable: *one life I know against one life I made, and the one I made is seven.*

She would have done the same. That was the truth under the grief, the stone at the bottom of the cistern. Put her daughter—she had no daughter, but if she had—in the balance against anyone, anyone at all, and Beatrix Blackwood would have pointed too.

"You're crying," said a rat, in the way rats say things—which is to say, she felt the observation land softly against her mind like a moth against glass.

"I'm allowed," she said.

"The water goes west," the rat offered, after a polite pause. It was, she realized, trying to comfort her. Offering the only thing it had: direction.

Beatrix laughed—a broken, hiccupping sound that echoed strangely off the cistern walls—and wiped her face with her sleeve. "Yes," she said. "West. Where does the water come out?"

*Beyond the wall,* the rats said together, and their meaning carried an image with it: grey light, reed-beds, a drainage mouth in the foundation stones of Ashvale itself, opening onto marshland that stretched flat and empty toward the horizon. *Beyond the wall is beyond the list. Beyond the list is nowhere. Nowhere is safest.*

"Nowhere," Beatrix repeated. She got up. Her legs shook; she let them. "All right. Take me to nowhere."

They flowed ahead of her into the dark, and she followed.

* * *

She came out at dusk through the drainage mouth, low in the city's western foundations, and stood waist-deep in freezing reed-water watching the sun go down behind the walls that had held her whole life. Ashvale's towers caught the last light and burned orange, beautiful, indifferent. Somewhere in there was a burnt stall and a man who loved his daughter and a list with names on it written by someone she'd never met.

She waded north through the marsh until the city was just a smudge, until the reeds gave way to gorse and the gorse to open ground, and then she walked, because stopping felt like dying. The moon rose. The cold worked into her bones. She had no plan beyond *west* and *away*, no money beyond what was sewn into her coat hem, no family except—

Except.

She stopped. On the ridge above her, black against the blacker sky, a wolf stood watching her. Not approaching. Not hunting. Watching, with the patient attention of something that has been watching for a very long time.

Beatrix stood in the open ground with her heart hammering and did not run, because some deep animal part of her—the part that spoke to silver and rats and had never once been given a reason why—looked at that wolf and recognized it.

"You," she said aloud, to the night, to the wolf, to whatever was listening. "It's been you. In the alleys. All these years."

The wolf didn't answer. But it didn't leave.

Behind her, far off, a hound bayed—one long note rolling across the flatland, and then another answering it closer. Huntsman's hounds. Corven Hale's hounds, which had never lost a trail, following the scent of the woman who had climbed out of a sewer into the dark.

The wolf turned its head toward the sound, then back to her, and took one deliberate step down the ridge toward her.

Beatrix looked at the wolf, and at the darkness between them, and made the first choice of the rest of her life.

She started walking toward it.
