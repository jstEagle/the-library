## 5. The Huntsman

The summons came at dawn, as summons to ugly work always do.

Corven Hale was in the kennels when the Warden's courier arrived, checking the split pad on a young hound named Bracken. He liked the kennels better than his quarters—liked them better than most rooms the crown had ever put him in. Dogs didn't lie about what they smelled. Dogs didn't smile while they decided whether to ruin you.

The courier stood at the fence with a scroll sealed in grey wax and an expression of practiced indifference. "From Lord Chancellor Veywick's office. You're to read it before the hour turns."

Corvin wiped his hands on his coat, took the scroll, and did not break the seal until the man had gone. It was a habit from the border years: never let them see you flinch at the weight of what they're handing you.

He broke it by the kennel door, where the light was thin and honest.

*By order of the Crown,* it began, *the Purge of the Gifted is hereby extended to the city of Ashvale and its surrounding parishes. All persons suspected of communion with beasts—by speech, by sign, or by sorcery—are to be registered, catalogued, and delivered to the Wardens for examination. Those who resist registration are to be hunted.*

And then, beneath the formal language, the part that mattered:

*Command of the Ashvale hunt is assigned to Corven Hale, First Huntsman of the Crown. The Chancellor notes that no man alive knows the wilder roads better, and expects results commensurate with your reputation.*

Corvin read it twice. Then he folded it along its original creases, put it in his breast pocket over his heart, which felt like a joke someone else had made, and went back to Bracken's paw.

The young hound watched him work with her head tipped. She could smell something on him—he was sure of it. Fear, maybe. Or its cousin, shame.

"There now," he said, though he wasn't talking to the dog.

Twenty years ago he had been the finest huntsman the crown had, and everyone had said so, and for a while he had believed it, because believing it was easier than looking too closely at what the job actually was. He tracked people. That was the whole truth of it, stripped of the heraldry. Men who owed taxes. Women who'd said the wrong thing to the wrong lord. And once—one time, only once—a girl who whispered to silver until the silver listened back.

He had been twenty-six, full of certainty and the crown's salt, sent to Ashvale market on a rumor. He'd found nothing but a stall of charms and a child behind it—no older than twelve, all elbows and sharp eyes, selling luck-knots and fever-silver to farmers. He'd questioned her. She'd answered every question like a girl who had rehearsed her answers in front of a cracked mirror for years. Too smooth. Too careful. But there had been one moment—one—when she'd picked up a fox-charm to wrap it, and her lips had moved, just slightly, the way people move their mouths when they're saying something they don't want heard.

He'd reported a suspicion. Nothing more. The file sat in some archive gathering dust, and the girl had grown into a woman, and the woman, apparently, had kept whispering to silver for two decades right under the crown's nose.

Until three nights ago, when she'd run.

*

The briefing was held in the Warden garrison's map room, which had once been a chapel and still smelled faintly of incense under the lamp oil. Someone had nailed a map of Ashvale and its hinterlands to the wall where the altar screen used to hang, and someone else had already marked it up in red: districts to sweep, roads to close, the river fords to watch. In the corner, a single red X marked the sewer outfall where the charm-seller had surfaced and vanished.

"The escaped speaker," said Lieutenant Vessa.

She stood at the head of the table with her hands flat on the wood, and Corvin studied her the way he studied any animal he was about to work alongside: the set of the shoulders, the angle of the jaw, where the strength lived and what it was pointed at. Vessa Dray was thirty-one, ambitious in the quiet way that scared him more than ambition usually did, and loyal—not to the crown, exactly, but to the idea that order was worth whatever it cost. She had come up through the purge in the southern provinces. There were stories about those provinces. Nobody told them loudly.

"Beatrix Blackwood," Vessa continued. "Charm-stall operator, Night Market, eleven years at the same pitch. A customer reported her." Something flickered across her face—there and gone, fast enough that Corvin might have imagined it. "The report says beast-speech. Confirmed how, we don't know. The customer wouldn't say."

"Customers rarely do," Corvin said. "Guilt talks less after it's spent."

Vessa looked at him. "You have history with this one?"

The room was watching him. Six officers, two clerks, a representative from the Chancellor's office who had the soft hands and hard eyes of a man who counted other men the way merchants count coin. Corvin had learned long ago that in rooms like this, the safest lie is the boring one.

"A file from twenty years ago," he said. "A suspicion I couldn't substantiate. I wrote it up and let it die." He shrugged, a small economical movement. "If she's the same girl, she's had twenty years of practice at being nobody. That makes her dangerous in exactly one way: she'll be good at hiding."

"And we're good at finding," Vessa said.

"We are," Corvin agreed. "That's why they pay us."

Someone laughed. The Chancellor's man did not. Corvin filed that away too—the ones who don't laugh are always the ones keeping score.

They worked the details for another hour. Sweep patterns. Informant networks. A bounty posted in every parish, high enough that neighbors would start studying neighbors the way crows study a field. By the end of it, Corvin knew the shape of the machine they were building, and it was a good machine, efficient, and every gear of it turned on ordinary people deciding that safety was worth a stranger's burning.

He signed the operational order because refusing would have accomplished nothing except replacing himself with someone worse. Then he asked for the old file, and got it, and carried it back to his quarters like a stone in his pocket.

*

His quarters overlooked the training yard, and he didn't look at them, and he didn't look at the letter from his sister that had arrived last week either, sitting unopened on the desk where he could pretend he simply hadn't gotten to it. Marta wrote him twice a year. Her letters always said the same thing in different words: *Come home, Cor. The farm needs hands, not heroes.* He kept meaning to answer. He had kept meaning to answer for six years.

Instead he opened the file.

It was thin. One page of his own handwriting, younger and more confident than the hand he used now. *Subject: female, approx. 12 years. Charm vendor, Ashvale night market. Suspected unauthorized communion with beasts (silverwork). No direct observation obtained. Subject displayed unusual composure for age. Recommend continued monitoring.* And then, at the bottom, in the same hand: *Recommend NO action. Subject poses no evident threat.*

He remembered writing that last line. He remembered the clerk raising an eyebrow at it, and him saying, "She's a child selling charms, not raising armies," and the clerk shrugging and stamping it. Twenty years of dust. Somewhere in those twenty years, the girl had become a woman, and the woman had built a whole life inside the crack he'd left her, and he had thought—God help him, he had genuinely thought—that leaving the crack open had been mercy.

Now the crown was coming through it with torches.

He should have arrested her. That was the thought that arrived first, dressed up as duty, and he made himself look at it squarely the way he'd look at a snare he'd found in the woods. Arrested her at twelve. Put her in front of an examiner. The examinations involved hot iron and questions that had no right answers. She would have confessed to anything or died denying everything, and either way—

Either way she wouldn't have spent twenty years selling luck to farmers and fever-charms to mothers, whispering to silver in the dark of a market stall, alone.

Alone. He'd seen her file photograph—or rather the charcoal sketch they'd made of her at the raid, pulled from a witness's description. Sharp face. Careful eyes. A mouth that looked like it had forgotten how to do anything but stay closed.

He'd spent his life reading tracks, and here was one he couldn't stop reading: a woman who had hidden so long that hiding had eaten the rest of her.

And now she was running, and the machine he'd signed for that morning was designed to run her down.

*

Three days later, they caught her trail.

Or rather, Vessa's informants caught it: a tinker in the low quarter who'd sold her passage on a coal barge, a beggar who'd seen a woman fitting her description slip into the undercity through a grate by the tanneries. The reports came in clean and orderly, and Vessa laid them on the map room table like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"She's still in the city," Vessa said. "Undercity, probably. The sewers connect to the old Veylmar-era drains—they go for miles. We flood them section by section and drive her toward the surface teams."

"Flooding takes four days," Corvin said. "And the undercity people will hide her if we give them four days' warning, which flooding does, because the rats leave before the water does."

"Then we go down ourselves."

"In the dark, in tunnels built by a dead kingdom, hunting a woman who can talk to every living thing down there." He met Vessa's eyes. "I've hunted in worse. But not much."

Something moved behind her expression again—that flicker, quick as a bird crossing a window. "You have doubts, First Huntsman?"

"I have assessments. Doubts are for priests." He leaned over the map. "We go down in pairs. Quiet. No torches in the main channels—she'll hear us coming a mile off if we announce ourselves. And we take the hounds only as far as the third junction; past that the smell will drive them mad."

"You've been down there," Vessa said. Not a question.

"Once. Twenty years ago." He tapped a line on the map, an artery running north beneath the city walls. "There's an outflow here that surfaces outside the walls, in the bonefields. If she knows these drains—and if she's had help, and someone helped her out of that market alive, then she has help—she'll make for it. Everything runs north eventually. Rivers, refugees, lies."

Vessa studied him for a long moment. "Set the plan, then. Your expertise."

So he did. He drew the search pattern with a charcoal stick, section by section, pair by pair. And when he drew the northern channel, he assigned it to himself and a green corporal named Aldous who was nearsighted and nervous and would follow orders slowly, and he made sure the schedule placed them there at the very end of the sweep, hours behind everyone else.

It was sabotage, if anyone wanted to name it that. Small, deniable sabotage. He was aware, distantly, that he was doing it, the way a man is aware he's falling before he hits the ground.

He told himself it was tactics. A single hunter moves quieter than a squad. He told himself that if they flushed her toward the northern outflow, she'd walk straight into him, and he'd take her alive, and alive meant a trial instead of a summary burning, and wasn't a trial better? Wasn't that mercy too, the same mercy as the crack in the wall, twenty years ago?

He was a good liar. It was practically the same skill as being a good huntsman—reading what people wanted to hear and giving it to them. He just hadn't used to aim it at himself.

*

They went down on the fourth morning.

The undercity swallowed sound. That was the first thing Corvin relearned, boots slick on ancient stone, the dark pressing in like water. The drains had been Veylmar's once—every old tunnel in Ashvale had been Veylmar's once, the dead kingdom's bones propping up the living city—and the masons had cut them with a care that felt almost reverent. Arched ceilings. Channels worn smooth. Here and there, carved into the stone, worn nearly to nothing by centuries of water: shapes. Four-legged shapes. Wolves, maybe. Running.

He ran his thumb over one as they passed. The groove was cool and deep, and something about it snagged in him like a hook, though he couldn't have said why.

"First Huntsman." Corporal Aldous's voice bounced weirdly off the wet stone. "Signal from junction six. They've flooded sections nine through twelve. Nothing surfaced."

"Nothing would," Corvin said. "Not yet."

They pressed north. Twice, far off in the black, they heard things moving—rats, probably, or worse—but the sounds always circled them at a distance, never crossing their path, as if the dark itself had been asked, politely, to keep them moving along. Aldous didn't notice. Corvin noticed, and said nothing, and hated himself a little more with each junction.

At the third-to-last junction, where the channel forked toward the outflow grate, they stopped. Aldous checked the schedule by feel. "Sweep team's not due at the outflow till dusk," he murmured. "We're hours early. Wait here?"

Corvin looked north, into the dark where the drain bent toward daylight and the bonefields beyond.

Every instinct he owned—the instincts that had made his reputation, that had found bandits in fog and deserters in deep snow—told him the space ahead was empty. That was the problem. His instincts never lied to him, and they were telling him, clearly and calmly, that whatever had passed through this tunnel had passed recently and with purpose, and that if he waited here at this junction with a lantern and patience, he would catch his quarry.

And underneath the instincts, underneath twenty years of service and the seal still in his breast pocket, was the other thing. The memory of a twelve-year-old's careful eyes. The memory of writing *no action* in a confident young hand and calling it mercy while he walked away and forgot her name for two decades.

If he caught her, he'd be doing his duty. He'd be handing a frightened woman to examiners whose methods he knew, because he'd trained half of them himself.

"No," he said. "We push to the outflow. Slowly. If she comes through, we take her." He paused. "Alive. Unmarked. The Chancellor wants speakers examined intact, and I'll not have a bounty-mangled prisoner blamed on my sweep."

"Yes, sir." Aldous sounded relieved to have orders at all.

They reached the outflow grate an hour later. Grey daylight leaked through the bars, falling in slats across the wet stone, and beyond it Corvin could see the bonefields stretching away under a low sky—the great plain north of Ashvale where the old wars had left their leavings, tussock grass and standing stones and, if you knew where to look, the sunken ribs of Veylmar's fortifications, wearing the earth like a shroud.

Empty. Wind moving the grass in long slow waves, like something breathing under a blanket.

But there—on the mud below the grate, half-washed by seepwater—a track. Boot prints, small, light, a day old at most. And beside them, pressed deep, a print that stopped Corvin's breath.

Wolf. Enormous wolf, bigger than any hound he'd ever handled, bigger than the wolves of the eastern forests by a hand's width at least. The print was clean-edged, unhurried. A wolf walking beside a fleeing woman through the sewers of a city that had killed its last wolf two hundred years ago.

Wolves belonged to Veylmar. Everyone knew that, the way everyone knew the sky was blue—as background, as story. The wolves had been Veylmar's guardians, the songs said, and when Veylmar fell, they'd gone wherever dead kingdoms go. Children's tales. Nursemaids' nonsense.

Corvin crouched and fit his palm inside the print, an old habit for measuring. It swallowed his hand.

"Sir?" Aldous whispered. "Is that—"

"It's a dog. Somebody's big dog." Corvin stood, and heard his own voice come out flat and official, and marveled at how easily it did it. "Escaped from a drover, likely. Note it in the log as such."

Aldous blinked. "Note it as a dog, sir?"

"You want to write *legendary wolf escorts fugitive through imperial sewers* in a crown document?" Corvin raised an eyebrow. "The Chancellor's man will have you reassigned to counting fish barrels in Salt Harbor."

That got a nervous laugh. Aldous wrote *large feral dog, possible drover stock* in the log, and Corvin watched him do it, and felt the lie settle into place around him like a well-fitted harness. Comfortable. Adjustable. Easy to forget you were wearing it, until you tried to take it off.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape, standing in the slatted grey light with a wolf-track swallowing his palm: he had lied twice today. Once to a corporal about a dog, and once, hours earlier, to a map room full of officers, when he'd drawn a sweep schedule with a hole in it shaped exactly like a fleeing woman. Two small lies. Deniable, forgettable, the kind of lies that dissolve into routine.

But he knew how tracks worked. He'd spent his whole life reading them, and a track was nothing but a story the ground told about where someone had been and where they were going. Two lies made a trail. Enough trails, and even a blind man could follow you home.

And somewhere out in that breathing grass, a woman who whispered to silver was running north with a wolf out of legend beside her, toward a country that was supposed to be dead, and Corven Hale—First Huntsman of the Crown, commander of the Ashvale purge, author of the operational order—stood at the mouth of her escape route with his hand on the grate, and did not raise the alarm.

"Dusk soon," he said instead. "Team's due within the hour. We'll hold position here and report the trail goes cold at the fork." He turned back into the dark, away from the daylight and the bonefields and the direction everything runs eventually. "Log it: subject lost in the northern channels. Heavy seepwater. No viable track."

"Yes, sir." Aldous scratched the charcoal against the page.

*No viable track.* Corvin walked back into the tunnel dark, and the carved wolves slid past overhead, worn smooth, still running after two hundred years, going wherever dead kingdoms go—which, he was beginning to suspect, was nowhere at all. They stayed right where they were. They waited under the living world, patient as stone, for somebody to remember they were there.

In his breast pocket, over his heart, the operational order rustled when he breathed.

He was going to need to write his first report tonight. He already knew how it would start: *Pursuit conducted per schedule. Trail terminated at junction fourteen.*

He already knew it would be the best-written lie of his career, and the first one he'd sign without flinching, and the first one that would keep him up anyway.

Behind them, far off in the black of the drains, something moved once—a soft, deliberate sound, like a door closing, or like a wolf setting its paw down carefully so as not to wake the city above—and then the undercity was silent, and the hunt went on without a quarry, and somewhere north, in the bonefields, in the grass, in the country that was supposed to be dead, a door was waiting to be found.
