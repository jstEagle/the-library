## 6. The Child in the Wall

The rats came first.

They always did, down here. Beatrix had learned that much in three days of living beneath Ashvale: the undercity had a rhythm, and the rhythm was vermin. They poured along the old aqueduct ledges at dusk like a gray tide, and you learned to sleep through the sound of their claws on wet stone or you didn't sleep at all.

But tonight they stopped.

She felt it before she understood it—the way you feel a door open in winter before you see it. The tide of small bodies froze mid-flow, hundreds of them, and every whiskered face turned east. Toward her. Or past her. It was hard to tell which, and both possibilities were bad.

Beatrix sat up slowly on her bedroll of sacking, one hand drifting to the knife she'd stolen from a Warden supply cache two nights ago. She'd never used a knife on anything but bread. She held it anyway.

"Hello?" she said softly.

The rats didn't answer. Rats never answered her—not properly. She could *ask* them things, and sometimes they'd do what she asked if she made her voice small and sweet and full of want, but they couldn't talk back the way the silver talked, or the way—

*Don't,* she told herself. *Not even in your own head.*

Then she heard the crying.

It was thin and ragged and coming from somewhere behind the collapsed archway at the end of the cistern gallery, and every rat within fifty paces was facing it. Not fleeing from it. Facing it. Like an audience.

Beatrix knew what that meant. She'd known since the night Marek's charm had gone cold in her palm and the Wardens' torches had bloomed above the market stalls like terrible flowers. She'd known when she'd begged passage from the sewer-rats and they'd carried word ahead of her, tunnel to tunnel, so that doors of rubble opened for her all the way down into the dark.

A gift, waking in someone new.

*Keep walking,* said the sensible part of her, the part that had kept her alive for twenty years by selling charms that worked only because she whispered to the metal in them, by lying small and often, by pretending not to hear the wolf in the alleys. *You are one woman with a knife and no name. You cannot afford—*

The crying hitched. Stopped. Started again, weaker.

She was moving before she finished deciding not to.

* * *

The archway had come down years ago; moss had stitched itself over the stones like a bandage. Beatrix squeezed through the gap sideways, knife first, and found herself in a chamber that must once have been something grand—a shrine, maybe, from before the Purges, from before Veylmar became a word mothers hushed. Candles had burned here recently; wax pooled in the hollows of a broken saint. And in the corner where two walls met, wrapped in a blanket that might once have been red, was a child.

Eight years old, perhaps. Filthy. Hollow-cheeked in the way of children who'd been hungry long enough to stop complaining about it. Big dark eyes fixed on her with a ferocity that would have been funny if it weren't so heartbreaking: the look of something small that has decided to be dangerous.

"Stay back," the child said. Voice cracking between registers. "I've got—I've got animals. I've got *lots* of animals."

"I know," Beatrix said. "I saw your rats."

The child blinked. "Those aren't mine. Those are—" A frown, working something out. "I think those are everybody's."

Beatrix lowered herself carefully to sit against the opposite wall, putting a full candle-length of distance between them. Her knees were grateful. Three days of running will do that. "What's your name?"

Silence. The child studied her the way she'd once studied Warden patrols—counting exits, weighing threats.

"Pip," the child said finally. No surname offered, which meant there wasn't one, which meant the story underneath was exactly as ugly as Beatrix guessed.

"Pip. I'm—"

"Don't tell me." Pip's chin came up. "Names are how they find you. Marek said."

The name landed like a stone dropped into still water. Beatrix kept her face very still, because she had twenty years of practice at that, and inside she was falling.

*Marek.* Kind, slow-smiling Marek who mended kettles and overpaid for luck-charms he didn't believe in. Marek whose hands had lingered on hers three nights ago while the criers shouted the Crown's Purge through the market, and whose eyes had been wet, and she'd thought—he'd bought a charm for his daughter, she'd thought he was only afraid.

He had been afraid. That was the whole arithmetic of it, she was learning. Fear plus love equaled betrayal, and the answer always came out the same.

"Marek sent you down here," she said quietly.

"He said go deep and don't light fires and don't tell anyone my name except—" Pip's voice wobbled. "Except if I found a lady who talks to animals. He said she'd know what I am. He said she'd help." The ferocity cracked, and underneath it was just an eight-year-old in a red blanket in a dead shrine, three days alone in the dark. "Are you her?"

Every instinct Beatrix owned said *no*. Said *you know what happens to people near you now*. Said *the Wardens pulled apart your stall board by board and put your neighbor's shop to the torch and you owe the world nothing, least of all another mouth to run with.*

"Yes," said her mouth.

* * *

Pip's story came out in pieces, over shared dried fish and the last of Beatrix's water, and each piece fit the shape Beatrix had already guessed. Parents gone—fever, then the parish, then the street. Marek had fed the child for a year off his tinker's cart, never asking why dogs and pigeons went strange and still whenever Pip passed. Then the proclamation, and Marek's face going gray, and the whispered instructions, and the purse of coins pressed into small grimy hands with *go, go now, don't look back.*

"They came to his shop," Pip said, staring into the candle. "The Wardens. I watched from the drain. His daughter was crying." A pause. "Did he give me up?"

"No," Beatrix said, and found she meant it. "He gave up me."

"That's worse."

"It's better," she said. "For him. For her." She turned the fish bone over in her fingers. "That's how it works now. Everyone's trading everyone."

Pip looked at her with those enormous eyes. "Are you going to trade me?"

And there it was. The question, laid out plain, the way children lay things out because no one has taught them yet to dress a question in kindness.

"I don't know," Beatrix said honestly, because lying to this child suddenly seemed obscene. "I hope not."

Pip nodded slowly, as if that were a reasonable answer, which after everything it probably was.

It was later—much later, the candles burned to stubs, the child asleep with the fierce total collapse of the young—that Beatrix heard it.

At first she thought it was the water dripping in the old aqueduct. Then she thought it was wind. Then she understood that the sound had *words* in it, faint and far away, and that every rat in the undercity had risen onto its hind legs at once, and every bat hanging in the vaults above had opened its wings without flying, and somewhere beyond the stone, in the world above, every dog in Ashvale was lifting its head.

They were all looking east. All facing the same point.

Facing Pip.

The child slept on, small chest rising and falling, and the pull rolled off Pip in waves Beatrix could almost see—like heat shimmer, like the moment before lightning. Every animal within streets turned to look, and Beatrix understood with a sick lurch that *looked* was the wrong word.

They were listening. Something in the child was calling, calling without words, calling down into whatever buried country beasts kept their souls in—and the country was answering.

*I was never this loud,* she thought, and the thought terrified her, because it implied a scale. Twenty years she'd hidden her whisper-gift under charms and silence, and she had never once made a dog turn its head from across the city. Whatever woke in Pip was not a whisper.

It was a bell.

And bells get heard.

* * *

She spent the next day doing the hardest thing she'd done since the sewers: nothing. She sat in the ruined shrine and watched Pip and thought about leaving about forty separate times, and each time she got as far as standing up, the child would say something like—

"You're leaving."

"—I'm stretching my legs, Pip."

"You walk to the archway when you're leaving. You've walked to the archway four times."

—or something like:

"The pigeons brought you a ribbon. I didn't ask them to. They just *like* you now. Everything likes you. Is that what it's like being you?"

"—No," Beatrix said, with more feeling than the question deserved. "Nothing likes me, Pip. That's rather the point of me."

—and she would sit back down.

Because here was the truth she kept circling: she knew what waited for a gifted child the Crown caught. She'd read the proclamation twice through the fear, memorized it the way you memorize the shape of a knife. Registration, then assessment, then *disposition*, and everyone in Ashvale knew what disposition meant even though the criers used such gentle words. There were eight-year-olds on the burning lists. She'd seen the lists.

And here was the other truth, the smaller, meaner one: taking Pip meant being followed. Not by trackers—by *everything*. A child whose waking gift rang out across whole districts was a lighthouse in a country hunting ships. Every hound, every hawk, every rat would bend toward Pip like iron filings, and where the animals pointed, the Wardens would ride.

Taking Pip was choosing a slower death with company in it.

She looked over at the child, who was teaching a one-eared cat to sit, badly, both of them delighted anyway.

Company in it.

"Oh, I'm an idiot," Beatrix murmured, and the cat agreed.

* * *

"We leave tonight," she said at dusk. "North tunnels, then the drainage cut, then up and out past the bonefields. There's a place beyond the walls. Don't ask what place. If I'm right, we'll know when we see it."

Pip was already bundling the red blanket with the efficiency of a child who'd packed to flee before. "The wolves' place?"

Beatrix went cold. "What did you say?"

"Wolves." Pip shrugged, shouldering the bundle. "I hear them sometimes. Under everything. One of them's been calling for days—it sounds like it knows you. It says—" The child frowned, reaching for it. "It says *the door is closing*. Over and over. *The door is closing, the door is closing.*" Pip looked up. "Is that your wolf? From the alleys? Marek said you had a wolf."

"I don't *have* a wolf," Beatrix said, in the voice of someone holding a door shut against a gale. "Nobody has a wolf. Wolves are legend. Wolves are stories mothers tell—"

Above them, faint through thirty feet of stone and earth, a dog began to howl. Then another. Then a dozen, a chorus rising across the sleeping city, all of them facing east, all of them facing the same point beyond the walls.

All of them facing, Beatrix realized with her skin crawling, not Pip at all.

Facing whatever stood out there in the dark, calling a child's name in a language only beasts remembered.

Pip took her hand. Small fingers, filthy nails, absolutely certain.

"It's time to go," the child said.

Beatrix went.
