## 8. Out Through the Bonefields

The gate at the city's edge was a mouth, and we went through it like something swallowed.

I had imagined this moment a hundred times over twenty years — always in daylight, always calm, always with a plan. What I had instead was a wolf loping ahead through the dark, a child on my back with their arms locked around my throat, and the bells of Ashvale starting up behind us. Not church bells. Warden bells. Three strokes, then silence, then three again: *prey sighted, hunt called.*

"Keep to my tracks," Sorrel said over his shoulder. "The ground lies past here."

The ground did lie. Beyond the last tannery and the ditch where the city dumped its ash, the fields began — the bonefields, they were called, though no one I'd grown up with could say why. Miles of pale grass gone silver with frost, broken by ribs of white stone that jutted from the earth like the carcass of something enormous buried long ago and only partly covered. In daylight it was ugly. In moonlight it was beautiful, which felt like a trap.

Pip's breath came hot against my neck. The child weighed nothing and everything. Eight years old, all elbows and silence, and every animal for six streets had turned to watch them as we ran — cats on fences, pigeons wheeling in formation, a carthorse screaming against its harness until its owner beat it quiet. That was how the Wardens had found our trail so fast. Pip couldn't help it. The child was a lantern in a world full of moths.

"Hold on," I said, and felt small fingers tighten.

Sorrel set a pace I didn't know a living creature could hold. He ran like water finding its level, choosing low ground without seeming to look, and I understood suddenly that he wasn't running blind. He knew these fields. Knew them the way I knew my stall — the way you know a thing you've walked your whole life. Which should have been impossible, because Sorrel had never been past the walls either. But when I'd asked him, back in the undercity, *how do you know where the Door is*, he had looked at me with those amber eyes and said:

*The same way you know how to whisper to silver.*

I hadn't asked again. Some questions are doors too, and you're not ready for what's behind them.

* * *

We ran for an hour before the hounds found us.

I heard them before I saw anything — a sound like tearing cloth, far off, rhythmic. Crying, almost. Warden hounds weren't dogs exactly; everyone said so and no one said what they were. Big as calves, gray as old ice, bred in kennels beneath the citadel and fed on things I preferred not to imagine. They didn't bark. They made a sound like grief being stretched out thin, and it carried for miles, and everything that heard it wanted to run, which was rather the point.

"They've got our scent from the market," Sorrel said. "Four of them. Handlers behind, maybe half a mile."

"How long?"

"At this pace? Before the second ridge."

I looked down at Pip's hands locked under my chin, at the frost glittering on the grass, at the white stones rising ahead of us like the bones of a drowned giant. And I felt it — that thing I'd spent twenty years strangling before it could reach my tongue. It rose now the way floodwater rises, patient and total, and it said, in a voice that was mine but older:

*I am tired of hiding.*

"Beatrix." Sorrel had stopped. He turned, and his eyes caught the moon. "Whatever you're thinking — think it fast. There's a split in the stones ahead. We can lose them in there if we're quick and lucky."

"And if we're not?"

"Then we learn what you are," he said, "sooner than I'd hoped."

There was no time to ask what that meant. The grief-sound swelled behind us, closer, and I heard the handlers' whistles now too — short, sharp, human, certain. Certain the way men are certain when they've hunted children before and it has always worked.

I put Pip down behind a stone rib and crouched, and took the child's face in my hands. Frost-nipped cheeks, wide dark eyes, that terrible listening stillness Pip wore like other children wore dirt.

"Whatever happens," I whispered, "don't call anything to you. Not one bird, not one mouse. Can you be quiet inside?"

"I'm always quiet inside," Pip said. "That's the problem."

I almost laughed. I almost cried. I did neither, because the hounds crested the first ridge.

* * *

They came down the slope like spilled ink given legs.

Four of them, gray-white, huge, moving with that wrong smoothness that made my skin crawl — no joy in it, none of the loose delight of a real dog running. These ran like tools. Behind them, torchlight bobbed: three handlers, black coats, the crossed-staff badge of the Wardens catching fire-light.

And the hounds stopped.

All four, at once, thirty yards out, as if they'd struck an invisible wall. Their heads swung toward me. Their nostrils flared. And I felt it happen — felt the space between us turn into something I could press against, the way you can feel a door before you open it. Every animal I'd ever spoken to had been small. Rats in sewers. Silver in charms. A cat once, briefly, whose name I never learned. This was different. These were weapons with teeth the length of my finger, and I was about to talk to them.

My whole life I'd believed the gift was a whisper. It isn't. It's a hand, and it goes into another creature, and whatever you touch in there, you don't get back.

"Beatrix," Sorrel said quietly, at my shoulder. "You don't have to."

"Yes," I said. "I do."

I opened my mouth and let the flood through.

What came out wasn't words, or wasn't only words. It was a pouring — scent and sound and meaning braided together, thrown across the frozen grass like a net. I told them the trail ended here. I told them the quarry had gone into the air, into the moon, dissolved. I told them the smell of Ashvale was behind them and home was behind them and the warm kennels and the meat and the dark were behind them, and ahead there was nothing but cold and a woman who smelled of iron and fear and *wrongness*, and wrongness was a wall, and walls were not for hounds.

I felt them take it. All four minds at once, blunt and gray and hungry, and I pushed, and they —

— turned.

Not slowly. All at once, like weather changing. They wheeled and flowed back up the slope toward their handlers, crying that stretched-thin grief-sound, and the torches scattered, and men shouted, and somewhere a whistle blew three sharp notes that meant *false trail* or *quarry lost* or *the world has come undone*, and the hounds began casting in circles, hunting a scent that led straight up into the sky.

It worked.

God forgive me, it worked, and it was the most exhilarating thing I have ever done. Twenty years of whispers to silver, twenty years of pretending my hands were just hands, and now I had reached into four great beasts and bent them like green wood, and they had *obeyed*, and every nerve in my body sang with it. I stood in the frost with my arms out and my heart going like a hammer and I thought: *this is what I am. This is what I was for.*

Then I tried to let go, and found I couldn't.

Not entirely. Something of them stayed in me — the hunger, the gray patience, the sense of the world as nothing but scent-lines and fear-lines radiating from my own body. I could feel the handlers now, faintly, the way you feel a tooth you've lost. I could feel the hounds' confusion as if it were mine, because part of it was. I had left a door open inside myself and something had come through it, and it was standing in a room in my chest looking around, taking notes.

"Sorrel," I said. My voice came out wrong. Flattened. "Something came back with me."

He was watching me with an expression I'd never seen on a wolf — something between awe and sorrow. "I know," he said. "I can smell it. You gave them a piece of yourself to make them believe you."

"Can I take it back?"

"No." He said it simply, without cruelty. "Nothing given to a wild mind comes home whole. That's the price. That's why the old ones were careful. That's why—" He stopped himself, ears flattening, and I understood he'd been about to say something about my mother, or Veylmar, or both, and had decided the bonefields at midnight, with handlers half a mile off and regrouping, was not the hour.

"Why the purge?" I asked instead. "If people like me used to be careful — if we were *wardens*, if that word means what you keep implying it means — why would the crown burn us?"

"Because someone remembers what we guarded," Sorrel said, "and wants it to stay forgotten." He turned his head north. "Walk. Talk later. The hounds will clear their heads eventually, and when they do, they'll be angry, and angry hounds don't cast circles. They come straight."

* * *

We walked most of the rest of the night, and I spent it learning the shape of what I'd done.

It sat in me like a swallowed stone. When Pip stumbled, I caught them with a hand that felt too strong, too sure, fingers knowing exactly how much force a small body could take — knowledge I'd never earned. When a nightjar burst from the grass ahead, I knew where it would fly before it flew. When Sorrel paused at a fork among the white stones and stood breathing the wind, I realized I was breathing it with him, reading it, splitting it into threads — fox, old death, rain coming, *men* — without deciding to. The hounds' craft had taken root in me overnight, grafted onto whatever I already was.

"This is what growing the gift feels like," Sorrel said when I finally told him. "Every use plants something. You don't choose what takes root."

"Then how did the old ones bear it?"

"They didn't do it alone." He glanced back at me, and there was something almost gentle in it. "A warden who works alone becomes the thing she hunts. That's the first law. Older than the Door. Older than Veylmar."

"A warden," I repeated. The word again. He kept handing it to me like a coin I hadn't earned.

"You'll understand by spring," he said, "or you won't live to."

Dawn came up the color of dishwater, and the bonefields changed. In full light the white stones lost their beauty and became what they probably were: grave-markers, hundreds of them, rows upon rows marching north, each carved with names worn nearly smooth. I stopped at one and brushed away the frost with my sleeve. The letters underneath were old — older than the crown, older than Ashvale — and the name read **MAREN BLACKWOOD**.

I stood very still.

"That's not possible," I said. "Blackwoods are from the south. My father's line. Farmers."

"Is it?" Sorrel said.

Pip touched the stone with two fingers, the way children touch things they shouldn't, and frowned. "It's humming," the child said. "Like the voice under the ice. Like the knocking." Those wide dark eyes came up to mine, unblinking. "It knows your name too. It said it before you did."

Behind us, far to the south, the grief-sound started again.

Not circling this time. Coming straight.
