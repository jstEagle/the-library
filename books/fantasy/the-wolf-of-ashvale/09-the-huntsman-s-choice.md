## 9. The Huntsman's Choice

The trail began at the sewer outfall on the Graywater side of the city, and it was a good trail—Corven Hale would have been proud of it if it had belonged to anyone else. Three sets of prints in the mud: a woman's boots, small feet that dragged a little on the left, and a child's bare toes, half-erased by rain. Beside them, pressed deep and deliberate, the print of a wolf.

He crouched and put two fingers in the mud the way he'd taught a hundred recruits to do, back when teaching was still allowed to mean something. The wolf's stride was long. Unhurried. Whatever had come out of that drain hadn't been running.

*Good,* he thought, and hated himself for thinking it.

Behind him, the troop waited in the drizzle, thirty riders and their hounds, breath steaming. Vessa sat her horse like she'd been poured into the saddle from molten iron, which as far as Corvin could tell was how she did everything. She'd been his lieutenant for six years. She'd followed him into the bonefields twice, into the fen-country once, into a burning granary to pull out a warden nobody else would go near. If treason had a face, he'd have wanted it to be hers, so at least it would be familiar.

"Read it to me," she said.

He read it. Woman, child, wolf, heading north-northwest along the Graywater toward the old drover roads. He kept his voice flat and professional and did not mention that his hands were not entirely steady, because the mud was cold and he was fifty-one years old and that was the whole of the excuse he intended to make.

"And?" Vessa said.

"And it's a market thief with a stolen dog," Corvin said, standing, wiping his fingers on his cloak. "The charm-seller ran the moment the Wardens knocked. Panicked people run toward the river because rivers look like escape. She'll follow the water until she figures out it doesn't lead anywhere, then she'll double back for the south gate and the carter roads." He pointed east, where there was nothing but wet grass and a shepherd's track that hadn't seen a sheep since the war. "We take the high ground here and cut her off before Ashford bridge."

It was a plausible lie. That was the trick of it—he'd learned this much already, in one night of lying—plausible lies are just true reports with the courage taken out. A panicked fugitive *would* follow the river. A huntsman *would* cut the angle. Every piece of it was sound except the destination, and the destination was the only part that mattered.

Vessa looked at him a moment longer than she needed to. Rain ran off the brim of her hood.

"You're sure?"

"I've been reading trails since before you could ride," Corvin said. "I'm sure."

She turned her horse and gave the order, and thirty riders went east into the rain after a woman who had gone north, and Corven Hale stood alone over the tracks for three more seconds, committing them to memory—the drag on the left boot, the child's small stride, the wolf's unhurried gait—and then he mounted up and rode east too, because a man who lingers over a trail he intends to misread is a man asking to be read himself.

***

Here is what no one tells you about treason: it doesn't arrive. It accrues.

Twenty years ago, Corvin Hale had stood in a night market very much like the one they'd raided last week, watching a girl with ink-stained fingers sell charms that shouldn't have worked, and he had known—they always know, the gifted can't help leaving fingerprints on the world—and he had let her walk away. He told himself it was mercy. It wasn't. It was tiredness. It was the end of a long shift and the thought of paperwork and the small human cowardice of a man who didn't want to watch a nineteen-year-old burn on a Tuesday.

He wrote her description down anyway. That was the job. You wrote everything down; the Crown decided later what the words were worth.

He'd forgotten her name within a season. He'd never forgotten the hands.

And now here he was, twenty years and one purge later, sitting his horse in the rain on the wrong road, having just sent thirty armed men chasing weather, and the arithmetic of it would not sit still in his chest. Because the order had come down in the spring, sealed with the privy seal, and it had said: *register all beast-speakers; those unregistered are to be taken alive where practicable.* And Corvin Hale had read that order and signed for it and begun making lists, because that was the job, and the lists had names on them, and the names had faces, and somewhere between the third name and the thirtieth he had stopped seeing the faces, because that is also the job, and the job is very good at teaching you how not to see.

Then he'd walked into a burned-out stall in the night market and found a scrap of silver wire in the ash, bent into the shape of a hare, and he'd picked it up and it had been warm. Not fire-warm. Hand-warm. The warmth of something that had been spoken to, recently, by someone who loved it enough to lie to it.

He'd stood in that ash holding a dead woman's charm and understood, all at once and far too late, exactly what kind of man the job had spent twenty years teaching him to be.

So: treason. Accruing. One misread trail at a time.

***

They made Ashford bridge by dusk and found, as he'd known they would find, nothing. Vessa didn't rage. That was the worst of her. She dismounted, walked the bridge end to end, studied the toll-house, studied the mud, and then came back and said, quietly, in front of the men:

"The trail went cold at the outfall. We lost it there or we never had it."

"We had it," Corvin said. "Weather took it."

"Maybe." She looked at him across the bridge rail, and there was something in her face he couldn't name—not suspicion, not yet, but the space where suspicion would go if it ever decided to move in. "Or maybe the best tracker in the kingdom looked at a wolf-print and saw what he wanted to see."

"He didn't see anything," Corvin said. "There was no wolf."

"There was a wolf-print."

"A big dog."

"Captain." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I've hunted beside you six years. I know what your certainty looks like when it's real. This wasn't it."

He should have lied better. He knew that even as the words left him. Instead he said the only thing he had: "If I'm wrong, we lose a day. If you're right—if I'm doing what you think I'm doing—then it won't matter what either of us says, because you'll prove it, and I'll hang, and you'll get my commission. So ask yourself whether you want to be right."

It was a cruel thing to say to a loyal officer, and it worked, which made it crueller. Vessa went quiet. The troop made cold camp under the bridge arches, and Corvin lay awake on his cloak listening to the hounds shift and whine, and somewhere past midnight he heard one of them lift its head and point, once, north, at nothing any man could smell—and he got up and walked among the dogs and spoke to them low, the way you calm animals, and told himself that was all it was.

But the hound had smelled the truth. North. And Corvin Hale lay back down and stared at the bridge stones and understood that he had perhaps two days before the hunt stopped believing him, and that everything after those two days would be chosen, not drifted into. Treason accrued. Then, all at once, it was committed.

***

He sent Vessa south the next morning.

This was the delicate part, and he handled it the way you handle all delicate things in the Warden service: with paperwork. There was a report due—a formality, a census of registered speakers in the river villages, overdue since midsummer, the kind of task that rots in everyone's queue until someone senior gets embarrassed. He assigned it to her. He assigned it warmly, publicly, with praise: *"Lieutenant Vessa has the sharpest eye for detail in this company; I want this done properly."* Two days' ride south. Clean. Deniable. She'd hate it and she'd do it, because she was Vessa, and by the time she finished being insulted and thorough, the trail would be two days deader and he'd have his window.

What he did not say, in front of the men, was that the river villages were nowhere near the fugitives and he knew it, and that sending his best officer to count farmers while the actual quarry slipped north was not diligence. It was surgery. He was learning to do surgery on his own conscience, and the terrifying thing, the thing he would carry into whatever cell or grave they eventually put him in, was how *good* he was getting at it.

The men noticed nothing. Men notice nothing; that's why officers exist. He split the troop—ten to sweep the drover roads at a pace calculated to find nothing, ten back to Ashvale to process the market arrests, and he took the last ten north himself, openly, officially, because the fugitive might double back, mightn't she, and someone reliable ought to cover the northern approaches.

Reliable. He almost laughed. Ten men rode north behind the most unreliable man in the kingdom, and every one of them trusted him with their lives, and he was going to spend that trust like a drunkard spends wages, and there was no version of the coming months in which some of them didn't understand, finally, what he'd done with it.

He'd stopped pretending there was. That was the other thing no one told you about treason: past a certain point, you stop planning how to avoid hurting people and start choosing *which* people. He chose, that morning, without ceremony, in the rain: he chose the woman with the dragging boot and the barefoot child and the wolf, whoever they were to each other, over ten men whose names he knew and whose funerals he could already picture.

He rode north and tried to feel like a traitor. What he felt instead was awake. First time in years.

***

He found them on the third day, or rather he found where they had been, and it was worse than finding them.

A shepherd's bothy above the high pasture, cold hearth, two days old. Inside: signs of three sleepers, no violence, food taken sparingly from the croft below and—this was the detail that undid him—payment left. Coins on the table, exact to the penny, weighted under a smooth stone. A carved wooden bird beside them, crude, a child's work, left as if coins alone weren't enough.

Thieves don't pay. Fugitives don't pay. People running for their lives from the Crown's Wardens do not stop to leave exact change and a toy.

He stood in that bothy a long time, holding the little wooden bird, and the rain came in under the door and the light went grey and then dark, and somewhere in there Corven Hale admitted the thing he'd been circling for three days: he wasn't sabotaging a hunt. He was escorting a family. He just hadn't been asked, and they didn't know it, and the difference between those things was going to kill somebody eventually.

His ten men found the same bothy an hour behind him and read it the way soldiers read: *they're scared, they're careful, they're close.* One of them, a young corporal named Dree with a hunter's patience, found the actual trail leading out the back—north, toward the old wall country, toward the borderlands, toward the one place in the map with a legend on it that everyone pretended was a joke.

*"Door of Ashvale,"* Dree said, tracing it with a dirty finger. *"Past the wall country. My gran used to sing about it. Door opens in the long winter, closes forever when the snow stops. Nursemaid nonsense."*

"Nursemaid nonsense," Corvin agreed, and watched the boy's finger on the map, and felt the floor of the world tilt, because the snow had started falling out of season two nights ago, first snow in a hundred years to fall before harvest, and every village they'd passed through had been talking about it in the low frightened voice people use for omens.

Nursemaid nonsense. Long winter. A door that closes forever.

He looked at the map a long time after the men slept. Then he took the pencil and amended the route report in his own hand—the one that would go back to Ashvale, the one Vessa would eventually read, the one that would decide where thirty riders and their hounds went next. He moved the search grid west. Away from the wall country. Away from the door. He wrote a justification so airtight that a tribunal could have eaten off it: terrain, supply lines, probability of crossing points.

One small lie. Neat. Complete. Filed.

He set the report aside to send with the morning rider, and lay down, and slept better than he had in twenty years, which frightened him more than waking would have.

***

Vessa came back early.

Of course she did. He'd known she would—known it the way you know a storm is behind a hill. She finished the census in a day and a half instead of two, because she was Vessa, and rode through the night, and walked into his camp at the bothy at dawn with mud to the knee and a question already loaded behind her eyes.

"West," she said, reading the amended report over his shoulder without asking. "You moved the grid west."

"Terrain," he said.

"Show me the terrain."

He showed her. He was good at it now—that was the horror, he was *good*—and she listened, and nodded in the right places, and then she walked the bothy, and read the table, and picked up the wooden bird, and turned it over in her strong careful fingers for a long time.

"They paid," she said.

"Yes."

"Full amount. Plus a toy." She set the bird down precisely where it had been, aligned with the table edge, and Corvin understood that she was not being tidy; she was building a case, and tidiness was evidence. "Scared people don't pay. Running people don't pay. Someone taught this child to leave payment, which means someone taught this child there are rules even when you're terrified, which means—" she turned, faced him, rain still dripping off her, eyes flat and bright as coin, "—these aren't thieves, Captain. These are *good* people. Good people running north, in the first out-of-season snow in a century, toward nursemaid nonsense. And you've moved the entire hunt west."

"Probability," Corvin said.

"Say it isn't what it looks like," she said. "Say it plainly, Captain, and I'll saddle up and ride west today and never think on it again. Say it."

The camp was waking around them. Men coughing, hounds stirring, the fire being fed. Any moment now one of them would look over and see their captain and their lieutenant standing toe to toe in the rain, and whatever happened next would happen in front of witnesses, and Corvin Hale, who had spent three weeks learning to commit treason in increments small enough to swallow, discovered that the increments had run out.

He could lie. He was good at it now. God help him, he was *good.*

Instead he heard himself say: "The snow's wrong, Vessa. You felt it too. Everyone feels it. And there's a child out there with no shoes walking toward a door out of a nursery rhyme, and I have spent twenty years doing what the seal on the orders tells me, and I am telling you—I am *asking* you—give me until the snow stops. Give me that much. Then do whatever you have to do."

The silence went on so long the fire cracked twice.

Vessa looked at him, and he watched her do the arithmetic he'd been doing for three weeks—the same sums, the same terrible ledger—and he saw her arrive, ahead of schedule, at the page where the numbers stop balancing. He saw her choose, for now, not to say it aloud.

"Until the snow stops," she said. "Then I file what I've seen. All of it."

"That's fair."

"It isn't," she said. "Nothing about this is fair. That's rather the point of it." She turned away, then stopped, her back to him, voice pitched under the wind. "The bird, Captain. Whoever carved that bird loves that child very much. Remember that, whichever side of this you land on."

She walked to her horse. Corvin stood in the rain beside his falsified report and understood that he had just confessed, that she knew, that the clock was running, and that the only road left led north—past the wall country, into the deepening snow, toward a door out of a nursery rhyme and the woman with the dragging boot and the barefoot child and the wolf.

Treason, he thought. Committed. All of it, at once, in the open, in front of the only witness who mattered.

It felt, appallingly, like putting down a weight.

He saddled his horse. Behind him, ten men rose and followed without question, because they trusted him, and ahead the snow was falling harder now, thick and silent and wrong, falling like a curtain coming down on one country and rising, if the nursemaids were right, on another.

North, then. While the snow still fell. Before it stopped.
