## 10. The Village That Isn't There

Pip knows the way the way you know a song you've never heard sung.

That's the only honest way to put it, and Pip has learned — eight years old and three days on the run — that honest is the coin that buys Beatrix's trust. So when the trees thin and the snow deepens and Sorrel stops at a frozen stream with his ears flat like he's listening to something under the world, Pip says it plain:

"It's left. Past the dead oak. Two more valleys."

Beatrix crouches in the snow, hood back, frost in her dark hair, and looks at Pip the way she always does — like she's checking for cracks.

"Two more valleys of what?"

"I don't know." Pip kicks at the ice. "But I know where. It's like — like when you know there's bread in the pantry even though the door's shut. I can just *feel* the village. It's warm."

"There's no village on any map I've ever seen," Beatrix says. "And I've seen most of them."

"That's why they don't get found," Pip says, and then, because honesty has limits: "Please. My feet hurt."

* * *

The thing about walking north with a wolf the size of a pony and a woman who talks to animals is that people assume you're the strange one. Pip has gotten used to it. Sorrel carries their bundle without being asked. Beatrix counts out the food every morning like a miser counting coins, and gives Pip the bigger half anyway, and pretends not to notice that Pip notices.

What Pip hasn't gotten used to is the listening.

It started in the undercity, before Beatrix found them — before the wall, before the rats, before everything. Every animal within streets would turn to look at Pip, all at once, like a tide going wrong. Pigeons on rooftops. Dogs behind fences. Once, horrifyingly, a horse mid-funeral procession, which got Pip chased three districts by an angry priest.

Out here it's worse. Or better. Pip hasn't decided.

Because now the animals don't just look at Pip. Now they *point*. Crows lift off a pine and wheel north. A fox crosses their path and sits, waiting, until they've passed, then trots ahead like an escort. And under it all — under the wind and the snow and Sorrel's breathing — there's the knocking again.

*Knock. Knock.*

Like someone under the ice, patient as winter, tapping on the underside of the world.

Pip hasn't told anyone about the knocking. Not because they're hiding it, exactly. Because every time Pip opens their mouth to say it, the words go soft and strange, like the memory is wrapped in wool. *There's a voice under the ground and it says my name* sounds mad even inside your own head.

So instead Pip walks, and points, and says "left past the dead oak," and hopes.

* * *

They find the oak at dusk. It's dead the way things are dead in stories — huge and black and split down the middle like lightning got bored halfway through it, standing alone in a white field with nothing else around for miles.

Sorrel won't pass it.

He stands at its edge, hackles up, growling so low Pip feels it in their teeth rather than hears it. Then he looks at Beatrix and makes a sound Pip has never heard from him — short, questioning, almost human.

"He's asking permission," Pip says, surprised at themselves.

"Of me?"

"No." Pip frowns, trying to hold onto the knowing. "Of the tree. Like it's a door too."

Beatrix walks to the oak and lays her palm flat against the split bark. She stands there a long time. Snow gathers on her shoulders. When she turns back her face is doing something complicated that Pip can't read yet — Pip is still learning her faces; there are forty of them and she thinks nobody's counting.

"The wood remembers," she says quietly. "It's old Veylmar oak. Someone planted it here as a marker." Her jaw tightens. "A road-sign for a country that doesn't exist anymore."

"Then we're going the right way," Pip says, and steps over the root line, and Sorrel, after one last low rumble, follows.

* * *

The valley opens like a held breath let out.

Pip feels it before they see it — warmth rising through the snow, real warmth, hearth-warmth, the smell of woodsmoke and bread and, underneath it, sheep and horses and chickens, a whole living congregation of animals that all turn at once to look at Pip as they crest the ridge.

Pip braces for the tide feeling, the wrong-way pull. It doesn't come. The animals look, and then they *bow*, almost — heads dipping, bodies settling — like a room full of people recognizing royalty they'd given up on.

"They know you," Sorrel rumbles. He's stopped at Pip's shoulder, and his voice has that reverent crack in it he gets maybe once a day and hates being caught in.

"They know *something*," Pip mutters. "I'm nobody."

"You keep saying that." Sorrel's amber eye slides toward them. "The world keeps disagreeing."

Below them, tucked into the valley's elbow where the wind can't reach, lies a village. Maybe thirty houses, stone and turf, smoke rising straight in the still air. Fields — *fields*, in this snow, black earth showing through in long stripes like the cold simply forgot to finish the job here. A church tower with no cross on it, topped instead with a carved stone that Pip's eyes slide off of no matter how hard they look.

And it isn't on the map. Pip knows it isn't, because they watched Beatrix burn the edge off Ysolt's map-page in the bothy fire two nights ago, hunting Warden markers, and there was nothing but blank vellum right here. Nothing but white space and a tiny inked note in the old woman's handwriting: *the valley keeps itself.*

"Well," Beatrix says. Her hand has drifted to the knife at her belt, which is what it does when she's frightened, though she'd deny it under torture. "Either I've finally gone mad, or—"

"Or the map was telling the truth," Pip finishes. "Come on. I can smell bread. Real bread. Not ration bread."

"You," Beatrix says, "have the survival instincts of a moth."

"And you have the face of someone about to cry, so we're even."

She doesn't cry. But she takes Pip's hand as they start down, and doesn't let go, and Pip decides not to mention it, because some things you file away for later, like coins in a boot.

* * *

The village sees them coming long before they arrive. Doors open along the lane one after another, unhurried, deliberate, like a wave of small decisions. People come out and stand in front of their houses — farmers, a smith with his hammer still in his hand, children hoisted up onto fences to see — and none of them run, and none of them fetch weapons, and every single one of them looks first at Pip.

Not at the wolf. Not at the armed woman with the wanted-poster face. At Pip.

An old man near the well takes off his hat. Just — takes it off, and holds it to his chest, and bows his head, and behind him, raggedly, like a fire catching, the others do the same.

Pip stops walking. Their hand goes tight in Beatrix's.

"Why are they doing that," Pip whispers. "Make them stop. Beatrix, make them stop."

"I don't—" Beatrix's voice has gone strange and thin. "I don't think I can."

Then a woman pushes through the crowd, moving fast despite her years, and Pip understands in one glance that this is the one who runs things here — not because she's the oldest, though she may be, but because the crowd parts for her the way water parts for a keel. She's small and straight-backed, with iron-gray hair braided crownwise and a ledger-book bound in green leather tucked under one arm like she was born holding it. Her eyes are the pale gray of ice over running water, and they go from Pip to Sorrel to Beatrix and back to Pip, and something in her face does a slow, terrible folding, like paper being creased.

"Oh," the old woman says softly. "Oh, you poor little mite. You've been carrying it all this way alone, haven't you."

"I don't carry anything," Pip says. "I own one knife and half a comb."

"Not in your pockets, child." The old woman crouches — joints cracking like green sticks — to look Pip level in the eye. Up close her smell is ink and lavender and cold stone, and underneath all of it, faint as the knocking, something *old*. "In your blood. In your ears. You hear it, don't you? The knocking under the world."

Pip's whole body goes cold, colder than the snow.

"How do you know that," Pip whispers. "I never told anybody. I never told *anybody*."

"You didn't have to." She reaches out and, with a question in her eyebrows that Pip grants with a nod, tucks a loose strand of hair behind Pip's ear, gentle as Beatrix has ever been. "Some doors knock. Some doors answer. You, my dear, are the answering kind." She rises, wincing, and turns to Beatrix, and her whole manner shifts — warier now, older, sadder. "And you'll be the charm-seller. The one the Wardens burned Ashvale market for."

Beatrix's chin comes up. "You've heard of me."

"We heard the bells from sixty miles. We heard the hounds." The old woman's mouth twists. "We hear everything the crown does to your kind. We've had sixty years of practice listening." She extends a hand, gnarled and steady. "Ysolt. And before you ask — yes, this village is on no map, because we make certain of it. Maps are how kings find things. We prefer to be lost."

"Lost villages don't have bread," Pip says, because their stomach has opinions and honesty covers those too.

Ysolt laughs — a dry, startling sound like pages turning. "No. Lost villages have *stores*. Come in out of the snow, all of you. Wolf included. He looks like he's been holding a grudge against the weather for a week."

"He has," Sorrel says, and several villagers flinch and then, visibly, decide to be delighted about it.

* * *

The hall they're fed in is the biggest building in the village, low-beamed and smoky-sweet, hung with dried herbs and carved wooden charms that Pip stares at for a long time before realizing what's wrong with them.

They're not fake.

Every one of them is *working*. Pip can feel it — the same whisper-feel Beatrix puts into silver, humming from the rafters, dozens of small protections layered over the hall like a quilt. Whoever lives here has been practicing the forbidden craft openly, for generations, right under the crown's nose.

Beatrix has noticed too. She stands in the doorway with snow melting off her boots, looking up at the charms, and her face does the complicated thing again — hope and grief fighting for the same chair.

"You're gifted," she says to Ysolt. "All of you. You practice in the open."

"We practice in the open because we already died once." Ysolt sets bowls of stew in front of them, thick with barley and lamb, real meat, and Pip applies themselves to it with the focus of a siege engine. "When Veylmar fell, the crown didn't just erase the country from the maps. They erased everyone who remembered it. Speakers, wardens, singers, ledger-keepers. Anyone whose blood was tied to the old country." She sits across from Beatrix, folding her hands. "Our grandparents ran. Hid. Built a village in a valley that keeps itself, and taught their children the names, and made us swear to write them down."

"Names of what?" Beatrix asks.

"People." Ysolt's voice goes quiet. "Everyone the kingdom erased. Everyone who walked into Veylmar the day it closed and never walked out."

The fire pops. Somewhere outside a dog barks once and falls silent, listening to Pip, and Pip listens back without meaning to, and gets the sense of it saying *safe, safe, safe* like a heartbeat.

Beatrix has stopped eating. "Why?" she says. "Why keep a list of the dead for sixty years?"

"Because forgetting is how they finished the killing," Ysolt says. "A person isn't truly gone while their name is spoken. The crown understood that better than we did. It's why they outlawed your gift, charm-seller. Not because beasts are dangerous." She leans forward, and the firelight catches the pale gray of her eyes, and Pip — who hears things, who can't help hearing things — hears the old woman's heart change rhythm, steady to fast, like a drum before a march.

"Your gift was never a crime in Veylmar," Ysolt says. "It was a *post*. An office. Your family held it."

Beatrix goes very still. "My family were market-traders. Charm-menders. My mother sold luck-knots."

"Your mother," Ysolt says, "was born Blackwood."

The name lands in the hall like a dropped glass.

Beatrix doesn't move. Doesn't blink. Pip watches her hands under the table, though — watches them close slowly, knuckle by knuckle, like something being gripped hard enough to keep from shattering.

"How do you know my mother's name," Beatrix says, very evenly, which Pip has learned is the most dangerous of all her forty voices.

"Because it's written down." Ysolt lifts the green ledger from beside her stool and rests one hand flat upon its cover, and does not open it. "Page one. Line one. Sworn to the wolves of Veylmar, keepers of the threshold, wardens of the Door." Her eyes hold Beatrix's, and there's something in them that looks almost like apology, and almost like fear, and mostly like a person deciding how much truth a stranger can carry before it breaks her.

"Blackwood," Ysolt says. "For six hundred years. And the last of the line walked through the Door of Ashvale the night it fell, and did not come back."

* * *

Later — much later, after Beatrix has asked eleven questions in a row and gotten four answers and seven *when we know you better*s, after Sorrel has been fed an entire shoulder of mutton by admiring children and pretended to be above it — Pip lies on a straw pallet by the hall fire and listens.

Beatrix and Ysolt are talking low by the window. Pip could sleep. Pip should sleep. But the knocking is louder tonight than it's ever been, and the fire-warmth has loosened something, and besides — Pip has spent eight years learning that grown-ups tell you less when they think you're asleep. So Pip breathes slow and even and keeps their ears open.

"...can't tell her yet," Ysolt is saying. "You saw her face when I said the name. She's carrying too much already. If I hand her the rest of it tonight she'll bolt, or worse, she'll do something brave."

"Brave is bad?" Beatrix's voice, dry as chalk.

"With Blackwoods, brave is *terminal*. It's in the ledger. Read it someday and count."

Silence. Fire-crackle. Then Beatrix, quieter: "You knew my mother. Don't lie to me — you went pale at her name. You went pale before you said it."

A long pause. When Ysolt answers, her voice has changed, gone careful, gone young somehow, like she's stepped back fifty years to say it.

"I knew *of* her. Everyone who kept the ledgers knew of the Blackwood daughter who slipped the purge and vanished into Ashvale with a babe in her arms." Another pause, longer. "I was the one who was supposed to help her reach the Door, child. I was twenty-three years old and I missed the rendezvous by a day, and I have spent my whole life writing names of the dead wondering if hers was on my account."

Pip's heart is pounding so hard they're sure it will give them away.

"But that's not why I went pale today," Ysolt goes on, softer now, almost to herself. "I went pale because of the little one. Because the knocking answers *them*, and there's only ever been one reason a child hears the Door calling — and if what I suspect is true, then the crown isn't hunting speakers anymore at all."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean," Ysolt says, "that they're hunting a *key*. And we've been carrying it through the snow like a lost kitten."

Pip lies in the dark with the fire dying and the knocking under the world going *knock, knock, knock* — patient, gentle, unmistakably calling their name — and for the first time since the wall, since the rats, since Beatrix's hand closing around theirs on the ridge, Pip is afraid.

Outside, far off across the snow, a crow screams once and goes silent.

And in the morning, Ysolt unrolls a map that isn't on any map, and taps one gray fingertip on the white space beyond the mountains, and says: "Three days' walk. If we leave tomorrow, and if the weather holds, and if nothing follows us—

"—we reach the Door of Ashvale."
