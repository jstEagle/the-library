## 11. Ysolt's Ledger

Ysolt had buried three ledgers in her lifetime and dug up four.

The arithmetic of that was the whole history of the village-that-wasn't-there, and she had learned it the hard way, kneeling in frozen dirt at nineteen with a shovel and a name-list wrapped in oilcloth against her heart. You buried a ledger when riders came. You dug up a new one when they left. Each copy was copied from the last, and each copy lost a little — a smudged letter here, a page eaten by damp there — so that the dead of Veylmar were slowly fading even in the only place that still kept them, wearing away like carvings on old stone. Ysolt was sixty-one years old and she could no longer read every entry in her grandmother's hand. Somewhere in those worn lines was a man named Aldous Fen who'd mended shoes in the Weftmarket district of Veylmar City, and she would never again know what his face looked like, because whoever wrote his description had written *a good face, kind*, and kindness is not a face.

That was why she did not sleep well on the night the charm-seller came to her kitchen.

She sat across the table from Beatrix Blackwood and watched the woman pretend to drink tea she didn't trust, and Ysolt thought: *she doesn't know.* Twenty-some years in Ashvale selling whispers to silver, and she didn't know what she was carrying under her own skin. The girl's hands were wrong for a market witch — too steady, too sure, the hands of someone who had never once doubted that what she did was work, real work, the old work. Ysolt had seen those hands before. She'd held them, once, small and sticky with honey, while their owner laughed in a kitchen very much like this one, in a house that no longer existed anywhere but ink.

"You're staring," Beatrix said.

"I'm an old woman. Staring is one of my few remaining pleasures." Ysolt topped up the tea herself, unasked, because Beatrix hadn't touched it and politeness required the fiction to continue. "Your friend the huntsman is sleeping in my barn. The child is asleep in the loft with the goats, which tells me the child has better sense than the rest of you. And you are sitting in my kitchen deciding how much to ask me."

Beatrix set down the cup. "You knew my surname. Yesterday, when Pip introduced us, you went white as the snow. I want to know why."

"Do you." Ysolt folded her hands. Outside, the out-of-season snow was still falling, soft and steady, and every flake of it was a word she'd heard her grandmother say: *when the long winter comes again, the ledger will be needed.* She had thought it a mad old woman's poetry for fifty years. "And if the answer costs you something? If knowing is heavier than not knowing?"

"Then I'll carry it. I've been carrying things all month." A pause. "All my life, probably. I just didn't have names for any of it until now."

Ysolt looked at her for a long moment. Then she rose, went to the cold hearth, and pressed the third stone from the left above the mantel. It swung inward on oiled hinges — her grandfather's work, the only clever thing he ever built — and revealed a cavity lined with lead foil, because Warden scrying-rods hated lead the way cats hate water.

She took out the ledger.

It was not large. That was the horror of it, the thing that struck newcomers dumb when she showed them: five hundred years of a kingdom, and it fit in two hands. Vellum boards gone soft as cloth, corners rounded by generations of gripping. She carried it to the table like a priest carrying a relic, because that is exactly what it was, and set it down between them.

"What is this?" Beatrix asked.

"A census," Ysolt said. "Of the dead. Not the dead-dead — the erased. Everyone the crown struck from record when Veylmar fell. Every name pulled off every monument, out of every parish book, out of every mouth they could reach. You know what the purge laws say. *The kingdom of Veylmar is declared never to have existed. Its names are forfeit.* They burned the archives in the capital. They burned the parish rolls. They paid good coin for family Bibles." She opened the cover. "They couldn't burn this."

Beatrix didn't touch it. Her hands stayed flat on the table on either side, framing it without holding it, the way you'd frame something hot or holy. "Why not?"

"Because we ran." Ysolt turned the first page. The ink there was brown-black, iron-gall, the handwriting tall and severe. "When the Wardens came through the border villages with their lists of forbidden names, my great-grandmother Maren gathered eleven families who remembered, and they walked into the fells in winter, and half of them died, and the ones who lived made a vow: *no name goes unwritten.* That's the village you're standing in. That's why it isn't on your maps. We are not hiding from the crown, charm-seller. We are a library."

* * *

Ysolt turned pages and named the country back into being, because that was the liturgy and she had never once skipped it, not even alone.

"Halden Ostrava, warden of the eastern fords, drowned holding a bridge at Carrowmere so that nine hundred people could cross. His wife, Ilsette. Their daughters, Brigid and Small Nan, called Small Nan because her sister was taller at age four and it became permanent." Page. "The Weftmarket guild of cobblers, forty-one names. Aldous Fen among them. He hid children in his boot cellar during the last week. The records don't say what happened to him. The records don't say what happened to anyone in the last week; the man who was writing them stopped." Page. "The wolf-wardens. Do you want the wolf-wardens?"

Beatrix had gone very still. "What are wolf-wardens?"

"The ones your gift comes from." Ysolt let that land. Watched it land. Watched the woman's breath go shallow and her hands press flat against the wood as if the table might tilt. "Every speaker of beasts in the living kingdoms thinks the gift was born in them, or cursed into them, or gifted by some forest spirit their grandmother prayed to. It wasn't. It was bred. Trained. Sworn. There was an order in Veylmar — the Wardens of the Wild, though everyone just said the wolves' folk — families bound by oath to the great packs, speaking for the animals and hearing them speak back, keeping the borders between the human country and the deep country where the old things lived. Five hundred years of service. And when the kingdom fell, the crown decided the fastest way to kill a legend is to kill its memory, and the fastest way to do that—"

"—is to hunt everyone who can still talk to the things that remember it," Beatrix finished. Her voice came out scraped thin.

"Now you see the shape of it." Ysolt found the tab she'd sewn into the vellum decades ago and never expected to use. "Blackwood."

She turned to the page and watched Beatrix look at her own name.

There were many Blackwoods. That was the first thing. Three columns of them, going back past Maren's severe hand into older scripts Ysolt could parse but not fully read. *Blackwood, Serra, sworn 312, pack of the Grey Vale. Blackwood, Edmun, sworn 340, same pack, died in the flood year. Blackwood, Tamsin —* and here the entries changed character, because after a certain point the ledger stopped recording oaths and started recording flight.

"Tamsin Blackwood," Ysolt read aloud, softly, "sworn daughter of the Grey Vale pack, fled the fall of Veylmar with a child. Crossed into the living kingdoms. The trail ends." She tapped the line. "Except it doesn't, does it. Because the child had a child, somewhere in Ashvale's sprawl, and that child sold charms in a night market and pretended not to hear a wolf talking to her from the alleys, and that wolf was almost certainly one of the Grey Vale line come looking, because the Grey Vale pack swore an oath too: *we do not lose our folk.*"

"My mother," Beatrix said. It wasn't a question. It was barely a word.

"Read it yourself."

Beatrix read. Ysolt watched her lips move over the entry — *Blackwood, Lira, daughter of Tamsin, born in exile, unsworn, unclaimed* — and watched something break open behind her eyes, the particular grief of learning that a mystery you'd carried all your life had a name and a date and three lines of ink. When Beatrix finally spoke, her voice was level in the way that ice is level right before it gives.

"She never told me. Not once. Not a story, not a lullaby, nothing. I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I used to ask her where I got it. The gift. I asked when I was small enough not to know asking was dangerous. She'd just say *from nowhere, Bea, eat your supper.* From nowhere." A laugh with no humor in it at all. "From a sworn line five hundred years old. She lied to me to keep me alive, and then she got taken anyway, and the last thing she ever said to me was *don't use it, don't be found using it,* and I obeyed her for twenty years, and it bought me nothing. The Wardens came regardless. So what was it *for?* What was any of it for?"

"That," said Ysolt, "is the question the ledger exists to answer. But not tonight, and not from me."

Beatrix looked up sharply. "You just spent an hour telling me—"

"I told you the shape. I told you your name. I will not tell you the rest, because the rest is not mine to give." Ysolt closed the ledger and laid both hands flat upon it, an old gesture, a sealing gesture, and felt the familiar ache in her wrists as she did. This was the burden, and it had always been the burden: to hold the truth and ration it, because truth delivered whole to an unready heart breaks it. Maren had taught her that. *A ledger is a door, girl, and doors open at the pace of the one walking through.*

"Then who gives it?" Beatrix demanded. "You've got a library of the dead sitting on this table and you want me to walk away?"

"No. I want you to walk north."

Silence. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere upstairs a goat complained in its sleep, and Ysolt thought she heard the faint answering stir of a child's gift reaching toward it even in dreams — Pip's power leaking out of them like warmth from a badly sealed door.

"There is a place," Ysolt said, "past the bonefields, past the shepherd roads, in the high country where the maps simply stop pretending. A standing gate of black stone in a valley where the snow falls upward. My grandmother called it the Door of Ashvale. The ledger calls it the Threshold of Return. The wolves call it other things that don't translate well into human mouths." She met Beatrix's eyes. "Veylmar is not entirely gone, charm-seller. The crown erased the *records*. Records were never the country. The country went behind the Door when it fell — the people, the city, the memory of it, all of it, folded away behind standing stones to keep it from being devoured. It has been waiting a hundred years for someone with the blood and the gift to come and open it. Someone sworn. Someone whose name is in this book."

"And you think that's me."

"I think you are the first Blackwood in a hundred years to stand in my kitchen. I think the long winter has begun falling out of season, exactly as the prophecy says it does when the Door grows impatient. And I think a wolf of the old line tracked you across a city and refused to leave you, which the Grey Vale pack only does for one reason." Ysolt leaned forward. "But I won't tell you what's true beyond the Door, because I've never been through it, and secondhand truth is how lies get started. You'll get the truth from the source. Or you'll die on the road, and the ledger will gain an entry, and I'll write it honestly: *Beatrix Blackwood, unsworn, died seeking.* Both endings get recorded. That's the bargain."

"That's not a bargain," Beatrix said. "That's a shove off a cliff with a nice speech attached."

Ysolt smiled, and knew it was the smile her grandmother used to give, the one that meant *yes, exactly.* "All inheritance is. You don't choose what's handed to you. You only choose whether to catch it." She pushed the ledger across the table. "Take it."

Beatrix stared at the book and did not move. "You're giving me five hundred years of your family's work. Your life's work. You buried this thing three times."

"Four times. Dug it up four times, too — you weren't listening earlier." Ysolt kept her voice dry because if she didn't it would crack. "I'm not giving it to you. I'm lending it. There's a difference, and the difference is that I intend to follow you."

Beatrix blinked. "You're sixty years old."

"Sixty-one, and I've out-walked men half my age across the fells since before your mother was born. Someone has to carry the pen." Now her voice did crack, slightly, and she let it. "Charm-seller. Beatrix. Listen to me, because I am old and I have earned the right to say things plainly. I have spent my whole life writing down the dead. Names, dates, descriptions fading into *a good face, kind.* Do you know what I would give — what my grandmother gave, what Maren gave, dying in the snow with her book under her — for one chance to make the dead *un*-dead? To open a door and find that Aldous Fen and his boot cellar and Small Nan and all of them are not gone, only waiting?" She stood, joints protesting, and began banking the fire with the brisk motions of a woman refusing to weep in front of company. "You aren't carrying a burden north. You're carrying the only chance this village has ever had to stop being a tomb with a roof on it. So take the book. Take the girl. Take the huntsman, if he can still walk. And when you stand in front of that Door and it asks you who you are —"

The knock came at the kitchen door. Three raps, spaced wrong — not a villager's knock, not a neighbor's. Ysolt froze with a log in her hand. Beatrix was on her feet with a knife out of nowhere, silent as smoke, and Ysolt thought: *sworn line, yes, it shows.*

Sorrel's voice came through the wood, low and rough, a sound like wind moving through a hollow tree, and Ysolt's arms prickled at the sheer impossibility of it, a wolf speaking words in a farmhouse kitchen.

*"Riders,"* the wolf said. *"On the south road. Six of them. Warden grey. They're a day behind us — but they've stopped at the ridge, and they're looking down at your village, and one of them has a glass."*

Ysolt closed her eyes. Four times buried. Perhaps a fifth.

"Well," she said, setting the log down gently in the fire, "it seems the lesson portion of the evening is concluded." She turned to Beatrix, and held out the ledger one final time. "Catch."
