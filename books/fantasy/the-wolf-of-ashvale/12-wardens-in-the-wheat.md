## 12. Wardens in the Wheat

The dogs started barking an hour past noon, and every animal in the village turned its head at once.

I was hauling water from Ysolt's well when it happened—a small, terrible choreography. The hens went silent mid-scratch. A cart horse lifted its head and stared north. Old Bram's sheepdog stopped worrying a bone and stood rigid as a fence post, ears pricked toward the wheat. And Pip, sitting on the step with their knees drawn up, went white around the mouth and said, very quietly, "They're coming."

"How many?" I set down the bucket. My hands were steady. I'd spent twenty years training them to be.

Pip's eyes had gone that glassy far-off look I'd learned to dread, the look they got when the world's animals were shouting through them all at once. "Riders. Eight. Maybe nine. Horses are scared of something behind them." A pause, a swallow. "Hounds. They brought hounds."

Ysolt came out of her house with a basket of wet laundry balanced on one hip like she hadn't heard anything at all, which was a lie so practiced it looked effortless. "Cellar," she said. "Now. Both of you. Sorrel's already gone."

He had—of course he had. The wolf had vanished into the treeline before the first dog finished barking, because a wolf hiding among chickens helps no one and gets everyone burned. I hated how sensible it was. I hated more that my first instinct was to call after him, and my second was to stand in the yard and meet the riders myself, because some old stupid part of me still believed you could talk your way out of anything if you found the right words for the right creature. But Warden horses were trained deaf. That was the first thing they did to a Warden mount: they broke its hearing for anything but the rider's voice. Even I couldn't reach them.

"Beatrix." Ysolt's hand closed on my arm, grip like a root. "Cellar. You'll get us all killed standing there looking brave."

That was the thing about Ysolt. She never said *you'll die*. She said what she meant.

* * *

The cellar under Ysolt's house wasn't a proper root cellar. It was older than that—the stones fit together in courses no mason in living memory used, and the door was oak black with age, iron-banded, opening on hinges that made no sound at all, which doors don't do by accident. There was a shelf of turnips. A barrel of salted pork. A crate of apples going soft at one end. And in the corner, half-hidden under sacking, a stack of ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, which I pretended not to see and Ysolt pretended not to notice me pretending.

"Quiet now," she whispered, kneeling at the trapdoor, her face a smudge of lamplight above us. "Not quiet-like-children. Quiet-like-dead. Can you do that?"

Pip nodded. Pip was very good at quiet when it mattered; it was everything else they couldn't control. I took their hand. It was cold and damp and it gripped mine back hard enough to hurt, and I was grateful for the pain because it gave me somewhere to put the fear.

The trapdoor shut. The lamplight became a seam, then nothing.

In the dark, we listened.

At first there was only the ordinary sounds of a village absorbing visitors—the creak of the gate, voices too blurred by earth and timber to parse, a horse blowing out its breath. Then hooves on the packed-earth street, many of them, unhurried. Wardens didn't hurry. Hurrying meant the quarry might escape, and Wardens preferred their quarry to feel the leisure of the trap closing. Corvin had told me that once, back when telling me things was still a thing he did, back when he was a name attached to a report instead of a man bleeding in my memory of a frozen ford. *They'll walk the horses slow,* he'd said. *Slow means sure. Sure means nobody runs.*

Nobody ran. I could hear it—the whole village choosing stillness at once, the way a field goes still when a hawk crosses it. Doors not quite closing. Conversations dying mid-word. Somewhere a baby started to fuss and was hushed with a desperation that made my chest ache.

Then the hoofbeats stopped directly overhead.

Pip's hand crushed mine. And I felt it happen—I felt it the way you feel weather change—the child's gift waking up and reaching out, unbidden, like a shout in a library. Every animal within streets turning to look. I'd felt it in the undercity of Ashvale, rats pouring toward us in a river of whiskers; I'd felt it on the road, crows gathering over our camp like punctuation marks of doom. Pip couldn't stop it. Pip was eight years old and frightened in the dark, and fear was the bellows on that fire.

"Pip," I breathed, barely sound at all. "Look at me."

I couldn't see them. That was the worst of it—in the black, I was talking to a shape, a grip, a trembling. "Pip. Listen to my voice. Just my voice."

"They're *here*—" The whisper cracked. "The horses can smell the hounds and the hounds can smell *us*, they're saying it, they're all saying it—"

"Then say something back."

Silence. Even the trembling paused.

"You talk to them," I whispered. "It's what you do. So tell them—tell the hounds—we're not here. Tell them we're smoke. Tell them whatever lie you want. You're better at this than I ever was, do you hear me? You were born speaking it."

"I'm scared."

"So am I. Do it scared."

For three heartbeats, four, five—nothing but the dark and the distant murmur of Warden voices overhead, and then, so faint I might have invented it, Pip began to hum. Not a tune. A sound like wind through grass, like the low conversational growl of a contented dog, pitched somewhere below hearing. And I felt the pressure of their gift change direction—not reaching out anymore but *pouring outward*, smooth as spilled water, a blanket laid over the cellar, over the yard, over the whole nervous village.

Overhead, one of the hounds began to bark—and then, absurdly, stopped. I heard it whine, settle, flop down with a jingle of harness. I heard a Warden curse softly and kick dirt at it.

Pip sagged against my side, spent, humming fading to nothing. I put my arm around them and held on.

* * *

They searched for two hours.

We knew because Ysolt had told us the church bell rang the hour, and through the floorboards we caught the muffled bronze tongue of it twice. In between: boots on Ysolt's floors. Drawers opened and shut with bureaucratic patience. The cellar door itself—her kitchen door, not ours—swung open above us at one point, and lamplight leaked down the stairwell in a thin yellow blade, and a voice called down, "Woman! Your stores!"

"Come count them yourself, Captain, if your men have nothing better to do than inspect a widow's turnips." Ysolt's voice, dry as August.

Boots descended three steps. Stopped. The light swung.

I had Pip's head pressed into my shoulder, my body curved over theirs, both of us wedged behind the barrel of salt pork. Above me the turnip shelf hung in shadow. If he came down—if he brought his lamp down those stairs—he would find two fugitives curled in the dark like the last of something, and eight people in this village would hang or burn for the sheltering of us, and I would spend the rest of my short life knowing exactly whose deaths sat on my shoulders.

I thought about Marek. I thought about his hands lingering over mine at the stall, the day before he sold me, and how kindness and betrayal had worn the same face. Fear does arithmetic, I reminded myself. This captain had his own arithmetic. Let him count his turnips and go.

"Huh," said the captain, to no one. The light swung away. Boots climbed back up. The door closed.

Only afterward did I understand what had happened, and it made me laugh—one silent, shaking, tearless laugh in the dark while Pip slept against my ribs. The hounds had smelled us. Any hound worth its harness would have dragged its handler straight to that trapdoor. But Pip had told them we were smoke, and hounds believe what they're told, because hounds are honest creatures and honest creatures trust.

The crown's own dogs had lied for us. There was a sermon in that, if anyone left in Ashvale were still allowed to preach.

* * *

When Ysolt finally opened the trapdoor, evening had come down blue and cold outside, and she looked ten years older and entirely unsurprised, which I was beginning to suspect was simply her face.

"Nine riders," she reported, handing down bread and hard cheese and watered wine like a woman feeding soldiers home from war. "Captain Vessa's column. They're quartered at the mill tonight. They'll ride the north road at dawn."

"Vessa." The name went through me like a fishhook. Corvin's lieutenant. The one he'd misdirected twice, the one sharp enough to keep re-sharpening the trail he tried to blur. "She knows we passed through here. She has to."

"She suspects. Suspicion isn't proof." Ysolt sat down heavily on the cellar steps, too tired for ceremony, and broke her own loaf. "She asked who'd come through lately. I told her peddlers, a tinker, a widow's cousin from the south. She wrote it in her book." A thin smile. "Everyone keeps books. Mine's just longer."

"The villagers—"

"Did what they've practiced for twenty years, which is know nothing beautifully." She chewed, swallowed. "Old Bram told Vessa his dog's been lame all season, which is why it didn't bark at strangers, and Vessa believed him because the dog is genuinely lame and liars work best with one true thing. Tam's boy showed her his beetle collection until she threatened him. Small heroisms." She looked at me then, really looked, lamplight catching the deep lines around her eyes. "But listen to me, girl, because I'll only say this once. That child pulled wool over nine pairs of Warden eyes today, and it cost them. Look at them."

I looked. Pip was asleep sitting up, gray-faced, lips faintly blue, breathing shallow as a bird's. My heart dropped through the floor of me.

"It's like a fever after," Ysolt said. "Like a muscle torn. They'll sleep till morning and wake hungry enough to eat my whole larder, and they'll be fine. But it won't always be fine. Gifts grow, and growing hurts, and untrained gifts grow crooked. Whatever you're carrying them toward—you'd best be quick about it."

North. The Door. The word sat in my chest where I kept it, warm and terrible. "How far?"

"With the map? Nine days, if the weather holds." Ysolt's gaze slid to the window, to the sky beyond it, and something crossed her face that I didn't like at all—something old and watchful and afraid in a way that had nothing to do with Wardens. "Which it won't. Snow's coming. Early snow. Wrong snow."

"Wrong how?"

She stood, brushed crumbs from her skirt, and didn't answer, which was its own kind of answer. At the top of the stairs she paused. "Sorrel will want to move tonight. He's right, much as I hate agreeing with a wolf. Riders quartered at the mill means sentries, and sentries mean someone watching the roads at first light. Go in the dark. Take the shepherd's track west, then north through the high pasture. And Beatrix—" She reached into her bodice and pressed something into my hand, small and hard and warm from her skin. A key. Iron, old, its teeth worn nearly smooth. "There's a bothy at the high pasture's edge. Locked since my grandmother's day. Feed yourselves, rest one night, no more. And when you open that lock, whisper thank-you to it. Everything in this country likes being spoken to kindly. That's not a charm-woman's trick. That's just the truth of the world."

I closed my fist around the key. "Why are you doing all this? You've fed us and hidden us and lied to a Warden captain. Your village bled for us today in small ways. What do you think we are?"

Ysolt smiled, and for a moment the smile was young, which on her face was almost frightening. "I think you're a Blackwood," she said, "walking north in the wrong season, exactly like the ledger says somebody would someday. Now get your wolf and your child and go, before I start liking you too much to let you leave."

* * *

We slipped out at moonrise.

Sorrel materialized from the hedgerow shadows as we crossed the last fallow field, falling in beside us without a word, though his hackles stayed up and his nose kept swinging back toward the village, toward the mill, toward the sleeping hounds that had dreamed us into smoke. Pip walked between us, wrapped in Ysolt's spare shawl, still hollow-eyed with spent magic but stubbornly refusing to be carried. Halfway across the pasture the child stumbled, and Sorrel shouldered under their arm without breaking stride, and Pip rode a wolf through the frost like it was the most natural thing in the world, and maybe for them it was.

At the top of the rise I stopped and looked back.

Ashvale lay far behind us, a smudge of woodsmoke against the stars. Between here and there, the village sat dark and small in its bowl of wheat stubble, every soul inside it pretending hard, holding still, keeping our secret at a cost I hadn't asked them to pay and couldn't repay. Nine Warden riders quartered at their mill. A captain with a book and a suspicion. Snow coming early, wrong snow, out of a sky that Ysolt watched like she was reading bad news in it.

"We shouldn't have stopped here," I said quietly. To Sorrel, mostly. To myself. "Every mile we bring them closer to someone. Every village is another debt."

Sorrel looked back too, amber eyes catching the moon. When he spoke, his voice was the low rumble I'd once spent twenty years pretending not to hear. "The doe doesn't apologize to the meadow for being hunted."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one." He turned north, and the wind off the high country stirred his fur, and beneath it—faint, unmistakable, impossible in early autumn—I smelled snow. "Walk, charm-seller. The winter's already choosing its road. We should choose ours faster."

Behind us, far off, the first flakes came down through the moonlight, fat and slow and falling like ash.
