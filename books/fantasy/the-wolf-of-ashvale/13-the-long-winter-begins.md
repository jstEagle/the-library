## 13. The Long Winter Begins

The first snowflake landed on Sorrel's nose at dusk, and he knew it was wrong the way a wolf knows a trap by its smell.

It was midsummer in the lowlands. He had crossed the wheat country three days ago and it had been gold to the horizon, heavy-headed, humming with bees. Now he stood on a ridge above a valley where frost silvered every blade of grass, and the sky had gone the color of old pewter, and the snow was coming down in fat, unhurried flakes that did not melt when they touched his fur.

Snow that melts is weather. Snow that doesn't is a message.

He shook his coat and growled low in his chest, a sound meant for no one, and then—because there was no one—he let himself feel the fear.

* * *

He found them half a mile down, camped in the lee of an old boundary stone. Beatrix had built a fire too small to be seen from the road and was feeding it twigs one at a time, as if even the fire needed to be kept quiet. Pip sat cross-legged beside her, wrapped in Corvin's spare cloak, talking with their hands the way they talked about everything—the rats of the undercity, the taste of stolen plums, the shapes of clouds. The child's voice carried. It always carried. Beatrix kept glancing at the tree line without interrupting, which was its own kind of tenderness, Sorrel thought. She let the child be loud because the world had been quiet at them too long.

Corvin stood watch with his back against the stone, arms folded, doing his best impression of a man who was not a hunted traitor scanning for hunters. He was bad at it. Huntsmen made poor prey; they kept looking along the trail instead of away from it.

Sorrel came out of the trees slowly, deliberately, letting them hear him before they saw him. He had learned that much about these soft-footed people: their hearts could not bear surprise.

Beatrix saw him first. She always did. Her eyes went to him the way other folk's went to doors—to check whether it was open or shut.

"You felt it too," she said. Not a question. The wind had turned, and her breath fogged, and her face had gone careful.

"The snow," Pip said, delighted, holding up a mittened hand. "In summer! That's magic, isn't it? Real magic, the kind from stories—"

"It's not story-magic." Beatrix's voice had an edge Sorrel recognized from the market stall, from the night she'd whispered to silver while watching the alleys. Fear dressed up as authority. "Sit closer to the fire, Pip."

Pip, being eight, did not sit closer to the fire. Pip ran out into the falling snow with both arms spread and their face tipped up, mouth open, catching flakes, spinning. Every animal within a mile had already turned toward the child—Sorrel could feel it like a tide pulling, the foxes in their dens, the rooks on their wires, all of them swiveling toward Pip the way iron filings swing to a lodestone—but the child noticed none of it. The child only laughed, white breath and white snow.

Sorrel watched Beatrix watch the child, and saw her do the arithmetic she hated: *the gift that draws every eye, in a season when eyes are hunting.*

"Tell me what you know," she said to Sorrel.

He settled onto his haunches at the edge of the firelight. Words still cost him. Twenty years of silence in a city where speech between wolf and woman had been beaten out of both their bloodlines, and now each sentence was like dragging something heavy up a hill. But she waited. She always waited.

"This is not the first time I have seen snow refuse to melt," he said. "I was young. My mother's mother told of it. The long winter."

Pip stopped spinning. Even the child had heard that word somewhere—in Ysolt's kitchen, probably, where old women said things in low voices over mending, thinking children weren't listening. Children were always listening. That was the whole trouble with children.

"In the songs," Pip whispered.

"In the warnings," Sorrel corrected. "There were songs once. Then there were only warnings."

* * *

He told it the way his grandmother had told him, lying in the den among warm bodies while the world outside was still green: that Veylmar had not simply fallen, the way a wall falls, all at once and done. That it had been *closed*. That those who erased it had not been able to destroy the country itself—it was too old, too rooted, too much held together by memory and oath—so they had shut it behind a Door and let winter do the killing. And the winter was not a season. It was a sentence, pronounced slowly. It would fall and fall and fall until nothing inside remained to remember anything, and when the last snowflake of the long winter touched the ground, the Door would seal forever, and Veylmar would become exactly what the crown claimed it already was: a legend. A story. Nothing.

"And the prophecy?" Beatrix asked. She had drawn her knees up, hands laced around them, the way she sat when she was refusing to shiver. "Ysolt spoke of a prophecy. *While the winter falls, the door stands open.*"

"That part my grandmother believed," Sorrel said. "Whether it was promise or trap, I don't know. But this—" he lifted his muzzle to the gray sky, to the fat unhurried flakes— "this is the winter starting to count. And I can tell you how fast it counts. Ask your huntsman how far we've come since the village. Ask him how many days. Then ask yourself how many winters Ashvale has known."

Beatrix went very still.

"Weeks," she said finally. "It's been weeks since the village. Since Ysolt's map. And this is—" she looked at the frost on the grass, the ice skinning the edges of the stream thirty paces off, the summer wheat valley behind them that would be white by morning— "this is months of cold arriving at once. Or more."

"More," Sorrel said.

* * *

Later, when Pip slept against Beatrix's side and Corvin had taken first watch with the rigid attentiveness of a man paying a debt, Sorrel walked the perimeter alone.

That was when he felt the old country stir.

It began under his paws—a tremor too deep to be sound, more like a change in pressure, the way the air feels before lightning though the sky stays clear. He stopped at the boundary stone and set his paw flat against it, and through the stone he felt it clearly: something vast turning over beneath the snow, beneath the earth, beneath everything. Like a sleeper shifting under blankets. Like a heart that had been beating once a year suddenly remembering it used to beat every day.

The stone was old. Older than the kingdom that had raised it, older than the border it marked. Someone had cut letters into it once and someone else had chiseled them out, but Sorrel's pads read what eyes couldn't: the ghost-grooves of words in the old script, the script of Veylmar, the script his grandmother had spoken in her sleep. He couldn't read them anymore. But he knew their shape the way you know the shape of a face you loved before you understood faces.

*They are waking up,* he thought. *The dead ones. The echoes. The whole closed country is feeling the winter come, and it knows what the winter means.*

He didn't know if that was hope or horror. Both, probably. Everything true was both, lately.

Far to the north—so far his nose couldn't reach it, so far even his grandmother's stories hadn't reached it—something answered the stirring. A call, or the memory of a call, moving through the ground like a wave through a frozen lake. And underneath the call, threaded through it like hair in bread, was a smell Sorrel had hoped never to smell again: pack-scent, but wrong. Pack-scent with ice in it. Pack-scent that belonged to wolves who served the winter instead of enduring it.

His own kind. Or what his own kind had become, north of here, in the service of the thing that counted snowflakes.

Sorrel lifted his head and howled—not a challenge, not yet, just the old announcement, the one that meant *I am here, and this ground has a keeper.* The sound rolled down the valley and came back changed. Thinner. As if the snow were drinking it.

* * *

"You didn't sleep."

Corvin's voice, low, from the edge of the firelight. Sorrel hadn't heard him leave his watch, which annoyed him. Once, the man couldn't have crossed ten paces of forest without Sorrel marking him. Age, or distraction, or the particular deafness that comes from carrying guilt—you stop listening outward because you're listening inward too hard.

"Wolves don't need much," Sorrel said.

"Liar. You need more than me. I've hunted your kind twenty years; I know your sleep better than mine." Corvin crouched a few feet away, forearms on his knees, close enough to talk, far enough to be polite. They had established that distance in the first days and neither had moved it since. "What's coming?"

Sorrel considered him. This was the strange arithmetic of their company: a disgraced huntsman who had once written lists of names that killed people, asking a wolf to share intelligence. And the stranger arithmetic underneath: Sorrel trusted him anyway. Not fully—never fully—but the trust existed, growing in the cracks like grass through flagstones, and Sorrel resented every inch of it.

"The winter is the hunt," Sorrel said. "Understand that, huntsman. Your Wardens chase us with dogs and horses. The winter chases the whole world. When it finishes falling, it shuts the Door, and everything behind it dies the second death—the one after forgetting. And it has started counting early."

"Early?"

"It should have been years yet. Generations, maybe. My grandmother thought she'd never see it begin." He looked at the sleeping shape of Pip, small and dark against Beatrix's flank of cloak. "Something hurried it."

Corvin followed his gaze to the child. His jaw worked.

"You think it's Pip."

"I think the voice under the ice has been calling, and Pip hears it, and things that hear can also answer. And I think whatever waits in the north—the ice-pack, the ones who serve the cold—they've heard the answering too." Sorrel rose, shook the snow from his ruff. "Your lieutenant. Vessa. Is she still behind us?"

"A week back. Maybe less, if she's stopped being cautious." Corvin's mouth twisted. "She was never cautious."

"Then hear me plainly, because I'll say it once. We are between two hunts now, and only one of them wants prisoners."

* * *

Dawn came gray and silent, and the world had gone white.

Not deep white—not the burying kind, not yet—but total. Frost on every stalk, snow filling every hoofprint and pawprint from the night, the stream frozen bank to bank with ice that sang faintly when the wind moved over it. The wheat valley to the south, which had been gold three days ago, gleamed like bone.

Pip stood in the middle of it, breath steaming, utterly transfigured with joy.

"It's beautiful," the child announced to no one and everyone. "It's the most beautiful thing I ever saw."

And that was the terrible thing, Sorrel thought. It *was* beautiful. That was how the winter worked—that was how it had always worked, according to the old stories. It didn't come looking like doom. It came looking like the prettiest thing you'd ever seen, so that you'd stand still and admire it while it counted.

Beatrix came to stand beside him at the edge of camp. She'd slept badly; he could smell it on her, the sour tang of broken sleep and worry. In her hand she held Ysolt's map, the half-finished thing with its ink-blots and its northern margin trailing off into blank vellum, and she was studying it as if staring hard enough might make roads appear.

"How long do we have?" she asked. "Before the Door seals. Honestly."

"Honestly? I don't know. Weeks. Maybe less." He nodded north, past the ridgeline, toward mountains that were already wearing new snow above the tree line. "The Door stands in a high valley. Deep country. We move slow—we have a child, a lame wolf, a man whose wound still weeps—and the winter will get worse the further north we go, because we're walking into the throat of it."

"But it's open. While it falls, it's open. Ysolt's page said—"

"Ysolt's page said a great many things, and she died making sure you got them. Don't waste her arithmetic on hope. Spend it on miles." He softened it as much as his voice allowed. "We can make it. Wolves have crossed worse in worse. But we cannot make it slowly, and we cannot make it loudly, and we cannot make it twice."

Beatrix folded the map. Her knuckles were white around it.

"Then we walk today," she said. "All day. And tomorrow."

Behind them, Pip shrieked with laughter, sliding across the frozen stream on Corvin's steadying hand, and every animal for a mile turned to look, and far to the north—so far it was less a sound than a pressure, less a pressure than a dread—the ice-pack lifted their heads from the snow and began, at last, to move.

Sorrel heard it. Felt it. Turned his face north and bared his teeth at the white horizon.

*Run, little company,* he thought. *The clock has started.*
