## 14. Northward

The map was a lie before it was finished.

Ysolt had said so herself, pressing the rolled parchment into Beatrix's hands with fingers gone stiff as winter twigs. "Half of this I drew from memory, and my memory is sixty years old and full of holes. The other half I drew from stories told by people who were children when Veylmar fell." She'd tapped the paper where a river wandered off the edge into blank vellum. "So when it stops making sense, don't assume you've lost the road. Assume the road lost itself."

Beatrix thought about that for most of the first morning north.

They walked in single file along a drover's track that was barely a track at all—two ruts drowned in frost-browned grass, hedgerows leaning over them like conspirators sharing secrets. Pip walked in the middle because Pip always walked in the middle now, tucked between Beatrix and Sorrel like a coin between two cupped palms. The child hadn't spoken much since they'd left the village that wasn't there. They kept glancing backward at its smoke, thin on the horizon, and Beatrix pretended not to see.

"Is Ysolt going to be all right?" Pip finally asked.

"She's tougher than the winter," Beatrix said, which was what Ysolt would have wanted her to say, and possibly even true.

"And the map? Is it going to be all right?"

Beatrix unrolled a corner of it without breaking stride, holding it against her thigh so the wind couldn't take it. Inked lines crawled across it like frost ferns: the wheat country behind them, then a smear of hills labeled only *the old shepherds' way*, then nothing but a hand-drawn wolf's head and, beneath it, three words in Ysolt's cramped script: *ASK THE WOLVES.*

"I think," Beatrix said carefully, "the map is going to be exactly as all right as we are."

Pip considered this with the terrible seriousness of an eight-year-old deciding whether a joke was safe to laugh at. Then they laughed, once, a small startled sound like ice cracking, and Beatrix felt something in her chest unknot by a single turn.

Sorrel trotted ahead, nose low, and did not comment. Wolves rarely commented. But his ears were up, and he kept checking the wind the way a man checks a door he knows someone has followed him through.

* * *

They made the hills by late afternoon, and the hills made them pay for it.

The drover's track gave out where the land tilted upward into gorse and broken stone, and after that there was only climbing—the kind where every step costs twice what it should because the ground is actively arguing with you. The wind came off the high moor in long flat shoves, smelling of iron and coming snow. Real snow, ordinary snow, the kind that belonged to the season and no prophecy at all. It had been threatening since noon.

"We should find shelter before dark," Beatrix said, and hated how much her voice had started to sound like a commander's. She wasn't a commander. She was a woman who had spent twenty years perfecting the art of being overlooked, and now she was leading a child and a legend through empty country while an empire hunted them. Somewhere between Marek's stall and the bonefields, everyone had apparently agreed she was in charge, and she had never gotten a vote.

"There." Sorrel lifted his muzzle toward a dark seam in the hillside, half a mile on—a fold of rock, maybe a cave, maybe just shadow playing tricks in the failing light.

"You're sure?"

"No," Sorrel said. "But it is better than here."

It was hard to argue with that math. They climbed.

The seam turned out to be a genuine cave—an old mine adit, maybe, or a shepherd's dugout from some generation before memory—with a floor of packed earth dry enough to sleep on and a ceiling low enough to hold warmth if they dared a fire. Beatrix weighed the fire question the way she'd learned to weigh everything now: not *is it good* but *what will it cost*. Fire meant warmth, warmth meant Pip sleeping instead of shivering, shivering prevented meant a child who could walk tomorrow. Fire also meant a beacon visible for a mile in every direction.

She built a small one, deep in the cave mouth, and banked it until it was more glow than flame. Compromise. Her whole life was compromise now, arranged in little stacks like the charms she used to sell.

Pip was asleep almost immediately, curled against Sorrel's flank, one hand buried in the fur of his ruff. The wolf bore it with the weary dignity of a creature who has decided a thing is happening to him and resistance would be undignified.

Beatrix sat with the map spread across her knees and studied it by firelight, and the longer she looked, the less she liked it.

Ysolt's lines were confident through the wheat country and grew hesitant in the hills, and past the hills they simply stopped pretending. There was a pass marked with a symbol Beatrix didn't know—a circle with a line struck through it—and a note in tiny letters: *the stag's road, if it still runs.* And beyond that, drawn freehand and clearly from secondhand dreams rather than any living eye: the mountains, white-inked, and in their midst a valley shaped like a cupped hand, and in the palm of the hand, a mark like a door.

The Door of Ashvale.

It looked so small on the page. She supposed doors usually did.

"The wolves know the way," Sorrel said from the darkness near the entrance. He spoke without turning his head, watching the snow start to come down outside—fat, slow flakes, unhurried, like the sky had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

"Ysolt seems to think so."

"Ysolt is right, which is not the same as it being easy." He turned then, eyes catching the firelight, green-gold coins in the gloom. "The packs of the high country are not my pack. They have not answered to the parliament in a long time. Some of them answer to older things."

"What older things?" Beatrix asked, and Sorrel looked at her with an expression she was beginning to recognize and dread—the look of a creature deciding how much truth a person can carry today.

"The cold," he said finally. "Ask me again when we're closer. Tonight, sleep. Tomorrow we cross the shepherd's way, and it will want all of your legs."

She banked the fire lower and lay down beside Pip, and the last thing she saw before sleep took her was snow falling past the cave mouth, steady and silent, each flake lit gold as it crossed the ember-light and then gone.

* * *

The storm caught them at midday on the open shoulder of the moor, and it was not polite about it.

One moment there was a gray sky and a cold wind; the next, the world ended. Snow came sideways, thick as wool, erasing the path, the horizon, the distinction between ground and sky. Beatrix walked with one fist knotted in Sorrel's ruff and the other gripping Pip's wrist, and the three of them moved as one blind animal, trusting the wolf's nose to find the far side of a world with no sides left.

"Keep walking," Sorrel said, again and again, his voice flattened by wind. "Keep walking. The track bends left soon. Keep walking."

"How do you know?" Pip shouted.

"Because I can smell sheep shit under the snow, and sheep shit means a wall, and a wall means the track." A pause. "Do not tell the others I said shit."

Pip laughed into the storm, wild and breathless, and Beatrix thought: *this is what courage looks like from the inside. Wet, freezing, and joking.*

The wall arrived exactly when promised—a dry-stone dyke shouldering out of the whiteness—and they followed it down into the lee of a ruined sheepfold, and there, huddled in its broken corner with the storm screaming overhead, they waited out the worst of it. Beatrix got Pip under her cloak and got her own back to the wind and tried very hard not to think about how many hours they'd lost, and how many hours the Wardens hadn't.

That was the arithmetic that lived in her chest now, always running, always compounding interest. Every hour they rested was an hour Vessa gained. Every mile of Ysolt's uncertain map was miles of certain pursuit behind them. She had seen what Warden hounds could do in the bonefields. She had felt what her own gift cost when she'd spent it turning those hounds inside out—that cold draining sensation, magic leaving her like blood from a wound she couldn't bind. She didn't have endless tricks. She didn't have endless anything.

"Pip," she said quietly, under the wind, "if I ever tell you to run and don't ask questions—"

"I know," Pip said. "Run and don't ask questions."

"And if I say hide—"

"Hide somewhere animals are, because they'll watch me and I'll feel them and I can make them quiet." Pip recited it in the bored sing-song of a child drilled past patience. Then, softer, not bored at all: "I don't want to leave you."

"I don't want you to leave me either." Beatrix pulled the cloak tighter around them both. "That's why we practice."

Above the fold's broken wall, the storm went on shouting. And beneath the storm, so faint Beatrix might have imagined it, she heard—or felt—something else. A pressure, rhythmic and patient, like footsteps on the underside of the world. Like knocking.

She decided she had imagined it. There were enough real things to be afraid of.

* * *

He came at dusk, when the storm had worn itself down to scattered flurries and the light was the color of old pewter.

Sorrel's head came up first. Beatrix knew within a heartbeat that something was wrong—not by anything she saw or heard, but by the way the wolf's whole body changed, the lazy sprawl of rest becoming a loaded spring. Then she heard it too: a single set of footsteps on the frozen track, unhurried, deliberate. The footsteps of someone who wants to be heard.

"Stay here," she breathed to Pip, and moved to the wall's gap with a skinning knife in her hand and no memory of having drawn it.

Corven Hale stood twenty paces off, hands empty and raised, snow settling on his shoulders like a crown he'd stopped believing in. No sword. No bow. No troop at his back—she checked the track behind him twice, three times, and found nothing but white distance. He had taken off his Warden grey. Underneath he wore the plain brown of nobody, of no one at all, and somehow that frightened her more than the uniform would have.

"Don't come closer," she said.

"I'm not coming closer." His voice carried flat and clear across the still air. "I'm going to stand right here, and I'm going to talk, and you're going to decide whether any of it's worth listening to. That's the whole arrangement."

"That's not an arrangement. That's a trap with better manners."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile. "Fair."

Sorrel flowed up beside her, hackles a ridge of silver-black bristle, a growl rolling out of him low and continuous, like distant thunder that had decided to take up residence. Corvin's eyes flicked to the wolf and stayed there a moment too long. He knew what Sorrel was. Or he knew enough.

"You lied in your report," Beatrix said. "Twenty years ago. You wrote that the charm-seller escaped into the river district and was presumed drowned."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because I didn't want to write what actually happened." He shifted his weight, careful, keeping his hands where she could see them. "What actually happened is that I had you in arm's reach in that alley and I let you go, and I've spent twenty years not examining why. And then the purge came, and I was ordered to lead it, and I found I couldn't stop not-examining it. So here I am. Unexamined, unarmed, and asking to help."

Beatrix's grip tightened on the knife. "Help. You. The crown's finest huntsman."

"The crown's former finest huntsman. As of roughly nine days ago, when I misread a trail badly enough to cost us four days, and then badly again, and then my lieutenant started looking at me the way you look at a charm that might be cursed." He took a slow breath, and the breath fogged white between them. "Vessa has the routes. All of them. The northern passes, the supply caches, the places you'd stop. I know which ones she'll use and which ones she thinks *you'll* use, and I'm the only person alive who can tell you both. That's what I'm offering. Not forgiveness. Information."

Behind her, in the ruin's shadow, Beatrix heard Pip stir. She put out a hand without looking, found a small shoulder, held it still.

"Say I believe you," she said. "Say I'm fool enough. What happens when we reach the Door? You report back. You give them the location. That's the trade, isn't it? Routes for coordinates."

"I burned my orders eight days ago," Corvin said. "You don't come back from burning your orders. There's no report to make. Only forward."

"People say that," Beatrix said. "Right up until the moment it's convenient to forget it."

"Yes," he said simply. "They do."

The wind moved through the gap in the wall, carrying snow and the smell of woodsmoke from somewhere far off southward—Warden fires, maybe, or maybe just the world going on. Sorrel's growl never wavered. And Beatrix stood in the doorway of a ruined sheepfold with a knife in her hand and a child behind her and a dead kingdom ahead of her, and did the arithmetic she was getting so terribly practiced at.

Here was the pride: every instinct she owned said *wolf in the fold*. He had worn the grey. He had led the hunt. Whatever he'd done twenty years ago, he had also, nine days ago, been hunting *them*—and men like Corven Hale did not stop being weapons. They only changed hands.

And here was the necessity: Vessa had the routes, and Ysolt's map stopped pretending halfway up a mountain, and the long winter was falling whether Beatrix understood it or not, and somewhere ahead was a door that opened only while the snow still fell. She could refuse to trust him and walk into the passes blind. Or she could take what he offered and spend every mile afterward waiting for the knife.

Both choices cost. That seemed to be the whole design of her life lately: a market stall of choices, all of them priced.

"No," Beatrix said.

Corvin's face didn't change. "No to the information, or no to trusting you while giving it?"

"Both." She stepped back from the gap, but didn't lower the knife. "You can walk behind us. Fifty paces, always in front of Sorrel, always where I can see you. You can tell us what you know when we ask. But you don't walk with us. You don't eat at our fire. And you don't—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through anyway, "—you don't stand next to the child. Not yet. Maybe not ever. That's the arrangement. Take it or walk back to the people you betrayed."

For a long moment Corven Hale said nothing at all. Snow gathered on his eyelashes. He looked past her, briefly, at the shape of Pip in the ruin's shadow—at the small face watching him with wide, unreadable eyes—and whatever passed over his expression then was gone too fast to name, but it was not the look of a man planning a betrayal.

"Fifty paces," he said. "In front of the wolf. Done."

"And Corvin." She let the silence stretch until he met her eyes. "If you lead us wrong—if one mile of your knowledge turns out to be a snare—I won't kill you. I'll do worse. I'll speak to the hounds they send after us, and I'll send them home, and then I'll keep walking, and you can live with what that makes you. Do you understand?"

Something flickered in his face—wonder, maybe, or recognition, the look of a man hearing a door close that he'd been leaning on. "Understood," he said.

He turned and began walking north along the track, fifty paces, no more, no less, his footprints stitching away across the snow. Sorrel watched him go with his ears flat, and said, quietly, so only Beatrix could hear:

"You are aware that a man can keep an arrangement perfectly and still destroy you."

"I'm aware," Beatrix said. She looked down at the map in her hand, at Ysolt's confident ink dissolving into guesswork, at the little door drawn in the palm of a mountain's cupped hand. "That's why he walks in front of the wolf."

They broke camp as the last light died, and followed the brown-clad figure north into the gathering dark—and it was Sorrel, a mile on, who suddenly stopped, nostrils wide, and looked back the way they'd come with an expression Beatrix had never seen on him before.

Not fear. Recognition.

"Beatrix," he said. "Someone else is on the road behind us. And they are not walking slowly."
