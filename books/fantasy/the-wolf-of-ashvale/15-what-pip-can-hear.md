## 15. What Pip Can Hear

The cold had teeth now. That was the first thing Pip noticed, three days after they left the village that wasn't on any map. The cold didn't just sit on your skin anymore — it bit down, worried at you like a dog with a bone, and somewhere past the second ridge it started whispering.

Pip knew better than to tell anyone.

They'd learned that lesson young, the way orphans learn everything: fast, and from getting hit. In Ashvale's undercity, the rats used to come when Pip was sad, dozens of them, pouring out of the drains to sit in a ring around wherever they were crying, and the other gutter-children had called it *rat-touched* and thrown stones until the rats turned their heads — all together, all at once, a hundred small skulls swiveling like weathervanes — and then nobody threw anything ever again. But being feared wasn't the same as being listened to. Pip had learned the difference early too.

So they kept it inside: the thing about the snow.

Because here was what Pip hadn't told anyone, not Beatrix, not Sorrel, not even Corvin who'd started carrying them on his shoulders when the drifts got deep and whose shoulders smelled like woodsmoke and old blood. Here was the secret, folded up small and kept behind the ribs where secrets go:

The snow wasn't silent.

It had been quiet at first, back when the out-of-season winter began falling over the village. Snow was supposed to be quiet — that was half of what made it snow, that hush, that blanket pulled up over the world's ears. But this snow talked. Not in words, not exactly. More like the way you could hear talking through a wall, when you couldn't catch the words but you could catch the *shape* of them, the rise and fall, the places where somebody laughed or went quiet. The snowfall had a shape. And underneath the shape there was something else, something deeper, something that came up through the ground and through Pip's boots and into the bones of their feet, and it said the same thing over and over, patient as water dripping through stone:

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

Like someone knocking on the underside of a door.

Pip lay awake the third night in the lee of a fallen pine, wrapped in a wool blanket that Ysolt had pressed into their arms at the last minute, smelling of lavender and dust and a life that had ended before Pip was born. Sorrel was a warm curve against their back, the wolf's breathing slow and deep. Corvin stood the watch, a dark shape at the edge of the firelight, and Beatrix slept sitting up the way she always did, like even her dreams were things she didn't quite trust herself to enter.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

Pip squeezed their eyes shut.

Go away, they thought at it. We're not coming. Nobody's home.

The knocking paused. Then it came again — softer now, almost apologetic, and underneath it Pip felt something new, something that made their chest go tight and hot:

It sounded lonely.

That was worse. That was so much worse than scary. Scary things you could hate. But the voice under the ice sounded like a child locked in a cellar calling up through the floorboards, hoping, every single knock, that this time someone would answer.

Pip knew what that was like. Pip had done that knocking, once, from the inside, in the years before Beatrix found them crouched in a wall-cavity behind a collapsed bakery, so starved their eyes had gone huge and strange, every rat in three streets gathered outside the rubble like mourners.

So Pip did the only thing they could think of, the thing they'd done their whole life when the world got too loud and too full of asking.

They answered back.

*Hello,* Pip thought, careful and small, the way you'd speak to a wounded bird. *I hear you.*

The silence afterward was total. Even the wind stopped. Even the fire seemed to lean closer.

Then, beneath the ice, beneath the earth, beneath everything — joy. A rush of it, wordless and enormous, the feeling of a door swinging open after twenty years of dark, and it came up through the ground and through Pip's body and it was so big and so glad that Pip's breath went out of them in a white plume and their hands shook and somewhere far away a fox screamed and every crow within a mile burst up out of the frozen trees at once, a black ragged explosion against the stars.

Oh no, Pip thought.

Oh no oh no oh no.

*

Beatrix woke like a knife coming out of a sheath. One moment asleep, the next upright, hand already on her knife, eyes finding Pip across the dead fire. "What did you do?"

"I didn't mean to." Pip's voice came out tiny. The crows were still settling, muttering, in the trees. "I just — it was knocking, and it sounded —"

"Start again," Beatrix said, and came to kneel in front of them, and whatever she saw in Pip's face made her voice drop and soften. "Slowly. From the beginning."

So Pip told her. All of it. The snow with its shape of talking, the knocking under the ice, the loneliness, the answering. They watched Beatrix's face while they talked, watched it do something complicated — fear and recognition fighting each other for space, and under both of them something older, something almost like grief.

"You've been hearing it since the winter started," Beatrix said. "Since the long winter. Since the snow began falling out of season." She wasn't really asking. She was doing the thing she did sometimes, assembling pieces, her eyes going distant the way they went when she was working out which charm to sell which customer, except nothing she sold had ever looked like this. "Three days. Three days, and every animal within — how far, Pip? When you answered it, how far did everything turn to look?"

"I don't know. Far."

"The crows went up," Corvin said quietly from the treeline. He hadn't moved from his watch, but he'd turned, and the firelight caught the scar down his cheek. "All of them. Everything with wings within a mile is awake right now. I've seen it happen twice before. Once when the Wardens flushed a speaker out of the granary district." He paused. "Once when the Door was mentioned by name, in the village. Ysolt's house. Remember? Every animal in the yard stood up and faced north."

Pip remembered. Pip had thought everyone did that.

Beatrix sat back on her heels. For a long moment she said nothing at all, and Pip, who could read silences the way other children read letters, read this one and felt it land in their stomach like a stone.

She's afraid of me, Pip thought. She finally figured out what I am and now she's afraid.

But that wasn't it either, because Beatrix reached out and took Pip's cold hands in her colder ones and held on, hard, like Pip was something the wind might take. "Listen to me," she said. "You don't answer it again. Whatever it says, however it asks, however lonely it sounds — you don't answer. Do you understand why?"

"No," Pip said honestly.

"Because voices under the ice don't call just anyone." Beatrix glanced at Sorrel, who had raised his head, ears forward, amber eyes reflecting the fire. "And because if you can hear it, then other things can hear *you*. Things that have been waiting under that ice for a very long time for exactly one person to say hello."

Sorrel made a sound low in his chest, not quite a growl, not quite a word. He'd been making those sounds more often lately — sounds that were almost language, sounds Beatrix seemed to be understanding more of every day. Now he rose and walked a slow circle around Pip, nose working, and then he did something he had never done before.

He lay down across Pip's legs. All his weight. Deliberate, heavy, warm, like a bar dropped across a door.

"That's him deciding," Beatrix translated softly. "He says you're under guard."

"From what?"

"From whatever answers next."

Pip looked north, past the black trees, to where the mountains stood up against the stars wearing their new snow. Somewhere out there was the thing that knocked. It had waited twenty years. It had knocked on the underside of the world for twenty years and nobody had answered, and the moment Pip had, it had been so happy —

"It's not a monster," Pip whispered. It wasn't defiance. It was just true, the way some things were just true, the way rain was wet. "It's sad. It's been alone so long. Beatrix, it's *lonely*, and I know what that feels like, and I can't just—"

"You can," Beatrix said. "For now, you can. Because I've spent twenty years hiding what I am, and I'll tell you the one thing I learned for certain: the gift isn't the danger, Pip. The gift is fine. It's what other people want to *do* with your gift that kills you." Her thumb moved over Pip's knuckles, rough and gentle. "The Crown hunts speakers. The wolves on the ridge wanted the child who hears. And now there's something under the ice of a dead country that heard an eight-year-old say hello, and I promise you — I *promise* you — we are not the only ones who felt that."

Corvin shifted at the treeline. When he spoke, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used for bad news. "If the Wardens have anyone left who can track resonance — and they will, they keep speakers of their own, chained ones — then as of tonight they know roughly where we are. And more than that." He met Beatrix's eyes across the fire. "They know *what* they're looking for. Until tonight they hunted a charm-seller and a runaway. Now they're hunting the key."

The word dropped into the firelight and sat there.

Key, Pip thought. Like a key fits a lock. Like a lock has a door.

"What does it open?" Pip asked.

Nobody answered. Sorrel pressed his muzzle harder against Pip's knee. Far to the north, faint as memory, the knocking came again — three soft taps, hopeful, patient, *waiting* —

And this time, beneath the knocking, so faint Pip might have imagined it, there was something else. Another sound. Not under the ice at all.

Behind them. South. Down the trail they'd broken through the deep snow.

Hooves.

A lot of hooves.
