## 16. The Frozen Ford

Corvin Hale had crossed the Vessa River eleven times in his life, and every one of those crossings had been on the crown's coin.

He knew the ford the way he knew his own hands. Two miles south of the shepherd's road it narrowed between granite shoulders, and in hard winters the ice went thick enough to bear a wagon team — thick enough, if you were desperate, to bear a fleeing charm-seller, a child who made animals turn their heads, and a wolf out of a dead country's stories.

They were desperate. He could see it in Beatrix's set jaw as they came down through the pines, and in the way Pip kept stopping to listen to nothing, and in Sorrel's low continuous growl, which Corvin had learned over the past days was not threat but weather-report: *something is coming.*

"Outriders," the wolf said now. "Four. Maybe five. Behind us by an hour, moving fast."

Corvin crouched at the river's edge and put his palm flat on the ice. Cold bit through his glove. Beneath the surface the current ran black and quick, and here and there the ice had thinned to a smoky gray that would take a man's weight only once, and briefly, and with luck.

"They'll expect us to run for the far bank," he said. "Open ground beyond it. They'll want to catch us in the middle."

Beatrix stood behind him with her arms wrapped around herself, breath steaming. "So we don't cross in the middle."

"So we don't cross where they think." He looked up at her. She'd stopped flinching from him somewhere on the glacier road, and he still didn't know what to do with that. "There's a shelf of rock under the east bank, thirty yards downstream. The water runs fast over it and never freezes clean. If we go there—"

"—we fall through," Pip finished.

"If we're careless, we fall through." Corvin kept his voice level, the way he'd once briefed green scouts before their first patrol. "If we're careful, the Warden hounds won't follow us onto it. They can smell the open water."

Pip's chin came up. Eight years old and already learning to weigh a bad option against a worse one. Corvin hated that. He hated that he was the one teaching it.

*You taught them worse,* said the voice that had been living under his ribs since the bothy. *You wrote names on a list, and those names ended on gallows and pyres, and one of them was her mother.* He pushed the thought down where it lived and stood.

"We cross at the shelf," he said. "Single file. I go first. Then the boy. Then you, Beatrix. Sorrel—" he glanced at the wolf, who was watching the treeline with ears flat, "—do what wolves do."

"I intend to," Sorrel said dryly, and padded downstream.

---

They were forty yards from the shelf when the horns sounded.

Not one horn — three, staggered, from the ridge behind them and then from both flanks, and Corvin felt his stomach drop through the frozen ground because that wasn't an outrider's signal. That was a net closing. Someone had read the trail correctly after all, someone had guessed they'd aim for the ford, and someone had brought enough men to make the guess matter.

"Wardens!" The shout came off the ridge. "In the name of the Crown! Stand where you are!"

Beatrix grabbed Pip's hand. Corvin turned, and saw them coming down through the pines — six riders in the king's gray, lances couched, breath pluming from their horses like small furious dragons. And at their head, riding with her visor up so she could be recognized, Lieutenant Vessa Crane.

He knew her face better than he knew most of his family's. He'd trained her. He'd drunk with her. He'd told her, two winters ago over cheap wine, that the purge would end when the lists ran dry, and she'd laughed and said good, because lists were easier than thinking.

Now she reined in twenty feet away and looked at him with an expression he'd worn himself a hundred times: the flat disappointment of a soldier finding exactly what she expected to find.

"Hale." Her voice carried across the ice. "Step away from the fugitives."

"Vessa." He stepped sideways instead — not toward her, but between her line of fire and Pip. It was not a subtle movement. Everyone saw it. He watched her see it, watched her face change by a degree, the way ice changes just before it cracks.

"You're under arrest," she said. "That report you filed about the bonefields. The misread trail. The lost hounds." She shook her head slowly. "I defended you. Did you know that? I told the captain you were being clever, working your way into their trust so you could take them all at once." A bitter almost-smile. "I'm good at believing what I want to believe about people I respect."

"I know," Corvin said. "I'm sorry for it."

"Don't apologize. Just move."

Behind him, Beatrix whispered something — to the ice, or to the silver in her own coat buttons, or to the wind. The horses began to stamp. One of the outriders' mounts shied hard, eyes rolling white. Corvin felt the old familiar pressure in the air, the taste of copper and pine sap that meant her gift was awake and reaching.

"It won't hold six of them," Sorrel murmured at his flank. "It holds horses. Horses are simple."

"Then we run for the shelf," Corvin breathed back. "On my word. Beatrix — when I say go, you get the boy onto the ice and you do not stop for anything. Not for me. Not for anything."

"No," Beatrix said immediately.

"Yes."

"Corvin—"

"Vessa won't order a volley while I'm standing in front of you," he said. "She'll close to take me alive. That buys you thirty seconds. Take them."

And because he had been the crown's finest huntsman for twenty years, and because he had finally, at forty-one years of age, learned to spend everything he had on the right target, he drew his knife, turned, and charged his former brothers-in-arms.

---

Later, he would remember it in pieces, the way men remember battles.

The first rider's lance, aimed to disable, glancing off his shoulder because Vessa shouted *hold, HOLD* a half-second too late and the man pulled the stroke wide.

Sorrel hitting the second rider like a landslide given teeth, horse and man going down together in a shriek of steam.

Beatrix's voice cracking across the field — not words, more than words — and three of the six horses simply refusing, sitting back on their haunches, done with the whole enterprise, and their riders cursing and fighting reins instead of men.

And Pip. Pip, who should have been running for the ice, standing rooted ten yards behind him with their mouth open, because every animal within half a mile had turned its head toward the child at once — crows lifting off the pines in a black sheet, foxes bolting from cover to stand staring at the battle like spectators, the Warden hounds in their trace-lines howling at nothing.

*The gift is waking,* Corvin thought wildly, parrying a saber cut meant for his thigh. *Ysolt said it would wake. This is what waking looks like.*

"Pip!" he roared. "RUN!"

Pip ran — but the wrong way. Toward him. Of course toward him; children always ran toward the person fighting for them, and Corvin had time to think *no, no, no* as the fifth rider wheeled his horse around the melee, leveled his lance at the running child, and put his heels in.

There was no thought after that. There was just Corvin's body doing the arithmetic it had been drilled into since he was fifteen years old: distance, speed, angle, intercept. He broke off from Vessa's blade, took three strides, and got between the lance and the boy.

The point took him under the left shoulder blade.

He heard it more than felt it — a wet crack, a sound like a boot breaking crust ice — and then the world tilted and he was down on his knees in the snow with Pip screaming somewhere very close and the cold coming up through the ground to meet the cold spreading through his chest, and he thought, with strange clarity: *so this is where the list ends. On my knees in a river valley, buying thirty seconds I won't even see spent.*

Then Sorrel arrived, and the world became noise.

---

He surfaced from the noise in stages.

First: weight on his back, warm, small, shaking. Hands pressing hard below his collarbone, and a child's voice saying *stay, stay, stay* in a rhythm that sounded less like pleading and more like instruction.

Second: Beatrix kneeling in the snow beside him, tearing the wadding from a charm — one of her own, one of the harmless ones — and holding it against the wound, whispering to it with tears freezing on her cheeks. "Work," she was saying. "You've lied for twenty years, lie once more. Work."

Third: quiet. The battle was over. He could hear Vessa's riders withdrawing up the far slope, horses blowing, one man groaning where the wolf had left him. And he could hear Vessa herself, standing at a distance, shouting something across the ice that he couldn't yet make sense of.

He opened his eyes. The sky was gray-white and enormous. Pip's face swam above him, streaked with snot and snowmelt.

"Told you," Corvin managed. "Thirty seconds."

"You idiot," Pip said, in the precise tone of Beatrix, and pressed down harder on the wound.

"He saved the boy," Vessa's voice said again, closer now, and this time the words resolved. Corvin rolled his head sideways. She had dismounted. She was standing eight feet away with her sword still drawn but pointed at the ground, and she was looking at him the way you look at a building after it has fallen — trying to find the shape it used to have.

"Lieutenant," he rasped. "Come to finish the job?"

"Shut up, Hale." Her jaw worked. "The lance was meant to pin the child's cloak. Aldric misjudged. You—" She stopped. Started again. "You took it in his place. You saw the throw and you moved into it."

"That's generally how shields work."

"Twenty years," Vessa said. "Twenty years, and I vouched for you in the bonefields, and I covered for the lost hounds because I believed—" Her voice did something it had never done in his hearing. It cracked. "My brother is in that country, Corvin. My brother was erased. And I have spent two years telling myself the purge was justice, because the alternative was admitting I helped burn a country that had my own blood in it."

The snow fell between them. Somewhere behind Corvin, Beatrix had gone very still, and Sorrel had risen to his feet with his hackles half-lifted, watching the lieutenant the way he watched thin ice.

"The Door," Corvin said. Each word cost him; he could feel the blood leaving with them. "It's real. We've seen it. Ysolt's map—"

"I know about Ysolt." Something moved through Vessa's face — grief, decision, terror at the decision. "I grew up on her ledger's street. I know what my family lost, even if we weren't permitted to say it aloud." She sheathed her sword, and the sound of it rang across the frozen river like a bell ending a service. "I'm going to ride back and report that the fugitives crossed at the rapids and were lost. All of them. Including you."

"Vessa—"

"Because you took a lance for a child," she said, "in front of six witnesses. And because somebody has to be left inside the walls who remembers what remembering costs." She swung up into her saddle. For one moment her eyes found Beatrix's over Corvin's body — held there, unreadable, a long look with a question buried in it — and then she wheeled her horse and was gone up the slope, and the gray coats went with her, and the valley was theirs and silent except for the river talking under its ice.

---

They got him to the shelf of rock, though crossing it was a nightmare he experienced mostly as cold and Beatrix's grip on his collar and Sorrel's teeth in his sleeve. The open water smoked around them, and Pip crossed last, walking precisely where Beatrix walked, and on the far bank they built a fire in the lee of a granite shoulder and cut his shirt off him and looked at what the lance had done.

"Not the lung," Beatrix said at last, her fingers red to the wrist. "Through the muscle, under the bone. You're lucky."

"Lucky," Corvin repeated. He was shivering now, properly, the delayed shake of a man whose body has decided the emergency is over. "She's going to hang for this."

"She's going to be fine," Pip said fiercely, from his other side. "She's clever. She lied to the whole army."

"People get hanged for smaller lies."

"People get *saved* by bigger ones," the child shot back, and Beatrix huffed something that was almost a laugh, and Corvin closed his eyes and let them argue over his head while the fire ticked and the river ran.

He drifted. Pain and fever took turns holding him down, and in the gaps between them he dreamed of a list — his own handwriting, twenty years younger, marching down a page in neat officer's script — and woke once with his heart hammering to find Beatrix watching him, her expression unreadable in the firelight.

"You said her name in your sleep," she said quietly. "Your mother's? Or hers?"

"Mine died when I was nine." He swallowed. "I said Vessa. And before that—" His honesty was a debt now; he could feel it accruing interest. "Before that, a name from the list. A woman named Maren Blackwood."

Beatrix did not move. The fire popped. When she spoke, her voice was the flattest he'd ever heard it, flatter than the ice, flatter than the sky.

"My mother's name was Maren."

"I know," Corvin said. "I've known since the bothy. I've been waiting to find the courage to tell you since the bothy, and I keep failing, so — there. There it is. I wrote her name down. I handed it up the chain. Whatever happened to her happened because I was good at my job, and I am sorry, and I know that sorry is worth nothing, and you are entitled to leave me in this snow."

Beatrix looked at him for a long time. The firelight moved on her face. Pip, mercifully, had gone to sleep against Sorrel's flank, one fist still knotted in the wolf's fur.

"I'm not leaving you in the snow," she said finally. "You took a lance for my child today, whatever else you are. But Corvin—" she leaned closer, and her eyes were bright and terrible, "—when we reach the Door, and if my mother's country gives me any say in what happens there, you and I are going to finish this conversation. Do you understand?"

"I understand," he said.

"Good." She sat back, pulled her cloak around her, and looked north, where the mountains had begun to show themselves through the dusk like the backs of sleeping animals. "Rest. We move at first light. Ysolt said the pass closes with the next snowfall, and the next snowfall—" she glanced at the sky, where the clouds had organized themselves into something patient and vast, "—is coming tonight."

High above the valley, out of a sky that had no business producing it yet, the first flakes of the long winter's snow began to fall.

They landed on the fire and did not melt.

Corvin, fevered and bleeding and colder than he had ever been, watched one land on the back of his hand — watched it sit there, perfect, refusing the heat — and understood that they were out of time.
