## 17. Fever and Firelight

The bothy appeared out of the white like something dreamed rather than built—a low stone hump with a turf roof, half-swallowed by drift, a crooked chimney exhaling the ghost of smoke. Beatrix saw it first and said nothing for a hundred paces, because saying things aloud had started to feel dangerous. Everything she named lately seemed to come true in the worst possible way.

But it held. Walls, a door, a roof that had survived more winters than any of them.

"In here," she called back, and her voice cracked on the second word.

Corvin hadn't spoken since the ford. He'd walked—she'd give him that, he'd *walked*—with his arm pressed to his side and his jaw set like a man refusing an invitation to die, but somewhere past the third mile the set of his jaw had gone slack, and by the fifth he was leaning on Pip's shoulder, and by the seventh Sorrel was simply walking against him so that the wolf's body did the work of standing. The wound was ugly. A Warden blade, long and deep along his ribs, and the cold had packed it with snow that melted red down his coat until Beatrix stopped looking at the trail behind them.

Pip got the door open. It groaned like something being woken from a long sleep, and inside there was straw, a stone hearth, a stack of peat gone soft with damp, and a shepherd's crook hanging on a peg as if its owner meant to come back for it. Maybe he would. Maybe shepherds still came up here, in the country between countries, where no crown reached and no map bothered.

"Bed," Beatrix said, and it wasn't a bed, it was straw and two folded sheepskins, but it was off the ground, and off the ground was all she asked of the world just then. "Get him down."

Sorrel lowered Corvin the way water lowers a leaf—carefully, without ceremony—and Corvin made one sound, a low grunt that might have been thanks or protest, and then his eyes rolled white and he was gone into whatever place fevers take people.

Beatrix knelt and put her hands on him before she decided to. That was the thing about her gift, the thing she'd spent twenty years strangling: it moved through her hands whether she willed it or not. She could whisper silver awake, could murmur a hinge quiet, could ask a lock to remember being open. And flesh—flesh was just another material that listened, if you knew its language. Not healing. She couldn't heal. But she could *ask*.

"Hush," she told the wound, the way she'd tell a frightened dog. "Hush now. Close. Come closed."

The flesh didn't close. Flesh never obeyed anyone but time. But the bleeding slowed, and the heat under her palms shifted—not gone, but gathered, drawn toward the surface where fever could do its work honestly instead of rotting him from the inside out. It cost her something. She felt it leave, a warmth pulled up through her arms like thread through cloth, and she sat back on her heels breathing hard, watching her own hands shake.

*Spending magic I cannot take back.* She was doing a lot of that lately.

* * *

They made camp the way people make camp when they're afraid the world is listening: small fire, low voices, everything stowed and ready to run. Sorrel took the watch outside, a gray shape at the doorway that Pip kept glancing at with the particular awe children reserve for things they've decided are miracles.

Beatrix boiled snow for water. There was a kettle in the bothy, dented and black, and dried yarrow in a tin on the shelf that some shepherd's wife had hung there against just such days. Yarrow for wounds. She steeped it strong and hated how much she knew about this, how many years she'd spent learning the quiet arts of tending things nobody thanked you for.

Corvin burned.

She'd seen fever before—in customers' children, in old Marek once, the winter his lungs went wrong—but she'd never been the one sitting through the night with it, and she learned quickly that fever has a shape, an architecture. It rises. It crests. It says things. And the men who say nothing all day say everything when it burns them.

"No," Corvin said, near midnight. His eyes were open, fixed on the rafters, seeing some other ceiling entirely. "Not the girl. Strike the name. Strike it, I'll not—" His hand clawed at the sheepskin. "I'll not write her down. She sells *charms*. Charms aren't—"

He broke off. Breathed. Began again, quieter, in the tone of a man dictating:

"*Blackwood. Maren Blackwood, weaver, Fenwick Lane.*"

The name went through Beatrix like a blade finding bone.

She knew it, of course. Everyone who'd ever lost someone knew the sound of their name arriving from a stranger's mouth. Her mother's name. Spoken in a fever-dream twenty years after the fact, in a voice flat with officialese, the voice of a man reading a list.

She sat very still. The fire popped. Outside, snow ticked against the window like fingernails.

*Strike the name,* he'd said first. And then he'd written it anyway. Or he'd already written it, years ago, and the dream was only replaying the ink.

Beatrix looked at his face—the face of the huntsman who'd let her slip through his fingers in a market crowd when she was sixteen, the face of the officer whose report had bought her twenty years of small lies—and she tried to hate him properly, the way she'd earned the right to. It wouldn't sit right. Hate needs a clean target, and Corvin Hale, burning on a bed of straw, mumbling apologies to a woman twenty years dead, was not clean. He was a man with a crack running through him, and she was starting to suspect the crack had been there since before the purge, since before the list, maybe since the day he'd watched a charm-seller vanish into an alley and written the truth of it in a report he then falsified.

"You're a fool," she said softly, to him, to herself, to the fire. "You're a fool and I don't know yet what that makes me."

* * *

Dawn came gray through the one window. Pip slept curled against Sorrel's flank, having finally lost the argument about keeping watch, and Beatrix fed the fire peat and watched Corvin's fever crest and break—or begin to break. Fever lies on the way down too. She'd learned that much from the yarrow tin.

It was midmorning when his eyes found her and stayed.

"You," he rasped.

"Me." She offered water. He drank like it was an argument he intended to win.

"How long?"

"A night. You talked."

Something crossed his face—dread, she thought, the specific dread of a man who knows what he says asleep. "What did I say?"

"You know what you said." She kept her voice level, market-stall level, the voice she used for prices. "Maren Blackwood, weaver, Fenwick Lane."

The silence stretched so long the fire had to be fed twice.

"That was twenty years ago," he said finally. "Before. When I was—" He stopped. Started over, and this time he spoke slowly, like a man dismantling a weapon to show it was empty. "I was a young officer. Clever. Ambitious. They gave me a task they said was clerical. Compile a register. Names of gifted persons in Ashvale and the surrounding districts. For census purposes, they told me. For protection." His mouth twisted. "I believed them. Do you understand? I was twenty-three and I believed them, because believing them was easier than being the kind of man who asks what a census of the forbidden is *for*."

Beatrix said nothing. Saying nothing was a skill; she'd had twenty years of practice.

"I rode door to door. I smiled at shopkeepers. I wrote down names in a fair hand." His eyes were on the fire now. "Your mother gave me tea. Did you know that? She sat me at her table and answered every question, because she was honest, because hiding would have been worse if the questions ever came again. And I wrote her name, and six months later the lists became warrants, and the warrants became fires, and I stood in a square and watched smoke rise from the pyre district and understood, all at once, far too late, exactly what my handwriting had weighed."

"And hers," Beatrix said. Still level. Barely.

"And hers." He turned his head and looked at her directly, and she made herself hold it. His eyes were fever-bright and utterly unflinching. "I falsified my report afterward. The girl in the market—I wrote that I'd pursued a suspected speaker and lost her in the crowd. I didn't write that I'd recognized her. I didn't write that I'd seen Maren Blackwood's hands on a younger pair of hands, teaching silver to sing, and chosen to look away." A breath. "One lie. Then another. Twenty years of small treasons, each one smaller than the last, until the treasons were bigger than the loyalty ever was."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked." He almost smiled; it failed halfway. "And because you should hear it from me and not from Vessa's interrogation reports. And because if I die on this straw, I wanted—" He stopped. Tried again. "I wanted the person whose mother I killed to know it was my hand. Not the crown's hand. Mine. The crown gives orders; it doesn't hold pens."

Outside, wind worried the eaves. Pip stirred against Sorrel and settled again.

"Do you know what I did with your tea?" Beatrix said at last. Her voice had come out strange, thin, the voice of the girl at the stall rather than the woman. "I mean—she'd have washed the cup. She washed everything. She was that sort. So there's nothing left of that afternoon except your memory of it and my absence from it. You sat in our kitchen and ate my mother's bread and then you killed her, and I was probably upstairs practicing whispering to a hairpin." She laughed, and it came out wrong, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eye until the wrongness stopped. "I keep trying to make it simple. You're either a monster or you're sorry, and monsters I can walk away from, and sorry I don't know what to do with."

"Then don't decide," Corvin said. "Deciding's for judges. I've met them. They're worse than officers."

Despite everything, something in her chest hitched—not quite a laugh. "That's the first sensible thing you've said."

"I'm feverish. Sensible leaks out." He shifted, winced, and his gaze drifted to the doorway, where Sorrel sat gray against the white. "The wolf. Does it speak to you?"

"It speaks to everyone. Most people have forgotten how to listen."

"And what does it say about me?"

Beatrix considered lying. It would have been easy, and she was good at it, and she was so tired of being good at it.

"It says you smell of penance," she said. "It says penance has a scent, and most men who carry it are wearing it for show. It says yours isn't for show." She stood, brushed straw from her knees, and looked down at him with twenty years of grief balanced carefully in both hands. "It also says wolves forgive the hunter who feeds them in a bad winter. It doesn't say they forget."

"I wouldn't ask it to."

"No," she agreed. "I don't suppose you would."

She turned to go, to check the peat, to wake Pip gently, to do the ten thousand small tasks that were becoming her way of not deciding anything. At the door she paused.

"The tea," she said, without turning around. "Was it good?"

A long silence. Then: "It was honeyed. She said honey helped the throat for talking. I've thought about that cup more times than I've thought about my own mother."

Beatrix nodded once, sharply, and went out into the snow, where the cold could be trusted to be exactly what it was.

* * *

They stayed two days. Corvin's fever broke fully on the second morning, and by noon he was upright, gray-faced, testing his ribs with the grim arithmetic of a man pricing his own body. He insisted on taking watch. Beatrix let him, mostly because arguing cost more strength than it recovered.

It was dusk of the second day, Pip cross-legged by the fire chewing the last of the travel bread, when the boy spoke around a mouthful.

"There's something under the ice," Pip said. "Back at the ford. Under everything frozen. I heard it when Corvin was bleeding. Like knocking. Like somebody's stuck behind a door and asking polite." Pip swallowed. "It's getting louder the further north we go."

Beatrix went still. Sorrel lifted his head from the doorway. Even Corvin turned, hand drifting to his bandaged side.

"Louder how?" Beatrix asked.

"Like it knows we're coming." Pip looked into the fire, and firelight made the child's eyes flicker gold. "Like it's been waiting a long time for somebody to answer."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the snow kept falling—falling harder now, falling with intent, the long winter gathering itself for whatever it meant to bury. And beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, Beatrix felt it too: not a sound, not quite, but a pressure, patient and enormous, rising up through the stone floor of the bothy like a knock from the other side of the world.

Someone was asking to be remembered.

And the winter, Beatrix understood suddenly, was the sound of the world deciding whether to forget.
