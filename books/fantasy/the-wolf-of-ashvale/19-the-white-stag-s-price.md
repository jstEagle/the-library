## 19. The White Stag's Price

The mountain had been watching them for two days before it showed them the stag.

Beatrix felt it first as a pressure behind her eyes, the same dull ache she'd carried since the frozen ford—since Corvin had bled half his life into the snow to keep a Warden blade from Pip's throat. They'd holed up in a shepherd's bothy after that, three days of fever and firelight, and she'd learned things about Corven Hale that sat in her chest like swallowed stones. Now they were climbing again, higher than any of them had meant to go, because Ysolt's half-finished map showed only one way through the range before winter closed its fist: a pass called the Hollow Throat, marked with a symbol Beatrix didn't recognize. A branching line, like antlers.

Pip saw it first.

"Beatrix." The child's voice came out strange, flattened, the way it did when something under the ice was speaking through them. Pip had stopped in the middle of the switchback trail and was staring up-slope at a stand of black pines, snow to their knees, breath smoking. "There's something in the trees. It's *bright*."

Sorrel's hackles rose. The old wolf had been quiet since the ridge—the strangers who served the ice still haunted him, Beatrix could tell, the way a scar haunts skin. He moved ahead of them now without being asked, placing himself between the child and whatever came out of those trees. Corvin shifted the girl onto his good arm, his other hand finding the hatchet at his belt even though he knew, as they all knew, that steel meant nothing here.

"It's white," Pip whispered. "It's so bright it's like looking at a hole in the sky."

Then it stepped from between the pines, and Beatrix forgot how to breathe.

The stag was wrong the way aurora light is wrong—beautiful, and not meant for living eyes. Its coat held no color at all, or every color, shifting like snow-light on snow. Its antlers branched wider than Sorrel was long, and in the tines hung small pale lights, drifting, like seeds of frost. It stood above them on the switchback, looking down with eyes the color of deep ice over deep water, and Beatrix understood, in the marrow-way that her gift understood things, that this creature was old. Older than Ashvale. Older than the crown. Old enough that Veylmar's collapse would have been an afternoon to it.

"Is it—" Corvin started.

"Hush," said Beatrix, and was surprised by her own voice: low, steady, the voice she used for frightened animals. She took a step forward. Sorrel made a sound low in his chest—not quite a growl—and she put a hand back toward him without turning. "It's all right. It's not going to hurt anyone. It wants something."

"How can you know that?" Corvin hissed.

"Because everything up here wants something," Beatrix said. "And it's been following us for two days waiting for us to be desperate enough to listen."

The stag descended the slope without disturbing the snow. That was the detail her mind snagged on, later: no prints. Eight hundred pounds of animal walking on fresh powder and leaving nothing behind, as though the mountain hadn't agreed to hold its weight. It stopped ten feet from her, close enough that the frost-lights in its antlers threw moving shadows across her face, and it looked at her the way no beast had ever looked at her—with appraisal. With *accounting*.

*Charm-seller,* it said.

Its voice wasn't sound. It arrived fully formed inside her skull, the way Sorrel's words always had, but colder, precise, each word placed like a coin on a scale.

"You speak," Beatrix said aloud. Her breath shook. "Of course you speak. Everything in this country speaks. I just never thought anything would want to talk to *me*."

*I speak because you are nearly in my country,* said the stag. *And because your road ends here unless we make a bargain.*

"The pass." She glanced up. The Hollow Throat yawned above them, a notch in the ridgeline where the wind screamed through with a sound like a thousand thin voices. "Ysolt's map marks it. But there's no trail."

*There is a trail,* said the stag. *There has always been a trail. It is simply not open to everyone. It opens for me, and it opens for what I carry, and it opens—* the great head tilted, one frost-light detaching itself and drifting down to hang between them, pulsing gently, like a slow heartbeat *—for a price.*

Corvin moved up beside her. He'd gone gray around the mouth; she could see him doing officer's arithmetic, weighing threats, exits, odds. "Whatever it's asking," he murmured, "the answer is probably no."

"The Wardens are two days behind us," Beatrix said quietly. "Vessa doesn't stop. And the long winter is coming down off the ice whether we climb or turn back. There's no *no* left, Corvin. There's just which price we pay."

She turned back to the stag. "Name it."

*Not from the child,* said the stag immediately, and something in its voice softened—or seemed to; it was hard to read warmth into a glacier. *The child is already spoken for. Not from the wolf—he has given enough. Not from the huntsman; he carries debts too heavy to add to.* The ice-colored eyes settled on her, patient and vast. *From you. One memory. Chosen by me.*

The wind filled the silence that followed, screaming through the notch above with those thousand thin voices, and Beatrix stood very still while the meaning of it worked its way down into her.

"One memory," she repeated. "Gone? Like it never happened?"

*Like it was never yours,* the stag corrected. *You may know that something is missing. You will feel the shape of the hole. But the contents will not return. Memory is the one coin that cannot be counterfeited, charm-seller. That is why I accept only it.*

"And if I refuse?"

*Then you climb the Hollow Throat unled,* said the stag, *and the Throat decides what you owe it. It has been collecting from travelers since before your crown existed. Some come out the far side. Most do not come out at all.*

Behind her, Pip made a small sound. Beatrix turned. The child was staring at her with an expression she couldn't read—fear, yes, but underneath it something else. Something almost like recognition.

"Ask it what it takes," Pip said.

"Pip—"

"No. Ask it." The child's chin came up, stubborn as a mule, and for a moment they sounded less like an eight-year-old than like someone very old wearing a child's face. "You gave me food when you didn't have it. You carried me across the ford. You don't get to decide alone anymore. Ask it what it takes."

So Beatrix asked. "What memory?"

The stag regarded her for a long moment. The frost-lights drifted in its antlers, and in their glow Beatrix saw—or thought she saw—images moving in them, tiny and distant: a burning city, a standing stone gate, wolves running through snowfall that fell upward. A country remembering itself.

*Your mother,* said the stag.

The word went through her like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

"I don't—" Her voice cracked. She steadied it. "I don't understand."

*Twenty years you have kept her face behind your eyelids,* the stag said, gentle now, terribly gentle. *Every night you look at it. Every morning you check that it is still there. You have built your whole concealed life the way a snail builds a shell—around the wound of losing her. It is the strongest thing you carry, charm-seller. That is why I ask for it. I do not deal in trinkets.*

"No," Beatrix said.

The stag waited. It could wait; it was a mountain's worth of patience.

"Choose something else," she said. "Take Ashvale. Take the market. Take Marek's betrayal—I'll give you the smell of the sewers, the taste of fear, take the whole day of the raid, I won't miss it, I *want* to miss it. Take my childhood. Take—" Her voice broke apart entirely, and she hated herself for it, and pressed on anyway. "Take anything but her face."

*I cannot,* said the stag, and for the first time it sounded tired—an ancient, glacial weariness, like wind that had been blowing since the world began. *The bargain must cost what it costs. A cheap price buys nothing. You want the pass; the pass wants the weight. That is the whole of the law, and I did not write it.*

"Then we go around," Corvin said flatly. "We find another way."

"There is no other way," Beatrix said. She was surprised how calm she sounded. Inside, everything was screaming. "You know there isn't. Vessa's behind us, the ice is above us, and Pip—" She looked at the child. Pip was watching her with those wide, knowing eyes, and Beatrix thought: *I promised this kid a safe place. I promised them the door.* "There's no other way."

She faced the stag.

"What happens to her?" she asked. "Her face. When you take it. Does it go somewhere? Is it kept?"

*Everything is kept,* said the stag. *Nothing given to the mountain is lost. It becomes part of the pass. Part of the light in my antlers. Your mother's face will walk these heights with me, charm-seller, until the end of the long winter and past it. She will not be erased. She will be—* the frost-lights pulsed *—remembered somewhere better than a hiding place behind your eyelids.*

Something in that broke her open, and something else in that began to heal her, and both happened in the same terrible instant.

Because that was the truth underneath everything, wasn't it? The whole rotten architecture of her life—Ashvale, the stall, the whispered silver, twenty years of pretending to be small—that had all been built to protect one picture. A dead woman's face, hoarded like contraband, hidden like her gift, guarded like the last ember of a fire someone might stamp out. She had spent two decades keeping her mother in a drawer.

And the stag was offering to let her out.

"Will I forget her name?" Beatrix asked. Her tears were freezing on her cheeks. She didn't wipe them away. "Her voice? Will I know I loved her?"

*You will know that you loved,* the stag said. *You will know that you lost. You will know that someone taught you the whispers, and that the teaching was love, and that the loss broke something in you that has been slowly healing ever since you met a child in a wall.* The great head lowered until the frost-lights brushed her hair. *But her face—her face belongs to the mountain now. Grieve her well, charm-seller. Grief is also memory. Only the shape of her will be gone.*

Beatrix looked back, one last time, at her people. At Sorrel, whose amber eyes held an old grief of his own and no judgment at all. At Corvin, whose jaw was tight and whose hand had drifted from his hatchet to hover near her shoulder, wanting to stop her and knowing he couldn't. At Pip, who nodded once, small and fierce, like a soldier giving permission.

"I'm ready," she said.

*Look at me,* said the stag.

She looked. Deep into the ice-colored eyes, down through them into cold blue distances where light moved like drowned stars, and the stag's antlered shadow spread over her like wings, and the frost-lights in the tines began to drift downward, all of them at once, falling around her like slow bright snow—

—and Beatrix Blackwood reached behind her eyelids for her mother's face, and found it, and let it go.

* * *

There was no pain. That was the cruelest part.

She had expected tearing, some violent extraction, a scream. Instead it was like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was carrying. Like a hand unclenching she hadn't known was clenched. The frost-lights swirled around her once, twice, and flowed upward into the antlers, and one of them—she was sure of it, she would spend years being sure of it—glowed warmer than the others, a soft gold-white, like firelight on a kitchen wall, like flour on hands, like a voice singing low over a washtub.

Then the stag stepped back, and the pressure of its attention lifted, and Beatrix was standing in the snow with wet cheeks and an emptiness behind her eyes shaped exactly like a woman she could no longer see.

"Well?" Corvin's voice, careful. "Are you—did it work?"

"I don't know what you mean," she said honestly.

He flinched. So did Pip.

"She had dark hair," Beatrix said slowly, feeling along the edges of the hole, testing its shape. "I think. I know she had dark hair, but I can't—there's nothing above her mouth. It's like a portrait with the face scratched out. I remember her hands. Flour, always flour. I remember she hummed when she worked. I remember—" her breath caught "—I remember she taught me the whispers. I remember loving her. I remember that losing her is why I hid for twenty years. I remember everything except the one thing I most wanted to keep."

Pip crossed the snow and took her hand. Small fingers, chapped and cold, gripping hard.

"You loved her lots," the child said fiercely. "I can tell. It's still all over you."

Beatrix laughed—a broken, wet sound—and pulled the child against her side. "Yes," she said. "Yes, I expect it is."

Sorrel pressed his great head against her hip, briefly, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that a wolf hadn't already said better with his body.

*Come,* said the stag. It had turned toward the Hollow Throat, and where it stood the snow had begun to move—drifting aside, opening a path upward that hadn't existed moments before, walled with packed white and lit faintly by the lights in its antlers. *The pass opens for what I carry. Walk in my light and the Throat will not collect from you. Straggle outside it and the Throat makes its own bargains. Stay close, charm-seller. Stay very close.*

They climbed.

The path the stag opened was impossible—a smooth-walled corridor through snowpack taller than a man, winding up through the notch, glowing softly gold-white from within. Beatrix walked near the front, near the light, and tried not to think about what was glowing. Tried not to wonder which flicker of brightness had been her mother's laugh. The wind screamed overhead with its thousand thin voices, and sometimes she could almost pick words out of it—*names*, she thought, hundreds of names, the Throat reciting its collection—but the stag's light held them safe, and the voices thinned and fell away, and they climbed.

Twice, things watched them from the walls of the corridor. Shapes in the snowpack, dim and humanoid, faces pressed outward like figures behind frosted glass. Corvin's hand never left his hatchet. Pip, walking between them, studied the shapes with frank curiosity rather than fear.

"They're not dead," the child reported at one point, matter-of-fact. "They're sleeping. The mountain keeps them warm."

"Let's hope it doesn't wake any of them," Corvin muttered.

One shape, near the top, turned its head as they passed. For half a heartbeat Beatrix caught its profile through the ice—dark hair, a strong jaw—and her heart slammed against her ribs with a recognition she couldn't source, a grief with no object, a love pointing at nothing. Then the shape closed its eyes, and the moment passed, and she stood in the glowing corridor shaking, holding Pip's hand, mourning someone she couldn't name.

"That one's yours," Pip said quietly. Not a question.

"Don't," Beatrix said.

"Okay." The child squeezed her hand. "But Beatrix? I think she's happy here."

Beatrix said nothing. She climbed.

* * *

They came out the far side of the Hollow Throat at dusk, onto a high shoulder of rock overlooking the descent, and the world beyond stole the breath they had left.

Below them the mountains fell away into foothills, and beyond the foothills, far across a plain going blue with evening, something burned on the horizon. Not wildfire-orange. Cold-fire, aurora-green and violet, rising in slow pillars from a point impossibly distant, and even from here Beatrix felt it in her teeth—in the whisper-place where her gift lived—as though something enormous had rolled over in its sleep beneath the ice.

"The Door," Corvin breathed. Beside him, Pip's eyes had gone wide and glassy, fixed on the distant light, and the child's lips were moving slightly, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear.

*The Door,* the stag confirmed. It stood beside her at the overlook, its frost-lights dimmed now, spent, its coat catching the last red light of sunset. In the tines of its antlers, Beatrix counted the drifting points of light—hundreds, maybe thousands, memories collected across centuries of bargains—and among them, gold-white and warm, hers. *It wakes faster now. The long winter hurries it. You have days, charm-seller. Perhaps fewer.*

"Thank you," she said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it.

*Do not thank me yet.* The stag turned its ice-colored eyes on her, and for a moment she saw in them not coldness but something achingly like pity. *The Door shows every traveler their deepest belonging before it opens. Most who look upon it cannot leave it. Some cannot enter it. What it will show you, I do not know—but I know what you traded to reach it, and so I will say this:* the great head bowed, so the antlered shadow fell over her like a blessing, *what the mountain holds, the mountain gives back to those who keep faith with it. Remember loudly, charm-seller. Remember out loud, in company, in defiance. The country you are walking into dies of silence. Do not let yourself die of it too.*

And then it stepped off the edge of the overlook and walked down the cliff face without disturbing the snow, fading as it went, until it was only light, and then only a suggestion, and then only wind.

They stood a long time at the top of the world, four travelers and a hole in one heart.

"We should move," Corvin said finally. "Light's going. If we make the lower slopes tonight—"

"Beatrix." Pip tugged her sleeve. The child was still staring at the distant green fire, and their voice had gone strange again, hollow, listening. "Beatrix, it knows we're coming. The voice under the ice. It says—" Pip frowned, struggling, and then looked up at her with eyes too old and too certain, and said:

"It says the door has been expecting someone named Blackwood. It says the door remembers her mother. And it says—" the child swallowed "—it says the door knows what she looks like. Even if you don't."
