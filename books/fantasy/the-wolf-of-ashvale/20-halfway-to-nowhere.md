## 20. Halfway to Nowhere

Pip counted the ways Beatrix didn't remember.

She didn't remember the sewer, and the way she'd held her hand out flat and said *rats first, then us*, like the rats deserved the courtesy. She didn't remember teaching Pip to whistle through a blade of grass so the pigeons wouldn't startle when they passed under the bell tower. She didn't remember that Pip hated the crusts of bread and that she'd started tearing them off without being asked, somewhere around the second night in the undercity, and had never once made a thing of it.

Mostly she didn't remember the wall. The gap behind the collapsed cistern where Pip had lived for a winter, small enough to fold up inside it, and how Beatrix had knelt down in the dark with a candle and said, *Hello. I'm not going to grab you. I'm going to put this bread here and walk ten steps back, and you can decide.* Nobody had ever let Pip decide anything before.

That was gone. That was all gone, paid out to a white stag in a mountain pass for a road through rock that no living map could find, and Pip understood — Pip did understand, that was the worst part — that the price had been fair, or fair enough, or at least survivable, which was what fair meant these days. A face. One face. Her mother's face, traded away so the rest of them could keep breathing.

But knowing didn't stop it hurting. Knowing was just the shape the hurt came in.

They were three days out from the pass now, walking down out of the high country along a river that ran grey with glacier milk, and Beatrix had not yet looked at Pip the way she used to look at Pip. Pip knew every version of her looking. There was the market look, quick and sideways, checking whether anyone watched. There was the tired look, aimed at fires and maps. There was the look she gave Sorrel, long and complicated, full of twenty years of pretending she hadn't heard him. And there was the Pip look — warm, crooked at one corner, like she was always half about to laugh at something Pip had done or was about to do.

The new Beatrix looked at Pip the way she looked at strangers on a road. Polite. Careful. Estimating distance.

Pip decided to hate her for it.

It lasted most of a morning.

* * *

"You're limping," Beatrix said at noon, when they stopped by the river to fill the skins.

"I'm fine."

"Your left boot's been dragging since the scree. Let me see your foot."

"Don't touch me."

Beatrix went still. Corvin, downstream, pretended very hard to be interested in the water. Sorrel lay with his chin on his paws and his eyes open, watching everything, saying nothing, because he was the only one who remembered everything and he had apparently decided that remembering out loud would make it worse.

"It's not an order," Beatrix said quietly. "I just want to check you haven't split the sole."

"You don't know I hate people touching my feet," Pip said, and heard their own voice come out too loud and too young, and hated that too. "You don't know anything about me. You traded me away."

"I traded a memory—"

"You traded *me*. You traded the day we met. You walked into that pass and you gave her the part of you that found me in the wall, and now I'm—" Pip's throat closed. They pushed past it the way you push through a bramble, fast, before it can catch you. "Now I'm some kid you're traveling with. Some stray. You keep doing the polite voice at me. You never did the polite voice."

Beatrix sat back on her heels. For a moment she looked like she might say something sensible, something adult, something about how memories weren't the same as people and how love wasn't only stored in the head. Pip could see the sensible thing assembling itself behind her eyes and wanted to scream at it before it got out.

Instead she said, "Tell me."

"What?"

"Tell me about the day we met." She folded her hands in her lap, deliberately, like a woman making herself stay put. "I'm not asking you to forgive anything. I'm asking you to tell me what I lost, so I know what it cost you to watch me lose it."

"That's not fair."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing out here is. But I'd rather hear it than pretend it didn't happen. Pretending is how Ashvale got the way it is." She nodded at the ground across the fire ring, at the space she was leaving open. "Sit if you want. Or stand. Or go be furious at the river for a while. Your choice. That part I'm told I was good at."

Pip hadn't known they were going to cry until it was already happening, hot and furious and silent, the way they'd learned to do it in the wall where noise meant death. They sat down across from her anyway. Not close. Across. There were rules now, and Pip was going to enforce every single one of them.

"The cistern had fallen in on itself," Pip said, staring at the river instead of at her. "There was a gap behind it, big enough for me and a blanket and a tin cup. I'd been there since autumn. People threw stuff near it sometimes. Bones, mostly. Once somebody threw a whole chicken."

Corvin snorted despite himself.

"And then you came," Pip said. "With a candle. And I could feel every rat in three streets turning toward you like flowers to sun, and I thought, oh no, oh no, she's one of the loud ones, she's going to bring the whole city down on my hole in the ground. But you talked to the rats like they were neighbors. Not pets. Neighbors. You asked permission." Pip's voice cracked again and they let it, this time. "Nobody asks permission."

"And then?" Beatrix said softly.

"Then you said hello. And you put the bread down. And you walked ten steps back." Pip finally looked at her. "And I decided. Me. I decided to follow you. That was mine. You didn't take it, you didn't trade it, the stag couldn't have it even if she ate the whole rest of your head. The deciding was mine and it still is. So don't you dare sit there being sorry at me like I'm a sad story. I'm not a sad story. I'm the best decision you ever made and then forgot."

The silence after that was enormous.

Downstream, Corvin had stopped pretending. He was staring at Pip with something that on any other man Pip would have called awe. Sorrel's tail moved once against the stones — a slow, deliberate thump, wolfish applause.

"All right," Beatrix said. Her voice had gone rough. "All right. Then here's what happens now." She pulled off her own left boot, turned it over, and showed Pip the sole — worn through at the heel, patched twice with waxed thread, failing. "I've been limping for two days and hiding it, because I don't want to be the one who slows everyone down. So. Fair trade. You show me your foot, I'll show you mine, and neither of us gets to do the proud thing. Deal?"

Pip glared at her. It was a trap. It was a kind, clever, infuriating trap, built exactly the way the old Beatrix would have built it, except the old Beatrix hadn't needed to build traps because she'd simply known.

It was also, Pip realized, working.

They swapped boots' worth of feet. Pip's heel was rubbed raw but unbroken; Beatrix's sole was worse than she'd let on. While Corvin dug needle and thread out of his pack and went to work on her boot with the frowning concentration of a man defusing something, Beatrix sat beside Pip with her stockinged foot in the cold shallows and said nothing, which was its own kind of skill, and slowly, grudgingly, like ice going out of a river, Pip's fury began to move.

"You'll get it wrong sometimes," Pip said at last. "Things I told you. You'll hand me bread with the crusts on."

"I'll learn again."

"You'll ask me my favorite color like a stranger."

"I already know it's green," Beatrix said. "Ysolt mentioned it. But ask me again in a month and I'll have earned it properly."

"That doesn't count."

"No," she agreed. "But it's a start. Starts are allowed to be ugly." She glanced sideways at Pip, and there — there, just for a heartbeat, flickering up like a match in wind — was the crooked almost-laugh. "For what it's worth, whoever I was, she had very good taste in strays."

"She wasn't a stray either," Pip muttered. "She was a person."

"So am I. New model. Slightly used. Fewer memories, better boots." She flexed her mended foot. "Come on. We should make the ford bend before dark. Corvin says there's shelter past it."

Pip stood, and after a moment — not forgiveness, not yet, forgiveness was a door that took time to unlock — held out a hand. Beatrix took it. Her grip was warm and callused and careful, and Pip thought: *she held my hand like this in the sewers, probably, and she doesn't know, and someday she will.*

Love, it turned out, didn't live in the memory. Love lived in the holding-on.

* * *

They made the bend by late afternoon. The shelter Corvin remembered from his hunting years turned out to be a drover's bothy, stone-walled, roof half-caved, but the windward corner stood and there was old peat stacked inside like someone had meant to come back. Corvin got a fire going small and smokeless, the way huntsmen do, and Sorrel curled across the doorway like a living bar, and for an hour the world contracted to warmth and chewing and Pip showing Beatrix, patiently, condescendingly, how to whistle through a blade of grass.

"You're a terrible teacher," Beatrix said, spraying spit.

"You're a terrible student. Old you learned it in an afternoon."

"Old me had motivation. Young me had a smug child mocking her."

"Yes," Pip said serenely. "That's the method."

Sorrel huffed — the wolf-laugh, silent and shaking — and Corvin smiled into the fire, and for a little while Pip let themselves forget, on purpose, that they were fugitives halfway between nowhere and nowhere, camped in a country that was forgetting how to be alive.

Then Sorrel lifted his head.

It happened without transition, the way it always did with him — one moment a tired old wolf sprawled by a fire, the next a statue with his nose into the wind, every line of him gone taut as wire. Pip felt it through their own gift a half-second later, the way they always felt things now, since the mountain: the world's animals turning toward them like weathervanes in a storm.

"Wolves?" Beatrix asked quietly, hand already finding Pip's shoulder.

"No." Sorrel's voice was gravel and regret. "Horses. Many. And iron." His ears flattened. "Behind us, downriver. Riding our back-trail."

Corvin was on his feet, peat fire dead under his boot-heel, face suddenly ten years older and twenty years harder. "How far?"

"Far enough that we have until dawn," Sorrel said. "No more." He turned his great head, and his amber eyes settled on Pip with something that was not quite fear but was its cousin. "And boy or girl or whatever you are — the horses aren't the problem. Something is riding ahead of them. Something the animals won't speak of." His lip curled back from his teeth. "Something wearing red."

Pip pressed closer to Beatrix without meaning to, and Beatrix's arm came around them, automatic, certain, the grip of someone who had decided again — a second time, a new time, all on her own — and this time Pip felt it land somewhere the stag's price couldn't touch.

"Dawn," Beatrix said. "Then we run."
