## 21. The Warden's Net

I smelled the troop before I saw them — leather and horse sweat and the particular sour tang of men who have been riding hard and resenting it. Twenty years of wearing the Warden grey teaches you that smell, and twenty years since taking it off has not taught me to forget it.

We were in a hollow below the pass road, Beatrix binding my shoulder where the ford wound had opened again, Pip dozing against Sorrel's flank, Ysolt's half-finished map spread out on a flat stone between us. The map was mostly blank. The blank parts were the ones that frightened her, and therefore me.

"Corvin." Beatrix's head came up first. She had the best ears of any of us, though she'd deny it — she'd spent so long listening for things she wasn't supposed to hear that hearing had become her native tongue. "Horses."

"How many?"

"More than us."

That was the whole truth of our company and always had been.

I got up too fast; my shoulder screamed and I let it, because pain is honest in ways reports are not. From the lip of the hollow I could see them coming down the pass road in column: Warden riders, grey cloaks dusted with snow, lances couched, and at their head a bay mare I knew better than I knew my own mother's face.

Vessa.

She rode like she always rode, upright as a fence post, chin down into the wind, no flourish wasted. She'd been my second for nine years. She'd been my student before that. She'd sat across from me at a hundred night fires while I taught her how to read a trail, how to read a man, how to read the space between what a person says and what they mean. And now she was reading mine, and finding the space wide enough to drive a troop through.

"They've seen us," Sorrel said. He didn't lift his head from his paws. His voice, when he chose to use it aloud, still startled people who hadn't gotten used to it — deep and rough as a stone dragged over stone. "They'll want to take you alive. All of you. Vessa's orders."

"You know her orders?"

"I know the shape of them. She stopped at the ridge an hour ago and let the hounds breathe. You don't let hounds breathe when you mean to kill." His yellow eyes came around to me. "You mean something else to her, Huntsman. Decide quickly what you mean to her."

The company assembled itself around me without anyone calling it together, the way it had learned to do. Beatrix stood with her hands loose at her sides, silver charms glinting faintly at her wrists — not the harmless market trinkets anymore, but things she whispered to now openly, because there was no more pretending left in her life. Pip woke and scrambled up, small fists full of map. Ysolt put herself slightly forward, which for a woman of seventy-odd was either foolishness or strategy, and I had learned by then never to bet against Ysolt.

Below, the troop broke from column into line. Twenty riders. Maybe more behind the rise. A horn sounded once, short and flat — *identified, halt and yield* — the oldest signal in the Warden book, older than the purge, older than me.

"Options," Beatrix said. It wasn't a question. It was how she asked for my opinion while making clear she already had her own.

"Run," I said. "They're mounted. We're not. Running buys us ten minutes and a knife in the back."

"Fight?"

"Twenty against four and a wolf and whatever Ysolt is hiding under those shawls. I've fought worse odds." I looked at her. "But I've never fought them with anything worth losing before."

Something moved in her face — not fear. Recognition. She nodded slowly.

"Then what?"

"Then I go down and stand in front of them," I said, "and tell the truth out loud for the first time in twenty years."

* * *

I walked down the pass road with my hands empty and open, which every soldier knows is either surrender or the beginning of something much worse. Snow squeaked under my boots. My shoulder throbbed in time with my heartbeat, and I let both keep time, because a steady man is harder to shoot than a limping one.

Vessa brought the line to a halt forty paces out and rode forward alone, exactly as I would have done, exactly as I had taught her to do. Up close she looked tired. Not weak — Vessa didn't have a weak setting — but worn through in a way the last months had ground into all of us who wore the grey honestly, and some of us who hadn't.

"Hale." Her voice carried the way voices carry in cold air, flat and far. "You're a hard man to find. Or an easy one to lose track of. The reports differ."

"The reports lie," I said. "Mine did."

"Yours especially." She dismounted. That surprised me. Vessa fought from horseback the way other men breathed; dismounting was her version of baring her throat, or drawing steel. She walked to within ten paces and stopped. Behind her visor of frost-stiffened hair her eyes went past my shoulder, cataloguing: Beatrix. Pip. Sorrel. Ysolt. "Where are your wounds, Hale? I read the ford report. Three dead outriders. Your blade-work or someone else's?"

"Mine."

"Against your own brothers."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was the question I had been rehearsing answers to for two hundred miles, standing watches, walking night roads, and every answer I'd drafted was a lie shaped like a truth, and looking at her now I found I couldn't say a single one of them. So I said the true thing instead, the naked thing, the thing I'd been circling since the day I wrote a list of names and set a country on fire with it.

"Because I helped start this," I said. "Years ago. Before the proclamation, before the purge had a name. There was a census. Quiet work. They asked me to identify the gifted — beast-speakers, charm-hands, hearth-witches, the small magics, the ones easy to find because they can't stop being kind. I made a list. Four hundred and eleven names." My voice held. I was almost proud of that. "Your name isn't on it because you're clean, Vessa. Their names aren't on it because they hid better than I hunted. But Beatrix Blackwood's mother is on it. And she died of my handwriting."

Silence, except the horses shifting and the wind combing the pass.

Behind me I heard Pip make a small sound, and Beatrix's hand settle on the child's shoulder — steadying the child, or herself, I couldn't tell and didn't turn to see. Some confessions should be witnessed straight-on.

"That's not in any report," Vessa said at last.

"No."

"Because you buried it."

"Because burying it felt like surviving." I took a breath. "It wasn't. It was just a slower way of dying. And then I met a woman who sells lies for coins and tells the truth to rats, and a child who makes mountains listen, and a wolf older than my commission, and an old woman carrying a ledger of everyone we erased — and I found out what I actually am, Vessa. I'm the list. Everything after that is just me trying to be something else."

"You're a traitor," she said. There was no heat in it. That was the worst part. "Under Warden law, that's a hanging word."

"I know the law. I helped write the amendments."

"And you expect me to believe this confession? Stand down twenty riders because Corvin Hale had an epiphany on a mountain?" Her hand rested on her rein, not her sword — I noted that, filed it away. "Give me something, Hale. Give me one reason that isn't sentiment."

"Her name was Maren Blackwood," I said. "She kept bees. She was on my list because a neighbor saw her talking to a hive and thought it charming until the criers made it criminal. When the Wardens came she didn't run. She sent her daughter out the back with a basket of charms and stayed in the doorway so they'd only burn one house." I watched Vessa's face do nothing, absolutely nothing, which is what her face does when everything inside is moving. "You asked for one reason. There it is. I don't expect it to move you. I expect it to sit in you the way it sits in me, and I know exactly how long that takes to kill a man."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, quietly, in the voice she used for reports rather than battlefields:

"I have orders, Corvin. Bring the speaker alive. The others—" She didn't finish it. She didn't need to.

"I know the others."

"So this is it." Her hand finally moved — to her sword. "You're defecting. Here. Now. In front of witnesses."

"In front of witnesses," I agreed. "Best way I know to make it stick."

And that was when Ysolt came down the hill.

* * *

I heard her before I saw her, and what I heard made the hair on my arms stand up under my coat, because it was singing — an old woman's cracked, unhurried singing, in a language I did not know and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a face in a dream. The villagers' grandmother, the ledger-keeper, the woman whose shawls I had privately catalogued as *hiding a bad hip*, came down the pass road with thirty people behind her.

Not villagers with pitchforks. Villagers with lanterns. In broad daylight, lit anyway, each flame burning white and steady in the wind that should have killed them.

Ysolt walked to the front of them and stopped beside me, and up close I saw that the song was coming from her, low in her chest, and that the lantern flames were keeping time with it.

"Wardens," she said pleasantly, to Vessa, as if greeting neighbors over a fence. "You're a long way from your garrison."

"Madam, disperse your—"

"Oh, hush." She sang one note higher. Every lantern flared. "You came hunting a net's worth of fugitives with twenty men. I came with everyone whose name is in my book. Do you understand the difference? You have orders. We have memories. Orders burn. Memories don't."

"What are you?" Vessa asked, and I will give her this: her voice didn't shake. Much.

"A keeper of names. Same trade as the girl behind me, different shelf." Ysolt's eyes glinted. "And I'll tell you a secret, Warden, since you look like a woman dying of secrets. My family kept bees too."

The lanterns rose. Not thrown — *rose*, lifting off their poles and handles and drifting upward into the grey air, thirty small suns arranging themselves in a ring above the pass road. Underneath them the snow began to fall sideways, then to hang, suspended, each flake caught mid-air like the country itself holding its breath. I had seen Warden hedge-mages work. This was not that. This was older, patient, domestic magic — the magic of hearths and hives and ledgers, the magic that had never needed a law against it because no one believed it existed.

Vessa's horse screamed and bolted sideways. Half the line followed it. Discipline fraying at the edges, exactly where I'd taught her to watch for it.

"Last chance, Hale," Vessa called, and her voice cracked at last, one hairline fracture, human. "Stand aside. I'll report you dead. It's more mercy than you'll get otherwise."

"I'm done being reported," I said. "Dead or otherwise."

She looked at me for a long moment across the floating light. Then she did the thing I will remember until I die, the thing that made me certain I had not wasted nine years teaching her: she looked past me, at Beatrix, at the child, at the wolf, at the old woman with her impossible lanterns, and she counted. I watched her count. Watched her weigh twenty riders against whatever this was, and orders against the crack in her own voice, and I watched her arrive at the arithmetic I'd arrived at on a frozen ford with blood in my mouth.

"Fall back to the ridge," she said. "Rally point Ash."

"Commander—" a sergeant began.

"*Rally point Ash.*" She turned her horse. At the edge of the lantern-light she paused, and did not look back, and said, loud enough for me alone: "This isn't finished, Corvin."

"No," I agreed. "But it's started."

The troop withdrew up the pass road, sullen, unbroken, alive. Ysolt let the lanterns down gently, one by one, like a woman bringing in washing. The snow resumed falling the ordinary way, which after the other thing looked almost obscene.

Beatrix came and stood beside me. For a while neither of us spoke. Below us the pass road lay empty, and the wind carried off the last of the horses, and Pip ran down the hill to demand of Ysolt, at top volume, how long she had been able to do *that* and why nobody ever told Pip anything.

"Well," Beatrix said finally. "That was either the bravest or stupidest thing I've ever seen, and I once watched a rat negotiate toll terms."

"Both, probably."

"She'll come back. With more. With worse."

"I know."

"And you did it anyway. Stood down there and handed her your worst self on a plate." She glanced at me sidelong, and there was something in her face I couldn't name — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but its groundwork, surveyors' stakes marking where forgiveness might someday break ground. "Why?"

"Because you can't build anything on a buried foundation," I said. "I tried for twenty years. Turns out the list rots everything built over it."

She considered that. "Maren Blackwood," she said quietly. "You said her name."

"I'm going to say a lot of names before this is over. Starting with hers."

Beatrix nodded slowly, and then Ysolt's voice cut across the hillside, sharp as a whip-crack, and every warm feeling on that slope froze solid.

"Everyone," the old woman called, and her hand was shaking, and she was staring north up the pass toward the high snowfields, where the light had gone strange — thick, blue, wrong, the color of deep ice under pressure. "Look north. Look at the sky."

We looked.

Snow was falling out of the peaks in a wall, silent and total, swallowing the ridgeline whole — falling, I realized with a hunter's old cold certainty, faster than any storm moves, falling like something *arriving*.

Sorrel lifted his head, and his hackles rose along his spine like a row of drawn knives.

"The winter's done waiting," he said. "It's coming for the Door. And it knows we're carrying the map."
