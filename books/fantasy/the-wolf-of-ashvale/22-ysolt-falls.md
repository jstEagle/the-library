## 22. Ysolt Falls

The pass had a name once. Ysolt said it was called the Shepherd's Throat, because the mountains narrowed there to a gap no wider than a market lane, and because shepherds had driven their flocks through it for a hundred generations before the maps burned. Now it was just stone and wind and the only way north.

"We hold here," Ysolt said, "or we don't arrive."

She said it the way she said everything — flat, unhurried, like a woman reading entries in a ledger. But Beatrix had learned to read what was under the flatness. Ysolt's hands were steady on her walking staff, but her knuckles were white around it. She knew what was coming up that pass as well as any of them did.

Below them, down the switchbacks they'd climbed since dawn, the Warden column moved through the valley like ink spilled on snow. Beatrix counted banners and stopped counting at nine. Vessa had not come with a patrol this time. She had come with an army's worth of spite.

"She'll send riders up the throat first," Corvin said. He crouched at the lip of the rocks they'd chosen, his wounded side bound tight under his coat, his voice clipped and professional in the way Beatrix now understood was how he hid everything else. "Two abreast. Maybe three. The horses will hate it."

"Good," Sorrel said. The wolf stood on the highest stone, gray against gray, his ears moving through the wind. "Let them hate every step."

"There's another path." Pip sat hugging their knees against a boulder, chin tucked, eyes shut — listening, always listening now. "Down by the river. I can hear them thinking about it. Not words. Just — the shape of it."

"How long?" Beatrix asked.

Pip opened their eyes. Eight years old, and they had learned to give bad news without flinching, which was the kind of education no child should receive. "Before dark. Maybe sooner. The horses are fresh."

Beatrix looked at Ysolt. The old woman had not taken her eyes off the column below.

"You could still go back," Beatrix said quietly. "The village will take you in. They'll hide you the way they hid us."

"The village fed you from my stores and sheltered you under my roof," Ysolt said. "If Vessa burns it for that, burning me beside it changes nothing except that I'd die a coward. I've been many things, girl. I don't intend to add that one." She shifted her grip on the staff, and Beatrix saw — really saw, for the first time — how thin the old woman's wrists had gotten. How much of her strength had been spent already, on the road, on the map, on the ledger she carried wrapped in oilcloth against her heart like a second heartbeat.

They made their preparations in the short gray afternoon. Corvin showed Sorrel where to stand so that a wolf appearing suddenly on the high rocks would turn a horse before a spear ever had to. He showed Pip how to roll loose stones down the scree slope in a controlled slide rather than a scream of them. He did not look at Beatrix when he explained where he himself would stand — front and visible, wearing nothing that marked him a Warden anymore, a deserter in plain view of the men he'd trained.

"They won't all shoot," he said. "Some of them hesitated at the ford."

"Some of them didn't," Beatrix said.

"No," Corvin agreed. "That's what I'm for."

* * *

The riders came an hour before dark, exactly as Pip had promised, and the first hour went almost the way Corvin had drawn it in the dirt.

Almost.

The throat of the pass swallowed them two abreast, just as he'd said, and the horses hated it, just as he'd said, and when Sorrel rose onto the skyline with the wind flattening his fur and gave voice — a sound Beatrix felt in her sternum more than heard, something older than language — the lead horses screamed and wheeled and unseated their riders into the rocks. Stones sang down the scree where Pip pushed them, precise as a bell-ringer. And Corvin stepped out into the open with his empty hands raised and his voice carrying down the defile, calling names — *Alder, Fenwick, Tammas Bright, I know your mothers' names, I know what Vessa promised you, and I know what she'll write in her report if you live* — and Beatrix watched the front rank of the column waver like a candle flame in a draft.

For one whole minute, she let herself believe.

Then the arrows came from the ridge above them.

Not the riders. A second party — Vessa's outriders, sent wide and high while everyone watched the throat, exactly the kind of thing Corvin would have thought of and had been too slow to say aloud, because he was standing in the open being a target instead of being a tactician. The shafts came hissing down through the failing light, and Beatrix screamed the warning, and everyone scattered, and it should have been enough, it would have been enough—

Ysolt did not scatter.

Later, Beatrix would go over it a thousand times, turning it like a charm in her palm, looking for the moment she could have changed it. Ysolt had been moving toward the scree slope, toward the place where the outriders would have to climb down if they wanted to press the attack, and she had been slow — slower than she used to be, slower than she believed herself to be — and one arrow found the gap between the stones she sheltered behind, and one found her.

Beatrix reached her before the echo of the second shot died.

The old woman was sitting up against the rock with both hands pressed to her side, which was wrong, Beatrix thought wildly, people clutching wounds always pressed too high or too low, but Ysolt had kept ledgers her whole life and she knew precisely where the entry was. Blood came through her fingers anyway, black in the dusk.

"No," Beatrix said. "No, no — hold still, I'll—"

"Don't be stupid," Ysolt said. Her voice was level. It cost her; Beatrix could see it costing her, see the tremor she refused to let reach her mouth. "Arrows are cheap. Don't waste the hour on me that you'll need for the mountain."

Above them, the fight had turned into noise — Sorrel's snarling, steel ringing, Pip screaming something that made the stones themselves shiver. Beatrix knelt in the blood and pressed her own hands over Ysolt's and whispered to the flesh the way she'd whispered to silver for twenty years: *hold, knit, stay.* And the flesh tried. She felt it try. But some things were not charms, and some wounds were past whispering to, and the blood kept coming up between their fingers warm as tea.

"Listen to me." Ysolt caught her wrist. For a dying woman her grip was astonishing. "You were going to get the truth at the Door. You're getting it early, and you're getting it fast, so listen."

"I'm listening." Tears blurred the pass, the torches coming up the throat, everything. "I'm listening."

Ysolt fumbled at her coat with her free hand. Beatrix helped her, hating herself for helping, and together they drew out the oilcloth bundle — the ledger, the book of erased names, twenty years of a village remembering what a kingdom had ordered forgotten. Ysolt unwrapped it with shaking hands and turned to the end, and tore.

The last page came free with a sound like a small rip in the world.

"My grandmother copied these pages when the burning started," Ysolt said. "She was a clerk in the palace at Veylmar. When the crown erased the country — the records, the maps, the names — she stole what she could and ran west, and she taught my mother, and my mother taught me: *the Door is not a door.* Everyone thinks it's a door. It isn't."

"What is it?"

"A threshold. A — a weighing." Ysolt coughed, and the cough brought red to her lips, and she wiped it impatiently with the back of her hand like a woman annoyed at spilled ink. "It doesn't open for strength. It doesn't open for blood alone — plenty carried the blood, and it stayed shut, and the winter never ended. Read it. Read the page. My grandmother's hand is small; you'll need light."

There was no light. There was only the burning torches far below and the aurora just beginning, faintly, at the northern edge of the sky, green as new leaves. Beatrix held the torn page close and made out the faded script line by line, and as she read, the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the mountain.

*The Gate of Ashvale opens only for one who carries Veylmar's name willingly — spoken by their own mouth, claimed by their own choice, borne knowing its price. The name is not inherited. It is accepted.*

"Carries Veylmar's name," Beatrix whispered.

"Your grandmother's grandmother took it," Ysolt said. "When the wolves chose the Blackwoods as wardens of the memory, they gave them a name out of the old country — a name the crown then erased along with everything else. Your family has carried it underground for four generations, hidden inside 'Blackwood,' passed down like a coin sewn into a hem." Her eyes were losing their focus, but her voice held, iron to the last inch. "I was going to tell you at the Door. Make you say it yourself, make you choose it with open eyes, because that's the whole point — it means nothing given, everything chosen. But I'm out of time, and you're about to be out of excuses."

"Tell me," Beatrix said. "Tell me the name."

"No." Ysolt almost smiled. Blood on her teeth, and she almost smiled. "Not mine to say. That's the cruelty of it, and the mercy. It's written on the page. In her hand. Say it when you're standing at the stone, and mean it, and understand what you're buying." She sagged, and Beatrix caught her, and the page crinkled between them, and Beatrix folded it desperately into the inner pocket of her coat, against her own heart, where the ledger had lived.

"Stay with me," Beatrix said. "Ysolt. Stay."

"I've stayed eighty-one years in a country that forgot me," Ysolt murmured. "I'd like to see if anywhere else remembers better." Her hand found Beatrix's again and gripped it once, hard, a signature at the bottom of a page. "Girl. Listen. Last entry. Whatever you find past that gate — the dead, the echoes, the wolves, all of it — do not seal yourself in with them out of grief. Grief is a liar. It dresses itself up as duty. Promise me you'll know the difference."

"I promise," Beatrix said, and meant it, and would spend a long time learning how hard promises are to keep.

Ysolt's grip loosened. Her eyes went past Beatrix, up the pass, to where the aurora was strengthening, and something in her face eased the way a ledger eases when the columns finally balance.

"There it is," she said. "Been falling toward us my whole life. The long winter." A breath. "Don't let it win because you're sad. Sadness is allowed. Winning is required."

Then the old woman who had kept the memory of a murdered country alive in a village that wasn't on any map closed her eyes, very gently, like someone setting down a pen at the end of a long day's work, and did not pick it up again.

* * *

They buried her above the treeline, where the ground was too stony for digging, under a cairn they built from the pass itself. Corvin worked in silence, hauling stones with his ruined side until Beatrix feared the wound would reopen; Sorrel paced the perimeter and would not let anyone come near; Pip laid sprigs of a mountain flower at the head of the cairn, flowers whose name Pip knew and Beatrix did not, and told Beatrix later that Ysolt had pointed them out on the road and said they bloomed only where nothing else bothered to grow.

Vessa's column did not press the attack that night. Whether it was the losses at the throat, or the dark, or the sight of a wolf standing vigil on a burial cairn like something out of a story the Wardens had been raised to disbelieve, Beatrix never learned. They heard the retreat horns down in the valley, thin and bitter on the wind.

By dawn the company stood at the cairn one last time. Snow was falling. Beatrix noticed it before anyone spoke — noticed it and went cold in a way the morning could not explain, because it was spring, and this snow fell straight down in vast unhurried flakes, silent as pages turning, and it did not feel like weather. It felt like intention.

"It's starting," Sorrel said quietly. "The long winter. Ysolt saw it coming. Now we can all see it." He looked north, where the aurora burned broad and bright across the whole sky, answering the snowfall like a signal to a signal. "Every day it falls, the Door grows harder to open. And when the winter finishes falling—" He did not finish. He didn't have to. Ysolt's map had finished it for him: *when the last snow lands, the threshold closes for good, and the dead country keeps its silence forever.*

Beatrix put her hand flat against her coat, over the pocket where the torn page lay against her heart. She thought of Ysolt's voice: *it means nothing given, everything chosen.* She thought of a name hidden four generations deep, waiting to be spoken at a standing stone by someone willing to pay what it cost.

"Then we walk faster," she said.

They shouldered their packs and turned north into the rising snow, four mourners and a debt, and the mountain received them the way the old woman would have approved of: without ceremony, and without mercy, and without forgetting.
