## 24. Ashvale Below

The mountain gave them the view as a kind of cruelty.

They had climbed since dawn through cloud and scree, Sorrel ranging ahead with his head low to the wind, Pip riding on Corvin's back because their legs had given out two hours past and they were too proud to say so. Beatrix walked last, the way she always did now, counting her people forward like coins she could not afford to lose. Four. She had four left. Ysolt's map was folded against her heart, half-finished and ink-stained, the last page of the ledger a blank rectangle where the old woman's blood had soaked through before she could write what came next.

Then the clouds tore open below them, and there was Ashvale.

She had expected it to look small. Cities always did from a height — she'd seen the capital once, from the ridge above the king's road, and thought it looked like spilled salt. But Ashvale did not look small. It looked like a wound that had learned to glow.

Smoke rose in nine columns. She counted them twice, three times, telling herself the number would shrink if she only counted properly, but it held at nine, and beneath each column the city burned in its own color: the tallow-yellow of the market quarter where her stall still stood empty under its canvas, the oily black of the tanneries, and along the river district a strange blue-white flame that Beatrix recognized with a lurch of the stomach because she had seen it only once before, twenty years ago, on the night the Wardens burned a hedge-witch named Odile out of the candlemaker's row. Warden-fire. It did not eat wood. It ate what was woven into things.

It ate magic.

"Oh," Pip said, very small.

Beatrix realized she had stopped walking. The wind pushed at her back, trying to move her onward up the pass, and she stood against it like something rooted, watching the city where she had spent her whole careful life being nobody.

* * *

Twenty years. That was the arithmetic she kept reaching for and could not finish. Twenty years of whispering to silver behind a shuttered stall, of charms that worked just enough to keep customers coming and never enough to draw notice, of learning every face on the market row and which ones could be trusted with a smile and which only with silence. Twenty years of pretending not to hear the wolf in the alleys. All of it built on one foundation: that if she stayed small enough, quiet enough, harmless enough, the fire would never find her.

And she had stayed. When the proclamation went up, when Marek's betrayal sent Wardens into her stall with iron gloves and a net, when every sensible instinct screamed *run* — some part of her had still believed the city itself would protect her. Not the walls or the guards. The ordinary weight of it. The thousand small kindnesses she had seeded through the market for two decades: the fever-charm sold cheap to the fishwife, the luck-knot given free to the boy who swept the tanner's yard, the whispered word to a lame mule that let it pull carts another six years. Surely all that accumulated gentleness counted for something. Surely somewhere in Ashvale's stone heart there was room for one more hidden thing.

The blue-white flames said otherwise.

"Is that the market?" Pip asked. Their voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a child who had learned early that questions could be dangerous. "The yellow smoke."

"Yes." Beatrix made herself say it steadily. "That's the market."

"My wall was near the market."

She knew. Pip's wall — the gap in the undercity brick where she'd found an eight-year-old curled around a rat like a stolen loaf — stood four streets from where her stall had been. Every animal within streets turned to look at Pip; that was how she'd found them, following a tide of staring pigeons into the dark. And now the whole district was burning, and Beatrix caught herself doing the terrible arithmetic again: *if we hadn't run, if I'd gone quietly to the registration, if* — and stopped, because the end of that road led to a cell and then to a pyre, and Pip would have been taken anyway, Pip with a gift too loud to hide even in a wall. There had never been a version of this where staying saved anything. But knowing a thing is true does not stop it from cutting.

Corvin came and stood beside her. He didn't touch her shoulder; he had learned that much about her by now. He simply stood, a big weathered shape against the wind, looking down at the city he had once served.

"That's reprisal," he said. His voice was rough, stripped of officer's polish. "Not a purge. A purge has lists, procedure. This—" He nodded at the nine columns. "This is punishment. Someone reported speakers harbored in Ashvale, and command decided the whole city had chosen sides."

"The whole city *did* choose sides," Beatrix said. "Most of them chose without knowing there was a choice."

"Which is why it works." He said it bitterly, an ex-soldier explaining the machinery of his own betrayal. "You burn a few streets, you don't need to burn the rest. Everyone else learns the lesson for free."

Below them, the wind carried nothing — too far, too high — but Beatrix imagined the sound anyway. Imagined Marek's workshop street, whether his daughter had been evacuated or merely relocated to some Warden registry where her father's debt would be collected over years. Imagined the fishwife with her fever-charm, and whether she'd thrown it into the gutter fast enough when the riders came. Imagined the boy who swept the tannery yard, and the luck-knot, and what a knot of wolf-whispered silver looked like to a man holding Warden-fire.

She had made those things to protect people. Now each one might be an accusation.

"I did this," she said.

"You didn't—"

"Not alone. Not on purpose. But I stayed, Corvin. Two decades I stayed, selling my little lies, and every charm I whispered was a thread tying me to that place, and when the thread finally pulled tight, everything tied to it pulled too." Her hands had closed into fists without her noticing. Her nails bit her palms. "Ysolt died on a mountain pass so I could reach a door. And meanwhile the thing I actually tried to save is—" She couldn't finish it. The sentence just stopped, the way the river district's lights had stopped.

Sorrel came back down the slope to stand on her other side. He didn't speak right away. The wolf had a way of letting silence do work that words would only bruise. When he finally spoke, his voice was the low rumble she had first heard outside the walls, the voice out of legend that she had once been afraid of and now leaned toward like heat.

"Grief lies," he said. "It tells you that you caused the wound because causing feels better than witnessing. Witnessing is helpless. Causing at least gives you something to carry."

"When did you get wise?"

"I'm old. It happens to wolves who outlive their packs." He turned his amber eyes from the burning city to her face. "But hear the truth underneath the lie, keeper. You did not light those fires. Men lit them. Men have been lighting them since Veylmar fell, and the reason they can is that no one remembers what was erased. That is the whole engine. Fear, then forgetting, then fear again. You are climbing this mountain to break the engine. Do not let them make you believe you are the fuel."

Beatrix wanted to answer him. What came out instead was nothing, because at that moment the wind shifted and brought the smell.

Even at this distance, even this high, it arrived: burnt tallow, wet ash, and under it something sweeter and worse, the smell of a city's stored life going up. Charms. Hundreds of them, thousands, two decades of the market's quiet commerce in hope — every luck-knot and fever-thread and love-token that had ever passed through her hands or anyone like her, burning together in the Warden bonfires. She could almost pick out individual threads in it. The rosemary oil she used on the memory-charms. The beeswax from the Chandlers' Row supplier who had never once asked what she did in the back of her stall.

Her stomach heaved. She turned aside and was sick on the scree, violently, undignified, while Pip watched with wide eyes and Corvin positioned himself between her and the view without seeming to move at all.

When it was done she knelt there, empty, shaking, the cold coming up through her knees.

*Everything I stayed to protect,* she thought. *Gone. Not threatened. Not endangered. Gone, and burned as a message about me.*

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a granite shoulder, below the snowline but close enough that frost furred the tent canvas Ysolt's villagers had packed for them. No fire. Corvin forbade it — Warden outriders would be sweeping the high passes within days, maybe already — so they ate cold travel-bread and dried apples and pretended it was a meal.

Pip slept against Sorrel's flank, one hand buried in the grey ruff of his neck, and the wolf lay utterly still around the child like a wall that had decided to be warm. Corvin took first watch, sitting with his back to a boulder, whetstone moving along his blade in long slow strokes. He had cleaned that blade eleven times since Ysolt fell. Beatrix suspected it wasn't about the edge.

She should have slept. Her body ached for it from heel to crown. Instead she sat at the camp's edge, facing north, where the pass climbed toward the glacier road, and beyond the glacier — according to Ysolt's half-map, according to Sorrel's fragmentary memories, according to a voice Pip heard knocking under the ice — the Door of Ashvale stood open in a valley where snow fell upward.

Behind her, south, the glow of Ashvale stained the underside of the clouds a dull orange, like a bruise showing through skin. Even with her back to it she could see it reflected in the frost on the stones. The city followed her. Of course it did. It lived in her now, in the part of her that had believed, right up until today, that smallness was safety.

She heard Corvin stop sharpening.

"You're blaming yourself," he said quietly. "I can hear it from here. It has a particular posture."

"Don't."

"I wrote a list." He said it plainly, the way men say the worst things about themselves when they've stopped expecting absolution. "Years ago. Names of the gifted, gathered for the crown's archives. Your mother was on it. Everything burning down there tonight started as ink on my page, Beatrix. So believe me when I tell you I know exactly what self-blame tastes like, and I know it doesn't feed anyone."

"And what do you do with it? If it doesn't feed anyone."

"You spend it." The whetstone resumed, slow. "Every mile north. Every name you carry. You don't get to be forgiven, so you get to be useful. It's a poor trade. Take it anyway."

Beatrix looked at him across the dark camp — the disgraced huntsman keeping watch over a witch, a wolf, and a child, guarding with the same diligence he'd once used to hunt them — and felt something shift in her chest that was not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but was adjacent to it. A door in a door.

"I don't know how to want anything anymore," she said. It came out before she could stop it, the hollow speaking aloud. "For twenty years I wanted one thing. To stay invisible and keep my people safe. Both halves of that are dead now. Invisible failed, and safe—" She gestured back at the orange sky. "So I'm walking toward a door in the ice because a child hears it knocking and a wolf says I belong there, and I don't actually want anything except for everyone I love to stop being flammable. Is that enough to build on? Is that what your prophecies meant, Sorrel?"

The wolf opened one eye. In the dark his gaze caught what little light there was, twin embers.

"No," he said. "The stories promised more than that. But stories start where wanting ends. You walk because there is nowhere else, and then, one morning, you walk because there is somewhere. I have seen it happen to creatures far more broken than you." He resettled his chin over Pip's sleeping body. "Sleep, Beatrix. Tomorrow we climb onto the ice, and the ice will ask us who we are. Best not to arrive hollow."

*Hollow.* She lay down in her cloak and stared at the frost-lit stone and understood that she already was. Something had been scooped out of her on the mountainside today, some load-bearing piece, and grief had rushed in to fill the space the way water fills a footprint. She was still walking. She would still walk tomorrow. But she did it now the way a ruined house stands — upright out of habit, waiting to learn whether it was a shell or a skeleton.

* * *

Dawn came grey and windless, the worst kind of dawn for climbing.

Beatrix woke to Pip standing at the camp's southern edge, small silhouette rigid against the orange-stained sky, staring down at the burning city with an expression Beatrix couldn't read. She rose quietly and went to them, and only when she was close did she see that Pip's fists were clenched white, and that every bird on the mountainside — every crow, every chough, a scatter of falcons off the crags — had landed in a wide silent ring around the child, all facing inward, all watching.

"Pip."

"They're still screaming down there." Pip's voice was thin and steady, which was somehow worse than crying. "I can't make it stop. The dogs, the horses, the rats in the sewers — they're all screaming, and it comes up through the ground like — like a bell you can't unring. I thought if we got far enough away it would go quiet." They turned their face up to her, and their eyes were wet but furious, furious in the way only children grieve. "It didn't go quiet, Beatrix."

She knelt. Took their small clenched hands in hers, and did the only thing she knew how to do anymore, the crime, the gift, the inheritance — she opened herself to the animals' chorus and listened.

It was exactly as Pip said. Grief came up through the rock of the mountain like floodwater through a cellar: terror and pain and loss from ten thousand throats, the city's beasts mourning a world of noise and fire. But beneath the grief, faint and stubborn, something else moved. Rats in the deep galleries, guiding children toward the river gates. Dogs slipping their chains and lying across doorways. A horse — she found it suddenly and wholly, a cart mare on the Tanners' Row with a burned blanket and a boy on her back, galloping north through fields before the riders woke. Carrying him out. Carrying whoever she could carry.

Not everything down there was dying. Some of it was running. And running, in a beast's grammar, was hope.

"Pip," Beatrix said. "Listen with me. Not to the screaming. Under it."

The child squeezed their eyes shut. Their grip on her hands tightened, loosened, tightened again. And slowly, slowly, the ring of birds around them shifted — heads tilting, wings settling, the watching becoming something calmer.

"There's a horse," Pip whispered. "Going north. With a boy."

"There's a horse going north with a boy," Beatrix agreed. Her own eyes were burning now, and she let them. "Ashvale isn't finished, little one. It's scattered. There's a difference, and we're going to spend everything we have left making sure it matters." She stood, and turned north, to where the pass narrowed and the first true ice showed white between the rocks, gleaming like a road.

Above the glacier, far off, the air shimmered strangely — snow drifting upward off the heights in fine bright threads, falling in reverse, toward a valley none of them could yet see.

Sorrel stood at the head of the trail, nose lifted, reading the wind.

"It knows we're coming," he said. "Whatever waits behind the Door — it felt Ysolt fall, and it felt the city burn, and it has begun asking who will arrive to answer for it." He glanced back at Beatrix, and his ember-eyes held hers. "Walk faster than your grief, keeper. From here, it climbs with us."
