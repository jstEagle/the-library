## 25. The Door of Ashvale

They came down out of the pass at dawn, or what passed for dawn beneath the long winter's ceiling of cloud—a gray brightening, a softening of the wind, the light like milk poured over stone.

Beatrix felt it before she saw it. That was the first strange thing, and by then she had learned to trust strange things. It began as a warmth in her palms, the same place where the whispers lived, except this was not a whisper. It was a hum. Low and patient, like a hearth fire banked for the night, waiting to be fed.

"Stop," she said.

Pip stopped so fast they nearly fell over their own boots. The child had not slept well—none of them had—but there was a brightness in Pip's face that Beatrix had come to dread and love in equal measure. "You feel it too," Pip said. Not a question. For three days now the child had been tilting their head northward like a compass needle, listening to something under the ice that no one else could hear.

"I feel it," Beatrix said.

Corvin said nothing. He stood a little apart, the way he always did when the land itself started behaving strangely, his hand resting on the knife he'd taken from a dead Warden at the ford. The wound in his side was healing crooked; she could see it in the way he favored himself without admitting it. He looked at her, and she shook her head slightly—not *don't speak*, but *not yet*. He nodded and kept his silence, which was its own kind of apology, offered fresh every morning since the bothy, accepted by neither of them and refused by both.

Sorrel padded ahead through the snow, nose down, and then lifted his great gray head and made a sound Beatrix had never heard from him before. Not a word. A note. Long and low and rising, the sound wolves made, Ysolt had once told her, when they sang to a country instead of to each other.

"He knows it," Pip whispered.

"He remembers it," Beatrix corrected, and immediately wondered why she'd said that, why the distinction mattered so much to her chest.

They followed Sorrel down into the valley.

* * *

The valley did not look like the rest of the frozen country. Everything above had been white on white—the glacier road, the bonefields beyond it, the buried villages, all of it erased under snow the way Veylmar itself had been erased from maps and ledgers and living memory. But here the snow lay thin, and between the drifts showed dark grass, still green. Still *alive*, after a winter that had been falling for weeks out of season, out of reason. Beatrix crouched and pressed her bare fingers against it and felt the hum again, stronger now, coming up out of the ground like a pulse.

"It shouldn't be green," Corvin said behind her. His voice was flat with the effort of keeping it flat. "Nothing should be green."

"The Door," Pip said simply, as if that explained everything, and maybe it did.

They walked on, and the grass grew thicker, and the air grew warmer, and then the valley turned and the trees began—birches, silver-barked, holding their last gold leaves as though winter had never reached them, as though autumn itself had been preserved mid-falling, the way the echo-citizens below in ruined Veylmar were preserved mid-collapse. Beatrix stared at a leaf turning loose from its branch and drifting *upward*, spiraling slowly toward the clouds.

"Sorrel," she said carefully. "Is the snow falling up?"

He sat back on his haunches and looked at her with those amber eyes that held twenty years of patience and a lifetime of old grief. *Yes,* he said, in the voice only she heard. *And down. And sideways. Time is thin here, little charm-seller. This is the seam where two countries were stitched together badly.*

She had stopped being surprised, somewhere on the ice road, that his voice in her mind felt less like magic than like memory. She was more surprised by how much she wanted to lean into it. How tired she was of standing alone inside her own skull.

The trees opened.

And there it was.

Two standing stones, taller than any man, taller than the market clock tower in Ashvale, leaning together at the top like two hands about to clasp—or like two people who had begun an embrace and been interrupted a thousand years ago and were still waiting to finish it. Between them hung no door, no gate, no bar of wood or iron. Just air. But the air was wrong. It shimmered faintly, the way heat shimmers off a summer road, except this was winter and the shimmer was cold light, aurora-light, green and violet threading through empty space like ribbons through water.

Snow fell upward around the stones. Not drifted—fell, deliberately, each flake rising past their faces in slow spirals, ascending into a sky that received them without comment.

Nobody spoke. Even the wind held off, as though the valley were a room someone had just entered and everyone within it was waiting to be announced.

It was Pip who moved first. Pip always moved first; that was the child's whole nature, the thing that had led them out of a wall in the undercity and onto an ice road and screaming into an avalanche. Pip walked straight toward the stones, arms out, face upturned, catching the upward-falling snow on their cheeks—and then stopped, three paces away, and went very still.

"Pip?" Beatrix started forward.

But the child wasn't looking at the Door anymore. The child was looking *through* it, or into it, or whatever you did with a threshold made of aurora and absence. And the expression on Pip's face—

Beatrix had seen that expression before. In her own mirror, once, years ago, late at night, when she'd let herself imagine a life she couldn't have.

Longing. Pure and unarmored and terrible.

"What do you see?" she asked softly.

Pip didn't answer right away. When the child finally spoke, the voice was small and wondering. "A garden," Pip said. "There's a garden. Somebody's teaching me things—I can't see their face. But I know them. I know them the way you know somebody in a dream." Pip's chin trembled. "I've never belonged anywhere my whole life. Not one single place. And it just—it knows that. It's showing me where."

Beatrix's throat closed. She looked at Corvin.

He hadn't moved from the treeline. He stood with his arms folded, wearing the stubborn set of a man determined not to be moved by anything, and she watched him watch the Door with the wariness of a soldier approaching enemy ground. Then, slowly, grudgingly, as if the stones themselves had ordered him to attention, he straightened. His eyes changed. Whatever he was seeing, it reached him through every wall he'd ever built, and for one unguarded moment his face was young—twenty years younger, a huntsman's son's face, open and hungry.

"A forest," he said, when he caught her looking. He said it like a confession. "Green. Before—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Before I helped burn the first list." His jaw worked. "There's a river I used to fish with my father. Before the crown took him for poaching and took me for a soldier and made me into what I am." He looked at the Door the way a man looks at a door out of his own life. "It's showing me who I would have been. If I'd never—"

"Stop," Beatrix said, not gently. "Don't finish that sentence. Not here."

He looked at her then, and there was so much pain in his face that she had to turn away, because she could not afford it yet. Forgiveness was a coin she was still counting.

Sorrel approached last. The old wolf walked to the stones as a pilgrim walks, head low, and when he reached them he did not look through the shimmer the way the humans did. He laid his muzzle against the left-hand stone, gently, the way you'd touch the face of someone sleeping, and a sound came out of him that broke Beatrix's heart in a place she hadn't known was breakable. It was the sound he'd made in the ruins of Veylmar, in the city of echoes, when the parliament of wolves had told them the truth of what beast-speakers were and what the long winter meant.

*Home,* he said, to no one, to the stone, to the dead. *I found it. Twenty years too late, and I found it.*

Then he stepped back, and sat, and waited for her.

Because it was her turn. She had known it since the treeline. The Door was not finished with them; it was working through them one by one, and it had saved her for last the way a host saves the seat of honor, or the way an executioner saves the blade.

Beatrix Blackwood walked to the Door of Ashvale.

* * *

Up close, the shimmer resolved into something almost like surface—like standing at the edge of deep water, if deep water glowed green and violet and gave back reflections of things that weren't there. Snow rose past her shoulders. The hum in her palms had become a chord, many-voiced, and underneath it she could hear—as clearly as she'd ever heard the rats in the sewers begging passage, as clearly as she'd heard Sorrel's first spoken word outside the walls—voices. Hundreds of voices. Thousands. Saying names.

Her name among them.

*Look,* the Door seemed to say, without words, the way Sorrel spoke, the way the hounds had understood her on the bonefields. *Look and know yourself.*

She looked.

She saw a house. Small, stone, smoke rising from a chimney that actually smoked, in a valley very much like this one. There was a stall outside it—not a hidden stall, not a market stall of harmless charms sold with a smile and a lie. An honest stall. Charms that worked, openly, because the woman who made them whispered to the silver and didn't have to whisper. Travelers came to her gate. Children came to learn from her. She was older in the vision, gray coming in at her temples, and her hands were sure, and no one had ever burned anything of hers, and no one had ever betrayed her, because there had been nothing to betray.

And she was alone.

That was the detail the Door did not soften, the truth it would not gild: in the whole warm picture, in the smoke and the travelers and the children learning, there was no one beside her. No one whose hand she reached for when the day ended and the stall closed and the fire was lit. The woman in the vision smiled easily and often, and every smile was the smile of a person who has gotten exactly what she thought she wanted and discovered that it isn't enough, and has decided, with tremendous discipline, not to think about that.

Alone. Safe. Free. Alone.

Beatrix's breath came out ragged. Behind her she heard Pip make a small hurt sound, and Corvin shift his weight, and she knew—they all knew—that the Door was showing each of them the same merciless arithmetic. What you want most. What it will cost.

"No," she said aloud, to the Door, to herself. "No, that's not—"

*Isn't it?* the Door asked, gentle as snowfall.

Twenty years. Twenty years of concealment, of a life built like a lockpick set, every relationship shaped around what it needed to hide. Marek across the aisle for fifteen years and she'd never once told him. Customers she'd liked, neighbors she'd loved in the ordinary way, and every one of them kept at arm's length because arm's length was survival. She had told herself it was wisdom. Standing here, seeing the house the wisdom bought, she understood it had also been a habit. A very long, very practiced habit. Loneliness worn so long it had grown into the shape of her and called itself peace.

"That's what I get," she said quietly, "if I seal it. Isn't it? One keeper, alone, forever. That's the bargain the wolves named. That's the house." She gestured at the vision, at the gray-templed woman smiling her disciplined smile. "That's what keeper means. That's what it's been showing me this whole time. Not a punishment. A *reward*. The safe life. The honest work. And nobody in it."

*One keeper,* the Door agreed, or the winter did, or the oldest law of Veylmar did. *One who carries the name willingly. One who holds the memory alone.*

"And the others?" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Pip goes through to a family that doesn't exist. Corvin gets his river and his father and his unburnt soul. Sorrel gets his dead country back. Everyone belongs somewhere except the one who holds the door shut."

*Someone must.*

"Yes," she said. "Someone always must. That's what the powerful say when the cost is theirs to assign and someone else's to pay."

The shimmer rippled. For a moment—one heartbeat, no more—the vision shifted. The house remained, the stall, the sure hands. But at the edge of it, at the gate, half-glimpsed, there were figures. A child running up the path. A wolf lying in the sun. A man with a crooked-healing wound splitting wood badly. They were outside the fence. They were passing on the road. They were near, and warm, and not hers, and the Door let her see them for exactly as long as it takes to understand that they were possible and then took them away.

She made a sound she hadn't made since her mother died.

"Beatrix." Corvin's hand hovered at her shoulder, not quite touching. He had crossed the distance without her hearing; even wounded, even heavy, the man moved like the hunter he'd been trained to be. "What did it show you?"

"Me," she said. "Happy. Alone." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "It knows me better than I do."

Pip pressed against her side, small and solid and smelling of woodsmoke and cold. "Don't take it," the child said fiercely. "Whatever it's offering. I heard what the wolves said. One keeper, alone forever. I'm not going through any door that needs you on the wrong side of it. I'll stay in this freezing country till I die, I don't care, I ate snow for a week once and I turned out fine—"

"You did not turn out fine, you turned out feral," Beatrix said, and her voice broke in the middle of it, and Pip hugged her around the ribs hard enough to hurt, and she let it hurt.

Sorrel came to stand at her other side, and his warmth was a wall against the wind, and his voice in her mind was quiet and old. *The Door shows truth, little charm-seller. But it does not show the only truth. It shows you what you believe you deserve. You have spent twenty years believing you deserved to be alone. It is only repeating your own lesson back to you.*

"Then it's a good teacher," she said. "Terrible lessons, perfectly delivered."

*And yet.* His amber eyes reflected the aurora-shimmer. *You are the first Blackwood in a generation to stand here. Your mother stood where you stand. So did hers. Ask yourself what they saw. Ask yourself what they chose.*

"My mother is an echo in a dead city who can't see me," Beatrix said, and the words came out flat and cold and final.

*Yes,* Sorrel said. *And she walked toward the Door anyway.*

The wind moved through the birches, and the gold leaves stirred, and somewhere far above, the upward-falling snow reached the clouds and became, impossibly, new snow falling down, a closed circuit, a wheel turning, memory feeding weather feeding memory.

Beatrix looked at the shimmer. At the house behind it, already fading, already dissolving back into green-violet light. At her own reflection ghosted faintly in the cold glow—stretched thin, travel-worn, twenty years of hiding written into her posture, and underneath all of it, unmistakable, the face of the girl in the forests of Veylmar who had talked to wolves before anyone taught her it was forbidden.

Behind that reflection, deep in the shimmer, something else moved.

Not a vision. Not a belonging. Something dark, and fast, and coming down the far side of the valley with the particular silence of creatures that had learned to hunt in snow. Shapes at the treeline. White shapes. Eyes like chips of the glacier.

Sorrel's hackles rose. A growl built in his chest, low and continuous, the sound of a border being drawn.

"They found us," Corvin said. His hand was on his knife. His voice had gone calm the way it went calm before blood, and Beatrix had learned, on the ice road, to hate that calm and be grateful for it in the same breath. "The alpha's pack. They've been shadowing us since the glacier."

"How long do we have?" Beatrix asked, not moving, still facing the Door.

"Minutes," Corvin said. "Maybe fewer."

Pip's hand found hers. Small fingers, cold, gripping tight. "Beatrix," the child whispered. "The knocking. Under the ice. It's louder here. It's been getting louder all day." Pip's eyes were enormous, reflecting the aurora. "I think it's not knocking to get in."

The shimmer breathed, green and violet, patient as winter.

"I think it's knocking to let us out."
