## 26. Vessa at the Gate

The last of her troop was eleven riders and a mule, and Vessa had stopped pretending the mule was a horse.

"Water the animals," she said, sliding down from the saddle into snow that came past her knees. "Then water yourselves. We don't move until the light comes back."

Sergeant Odo caught her arm as she passed him. "Captain. Three days' hard ride behind us, no supply line, and we're chasing a rumor up a glacier. The men are asking—"

"The men can ask me." She pulled free of his grip, gently enough. Odo was a good soldier who'd followed her out of Ashvale when half the garrison decided a frozen mountain was beyond the Crown's pay. He deserved better than a snapped answer. But she didn't have a good answer to give him, so she walked away instead.

They'd come up the ice road for six days. Six days of white silence and the feeling — which Vessa had never once put into words, not even to herself until this climb — that they were being watched from underneath. The glacier creaked at night like a ship's hull. Twice she'd found wolf tracks that ended in nothing, as if the animal had simply decided to stop existing mid-stride.

And twice, in the blue hour before dawn, she'd heard something under the ice. Not a sound exactly. More like a word she'd known as a child and forgotten on purpose.

Her brother's name was Tam. That was all she could hold onto. Tam, who was four years older and could whistle through two fingers and had once carried her a mile home on his back after she broke her ankle falling off the granary roof. Tam, who had been born in Veylmar, before the fall, before the erasure, before the Crown decided that a country could be unwritten like a bad entry in a ledger.

She had been three years old when it happened. She remembered nothing of Veylmar. She remembered only the hole where it should have been — the way their mother would go quiet at certain words, the way their father burned a box of papers one autumn and never explained why, the way Tam would stand at the window on snowy nights with an expression she couldn't read and wouldn't ask about.

Then Tam went into the ice himself. A fever, the physician said. Eleven years old. And at the funeral, her mother had leaned over the small coffin and whispered something Vessa hadn't been meant to hear, a word in a language that wasn't Ashvale's, and Vessa had spent thirty years telling herself she hadn't heard it.

She heard it again now. Under the ice. Every night since the long winter began.

*Vessa.* Just that. Her own name, spoken the way Tam used to say it when he wanted the last honey-bun split fairly.

"Captain." Odo again, at her shoulder. "You should see this."

He led her to the ridge above their camp, and Vessa looked down into the valley, and everything she had organized her life around quietly came apart.

Snow was falling upward.

It rose out of the valley floor in slow spirals, drifting against every law of weather she knew, climbing past them toward a sky the color of a bruise healing. And at the center of the valley stood a gate — two pillars and a lintel of black stone, ancient and unweathered, standing alone in the white like the last tooth in a jaw.

Around it, faintly, hung light. Not torchlight. Not moonlight. It moved the way the aurora moved, green and violet, except it came from the ground.

"What is it?" Odo whispered. He was forty years old and had fought in two border wars, and he sounded like a boy.

"I don't know," Vessa said. It was the first honest thing she'd said to him in days. Then, because honesty was apparently loose in her tonight: "I think I do."

She had grown up on the official story the way everyone had. Veylmar fell because its kings grew proud and its magic turned rotten. The Crown erased it for the people's protection. There was no Door. There were no beast-speakers worth mourning. The gifted were a sickness and the purge was medicine.

But she had also grown up on her mother's whisper at a coffin, and on a brother's name spoken by ice, and somewhere beneath the story the Crown told there had always been a second story, and she had spent her whole career stomping it flat because the alternative was unbearable.

Because if the Door was real — if any of it was real — then the purge wasn't medicine. It was murder with paperwork. And she had signed the paperwork.

"Set watches," she said. Her voice came out level. She was grateful to it. "Nobody approaches the stones. Nobody touches anything."

"And our orders?"

Our orders. Bring back the speaker alive. *Alive*, the Marshal had emphasized, because dead speakers told no secrets and the Crown was suddenly very interested in secrets. Bring back the woman Blackwood and anyone traveling with her. Let nothing else cross the threshold. Burn what cannot be brought back.

Burn. Always burn. It was the Crown's only verb.

"We hold position," Vessa said. "We wait for them."

Odo frowned. "Ma'am, respectfully — if they're here, why wait? Take them now, while—"

"Look at it, Sergeant." She pointed down at the upward-falling snow. "Tell me you want to fight a battle in that."

He looked. He didn't answer. Good soldiers know when a question is rhetorical.

* * *

She took first watch herself, partly because she didn't trust her sleep and partly because she wanted, with an appetite that shamed her, to look at the gate.

It did things when she looked too long. The darkness between the pillars wasn't shadow; it was depth, a vertical pool of night that occasionally showed her things. A wheat field under a summer sun. A harbor town she almost recognized. Once, unbearably, a kitchen — low ceiling, iron stove, a woman's hands kneading dough — and the woman humming a tune Vessa knew, had always known, had hummed to herself her whole life without knowing where she'd learned it.

Their mother's tune. Their mother's kitchen.

Vessa sat down hard in the snow.

There was a version of her life where she rode down into that valley right now, alone, unarmed, and stepped between the pillars just to see. She understood, sitting there with cold working into her bones, that this was exactly what the gate was for. It showed you your deepest belonging. Ysolt's ledger — the pages Corvin Hale must have told her about, before he went traitor, or maybe the reason he went traitor — spoke of thresholds that tested the heart.

She thought about the Marshal's orders. Bring back a speaker alive.

She thought about the list. She had never written it herself — that had been Hale, years ago, though the whole officer corps had fed him names — but she had executed off it. House raids. Registrations. The pyre outside Ashvale where they'd burned a hedge-witch last spring while her neighbors watched with the particular stillness of people memorizing everything so they could hate properly later.

Vessa had stood at that pyre too. In dress uniform. Saluting.

A fox crossed the valley below her, moving toward the gate, and paused at its edge, ears forward. Then it turned and left. Even the animals seemed to understand there were rules here.

"Tam," she said aloud, very quietly, to the empty white.

The snow spiraled upward. Somewhere beneath the ice, patient as stone, something that wore her brother's voice waited for her to say more.

She didn't. She went back down to camp and drank tea that tasted of smoke and made herself plan the arrest she no longer believed in.

* * *

They came at dusk on the third day.

Vessa saw them first from the ridge — five figures moving single file along the far slope, slow, burdened, one limping. She raised her glass and counted: a tall man favoring his side, gray in his beard even at this distance. A big wolf, gray and scarred, moving like a soldier. An old woman wrapped in layers. A child, small and quick, ranging ahead and being called back.

And a woman in a patched coat walking beside the wolf, head bare to the wind, looking at the gate the way pilgrims look.

Beatrix Blackwood. The charm-seller. The speaker. The reason for the winter's march and the eleven exhausted riders and the mule.

Vessa lowered the glass. Her hand was steady. She noted that with distant professional approval, the way you note the behavior of a stranger.

"Riders form up," she called down. "Odo, with me. The rest hold the ridge unless I signal."

"You're going down with six arrows' distance between us and them," Odo said. It wasn't quite a question.

"They're not going anywhere." She nodded at the gate. "That's where they were headed. They'll want to reach it. Which means they'll talk first."

"And if they run for it?"

Vessa looked at the dark between the pillars, at the upward snow, at the place where her mother's kitchen sometimes hung like a reflection in deep water.

"Then I suppose we find out what the Crown's writ is worth here," she said.

They rode down. The snow received the horses without complaint, rising in spirals around them, and Vessa felt it pass over her skin — warm. The snow was warm. Up close, each flake glowed faintly, like embers that had forgotten how to burn.

Blackwood's party had stopped perhaps fifty paces from the stones. They'd seen the riders; the wolf had gone rigid, lips lifting off its teeth, and the old woman had put a hand on the child's shoulder, and the tall man — Hale, it had to be Hale, the deserter, the traitor — had stepped forward to put himself between Vessa's line of approach and the others. Instinctive. Unthinking. Twenty years of palace service and he still moved like a shield wall.

"Hale," Vessa called, reining in at thirty paces. She dismounted. Some conversations you have on foot or not at all. "Corvin Hale. You're under arrest for desertion, treason, and the murder of Warden Brennick at the ford."

"Brennick pulled steel on a child," Hale said. His voice was rough but level. "I'll answer for it gladly. But not today, and not to you."

"To whom, then? The wolves?" A bitter laugh got out of her before she could stop it. "Listen to yourself. Twenty years an officer of the Crown and you're standing guard for a witch and a wild dog at the edge of a fairy story."

"It's not a story, Captain." He said it gently, which was worse than if he'd shouted. "Come and see."

"I've seen it." The words left her before she'd chosen them. Hale blinked. Behind him, Beatrix Blackwood tilted her head, and Vessa realized with a lurch that the woman could probably hear her heartbeat, or whatever it was speakers heard. "Three nights I've watched it. I know what it shows."

"Does it show you Tam?"

The name hit the air like a dropped glass.

The child made a small sound. The old woman's grip tightened on the small shoulder. And Beatrix Blackwood stepped forward, out from behind Hale's shielding body, into the open space between them, and Vessa's hand went to her sword hilt and stayed there, useless, because the woman wasn't threatening anything. She was just looking at Vessa with an expression Vessa recognized from mirrors — the look of someone doing arithmetic with grief.

"How do you know my brother's name?" Vessa said.

"I didn't." Blackwood's voice was low, unhurried, the market-stall voice, the charm-seller's voice, warm as banked coals. "But the gate does. It's been showing you him, hasn't it? Nights, on watch. You thought you were imagining it."

"Shut your mouth."

"He's not gone, Warden. None of them are. That's the whole—" Blackwood stopped herself. Vessa saw her choose, visibly, to say less rather than more, and hated her for the mercy of it. "My name is Beatrix. What's yours?"

"You know my rank well enough."

"That's not what I asked."

Wind moved through the valley. The upward snow thickened, spiraling between them, glowing, and for one strange suspended moment Vessa stood inside it and felt like she was standing inside a memory that belonged to somebody else — somebody who had lived in a country with a harbor and wheat fields and a low kitchen with an iron stove, somebody whose mother had hummed while she worked.

"I had orders," Vessa said. The sentence came out of her like a confession, because it was one. "Bring back a speaker alive. Burn what won't come. Those are my orders. I have eleven soldiers on that ridge who followed me up a glacier because I'm supposed to be the one who doesn't waver."

"And do you?" Blackwood asked. "Waver?"

"I don't know what I do anymore." It was the truest thing she'd ever said on duty, and it terrified her more than the gate did.

Hale shifted. "Vessa. When we rode together, before all this — do you remember what you told me about the pyres? You said, *we're not burning witches, we're burning evidence*. You said it drunk, after Kettering. You remember."

She remembered. She remembered everything. That was the curse of her — a perfect recall for every order she'd relayed, every door her raiders had kicked in, every name she'd added to the lists with a clerk's neat hand. Other officers learned to forget. She'd never managed it.

"I remember," she said.

"Then you already know which side of the fire you belong on." Hale spread his hands. "I'm not asking you to defect. I'm asking you to lower your sword and watch."

"Watch what?"

Behind Blackwood, the child stepped forward.

Small, ragged, eight years old maybe, with eyes that were too old in a face that was too young — the kind of child the undercity produced and then discarded. The old woman reached for them and the child slipped the reach easily, the way children do, and walked out ahead of everyone, adults and wolf and armed wardens alike, straight toward the black pillars, and stopped just short of the dark between them, and cocked their head as if listening.

"There's someone in there," the child said. The voice carried across the valley, clear as a bell in the still air. "Lots of someones. They've been waiting such a long time." The child turned, and looked directly at Vessa, and smiled, and it was the smile that did it, that cracked something she'd been holding closed for thirty years. "One of them knows you, lady. He says you got tall."

The sword came out of Vessa's hand. She didn't drop it, exactly. Her fingers simply opened, and it fell, and the snow caught it, glowing flakes closing over the steel like water.

Odo, up on the ridge, shouted something. She didn't hear the words.

The child turned back to the gate. Took one step. Another.

"No," Vessa heard herself say — and didn't know if she meant the child, or the Crown, or the winter, or the thirty years of her own life. "Wait—"

The child stepped into the dark between the pillars.

And the dark opened.
