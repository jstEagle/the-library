## 27. Through

The voice under the ice had been calling Pip's name for three days, and on the fourth morning it stopped calling and started *waiting*.

Pip felt the difference the way you feel someone standing behind you. The cold coming off the Door wasn't weather anymore. It was attention.

They stood at the valley floor where snow fell upward, drifting from the ground to the sky in slow silver threads, and Pip's breath came out in little ghosts that rose instead of sank. Beatrix held their hand so tight it hurt. Sorrel pressed against their other side, hackles up, staring at the standing stones like they owed him money. Corvin had his sword drawn even though there was nothing to fight, which Pip thought was very like him. Vessa stood apart with her empty hands hanging at her sides, looking at the gate the way people look at a grave they dug themselves.

The Door was two pillars of black stone and a lintel across the top, older than anything Pip had ever touched. Between them hung no door at all — just air, and beyond it a light that wasn't sunlight. It moved. It breathed green and violet like something alive behind frosted glass.

*Come through,* said the voice under the ice, and this time it didn't say it to Pip. It said it to all of them. *You are remembered here.*

"I don't hear anything," Corvin said quietly. "Do any of you—"

"The child hears," Sorrel said.

"Of course the child hears." Beatrix's laugh came out cracked. "Why would anything about this be simple."

But she knelt down then, right in the upward-falling snow, and took both of Pip's hands, and looked at them the way she'd been looking at them since the pass, since Ysolt died, since everything — like Pip was something she'd been given back and might lose twice.

"You don't have to go first," she said.

"Yes I do." Pip had known it for days. "It's asking for me. If I go first, it'll know we're not invaders."

"And if it's a trap?"

"Then it's been very patient for twenty years," Pip said, "and that's a stupid kind of trap."

Sorrel made a sound that might have been a wolf laughing.

Beatrix didn't let go of their hands. "What does it feel like? The voice. Tell me exactly."

Pip thought about it properly, because Beatrix always wanted exact things. "Like knocking. Like when Marek used to tap on our shutters before dawn so the Wardens wouldn't hear. Like someone who can't get in but won't stop trying." Their throat went tight. "Nobody knocks forever unless they want you home."

Beatrix closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, and she nodded once, hard, the way she did when she'd decided something and hated it.

"All right," she said. "All right. We're right behind you. Every step. You hear me, Pip Blackwood?"

That wasn't their name. Not really. They'd never had one until she gave it to them in the undercity, half a joke, and now it fit better than skin.

"Pip Blackwood," Pip agreed, and stepped forward.

* * *

Crossing the threshold felt like walking into a memory of winter rather than winter itself.

Cold, yes, but a clean cold, the cold of the inside of a church bell. The light swallowed them — green, then violet, then a white so complete Pip couldn't see their own hands — and somewhere in that whiteness the voice finally spoke without ice between them:

*Welcome home, little listener.*

And then their boots were on grass.

Grass. Frosted stiff and pale, but grass, in a country where it should have been ruin and ash. Pip stood very still while the world assembled itself around them.

A valley. Hills rolling away north under a sky that was wrong in the most beautiful way — ribbons of aurora pouring across it, green and gold and bruise-purple, moving like slow rivers though there was no wind. And below, spilling down the hillsides to a frozen river —

A city.

Veylmar. The dead kingdom. Towers with their tops broken off, streets furred with frost, a great citadel on its crag with banners still hanging from the walls, frozen mid-rip as if the wind that tore them had simply stopped one day and never started again. Everything preserved at the moment of its dying. A candle halfway through falling off a windowsill, still falling. A cart in a square with its apples scattered and frozen mid-bounce, hovering a finger's width above the cobbles.

"It's *here*," Pip whispered. "It's all still here."

Behind them, one by one, the others came through. Pip heard Beatrix's sharp breath. Heard Sorrel's low whine. Heard Corvin say, softly, a curse and a prayer tangled together.

Then Beatrix was beside them, gripping their shoulder, and her face —

Her face was doing something Pip had never seen it do. Not fear. Something worse.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, look at them."

Pip followed her gaze down the hillside, to the road leading toward the gates.

People were walking on it.

Hundreds of people. Thousands. Coming out of the city and going into it, climbing the hills, crossing the fields, all of them moving with the strange smooth patience of things in a dream. A woman carrying a basket of bread that was only the idea of bread. A man leading a horse made of frost and certainty. Children running through a doorway that had burned away, over and over, always arriving on the other side unburned.

They weren't alive. Pip could hear it, or rather *not* hear it — every living thing sang a small song to them if they listened, mice and men alike, a warm buzzing note. These people were silent. Echoes. The last day of Veylmar, played again and again by a country that refused to finish the sentence.

"They can't see us," Beatrix said, and her voice broke on *us*. "Twenty years I thought — everyone said it burned, they said—"

An echo-woman passed close, so close Pip could have touched her, her sleeves embroidered with wolves, her face turned up toward the aurora. She walked through Sorrel as if he were smoke. He shivered all down his spine and sat down hard in the frost.

"She smelled of my packmother," he said, very quietly. "The old blood. The ones who ran with the wardens." His golden eyes fixed on the city. "I was a cub when it ended. I thought I dreamed it. I did not dream it."

Vessa hadn't come through yet. She stood on the far side of the threshold, on Ashvale soil, and Pip watched her stare at the city of her erased brother, and watched her take one step, and another, and cross — and the Door let her, because the Door, Pip understood suddenly, had never been locked against anyone. Only against forgetting.

Corvin drew a line in the frost with his boot and stared at it. "This is what we erased," he said. "My whole career. My whole *crown*." He looked old in the aurora light. "Gods. What did we think we were burning?"

"A story that embarrassed them," Beatrix said flatly.

Down in the city, a bell began to ring. Not an echo-bell. A deep true bronze note that rolled up the valley and made the frozen river hum, and Pip realized with a lurch that the sound was coming from *underneath*, from beneath the ice and the stone, from the voice that had knocked for twenty years.

*The keeper is coming,* the voice said, gentle as snowfall. *Bring her down. She has names to hear.*

Beatrix went very still.

"What keeper?" she said.

The echoes all stopped walking at once.

Every person on the road, every figure in every window, every child in every burned doorway — thousands of faces turning together, up the hill, toward the five of them. Silent faces. Waiting faces. And though none of them had eyes that saw, Pip felt every single one of them looking straight at Beatrix Blackwood.

"They know her," Pip whispered.

One echo — the woman in the wolf-embroidered sleeves — raised her arm and pointed, past the citadel, to a tower of black stone at the city's heart, where the bell-note lived. Where something that had waited twenty years under the ice finally, finally opened its eyes.

"That's where your mother's name is written," Sorrel said softly, to Beatrix. "I'd wager my last winter on it."

Beatrix looked at the tower. Then at Pip. Then at the thousand silent faces who had waited two decades for her to arrive, and who were waiting still.

"Right," she said, and her voice shook only a little. "Then we shouldn't keep them."

She took Pip's hand, and they started down together into the city of echoes, while the aurora poured overhead like a river of everything Veylmar had ever been — and beneath their feet, faint and patient, the bell kept ringing.
