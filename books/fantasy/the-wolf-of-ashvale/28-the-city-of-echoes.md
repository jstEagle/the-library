## 28. The City of Echoes

Veylmar had been a city once. You could tell by the bones of it—the avenue that ran from the broken gate straight as a spear toward the hills, the foundations of houses laid out in neat rows like teeth, the arches of an aqueduct marching off into the aurora-lit dark. But the collapse had caught it mid-breath. Roofs hung open to the sky. A fountain still stood in a square choked with snow, its water frozen mid-fall, a sculpture of motion that had never finished.

And the people were still here.

Beatrix saw the first one before they'd walked a hundred paces past the gate: a woman sweeping a step that no longer existed, her broom hissing over bare stone, her face turned up toward a doorframe with nothing behind it but stars. She swept in slow loops. Her breath did not fog in the cold. When Sorrel padded past her, she looked through him as if he were weather.

"She can't hear us," Pip whispered, pressed close against Beatrix's side. "I tried. I tried so hard."

"I know." Beatrix's hand found the child's shoulder and stayed there. It was all she could offer, and it wasn't much.

The echo-sweeper paused—some rhythm only she could feel—and began again.

They came down the great avenue in a loose knot: Beatrix in front with Sorrel's shoulder brushing her hip, Corvin a wary pace behind with his hand nowhere near a sword because he carried none, and Pip between them like a spark carried in cupped hands. The aurora poured green and violet light across the ruins, so bright they cast shadows at midnight, so silent that Beatrix could hear her own heart working.

"They're everywhere," Corvin said quietly. He had gone gray around the mouth since they'd come through. "The whole city."

"Everyone the crown erased," Beatrix said. "Ysolt's ledger. All those names." She thought of the old woman's handwriting, row after row, dates of birth and no dates of death because officially these people had never lived at all. "They didn't go anywhere. They've been here the whole time. Twenty years of this."

She watched a man cross the avenue ahead of them carrying nothing—a baker's walk, she decided, the rolling gait of someone who'd spent his life hauling trays—and vanish into the wall of a collapsed granary without disturbing a stone.

"It's not them," Corvin said. "It can't be them. It's—" He searched for the word and hated whatever he found. "It's the last hour of their lives, played over and over."

"The day it fell," Beatrix said.

* * *

The city kept its last day the way amber keeps a fly.

They saw it in fragments as they worked their way toward what had been the palace district—Sorrel seemed to know the way, or something in him did, turning confidently at corners where every landmark had fallen. A fishmonger arguing cheerfully with a customer who wasn't there, gesturing with a knife at empty air. Two children playing knucklebones on a stoop, laughing at some joke the cold had swallowed; their laughter reached Beatrix thin and distant, like music played in another room. A guardsman on a corner that had once held a checkpoint, pacing his post, hand resting on a hilt that had rusted away to a stain.

None of them saw the living. Not one glance, not one flinch when Sorrel passed close enough to touch. The echoes moved through the ruin of their own lives like sleepwalkers through a house burning behind their eyes.

Pip stopped walking.

"Pip." Beatrix turned. The child stood rigid in the middle of the street, fists clenched, eyes squeezed shut. Every animal for half a mile would be feeling that pull right now—but there were no animals here except Sorrel, and Sorrel simply leaned against the child's legs and waited it out, patient as a wall.

"There's so many voices," Pip breathed when it passed. "Not words. Just—people-shaped. Like standing in a bell tower while all the bells ring wrong." Their eyes opened, wet and furious. "Why won't they look at us? I'm right *here.*"

"I don't know," Beatrix said, which was the truth, and hated how often it had become the truth lately.

"Maybe they can't see what isn't part of their day," Corvin offered. His voice was careful, the voice of a man handling his own guilt like a blade with no grip. "We weren't there when it fell. We're not in their last hour. So we're not real to them."

"That's the cruelest thing you've said yet," Beatrix told him, "and you've said plenty."

He took it without flinching. He'd been taking everything she threw at him since the bothy, since the confession she hadn't forgiven and hadn't stopped turning over either. She almost respected him for it. She wasn't ready to.

They moved on.

* * *

The palace district opened before them like a wound that had learned to be beautiful.

The palace itself had not burned so much as *unmade*—half of it simply gone, sheared away along a clean diagonal, as if the world had taken a bite and left the rest standing out of politeness. What remained rose against the aurora: towers intact, banners long rotted, windows full of green light. Snow fell upward in the courtyard, drifting from the ground to the sky in slow silver threads, and Beatrix understood now that this whole country sat on the far side of some rule the world outside had forgotten.

In the courtyard, hundreds of echoes had gathered.

They stood in clusters, faces turned toward the palace, toward a balcony that jutted from the surviving wing. Waiting. Some held hands. Some held children who were also echoes, small and bright-eyed and forever mid-anticipation. An old soldier stood at attention near the base of the steps. A woman held a lantern though the aurora made it pointless.

"They're waiting for a speech," Corvin said hoarsely. "This is the evacuation. This is where they were told to run north, to the Door, to—"

He stopped.

"To Ashvale," Beatrix finished.

Because that was the shape of it, suddenly, horribly clear. The kingdom hadn't just fallen. It had been *folded shut*—its people sent fleeing through a threshold into another country, and then erased from every record, every map, every memory the crown could reach. Not killed. Unwritten. And the ones who couldn't get out in time, or wouldn't leave, had been left here in the folding, replaying their final day like flies in amber.

Ysolt's ledger wasn't a list of the dead. It was a list of the *disappeared*, and every name on it had a body standing somewhere in this city, waiting for orders that had been given twenty years ago.

"We have to go," Beatrix said, because her throat was doing something dangerous. "Sorrel. The wolves' parliament. Where?"

Sorrel's ears swiveled toward the palace's surviving wing. Then, unexpectedly, he turned his head the other way—toward a narrow lane running along the district's edge, toward a cluster of modest rooftops below the palace walls.

He made a low sound. Not a word. But Beatrix had been pretending not to hear him speak for twenty years, and she heard it anyway.

*No. First this.*

"Sorrel—"

He was already moving.

* * *

The house stood where the lane bent, small and stone-walled, its roof half caved but one room clearly still whole. Smoke curled from a chimney that should not have been able to make smoke. There was a garden beside it, absurdly, defiantly green under the snow—winter beans, Beatrix saw, and leeks, and a row of something flowering white that had no business blooming in a country where winter had lasted twenty years.

Her feet stopped working before her mind caught up.

She knew the door. Knew the crooked shutter on the west window. Knew the herb bundle hanging by the lintel—feverfew and yarrow, tied with red thread, the way her mother had tied every bundle in every kitchen they'd ever shared.

"No," Beatrix said aloud, to no one, to everyone. "No, she died in Ashvale. She died when I was nine, she got sick, she—"

*You were told,* Sorrel said softly. *Told is not the same as true.*

Corvin had gone very still. Even he understood—or thought he did, or feared he did. "Beatrix," he started.

"Don't." She held up a hand. It was shaking. "Just—don't talk. Nobody talk."

Pip slipped a small warm hand into hers. They didn't say anything either. They just held on, and Beatrix walked up the path her feet remembered better than her mind did, and knocked on the door of her childhood.

Footsteps inside. The particular uneven rhythm of them—she knew it, knew the slight drag of the left heel, knew the sound of her mother coming to answer the door on a winter evening—

The door opened.

Maren Blackwood stood in the lamplight looking exactly as Beatrix remembered her and exactly as Beatrix had spent twenty years failing to remember her: dark hair going gray at the temples, a smudge of soil on one cheekbone, sleeves rolled to the elbow, eyes the same storm-gray as her daughter's. She was holding a wooden spoon. Behind her, the room glowed with firelight and smelled of onions and thyme and woodsmoke, the smell of safety, the smell Beatrix had chased through two decades of market stalls and hidden magic and careful, careful lies.

"Mama," Beatrix said. Her voice came out nine years old.

Her mother looked directly at her.

Through her.

Maren Blackwood's gaze passed over her daughter's face the way light passes through glass, and settled on the middle distance, and softened into the particular smile of a woman listening to a child she believed was asleep upstairs.

"Dinner's nearly ready, love," she said—to the empty doorway, to twenty years ago, to no one. "Wash your hands. And don't let the cat in, he'll be at the beans again."

Then she turned back into her kitchen, spoon in hand, and began stirring a pot that Beatrix could see through the doorway had nothing in it at all.

* * *

Beatrix didn't remember crossing the threshold. She was simply inside, suddenly, kneeling on the flagstones of a kitchen she'd dreamed about so often she'd built a false version of it in her head, and the false version had gotten the window wrong and the shelf heights wrong and the burn mark on the third floorboard wrong, and the real one was here, under her palms, and it broke her open.

"Mama. Mama, look at me. It's Bea. It's Beatrix, I'm—I came back, I'm right here—"

Maren stirred the empty pot. Hummed. The tune was a lullaby, the one about the wolf who guarded the bridge, and hearing it in this place, in this city, with everything Beatrix had learned in the last week, cracked the song open into something else entirely. A lullaby about wolves guarding Veylmar. Sung to a child of the Blackwood line. She'd been singing her the truth all along and Beatrix had been too young to hold it.

"Mama, please." She grabbed the hem of her mother's apron. The fabric was solid. Warm. Real as anything. "Please see me. I'm sorry I ran. I'm sorry I hid it, the gift, I hid everything because they said it was forbidden, I thought—Mama, I sold charms in a market for twenty years and pretended I couldn't do what you taught me, and I'm so tired, and I need you to *look at me—*"

Maren set down the spoon.

For one impossible heartbeat she turned, and her eyes—her eyes, storm-gray, tired, kind—came around and fixed on her daughter's face, and Beatrix's heart slammed against her ribs so hard it hurt.

But the focus was wrong. The warmth in them was aimed elsewhere, at a ghost of a moment, a girl asleep upstairs, a night twenty years gone. Maren smiled at the space just past her daughter's cheek.

"You'll catch cold sitting on the floor," she said gently. "Up to bed with you."

And she reached out and brushed Beatrix's hair from her forehead—the gesture, the exact gesture, fingertips light at the temple, the goodnight-blessing Beatrix would have given a year of her life to feel again—and her hand passed through her daughter like smoke through a window.

Cold flooded in where the touch should have been. Not the cold of the room. The cold of the truth.

Beatrix stayed kneeling on the flags long after the footsteps had resumed their circuit of the kitchen. Long after the humming faded into the clatter of imagined dishes. Corvin stood in the doorway and, to his credit, said nothing. Pip pressed against her back, small arms around her shoulders, holding on the way children hold on when they understand grief better than adults ever admit.

"She can't see me," Beatrix said finally. Her voice sounded scraped hollow. "She's not even—she's not *there,* she's just the shape the day left behind. She's been standing in this kitchen for twenty years waiting for me to come downstairs, and I finally did, and she can't—"

Her voice went. She let it.

Sorrel crossed the kitchen and lay down against her side, heavy and warm, the weight of him an anchor. *She is not gone,* he said—not aloud, not in words, in the old way, in the way of the forests before the fall. *What you are touching is the memory of the day. But the day is not the whole of her. Nothing kept in memory is only its last hour.*

"Then where is she?" Beatrix whispered. "Where's the rest of her?"

Sorrel looked toward the palace. Toward the deep places of the country, where the aurora pooled like spilled light and the snow fell upward and something ancient was waiting in the ice with questions that would change the shape of her life.

*With the wolves,* he said. *Everything Veylmar kept, it kept with the wolves.*

Outside, in the courtyard, the echo-crowd murmured and shifted, still waiting for a speech that had ended twenty years ago. And beneath the murmur—so faint Beatrix might have imagined it, might have wanted it too badly—Pip suddenly stiffened, head cocked, eyes wide, staring at the palace's dark surviving wing.

"She's knocking again," Pip said. "Under the ice. And Beatrix—it's not just calling me anymore."
