## 29. The Wolves' Parliament

Sorrel smelled the parliament before he saw it.

That was the way of the old country. Everything arrived as scent first—memory, warning, grief. The wind off the glacier carried pine-rot and old snow and, beneath it, something he had not smelled since he was a cub: *pack*. Not his scattered kin, half-starved in the hills of the living world, but pack as it had been before the fall. Dozens of wolves. The deep musk of a den grown large. The iron tang of authority that clung to an alpha's coat like frost.

He stopped at the ridgeline above the ruined city and stood with the wind flattening his fur against his ribs.

Below, in the bowl of the valley where Veylmar's great hall had half-collapsed into its own foundations, the wolves were gathering. He counted thirty, then gave up counting. They came down out of the aurora-lit dark from every quarter of the dead country, moving with the unhurried certainty of creatures who had been summoned by something older than a howl. Their coats were white—not the white of age or of winter camouflage but the white of *ice*, faintly blue at the roots, catching the green fire overhead and throwing it back.

The wolves of the ice.

The same kind that had demanded Pip on the ridge. The same kind whose alpha had buried him under a mountain's worth of avalanche and would have buried Sorrel beside him if a child had not screamed the mountain awake.

*They are not your enemies,* said the voice under the ice, the one Pip trusted, the one that had been calling through the Door since they first set foot in this country. It was not a comforting voice. It was a voice like a door knocking—patient, enormous, indifferent to whether anyone answered. *They are your elders.*

"My elders tried to kill me," Sorrel said aloud.

Beatrix looked up from where she crouched at the base of the standing stone, feeding splinters of driftwood to a small fire. Her face was thinner than it had been in Ashvale. Everyone's face was thinner. "You're doing the talking-to-yourself thing again."

"The talking-to-the-country thing," Sorrel corrected. "It talks back. That's the problem."

Pip sat cross-legged on the far side of the fire, wrapped in Corvin's cloak, watching the ridge with the terrible stillness children have when they have learned to be afraid quietly. "Are those the ones from the mountain?"

"No," Sorrel said.

"Yes," Beatrix said.

"They won't touch you while I'm here." He did not know if that was true. He suspected it was true only for as long as it suited them. But the child needed to hear it, and so did the woman, and lies told for shelter were still shelter. He had spent twenty years in the living world learning that lesson from watching her.

Corvin Hale stood at the edge of the firelight with his arms crossed, a man who had been a huntsman so long that even disgraced, even wounded, even defected, he stood like a spear planted in the ground. His shoulder was still bound where the ice-wolves' alpha had opened it. "How many?"

"More than thirty. Fewer than a hundred."

"And they're all—" He made a vague gesture at the sky, the ruins, the whole impossible country.

"Older than your crown," Sorrel said. "Older than Ashvale. They were here when the Door was young."

Corvin absorbed this the way he absorbed everything: silently, and then with a single practical question. "Do they want us to come?"

"Parliaments don't summon prey," Sorrel said. "They summon members."

He started down the ridge before anyone could argue, because if he waited for argument he would lose his nerve, and a wolf without nerve was just meat on four legs.

* * *

The valley floor was soft underfoot, which was wrong. Snow should crunch. This snow absorbed sound the way deep water does, and each step felt like walking on the skin of something sleeping. The ruined hall rose ahead of them—a ring of black stones taller than any wolf, taller than a man on horseback, their surfaces carved with shapes that Sorrel's eyes kept sliding away from. He knew what the carvings were. Every wolf of Veylmar's forests carried fragments of them in his blood: the pact, the names, the long list of the remembered.

His mother had sung fragments of it to him when he was small, in the years before the forests burned. He had thought they were lullabies. He had been wrong about many things.

The wolves parted to let them through.

It was the courtesy that frightened him most—the silence of it, thirty-odd great white beasts making room without a growl, without a bared tooth, the way courtiers make room for someone they intend to judge. At the center of the stone ring, where a hearth had once burned in the days of men, sat the alpha of the ice.

Sorrel had met her on the mountain. Up close, buried, dying, he had known her only as weight and cold and yellow eyes. Now he saw her whole: larger than any wolf had a right to be, her coat so pale she seemed carved from the glacier itself, her eyes the color of the sky before a blizzard. Scars crossed her muzzle in patterns too deliberate for battle.

She was old. Older than the ruins. Possibly older than the winter.

"You brought them," she said. Her voice was low thunder rolling under ice. "The speaker. The key. The hunter. And yourself—the last of the forest line, returned after twenty years of hiding among two-legs like a whipped dog."

"I came when called," Sorrel said. "Which is more than can be said for the rest of you, who stayed in a dead country gnawing on its bones."

A ripple went through the assembled wolves—not anger, exactly. Recognition. The unpleasant recognition of a truth spoken plainly.

"The country is not dead," the alpha said.

"It sleeps."

"It is *held*," she said, and the word landed on the valley floor like a dropped stone. "Held by the Door. Held by the winter. Held by the pact your mother sang to you, whelp, if you had ever listened past the melody." She rose, and rising, she seemed to grow; the aurora overhead bent toward her like a listener. "You have walked these ruins a week and think you understand what fell here. Sit. Listen. Then decide whom you despise."

Sorrel sat. Beatrix settled beside him with her spine straight and her hands folded—market-stall posture, he thought suddenly, the posture of a woman who has spent her life being underestimated and has made a weapon of it. Pip pressed against her side. Corvin remained standing until the alpha's gaze made sitting seem less like submission than survival.

* * *

What they told, the parliament told in the old way—not with words alone but with scent and memory pressed directly into the mind, so that Sorrel lived it rather than heard it. He felt Beatrix shudder beside him as the memories took her too. Even Pip. Even Corvin, who gripped his own knee hard enough to bruise.

They lived the country as it had been.

Veylmar green and vast, its cities lit by lanterns fed on captured summerlight, its borders guarded not by walls but by *listeners*—men and women born with the gift of speech with beasts, sworn to the packs, trained from childhood. They were called wardens, and the name had nothing to do with the crown's Wardens who hunted Beatrix across the bonefields. The irony was old and bitter: the thieves had stolen the title of the thieves' victims.

The beast-speakers were Veylmar's wardens. That was the first truth.

The second truth came colder.

For a thousand years, the wardens and the packs had kept together the Great Remembering—an act of shared memory so vast and constant that it held the country's magic in balance, the way a keystone holds an arch. Every warden remembered every wolf. Every wolf remembered every warden. And the land itself, which was alive in the way a sea is alive, drew breath from being known.

Then came the fall. Sorrel tasted it secondhand and still flinched: the civil war, the crown that feared the gift it depended on, the purge that made Ashvale's little burning look like a candle held to a bonfire. The gifted were slaughtered. The packs were scattered and hunted. And the country, unremembered, began to die—not quickly, not cleanly, but the way a fire dies when no one feeds it, sinking down into itself, folding its living light away into one last vessel.

The Door.

"The Door is not a passage," the alpha said, and now the memory-sharing stopped and there were only words again, words falling into silence like stones into a well. "The Door is a heart. While it stands open, Veylmar holds its shape—ruined, dreaming, but holding. While it stands open, the long winter falls here and only here, a wound bound in snow." Her yellow eyes turned, deliberately, one by one, to each of them. "And while it stands open, the winter is patient."

"And when it finishes falling?" Beatrix asked. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not.

"When the last snow lands, the Door seals itself. What is sealed cannot be remembered from outside. Veylmar will pass out of the world entirely—not destroyed. *Erased*. As though it never was. And the winter—" the alpha's lips peeled back from teeth the length of Sorrel's forearm, "—the winter does not die with the country it guards. A wound bound in snow, cut loose from the body, goes looking for another body. It will spread. Your cities, speaker. Your markets. Your fires. A cold that eats memory, and nothing left to remember anything at all."

Silence. The aurora churned overhead. Somewhere in the ruins, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

"There is a third path," Beatrix said.

"There is one path." The alpha stepped closer, and the snow dimpled under her paws. "The Door must be sealed by a hand, before the winter seals it by itself. Sealed by one of the blood—one who carries Veylmar's gift, one who speaks with beasts, one who *chooses*. A sealing chosen freely binds the winter forever inside this country. It saves everything you love, little charm-seller. It costs one life, given willingly, held here alone in the dark with the echoes, forever."

Beatrix did not look at Pip. Sorrel saw her *not* look at Pip, the effort of it visible along her jaw. "One of the blood," she repeated. "You mean me."

"I mean whoever stands at the threshold when the last snow falls," the alpha said. "I mean whoever has the courage. I do not choose. I only remember—and I am telling you what I remember: that the Blackwood line was sworn to the wolves before Ashvale had a name, and that the debt comes due in every generation, and that yours is the generation the ledger promised."

"The ledger," Beatrix said softly. Ysolt's ledger. Ysolt's last page. *The Door opens only for one who carries Veylmar's name willingly.* She had read it aloud to them on the glacier road, and none of them had understood it fully, and now the understanding arrived like a blade sliding between ribs.

Destiny, Sorrel thought, was not a summons. It was a sentence. Pronounced long ago, by judges long dead, on people who had not been consulted.

Pip raised their head. "If the Door closes," the child said, "we can't come back."

"No."

"And everyone inside stays inside."

"Everyone," the alpha said, "is already inside. That is what sealing means. The echoes remain. The keeper remains. The remembering stops."

"But we'd be safe," Pip said. "Out there. In Ashvale. If it's sealed right, the winter never touches us, and nobody hunts anybody, and—" the child's voice cracked, and Sorrel's heart cracked with it, "—and it would work. It would actually work."

"It would," the alpha agreed, with terrible gentleness.

Beatrix closed her eyes.

Sorrel watched her and understood, with the helpless clarity of the ones who love the self-destructive, exactly what she was doing behind her closed lids: counting. Weighing herself against everyone. Running the market-day arithmetic of a woman who had spent twenty years pricing her own life at less than the lives around her. He had seen her give up her mother's face to a stag for a mountain pass. He had seen her speak hounds into confusion knowing the magic would never come back. She was already halfway to the threshold in her mind. She was already saying goodbye.

*No,* he wanted to say. *Not this time. Not you.*

But the parliament was watching, and the words of a forest-line wolf carried no weight here, and besides—he had run from his own destiny for twenty years. Who was he to stand between a woman and hers?

The alpha turned away, dismissing them, and the dismissal itself was a verdict: *Go and decide. The winter decides faster.*

* * *

They climbed back toward their camp in silence. Above the ruined city, the snow was still falling upward through the Door-valley beyond, flake by flake returning to the sky, and Sorrel thought: *even the winter is counting down.* Each flake that rose was one less in the account. The long winter was nearly finished falling.

At the top of the ridge, Beatrix finally spoke.

"One keeper," she said. "Alone. Forever. Holding a whole country's memory in the dark." She laughed, a small broken sound with no humor in it at all. "Twenty years I hid my gift in silver charms and whispered to spoons. Twenty years being careful, being small, being *nothing*, so I wouldn't owe anyone anything. And it turns out I was born owing. Born into debt. Sworn before I could walk." She looked out at the aurora. "There's no third option. There's no clever trick. There's the door closing on my terms or the winter closing on everyone else's."

Pip's small hand found hers.

Beatrix smiled down at the child—a smile Sorrel recognized, because he had seen it in the market stall a lifetime ago: the smile she wore when she had already decided to sacrifice something and didn't want anyone to stop her.

"Get some sleep," she said. "All of you. Tomorrow I'll want to see the Door again."

She walked toward the tents. Corvin followed with his eyes, and Sorrel read the huntsman's expression easily enough: he had seen that smile before too, and he was already, silently, looking for ways to spend himself in her place.

Which left Pip, standing in the snow, staring at the ridge where the white wolves had vanished, and Sorrel.

"Sorrel," the child said. "The voice under the ice. It talked to me tonight. While the parliament was talking to everyone else."

Sorrel went very still. "What did it say?"

Pip hugged Corvin's cloak tighter around their shoulders. When they spoke, their voice was very quiet, and very certain, and it fell into Sorrel's chest like a stone into a well.

"It said the alpha is lying. It said the Door doesn't need one keeper." The child looked up, and their eyes reflected the aurora, green fire in grey. "It said it needs many who *choose* to remember—and that it's been waiting twenty years for someone stubborn enough to ask."

Below them, in the valley of the parliament, the great white wolves began to howl—not in anger, Sorrel realized, but in alarm. The snow over the Door-valley had begun to fall upward faster, flake upon flake, the long winter racing toward its end.

Whatever Beatrix decided, she would have to decide it soon.

And the voice under the ice, whatever it truly was, had just told an eight-year-old child that everything the oldest power in Veylmar believed was wrong.
