## 30. The Keeper's Bargain

The parliament of wolves spoke the truth the way avalanches speak: once, and without taking it back.

*One of the blood must hold the Door,* said the eldest among them — a she-wolf gray as old pewter whom the others called Marenna. *The seal is not a stone or a spell. It is attention. Someone must stand at the threshold and remember Veylmar, name by name, day by day, for as long as the world lasts. Memory kept alone keeps the winter in.*

They stood in the ruined great hall of the wolf parliament, a vault of shattered arches where snow fell upward through holes in the roof and drifted into the sky like smoke returning to its fire. Around them, on broken thrones of granite, the great wolves sat in a half-circle, their eyes reflecting aurora light that had no sun to source it. Beyond the hall's open doors, the echo-citizens of Veylmar walked their final day again and again — a baker setting out loaves that crumbled before anyone could buy them, a woman shaking dust from a rug in a house that was mostly ceiling now, children chasing a hoop down a street that ended in rubble.

Beatrix had watched them for three days. She had learned not to weep each time one passed through her, which they did sometimes, because grief has weight but echoes do not.

*You are Blackwood,* Marenna had said, when Beatrix asked why her. *Your line was sworn to us before Ashvale had walls. The Door will open its seal to your hand because your blood remembers what your mind never knew.*

Now, alone in the antechamber while the wolves conferred in their low thunder-voices, Beatrix turned the bargain over the way she'd once turned charms over a market table, looking for the flaw in the silver.

There wasn't one. That was the terrible part. It was clean work, honest work, the first fully honest thing she'd been offered in twenty years of small lies. Seal the Door. Hold the memory. Keep the long winter from spilling into the living world and freezing rivers in high summer, from sending the ice-wolves' hunger south into villages full of children like Pip.

And stay. Alone. Forever.

She did the arithmetic the way she'd always done it — quickly, privately, without letting her face move. Corvin could go back. He had penance to perform in the living world, names to register instead of hunt, and he was better with people than he believed; he simply hadn't been around honest ones lately. Ysolt was gone — Beatrix pressed the thought flat whenever it rose, like smoothing a wrinkle out of cloth — but her ledger needed carrying home, page by page, to families who still waited to learn whether an erased name had survived somewhere. And Pip —

Her hands closed slowly in her lap.

Pip would hate it. Pip would fight it. Pip would stand in front of the Door with that stubborn chin and that voice that cracked mountains open and refuse, loudly, in front of everyone, and Beatrix would have to lie to a child who could hear lies in the voices of animals, which meant lying carefully, which meant lying well.

So don't tell them, said a voice that sounded reasonable and was not. Tell them the sealing needs preparation. Days of preparation. Send them through ahead — Corvin first, wounded and slowing them down anyway; then Sorrel, whose place was with his scattered kin beyond the gate; then Pip, because the living world had schools and gardens and winters that ended. Then close it behind them gently, the way you bank a fire when you leave a room, and let them discover the truth later, when it couldn't be argued with. When it would only hurt, not change anything.

It was a good plan. It was a coward's plan wearing a martyr's coat, and Beatrix knew the difference, and chose it anyway.

Because here was the thing she could not say aloud in the parliament hall, not even to wolves: some small, shameful chamber of her heart had heard *alone forever* and felt relief.

Not relief at leaving them. Relief at finally having permission to stop running. Twenty years of concealment, of whispering to silver in the dark of a market stall, of flinching every time a customer lingered too long over her hands — she had built a whole self out of hiding, and hiding had become load-bearing. If she stayed here, she would never have to learn who she was in a world that didn't require her to be small. She would never have to sit across from Corvin at a morning fire and decide, finally, whether forgiveness lived in her or not. She would never have to re-earn Pip's trust, day after ordinary day, knowing there were memories she'd sold for passage through a mountain pass and might never get back.

Alone forever was just the last and largest room in the house of hiding. And she had always been good at houses like that.

*You are deciding,* said Marenna, from the doorway. Wolves moved silently; Beatrix was learning that with Marenna it was a courtesy rather than a trick.

"I'm doing sums," Beatrix said.

*We know the shape of this choice. We have seen keepers chosen before.* Something moved behind the old wolf's eyes, some weather far offshore. *The last one was a woman named Ilseva. She sealed the Door during the first collapse, when the kingdom tore itself and the crown came with hammers and ledgers. She held the country's memory alone for two hundred years.*

"Where is she now?"

Marenna was quiet long enough that the upward-falling snow seemed loud.

"She is the Door," the wolf said at last. "That is what holding means, in the end. First you remember for Veylmar. Then you remember as Veylmar. Then the remembering eats the shape of you, gently, the way water eats a stone — kindly, patiently, until one morning there is no keeper standing at the threshold. There is only the threshold, and it dreams of a market stall in another country, and does not know why."

Beatrix looked at her hands. They were steady. She made herself say it: "Then it's a fair price."

*Nothing about it is fair,* Marenna said. *But it is real, and real things can be borne. Will you accept?*

"I accept."

The word went out of her like a coin laid on a counter, final, no take-backs. Somewhere in the ruined city, a bell that had no ringers rang once — or perhaps it was only the ice settling, or the Door acknowledging a contract written in older ink than language. Marenna bowed her great head, and the other wolves in the hall bowed theirs, and it should have felt like coronation. It felt like burial. She noticed that she stood very straight for it, the way her mother must have taught her to stand at funerals back before her mother became a faceless absence in her own mind.

*Then prepare your people,* Marenna said. *The winter finishes falling within days. When the last snow lands, the Door seals — with a keeper inside, or not at all.*

---

She told Corvin first, because he was easiest, and she hated herself a little for ranking them by ease.

They sat in what had been a guardhouse near the Door's valley, under a roof that held in most places, around a fire fed on driftwood that the upward wind carried in off the plains. His wound had closed to an angry red seam along his ribs, and he'd stopped pretending he could lift his sword arm above his shoulder, which from Corvin was a kind of honesty.

"The Door needs a keeper," she said. "Someone of the blood. Someone who stays."

His eyes came up fast — hunter's reflexes, even sitting down. "Stays how long?"

"For good."

"And after that?"

"There isn't an after that."

He looked at the fire. Corvin had a way of going still when something landed hard, a stillness he'd probably been trained into, all those years of receiving orders that would get men killed. When he spoke again his voice was rough. "I wrote a list once. Names of the gifted, for the crown. Your mother's name was on it." He'd confessed this already, fever-bright in a shepherd's bothy, but he returned to it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. "Let me be the one who pays. I'll ask them—"

"You're not of the blood. It wouldn't hold."

"Then I'll find someone who is, and pay them, and carry the cost myself besides—"

"Corvin." She put the word down between them like a stone. "This is mine. Not penance. Mine. I'm the one who spent twenty years hiding what I am while other people burned for it. Let me spend something worth spending."

He studied her for a long moment, and she saw him almost see it — the wrongness underneath, the coat on the coward — and then look away, because he loved her, or something adjacent to love, and love makes men agreeable exactly when they shouldn't be.

"When do we go through?" he asked.

"Tomorrow. You first, at dawn. Then Sorrel. Then Pip." She kept her voice level by feeding it facts. "The winter finishes soon. After the last snow, no one crosses."

"Pip won't leave without you."

"Pip will do what I say." Even as she said it she heard how hollow it was, how much it sounded like a charm that worked only because nobody tested it.

Corvin nodded slowly. He reached across the fire and gripped her wrist, brief and hard, huntsman's grip, saying everything he didn't have words for. "Twenty years ago I tried to arrest you in a market square," he said. "Best mistake I ever made."

"Go sleep," she said, because if he talked longer she would crack, and cracking was not in the plan.

---

Sorrel took it differently. Of course he did. Sorrel had known her since she was six years old and had climbed into the winter forest outside Veylmar to talk to wolves, back when the Door still leaked and the borders still blurred and a child's gift could wander where it liked.

She found him on the ridge above the valley, watching the aurora. Snow fell upward past them both, unhurried, patient as everything in this country.

"You've heard," she said.

*I hear everything you decide,* Sorrel said. *Half before you do.* He turned his head, amber eyes catching the green light. *You mean to send us through and shut the door behind us.*

"Yes."

*Without telling the child.*

"They'll grieve faster than they'd argue. Grief heals. Arguments don't."

*That is a lie you have practiced,* Sorrel said, without heat. *I have heard you practice it for years, at your stall, to customers, to yourself. It is one of your best.* He rose, shook the strange snow from his coat. *I will go through, Beatrix. My kin are there, and my hips are old, and I have earned a warm death in a warm wood. But I will not help you lie to Pip. That price is yours to pay alone, and I think you have paid enough alone for one life.*

"Sorrel—"

*When you are the Door,* he said, padding toward the trail down, *and you dream of a market stall — come find me in the dream. I'll be the wolf in the alley. I was always the wolf in the alley.* He glanced back once. *You were never alone, little speaker. You only kept choosing it.*

Then he was gone down the slope, and Beatrix stood in the upward snow and let the words hit where they'd aimed, because she deserved the bruise.

---

Pip was hardest, so she saved it for last, and she lied.

She told them at dusk, in the guardhouse, with Corvin conveniently asleep and Sorrel conveniently elsewhere, the way she'd arranged it — arranged it, she noted distantly, like a woman staging a room before buyers came through. She knelt so they were eye to eye. Pip was eight, all elbows and sharp chin, hair that defied every comb, and they were watching her with those eyes that missed nothing, the eyes of a child whose gift was keyed to a dead country's heartbeat.

"Here's what happens tomorrow," she said. "You go through the Door with Corvin at dawn. Then Sorrel. The Door needs sealing, and the sealing needs time, and I have to be the last one through. But I'll follow. I promise. A day, maybe two."

Pip's face did something complicated — hope and doubt wrestling in a small arena. "Why you last?"

"Somebody has to hold it open while the rest walk through. That's the rule. Old magic. Boring." The lies came easily, smoothly, and she despised how easily, how smoothly. "You'll be first on the other side. You can pick where we build the house."

"Near the garden spot? With the thawed ground?"

"Near the garden spot."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a treaty negotiation. Then they threw their arms around her neck, fierce and trusting, and said into her shoulder, "You promise-promise? Not market-promise?"

Beatrix's chest did something structural.

"Not market-promise," she said, and held the child, and stared over Pip's head at the fire, and understood that this was the moment the plan died — right here, in the gap between what she'd said and what her body did while saying it, some tremor in her voice or tension in her arms that a child attuned to the truth beneath all voices could not fail to notice.

Pip pulled back and looked at her. Just looked. Eight years old, and reading her like weather.

"Okay," Pip said finally. Brightly. Too brightly. "Dawn, then. I'll pack."

And went whistling — actually whistling, a wobbly little tune — out the guardhouse door into the upward-falling snow.

Beatrix sat by the fire a long time, telling herself the whistle meant the lie had worked.

It did not occur to her — not yet, not in that warm and doomed hour — that a child who could hear the mountain waking, who had screamed an avalanche loose to save them, who listened under ice to a voice like knocking — that such a child might already know what the Door demanded. Might have heard it whispered by the stones themselves, days ago, in the language beneath language.

It did not occur to her that the whistling was for her benefit.

And it did not occur to her, as she banked the fire and lay down and did not sleep, that somewhere in the frozen corridors of the ruin, small determined footsteps were already heading for the wolf parliament, and that in the morning, when she brought Pip to the threshold with her rehearsed farewell ready, the child would turn at the Door's edge with tears cutting tracks through the grime on their face and say, loud enough for wolves and echoes and the whole dying country to hear:

*"You promised me memory kept alone is just a tomb. I heard you tell the wolves. I'm not letting you get in it."*
