## 31. Pip Refuses

Pip had learned early that grown-ups went quiet in two ways. There was the quiet of anger, which came with tight mouths and loud boots. And there was the quiet of a decision already made, which came with soft voices and careful hands and everyone looking at you like you were made of spun sugar.

The second kind was the dangerous one.

Pip heard it the moment they crept up the broken stair of the echo-house, where they'd all been sleeping since coming through the Door. Beatrix's voice drifted down from the upper room — the one with the collapsed roof that let the aurora pour through like spilled milk — and Pip knew before a single word landed that something bad was being arranged without them.

They should have gone back to bed. Grown-ups always said *you'll understand when you're older*, as if understanding were a thing you could wait for like teeth coming in. But Pip had spent eight years learning that nobody tells children anything until it's too late, so they pressed themselves into the shadow of the stairwell and listened.

"—and once I take the keeping, the Door seals behind you," Beatrix said. "All three of you go through to Ashvale. The winter ends on this side. You live."

Corvin's voice, low and rough: "And you stay here alone. In a country of ghosts. Forever."

"It isn't forever," Beatrix said. "It's just — always."

Pip's chest went hot and small at the same time.

"That's the same thing," Corvin said.

"It's the bargain," Sorrel rumbled. His wolf-voice was like stones rolling downhill. "The parliament will not bend it. One keeper, of the blood, who carries the name willingly. Beatrix has the blood. She has the name. She is willing." A pause, long and unhappy. "I do not like it either, huntsman. But the old laws are not ours to rewrite."

"Everything is ours to rewrite," Corvin said. "That's how laws got written the first time. Somebody rewrote them."

"And look what it bought us," Sorrel said.

Pip didn't hear Beatrix answer right away. When she did, her voice had gone soft in the worst way, the way she talked when she'd already decided something and was only waiting for the world to catch up. "It's done. Tomorrow morning. You'll take Pip through first — Pip goes first, promise me, because otherwise the little beast will argue until the sun burns out—"

Pip didn't wait to hear the rest.

* * *

They found Beatrix at dawn, or what passed for dawn in Veylmar — the aurora dimming to a pale green wash over the ruined rooftops, snow falling upward out of the valley in slow silver threads. She stood at the edge of the echo-square where the Door rose against the sky: two pillars of black stone and a lintel between them, carved with names so old the letters had worn smooth. Frost climbed its face in patterns like fern leaves. It was beautiful. Pip hated it.

"You're doing it," Pip said.

Beatrix turned. Up close her face looked like paper someone had folded and unfolded too many times. "Pip. How much did you hear?"

"All of it. Every word." Pip planted their feet the way Sorrel did when he meant business. "You're going to leave us. You're going to send us away and lock yourself inside a graveyard with a bunch of echoes who can't even see you."

"They can see me now. That's part of the keeping—"

"That's worse!" The word tore out of Pip bigger than they meant it to, big enough that every crow on the ruined bell-tower turned its head at once, big enough that somewhere down the frozen street a cat lifted its face from a windowsill and stared. Pip felt their gift flare like a struck match and didn't try to smother it for once. Let everything listen. "Beatrix, they can't talk to you. They can't eat breakfast with you. They do the same day over and over — I've watched them. The baker woman drops her bread and picks it up and drops it again. Forever. That's your forever. That's your *always*."

"Pip—"

"You promised." Pip's eyes were burning and they let them burn. "In the root cellar, when the Wardens were walking over our heads and you held my hand and I was so scared my teeth chattered, you said *nobody gets left, little beast*. You said it. You don't get to say things like that and then decide they don't count because the wolves made a rule."

"The wolves didn't make the rule." Beatrix crouched down to Pip's level, which she probably thought was kind and which Pip knew was a trick, because it was harder to scream at someone whose eyes were level with yours. "The Door did. Veylmar did. There has to be a keeper or the winter spreads past the gate and kills everything green in Ashvale. Everything. Your Marek. His daughter. The market. All of it. Do you understand what I'm trading? I'm trading my always for everybody else's tomorrows."

"No," Pip said.

"Pip—"

"*No.*" And then it all came pouring out, eight years of it, the whole flood: "You think I don't know about being left? I know everything about being left! My mother left. The city left. Every door in Ashvale closed in my face until the rats were nicer to me than people were. And then you came down into the dark under the city with flour on your sleeves and you looked at me like I was worth finding, and you said *nobody gets left*, and I believed you, because I was stupid, because I was eight and stupid—"

"You weren't stupid."

"I believed you!" Pip's voice cracked wide open. "And now you're standing there making lists of reasons it's fine, and I want you to hear me, Beatrix Blackwood, I want you to hear it so deep it gets stuck in your bones: memory kept alone isn't memory. It's a tomb. You said it yourself — the whole reason we walked across a country and lost Ysolt and bled on the ice road was so Veylmar wouldn't be forgotten. Forgotten by *who*? By people. By us. Out there. If you seal the Door, who remembers? The echoes remember nothing, they just repeat. You'll be the only remembering thing in the whole country, holding a million names in your head with no one to say them to, and that's not saving anybody, that's just the loneliest grave ever dug, and I am not going through that Door without you. I'm not. Chain me to Sorrel. I'll bite him. He knows I'll do it."

Behind Pip, a low huff of breath that might have been an offended wolf or a laughing one.

"Pip." Beatrix's voice wobbled. Good. Wobble back. "Sweetheart. Listen to me. If I don't do this, the winter doesn't end. People die. Real people, warm ones, the ones with tomorrow—"

"Then find another way!"

"There isn't one. The parliament said—"

"The parliament also said the Door needed one keeper, and Ysolt's page said it needed somebody willing, and those are different things, aren't they?" Pip jabbed a finger at the black pillars. "Rules break. Rules cracked before. You told me that story on the glacier — about how the Door used to be open, and the wardens of Veylmar walked both countries like it was nothing, and then the crown got scared and lied, and the Door started closing because *they stopped remembering out loud*. So the rule isn't one keeper. The rule is somebody remembers. Somebody has to be willing. Well—" Pip spread their arms, small and furious and shaking. "*I'm* willing. I hear it, Beatrix. Under the ice. I've heard it since the undercity, the knocking, the calling — it talks to me and not to you, and maybe that means something, maybe the Door wants more than one of us, but you didn't even ask! You sat upstairs with the wolf and the huntsman and you decided, like grown-ups always decide, like everybody always decides about me, and you didn't ask!"

"I was trying to protect you."

"I KNOW." The word hit the square like a hammer on a bell. Every echo in Veylmar seemed to flinch; the upward-falling snow stuttered in the air, hung, swirled. Somewhere beneath the ice, faint and vast, something knocked. Once. Twice. Like a fist on wood. Like applause. Pip barely noticed. "Everybody protects me! Marek protected his daughter by selling you! The crown protects Ashvale by burning us! Protecting is just leaving with better excuses, and I am DONE being protected!"

Pip stamped their foot.

It shouldn't have mattered. An eight-year-old's foot on old flagstones. But Pip's gift wasn't a charm whispered to silver, wasn't a trick learned and controlled — it was raw and waking and keyed, Ysolt had said, to Veylmar itself, to the deep stone and deeper cold, and when Pip's fury hit the ground it went down through the flagstones like a shout through water.

Cracks ran out from their boot.

Thin at first, white lines in the frost, spidering fast across the square. Then the sound came — a groan out of the earth, deep as a whale-song, and the Door itself shivered. Frost slithered off the black pillars in sheets. A seam of pale light split the lintel stone from end to end with a crack like the sky snapping shut, and one shard of ancient granite, older than the crown, older than the ledger, older than the long winter, dropped from the carved arch and shattered on the stones at Beatrix's feet.

Silence. Total. Even the aurora seemed to hold still.

Pip stood in the middle of it, breathing hard, fists clenched, absolutely horrified at themselves and absolutely not sorry.

Sorrel padded forward and sniffed the fallen shard. Then he looked up at Beatrix, and his amber eyes were very old and very certain. "Well," he said. "There's your answer about whether the laws can be rewritten."

Beatrix hadn't moved. She was staring at the crack in the Door — the long bright seam where light leaked through stone that was supposed to be eternal — and then slowly, terribly, she turned and looked at Pip. Not like spun sugar. Not like something to protect. Like something she'd underestimated.

"Pip," she said quietly. "What did you just do?"

"What you wouldn't," Pip said. Their voice shook. They let it. "I knocked back."

For a moment nobody breathed. Then Beatrix crossed the square in four strides and knelt — really knelt, not the talking-down kneel — and pulled Pip against her, hard, flour-and-ash smell and racing heartbeat, and Pip grabbed onto her coat with both hands and finally, finally let the crying happen.

"I'm still furious at you," Pip mumbled into her shoulder.

"Good. Stay furious. Fury's useful." Her voice was wet. "But Pip — if the Door cracks, the winter doesn't just stop. It could tear loose. Both countries. We might have made everything worse."

"Or better," said a new voice.

Ysolt's ledger lay open on the well-head where they'd left it, and no wind moved in the sealed valley, but the last page — Ysolt's dying gift, the page about willingness — turned over by itself and lay flat.

And from beyond the Door, from the deep places under the ice where the calling lived, the knocking came again. Three times now. Patient. Almost pleased.

Almost like an invitation.
