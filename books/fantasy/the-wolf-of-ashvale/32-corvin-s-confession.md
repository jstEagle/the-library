## 32. Corvin's Confession

Corvin had been a soldier long enough to know that the hardest words were never the ones you said in battle. Battle was easy. Battle was clean. You drew, you struck, you bled, and afterward there was always someone to tell you it had meant something.

The hard words were the ones you said by firelight to people who had every right to hate you.

He sat apart from the others, at the edge of the ruined chapel where they'd made camp, turning his wounded arm over and over though the bandage needed nothing more done to it. The wound Vessa's blade had given him at the ford was healing crooked — he could feel it knitting wrong beneath the linen, a tightness when he flexed his fingers. He'd let it knit wrong on purpose. It felt like a fair price.

Across the hollow, Beatrix sat with Pip asleep against her shoulder, her eyes on the Door-road ahead of them even now, as if she could see through the mountain to that standing gate where snow fell upward. Sorrel lay between them and the dark, ears moving in his sleep, tracking dreams the way he once tracked deer.

Tomorrow they would reach it. Tomorrow Beatrix Blackwood would walk up to the threshold of a dead country and learn what it asked of its keeper.

And Corvin Hale would not be there carrying a lie.

* * *

He had rehearsed this confession for eleven days.

He knew because he counted. Eleven days since the ford, since he'd gone down shielding a child who should have been his prisoner, since Vessa had looked at him across the ice with an expression he'd seen only once before — on the face of a young corporal he'd ordered flogged for mercy, back when Corvin still believed the crown's arithmetic.

Eleven nights of lying awake composing sentences and discarding them. *I'm sorry* was useless. *I didn't know* was a lie. *They made me do it* was the refuge of every man who had ever fed someone to a machine like the Wardens and wanted to sleep afterward.

There was no sentence that fixed what he had done. He understood that now. What remained was only whether he would stand in front of the people he'd broken and let them see the whole of him, or keep the decent mask on until the Door closed behind them all.

His whole career, he had chosen the mask.

It started, as these things did, small. He told himself that first night, watching the fire: *I was twenty-six years old. I believed in the crown.* And then, because he was tired of lying even to himself: *No. I liked being good at something. That was all. They handed me a task and I did it better than anyone, and being good at it felt better than asking what it was.*

The list.

Gods, the list. He could still write it from memory, every name, thirty-one lines in his own careful clerk's hand. He'd spent three months compiling it — interviews, informants' reports, market gossip, the testimony of frightened neighbors against frightened neighbors. A census of the gifted of Ashvale and the surrounding provinces, commissioned quietly after the third beast-speaker scandal, before the Purge had a name. He'd thought of it as police work. He'd thought of himself as thorough.

He had not thought of it as a scythe being sharpened. Not until they started using it that way.

Thirty-one names. He'd checked the count so many times over the years that the number had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket. Thirty-one people whose lives he had weighed and written down in ink, and whose weight had never once appeared on any scale he answered to.

Line nineteen was *Blackwood, Maren — charm-seller, Market Row. Beast-speech suspected, unconfirmed.*

Unconfirmed. He'd written that word himself, and it hadn't saved her, because confirmation turned out not to matter. The list wasn't evidence. The list was permission.

Maren Blackwood had burned in the Warden Yard on a Tuesday in autumn, in front of a crowd given bread and ale to make a festival of it. Corvin had stood at the edge of that crowd in plainclothes, because his commanding officer told him he ought to see his work completed, and he had watched a woman with her daughter's grey eyes refuse the blindfold, and he had gone home and drunk until the room stopped spinning and reported for duty the next morning.

The girl had been there too. He'd seen her — a thin child of maybe ten, held back by neighbors, screaming. He had told himself she'd be taken somewhere safe. He had known perfectly well where children of burned women were taken.

Twenty years later she'd slipped through his fingers in a sewer tunnel smelling of rat and river, and he had lied in his report to protect her, and he had told himself the lie balanced something.

It balanced nothing. He knew that now. A man who writes thirty-one names does not erase one of them by hiding a fugitive. The ledger doesn't work that way. He'd learned that from Ysolt, actually — the old woman with her book of erased names, her fierce bookkeeping of the dead. He understood her better than she would ever want to understand him. They were both clerks of a kind. She recorded to save. He had recorded to kill.

* * *

Pip woke once in the night, whimpering, and Beatrix hummed something low until the child stilled. Corvin watched from his distance and thought: *that child hears the world knocking under the ice, and I helped build the world that hunts children for hearing things.*

The thought did not feel like guilt anymore. Guilt was a younger man's luxury, a hot private weather. This was colder. This was just fact, sitting in his chest like swallowed stone.

Sorrel raised his head when Corvin finally stood. The wolf's eyes caught the firelight, gold going red.

"Walk with me," Corvin said. He didn't know if the wolf would. Wolves didn't take orders from him; that was rather the point of everything.

Sorrel rose, stretched, and padded after him without a sound, which Corvin chose to read as agreement.

They walked the ridge above the chapel, snow squeaking under their boots and paws, the sky overhead bruised black and salted with stars. Below them, far off, a faint green shimmer touched the horizon — the aurora bleeding out of Veylmar, leaking into their world through cracks in winter. The Door was close. He could feel it the way old soldiers felt weather in their scars.

"I'm going to tell her," Corvin said to the wolf. "All of it."

Sorrel looked at him.

"She deserves to hear it before tomorrow. Before—" He stopped. Before the Door showed her whatever it showed. Before she learned what keeping cost. "Before anything happens that makes my confession about me instead of about her mother."

The wolf made a low sound, not quite a growl. Then he turned and led the way back down, which Corvin took for the closest thing to blessing he was going to get.

* * *

Beatrix was alone when he came down. Pip slept wrapped in both their cloaks now, a small mound of breathing wool near the fire, and Beatrix sat feeding it sticks one at a time, her hands steady, her face lit orange and hollow.

She looked up when his boots crunched. She didn't smile. She hadn't smiled at him since the ford, but she'd bound his wound, and fed him, and walked beside him, and he had come to understand that this was her version of trust — not warmth, but inclusion. She included him the way you include a dangerous animal at the edge of camp. Watchfully.

"Hale," she said.

"Blackwood." He sat down across the fire from her, close enough for honesty, far enough for flight. "I need to say something, and I need you to let me finish it."

Her chin lifted slightly. Waiting.

"When I was twenty-six," Corvin said, "I compiled a list."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, wind moved through dead branches.

"The crown wanted a census of the gifted. Quietly. I was the best investigator they had, so I got the commission, and I treated it like police work. Three months of interviews and informants and neighbor against neighbor. Thirty-one names." His voice stayed level. He made it stay level. "I wrote every one of them myself. In my own hand. I was proud of how neat it was."

Beatrix had gone very still. He watched her stillness and recognized it, because he'd spent twenty years studying people in the moment they understood what was coming. Hunters knew that stillness. Prey knew it too.

"Your mother was line nineteen," he said. "*Blackwood, Maren. Charm-seller, Market Row. Beast-speech suspected, unconfirmed.*" He recited it the way he recited everything he couldn't bear to forget — flat, exact, complete. "Unconfirmed. I wrote that word thinking it would matter. It didn't. The list wasn't proof, Beatrix. It was permission. Everything after — the arrests, the trials, the burnings — they used my handwriting as justification. I stood in the crowd when they burned her. My officer made sure I saw it finished. And your mother refused the blindfold, and I went home and drank and slept and did it again the next week with the next name."

The silence that followed was total. Even the fire seemed to burn quieter.

He kept going, because she had let him start, and stopping now would be the last and worst mercy he could offer.

"I've lied to you since the sewer. I lied to Vessa for you. I fought my own brothers at the ford. And every one of those things felt like payment, and none of them were, because a man who writes thirty-one names doesn't erase one of them by saving one person. I understand ledgers now. Ysolt taught me that much. You don't balance a book by tearing out a page. You carry it forward." He spread his wounded arm, the crooked-healing one, the one he'd let knit wrong. "So here is mine, carried forward. I wrote the list. Your mother died of it. There is no version of sorry that covers that, and I'm not going to insult you by pretending there is."

Beatrix's face had not moved. But her eyes — her mother's grey eyes — were wet, and she let them be wet without shame, and somehow that was worse than anger. Anger he could answer. Grief he could only kneel in front of.

"You're telling me this now," she said finally, and her voice was rough and quiet, "because tomorrow I might die."

"Because tomorrow one of us might have to stay," Corvin said. "Behind the Door. Holding whatever it holds. Alone. Forever." He leaned forward. "I've thought about nothing else for eleven days. So hear my offer, and then hate me freely, whichever order you prefer."

He took a breath.

"If the Door needs a keeper — if someone has to remain in that country, alone, holding its memory — then it should be me. Not you. You have a child asleep ten feet away who loves you. You have a wolf who crossed a kingdom to find you. You have a life that people fought and bled to make possible. I have thirty-one names and a crooked arm." His mouth twisted. "I am the one man in this company whose solitude would be justice instead of tragedy. Let me carry it. It's the only page I can add to the ledger that isn't a crime."

For a long moment Beatrix said nothing. She looked at him across the fire — looked *through* him, the way she must have looked through charms all her life, seeing the true thing inside the pretty shell.

"You watched her burn," Beatrix said.

"Yes."

"And you're only telling me now."

"Yes."

Something moved behind her eyes — fury, grief, exhaustion, all of it grinding together like stones in a mill. When she spoke again, her voice had gone very soft, which was infinitely more frightening than shouting.

"Do you know what I remember? Not her face — the stag took her face, I gave it up on the mountain, and I will never forgive that stag or myself. But I remember her hands. I remember her whispering to silver the way I whisper to silver, and I remember thinking we were the same, and I remember the morning the Wardens came, and I remember that I hid, because she told me to hide, and hiding saved my life and broke everything else." Her jaw tightened. "You wrote her name down. You gave her hands to the fire. And then you found me in a sewer and decided you could pay for it by lying well."

"Yes," Corvin said. "That's exactly what I decided. And it was contemptible."

"It was," Beatrix agreed.

The word landed like a blow. He took it. He'd earned it, and worse.

"But," she said.

He waited.

"But I watched you step in front of Pip's body while the ice was still cracking under your feet. I watched you fight men who loved you, and lose, and get up. I watched you lie to Vessa's face with your heart in your throat. And I don't believe people change, Hale. I'm a charm-seller; I deal in what things are, not what they wish they were. But I've watched you spend twenty years of penance already, one small treason at a time, and I think maybe the list didn't make you. Maybe it unmade you, slowly, and everything since has been you trying to find the shape of the man underneath it." She exhaled, long and shaking. "So I'm not forgiving you. Don't ask me to. But I'm not sending you away either, and that's the most I can carry tonight."

"That's more than the ledger requires," Corvin said quietly.

"The ledger," Beatrix said, "is exactly the problem. Everyone keeps treating this like a book that has to balance. Ysolt died balancing it. Someone always has to be the payment, someone always has to stay behind, someone always burns so someone else runs." She looked toward the east, toward the faint green stain on the horizon, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I'm starting to think the Door wants us to believe that. I'm starting to think that's the trap."

Corvin stared at her. Something cold walked up his spine.

"What do you mean?"

"I don't know yet." She poked the fire, sending sparks spiraling up into the dark like upward-falling snow in miniature. "But if it comes down to choosing who stays behind — if it really comes to that — I'll choose no one. Do you understand me? No one. Not you, not me, not the child. I didn't spend twenty years hiding and two weeks running to trade one tomb for another." Her eyes came back to him, grey and burning. "Keep your offer, Hale. Keep it in your pocket like a knife. Because if there's a third way through this, I'll find it. And if there isn't—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. He understood: *if there isn't, we'll die refusing it.*

Pip stirred in the sleep-cloaks and murmured something — a name, half-heard, that neither of them recognized. The child's gift reaching even into dreams, listening to the country under the ice.

Above the eastern ridge, the aurora brightened. Green light spilled over the snow like water finding a crack in a floor, and far away, so faint it might have been wind, came a sound Corvin had heard only once before in his life, twenty years ago, in a report he'd dismissed as hysteria:

Knocking.

Steady. Patient. From beneath the mountain, from beneath the ice, from beneath the world.

Three knocks. A pause. Three more.

As if something on the other side knew they were coming — and was counting.
