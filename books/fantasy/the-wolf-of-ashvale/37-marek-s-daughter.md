## 37. Marek's Daughter

I saw the column from the roof of my house by the gate, three days after the winter broke.

Word had gone out ahead of them the way word does now—travelers passing through the gate carrying news like embers cupped in bare hands—and so I was not surprised when Sefa, one of Corvin's registrars, came up the ladder breathing hard and said, "They're coming. Ashvale folk. A hundred and more. And there's a man at the front with a tinker's cart who keeps asking for you by name."

The name did it. Not *the charm-seller*, not *the woman who ended the winter*. Just my name, plain as bread.

I stood on the roof a long moment, watching the road where it came down out of the hills toward the valley of the gate. Spring in Veylmar was doing things I still hadn't gotten used to: green climbing the ruin walls, water talking to itself in the old canals, birds nesting in the ribs of buildings that had been dead twenty years and were now, improbably, becoming architecture again. The aurora still came at night sometimes, fainter now, like a country remembering how to sleep.

The column was a mile off, maybe two, moving slow. I could see the shape of it even from that distance—carts, children on shoulders, someone leading a goat that clearly had opinions about the pace. Refugees. I knew the shape of refugees. I had been one, once, walking out of Ashvale with nothing but a wolf and a child and a terror I mistook for a plan.

"They'll want food," Sefa said. "And names."

"Everything here wants food and names," I said. "Get Yenna organizing the kitchens. And tell Pip—" I stopped, because Pip didn't need telling anymore. Pip was already down among the wolves, apprenticed now to the parliament, and if a column of strangers crossed the border Pip would hear about it before any human did. The animals talked to Pip the way rivers talk to their banks: constantly, and without much respect for hierarchy.

"I'll go down," I said.

"You don't have to," Sefa said gently. "Corvin could meet them."

"Corvin meeting a column of Ashvale refugees would go about as well as you'd expect." Some of them would know his face. Some of them would know his list. "It should be me."

Sefa looked at me the way people had started looking at me since the Naming—with something between reverence and worry—and said, "You know who it is, don't you. The tinker."

I had known since she said the cart.

"Marek," I said.

* * *

He looked older. That was the first thing, and it felt like an accusation aimed at time rather than at him—twenty years is twenty years, and I had no right to be surprised that they'd spent him the same way they'd spent me. His hair had gone grey at the temples. His hands, which I remembered as quick and sure over small mechanisms, were scarred across the knuckles in the way of hands that work metal in bad light. He walked at the head of his column with a limp he was trying to hide, and when he saw me standing at the roadside with the gate behind me and the spring wind pulling at my hair, he stopped walking altogether.

Behind him, a hundred and forty-odd people stopped too, because you stop when the person in front of you stops, especially when the person in front of you has just frozen solid in the middle of the road.

We looked at each other across maybe thirty feet of Veylmar mud and new grass.

He'd sold me. That was the sentence I had carried out of Ashvale, through the sewers, across the bonefields, up the ice road, all the way to this valley where snow once fell upward. *Marek sold me.* It had been the simplest fact in my life for a long time, simpler than hunger, simpler than cold. I had built rooms in my head around it the way you build rooms around a load-bearing wall. Pull it out and everything above it comes down.

And then, later—much later, in the bothy, in the fever-light, listening to Corvin confess to a list—heavier truths had come along and rearranged the furniture. Not erased it. Nothing gets erased here; that was the whole point of everything I'd done. But rearranged. Marek had bought a charm from me every month for six years and never once let his eyes linger too long. Marek had lingered too long over my hands on the day the proclamation went up, and I had read that lingering as calculation, and I had been wrong about what it was made of—or right, and it didn't matter, because fear does its own arithmetic and always balances the books against the people you love.

His daughter had been nine years old. Nine, and gifted—the way Pip was gifted, the way I was gifted—and the crown had announced that the gifted must be registered or hunted, and registration meant the burning pits eventually, everyone knew it even then, before the pits filled. And Marek had known a Warden captain who drank, and the Warden captain had wanted a name, any name, a speaker delivered alive being worth more than a speaker burned, and Marek had known exactly whose name to give because he had watched her whisper silver awake for six years.

He had traded me for her. One life weighed against another, on scales he never asked to hold.

"Beatrix," he said. His voice cracked on it. Thirty feet, and the crack carried.

"Marek."

"I—" He took a step and stopped himself, like a man testing ice that had already broken under him once. Behind him the column murmured. Somewhere in it, a child's voice said *Mama, who is that?* and got no answer.

I held up a hand—not to stop him, just to hold the space, the way you'd hold a door. "Let them keep moving," I said. "There's food at the gate, and beds, and Corvin Hale will take your names himself, and yes, I know how that will land with some of you, and he knows it too. He'll be standing in the open with his sleeves rolled up while he does it. That's not nothing. Let them come."

Marek turned and passed the words back down the column, and the column moved again, shuffling past us, and I stood there and let them look at me. They did look. I could feel it, the weight of a hundred and forty pairs of eyes, most of them hollowed out by the road and the purge and whatever version of Ashvale's burning they'd walked away from. Since the Naming I had become a story to people, and stories get stared at. I had mostly made peace with it. Mostly.

A girl of about fourteen stopped in front of me. She had Marek's jaw and none of Marek's caution, and she looked me up and down with the frank appraisal of someone who had survived something and therefore considered herself entitled to an opinion.

"You're smaller than the stories," she said.

"Everyone's smaller than the stories," I said. "That's what makes them stories."

Her father made a strangled noise. The girl ignored him. "Wren," she said, and stuck out her hand.

I shook it. Her grip was strong and her palm was calloused in the pattern of someone who'd been doing adult work since she was small, and when our skin touched I felt it—a flicker, a static charge, the way the world felt when another speaker was near. She felt it too. Her eyes went wide, and then narrowed, and then she did something that undid me completely: she glanced over her shoulder at her father, quick and guilty, the way I used to glance over my shoulder at the alleys whenever Sorrel spoke.

She'd been hiding it her whole life. Of course she had. She was Marek's daughter.

"Go on up to the gate," I told her, as kindly as I could. "Ask for Yenna in the kitchens. Tell her you're mine."

*Mine.* I heard the word leave my mouth and wondered at it. Wren grinned—sudden, bright, a crack of sun—and went, and Marek watched her go with his whole body turned toward her, the way a plant turns toward light, and I understood that I was looking at the arithmetic itself. Not the sums. The reason anyone bothers to do sums at all.

* * *

We talked in the kitchen garden behind my house, because I didn't want to do it indoors, and I didn't want to do it at the gate where the whole arriving world could watch. There were two benches by the bean rows Pip had planted that first spring, and a pear tree that had grown from a cutting of a tree that had died twenty years ago in another country, which felt right. The tree was blossoming. White petals came loose in the breeze and landed on the bench between us like small, patient snow.

Marek sat down like a man sitting for judgment. He put his scarred hands flat on his knees and stared at the beans.

"I practiced this," he said. "Every mile of the road, I practiced. Had a whole speech. Wren made me say it out loud twice so I wouldn't lose my nerve." He laughed, and it wasn't a good laugh. "Can't remember a word of it."

"Don't use the speech."

"No?"

"Speeches are for crowds." I picked a petal off my sleeve. "Just say the true thing. Whatever it is. You've carried it far enough."

For a long moment the only sound was the bees working the blossoms and, far off, the gate-bell ringing as more arrivals registered. Then Marek said, "I told myself it wasn't really betraying you. That's the first lie. I want you to know I know it was a lie. I told myself you were clever enough to get away—you'd dodged Wardens for twenty years, you had escape routes I probably helped pay for without knowing it, buying charms, keeping you solvent. I told myself one name given alive might buy leniency for the ones who registered late. I told myself—" His breath went ragged. "I told myself a hundred things, Beatrix, and every single one of them was built to make room underneath a single fact, which is that a man asked me for your name and I gave it to him, and I slept that night. Maybe not well. But I slept."

The petal sat on my sleeve. I looked at it instead of him, because looking at him was going to cost something and I wanted to spend it deliberately.

"Wren had started talking to the neighbor's dog," he went on, quieter. "Out loud. In the street. She was nine. She didn't understand why that was dangerous—why would she? And the dog understood her, Beatrix, you could see it, the way it would sit and watch her mouth move like the words mattered, and so did I, and so did the cobbler's wife, and the cobbler's wife had a brother in the Wardens." He finally turned to look at me. His eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "I did the sums. You were grown. You were careful. You'd survive. She was nine and she was careless because nine-year-olds are careless, and the only variable I could control was which name hit the ledger first. So I gave them yours."

"And then you left," I said. Not accusingly. Just placing it, the way you place a stone in a wall.

"And then we left." He nodded. "Two days later. Before the raid even—I knew the timetable. We went south first, then west, kept ahead of the lists. Kept ahead until there was nowhere left to keep ahead to, and then the word started coming up the refugee roads about a gate in the north where the winter ended, and a woman who spoke the names of the dead and un-erased a whole country." He huffed something that was almost a smile. "Wren made me ask for you by name. She said if I couldn't say your name to your face, I had no business bringing her here."

"She sounds wise beyond her years."

"She sounds like her father's conscience with legs." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Say what you're going to say. Please. I've imagined every version of it for four years. The mercy of getting the actual thing instead of the imagining—"

"I forgave you before I understood it," I said.

He went still.

"In the bonefields, the night after I spoke the hounds into confusion—there's magic you spend and don't get back, and I spent it, and I lay in the wet grass shaking and I thought, somewhere in Ashvale there's a man who did a terrible thing for love, and I am going to become him or I am going to die, and either way I will never be able to hate him properly again." I set the petal on the bench between us, deliberate as a coin. "Because that's what the sums do, Marek. Fear does arithmetic, and the arithmetic is always correct, and it is always monstrous, and the only people who get to judge it are the ones who've never had anything they couldn't afford to lose. I had a wolf and a child and nothing else. You had a daughter. I'm not going to insult either of us by pretending those weigh the same on the scale, because they don't—they weigh exactly the same, and that's the horror of it."

The bell rang again at the gate. A bee droned past. Marek's hands had come up off his knees without his seeming to notice, hovering half-raised, the way hands hover when a man wants to reach for something and doesn't believe he's allowed.

"But," I said.

His hands dropped.

"But forgiven isn't forgotten, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise, because forgetting is the whole crime I walked into a dead country to undo. If I wipe the slate, I'm doing what the crown did to Veylmar—deciding a memory is too inconvenient to keep." I met his eyes. "So here's what I can give you, and I think it's worth more than forgiveness anyway. I remember. All of it—the six years of charms, the day you lingered over my hands, the rats in the dark, the raid. I remember it and I'm keeping it, the way I keep the ledger names. Not as a wound. As a truth. You're going to live next to me in this valley, probably for the rest of our lives, and some mornings I'm going to look at you and see the man who sold me, and I'm going to choose to also see the man who led a hundred and forty people through a war zone to a door in the mountains because he refused to let his daughter grow up hunted. Both of those men are real. I'm not going to erase either one."

"That's—" His voice failed. He tried again. "Most people would just say 'I forgive you' and be done."

"Most people haven't spent a winter arguing with an entire parliament of wolves about whether memory needs a keeper." I almost smiled. "They ruined simple answers for me forever."

Marek laughed then—a real one, cracked and wet at the edges—and he reached out, hesitantly, and closed his hand over mine where it rested on the bench. His palm was warm and rough and it shook slightly, and I let it shake. Under the pear tree, petals came down on us both like a small, forgiving weather.

"Wren talks to everything," he said. "Birds, horses, the goat—especially the goat, they argue constantly. I don't know how to help her learn. I never dared find anyone who could."

"There are wolves here who've been teaching speakers since before Ashvale had walls," I said. "Pip will take her out tomorrow. Fair warning: Pip is fifteen now and insufferable about it."

"Pip. The child from the wall." He shook his head slowly. "You found each other in the undercity and now they apprentice to wolves. This place is mad."

"This place is honest," I said. "It takes some getting used to."

We sat a while longer, not talking, and it was a different silence than the one we'd earned in his shop over six years of charms—the old silence had a cellar under it, things hidden below the floorboards. This one was just silence, spring air, the sound of a country coming back to life. Reconciliation without forgetting. I thought: *this is what the third way looks like when it's small. When it's just two people at a bench refusing to round the numbers off.*

Then Wren came pelting down the garden path at a dead run, cheeks flushed, eyes enormous, with Pip loping behind her at a jog and, trotting sedately at her heels with the expression of a chaperone at a scandalous party, a wolf.

Not Sorrel—one of the younger parliament wolves, brindled grey, tolerating Wren's presence with visible magnanimity. Wren skidded to a stop in front of the bench.

"He TALKED to me," she announced. "The wolf. He said my name. He said—" she faltered, suddenly shy, glancing at her father, and then plowed on because she was Marek's daughter and caution had skipped a generation, "he said the parliament wants to meet me. Tomorrow. At dawn. Pip says nobody meets the parliament at dawn unless they mean it."

"The parliament is showing off," I said. "Ignore them. They've gotten dramatic since the thaw."

The brindled wolf made a low huffing sound that was unmistakably laughter.

But then Wren's expression changed. The flush drained out of her face, and she gripped the edge of the bench, and I watched a girl who had spent her whole life translating between species go very carefully still—the way I went still, the way Pip went still, the particular stillness of a speaker receiving something large.

"What is it?" Marek was on his feet instantly. "Wren—"

"It's the birds," she whispered. "There's a flock coming down the valley. From the north. From past the gate." Her eyes lifted to mine, and in them was the exact expression I'd seen on Pip's face years ago outside the walls of Ashvale, the look of a gift arriving too big for its owner. "Beatrix, they're saying there's someone else on the road. Walking alone. And the birds say—" she swallowed— "the birds say he's wearing a Warden's coat, but he keeps stopping at the boundary stones and reading the names carved on them. Out loud. Like he's checking them against something he memorized."

Marek's hand found my shoulder. The petals kept falling. Far off at the gate, the bell rang on, indifferent, and somewhere down the valley a man in a dead empire's uniform was walking toward us reciting the names of the erased—and every bird between there and here was telling my secret to the sky.
