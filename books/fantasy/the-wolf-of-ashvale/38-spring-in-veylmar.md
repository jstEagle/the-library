## 38. Spring in Veylmar

The first thing you learn as an apprentice to the wolf parliament is that wolves do not hurry.

The second thing you learn is that they expect you to learn the first thing immediately, and they have no patience for apprentices who keep trying to rush the thaw.

"Again," said Maelis, the eldest of them, gray to the elbows of her forelegs, her muzzle gone silver like frost that had decided to stay.

I crouched in the mud of what had once been the Palace Square of Veylmar — though "palace" was generous now, since half its stones were scattered and the other half had daffodils coming up through the cracks, which I thought was a definite improvement — and pressed both palms flat against the ground.

"Again," I repeated, because apparently apprentices also repeat things.

"No," Maelis said. "*Feel* again. There is a difference. The ground is not a door you knock on. It is a sleeper you are waking gently."

"I'm being gentle."

"You are being *eager*. Eager is gentle's loud cousin who arrives early and breaks the cups."

Sorrel, sprawled on the flat stone where the sun came through the broken colonnade, made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter could be done by a wolf with his eyes closed. He was old now. Not ancient — I refused to allow it — but old in the way that showed at the edges: the silver spreading up from his paws, the way he rose from lying down in two careful movements instead of one easy one, the way he slept more and hunted less and liked best of all to lie in the sun while I worked and offer commentary he pretended was wisdom.

"The child knocks like a Warden battering a gate," Sorrel said to no one in particular.

"The child," I said, "is standing right here."

"The child has dirt on their face."

"The child is *gardening*."

"Gardening." Sorrel opened one eye, amber and amused. "In the parliament square. With your whole self pressed to the earth like a fallen tree. Yes. Gardening."

Maelis swatted him without looking. It was an old married-couple gesture except they weren't married, they were rivals-turned-colleagues over three hundred years of parliament sessions, and the swat was affection, and everyone knew it, and neither of them would ever admit it.

"Again," Maelis said to me. "Gently."

So I tried again.

Here is what nobody tells you about Veylmar in spring: the country remembers how.

That sounds like nothing when you say it plainly, but stand in it and you'll understand. This was a land that died mid-collapse — mid-*scream*, some days, if you listened to the echoes wrong — frozen under a long winter that lasted years, sealed behind a Door that only opened for people willing to carry names. And then Beatrix stood on the threshold and spoke the ledger aloud until her voice cracked and bled, every erased name in Veylmar ending with her own, and the winter broke like a fever breaking, all at once, in the middle of the night, so that we woke to water running off the eaves of ruined houses and the sound — I will never forget the sound — of ice groaning loose from the mountains like an old man getting out of a chair.

And underneath all that death and all that cold, the country had been holding its breath, holding its seeds, holding everything green and waiting. The moment the winter lifted, Veylmar remembered itself. Grass came first, reckless and everywhere. Then moss on every stone, softening the ruins like the country pulling a blanket over its dead. Then the trees, which had been standing frozen for years with buds locked shut, let go all at once, so the whole valley turned gold-green in the space of a week while the echo-citizens walked their loops through the wreckage and sometimes, just sometimes, paused and looked up like they'd noticed the light had changed.

The parliament said this was unprecedented. The parliament said a lot of things were unprecedented. Being apprenticed to a body of immortal memory-wardens meant hearing the phrase "this has never happened before" roughly nine times a week, usually followed by Maelis looking at me like I was personally responsible.

Which, fine. Sometimes I was.

But the garden — the garden was mine.

* * *

It started with a seed pocket.

Ysolt's villagers had come north through the gate in the first spring, carrying everything they owned, and among the things Ysolt's granddaughter carried was a coat with a torn lining, and inside the torn lining were seeds. Barley. Peas. A handful of apple pips rattling in a twist of waxed cloth. Seeds saved by people who'd fled a country before it fell, kept warm against skin for a generation, waiting for someone to plant them somewhere worth planting.

"They're Veylmar seeds," the girl told me, pressing the cloth into my hands. She was maybe twelve and had the fierce practical stare of someone raised by Ysolt's village, which is to say raised by ledgers and stubbornness. "They should go home."

I didn't know what to say to that. So I did what I always did when words failed me, which was go find the biggest empty patch of ground I could and start digging.

The parliament watched me do it with the expression of elders watching a kitten attack a boot.

"You intend to grow food," Maelis said, "in the palace square of the dead city."

"It's not a dead city anymore. It's a *sleeping* city, you said so yourself, you said the difference matters."

"I said the difference matters regarding the ground's memory. I did not authorize agriculture."

"Well." I jabbed the spade into the soil. "Consider yourself informed."

Sorrel laughed out loud that time, an actual bark-and-huff, and got swatted again.

Here is the thing about planting a garden in a country that spent years remembering how to be alive: the ground *helps*. I don't mean magic, exactly, or not only magic. I mean that the soil of Veylmar had been holding its breath for so long that when you finally gave it something to do — gave it seeds, gave it water, gave it an eight-year-old (nine-year-old; I'd had a birthday in the bothy, between the fever and the pass, and Corvin had carved me a wooden wolf with his knife and pretended it was nothing) — the soil threw itself into the work like a drowning man thrown a rope.

The peas came up in four days. Maelis measured them against her paw and said, quietly, "This has never happened before," and I grinned at her until she cuffed my ear.

By midsummer we had rows. By late summer we had the first apples any orchard in Veylmar had grown since the fall, small and hard and so sharp-sweet they made your teeth ache, and I took the first one to Beatrix, because obviously I took the first one to Beatrix, and she held it in both hands for a long time before she ate it, and she cried a little, and she pretended she hadn't, and I pretended too, because that's family.

* * *

Now it was spring again — the second spring, or the first true one depending on who counted — and I was learning the parliament's work, and the work was this:

Names.

You'd think a parliament of great wolves would spend its days on grand things. Prophecies. Borders. The metaphysics of doors. Mostly they spent their days on names. Every echo in the city had one, and every name had to be spoken regularly, kept warm, kept *current*, because that was the whole lesson of the long winter: a name unspoken goes cold, and a name gone cold starts to fade, and a faded name leaves an echo walking blind through its last day forever with no one to witness it.

So the apprentices learned the rolls. There were four hundred and eleven names in the city proper. I knew three hundred and ninety by heart. I stumbled on the weaver's daughters — there were six of them and four shared a name — and Maelis made me recite that family every morning until I dreamed in it.

"And why?" she asked, for the ten-thousandth time. "Why does the parliament speak names, Pip?"

"Because nothing truly ends while someone remembers it aloud."

"Why else?"

I hesitated. This was the question with layers. "Because... remembering isn't keeping a list. It's keeping company."

Maelis's ears tipped forward. That was her version of applause. "Say more."

"A list is a stone with marks on it. Company is—" I looked down at the seed row, at the peas curling their way up their strings. "Company is sitting with somebody while they do the thing they always did, even if they can't see you. The echoes aren't ghosts. They're neighbors. You don't file your neighbors. You greet them."

Sorrel had stopped pretending to sleep. Both eyes open now, watching me with that look he got — the one from the bonefields, from the ridge, from the glacier road — the look that said he was seeing somebody else in me, somebody older than I was.

"When I was young," he said, "the wardens of Veylmar walked the streets at dusk and greeted every soul by name. Human and beast alike. It was considered the highest office in the country. Not king. Not general. *Greeter*." His tail moved slowly through the dust. "My pack called it the walking of the rounds. We did it alongside them. The humans spoke the names of people. We spoke the names of everything else — the horses, the hawks, the river fish, the bees in the palace walls."

"The bees," I said. "Somebody had to greet the bees."

"Somebody had to greet the bees," Sorrel agreed solemnly. "And now, it seems, that somebody is you."

"I have a lot on my plate."

"You are a child who talks to animals and plants gardens in ruins. Your plate is exactly as full as you built it."

I threw a clod of dirt at him. He caught it out of the air, which was showing off, and ate it, which was disgusting, and Maelis sighed at us both, which was tradition.

* * *

The afternoons belonged to Sorrel.

This wasn't official. Nothing about Sorrel was ever official. But somewhere in the last year it had become simply understood that when the sun crossed past the colonnade and lay warm on the big flat stone, the lessons ended and the old wolf went to his rock, and if I happened to be free, I sat with him, and if I happened to be busy, I became mysteriously free.

He never asked for company. That was the pride in him. But his ears tracked me across the square whenever I moved, and when I settled next to him he arranged himself with his chin heavy on my knee like he weighed nothing, like he wasn't a wolf who'd once brought down a stag on the ice road, and we watched the light move through the broken arches.

"Tell me a story," I said, one afternoon, because the sun was honey-warm and the peas needed nothing and the echoes were walking their loops below us, the baker and the lamplighter and the six weaver's daughters, all of them greeting each other in their endless final evening, and it was the kind of scene that wanted a story laid over it like a blanket.

"You've heard all my stories."

"Lie better ones."

He huffed. But he thought about it, head cocked, and then he said, "Did your Beatrix ever tell you how she found me? Truly found me, not the alley version."

"She says you found her."

"Mm. Everyone believes that version. It flatters the charm-seller." His eyes half-lidded, sun-drunk. "Twenty years I searched the living world for her. Do you understand what that is, for a wolf? Twenty winters is most of a life. My pack scattered, my mate died in the border hills, and I kept going because I had seen a girl in a forest talk a wounded hawk down out of a pine tree, and I knew — I *knew*, the way you know thunder before it sounds — that she was the one the stories promised. The warden-blood come back. And then I found her selling whisper-charms in a night market, hiding behind a stall like a candle behind a shutter, and she would not so much as look at me."

"Because she was scared."

"Because she was lonely," Sorrel said. "Scared is fast. Lonely is slow. Loneliness had been teaching her for twenty years that wanting things was how you got hurt. So she pretended not to hear the one voice that had crossed a kingdom to reach her." He shifted his chin on my knee. "Never let anyone teach you that lesson, small one. Whatever it costs, however long it takes — answer the voices that cross kingdoms for you."

I scratched behind his ear, where the silver was thickest. "Is this a story or a lecture?"

"It's an old wolf using story-shaped wrapping on a lecture. You knew that when you asked."

"I did," I admitted. "Keep going anyway."

So he did. He told me about the forests of Veylmar before the fall, the deep green ones, the ones with elk in them and rivers that ran loud with salmon, and how the wolf packs and the human wardens had divided the nights between them — the wolves taking moonrise to dawn, the wardens taking dusk to midnight, and the hours in overlap belonging to everyone, the whole country singing to itself in two languages. He told me about the fall, briefly, in the flattened voice he used for it, and I didn't push, because some stories are load-bearing and you don't lean on those.

And then, because the sun was low and golden and he was feeling honest, he said the thing I think he'd been carrying all year:

"When I am finished here — when the walking of the rounds passes fully to you, and my legs are done — I want to be buried in that garden of yours. Under the apple trees. Where the ground remembers things well."

The words went through me like cold water.

"Don't," I said.

"Pip—"

"*Don't.*" My voice cracked, traitor. "You're not finished. You're — you taught me the rolls. You still chase rabbits in your sleep. You're not allowed to schedule your dying like it's a parliament session, that's not — that's not how family works—"

"Small one." Gentle, now. The gentleness was worse. "Nothing truly ends while someone remembers it aloud. You recite it daily. Do you believe it or merely memorize it?"

I wiped my face with my sleeve, hard, the way Beatrix did. "I believe it."

"Then burying me under the apple trees is not an ending. It's a *planting*. Everything you grow will stand on me. Every child who eats that fruit will eat a summer I helped make." He licked my hand once, rough and brief. "Wolves do not fear the ground, Pip. We are the ones who teach it patience."

I couldn't answer. So I put my arms around his neck and buried my face in the ruff of him and stayed there while the sun went down and the lamplighter-echo lit lamps that weren't there, and Sorrel held very still, the way he did, the way he had on the ridge when I screamed the mountain awake — steady, steady, the whole world shaking and him steady — until I was done.

"All right," I said finally, muffled. "Under the apple trees. But not yet. Swear it. Not till the garden's given fruit five years. Five good harvests. Those are the terms."

Sorrel's chest shook under my cheek with silent laughter. "Bargaining with a dying wolf. You *are* your mother's apprentice."

"She's not my—" I started, and then stopped, and then didn't correct it, and Sorrel's tail thumped once against the stone, satisfied, having won whatever he'd won.

"Not yet," he swore. "Five harvests. On my honor and my hunger."

* * *

I ran home through the blue end of the day, because that was the other thing about Veylmar now — you could run anywhere and it was safe, mostly, and beautiful, always — past the market street where the echo-bakers sold bread to each other, past the fountain that ran again since the thaw, real water now, not memory-water, cold enough to hurt your teeth. The house Beatrix kept by the gate glowed orange at its windows. Corvin's silhouette crossed the glass, doing his evening rounds of the gate register, and I heard Beatrix laughing at something inside, and I burst through the door with the day all backed up in me and no idea which part to tell first.

"There's a traveler," Corvin said, before I got a word out.

He was standing at the window, and his voice had a particular flatness I'd learned to read. It was the voice he used when the huntsman in him woke up, the one he kept chained these days but never quite killed.

"Travelers come through all the time," I said. "Half of Ashvale's walked the pass since the Naming."

"Not like this one." He turned. In the firelight his scarred face was hard to read, but not impossible; I'd gotten good at him too. "She arrived alone. No column, no cart. Says she's come a long way to see the woman who ended the winter." A pause. "Pip — she's wearing a Warden's ring. Old style. And she asked for Beatrix by a name nobody's used in twenty years."

Behind me, out in the darkening square, Sorrel lifted his head from his stone and began, very softly, to growl.
