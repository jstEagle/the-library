## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The market town of Dunhollow smelled, on the last morning of the old world, of wet wool, frying onions, and rain that hadn't fallen yet but had been threatening since dawn. Noor Ashgrove moved through it the way she moved through every town: like smoke through a keyhole. Present enough to be useful, absent enough to be forgotten.

She'd learned early that being remembered was the same as being caught. A face that lodged in someone's mind was a debt waiting to be called in. So she kept her hood up against the drizzle, kept her stride unremarkable, kept her voice pitched low and pleasant when she needed to use it at all. In three years of passing through Dunhollow's market square, no one could have described her beyond *youngish, dark-haired, forgettable*. That was not an accident. That was craft.

The widow was easy to find because grief always sits in the same place: near the edge of a crowd, doing something repetitive.

This one stood at a stall selling candles, counting out coppers one by one with hands that trembled slightly, buying tallow lights for a man who couldn't burn them anymore. She was maybe fifty-five, gray-streaked hair pinned back with a bone comb missing two teeth, and her eyes had the particular shine of someone who had cried herself dry and now simply leaked. Her name, Noor would learn later, was Maren. But names came after. First came the taking.

Noor drifted closer along the candle stall, pretending to weigh a stub of beeswax between her fingers. Around them the market roared its ordinary morning—hawkers crying the price of eels, children darting between cart wheels, a fiddler tuning for coin—and beneath all of it ran the quiet current Noor had been trained into since childhood: the hum of other people's memories, thick as river water, everywhere and available if you knew how to dip your hand in.

Her mother had called it *the deep*, before her mother stopped calling anything anything. The brokers called it stock. Noor thought of it as weather. It moved over everyone; some people were soaked in it.

Maren was drenched.

Grief-memory was the most reliable currency in the trade—not because anyone wanted to *keep* it, but because it sold fast. There was always a buyer for sorrow they didn't have to live through: collectors who liked the taste of big feelings without the cost, brokers who distilled grief into essence and vials, the occasional noblewoman who paid well for a tragedy to weep over at parties so she wouldn't have to feel her own small ones. Noor didn't judge the buyers. Judging required standing still long enough to look at yourself.

She set down the candle. She let her hand hover near the widow's sleeve—close enough that their shadows touched, which was all the contact the art required—and she breathed out slowly, emptying herself the way she always did. This was the part apprentices got wrong. They thought stealing a memory was about reaching in. It wasn't. It was about making yourself hollow first, so the memory would fall toward you like water finding a lower place.

*Never keep what you steal,* rule one. *Never be remembered,* rule two. Rule three was hers alone, unwritten, held only in the blank space where other people kept their childhoods: *never take from someone what they cannot survive losing.*

She took a breath and reached.

The memory slid into her like cold water into a boot.

---

A kitchen. Late autumn light, thin and gold, coming sideways through a window fogged with steam. A man's hands—broad, scarred at the knuckles, a woodcutter's hands—peeling apples in one long unbroken spiral, because he'd once promised his daughter he could do it and now did it every year on principle. He is humming. Off-key. The woman watching him—younger then, whole then—is laughing at something he said, and the laugh is still in her mouth when—

When the tree comes down wrong. When the men carry him home on a hurdle made of branches. When the humming stops forever and the house fills with a silence so complete it has texture, like felt, like snow.

And the daughter—the daughter isn't there. She left in spring, over a quarrel neither of them can now remember the shape of, and the woman will spend every remaining day of her life unable to decide whether that makes the grief better or unbearable.

Noor surfaced from the memory standing perfectly still among the market crowd, tears running freely down her face, her body having grieved before her mind consented to. She wiped them away with the heel of her hand, annoyed. Grief-memory always did that. It used your own tear ducts whether you invited them or not.

But there was more. Beneath the apples, beneath the silence, tucked into the corner of the widow's remembering like a pressed flower in a ledger, was something else—a fragment, secondhand, half-eroded:

*A story told by the dead man himself, years ago, by firelight, to make his wife laugh:* his grandfather had sworn that as a boy he'd seen the gates of Caerlum open. Not the mountain—everyone knew the mountain, its bald gray crown above the Thornfell passes, its slopes too steep for anything but goats and older superstitions—but the *gates*. Doors of black iron banded with silver, set into the mountainside where no doors should be, opening onto a stair that went down instead of up, lit by a light that wasn't torchlight, wasn't lamplight, wasn't anything the surface world had words for. And coming up the stair, or so his grandfather swore on his deathbed, had come music. A lullaby, he'd said. Sung by no throat he could see. And he'd run home and never told a living soul until this one, and the woodcutter had laughed and said *your grandfather was full of elderwine, love*, and the widow had laughed too—

Noor stopped walking.

The market went on around her—eels, onions, the fiddler striking up a reel—but she stood in it like a stone in a stream, and inside her chest was an ache she had no name for and no ledger entry to file it under.

It wasn't longing exactly. Longing she knew; longing was wanting something you'd seen. This was stranger. It was the feeling of hearing your name spoken in a crowd and turning around and finding no one—except she hadn't heard her name. She'd heard a dead woodcutter's secondhand ghost-story about a door under a mountain, and her body had reacted as though something behind her ribs had *turned over*. As though some hollow thing in her had heard a sound it recognized from very far away.

Caerlum meant nothing to her. She checked, the way she checked everything, turning the word over in the blank rooms of her interior. Nothing. No memory of it, no broker's tale attached, no reason whatsoever for her hands to be trembling.

Professionalism, then. That was what she told herself, resuming her walk toward Sef's shop with the widow's grief packed tight and cold inside her. It was professionalism to notice that a memory contained something unusual. Unusual meant valuable. Valuable meant she should know why, because knowing why was how you avoided being cheated, and being cheated was the only sin the road punished reliably.

It was not, she told herself firmly, anything else.

* * *

Sef kept his shop behind the tannery, which was either a joke about the smell of his business or a practical choice about the smell of his neighbors; after thirty years in the trade, no one could tell anymore. The sign above the door read SEFARIN — APPRAISALS, FAIR & DISCREET, the *FAIR & DISCREET* added later in different paint, in Sef's own hand, in what Noor suspected had been a moment of optimism.

He was at his scale when she entered, weighing out a vial of somebody's first kiss, and he didn't look up. "Closed."

"Your door was open."

"My door is always open. That's not an invitation, it's a structural problem." He capped the vial, labeled it in his cramped clerk-hand, and finally raised his eyes. They were pale, watery blue, set in a face like a dried apple—sixty years of distilling other people's feelings had left his own worn smooth and faintly regretful. "Ashgrove." The name landed on her like a hand on her shoulder, and she suppressed the flinch. Only Sef used it. Only Sef had ever known it. That had seemed safe once. "What have you got?"

"Grief. Fresh. Full arc—joy before, silence after, a daughter-shaped wound in the middle." She recited it flatly, the way you'd list a horse's teeth. "High quality. I'm asking twelve crowns."

Sef grunted. "I'll give you six and passage money to wherever you're going, because I know you, and you're already halfway out of Dunhollow in your head."

"Ten."

"Eight, and I don't ask where."

"Nine, and I don't tell you even if you ask."

That almost smiled him. He held out his hand, palm up, the universal gesture of the trade, and she leaned forward and breathed the memory out of her, letting it pour from her mouth into his cupped hands as a faint silver mist that smelled, briefly, of apples and autumn light. Sef caught it, coaxed it into a waiting glass, stoppered it, and held it up to the lamp. His expression flickered—just once—as whatever was in the vial brushed against whatever was left of him.

"Good stock," he said quietly. "Whoever she was."

"A widow," Noor said. "Candles."

"Mm." He counted out nine crowns onto the counter, and then, instead of pushing them across, left his hand flat beside them. "You look tired, girl."

"I always look tired. It's cheaper than looking interesting."

"You look tired like something's *missing*, is what I mean." His watery eyes stayed on her a moment too long, and Noor felt rule two twitch at the base of her spine. "There's talk," Sef said, "in the trade. Brokers saying memories are going strange. Sold stock going cloudy in the bottle. One in Vell says a memory he bought off a shepherd ten years ago just—" he turned his hand over, helplessly— "*left*. Vial sealed, shelf undisturbed, and the thing's gone. Just glass and a smell of rain."

"Memories fade. You've told me that often enough when shorting my prices."

"Not like this. This isn't fading. Fading goes gray at the edges. This goes like—" he searched— "like something called it home."

The ache behind her ribs turned over again. Noor picked up her crowns, one by one, slow, so her hands had something to do that wasn't shaking. "Superstition," she said. "The trade runs on it. Good evening, Sef."

"It's morning."

"Then good luck to both of us." At the door she paused, because there was one more thing, and rule two said never ask questions that get you remembered—but rule three, her own private rule, had lately been feeling less like a rule and more like a splinter. "The mountain," she said, not turning around. "Caerlum. Do you deal in anything out of Thornfell? Anything from under it?"

The silence behind her lasted three heartbeats too long.

"No," Sef said.

Which was, Noor reflected as she stepped out into the darkening street, exactly how a man said *yes* when he'd decided you shouldn't know it.

Outside, the rain had finally arrived, fine and needling, and the afternoon light had gone the color of dirty pewter. Above the rooftops to the north, where the peaks of Thornfell should have been visible on a clear day, there was only cloud—or what she assumed was cloud. It sat wrong, somehow. Too low. Too smooth. Like a lid.

She pulled her hood up, counted her nine crowns, and began walking south out of Dunhollow while the light lasted.

Behind her, unnoticed by anyone—including, for another few hours, herself—the first ward-stone on the town's northern boundary gave a single soft crack, like ice on a pond, and went dark.
