## 2. Wards Like Broken Glass

Noor Ashgrove had a rule about moonrise: never be somewhere you couldn't leave in three breaths.

She broke it that night, because the smuggler's ridge above the Thornfell passes was the only high ground for twenty miles, and she wanted to see the border one last time before she put it behind her. Below her, the wards of Thornfell burned along the mountain spine like a seam of green fire, mile upon mile of them, old as the kingdom and twice as stubborn. Travelers said you could feel them in your teeth if you stood too close—a low hum, like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Noor had crossed under them a hundred times through cracks no ward-keeper ever found, and she had always felt it anyway, that faint vibration in her jaw, as if the land itself were muttering *not you, not you, not yet*.

Tonight, the muttering had stopped.

That was the first wrongness. She noticed it the way a thief notices silence in a house that should be snoring—the absence arriving before the understanding. She crouched among the wind-stunted pines at the ridge's edge, her pack already buckled, her boots laced tight, and waited.

The moon came up fat and white over the eastern crags. And at the moment its light touched the highest wardstone—she would remember this, later, with the terrible clarity of things witnessed once and never forgotten—the seam of green fire *shivered*.

Not flickered. Shivered. Like a horse shaking off flies. Like skin trying to crawl away from something underneath.

Then it broke.

There was no sound she could have described to anyone who hadn't heard it. It was glass, but deeper than glass; it was a bell, but wrong; it was the sound a bone makes when it goes, translated into light. The wards shattered from east to west in a wave, a running crack of green splinters that fell out of the air like burning snow, and each splinter winked out before it touched ground, so that the mountains stood suddenly naked under the moon—bare rock and bare dark where ten thousand years of protection had hung.

And then the stars went out.

All of them. At once. Not dimmed, not veiled by cloud—*out*, snuffed with the same clean finality as candles pinched between wet fingers. The sky over Thornfell became a smooth black nothing, starless and depthless, and the moon alone remained, hanging there pale and stunned, the last lit thing in creation.

Noor did not scream. Screaming was a luxury for people whose names weren't worth anything, and hers wasn't. But her hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain air, and her breath came out of her in small, unwilling pieces.

Because she knew what stars were. Every thief on the memory roads knew, though they pretended otherwise, the way children pretend not to know where meat comes from. Stars were the sky's memories—the oldest ones, the first stolen moments of the world, hoarded up there where no one could trade them. And someone had just robbed the whole sky at once.

Nobody steals like that, she thought. Nobody *buys* like that either. Which left only one kind of taking.

The kind done by right.

* * *

The refugees began coming within the hour.

They poured through the passes like water finding a breach—not fleeing the broken wards themselves, Noor realized, but fleeing what the breaking meant. They carried their children slung against their chests and their grandmothers on handcarts; they drove oxen that lowed in confusion at the starless dark; they carried lanterns they didn't need yet, because the moon still shone, because some part of them still believed the night would end the way nights always ended.

From her ridge, Noor counted the ways they moved. That was habit. You learned to read crowds the way you read locks: where the weak points were, where the valuables traveled, who watched and who wept. The main column funneled down the King's Road toward Dunhollow and the river towns. Smaller streams bled off into the shepherd trails. A few desperate knots were already striking out across open country, following nothing but terror, which is the worst compass in the world.

And every single one of them was using the same roads. The known roads. The mapped roads, the guarded roads, the roads that led past tolls and soldiers and the eyes of everyone who had ever been taught to look at faces.

It hit her then, standing in the ruined starlight with the cold working into her knuckles: none of these people could slip through unnoticed. None of them knew the hollow way under Harrow Fell, or the drover's cut behind the waterfall at Sennet Mill, or the forty-foot crawl through the old mine adit that surfaced inside a shrine nobody prayed at anymore. Those roads belonged to her. To people like her—to thieves and smugglers and the unremembered, the ones who had spent lifetimes learning the world's back doors precisely because the front doors were barred to them.

Which meant, she thought, and the thought went through her like a blade slipped between ribs: whoever had passed those broken wards tonight without being seen—whoever had gone *into* whatever was coming, or come *out* of it, quiet as a debt called due—that person had walked her kind of road.

Someone like me.

She hated the thought and could not put it down. It sat in her chest and turned over and over, heavy as a stolen coin. All her life she had built herself around a simple architecture: she took what others kept, and kept nothing anyone could take. Never be remembered. Never be seen. And now the whole border lay open like a picked lock, and the only people in the world who could move through that opening unseen were exactly the sort of person she had spent nineteen years being, and she could not decide whether the realization made her safer or marked her like a brand.

Below, the refugee column stalled. She heard the crying before she understood it—a thin, rising wail from the head of the King's Road—and saw the crowd compress around something, hundreds of bodies pressing inward the way bodies always pressed when there was hurt at the center.

Noor told herself to stay on the ridge. She told herself very firmly, in the flat professional voice she used for such instructions, that whatever was happening below was none of her business, that witnesses were liabilities, that compassion was a currency she had never carried and couldn't spend.

Then she was going down the scree on her hands and heels, moving through the dark like water finding its level, because apparently even a woman with no soul to speak of had one part that refused to obey her rules.

* * *

The ward-keeper lay behind an overturned cart, half-sheltered by a dead horse, and he was dying the way fires die—unevenly, in guttering pulses.

He was old, gray-bearded, wearing the faded green sash of the Thornfell wardens, and there was a wound under his ribs that his own pressed hands were failing to hold. Around him the refugees surged and scattered; a healer-woman knelt briefly, shook her head, moved on to someone she could save. The crowd's attention slid off him the way attention slides off dying men, and that was why Noor reached him first—or perhaps, she admitted in the small honest room at the back of her mind, because some instinct older than her rules told her this one mattered.

She knelt. "Water?"

His eyes found her. They were pale eyes, washed-out blue, and they focused on her face with an effort that seemed to cost him more than holding his wound closed. His lips moved. She leaned close, because the roar of the crowd was enormous, because the sky overhead was black and wrong, because she was a fool.

"You weren't there," he whispered. "At the breaking. I'd have seen—I know every face that crossed—" A cough took him and brought red with it. "But the wards knew you. When you crossed. All those times. They *knew*."

Cold walked up Noor's spine. "You're raving, old man."

"The wards sing," he said. "We keepers learn the song. Every soul that passes makes a note." His hand came off his wound—blood-slicked, trembling, unstoppable—and closed around her wrist with a grip that had no business being that strong. "Yours made no note. Nineteen years of crossings and yours made *no note*, do you understand? A hole in the song. A place where the music goes around something." His eyes were wide now, fever-bright, and full of something worse than pain. Recognition. "I reported it once. Years ago. The archivist laughed. Said the girl was probably just—"

"Let go of me."

"Ashgrove," he whispered.

The word landed in her like a stone dropped down a well. Her mother's surname. The name she had buried under three false identities and a decade of careful blankness. She had never spoken it aloud in eighteen years; she had never let anyone speak it near her.

"How do you—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "How do you know that name?"

"Because it isn't just a name, girl." He pulled her closer, close enough that she could smell the blood and the green sash's old wool and the strange burnt-honey scent that clung to him—the wards' death still leaking out of his skin. "Ashgrove of Caerlum. That's what your line was called. Before. Ward-keepers' oldest records, older than my office, older than the kingdom—they say the family that tended Caerlum's gates sold something to the court beneath the mountain. Something precious. Something that made a *hole* in the song." He was smiling now, terribly, as if the joke had finally explained itself to him at the end. "I thought it was legend. Then the wards broke tonight, all at once, from the inside, called home like debts—and I looked at the black sky and I thought: *of course*. Of course it's her. Of course she finally came back for what she was owed."

Noor's heart was slamming. "I don't owe anyone anything. That's the whole point of me. I don't *keep* anything long enough to owe—"

"They're not collecting what you kept." His grip was loosening now, sliding, but his eyes held hers with the last of his light. "They're collecting what was *taken from you*. Go look in a mirror sometime, Ashgrove. Really look. There's a space behind your eyes where a person should be, and I've watched you walk past me for years wondering why I couldn't stop looking for it."

The sounds of the crowd went distant. The moonless, starless dark pressed down like a lid. Noor knelt in spilled blood and lamp oil and trampled mud, and felt the ground of her entire self—the careful blankness, the professional emptiness she had always told herself was discipline—shift beneath her like scree starting to move.

"What are you saying?" she asked, and her voice sounded young and far away. "What was taken?"

But the ward-keeper's eyes had gone past her, up, to the empty sky. His mouth opened. What came out was barely breath:

"*Caerlum.*"

His hand slipped from her wrist. The light in him guttered, pulsed, and went out as cleanly as the stars had—as if everything in the world that night answered to the same snuffing.

Noor stayed kneeling a moment longer. Then she took his sash—because it was wool, and wool was warm, and sentiment was a currency she didn't carry—and she rose, and she ran.

Not down the King's Road with the refugees. Not toward Dunhollow and safety and the life she had built out of other people's leavings.

Back. Toward the ridge, toward the high passes, toward the drowned mines and forgotten gates of the mountain that had just eaten the sky.

Because there was only one person alive who could tell her what had been taken from her before she was born, and Noor Ashgrove had spent nineteen years learning exactly how to find the people who kept what wasn't theirs.

Sef. The broker. The man who bought her childhood one memory at a time.

She was going to make him show her the ledger.
