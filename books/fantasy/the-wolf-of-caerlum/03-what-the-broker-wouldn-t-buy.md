## 3. What the Broker Wouldn't Buy

Sef kept his shop in the crook of a street that had no name and wanted none, down where Dunhollow's cobbles gave way to packed earth and the eaves of the houses leaned so close they nearly kissed. Noor had known him for eleven years. She knew the way his shutters hung—one loose, one true—and she knew that if you knocked three times, paused, then twice more, he would open before you'd finished the second pause. She had never once come to his door without something to sell.

Tonight her hands were empty, and it felt like arriving naked to a funeral.

The stars were still gone. Three nights now, and people had stopped saying *surely they'll come back* and started saying nothing at all, which was worse. The dark over Dunhollow was not the ordinary dark of clouds; it was a dark with texture, a dark like felt pressed against the world, and Noor had begun to feel it pressing on her especially, as though it knew where she lived.

Three knocks. A pause. Two more.

The shutter slit opened, and one of Sef's eyes appeared there, small and wet and quick. "We're closed."

"Open the door, Sef."

"We're *closed*," he said again, but his voice did the thing it always did when he lied—climbed half a note at the end, like a question he didn't want answered. "There's a curfew. There's no moon. Go home, girl."

"I don't have a home." She leaned close to the slit. Her breath fogged the wood. "You know that better than anyone. You built me out of what other people threw away."

A long silence from inside. Then bolts—three of them, drawn back in the order she could have named blindfolded—and the door swung inward onto the smell of the shop: tallow smoke, dried lavender, and underneath it all that faint cold scent like air off a cellar lake, the smell of stored memories.

Noor stepped past him without waiting to be asked. That was their old choreography too.

* * *

The shop had changed. She noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth in an old friend's smile. Where once the shelves had run floor to ceiling with jars and vials and little leaded boxes, each labeled in Sef's cramped hand—*grief, first frost, a name said wrong at a wedding*—now whole rows stood bare, dusted with the pale outlines of things removed. And the things that remained had been pushed to the back walls, crowded together like sheep before wolves.

"You've been selling everything." She turned in the middle of the room. "Since the stars went."

"Everyone's buying." Sef shut the door and shot the bolts again, all three. He was older than she remembered letting herself see—the last two years had salted his beard white and stooped him, and his hands, which had once been steady enough to decant a memory through a needle's eye, now trembled unless he pressed them flat to something. "People are frightened. Frightened people want to feel something that isn't fear. Or they want to forget the fear entirely. Either way—" he spread those trembling hands, "—business has never been better. Isn't that the joke of the age?"

"It's not why I came."

"No," he said quietly. "I don't suppose it is."

She made herself look at him. Made herself say it plainly, because circling would give him time to build walls, and Sef could build walls faster than any mason in Thornfell.

"The widow's memory I sold you last week. Before the wards broke. In it, there's a glimpse of Caerlum—a gate, a mountain, a road going under. She saw it forty years ago on a pilgrimage." Noor's voice held. She'd have been proud of that, later, if later had allowed pride. "When I carried that memory to you, Sef, I *ached*. Not like wanting coin. Like a bone remembering a break. And since the night the stars went out, it hasn't stopped. So I want my childhood records. Every trade. Everything you ever bought from me or about me. All of it."

Something crossed Sef's face that she had never seen there before, and it took her a moment to recognize it because it had no business being there.

It was pity.

"Noor—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of her like a whip. "Don't do that. Don't look at me like I'm someone you feel sorry for. I'm your best supplier in eleven years. I'm the reason you ate through two bad winters. Give me the ledgers."

He stood very still. The tallow candle between them guttered and rightened, and its light moved across his face like water. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped into a register she'd only heard him use once before—the day he'd told her a buyer had died holding her stolen memory, drowned in someone else's grief.

"There are records," he said, "and there are *records*. Which ones you're asking for decides whether this conversation happens or doesn't."

"All of them, Sef. I'm not leaving until I've read every page."

"That," he said, "is what I was afraid of."

* * *

He went behind the counter, into the back room she'd never been permitted to enter—not once in eleven years, though she had stolen her way through stronger doors than his—and returned carrying not a ledger but a strongbox, iron-banded, old enough that its lock had rusted into a suggestion rather than a mechanism. He set it on the counter between them. He did not open it.

"Before you look," he said, "you'll hear me out. Because after you look, you won't listen to anything I say ever again, and I'd like one last conversation with you while you still can hear me."

"Sef—"

"*Hear me out.*" It wasn't anger in his voice. It was something rawer. Fear, maybe, wearing anger's coat.

Noor folded her arms and waited. Outside, somewhere above the nameless street, the starless dark pressed down on the roof tiles like a hand learning the shape of the house.

"You came to me first when you were nine years old," Sef began. "Do you remember?"

"I remember everything about that day except how I got there." She frowned. "That's strange. I've never thought about that before. I remember the smell of this room. I remember you weighing a memory on your little brass scale. I don't remember walking here. I don't remember deciding to come."

"Yes," Sef said softly. "I've wondered about that for eleven years myself. Nine-year-olds don't find memory-brokers, Noor. Memory-brokers find nine-year-olds—or something sends them. And when you walked through my door with that first trade already in your pocket, already wrapped proper, already *priced*, I should have asked who taught you. Instead I paid you four coppers and asked no questions, because you were the finest natural talent I'd ever seen, and because—" his mouth twisted, "—because I told myself a child selling memories must be fleeing something worse. People like us are very good at telling ourselves stories that let us keep what we want."

"What was the memory?" Noor asked. "My first one. What did I sell you?"

And there it was again, that terrible gentleness. "That's in the box."

He opened it.

Inside lay papers—dozens of them, decades deep, trade-slips and tally-sheets and letters, all in his own cramped hand except for a few in a hand she didn't know, a fine slanted script that nonetheless made the back of her neck prickle with recognition she couldn't place. On top sat a ledger bound in grey leather, its spine cracked, its pages soft as cloth with handling. Sef opened it to a marker near the front and turned it toward her without meeting her eyes.

The entry was dated the week she'd arrived, twenty-two years gone. But it wasn't her handwriting, and it wasn't Sef's.

*One (1) memory, first theft of subject, age 4 yrs. Subject stole moment from mother: mother singing, evening, kitchen. Moment intact, sealed. NOTE — DO NOT RESELL. This item is the seal. Hold it. Hold it until called for.*

Below that, in different ink, added years later:

*Called for. See contract.*

Noor read it three times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into sense. "What seal? What contract? Whose handwriting—"

"Read further back," Sef said. "It goes back further than you. That's what I've been trying to tell you. It goes back to before you were born."

She turned the pages. The grey ledger went back and back, and her name ran through it like a thread through cloth—but not only her name. *Ashgrove.* Over and over, in entries decades old: *Ashgrove, payment received. Ashgrove, balance outstanding. Ashgrove, the debt stands.* And then, near the beginning of the book, a page darker than the rest, written in ink that seemed almost brown, in that same fine slanted hand:

*Contract of sale, executed under Caerlum, witnessed by the Quiet Hours. Item: one soul, unborn, female line of the house of Ashgrove. Sold by the mother, freely and in full knowledge, in exchange for the release of the father from Undercourt justice. To be delivered upon the child's majority, or upon the calling of the debt, whichever comes first. The seal of this contract is the child's first theft, held in trust beneath the mountain.*

Noor's hands stopped working. They simply stopped, mid-page, as if the order to move had been sent somewhere else.

"She didn't sell my memories," Noor heard herself say. Her voice came from far away, from the bottom of a well. "She sold my *soul*. Before I was even born. And I've been walking around without one for twenty-two years, and everyone could probably *tell*—"

"No." Sef's hand came down over hers, pinning the page flat. His fingers were ice. "Listen to me. Listen. A soul isn't the part of you that feels—I don't know what it is, maybe it's the part that *belongs*, the thread that ties you to a place and a people and lets you be *seen*. You've spent your whole life invisible, Noor. Unrememberable. You told me once it was a talent. It was never a talent. It was the *shape of the hole*."

The ground of her identity did not crack. Cracking implies something sudden, a single clean fracture. This was slower and more total: the slow realization that the floor had never been stone at all, that it had been water all along, that she had been standing on it for twenty-two years and calling it standing.

Her rules. Her beautiful, careful rules. *Never keep what you steal. Never be remembered.* She had thought she chose them. She understood, in that moment, with a clarity that felt like drowning, that she had never chosen anything—that the hole in her had chosen, and she had merely decorated it and called it a philosophy.

"The stars," she whispered. "The wards breaking. It's the debt being called, isn't it. Caerlum is calling in the debt."

Sef didn't answer. He didn't have to. His silence had the weight of a confession twenty years overdue.

* * *

Sef watched her go, and hated himself with a thoroughness that felt almost restful, the way a man might rest after setting down something very heavy.

He had known for eleven years. That was the fact of it, the plain ugly fact, and no amount of good intentions could sand its edges smooth. He had known from the first week—the grey ledger had come into his hands along with the shop itself, inherited from the broker before him, a dead man's obligations—and he had known exactly what the girl who walked through his door at nine years old was, and he had traded with her anyway. Bought her stolen years at fair prices. Watched her grow lean and watchful and alone and told himself he was keeping her safe by keeping her ignorant. Told himself that if Caerlum ever called the debt, he'd warn her.

And then the stars had gone out, and he had sat in his shop for three nights doing arithmetic with his courage, and discovered it came up short.

She had taken only one thing when she fled—the torn page from the contract, wrenched free with hands that shook so badly the paper had ripped jagged. He let her take it. He could have stopped her; the door was bolted and he was between her and it. But some debts you can't pay, and some you can only carry, and Sef had decided, watching her vanish into the starless dark with a piece of her own damnation crumpled in her fist, that he was done adding to his pile.

He looked at the empty doorway a long time.

Then he took down the grey ledger, blew out the candle, and began, in the dark, to pack. There was a page missing from the middle of the book—the first theft, the seal, the song—and he knew precisely where it was kept, and he knew precisely who would need it, and he knew that a man who had failed one test of courage could fail a second, or he could finally, at fifty-eight years old, with trembling hands and a bad heart, walk toward the mountain everyone else was fleeing.

Outside, the dark waited. Somewhere far north, under a crown of vanished stars, the gates of Caerlum stood open for the first time in living memory.

Sef put on his coat.
