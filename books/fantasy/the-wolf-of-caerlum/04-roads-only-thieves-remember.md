## 4. Roads Only Thieves Remember

The road out of Dunhollow was a river of the frightened, and Noor meant to swim against it alone.

She had her pack, her passage money still folded where Sef had pressed it into her hand along with that torn ledger page, and a head full of hidden ways through the Thornfell hills—ways she had learned by stealing them from smugglers and poachers and one memorable goat-thief who had known every sheep-track between here and the coast. She owed none of these people anything. That was the rule. Never keep what you steal; never be remembered; never, under any circumstance, let yourself be needed.

She was three hundred yards past the last burning farmstead when the widow found her.

"You're going up," Maren Ashwell said. Not a question. The woman had followed her out of the market square with a shawl over her hair and her dead husband's grief-memory sitting in Noor's skull like a stone swallowed whole. "The passes. You looked at them like you knew them."

"I looked at them like I wanted coin," Noor said. "That's different."

"There is no coin left in Dunhollow." Maren said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. Behind her, at the edge of the torchlight, stood perhaps a dozen others—a miller with flour still ghosting his sleeves, two children clutching a grandmother's skirts, a boy of maybe fifteen with a broken arm bound in a sling made from a curtain. They had heard. Of course they had heard. Frightened people hear everything.

Noor looked past all of them at the sky, because looking at the sky was easier than looking at their faces.

It was still wrong. It had been wrong for two nights now. Where stars should have wheeled above the ridge of Thornfell there was only a smooth black, a ceiling of unbroken dark, as though someone had drawn a cloth over the world. The refugees spoke of it in whispers, as if the darkness might be listening—as if it might be embarrassed to be caught being so empty.

"The soldiers say the wards will be rebuilt," the miller offered. "They say—"

"The soldiers are dead," Noor said flatly. "All of them on the border. I watched the ward-keepers die when the glass went." She hadn't meant to say it aloud. But the memory of the dying man's grip on her wrist was still fresh—*Ashgrove of Caerlum*—and terror makes people honest, or cruel, or both.

Silence. A child began to cry and was hushed.

Maren stepped closer. Up close, Noor could see the woman's eyes were red-rimmed and steady at once, the way eyes get when they've already lost the worst thing and are simply waiting to see what comes next. "I don't know your name," Maren said. "You took my grief two days ago and I never asked what to call you."

"Noor." It came out before she could stop it. She hadn't given her name to anyone in six years.

"Noor." Maren tested it like a coin bitten for gold. "There's a path over the hills that avoids the main pass. My husband used to run wool over it in the famine years. He called it the Tinker's Stair. If we take the main pass, we'll be trampled or robbed or worse by morning."

"I know the Tinker's Stair," Noor heard herself say. "It floods below the second switchback this time of year. There's a better way—a drover's cut through the gorse, then an old charcoal-burner's track. Smugglers' roads. They don't appear on any map because maps get confiscated." She stopped. Closed her eyes briefly. *What are you doing.*

"Will you lead us?" Maren asked simply.

Every instinct Noor owned screamed the same word: *no*. Be a ghost. Slip away up the mountain alone, find whatever waited for her at Caerlum alone, die if it came to that alone. Being needed was how people got remembered, and being remembered was death for someone like her.

But the dying ward-keeper had already spoken her name aloud to the night, and the night had taken the stars down like shop shutters closing, and somewhere beneath the mountain a debt was coming due with her name on it. Ghosts, she thought bitterly, were for people whose names weren't already written in someone's ledger.

"Dawn tomorrow," she said. "Gorse thicket behind the shrine. Anyone not there gets left." And she turned away before her face could give away how much she already regretted it.

* * *

The drover's cut was exactly as treacherous as she remembered, and she remembered it perfectly, because it wasn't hers.

That was the thing about stolen memories: they stayed sharp forever. Her own recollections were soft-edged things, watercolor impressions of a childhood she'd sold off in pieces before she was old enough to understand what she was selling. But other people's pasts—those she kept flawless. The goat-thief's knowledge of these hills sat in her mind with every stone and root intact, twenty years of a stranger's life rendered in perfect detail, and she navigated by it now, calling turns to the column behind her: *"Left at the split ash tree—the right path washes out. Mind the wire; poachers strung it years ago and never came back to pull it."*

She did not tell them the memories were borrowed. Let them think she was competent on her own account.

They climbed through the gorse as the starless dark deepened around them, and it deepened strangely—not like night falling but like night *concentrating*, thickening until the lantern-light seemed to bend. By the second hour even the moon had gone dim, a bruised coin behind gauze. The children stopped asking questions. The grandmother sang something low and tuneless to them, more rhythm than melody, and Noor caught herself listening despite everything.

At the charcoal-burner's clearing they rested, and it was there, sharing out Maren's bread, that Noor felt the first thread come loose.

It happened mid-chew. One moment she possessed, whole and gleaming, the memory of a fisherman's last morning with his son—she'd sold it three years ago in Cael Ford, a good price, the man had wept while handing it over—and the next moment there was a hole where it had been. Not forgotten. *Unmade.* She reached for it the way you reach for a step in the dark that isn't there anymore: the grey dawn on the harbor, the boy's laugh, the specific smell of tar and cold iron—all of it gone, leaving not emptiness but the *shape* of emptiness, a negative space printed on her mind like a fossil in stone.

She sat very still.

Another one went. A bride's memory of her wedding day, sold in Dunhollow itself last spring. Then a cooper's recollection of his father's hands teaching him to plane wood. Then another, and another, each dissolving not gradually but all at once, like frost giving way to sun, and with each one Noor felt a faint tug, directional, unmistakable—a current pulling east and down, toward the black bulk of Caerlum mountain on the horizon.

*As if they're being called home,* she thought, and the thought arrived with its twin, colder: *as if everything I ever sold belongs to something else.*

"What is it?" Maren was watching her. Damn the woman's eyes. "You've gone white."

"I'm fine."

"You're holding the bread like it bit you."

Noor set it down. Around the dying fire, the refugees slept in heaps, trusting, wretched. She should have been asleep too. Instead she sat cataloguing the losses like a merchant counting a looted till, and what frightened her most was not the losing but the *relief* threading underneath it. Each memory that dissolved was a debt cancelled, a weight lifted. For six years she had carried dozens of lives in her skull, rented rooms crowded with other people's joys and terrors, because keeping them was the only way to prove to herself that anything mattered enough to be remembered. Now the rooms were emptying, and underneath all that borrowed furniture she could feel the original floor of herself: smooth, bare, and hollow as a struck bell.

Hollow where something should sit. Hollow like a soul-shaped absence.

"Don't follow me when we reach the high valley," she said abruptly.

Maren blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"When this is done—if it's done—you take these people and go wherever people go, and you forget my face. That was the arrangement. Guide, not companion."

"Was it?" Maren pulled her shawl tighter. In the lantern light her face was all planes and patience. "Because the arrangement I understood was: a woman who knows the roads is walking us out of a country where the stars have been switched off, and asking nothing, which frightens me far more than if she'd asked for coin."

"It shouldn't. Asking is how it starts. First coin, then favors, then—" Noor stopped herself. *Then they remember you. Then they need you. Then you have something to lose.*

"Then what?" Maren asked softly.

"Nothing." Noor lay back and stared at the blank sky, arms crossed like a woman guarding a door. "Sleep. We move at first light."

Above them, where the Milky Way should have spilled its river, the darkness pulsed once—faint, slow, patient. Like breathing. Like something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the mountain, dreaming of debts.

* * *

They gained the high valley on the third day, and that was where Noor stopped pretending she didn't care.

It happened small, the way these things do. The boy with the broken arm—Tam, he was called—stumbled on the scree above the old drovers' ford, and Noor was simply *there*, hand fisted in his collar before she'd decided to move, hauling him back from the drop with a strength that surprised them both. He stared up at her with dust on his face and gratitude so naked it was almost accusatory.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't mention it," she said, meaning it literally, meaning *never, to anyone, ever*—but her voice cracked on the second word, because at that exact moment another memory burned away inside her: a lighthouse-keeper's vision of his daughter's wedding, sold for enough to winter in Vell. Gone. And she realized she was gripping Tam's sleeve not to save him but because saving him was the only real thing her hands had done in days, maybe in years, and she didn't want to let go.

She let go. Of course she let go. But she walked beside him for the next mile, matching his pace, saying nothing, and when the column halted at dusk among the ruins of a shepherd's bothy, she found herself dividing the bread evenly instead of hoarding, and checking the children's boots for stones, and telling the grandmother—inexplicably, uselessly—that the singing was nice.

It was not nice. It was thin and wavering and half-remembered. But the old woman smiled as if Noor had crowned her, and something in Noor's chest ached in a place no stolen memory had ever reached.

*This is how it happens,* she thought, appalled. *This is how ghosts get caught. Not with nets. With kindness, doled out in bread and miles.*

* * *

On the fourth night, camped in the shadow of the last ridge before the high valley, Noor woke to silence and knew immediately something was missing.

Not a sound. A memory.

She ran the inventory in the dark, heart hammering: the fisherman—gone. The bride—gone. The cooper, the lighthouse-keeper, the goat-thief, the smugglers, the poachers—one after another, all of them, every purchased past she'd carried since girlhood, *gone*, their shapes fading even as she named them. Twenty years of other people's lives, unravelling thread by thread, streaming east and down toward Caerlum like rivers finding the sea. She was nearly empty now. Nearly just *herself*, whatever that amounted to: a woman with a torn ledger page in her pack, a name a dying man had given her, and a hollow where her soul should have been.

And one thing left. One memory that refused to dissolve, sitting stubborn and strange in the deepest room of her mind—a fragment she had never been able to sell because she had never understood it: a snatch of song, a woman's voice, warm and low, and the feeling—impossible, unbearable—of being *held*. It wasn't hers. It couldn't be hers; she'd stolen nothing like it. Yet it glowed in her like an ember refusing the wind, and tonight, for the first time, it was pulling too. Pulling harder than all the rest.

East. Down. Beneath the mountain.

Noor sat up in the freezing dark. Across the ashes, Maren slept with one arm around Tam. Beyond them the refugee column breathed in its ragged unison. Ahead, Caerlum's silhouette blotted out what little sky remained, and at its base, invisible to anyone but someone carrying a thief's map of forbidden roads, the sunken gate waited with its debt and its jars of stolen light.

Whatever was calling her memories home, it wanted the last one most of all.

Noor shouldered her pack and walked out of the camp without waking anyone—but at the edge of the firelight she stopped, looked back a long moment at the sleeping people she had sworn to abandon, and carved a charcoal arrow on the bothy wall pointing down the safe road, with the sign for *water ahead* beneath it.

Then, before tenderness could talk her into staying, she turned east into the starless dark, following the ember of a song she did not remember learning, toward the court beneath the mountain.
