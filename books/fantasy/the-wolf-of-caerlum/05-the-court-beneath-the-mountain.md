## 5. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The gate of Caerlum did not stand on the mountain. It stood beneath it, and it had been waiting for her.

Noor felt that truth in her teeth before she saw anything at all—a low hum through the stone, like a struck bell that had never quite finished ringing. She had climbed the last mile of the drowned road with the black water rising past her ankles, past her knees, the starless sky above so absolute that she could not tell where the night ended and the mountain began. The refugees she'd led this far had stopped at the tree line, refusing to go farther, and she had not argued. This part of the road was hers alone.

The water parted for her.

That was the first wrong thing. All the way down the smugglers' stair—steps cut into the living rock by hands older than any kingdom she'd robbed—the floodwater should have soaked her to the waist. Instead it drew back from her boots as though from a hot iron, pooling sullenly against the walls, leaving a dry path exactly the width of one person walking alone. As if the mountain knew whose debt it was collecting.

The gate itself was older than grief. It rose out of the flooded cavern in two leaves of black bronze gone green at the edges, taller than the pines on the ridge above Dunhollow, and carved across its whole face with figures she recognized without wanting to: thieves, singers, mothers, children—all of them with their hands open. Giving. Taking. The same gesture, if you looked long enough. Noor looked away first.

She pressed her palm to the metal, meaning only to test its cold.

The gate knew her.

It opened inward without a sound, and the water behind her sighed shut, and Noor Ashgrove—who had spent twenty-three years making sure no door ever opened for her, who had slept in hedgerows precisely because no innkeeper would remember her face—walked into Caerlum because it let her.

*Never be remembered,* said the voice in her head, her own voice, worn smooth by years of use. *Never be seen.*

But she had already been seen. The dying ward-keeper's grip still ached around her wrist like a brand. *Ashgrove of Caerlum.* And now the mountain itself had said her name back to her in the language of opening doors.

She went in.

* * *

The Undercourt was lit by what she had stolen from the sky.

Noor stopped three steps inside the great vaulted hall and forgot how to breathe. Every star that had vanished at moonrise on that terrible night hung here instead—not scattered across a ceiling but *arranged*, ranked along shelves that ran the length of the hall like jars in an apothecary's shop, each one sealed in glass the color of deep water, each one burning with a small, patient light. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The whole night sky, shelved and catalogued, and among them—

Noor's hand went to her chest without her permission.

Among them were other lights. Smaller. Dimmer. Lights that flickered rather than burned, that pulsed slow and arrhythmic, like something breathing in its sleep. Souls. She understood it the way you understand a word you've never read but have heard spoken all your life: those dim, fluttering lights in their jars of starlight were souls, kept here the way a broker keeps memories—in trade, in trust, or in theft.

"You are later than your mother was."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way voices do in large empty places, and then it came from somewhere after all: a woman seated on a throne of unlit candles at the far end of the hall. She was pale the way moonlight is pale, the way paper is pale—the Queen of Quiet Hours, though no one had needed to tell Noor her name either. Her gown moved when there was no wind. Her eyes were the gray of the moment just before dawn, when nothing is decided yet.

"I'm not late," Noor said, and was surprised to find her voice held. "I didn't know I was invited."

"Everyone born under a sold contract is invited." The Queen rose. The candles did not light. "They simply arrive at different speeds. Some crawl here in sixty years. You came in six weeks. That is fast, for your kind."

"For my kind." Noor descended the shallow steps into the hall proper, forcing her legs to carry her like a thief's legs, steady, unhurried, belonging anywhere. "And what kind is that?"

"The hollow kind." The Queen walked the length of the shelf nearest her, trailing one white finger along the jars, and where she touched them the stars brightened eagerly, like dogs recognizing their keeper. "You have carried it since the day you were born, little thief—that absence in the center of you. Don't you wonder why you steal? Why you cannot keep? Why every memory you take feels, for one hour, like being *full*?" She turned. "You have been trying to fill the space where your soul was sold. It has never once worked. But oh, how hard you have tried."

Noor's rules rose up around her like old walls: *never keep what you steal, never be remembered.* Rules built, she saw now with terrible clarity, by a child who had learned early that everything given to her would be taken away, so she had better take first. She hated the Queen for saying it out loud. She hated her mother more, wherever her mother was, wherever her mother *was*.

"The contract," Noor said. "Show me."

The Queen smiled, and it was not unkind, which was somehow worse. She lifted one jar from the shelf—not a soul, not a star, but something between—and from its light drew a sheet of vellum that unfolded and unfolded and unfolded, until it hung nearly to the floor.

The signature at the bottom was written in brown ink gone almost black with age. Noor knew that handwriting. She had spent her whole childhood resenting it on the flyleaves of books, on pawn tickets, on the one letter her mother had ever written her, all apologies and no explanations.

*Maren Ashgrove.*

Below it, in ink that still gleamed wet after twenty-three years, a second line waited blank. A line for another signature.

"The debt is due," the Queen said. "The father's crime was forgiven. The price was named. One daughter, one soul, payable upon the collapse of the border wards—which my court arranged, I confess, with some embarrassment at the crudeness of it. Stars must come home sometime. Souls keep better than stars. They don't burn down." She rolled the vellum closed. "You are here to pay, or to bargain. Those are the only two doors, Noor Ashgrove. Choose which one you came through."

"No," Noor said.

The Queen's eyebrows rose, very slightly, like ripples on still water.

"No," Noor said again, louder, and the sound of her own defiance rang strangely sweet in the vast dark hall. "I didn't crawl down a drowned smugglers' stair past a gate that opened for me like an old dog to be handed over like a ledger debt. I am a thief. I came to make a *trade*. That's what I do. It's the only thing I've ever done well."

"And what," said the Queen softly, "do you imagine you have, that a court of quiet hours could want?"

Noor reached into her coat and pulled out the torn ledger page—Sef's record of her earliest trades, the ones that had dissolved to ash at the threshold upstairs, the ones Caerlum had already called home. She let it fall. Then she spread her empty hands.

"Memories," she said. "I have taken hundreds. Sold most. But there are rare ones. A king's coronation, stolen off his own head. The last flight of a dragon over the Thornfell passes—I've carried it eleven years and never found a buyer rich enough. Griefs that would buy a house. Joys that would buy a kingdom." Her heart was slamming against her ribs and she made her voice stay flat, professional, the market voice, the broker's voice. "Name your price in memory. I'll pay it. And you give me back what's mine."

The Queen regarded her for a long moment. Around them, in their jars, the stars seemed to lean closer. Even the souls had gone still, listening.

"A rare memory," the Queen repeated. "For a soul." She began to walk, slowly, the hem of her impossible gown whispering over stone. "There is a memory so rare that it exists twice in all the world, Noor Ashgrove, and both copies belong to me. There is a seal on your contract—every contract has one, a thing of value held in trust until payment—and yours is a memory. Not any memory. *The* memory. The first thing you ever stole, before you knew stealing had a name. Your mother's voice, singing, on a night when you were four years old and the world was still soft."

The floor tilted under Noor. She had no memory of any such night. Of course she didn't. She had been four; she had sold everything sellable since; the earliest pages of Sef's ledger had been torn out or lost or—

Or kept. Held in trust. Sealed in a jar of starlight in a vault beneath a mountain.

"That memory is the lock on your contract," the Queen said. "Bring it to me freely given, and the second line takes your signature instead of hers, and your soul walks out of here in its jar, warm and blinking, back where it belongs. Refuse—" she smiled again, dawn-gray, patient as centuries, "—and the debt collects itself. Slowly. Over sixty years, perhaps. You will feel it as a chill. Most people call it growing old."

"And if I bring it," Noor said, "what happens to it?"

"It stays." The Queen said it gently, the way you tell a child the dog has died. "Given means given, little thief. It will hang on my shelf beside your mother's other payments, and it will be sung in this hall on quiet nights until the last of it fades—and you will leave knowing that you loved a song, but never again how it went. That is the price. That is the *only* price. There is no rarer memory in your possession, because there is no memory more yours."

Noor stood very still. Above her, ten thousand stars burned in their jars, and none of them were hers, and one small song was, and the price of her own soul was to give up the oldest piece of it forever.

Somewhere in the dark water behind her, faint as a thought, she thought she heard—impossibly, absurdly—the first notes of a lullaby, rising up the drowned stair like bubbles seeking air.

The Queen heard it too. Her gray eyes widened, just slightly, for the first time.

"Well," said the Queen of Quiet Hours. "It seems someone else remembers your song, Noor Ashgrove. I wonder how."
