## 6. A Debt Written in Milk Teeth

The Queen of Quiet Hours did not ask permission. She simply opened her hand, and Noor felt the vault take her.

It was not like falling. It was like being *remembered*—pulled backward through years the way a thread is drawn through cloth, gathering everything it touches. The jars of starlight receded into a soft blue distance. The pale Queen's face, so close a moment ago, thinned to a smear of white like frost on glass.

And then Noor was somewhere else entirely, and someone else was breathing with her lungs.

---

The market town of Dunhollow existed twenty-two years before Noor knew it, but in this memory it was raining, and the rain smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, and a young woman with Noor's own dark hair stood beneath the eaves of a chandler's shop with her hand pressed flat against her belly.

Her name, when the memory offered it up like a coin from a stranger's palm, was Wren Ashgrove.

She was twenty-three years old. She was four months gone with child. And she was waiting for a man who had promised to meet her at the chandler's door at the striking of the evening bell, and who had not come.

Noor knew him. That was the cruelty of it—the memory gave his face to her freely, a lean face with a crooked smile, a face she had spent her whole life refusing to wonder about because wondering was a door that led nowhere. Her father. Standing in the rain of a night two decades past, in her mother's memory, wearing the look of a man about to do something unforgivable.

He came around the corner at last—not alone. Two figures walked beside him, gray-cloaked, their hoods drawn low, and where their faces should have been there was only a smooth blankness like river stones. Undercourt wardens. Noor had seen their like only once before, in Sef's stories, told in a whisper after too much drink: *the ones who collect what's owed.*

Wren pushed off from the wall. "Tam." His name cracked in half when she said it.

"Don't." He wouldn't look at her. Rain ran off the brim of his hat between them like a curtain he was grateful for. "Don't say anything yet. Let me get through it."

"Get through *what?*" But she already knew; Noor could feel her knowing, could feel it the way you feel a stair missing in the dark. "What did you do?"

"I borrowed," Tam said. "From the wrong lender."

"Borrowed *what?*" Wren's voice climbed. A passerby glanced over and hurried on. Even then, even in Dunhollow, people knew better than to hear too much. "Tam. We have nothing. What could you possibly have—"

"A future," he said. "I borrowed a future. Mine. I sold three years of my own life to pay a gambling debt, and when three wasn't enough I sold five more, and the broker I went to—" His laugh was terrible, hollowed out. "The broker I went to was fronting for the Undercourt. They don't deal in days, Wren. Not really. They deal in *names*. And mine has come due."

The bell above the chandler's door began to strike. Neither of them moved.

"What does that mean?" Wren whispered. "Come due. What happens when it comes due?"

Tam finally looked at her, and Noor looked back through her mother's eyes and saw her father clearly for the first time: a man drowning and ashamed of the splash. "They'll take me down under the mountain," he said. "And they don't give back what they take down under. You've heard the stories. Whole lives, Wren. They keep whole lives in jars down there, and the person walks around afterward with nothing inside—smiling at walls, forgetting their own children's faces. I'd rather be dead."

"Then run." Wren grabbed his sleeve. Rain soaked through her bodice; she didn't seem to notice. She was pregnant and desperate and twenty-three, and Noor stood inside her skin feeling every ounce of it—the fear like cold iron in the gut, the love that made the fear unbearable, the fierce animal arithmetic of a woman calculating what she would burn to keep a child warm. "Run north, run over the passes, I don't care. I'll follow. The baby and I will follow. We'll—"

"They have my name written in the contract, love." He touched her cheek, once, gently, the way you touch something you're memorizing. "They don't chase bodies. They chase names. I can run to the edge of the map and my name will still be theirs, and they will come for me on the appointed hour no matter how far I've gone, and they'll take me in front of you, in front of the baby, and make you watch." His voice broke clean through. "Unless the name stops being mine."

Wren went very still.

Noor, riding inside her mother, felt the thought arrive before the words did—felt it form like ice spreading across a pond, one terrible crystal at a time.

"The contracts," Wren said slowly. "They can be transferred."

"Wren. No."

"*Can't they.*"

"Not to an unborn child. The soul isn't seated yet—it's not *formed*—they won't—" He stopped. Because they both knew, in the way of people who have just discovered a door they wish they'd never seen, that the Undercourt would do many things, and among them was almost certainly *this*.

"They'll hold the debt against a soul that hasn't landed yet," Wren said. Her hand moved on her belly. Noor felt the small flutter beneath it, felt her mother feel it, and understood with a lurch that she was feeling herself being decided over. "And you walk free. And by the time it comes due, we'll be long gone, or we'll find another way, or—or it won't matter, because you'll be alive, Tam, you'll be *alive*, and that's worth—"

"It is not worth that." He said it flatly, absolutely, a man laying a stone across a road. "I know what I did. I'll carry it. You don't get to carry it for me, and you especially don't get to—" his eyes dropped to her belly, and something in his face crumpled and reformed as fury, "—you don't get to spend *her*."

"Her," Wren repeated, and smiled, and it was the saddest thing Noor had ever seen. "You said her."

"That's not the point—"

"You said *her*." Wren laughed, wet and wild, and pulled him close, and pressed her forehead to his chest, and Noor felt her mother's heart hammering against her father's ribs like something trying to escape a locked room. "Then listen to me, Tam Ashgrove. Listen. I am not spending her. I'm *lending* her. There's a difference. Debts can be paid back. Contracts can be broken. Souls can be reclaimed—I've asked, I've spent every coin I have asking, in every tea shop and hedge-chapel in this valley, and they all say the same thing: a debt held by the Quiet Hours can be ransomed, if the ransom is rare enough. If the thief is clever enough." She lifted her head. "So she'll be a thief, then. Our girl. Clever enough to steal back her own soul. And until then, you live. That's the trade. That's the whole trade. Say yes."

"Noor felt her father's silence stretch out, long as the rain. Felt him understand that his wife had already done the arithmetic and would never un-do it. Felt the exact moment he surrendered—not with words, but with his arms coming up around Wren's shoulders, holding her and the unseen child between them, and saying, into her hair, in a voice scraped raw:

"She'll hate us."

"She'll be alive," Wren answered. "Hate later. Live first."

* * *

Present-day Noor surfaced from the memory gasping, and found herself kneeling on the vault floor with tears running down her face that she had no memory of starting.

The Queen of Quiet Hours watched her from beside a shelf of glowing jars, hands folded, expressionless as the moon. Around them, the starlight souls pulsed softly in their glass, each one a captive sky.

"You showed me that," Noor said. Her voice came out wrecked. "Why show me that?"

"You entered my vaults asking what you were," the Queen said. "I dislike incomplete answers." She drifted closer, robes making no sound at all, which was somehow worse than sound. "Your mother came here herself, you know. Eight months along. I remember her feet were swollen and she would not sit down. She bargained like a fishwife and cried only once, and that was when I asked her for a token to seal the contract—a memory of the child, held in trust, to serve as the bond. Something precious enough to anchor the debt." The Queen tilted her head. "You were four years old when she brought me the seal. Do you know what she chose?"

Noor did not answer. Her throat had closed.

"She chose the moment you first stole from her," the Queen said. "A small thing. A song. You took it without meaning to—you were barely more than a baby yourself—and she felt it go, felt her own lullaby lift out of her mouth mid-verse and vanish into her daughter, and instead of being frightened she was *delighted*. 'There,' she told me. 'That one. If my daughter can steal that from me at four, she can steal anything. Even this.'" The Queen's pale lips curved, though whether in admiration or distaste, Noor could not tell. "Your mother believed in you, little Ashgrove. Cruelly, desperately, completely. She built your whole life on the wager that you would come here one day and rob me blind."

"And my father?" Noor managed. "Where is he?"

The Queen was silent a moment. Then she gestured, unhurried, toward a shelf near the vault's far wall—to a jar whose light burned a dull, exhausted red, flickering like a candle in a draft.

"Debts deferred are not debts forgiven," she said. "His name was struck from the contract when yours replaced it. But the Undercourt remembers everyone who ever owed us anything. He lived eleven more years. He drank. He left, eventually—he thought leaving would protect you both, and men often think that." Her voice softened by a degree so slight it might have been imagined. "What remains of him is here now. He arrived quietly, of old age, six winters past. I keep him because keeping is what I do. Whether you choose to visit is your affair."

Noor stared at the red jar. Twenty-two years of not wondering about her father, and here he was, a guttering light in a glass jar, an arm's length away, and she could not make herself move toward it. Grief for a man she'd never known tangled with grief for the woman she'd only resented—that hard, frightened, furious mother who had taught her to steal, to hide, to never keep what she took, to never be remembered. All of it, Noor saw now, all those rules drilled into her since before she could talk—they weren't the habits of a criminal. They were a *training regimen*. A survival plan written by a woman who knew exactly what her daughter would someday need to be: clever enough, ruthless enough, hungry enough to break into a court beneath a mountain and take back what was hers.

Every stolen memory. Every broker's coin. Every cold campsite and closed face. It hadn't been a life. It had been a *preparation*.

And her mother had died believing it would work.

"When the wards fell," Noor said slowly, "and the stars went out. That was you. Calling in the debt. My debt."

"Contracts mature," the Queen said simply. "Yours matured. Though I confess, little thief, you have proven... more interesting than most debtors." Her gaze slid to the satchel at Noor's hip, where the torn ledger page lay folded, and something ancient and amused moved behind her eyes. "Which brings us to terms. You wished to ransom your soul. I am prepared to name my price. But I warn you"—she turned, robes flowing, toward the deepest shelf of the vault, where one jar burned brighter than all the rest, its light a warm gold Noor recognized in her bones before she knew why—"my price is always the same thing. The thing the debtor treasures most."

Noor followed the Queen's gaze. And in the brightest jar in all the vaults of Caerlum, she saw a scrap of memory glowing gold as lamplight: a rainy kitchen, a young woman's voice rising in song, a small dark-haired girl listening in the doorway with her hands full of something stolen.

Her own first theft. The seal on the contract. The price.

"No," Noor whispered.

"Yes," said the Queen of Quiet Hours, gently, terribly. "Now let us discuss how badly you want your soul."
