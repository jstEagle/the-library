## 7. Everything I Ever Sold

The first buyer was a ferryman on the Graywash, a broad-shouldered man named Odo who had once paid three coppers for the memory of a wedding he'd never attended. Noor found him mending nets in the grey starless dawn, and when she said her name his hands went still.

"You're the one who sold it," he said. Not an accusation. Wonder, almost.

"I need it back."

He looked at her a long moment, then at the sky, where no stars had been for eleven days. "Everyone's needing things back now," he said. But he closed his eyes and pressed two fingers to his temple the way memory-brokers did, and drew it out — the wedding, the bells, the bride's yellow ribbon — and held it cupped in his palm like water.

It cost her every coin she had left. She didn't haggle. That alone should have frightened her; haggling was the oldest of her rules, the armor of a woman who never let anyone see what a thing was worth to her.

She carried the memory wrapped in oilcloth, warm against her ribs, and walked east toward Caerlum.

* * *

The mountain showed itself before anything else did. Even under the lightless sky it seemed to gather darkness around itself like a cloak, a blacker black on the horizon, crowned with nothing. The refugees on the road bent their steps away from it without seeming to know they were doing so, the way water bends around a stone.

Noor walked toward it.

By the second day she had bought back four memories: the wedding from Odo; a rainstorm over a barley field from a grain-wife in Tarrow Mill; a child's fevered night and its breaking morning from an old soldier who wept while he sold it and thanked her after; a market-day in Dunhollow, bright with apples, from a girl of maybe fifteen who asked no coin at all, only that Noor remember her name.

"I will," Noor said, and found she meant it, and found that meaning it felt like stepping off a ledge.

Five memories, wrapped and carried against her heart. She could feel them through the cloth, faint warmth, faint pulse — other people's yesterdays, bought home at last. It made no sense. She had spent her whole life believing that what you gave up stayed given up, that regret was just interest paid on a bad trade. And yet here she was, spending everything to gather them again, as though a soul could be rebuilt from salvage.

Perhaps, she thought, it could. Perhaps that was all a soul ever was.

* * *

Caerlum's threshold was not a gate. It was a place where the ground simply decided to belong to the mountain, a ring of standing stones half-swallowed by hillside, older than any road. The drowned mines opened somewhere below it, Sef's torn ledger said, and the Undercourt beneath. Noor stood at the ring at dusk — dusk being now indistinguishable from any other hour — and unwrapped the wedding memory with hands that would not quite steady.

She lifted it out. In her palm it glowed soft gold, the bells ringing silently inside it, the yellow ribbon turning like a fish.

She stepped across the stones.

The memory crumbled.

Not all at once — first the color drained, then the sound, then the shape of it, and then it was only a pinch of ash in her hand, grey and weightless, blowing away between her fingers. She stood there holding emptiness while the wind took the last of a wedding that had happened forty years ago to people whose names she had never known.

She tried the next one. Ash. The third. Ash. By the fourth she was kneeling, and by the fifth she had stopped feeling her knees against the cold stone, because there was nothing in her hands but grey, and grey, and grey.

"Calling them home," Sef had said, drunk and ashamed, in chapter three of this ruin. "Everything you ever sold, girl — it's not yours anymore. It never stopped belonging to whoever holds it. You just borrowed the keeping."

But these weren't sold anymore. She'd bought them back. Paid true coin, begged true bargains, carried them here herself. They were hers. They were supposed to be hers.

They crumbled anyway, and Noor understood, finally, with the particular cruelty of understanding that comes too late: it wasn't about ownership. Caerlum was showing her the arithmetic of her whole life. Everything taken turns to ash at the door of the deep places. Everything stolen, everything bought, everything wrenched loose from someone else's living — none of it survives crossing over. The Undercourt dealt only in what was given.

And her soul sat in a jar beneath the mountain, and she had come to take it back.

* * *

She was still kneeling among the five small heaps of ash when the widow found her.

Maren — Noor knew her name now, had known it since the smuggler roads, since the night on the ridge path when the column slept and Maren had shared her blanket without being asked — Maren came down the hillside with a lantern she had no business lighting, in a world where light meant raiders, and set it on the stone beside Noor like an offering at a shrine.

"You followed me." Noor's voice came out scraped raw.

"You walk loud when you're grieving," Maren said. "Louder than when you're stealing."

"I don't grieve."

"No." Maren looked at the ash heaps, at the mountain, at Noor's face. "Of course not."

She sat down on the cold grass without ceremony, the way she did everything, a widow of sixty years with a face like a folded map and hands that had buried a husband and raised three children and still, somehow, reached out to strangers on dark roads. From her coat she brought out a twist of cloth.

"I've been carrying this since Dunhollow," she said. "Since before, truthfully. Since the day I met you in that market and watched you lift my grief right out of my head like a splinter."

Noor flinched. "I needed passage money."

"I know. I've known since the second night, when you thought I was asleep." Maren turned the cloth twist over in her fingers. "You want to know what I've been carrying?"

"Maren—"

"My wedding day," the widow said. "My own, mind — not one you sold, not one you stole. Forty-one years ago. Tam with flour on his sleeve because he'd kissed me in the bakery first. The bells. Our mothers crying in the same pew and pretending it was the incense." Her voice caught and steadied, the way old grief does, worn smooth as river stone. "I kept it through his dying. Through the burying. It's the last thing I own that's entirely mine, and I am offering it to you freely, girl, because I think that's the only coin your mountain accepts."

"No." The word came out of Noor faster than thought. "I can't. I won't take that."

"You're not taking. I'm giving. There's a difference, and I think you've finally learned it, or you wouldn't be sitting in the ashes of five memories you bled to get back."

"It would turn to ash too—" Noor stopped. Because that wasn't true, and they both heard it. Given things crossed over. That was the whole terrible lesson of the threshold. If Maren gave it, it would survive. It would go into the vaults below, into the Queen of Quiet Hours' keeping, a bright jar among ten thousand stolen stars.

And Noor would owe it. Forever.

"Maren, I have spent my entire life making sure nobody ever gave me anything," Noor said, and heard how it sounded, and couldn't stop it. "Gifts are debts. Debts are hooks. Hooks are how they find you." She gestured at the mountain. "Look where debt got me. Sold before I was born. My mother signed me away and I've been running the arithmetic of that bargain my whole life without knowing it — every theft, every blank year, every rule. Never keep. Never be remembered. Because some part of me knew there was a hole where I should have been, and if I stood still long enough, something might notice."

Maren listened without interrupting, which was her gift, and when Noor finished she reached out and took her hand — the hand still grey with ash — and held it.

"And yet," Maren said, "you walked back through a burning pass for us. You carried the old man Harl on your shoulders for six miles of smuggler road. You learned every one of our names." She smiled, small and dry. "Thieves don't do that, Noor Ashgrove. Whatever's missing in you, whatever they keep in their jars — something else grew in its place. Something they can't hold."

Noor looked down at their joined hands. At the lantern light. At the ash.

"If I take your memory and it buys my soul," she said quietly, "then I'll be whole, and I'll owe a widow her wedding day until I die, and I'll deserve it."

"So don't take it," Maren said. "Ask for something else. Ask for help. Ask Sef, ask me, ask that ragtag column of fools following you down the mountain whether they'd rather lose their memories or lose you. Some things must be given, girl — but giving goes both ways, if you'll let it."

For a long moment Noor said nothing. The starless sky pressed down, vast and blind, and the mountain breathed its cold breath against her back, and somewhere beneath her feet, in jars of captured starlight, her soul waited in the dark like a lamp in a locked room.

Then she heard footsteps on the hillside above. More than one pair. Light, quick, familiar — and behind them, heavier, a shuffling gait she knew better than her own heartbeat, a gait that had haunted every broker's den from Dunhollow to the sea.

Sef came over the crest of the hill with a lantern in one hand and a ledger page in the other, and behind him, impossibly, absurdly, the refugee column strung out along the ridge like beads on a thread — Harl leaning on his stick, the girl from Tarrow Mill, a dozen more, all of them walking toward the mountain everyone else in the world was fleeing.

"The missing page," Sef called down, his voice cracking on the words. "Noor. Girl. I found what she stole first."
