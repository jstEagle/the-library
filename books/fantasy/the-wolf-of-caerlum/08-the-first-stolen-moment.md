## 8. The First Stolen Moment

Sef came to her in the hour before dawn, when the starless dark was thickest and the refugee camp on Caerlum's flank had gone quiet as held breath.

Noor heard him before she saw him—the particular drag of his left boot, the one with the loose heel she'd told him a hundred times to fix. She sat up in the lee of a fallen wall, her knife already in her hand, and there he was: smaller than memory made him, grayer, wrapped in a broker's oilcloth coat that had seen better decades. In his fist he clutched a scrap of paper like it was burning him.

"You're a fool for coming here," she said, low.

"And you're a fool for going down into that mountain," Sef said, "so I suppose we're matched." He crouched beside her, joints cracking, and held out the paper. "I lied to you. Not about your mother—not about the soul—but about the ledger. There was a page I kept back."

Noor took it without touching his hand. The vellum was soft with age, its ink faded to the brown of old tea, but the broker's cipher was Sef's own, unchanged since he'd taught it to her at nine years old so she could keep her own accounts. She read it twice. Her mouth went dry.

*First acquisition. Subject: N. Ashgrove, aged four years. Acquisition: one moment, maternal—song, hearthside, unrecorded title. Buyer: the Undercourt of Caerlum. Note: retained as seal upon contract. Do not trade. Do not speak of.*

"Her singing," Noor said. The word came out cracked. "My first theft was her *singing*?"

"You were four," Sef said quietly. "You didn't know what you were doing. Children that age don't understand that taking a moment takes it *away*. Your mother sang you to sleep one night, and you wanted to keep it, the way children want to keep fireflies. So you reached into her the way you'd later reach into strangers—and you took the whole song. Every note. And when she woke the next morning she couldn't remember ever having sung it at all, and neither could you, because you sold it to me within the year. To me, who sold it down the mountain." He rubbed his face with a hand rough as bark. "The Queen keeps it as the seal on your contract. A soul-deed is bound by its first consideration. That song isn't just a memory, girl. It's the wax on the letter. It's the *lock*."

"Then it's the key too," Noor said.

"That's why I kept the page back," Sef said, and for the first time in thirty years of knowing him, he looked ashamed clear through. "Because if you knew what it was, you'd go after it. And no one who goes into the vault comes back. I thought—I told myself—if you never knew, you'd live. Half-live, the way you always have. But half-living isn't living, is it. I watched you all these years. You've been a ghost wearing a girl's skin."

Noor folded the page along its old creases and tucked it inside her coat, against her heart, where the torn ledger leaf from Sef's shop still sat. Two pieces of paper. The whole architecture of her life, written small enough to fold away.

"The drowned mines," she said. "There's an entrance. Old smuggling route—the miners cut it before the mountain sank, and the water came up through the lower galleries when the wards failed. I've run goods through the dry levels above it."

"I know which ones you mean." Sef's voice went strange. "Your father mapped them. Did you know that? Before they took him below for judgment, he drew every passage. Your mother sold your soul to buy mercy for a man who then spent his last free month drawing maps so his daughter could someday get *out* of places like this." He laughed, a terrible small sound. "Or maybe so she could get in. Who can say what parents intend."

Noor looked up at the mountain. Caerlum rose against the starless sky like a closed fist, its crown lost in cloud, its flanks stitched with the lights of the Undercourt glowing faintly through the rock—cold white points, like stars imprisoned in stone. Which, she now understood, was exactly what they were.

"Go back to the camp, Sef," she said. "Stay with the widow's people. If I'm not out by the third dawn—"

"Don't say it."

"—then burn my pack and tell them stories about someone else."

She left him sitting against the fallen wall with his head in his hands, and did not look back.

* * *

The mine mouth opened in the hillside above a black tarn, hidden behind a curtain of rowan saplings that had grown up across it in the years since anyone passed. Noor went in at dusk, carrying a shuttered lantern, a coil of oiled rope, and a stoppered bottle of air that an alchemist in the refugee camp had traded her for three memories she could ill afford to lose—a river crossing, a friend's face, the taste of plums. She mourned none of them. They felt less like losses than like shedding.

Inside, the mountain breathed cold. Her boots found the old rails of ore-carts long rotted to red lace, and the walls glistened with mineral sweat that caught her lantern light like a scatter of dull coins. The smugglers' marks were still there—her father's marks, though she hadn't known it until this year—chalk arrows worn to ghosts, a triple-notch meaning *water ahead*, a spiral meaning *turn back*. She followed them downward, deeper than any smuggler had ever needed to go, into passages where the air tasted of iron and old grief.

The water began at the third spiral.

It lay across the tunnel floor, utterly still, black as poured obsidian, and it rose as she descended—ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then to her thighs, so cold it burned. The lantern showed her nothing beyond its own trembling reflection. Somewhere ahead, in the dark beneath the dark, something shifted with a sound like slow turning.

At the fifth gallery the ceiling came down to meet the flood. There was a gap—a hand's width of air between black water and wet stone—and beyond it the tunnel bent and vanished. The old maps called this the Long Breath. Smugglers had swum it once, holding candles in their teeth. None of them had been swimming toward the Queen of Quiet Hours.

Noor stripped off her coat, wrapped the ledger pages and the widow's gift—a small tin of salt, given freely, meant for protection—in oilskin, and bound the bundle tight against her chest. She took three deep breaths of the dead air. Then a fourth. Then she pinched the shutter closed on her lantern, hooked it to her belt, and slid under.

The water was a living thing. It pressed on her ears, her chest, the backs of her eyes. The gap was narrower than it had looked, and the stone scraped her shoulders as she pulled herself through by feel alone, the rope trailing behind her as her single thread back to the world of breathing things. Her lungs began their argument almost at once—first a polite request, then a demand, then a screaming. In the blackness she saw faces: her mother, young and desperate in the Queen's memory-vaults; Sef, gray and ashamed; the widow Marta saying *take it freely, child*, and herself refusing. She thought: *this is how it ends, drowned in my father's tunnels, and no one will even remember me, because I built my whole life on never being remembered—*

And then she thought of the song.

She didn't know it. That was the whole wound of her existence—she did not know it, had never known it, only knew that it existed and that it had been hers and her mother's and that it sat locked in a jar of starlight under this mountain while the sky above stood empty. But she wanted it. Wanting rose in her like a shout, like a struck bell, and she kicked hard against the stone, and the gap bent, and opened—

Her head broke water in a low chamber where the air smelled of minerals and something sweeter, something like honey and candle-smoke. She dragged herself onto sloping rock, coughing, shaking, alive, and laughed—a ragged, disbelieving sound that echoed off walls she could not see.

Then she saw the light ahead, around the next bend, and stopped laughing.

It was starlight. Hundreds of points of it, drifting in the air of the great cavern beyond like snow falling upward, and among them, moving slowly, came shapes—tall, pale, patient. The vault-keepers of the Queen of Quiet Hours, tending their jars.

Noor pressed herself against the wet rock, wrung out her hair, and did what she had done her whole long hollow life. She made herself quiet. She made herself unseen. But this time, for the first time, being unseen was not the point—it was only the road. Ahead, past the drifting keepers, rows upon rows of glass jars glowed on shelves carved into the living stone, each one holding a swirl of captured starlight, and somewhere among those thousands of small prisons waited a jar with a song inside it, a lullaby with no name, sealed in wax older than her grief.

She moved between the shelves on silent feet, reading labels written in silver script: *a wedding, 1819. A betrayal, twice forgiven. The smell of bread.* And then, on the lowest shelf, in a niche apart from all the others, set behind a lattice of black iron:

*A first theft, aged four. One moment, maternal. Song, hearthside, unrecorded title.*

The jar was small, hardly bigger than her two fists. Inside it, instead of the cold starlight of the others, glowed something warm—a golden flicker, pulsing gently, like lamplight through a window on a winter night. Like a hearth. Like the exact shape of home.

Noor's hands trembled as she lifted the jar free of its cradle. Behind her, across the vast dim vault, one of the tall pale keepers turned its head.

Somewhere far above, through miles of stone, the mountain shuddered—and every jar of starlight flickered at once, as if the whole buried court had drawn a single breath.
