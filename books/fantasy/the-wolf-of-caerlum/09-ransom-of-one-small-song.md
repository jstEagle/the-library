## 9. Ransom of One Small Song

The Undercourt did not echo. That was the first thing Noor understood about it, standing at the edge of the Queen's hall with water still dripping from her hem—sound here went nowhere. It rose from her lips and was gathered in, swallowed by walls of pale stone that drank noise the way sand drank rain. Even her heartbeat seemed to be taken from her before she could hear it.

The hall stretched long and low beneath the mountain's root, its ceiling ribbed like the inside of a shell. And along those ribs, set into niches by the hundred and the thousand, burned the stars.

Not images of them. The stars themselves—or what remained of them—each one cupped in a jar of glass so old it had begun to weep, light bleeding down the sides in slow golden tears. They were dimmer than Noor remembered stars being. They flickered like candles in a draft. But they were *hers*, in the way that everything under the sky had once been hers, and something in the hollow where her soul should have been turned toward them like a sunflower toward a sun that no longer existed.

At the far end of the hall, on a throne carved from a single block of moonstone, sat the Queen of Quiet Hours.

She was not beautiful in any way Noor could have described. She was pale the way deep water is pale, the way bone is pale, and her face had the terrible smoothness of something that had never once been surprised. Around her shoulders hung a cloak made of hours—not fabric but time itself, moments stitched to moments, and Noor felt her skin crawl with recognition. She knew that craft. She had spent her whole life handling stolen time, folding other people's yesterdays into small enough shapes to sell. The Queen wore the trade like a queen wears ermine.

"You came through the drowned mines," the Queen said. Her voice was not loud. It did not need to be; it arrived inside Noor's chest rather than at her ears. "Few choose the wet road. Fewer survive it."

"Fewer are stupid enough to try," Noor said. Her voice cracked on the stone floor and lay there, small and mortal. She made herself lift her chin. In her fist, wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, was the memory she had pulled from the vault—the first stolen moment, age four, her mother singing. It pulsed against her ribs like a second pulse. "I've come to settle an account."

"I know." The Queen's eyes were the gray of ash. "You have been settling accounts your entire life, Ashgrove of Caerlum. It is the only inheritance you ever received."

Noor walked forward. Every step cost her something; the hall resisted her the way deep water resists a swimmer, as if the mountain itself preferred that debts remain unpaid. Halfway down the aisle between the star-jars, she stopped, because stopping was also part of it—you did not rush into the presence of the thing that owned you—and she unwrapped the oilcloth.

The memory lay in her palm like a coal that refused to die. She could feel it singing without sound: a kitchen in Dunhollow, twenty-two years gone, flour on the table and her mother's voice rising through some half-remembered tune about a lark and a winter rose. Four-year-old Noor, not knowing yet what she was, reaching out with hands that had never been taught and taking it. Taking the moment whole, leaving her mother mid-verse with a strange emptiness in her eyes, a song she would never finish.

*This,* Noor thought, *is the seal.* This was what Sef's torn ledger page had shown her, this was what the contract needed, this small theft committed by a child who didn't know better. Everything after—all the widow's griefs and soldiers' loves and dying men's last mornings—had been practice for this. Or escape from it.

"My soul," Noor said. "You hold it. I want it back."

The Queen rose. The movement was slow and wrong, like watching a season change. "Do you know why I keep souls in jars?" she asked. "Not for cruelty. Souls spoil in bodies that don't want them. Yours has been rattling around in a hollow girl for twenty-two years, knocking against her ribs, asking to come home. I kept it safe the way one keeps milk cool." A pause, and something almost like amusement moved across that smooth face. "It is a very quiet soul. It takes after you."

"Then give it back and I'll stop disturbing the quiet."

"The debt is real, thief. Your mother signed it in blood and milk-teeth, and contracts written so do not simply dissolve because the debtor grew a spine." The Queen descended from the throne, her cloak of hours whispering with borrowed voices. "But debts can be *paid*. You carry the seal. Bring it to me, and I will release what is yours."

Noor's fingers closed over the memory. "There's a price. There's always a price with brokers. My mother learned that. I learned it in every market from Dunhollow to the sea."

"You did learn," the Queen agreed. "So listen well, because this price is simple and it is total." She extended one pale hand, palm up. "A memory given freely cannot be returned to the giver—it becomes part of the place that received it. This is law older than my court. If you surrender the first stolen moment to me, it passes into my vault *forever*. Not sold. Not traded back. Sung aloud, hour upon hour, until the singing wears it smooth as riverstone, until even I forget the words. You will remember that you loved it. You will never remember how it went."

The hall was silent in that way that ate silence. Somewhere above them, through a mile of living rock, the starless sky pressed down like a held breath.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk out of Caerlum hollow and light, and you live a long life as a remarkable thief, and you die empty, and the stars stay dark, and the widow you've been dragging toward dawn walks into a winter with no morning in it." The Queen tilted her head. "I am not cruel, Ashgrove. Cruelty is a waste of good hours. I am only exact."

Noor looked down at the memory in her hand.

She thought about her mother's voice. That was the trap of it—that was the knife's true edge. She had carried the memory out of the vault through black water, had nearly drowned twice holding it above the flood, and the whole way she had been *listening* to it, hoarding it, letting it play against the inside of her skull like a child pressing her ear to a music box. Twenty-two years of theft and she had never once kept anything. Now she finally held the one thing that was hers-by-right, and the keeping of it would cost everyone else the sky.

*Never keep what you steal,* she had told herself her whole life. But this wasn't stolen anymore. This was ransom. And ransoms, by their nature, are paid and not recovered.

Her mother had sung it badly anyway, she thought wildly. Off-key, forgetting verses, laughing in the middle—

That laugh. Oh, that was the part that would break her. Not the melody. The laugh in the middle of it, when her mother forgot the words and made up worse ones and four-year-old Noor had laughed too, and *that* was the moment she'd actually stolen—not the song, the laughing together. The belonging.

And belonging, she understood suddenly, was not a thing you could hold in a jar. It was a thing you gave away and got back multiplied, or hoarded and lost entirely.

"Noor," said a voice behind her, ragged and human and impossibly out of place.

She turned. Sef stood at the mouth of the hall, soaked to the bone, his broker's ledger clutched to his chest like a shield, flanked by the widow Marta and a handful of refugees who should never have found the drowned road—who had followed her anyway, because she had been unable, in the end, to leave them. Marta's face was streaked with mine-dust and tears.

"You don't have to," Marta called, and her voice echoed strangely in the sound-eating hall, arriving small and stubborn. "Girl, you don't have to pay alone. Take mine. I offered it free, I meant it—"

"That's not how it works here," Noor said. Her throat was tight. "I asked. Some things have to be given—" she looked at the Queen— "*by the person who owes.*"

She walked the last steps to the throne. The Queen waited, palm open, patient as geology.

"If I give it," Noor said quietly, "the stars go home."

"All of them. Through the mountain's crown, back to their places in the sky. The wards will reknit themselves by morning. Your people will have a spring again." The Queen's gray eyes held hers. "And you will have yourself. I warn you, thief—a soul is heavier than any memory. Most who receive theirs late spend days weeping under the weight of simply *being*. Are you prepared for that?"

"I've been carrying the weight of not being for twenty-two years," Noor said. "One more day of heavy won't kill me."

She opened her hand.

The memory rose from her palm on its own, slowly, like a bubble through deep water, trailing light. As it lifted, the song came with it—not silent anymore but sounding, filling the sound-drinking hall despite itself, a woman's off-key voice singing about a lark and a winter rose, a child's laughter caught in the middle of it like a bee in amber. The refugees along the wall began to cry. Sef dropped to his knees. Marta pressed both hands over her mouth.

"It's a fair song," Noor said, and her voice broke clean in half. "My mother had a terrible voice. Keep it somewhere warm."

"I keep nothing warm," said the Queen—but gently, which from her was a kind of blessing. "Only exactly."

The memory crossed the last span of air and settled into the Queen's open hand, and the Queen closed her fingers around it, and then she did the thing Noor had not expected, the thing no broker in all her years of trading had ever done.

She bowed her head. Briefly. Precisely. An acknowledgment between professionals.

"Now," the Queen said, "we conclude."

With her other hand she reached into the air beside the throne and drew out a jar. It was small—smaller than the star-jars, no bigger than a lantern—and inside it something moved that was not light and not liquid but *presence*, turning slow and silver-gray like smoke that had decided to be alive. Noor's body recognized it before her mind did. Every hair on her arms stood up. Her lungs forgot their work.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, that's me."

"It is." The Queen unstopped the jar.

The soul came out like a bird released, circled the hall once—past the dimmed stars, past the weeping refugees, past Sef still kneeling in the wet—and struck Noor in the center of the chest hard enough to stagger her.

The world went white and then went *full*.

She heard colors. She tasted names. Twenty-two years of hollow living filled in all at once, and it hurt, it hurt like a frozen hand thawing, every nerve screaming awake, and she went down onto the moonstone floor with her arms wrapped around herself, sobbing, laughing, both at once, while something vast and quiet settled into the space behind her sternum and *stayed*.

And above her—above all of them—the mountain answered.

It began as a hum in the stone, a note struck on some instrument the size of a country. Then the ceiling of the Undercourt split along a seam no one had seen, a fissure running up through the mountain's crown toward the buried sky, and the star-jars along the walls began to sing. Each jar brightened, trembled, and let go—one by one by one, a thousand points of light rising off their shelves and streaming upward through the crack, pouring out of Caerlum like reversed snowfall, climbing the dark chimney of the world.

Through the fissure Noor could see it happen: the black sky stitching itself shut. Star after star finding its old socket in the heaven, flaring as it seated home, until the whole roof of the night blazed with returning fire and the light came pouring back down the mountain's crown in a column so bright the shadows fled to the corners of the Undercourt and hid.

Marta was laughing and crying. Sef had his forehead pressed to the floor. And Noor knelt in the middle of it all, full to bursting, whole for the first time in her life, and discovered that the Queen had told one truth too few:

A soul does not just fill you. A soul *sings*. Hers was singing now, low and wordless, and though she would search for the melody for the rest of her life, she already knew its shape. A lark. A winter rose. A terrible voice laughing in a kitchen in Dunhollow.

Gone from her forever, and somehow, impossibly, still hers.

Above, the last star slid home into the sky. Below, in the Queen's closed fist, a small memory began its long work of being sung.

The Queen of Quiet Hours looked down at Noor, and for the first time in an age, something moved across that smooth face that might, in a better-lit world, have been called a smile.

"The debt is paid, Ashgrove of Caerlum," she said. "Now go up. Morning is coming—and you have people waiting for you in the light."

Noor rose on unsteady legs, soul-heavy and light-headed, and turned toward the long climb out of the dark—toward the refugees, the widow, the ruined passes, the impossible task of building a life where anyone could see her.

Behind her, faint as riverstone wearing smooth, the Queen began to sing.
