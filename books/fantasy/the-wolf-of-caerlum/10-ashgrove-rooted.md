## 10. Ashgrove, Rooted

Noor woke to the smell of woodsmoke and wet stone, and for a long, unmeasured moment she did not know where she was.

That was not unusual. She had woken in a hundred ditches and doorways, in haylofts and smugglers' cellars, always with the same practiced blankness, the same careful emptiness she had spent her whole life mistaking for safety. But this time the blankness was different. This time it had a shape. It was not the hollow of a stolen thing but the smooth, healed-over place of something given away—like the flat of her palm where a scar had faded but never quite forgotten it had once been a wound.

She opened her eyes.

The sky was full of stars.

They hung above the shoulder of Caerlum mountain in their old constellations, thick as spilled salt, so many of them that the dark between them looked almost blue. Noor lay on her back on a slope of coarse grass above the drowned mines' entrance and stared up at them until her eyes stung, because she could not remember the last time she had seen them—or perhaps she could, and that was the trouble: every memory she owned now felt new-minted, bright-edged, hers in a way nothing had ever been hers before.

Her soul sat inside her like a banked fire. She could feel it when she breathed—a warmth behind her sternum that had no name and needed none. It hummed faintly, and if she listened with more than her ears, she thought the hum sounded almost like singing.

Almost.

She reached for the lullaby the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue.

It was gone.

Not hidden, not misplaced—*gone*, scooped clean out of her the way the Queen had promised, traded into the vaults beneath Caerlum to be sung aloud until it frayed to nothing. Noor searched for it anyway, the way a tongue worries an empty socket. She knew there had been a song. She knew her mother had sung it, low and rough-voiced, in a kitchen that smelled of tallow and rain. She knew—she would know until she died—that she had loved it more than she had ever loved anything she'd stolen or kept or sold. She remembered loving it the way you remember a person's face from a dream: the feeling entire, the details dissolved.

She could not have hummed three notes of it to save her life.

Noor lay still and let that ache move through her, expecting it to break her open. It didn't. It settled instead, deep and quiet, the way grief settles when it has finally been *used*—when it has bought exactly what it was meant to buy. She pressed one hand flat against her sternum, over the warm hum, and said aloud, to the stars, to the mountain, to nobody:

"Worth it."

Her voice came out cracked and rusted. She laughed at the sound of it, a short, disbelieving breath, and then she became aware of other sounds—the ordinary, astonishing sounds of the living: canvas snapping in the wind, a child complaining sleepily, someone chopping something against the side of a pot. The refugee column had not moved on. They had done something far stranger.

They had built.

* * *

The camp stood in a fold of the mountainside where two forgotten smuggler paths met, half-sheltered by a stand of wind-bent rowans. When Noor walked down into it, stiff and hollow-legged and dressed in clothes still dark with mine-water, the work stopped—not all at once, but in a slow ripple, faces turning toward her the way faces turn toward weather.

She braced herself to be invisible. It was reflex; forty years of never being remembered do not unlearn themselves in a single night, even a night with stars in it.

Instead, a boy of maybe seven dropped his armload of kindling and shouted, "She's awake! The star-woman's awake!"

*Star-woman.* Of course. Half the column had seen the sky refill through the mountain's crown, had watched the stars pour out of Caerlum like light out of a broken jug. Noor had been somewhere in the middle of that river of returning light, though her last clear memory of it was the Queen's pale hands releasing the jar, and then singing—her own voice, ragged and off-key and utterly certain—and then nothing, only grass and cold and this morning.

An older woman set down the basket she was carrying and crossed the trampled ground to her without hurrying. Maren. The widow from Dunhollow, from the market stall and the grief Noor had lifted out of her head for coin a lifetime ago—or three weeks ago, or however long the starless dark had lasted. Maren's face had changed since Dunhollow. The grief was still in it, but it sat differently now, worn openly, like a tool rather than a wound.

"You look terrible," Maren said.

"I feel wonderful," Noor said, and found, to her mild surprise, that it was true.

Maren studied her a moment longer, then jerked her chin toward a fire ringed with stones. "Sit down before you fall down. There's barley soup. It's bad. Everyone pretends otherwise, but it's bad."

Noor sat. Someone put a bowl in her hands before she had finished sitting. That alone nearly undid her—being handed things, being anticipated, being *counted*. She ate the bad soup slowly and let the camp resume around her, and no one asked her what it had cost, and no one looked away from her either. They simply made room. She kept waiting for the old instinct to rise—the itch to finish eating and be gone before anyone learned her face—and each time it rose she looked at the stars, still visible even now in the paling morning, a few stubborn ones hanging on near the western ridge, and the instinct lay back down.

"Did it work?" Maren asked quietly, settling beside her. "What you went down there to get?"

Noor considered lying. It was the oldest habit she had. Instead she said, "Yes," and then, because Maren had earned more than one word: "I got all of me back. Except one song. I paid it. It stays under the mountain now, and I don't—" Her throat tightened unexpectedly. "I don't remember how it goes."

Maren was quiet for a moment. Then she frowned, tilted her head slightly, the way a person does when listening to something just past the edge of hearing.

"That's strange," she said. "Because I've had a tune stuck in my head since yesterday. Since the stars came back. I thought it was one of my husband's—he used to hum while he mended nets." She paused. "It isn't his."

"What is it?" Noor asked, though her heart had already begun to pound.

"It goes like this," Maren said, and hummed.

Four notes. Maybe five. A small rising fragment, simple as a stair step, sweet and minor-keyed and unmistakably—

Noor's bowl nearly slipped from her hands.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's—it. How do you *have* that?"

"I don't know." Maren looked troubled and awed in equal measure. "When the stars streamed past us on the mountainside, it was like... like light carrying sound. Like everything coming out of that mountain passed through us on its way home. Most of it kept going." She touched her own temple. "This bit stayed. Lodged, like a burr in wool. I've been humming it all morning. Drove Tam crazy."

"Sing it again," Noor said. "Please."

Maren sang it again, and again, and Noor closed her eyes and held each note as it passed, knowing even as she did that it would not stay—knowing the Queen's bargain was iron, that the song belonged to the vault now, sung aloud there forever until it wore to silence. But four notes had escaped. Four notes had caught in a widow's memory like a seed in fleece, and no contract in the Undercourt had counted on that. Noor held them the way you hold water in cupped hands, grateful for every second before it drains.

When she opened her eyes, Maren was watching her with a small, fierce tenderness.

"It's yours," Maren said. "However much of it I've got. You take it whenever you want it. That's not stealing, mind—I'm giving it."

Noor's chest ached. "I know," she said. "I'm learning the difference."

* * *

They named the settlement three days later, around the evening fire, when it had stopped being a camp and started being a place: footings dug for two longhouses, a well begun, a palisade sketched in the dirt and argued over by six people who had never built anything together in their lives.

There were other names proposed. Thornfell Rest. New Dunhollow. Starfield, which the children liked and everyone else pretended not to.

It was the boy with the kindling who settled it, the way children settle things—by accident, by truth. He had taken to following Noor around the site, asking questions about which paths went where, because she knew every road on this mountain that the maps had forgotten. "Ashgrove," he called her, because she had told him her surname at last—told it freely, loudly, in front of witnesses, the first time in her life she had done such a thing—and because there were rowans all around the settlement, and rowans, he informed her solemnly, were ash trees, and he was seven and did not care about the difference.

"Ashgrove," he announced to the fire, when the naming argument had gone in its fourth circle, "is already the name. She's the one who brought the stars back. It should be called what she's called."

Silence around the fire. Noor felt every eye turn to her and fought, one final time, the lifelong flinch—the urge to deny, deflect, dissolve.

Then she looked up at the sky, where the stars stood in their ranks, whole and unhidden. She thought about roots: how an ash tree stands in one place its entire life and is none the poorer for it. How it holds ground. How it is *found* by everything—bird, wind, weather—because it does not run.

"All right," she said, and her voice only shook a little. "Ashgrove."

The name went around the fire like a flame going wick to wick, and someone began beating time on a pot, and the children danced badly in the dirt streets-that-were-not-yet-streets, and Noor Ashgrove sat among them in full view of everyone, seen, known, staying.

Later, after the fire had burned to orange coals, Maren came and sat beside her again and hummed the four notes, soft, twice through. Noor hummed them back. Between the two of them they almost had five.

"There'll be others," Maren said. "Others the song lodged in, coming out of the mountain that night. Word travels. People will come here—to Ashgrove—carrying pieces you can gather. A note from one, a bar from another." She smiled, tired and crooked. "You might spend years collecting your mother's voice back, piece by piece, gift by gift."

Noor watched the sparks climb toward the stars and felt the warm hum behind her sternum answer them, patient as a rooted thing.

"Good," she said. "I've always been good at finding what other people carry." She fed another branch to the fire. "But this time, I mean to keep it."

Far below them, deep beneath the mountain, something in the vaults of the Undercourt sang on in the dark—and far above, on the ridge road, the first of the travelers who had heard the stars sing was already walking toward the fires of Ashgrove, humming.
