## 1. The Cargo That Knew My Name

The pass had a smell to it that Theo Dunmore knew better than their own mother's face—which was saying something, since they couldn't remember her face at all.

Snowmelt and pine rot and cold stone sweating in the dark, and underneath it all the faint iron tang of the old wards, the ones the wolf-folk had tended once upon a time and nobody tended now. The Thornfell passes breathed that smell year-round, like a sleeping animal. Theo had been breathing it since they were nine years old, small enough to squeeze through the smugglers' gaps in the border cairns, light enough not to crack the ice bridges over the falls. Twelve years later they were still squeezing through gaps. Just wider ones now, and carrying more expensive things.

Tonight's cargo was a strongbox the size of a loaf of black bread, wrapped in oilcloth and lashed inside a false-bottomed grain sack, and its owner was dead.

That fact should have made everything simpler. In the trade, a dead client meant a dead contract, and a dead contract meant the goods reverted to whoever held them—guild law, older than any of them. But Guildmaster Corren had been strange about this one. Quiet about it. He'd pressed the box into Theo's hands two nights ago with none of his usual patter about tolls and splits, just looked at them a long moment across the lamplight of his study, firelight catching the gray coming in at his temples, and said, *Deliver it to the waystation at Hollowmere. You alone. Tell no one what you carry.*

Theo alone. In fourteen years of running cargo, Corren had never once sent anyone anywhere alone. Alone was how people got robbed. Alone was how people got killed. Alone was how people got *asked questions by men in gray coats* who didn't bother with guild law.

So Theo had said yes, because you always said yes to Corren, and then they'd spent two days being careful, which was the only inheritance their mother had ever left them: a talent for carefulness so deep it lived in the bones. They'd taken the high route past the shattered watchtower instead of the low road. They'd doubled back twice through the scree fields where footprints filled in with wind-blown grit before an hour passed. They'd camped cold, no fire, chewing frozen sausage and watching the stars wheel over the jagged teeth of the Thornfells like slow white sparks off a whetstone.

And still, on the third morning, crouched in the lee of a granite shoulder while sleet hissed down the valley below, Theo heard hoofbeats.

Four horses. Moving fast for the terrain. Moving with the particular confidence of riders who knew exactly where they were going, which no one did in Thornfell unless they had a map—or a tracker.

Theo went still against the stone and let the mountain do what mountains did best: hide things. Gray cloak over gray rock, breath held shallow, the sleet needling the back of their neck. The riders came up the valley trail below at a canter, and through the shifting veils of weather Theo counted them and felt the cold slide from their skin into their chest. Four men in gray coats, hoods back despite the weather, riding unarmored and unhurried. No soldier dressed like that. No soldier rode with that kind of certainty, either, as though the mountain itself had told them which way to go.

Inquisitors. Malvane's. This far north, this deep into the free territories, when everyone said the Usurper wouldn't move on the mountain passes until spring.

They were heading toward Hollowmere. Toward the waystation. Toward the delivery point that exactly two living people knew about—one of whom was crouched behind a rock, and one of whom was sitting warm in Highmoor, a hundred miles south.

Theo waited until the hoofbeats faded into the sleet-silence, and then they waited another hour besides, because carefulness lived in the bones. When they finally moved, they didn't move toward Hollowmere. They moved up. There was a cave above the tree line, an old smugglers' cache marked in their mother's hand on a route map Corren kept locked in a drawer he thought Theo didn't know about—and Theo had known about it since they were eleven, because knowing things quietly was also part of carefulness.

* * *

The cave smelled of old ash and older secrets. Someone had used it within the decade; there were candle stubs fused to a shelf of stone and the ghost-prints of boots in the dust near the entrance. But it was dry, and defensible, and invisible from below unless you knew to look, and Theo built a fire the size of a cupped hand anyway, because their fingers had gone wooden with cold and a dead smuggler was no use to anyone.

They set the grain sack down and looked at it for a long time.

Corren's face kept surfacing in memory—that long look across the lamplight, the unusual silence of him. Fourteen years, and Theo could count on one hand the number of times the guildmaster had been silent when he could have been talking. Silence from Corren wasn't caution. Silence from Corren was fear wearing its good coat.

"Dead client," Theo said aloud, just to hear a voice in the dark. "Dead clients don't get followed."

They cut the lashings with their belt knife. The oilcloth came away stiff and crackling. Beneath it: black lacquered wood, iron corners gone soft with verdigris, and a seal pressed into a wafer of red wax across the lid—a seal Theo didn't recognize, a tower beneath a crescent moon, worked in such fine relief it must have been stamped with a signet worth more than the whole guildhouse. And under the seal, inked directly onto the lacquer in a spidery antique hand, a single word:

*Dunmore.*

Theo's own knife-hand didn't shake. It hadn't shaken since they were twelve and a warder in Lowmark had nearly caught them lifting tallow candles. But something behind their ribs went loose and weightless, like the first step onto ice that looks solid and isn't.

Because Dunmore was not a common name in the free cities. It was barely a name at all anymore. As far as Theo had ever been able to learn—and they had asked, once, carefully, the way you ask about anything that matters—it belonged to a woman who had run the Thornfell routes twenty years ago and vanished into them, leaving behind a map drawer full of her handwriting and a son or daughter she never came back for. Corren never spoke of her. The old-timers crossed themselves with two fingers when her name came up, which was itself an answer of a kind.

The seal cracked under the knife with a sound like a knuckle popping.

Inside, packed in wool gone gray with age, lay a book.

It was bigger than it had any right to be—the strongbox had seemed too small for it even as Theo lifted it out, and that wrongness sat in the hands strangely, the way a sleeping cat weighs more than it should. Bound in vellum stretched over boards, its cover scarred and water-stained but intact, corners reinforced with tarnished silver. No title. No clasp. Just a faint warmth, as if the thing had been sitting near a fire, though the box had come out of a cold warehouse in Highmoor.

Theo turned it over twice. Smugglers learned quickly what things were worth by heft, and this hefted like nothing—like a promise, or a debt.

"Right," they murmured. "Let's see what you are."

The pages were vellum too, fine as frost, covered edge to edge in handwriting that shifted as the book went on—dozens of hands across decades, maybe centuries. Some entries were ledger-dry, inventories and dates. Others sprawled in great agitated loops, margins crowded with little diagrams: mountain passes, standing stones, lines of force drawn between them like the threads of a spiderweb flung over the whole Thornfell range. Theo read fragments without meaning to. *…the wards hold if the oath is renewed at the turning of each season…* *…the wolf-folk keep the northern stones and the humans the southern, and between them the covenant…* Words that belonged in chapbooks and nursemaids' tales, written here in the flat practical hand of someone taking minutes at a meeting.

Then Theo reached the last written page, and stopped breathing.

The entry was fresh. Not fresh like recent—fresh like *now*. The ink had a faint sheen, as if it had dried within the hour, within the minute, though the box had been sealed for years; Theo knew that because Corren had told them the client had died with it already crated and addressed. The handwriting was elegant and utterly unfamiliar, and it said:

*On the third night of the Thaw Moon, in the year the usurper's shadow crosses the passes, the Covenant-Bearer comes to the mountain. She carries the book her mother carried, and her name is*

—and there, in letters that seemed to sit slightly deeper in the vellum than ink had any right to sit, was written:

**Theo Dunmore.**

Below the name, a date. Tonight's date. The third night of the Thaw Moon, which Theo knew because they'd been counting the nights since Hollowmere, counting them the way you count anything when your luck feels thin.

And below the date, a sketch, done in a few swift confident strokes of charcoal: a narrow face, sharp-chinned, hair bound back for travel, a scar through the left eyebrow from a fall off the Kettlebridge wall at thirteen years old.

Their own face. Drawn by a hand that had died years ago, or sealed years ago, or—

Theo set the book down on the stone very gently, the way you set down a snake you aren't sure is asleep, and stood up, and walked three paces to the cave mouth, and stood there looking out at the sleet and the dark and the black serrated skyline of the Thornfells while their heart tried to climb out through their throat.

All their life they had been a person made of gaps. No family name that anyone spoke twice. No history before age nine, when Corren had found them half-starved in a Highmoor alley and—whatever else you said about the man—fed them first and questioned them after. A face forgettable on purpose, a voice pitched to be overlooked, a whole self built like a smuggler's tunnel: narrow, dark, and leading nowhere visible. Invisibility wasn't a curse. Invisibility was *safety*. It was the one wealth Theo had ever owned outright, and they had spent twelve years adding to the hoard, one unremarkable day at a time.

And now some dead stranger's book had written their name in wet ink, sketched their scar, and dated it to the exact night they sat shivering over a fire no bigger than a fist. Every gap in their life had just been outlined in charcoal. A life built on being illegible, suddenly rendered in a fair hand for anyone to read.

"They know," Theo whispered to the mountain. The word came out ragged. "Whoever wrote this—they *knew* I'd have it tonight. Which means someone knows where I am *right now*."

Which meant the four riders in gray coats weren't hunting the cargo at all.

They were hunting the courier.

Behind them, in the dark of the cave, the Vellum Codex lay open on the stone, its final page gleaming faintly, and Theo could have sworn—could have sworn on Corren's grave and their mother's empty one—that the ink of their own name was still wet.
