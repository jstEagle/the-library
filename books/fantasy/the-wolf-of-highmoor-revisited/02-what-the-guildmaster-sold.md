## 2. What the Guildmaster Sold

The guildhouse smelled of tallow and wet stone the way it always had, but tonight Theo noticed how much it smelled of endings.

They came in through the coal door as they had a thousand times before, boots quiet on the stairs, the Codex wrapped in oilcloth against their chest like a second heartbeat. Above them, through the floorboards, they could hear Corren's voice—low, measured, the voice he used for settling disputes between crews or negotiating with harbormasters who wanted too large a cut. It was a good voice. It was the voice that had taught Theo to haggle, to read a mark, to lie with their whole face.

It was also, Theo now understood, the voice of a man who had been selling them for years.

They had pieced it together on the road down from the passes. The inquisitors had known which trail. Not guessed—known. Known the way someone knows a thing they've been told by another mouth, in another room, over many small and comfortable transactions. Theo had turned the thought over all the long descent from Thornfell, refusing to look at it directly, the way you refuse to look at a wound until you're somewhere safe enough to bleed.

There was no safe enough. There never had been.

Theo pushed open the door to Corren's study without knocking.

The old guildmaster sat at his desk with a lamp turned low, a cup of something dark at his elbow, and he did not startle. That was the first confirmation. A guilty man startles. Corren only looked up with tired eyes and said, "Shut the door behind you, lad. The draft comes straight off the harbor."

"Don't." Theo's voice cracked on the word. They set the wrapped Codex on the desk, deliberately, so he would know what they carried. "Don't call me *lad*. Don't do any of it."

Corren studied the bundle for a long moment. In the lamplight his face looked older than Theo had ever let themselves see—the deep lines around his mouth, the gray creeping into the beard he kept trimmed neat as an honest merchant's. He had been handsome once, the story went. He had been dangerous once. He had pulled a fourteen-year-old orphan off the freezing docks and given them a trade, a name, a place at a table where no one else would have them.

"Sit down," Corren said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Of course you would." He leaned back, and the chair creaked under him. "You've been to see the Codex opened, then. I can tell by your face. You wear every thought like a scarlet cloak, Theo Dunmore. Always have. It's why you were such a poor spy and such a good runner—you feel everything at full volume and move fast enough that nobody has time to notice."

"How long?" Theo asked. "How long have you known what it says?"

"Known about it? Since before you could walk." Corren reached for his cup, considered it, didn't drink. "Your mother left it with me the night she died. Told me if anything ever happened to her, there was a strongbox in the Deepwater run that must go to no one unless—" He stopped himself. His jaw worked. "Unless the page bore a name. Her words were: *if my child's name is written in it, then the debt has come due, and you give it to them and you let them choose.*"

"And the rest of it," Theo said quietly. "The part about the inquisitors."

Something flickered across the old man's face—not shame exactly. Shame would have been easier to bear. It was more like the weary arithmetic of a man who had done sums so long he'd forgotten numbers once meant anything.

"You went up the Thornfell pass three nights ago," Corren said. "Two days ago, an inquisitor's courier rode out of Highmoor toward the coast with a report. Yesterday, riders came back down the pass asking after a young runner with a scarred left hand carrying a sealed box. I told them you'd taken the coast road south."

"But you'll tell them different tomorrow."

"I'll tell them whatever keeps forty families fed and this house standing." Corren's voice stayed level, and that was somehow the worst of it. No rage. No gloating. Just the flat tone of a man reciting terms he'd memorized years ago. "You think Malvane's men found us because we're careless? We are the last smugglers' guild on the whole western seaboard, boy. Every other one burned or rotted in a cell. Ours survived because I made myself useful to the new order. Routes I don't use. Names of dead clients. And when they asked after the Vellum Codex—and oh, they asked, they've been asking for fifteen years—I gave them the one thing worth more than all the rest."

Theo's hands had gone cold. "Me."

"The movements of Yara Dunmore's child." Corren finally drank, grimacing at it like medicine. "Small things, at first. Which roads you favored. Where you slept when you were drinking. Then larger things. They never asked me to hand you over, understand that. They only ever asked where you were going. What you did after that was your own affair." He set the cup down with a soft click. "I told myself it was protection. Watched, not hunted. A leash is kinder than a knife."

"And now?"

"Now they've stopped asking politely." He rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the window overlooking the harbor. Below, Highmoor's lamps were coming on one by one, golden and ordinary, a city that did not yet know its luck had changed. "The man who questioned me yesterday wasn't one of the usual clerks. He wore gray, and he smiled too much, and he used the word *Covenant-Bearer* like he'd been waiting his whole life to say it aloud. They know, Theo. Whatever your mother started, whoever wrote your name in that book—it's finished hiding. Within two days, maybe three, they stop watching you and come take you. And when they do, they'll burn this house down around everyone in it whether I've helped them or not. The usefulness ends when the quarry is caught. That's how dogs are treated."

"So what do you want?" Theo heard their own voice go hard and strange. "A blessing? My forgiveness? You want me to thank you for selling me slowly?"

"I want you to *run*, you stubborn little fool." For the first time the level voice broke, and beneath it was something raw and old. "I want you out of Highmoor tonight, before dawn, before the gray coats wake. Take the Codex and take nothing else, because anything else will slow you. Go north into Thornfell where their horses can't follow, and don't come back. Don't send word. Don't be clever. Be gone."

"Why?" The word tore out of Theo sharper than they intended. "Why warn me at all, if you've spent years making me legible to them? Why does it matter now?"

Corren turned from the window. In the lamplight his eyes were wet, and Theo realized with a lurch that he had perhaps been sitting alone in this room for hours deciding how to say what came next, rehearsing it, failing to make it clean.

"Because I sold your mother's road to save my own skin, and she knew I would," he said. "That's why she gave me the box instead of the truth. She knew what I was. She trusted me anyway—or trusted the guild anyway—and I told myself for twenty years that those were the same thing." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I am not going to sell you twice. Whatever the prophecy wants from you, whatever it costs—it will cost less than what I've already paid. Now get out of my house before I remember how to be the man they bought."

* * *

Theo took the coal stair down and the tunnel out, the old ways, the ways their mother had cut through Highmoor's bones when Theo was still in swaddling clothes. Their body knew the route without instruction—duck here, count paces there, hold breath past the cistern grate—and some traitorous part of them grieved even while running, grieving the thousand small kindnesses folded into these stones. The chalk marks Corren refreshed himself though he could have ordered it done. The loose brick near the third landing that hid coins for runners caught short. All of it built by the same hands that had fed them, piece by piece, to the wolves in gray.

The guildhouse was already burning when Theo surfaced in the tanner's yard behind Salt Row.

They stood in the shadow of a drying shed and watched the windows bloom orange, one after another, like eyes opening. There was no screaming, which meant the house had been emptied first—Corren had seen to that, had sent everyone to the Drowned Lantern with some pretext, had waited until the building held only himself and ledgers and forty years of careful, damnable survival. Smoke rolled thick and black into the winter sky, and figures moved in the street below—gray coats, six of them, unhurried, watching the fire with the satisfaction of men crossing an item off a list.

Then the door of the burning house opened, and Corren walked out into the light.

He was not running. He was not armed. He stood on his own doorstep with his hands visible and his chin lifted, an old man silhouetted against his own ruin, and he raised his voice just enough to carry to the shadows where Theo crouched.

"He went north!" Corren called to the gray coats, clear and carrying and unmistakably false. "The Dunmore whelp took the coast road north—do you hear me? North! You'll want fresh horses!"

One of the gray coats laughed. Theo saw the flash of teeth in the firelight, saw the leader step forward with something glinting in his fist, and Theo understood in one white-hot instant what Corren was buying with the last coin he had. Every eye on the doorstep. Every blade pointed at the guildhouse. Every heartbeat of the hunt spent on a lie delivered by a dying man.

Theo ran.

They ran north along the frozen canal with tears freezing on their cheeks and the Codex hammering against their ribs, and behind them Highmoor lit the clouds red, and ahead the mountains rose black against blacker sky, wearing their snow like a wound dressing. Somewhere up in that darkness were the wolf-folk whose covenant book weighed against Theo's chest. Somewhere up there were wards broken by a Dunmore's hand. Somewhere up there, inquisitors' outriders were already fanning through the passes, hunting a name that was theirs alone in all the world.

Grief came later. Grief always came later; Corren had taught them that too, though he'd never meant to. What came now was something harder and cleaner, forged in the orange light of everything burning: the understanding that there was no road back, no guild, no name to hide behind, no one left to betray them because everyone who could had. Only forward. Only the mountain. Only the terrible, liberating fact that a life rendered legible could no longer pretend to be small.

At the canal's end, where the city gave way to scrubland and the first foothills, Theo stopped once and looked back. Firelight haloed the rooftops. Riders were spilling out of the north gate already, torches bobbing like a string of angry stars, fanning onto the wrong road.

"They'll figure it out by morning," Theo whispered to the Codex, to the mother inside it, to no one. "So by morning, I'd better be a legend instead of a target."

The wind off Thornfell answered with a sound almost like laughter—or like something four-footed moving through the dark above the treeline, keeping pace, watching the small human shape climb toward it with a stolen book and a borrowed resolve.

By the time the moon rose, the passes had swallowed Theo whole.
