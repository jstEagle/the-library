## 3. Teeth in the Thornfell Dark

Theo had been running for three days when the snow turned against them.

It came down in sheets off the high shoulders of Thornfell, dry and hissing, filling every track behind them within minutes—and filling every track ahead of them too, until the pass became a white corridor with no memory and no direction. Theo pulled their cloak tighter, hunched the satchel holding the Codex beneath layers of oilcloth and wool, and kept climbing. The cold had long since stopped feeling like cold. It was simply a fact of the body now, like breath or heartbeat: *you are freezing, you are freezing, keep walking.*

Behind them—somewhere below the tree line, somewhere in the gray murk—the bells of the inquisitors rang. Not church bells. Hunt-bells, small bronze things tuned to a pitch that slid under the skin and sat there like a splinter. Malvane's inquisitors did not shout as they tracked a fugitive. They rang. It was worse, somehow. It made the mountain itself feel complicit.

*You are legible now.* The thought arrived unbidden, the way it had been arriving since the night they'd opened the strongbox. For twenty-two years Theo Dunmore had been a ghost by trade—a smuggler's apprentice trained to leave no shadow, no ledger-line, no name worth writing down. And then one sealed strongbox from a dead client, and suddenly their name was ink on century-old vellum. Their face, sketched by some dead prophet's hand. Tonight's date, written before tonight existed.

They stopped at the lip of the pass to breathe and nearly didn't start again. The wind here screamed through a notch in the rock like something being flayed alive. Below, the valley was a sea of cloud. Ahead, the trail narrowed to a ledge barely wide enough for a goat, hugging a cliff face streaked with ice.

The Codex shifted against their spine as they climbed, and its weight felt less like paper than like a hand pressed between their shoulder blades, pushing.

*You knew this road,* they reminded themselves. *Mother's road. She ran this pass a hundred times.*

That was the other thing that wouldn't stop circling: the growing suspicion that everything Theo knew about smuggling, about these mountains, about surviving—they had learned it all from a woman who had walked this same path carrying the same book, a generation ago, for reasons no one had ever explained.

The bells changed tone.

Theo froze on the ledge, one hand flat against the cliff. Below and behind, perhaps two hundred yards down the switchback, torchlight bloomed through the snowfall—not the scattered orange of searchers, but a disciplined crescent of it. Six figures, maybe eight, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who already knew where the quarry would be. Inquisitors didn't chase. They *herded*. They closed every exit except the one they wanted you to take, and the one they wanted was always the one with no bottom.

The ledge ended forty feet ahead at a sheer wall of granite. No way forward. The switchback below was closing. Above, the cliff rose smooth and black into the storm.

"Ah," Theo said quietly, to no one. "So this is where I die. On schedule."

Because that was the worst of it—the Codex had given them tonight's date too, weeks ago, in its patient old script. They had told themselves it meant something else. A meeting. A crossing. Anything but this: a narrow ledge, a closing crescent of torchlight, snow falling like ash.

The first crossbow bolt took the rock beside their head and burst into splinters.

Theo ran—not forward, there was no forward—back along the ledge toward the torches, because a quick death beat falling, and fell instead onto their knees behind a spur of stone as a second bolt hummed past where their chest had been. The bells were ringing louder now, gleeful almost, and through the snowfall Theo could see the lead inquisitor clearly: gray robes, iron half-mask, and hands bare despite the cold, hands that moved in small precise gestures like a man conducting music only he could hear.

Ward-work. He was sealing the ledge. Theo felt it happen—a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the air, the old smuggler's instinct screaming that every route out of this place had just quietly ceased to exist.

The inquisitor raised one bare hand.

And something else answered from above.

It came out of the storm like the storm's own voice made flesh—a howl that started low and rose until the cliff itself seemed to shudder, and it was not one voice but many, layered, harmonics stacked on harmonics, the sound of a thing that had learned to sing with an entire winter in its throat. The torches guttered. The ward-pressure snapped like a cut harp string. And on the ledge above the inquisitors, silhouetted against the snow-glare, stood a figure that was neither wolf nor woman but both, fur silvered at the throat, amber eyes catching the torchlight and throwing it back doubled.

The inquisitor's crescent broke apart all at once, discipline dissolving into the oldest human panic. Bolts flew upward and found nothing. Something large and fast went through the snowfall at the edge of vision—one inquisitor screamed, dropped his torch, fled. Then another. Then the gray-robed leader stood alone on the switchback, hand raised again, and the wolf-thing dropped from the rocks onto him with a sound like a door slamming, and the bell-tone cut off mid-ring.

Silence, except for wind and snow.

Then the creature turned its head, and looked directly up at Theo crouched behind the spur of stone, and said, in accented but perfectly clear Common Tongue:

"You're bleeding on my mountain. Get up before you freeze."

* * *

Wren had known the man—no. She corrected herself, even in her own head. Wren had known the *Dunmore cub* was coming for three days, ever since the scouts on the eastern crags had reported smoke from a fire built the way humans built fires, badly, and a single traveler moving through the high passes with a wrapped burden they guarded like a second heart.

She had not expected the inquisitors. That changed things. Iron-masked witch-hunters did not climb into Thornfell for smugglers.

She watched the human now across the small fire she'd finally permitted, in a hollow beneath an overhang where the flames couldn't be seen from the valley. The Dunmore cub—young, sharp-faced, frostbitten at the fingertips—sat with their arms around their knees and their wrapped bundle in their lap, and had not once, in two hours, let go of it.

"The bells," Wren said. "Why do his hunters want you? Smugglers they hang in the low country. You they chased into my mountains in winter."

Theo's laugh had no humor in it. "You know what this is." They touched the bundle. "You called it something, when you landed on him. Before you broke his jaw."

"I called it what it is." Wren's hackles wanted to rise and she made them lie flat. Old discipline. *A scout does not show her teeth to a guest, even one she does not trust.* "Say its name, if you know it."

"I know what your people call it," Theo said slowly. "I read enough. The Vellum Codex. The covenant-book. I know it names me, which is why I'm being hunted, and I know—" they hesitated, and Wren scented the fear-sour shift in their sweat, sharp as vinegar, "—I know it says I'm supposed to do something. Reforge something. Before Malvane crosses."

The fire popped. Somewhere far below, an avalanche released with a sound like slow thunder, and neither of them flinched, though for different reasons.

"That book was stolen," Wren said. Each word placed carefully, like a foot on river ice. "Fifty years ago. Taken from the den-holds by a human smuggler who was trusted. Who was *loved*, cub, by the ones she betrayed. My grandmother still has scars from the night the wards failed, when the book left the mountain and everything we had guarded for nine hundred years came apart in a single season." She leaned forward, and let her eyes catch the firelight the way they naturally did, the doubled amber glow, and did not apologize for it. "Do you understand what you are carrying?"

"A book."

"A wound. Carried in human wrapping, guarded by human hands, for fifty years." She sat back. "And now it turns up in the hands of a Dunmore. Do you know that name means anything to us?"

Something crossed the human's face—too fast to read, too honest to hide entirely.

"No," Theo said.

Wren held their gaze for a long moment. Her grandmother's voice was in her ears: *Never trust a Dunmore. Never trust the line that broke the world.* But her grandmother had also taught her to read sign, and the sign here said: *this cub is starving, frostbitten, hunted, and afraid, and chose to come to Thornfell anyway rather than sell what they carry.* Fear could be feigned. Frostbite could not.

"The elders will decide what you are," she said at last. "Not me. I'm a scout. I find things; I don't judge them." She rose, shaking snow from her fur, and pointed up the dark slope with her muzzle. "We walk tonight. Three days to the den-holds, through country that will kill you twice before noon. If you fall behind, I am ordered to leave you. If you betray us—" she showed her teeth, briefly, letting the fire see them, "—the mountain keeps what it catches."

"And if I keep up?" Theo asked. They were already standing, shouldering the bundle, wincing at their frozen feet. Stubborn. That was very Dunmore, her grandmother would have said, in the voice that meant grief.

"Then maybe," Wren said, turning into the storm, "we find out whether a debt can be paid by someone who wasn't born when it was made."

The human followed her into the dark, limping, clutching the stolen book, and Wren ranged ahead through the snowfall listening—to the wind, to the passes, to the absence of bells—and tried to remember that hope, among her people, had always been considered a dangerous thing to carry.

Like the book, she thought. Like everything worth carrying.

Three days south, in the den-holds beneath Thornfell, the elders were waiting. And Wren had seen, in the last flicker of the firelight before they left, the name written inside the human's collar where the ink had faded: *Mara Dunmore*—and recognized it, with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, as the name her grandmother cursed every year on the anniversary of the breaking.
