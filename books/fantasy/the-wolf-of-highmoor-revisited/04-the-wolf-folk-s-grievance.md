## 4. The Wolf-Folk's Grievance

The descent into Thornfell's heart took the better part of a day, and Wren spent every hour of it listening to the human breathe.

Not the breathing itself—Theo Dunmore was quiet enough, for a two-leg, moving over scree and root with the surefootedness of someone who had learned the mountains as a smuggler learns them: as roads, as wages, as ways not to die. It was the *quality* of the breathing that occupied Wren. The quick shallow catch when the trees thinned and the crags of Thornfell reared up gray against the sky. The held breath at the river crossing, where the water ran loud enough to drown the sound of pursuit. And once, in the shadow of a lightning-split pine, a long exhale that sounded almost like a word—the beginning of a name, maybe, swallowed before it could be spoken.

Grief, Wren thought. Or guilt. In her experience the two smelled identical on humans: something sour under the skin, like milk turning.

*You are bringing a stranger into the den-holds,* her grandmother would say, if her grandmother were alive to say it. *You are bringing the thief's grandchild into the last room of our house.* But her grandmother was three winters dead, and the elders who remained had sent Wren out with one instruction—*find what was stolen*—and what she had found was this: a human with a scarred knuckles and a book that hummed against Wren's senses like a struck bell that would not stop ringing. The Vellum Codex. The covenant-book. Her people's memory, bound in calfskin and kept warm in a smuggler's pack for a generation while the wards above Thornfell rotted like teeth.

She did not let herself think about how the human had come to hold it. She would think about that later, or she wouldn't.

* * *

The entrance to the den-holds was nothing—a seam of darkness behind a curtain of falling water, so narrow that Theo had to turn sideways and suck in breath. Wren watched the human flinch as the rock closed around them, walls pressing close enough that an outstretched hand brushed stone on either side, and understood with a small cold satisfaction that no amount of mountain-craft could make a burrow feel like home to something built for open sky.

Then the passage opened, and opened again, and opened a third time, and Theo stopped walking altogether.

Wren had forgotten, a little, what the first sight did to people. The den-holds were not caves in any way a surface-dweller meant the word. They were a city turned inside out and buried: vast galleries vaulted in living rock, their ceilings glittering with seams of quartz that caught the light of phosphorescent moss and scattered it back down in slow constellations. Terraces of dwellings rose in spirals along the gallery walls, doorways hung with dyed-hide curtains, cook-smoke drifting up through clever vents until the whole cavern smelled of woodsmoke and roasting fish and something resinous and sweet. Wolves in their half-shapes moved everywhere—tall figures with amber eyes and ears that swiveled toward every echo—and wolves in their full shapes loped between the terraces with the unbothered ease of dogs in a market square, except these were nobody's dogs, and everyone knew it.

"By all the free cities," Theo whispered.

"The free cities never looked," Wren said. "That was always the trouble."

Heads turned as they descended the long ramp into the heart of the hold. Conversations faltered. A child tugged her mother's hide-skirt and pointed, and the mother pulled her back with a hand that trembled—not with fear, Wren saw, but with the effort of not snarling. By the time they reached the Elders' Terrace, a silent crowd had gathered along the upper galleries, dozens of amber eyes tracking the human's every step.

Three elders waited by the great hearth at the terrace's center, where a fire burned in a ring of blackened stones that had clearly been burning for a very long time. Wren knew them all. Old Sorrel, gray-muzzled and bent, who had been a ward-keeper before the breaking. Maera, still strong-shouldered, whose mate had died on the night the mountains went dark. And Harrow, eldest of the three, who had lost more than anyone and therefore spoke least.

Wren set the Codex on the stone between them—or rather, she took it from Theo's pack herself, because the human's hands had gone strange and slow, and laid it down where the firelight could reach it.

"It was in a dead client's cargo," she said. "Carried through our passes like salt pork or silk. Bound for Highmoor." She did not look at Theo when she said it. "The bearer claims to have found it sealed, with no knowledge of what it was."

"Claims," said Maera.

"I *claim*," Theo said, and Wren heard the flinch in her own ears—the human's voice cracking on the word, steadying itself. "I'm a smuggler, not a scholar. I opened a strongbox because that's my trade. If I'd known—" Theo stopped. Started again. "If I'd known what it was, I'd have—"

"Brought it home sooner?" Maera's voice was soft, which somehow made it worse. "Sold it dearer? Which?"

Silence stretched. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the high galleries, the child was still watching, too far away to be pulled back.

Old Sorrel stirred at last and touched the book's cover with one long finger, the way you touch a sleeping thing you are afraid of waking. "Open it," she said quietly.

Theo hesitated, then stepped forward and turned the pages with a care that surprised Wren—thumbs angled to avoid the margins, spine supported, the handling of someone who had grown up around things that could be ruined by rough hands even if they couldn't be bought. The vellum caught the light, and the script upon it seemed almost to move, ink shifting like current beneath ice.

"The covenant," Sorrel said, and her voice took on the cadence of recitation, worn smooth by decades of telling. "Read the terms, human. Read them aloud, so the hold can hear whether you understand what your kind carries so carelessly in its packs."

Theo read. "*Let there be one pact between the free cities of men and the wolf-folk of Thornfell. Let the wolf-folk keep the mountain wards, kindling them season by season, that no army nor dark-working may cross the heights. Let the cities send each year the tithe of salt, iron, and seed, and let neither people raise claw nor blade against the other while the wards stand.*" A pause. "*For the wards are not walls. They are a promise, kept in fire.*"

"You see?" Sorrel said to the galleries, and her voice carried to the highest terraces without effort. "A promise kept in fire. Our people fed those fires for nine hundred years. Nine hundred years of climbing to the ward-stones in the dead of winter, of singing the old sequences until our throats bled, of dying on the heights when the storms came wrong. And what did we ask?" She looked at Theo, and her eyes were terrible in their gentleness. "Salt. Iron. Seed. Enough to live. That is all the covenant ever asked of your cities, and for nine hundred years they paid it, because they liked living behind our fire."

"And then," Maera said, rising, "a generation ago, the tithes stopped."

"They didn't just stop," someone called from the galleries—a young male voice, raw with old anger. "Ask her why they stopped. Ask the human why the ward-stones went cold in a single season, why the dark-things came down the western slopes, why we buried forty of our own in one spring!"

"Because a Dunmore broke faith," Maera said, and the words fell into the silence like stones into a well. "The Covenant-Bearer of that generation. A woman of your free cities, human, sworn keeper of the treaty-road, trusted above all others to carry the tithe and speak for both peoples. And in the space of a single moon she emptied the ward-vaults, cut the tithe-lines, and vanished. When our keepers climbed the heights to learn what had happened, they found the covenant-stone above Highmoor defaced and the fires gone out. Forty of ours died that year holding the passes bare-handed against what came down from the ice." Her voice did not rise; it lowered instead, until the whole hold leaned in to hear it. "Your cities called us liars when we came asking. Called us beasts making excuses. Then Malvane's men started paying gold for wolf-pelts, and your cities stopped calling us anything at all."

Wren watched Theo's face across the fire, and what she saw there frightened her more than any inquisitor's crossbow.

It wasn't denial. That was what frightened her. The human had gone white to the lips, and their hands had risen halfway as if to frame some protest, and then simply—stopped, hanging in the smoky air, empty. Theo's mouth worked once, twice. And then the smuggler said, in a voice stripped down to bare boards:

"Dunmore. You're certain it was a Dunmore."

"It is written," said Harrow, speaking for the first time. His voice was low and gravel-heavy, like a rockslide heard through a wall. He nodded at the Codex. "In your own book. The breach-page records the name of the breaker. Look."

And Theo looked. Wren watched the human's finger trace down the page, watched it stop, watched everything in that face rearrange itself around something new and unbearable. The fire popped. The galleries were absolutely silent, hundreds of wolf-folk waiting, and Theo Dunmore stood alone on the Elders' Terrace with a century-old prophecy in their hands and read aloud, barely above a whisper:

"*The pact was broken by Maren Dunmore, Covenant-Bearer, in the year the tithe failed.*"

"My mother," Theo said. Nobody needed it explained. "My mother's name was Maren."

The silence afterward had weight and texture. Wren felt it settle over the hold like snowfall. Around her, the crowd's stillness curdled into something harder; she saw ears flatten, saw lips lift from teeth along the upper galleries, saw old grief remembering that it was also rage. Someone began a low growl that spread outward through the terraces like a crack spreading through lake ice.

"She was there," Theo said, to no one, to the fire. "When it shattered. She *did* it. All those years—all those stories about her runs, her routes—" The human pressed the heel of a hand against their eyes. "She taught me the mountains myself. Every pass. Every cache. And she never—" The voice failed, gathered itself, failed again. "She never once said."

"Would it have mended the wards, do you think?" Maera asked, merciless as frost. "If she had said? Would knowing have warmed the stones?"

"No." Theo lowered the hand. Their eyes were wet but level. "No. It wouldn't have."

Harrow rose then, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and the growling in the galleries died away, because when Harrow stood, everyone listened. He came around the fire until he stood close enough to Theo that Wren's hackles rose on instinct—close enough to smell the fear-sour on the human's skin, close enough to end this with one motion of one hand.

"We sent Wren to find what was stolen," he said. "She found it. She found it in the pack of a smuggler named Dunmore, walking a road that a Dunmore walked when she betrayed us, carrying a book our dead bled for." He let that stand in the smoke. "The Codex will stay in Thornfell. That is not a request. As for you—" his amber gaze did not waver, "—the elders will consider what is owed. Whether a debt can be collected from a child for a parent's theft is a question older than I am. Until it is answered, you sleep under guard, you eat what prisoners eat, and you do not walk these galleries alone."

"Harrow," Wren started—

"Enough, cub. You did well. Better than well." Something flickered behind his sternness—pride, quickly buried. "But the road you chose does not become safe because you chose it."

Guards flanked Theo—two young wolves with winter-pale eyes who took up positions with the unhurried menace of professionals. The human did not resist. Theo only gathered the last look of the fire, and the last look of the Codex lying closed among the blackened stones—home now, or something like it—and followed the guards up the spiral ramp with their shoulders bent like someone carrying water a very long way.

At the top of the terrace, before the galleries swallowed them, Theo paused. Turned back. Found Wren's eyes across the firelit distance, and mouthed something the hold was too loud with murmur for Wren to hear—but she read the shape of it easily enough.

*I didn't know.*

* * *

They gave Theo a chamber off the third gallery, dry and warm, hung with cured hides, with a barred vent that looked out onto nothing but rock. A prisoner's comfort, but comfort nonetheless. Wren brought the evening meal herself, over the objections of the kitchen elders, and set it down inside the door: smoked fish, dark bread, a clay cup of the resin-sweet tea.

Theo sat on the edge of the sleeping-bench, and in the moss-light their face looked carved out of tiredness.

"She used to sing me a song," Theo said, unprompted, staring at the cup without lifting it. "When I was small. A counting song about ward-stones—nine stones, eight stones—I thought it was nonsense. Just a rhyme mothers make." A hollow almost-laugh. "She was singing me the litany, Wren. The ward-keeping sequence. She'd broken the pact and she sang the keeping-song to her kid like a lullaby. What does a person do with a guilt like that?"

Wren stood in the doorway and did not answer, because she did not know, and because answering felt like a mercy she hadn't yet decided this human deserved.

"I need to see the breach-page again," Theo went on. "All of it. There was more writing under her name—smaller, cramped, like it was added later. I couldn't read it in the firelight, and Harrow isn't going to hand me the book." The amber eyes lifted, and there was something new in them that hadn't been there on the mountain: not the vertigo Wren had seen in the pass, but a direction. "But you've already carried it once. And you're the one they trust to run the galleries."

"You're asking me to steal our own covenant-book back out of our own hold," Wren said, "for a Dunmore."

"I'm asking you to help me find out why my mother did it." Theo's voice dropped, and the moss-light made shadows pool in the hollows of their face. "Because the way Maera tells it, a woman emptied the ward-vaults out of greed and vanished. But I knew her. And whoever she was—whatever she was—she was not that. Somebody made her break it, Wren. Somebody *made* her. And if I don't learn who and how before Malvane's inquisitors finish climbing these mountains, then everything she paid, and everything your forty died for, gets paid all over again—by both of us."

Outside, somewhere deep in the mountain, a bell began to toll the changing of the watch, and Wren stood in the doorway a long moment, torn between the hold that had raised her and the stranger who had fallen into her life out of a snowstorm carrying a stolen god.

Then she slipped inside and barred the door behind her.
