## 5. My Mother's Road

The route began where the maps stopped.

Theo had known that since childhood—had watched their mother roll up her charts at the guildhouse table and tuck a second, unmarked strip of oilskin into the lining of her coat, as if the true road were something shameful, something to be hidden even from the family that lived by it. *Maps are for people who want to be found,* she used to say, tapping Theo's nose with one ink-stained finger. *The good roads are for people who want to arrive.* She had never explained what she meant. She had died before Theo was old enough to ask properly, and the question had gone underground like everything else about Sera Dunmore, waiting in the dark with its roots wrapped around Theo's heart.

Now, with Wren's grey eyes watching from the tree line and Malvane's hunters somewhere below them on the pass, Theo stood at the place where the maps stopped and understood, at last, what their mother had been hiding.

It was not a smugglers' path. Smugglers' paths were practical things—goat-tracks widened by generations of greedy feet, blind trails behind waterfalls, culverts under the old king's highway. This was none of those. This was a road. It descended from the shoulder of Thornfell in long, deliberate switchbacks, cut into the living stone, and every few hundred paces a pillar of granite rose at its edge, carved with spirals worn shallow by centuries of weather. Wolf-work. Old wolf-work, older than the covenant itself if Wren's sharp intake of breath meant anything.

"You said this was your mother's route," Wren said quietly. Her hand had gone to the knife at her hip, though her voice held not threat but awe. "You said she ran contraband here."

"She did." Theo touched one of the pillars. The stone was warm beneath their palm, faintly, impossibly warm, like a sleeping animal's flank. "I think I was wrong about which cargo."

They went down together, because there was no going back, and because Wren had sworn—with visible reluctance and a great deal of teeth—to see the Covenant-Bearer to whatever end the Codex demanded. The road took them through a hanging valley where the pines grew so thick the daylight came down green as sea-glass, past cairns stacked in shapes Theo almost recognized, past a streambed where the water ran clear over stones veined with silver. Twice Theo felt it: a prickle along the spine, a sense of being *remembered* by the landscape, as though the mountain were turning an old ledger page and finding a familiar name.

The cache, when they reached it, was hidden exactly the way their mother hid everything—in plain sight, marked by something too ordinary to look twice at.

A shrine. A small roadside shrine such as farmers built all across the free cities: a niche in the rock, a chipped figure of some hearth-saint whose face had weathered away, a shelf for offerings. Moss had taken the whole of it. But when Theo knelt and pressed their thumb into the saint's blank face—the way their mother had once pressed her thumb into wax seals, into Theo's palm to make her swear, into everything—the stone swung inward on a hinge greased within living memory.

Cold air breathed out. The smell of cedar, of paper, of a house that no longer existed.

"Theo." Wren's voice, low. "What is this?"

"My mother," Theo said, and their voice cracked on the word, "was better at hiding than anyone ever knew."

* * *

Inside was a smuggler's trooper's dream—a strongroom cut into the mountain's root, dry as a crypt, lined with shelves. Coin enough to buy a district. Silk, spice, uncut garnet. Theo barely glanced at any of it. What mattered lay on a writing desk in the center of the room, arranged with terrible neatness: a bundle of letters tied in blue ribbon, a journal bound in wolf-hide, and a folded cloak that Theo knew instantly, gut-deep, because they had buried it eleven years ago.

They sat down at their dead mother's desk and opened the journal first, because letters could wait and the truth could not.

The handwriting was hers. That alone nearly undid them—the confident leftward slant, the way she crossed her *t*'s like little swords. Theo had spent years learning forgery under Corren's tutelage, years copying hands, and still nothing they had ever faked looked half so alive as these pages did.

*I have carried the Vellum Codex for nine years,* the first entry read. *Nine years of reading my own name in a dead man's book, and tonight I finally understand why it is written there. I am the Covenant-Bearer. God help me.*

Theo read on. The entries came slowly at first, then faster, a woman's life unspooling in ink: the terms of the covenant laid out in her careful script—the wolf-folk tended the mountain wards, the wards kept the free cities safe from the things beyond the high passes, and in exchange the cities gave Thornfell grain, salt, iron, and open gates. A pact sealed in blood at a standing stone above Highmoor. A pact renewed each generation by whoever bore the Codex.

And then, dated the winter Theo was born:

*Malvane has my son.*

The next lines were blotched. Theo's vision blurred too, and they let it.

*He does not know what he holds. He thinks the babe is only leverage against a smuggler. He does not know the child is the next Bearer, that the Codex names him before his birth as it named me. If I refuse him, he will kill Theo and take the book, and the covenant will break anyway—broken by violence, unrenewed, and the wards will fail in a decade or two regardless. If I give him the Codex, he seals the wards forever with the wolf-folk shut outside them, and everything my line has guarded dies quietly while the cities sleep safe and stupid.*

*There is a third way. There is always a third way; Mother taught me that. Break the pact myself. Renewal refused is not the same as renewal stolen. The wards will weaken slowly instead of failing all at once—I can buy thirty years, forty, time enough for Theo to grow, for a new Bearer to be found. The price is written plain in the old tongue and I will pay it: the Bearer who breaks the covenant forfeits her name from the memory of everyone she loves.*

*Corren will forget me. The guild will forget me. My child will grow up thinking his mother was a stranger who died on the passes, because that is what the magic requires—that is what I am buying his life WITH. He must never know me well enough to grieve me. God forgive me. I am doing this so that someone else, someday, will not have to.*

*If you are reading this, Theo—if the road brought you here the way it brought me—then it has come back around, and I am sorry, my love, I am so sorry. I hoped you would be spared. I hoped the debt ended with me. Debts written are debts that can be rewritten, the Codex says, but it does not say they can be forgiven.*

Theo set the journal down very carefully, as though it might bruise.

So much made sense now that it hurt to look at directly. The way their mother had kept them at arm's length even as a child—not cold, never cold, but careful, as if handling something she was afraid to hold too tight. The way she'd taught them the roads with a fierce urgency that outstripped any smuggler's pragmatism. The way Corren had gone strange and quiet whenever her name came up, mourning someone without knowing whom, the way a body aches in a limb that is no longer there. The guildmaster hadn't merely lost a friend. He had lost a friend and been robbed of the grief itself, a wound with the memory of hurting cut away.

"They told us she broke faith with the wolf-folk," Wren said from the doorway. She had read over Theo's shoulder—of course she had; wolves were not polite about other people's grief. Her ears were flat, her tail utterly still. "My elders say a Dunmore shattered the wards. They say it like a curse."

"She did shatter them." Theo's throat was raw. "To save my life. Malvane held me hostage—he's wanted the wards sealed since before I could walk, Wren. Everything—the inquisitors, the hunt, Lowmark burning—it started the day I was born."

"Then your mother bought thirty years," Wren said softly, "and the thirty years are up."

"Yes."

They sat in silence for a moment, human and wolf, in a room full of a dead woman's secrets. Then Theo untied the ribbon and drew out the top letter, addressed in their mother's hand: *For my child, if the road is kind.* Inside, a single page.

*My love. If you are reading this, then the Codex has found you, and you know now what I paid and why. Do not hate me for it. Do not hate yourself. And listen to me: the rite demands a price equal to the breach. When you reforge the covenant—and you will, because you are mine and you are stubborn—you will be asked to pay what I paid. Memories. Everyone you love, wiped clean as slate. I chose it for you once. You will choose it again for everyone. That is what a Bearer is: the one who pays so no one else must.*

*But hear the rest, the part the elders will not tell you: the price is paid, not destroyed. The Codex keeps what it takes. Nothing written is unwritten; it is only moved to another page. Find the book after. Read the blank places. What was yours can be carried back across, story by story, if someone loves you enough to tell them—and someone will. Someone always does. That is the covenant's oldest clause, older than the wards, older than the wolf-folk: no debt stands alone. There is always a witness.*

*Go north to the covenant-stone. Trust the wolf-child; trust is the whole of the magic. And Theo—when you stand at the stone and it asks for its price, do not flinch. I didn't. Paying for the people you love is the last and greatest thing you can do for them.*

*All my love, across the forgetting,*
*Mother*

Theo folded the letter with hands that shook and did not quite feel like their own.

Above them, far above, a horn sounded from the pass—one long note, then two short. Inquisitor-sign. They had perhaps an hour, maybe less.

Wren was already moving, nostrils flared toward the sound. "We run for the stone tonight, or we don't run at all."

Theo rose, tucked the letters inside their coat over their heart, and took up the wolf-hide journal—their inheritance, their mother's whole hidden life. At the mouth of the cache they paused, looking back at the neat desk, the patient shelves, the shrine with its faceless saint.

"I'm coming back for you," they whispered—not to the room, but to the woman it remembered. "Every word. I swear it on the road."

Then they stepped out into the falling dusk, and began to climb.
