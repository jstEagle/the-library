## 6. The Usurper's Summons

Malvane did not sleep in the hours before the parley. He never slept before a negotiation, though he had long ago stopped calling it nerves. It was, he told himself, simply respect for the work—like a surgeon sharpening his blades by candlelight, turning each one over in his hands until he knew its weight and its edge and the precise angle at which it would bite.

He stood at the window of the governor's residence in Lowmark's occupied quarter, watching the smoke of the town he had taken three days ago still smoldering against the dawn. The fires had been necessary. He regretted them the way a man regrets amputating a limb: not with guilt, exactly, but with the cold acknowledgment that the body was healthier without the rot. Lowmark had harbored smugglers. Smugglers carried things across the passes that should have stayed buried. And somewhere in those mountains, in some smuggler's grubby hands, was a book that could end the oldest war in the world—one way or the other.

"Your Excellency." Captain Serevan at the door, armor freshly polished, which Malvane noted and approved of. Appearances were arguments too. "The courier confirms. The Dunmore child crossed into Thornfell territory two nights ago. Our trackers lost them at the Grey Stair."

"Wolves took up their trail there?"

"Yes. Whether as hunters or rescuers, we couldn't say."

"They'll be rescuers." Malvane turned from the window. "The wolf-folk have waited a generation for someone to carry that book home. They'll guard the bearer like a relic." He smiled faintly. "Which is precisely why I must speak to the bearer myself. Relics can be argued with. Fanatics cannot—but Theo Dunmore is no fanatic. The guild records describe a careful child. Careful children do arithmetic."

"And if the arithmetic goes against us?"

"Then I have an army, Captain, and they have a mountain. But mountains are expensive, and I would rather buy than bleed." He drew on his gloves, grey kid leather, immaculate. "Have the table set in the open courtyard. Two chairs, facing the peaks. I want the child to see Thornfell while we talk. Let them feel how close the answer is to their hand—and how far."

* * *

The courtyard of the old tollhouse sat on the last shelf of habitable stone before the pass narrowed into wolf-country. Malvane had chosen it for its symmetry: behind him, the road down to the smoking lowlands and his waiting legions; ahead, the grey wall of Thornfell rising into cloud. Between the two, a small round table, two chairs, a pitcher of watered wine, and a plate of honey-cakes that had cost him three days' supply train and were worth every wagon.

He sat alone. That was the point. Emperors who arrived flanked by guards were making a confession about their fear. Malvane arrived with nothing but a bookless table and an open face, because he wanted the Dunmore child to understand that this meeting was not a trap. Traps were for catching things. He intended to give something away.

They came at midmorning, out of the treeline where the shadow began: two figures, one human and slight, moving with the low-shouldered caution of someone raised in attics and culverts; the other tall and grey-wrapped, a wolf-folk scout with amber eyes that caught the light like struck flint. Inquisitors hidden along the ridge reported the human's face—young, sharp-boned, wary—and Malvane felt the particular pleasure of a plan assembling itself.

"Theo Dunmore," he said, when they had stopped at what they judged a safe distance. He did not rise. Rising was for supplicants. "You've cost me two hundred soldiers and six weeks of weather. I'd call that a promising start to a career."

"I haven't started anything," the young smuggler said. Their voice was steady, which pleased him more than he showed. "I'm passing through."

"You're carrying a book through my war." He gestured to the opposite chair. "Sit. Eat something—you look half-starved, and I need you thinking clearly. What I'm about to propose deserves your best arithmetic."

Wren—the scout, according to his informants—made a sound low in her throat, half growl, half warning. Theo glanced at her, then at the chair, then at the peaks beyond, doing exactly what Malvane had hoped: weighing distances, exits, the reach of hidden crossbows. Then they sat. Not because they trusted him. Because careful children always wanted to hear the whole offer before refusing it. Refusal, after all, was also information.

"Honey-cake?" Malvane asked, and slid the plate across.

* * *

He talked for a quarter of an hour before touching the book at all. This was craft. He told Theo Dunmore about the free cities' debts, the guilds rotting from within, the border towns that burned every time the old pact flickered and failed. He told them about the night his own garrison at Coldmere had been overrun—not by wolves, he was careful to say, the wolf-folk kept faith better than anyone credited, but by the chaos the broken covenant bred, the lawlessness that pooled in the mountain dark like meltwater. He told them about the refugees on the Lowmark road, and he watched the young smuggler's jaw tighten, because Theo had walked that road too, recently, and had seen what he'd seen.

Only then, when the ground was prepared, did he lean forward and say the word.

"Amnesty."

He let it sit in the air between them like a coin on a table.

"Full pardon. For you, for whatever remains of the Highmoor guild, for every name on Corren's rolls. The guild restored under charter—your charter, if you want it. You're the last apprentice of a dead tradition, Theo Dunmore. I'm offering to make you the first master of a living one. Roads reopened. Passes licensed. Your people fed and legal and safe." He paused, precisely. "All I ask is the Codex."

Theo's hands had gone still around the cup of watered wine. "And if I don't?"

"Then I take it from Thornfell's ashes, and the guild stays dead, and the cities burn one by one until someone gives me what I would have given them freely today." He said it without heat, the way one reports rainfall. "But I think you will give it to me. Do you know why? Because you've read it. You know what it asks."

"The rite requires a price," Theo said carefully.

"It required one," Malvane corrected gently. "A century ago, when the wards were first raised, the price was paid by people who understood what they were buying. Peace. Wards strong enough to keep the mountain-folk and the valley-folk from each other's throats. A generation of quiet." He spread his hands. "Then your mother broke it, and everything since has been interest compounding on her default. Every dead town, every burned caravan, every orphan on the Lowmark road—that's the debt she left you. I'm offering to close the account."

"You want to seal the wards," Theo said. "Forever."

"Against the wolf-folk, yes." He met the scout's flint-colored stare without apology. "Not out of hatred, scout. Out of exhaustion. Two peoples who cannot live side by side, separated by a wall neither remembers how to lower without bloodshed. I have read the histories. Every attempt at reunion ends in massacre—sometimes ours, sometimes yours. Forever-sealed wards mean forever-separated peoples, and forever-separated peoples mean forever peace. No more breaches. No more covenants to break. No more children paying their parents' debts."

He watched that land. Watched it land hard, because it was designed to. He had learned long ago that the most seductive lie is not the flattering one—it is the merciful one. Power corrupts only the stupid; the clever are corrupted by compassion, by the whisper that says *you could spare them all this*. He leaned in now, voice dropping to something almost intimate, almost kind.

"No one else has to pay, Theo. That's the difference between us and the prophecy. The Codex wants another sacrifice—it always wants another sacrifice, that's what covenants are, debt passed hand to hand down the generations like a cursed heirloom. Your mother paid. Now the book comes for you. And someday it would come for whoever you love." He let that hang. He knew—from Corren's reports, from the guild's gossiping ledgers—exactly who Theo loved. "Refuse me, and you pay it. Give me the book, and no one pays ever again. The debt dies with the covenant. Isn't that what you actually want? Not the reforging. The ending. An end to owing."

For a long moment the courtyard held only wind off the peaks and the distant murmur of his encampment below. Theo Dunmore looked at the honey-cakes untouched on the plate, at the grey massif of Thornfell, at the scout whose people had every reason to hate them and had sheltered them anyway. Malvane watched the arithmetic move behind those careful eyes and permitted himself, inwardly, the smallest satisfaction. The offer was good. That was the horror of it. He had made it genuinely good, because the best cages are built of real gold.

"The wards sealed forever," Theo said slowly. "That means the wolf-folk can never come down. Never trade, never speak, never—" They stopped.

"Never be betrayed again either," Malvane said softly. "Consider what your mother's generation did to them. Consider what humans did to *you*, selling you to my inquisitors for scraps of survival. Walls protect both sides from themselves." He rose, finally, signaling the audience's shape: generous, unhurried, concluded. "Three days. Meet my herald at the Grey Stair with the Codex, or don't. If you don't, I cross the pass, and everything after that stops being negotiable." He inclined his head to the scout as well—a courtesy that cost him nothing and meant everything, showing the wolf-folk watcher that he saw her, that her people were in his arithmetic too. "Scout. When your elders weigh this, remind them: I am the first human in a century to offer them certainty instead of promises. Certainty is worth more. Promises are what got their wards broken."

They withdrew up the slope together, the human and the wolf, two silhouettes climbing toward the cloud-line. Malvane remained standing in the courtyard a while longer, alone with the untouched honey-cakes and the cold bright air.

Captain Serevan emerged from the archway. "Do you believe they'll take it, Excellency?"

Malvane considered the question honestly, which was rare for him. "They'll want to. That's more dangerous than wanting power or gold—wanting mercy makes people stupid in the most honorable ways." He picked up one honey-cake, examined it, set it back down. "But they'll go up that mountain and ask the wolf-folk what they think. And the wolf-folk will tell them about the sealing. About what forever means, locked behind stone while the world forgets you." His mouth curved slightly. "So it becomes a question of whose story is crueler. Mine says: seal the wound. The Codex says: pay the blood-price and heal it. And there's one thing the child hasn't counted yet."

"Which is?"

"That I already know what the price is." He pulled on his gloves again, smoothing each finger. "Corren told my inquisitors everything the guild ever recorded about the covenant-stone—including the rite's original terms. I know exactly what the reforging will demand of Theo Dunmore. Which means, Captain, that whichever way the child chooses, I hold the ledger." He turned toward the door, toward the maps and the waiting army and the mountain. "Prepare the vanguard. If they refuse, we march in four days. And send a rider to Corren. Tell him his amnesty stands—provided the guildmaster's final delivery arrives on schedule."

Below in the valley, the first campfires of the evening were lighting, hundreds of them, patient as stars. Above, the clouds over Thornfell had thickened, and somewhere past them, on a shelf of ruined stone older than any kingdom, a covenant waited to be paid for or killed.

Malvane went inside to write letters. He was, after all, a man who believed in closing accounts.
