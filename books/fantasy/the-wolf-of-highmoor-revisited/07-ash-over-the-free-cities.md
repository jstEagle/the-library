## 7. Ash Over the Free Cities

Theo smelled Lowmark burning before they saw it.

They had been three days in the high passes above the border town, Wren moving ahead of them through the scree like a rumor, mapping the routes their mother had once carried salt and secrets along. The plan had been simple: find which passes remained open, mark where the wolf-folk's old ward-stones still stood, and be gone before Malvane's outriders reached the mountains. Smuggler's work. Theo had done it a hundred times with cargo that weighed less than what this knowledge now weighed.

Then the wind shifted, and brought them smoke.

Not hearth-smoke, not the clean bite of a forge or the sweetness of kilns. This was thick and oily, threaded with something sweeter underneath—thatch, tar, and Theo's stomach turned as it named the third thing: burned grain stores, an entire harvest going up in a single afternoon.

"Lowmark," Wren said. She had stopped on the ridge ahead, ears pricked forward, her grey fur rimmed gold by the low sun. Her voice was flat in the way Theo had learned meant she was holding something down hard. "That's Lowmark."

"You can't know that from here."

"I can smell the tanneries." She looked back at them, and her yellow eyes held no comfort at all. "You told me the tanneries were on the river side of town. The wind's carrying it over everything else."

Theo stood very still on the broken slope, the Codex heavy against their ribs where it rode in its sling beneath their coat. They had known, in the abstract way one knows that winter follows autumn, that refusing Malvane would cost something. They had imagined armies, sieges, the great chessboard moves of lords and usurpers. They had not imagined the smell of a town's whole year of bread burning.

"We should go around," they heard themselves say. "We're two people. Two—people and—" They gestured at Wren, helplessly. "If we're caught—"

Wren was already moving down the slope.

"Wren—"

"They'll have driven them toward the passes," she said over her shoulder. "That's how it's done. You burn the low ground and the people run uphill, and then you take the high ground behind them at your leisure. Your mother's roads run straight into it. If there are refugees on those trails, I want to know before their riders do." A beat. "And if you'd rather not come, Dunmore, wait here. But you opened the book. So I think you'll come."

Theo came.

* * *

The first refugees found them rather than the other way around—a family materializing out of the dusk on the switchback below, a woman hauling a handcart with one wheel missing, two children asleep upright against sacks of whatever they'd grabbed when the screaming started. The woman saw Theo and made a sound like a wounded animal and nearly turned back down the mountain before Theo raised empty hands and said, in the flattest smuggler's voice they owned, "Roads are clear to the second cairn. After that, ice. Keep left at the split."

The woman stared. Then she nodded, once, and kept climbing, pulling her children past as though Theo might infect them.

"Charming," Wren murmured. She had pulled her hood deep and wrapped her muzzle in scarves until she was just another traveler bundled against the cold—one of the small mercies of a world where everyone was too frightened to look closely at anyone.

"She's right not to trust me."

"She's right to keep walking." Wren's eyes tracked up the trail, counting shapes in the failing light. "There will be more. There are always more."

There were always more. By full dark the pass had become a river of them—farmers with bloodied hands from dragging neighbors out of collapsing houses, a priestess of the Hearth carrying someone else's baby because its mother hadn't come out of the burning quarter, old men who had stayed to fight with hay-forks and now climbed with the particular silence of the gutted. Theo stood at the fork in the road for hours, directing them left toward the sheltered route, and every face that passed was a ledger entry in a debt they hadn't known they were signing.

A boy of perhaps ten stopped in front of them. He had soot on his cheeks except where tears had cut it clean.

"Is it true the wolves did it?" he asked.

Theo's throat closed. Behind him, Wren went very still.

"No," Theo said. "It was men. Men in black and silver. The wolves have been gone from these hills since before you were born."

"The man on the horse said the wolves broke the wards and let the fire in."

"The man on the horse," Theo said carefully, "is lying to you. It's what he does."

The boy searched their face with the terrible directness of children, then ran on after his family. When Theo turned, Wren was looking at them with an expression they couldn't read.

"You didn't have to do that," she said quietly.

"Yes, I did."

* * *

Wren had smelled death before. She had been four when the hunters came to the outer dens, and she remembered her mother pressing her into a root-cellar while the screams happened somewhere far above, and being told afterward that remembering was a gift some wolves were given and some were spared. She had spent her life deciding she was one of the given ones.

Lowmark tested the decision.

She left Theo at the fork—the human faces, the flinches, the way even desperate people gave her wide berth once the scarves slipped—and went down alone through the pine shadow toward the burning edge of things, because the elders had sent her to count, and because counting was all she could bear to do.

What she counted: the vanguard had not bothered with a siege. Malvane's soldiers had come at dawn, taken the wall at the river gate almost as an afterthought, and then spent the day doing something worse than war. She watched from a granite shelf above the east fields as torch-parties moved through the outlying farms with methodical calm, driving survivors ahead of them toward the mountain roads—not slaughtering everyone, but choosing, the way you cull a herd. Leaving enough alive to carry the story. *The wolves broke the wards. The wolves did this.* She could hear the soldiers saying it to the ones they spared, patient as schoolteachers.

And then, near midnight, she saw the thing she would carry the rest of her life.

Firelight moved wrong on the western wall—not looting-fire but fighting-fire, steel ringing on steel—and she crept close enough along the goat-tracks to see that a handful of Lowmark's militia had never surrendered. Maybe forty of them, farmers and tanners and one enormous woman who swung a ship's anchor chain like a skipping rope, holding the wall-walk against a black-and-silver company that had expected no fight at all.

And among them, on the battlements, wolves.

Six of them, maybe seven—scouts from the southern dens, Wren realized, the young hotheads who had ignored the elders' counsel and gone to watch the human towns burn, and who had apparently found they could not simply *watch*. One of them fought beside the woman with the anchor chain, shoulder to shoulder, taking a spear-butt across the ribs to keep a blade off her back. Another crouched over a fallen militiaman, shielding him with his own body until two humans dragged their comrade clear. None of them wore anything that marked a den or a pack. They were just wolves, dying—if the shape of the fight told true—in a human town, for humans who would be told by morning that wolves had burned it.

Wren lay flat on the cold stone and shook.

She thought of the elders in the warm dark of Thornfell, reciting the grievance like a litany: *they broke faith, they took our book, they sealed the passes.* All of it true. All of it true, and none of it large enough to hold what she was seeing—that faith was not a thing broken once, a century ago, by people long dead. Faith was this. It was a wolf bleeding on a tanner's wall next to a woman with an anchor chain, both of them defending people who would curse them by sunrise.

She thought about the Codex under Theo Dunmore's coat. She thought about the word the elders used for it: *stolen.* And she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the book had never really belonged to anyone. It belonged to whoever was willing to pay for it.

Below her, the wall finally fell. The wolves broke away south into the trees—she counted four living, then five—and the militia scattered into the hills, and Lowmark's last resistance guttered out the way a lamp gutters, ordinary and total. The big woman with the chain was the last off the wall, walking backward, daring the whole black-and-silver line to follow her.

Wren memorized her face. Someone would need to remember it correctly.

* * *

Theo was waiting at the tree-line when Wren came back up, and whatever was in Wren's face made them stand instead of ask.

"There were wolves on the wall," Wren said. "Fighting for the town. Four got out." Her voice cracked on the number, and she hated it, and let it crack anyway. "Your people and mine, dying in the same ditch, and by tomorrow every survivor in that column will believe we lit the fire."

Theo sat down slowly on a fallen log, as though their legs had been quietly removed. For a long moment they said nothing, and Wren watched them do the arithmetic she had watched humans do all week—the same sum, different numbers.

"I refused Malvane," Theo said at last. "In my head, I mean. I've been telling myself I refused him. Standing in the pass feeling noble while other people paid the bill." They looked up, and their eyes were red-rimmed and terribly steady. "He sacked a town to make a point about a book. And the point is landing, Wren. That boy believed him. Their priestess will believe him. In a month, the free cities won't just fear Malvane—they'll *hate* the wolf-folk, and any covenant we reforge will be born already drowned."

"Then we make the truth louder than his lie."

"With what? We're two—"

"We're not." Wren pulled the folded scrap of cloth from inside her jacket—the banner-scrap she'd torn from a dead soldier's standard, black and silver—and dropped it on the log between them. Then she told Theo about the four wolves running south, about the anchor-chain woman walking backward off the wall, about what faith looked like when it wasn't a hundred years old. "The elders think the covenant is a book. Malvane thinks it's a weapon. But it isn't either one. It's *this*—people standing where they shouldn't have to stand, together. The book is just... the receipt."

Theo picked up the scrap of black and silver, turned it over, and something settled in their face—the look Wren had begun to recognize, the smuggler finding the load path no one else had thought to try.

"Covenant-Stone above Highmoor," Theo said slowly. "The rite needs witnesses. Two peoples. That's what the old text says—*two voices, freely given.*" They looked up, and for the first time since the guildhouse burned, there was no vertigo in their eyes. Only direction. "Four wolves running south. Forty militia scattered in these hills. A woman with an anchor chain who owes her life to one of them. Wren—what if we don't carry the truth to Highmoor?"

Wren's ears came forward.

"What if we carry Highmoor's proof *with* us? Every survivor we can gather. Every witness. Let Malvane's lie walk into the reforging and die there in front of everyone." Theo stood, and the Codex shifted under their coat like a heartbeat. "My mother's roads run all through these mountains. I know every cache, every shelter, every blind trail. We start tonight. We gather them."

Wren looked north, toward Thornfell and its warm dark and its elders who would call this madness. She looked south, toward the smoke and the four wolves running home with wounds and a story no elder had authorized.

"The elders will refuse you," she said.

"Probably."

"And mine will refuse me."

"So we don't ask permission." Theo held out their hand—human fingers, blunt-nailed, steady. "Debts written are debts that can be rewritten. You said your scouts on that wall already rewrote a page of it. Come write the rest."

Far below, Lowmark burned on, a wound of orange in the black valley. Above it, on a cold ridge between two peoples' griefs, Wren took the hand.

By dawn they had eleven refugees, one deserter from Malvane's own ranks who claimed he'd seen what the vanguard did to the tanneries and couldn't stomach the flag anymore—and, limping out of the pines with an arrow-scarred flank and defiance rolling off him like heat, one of the four wolves from the wall of Lowmark, who looked at Theo's outstretched hand the way one looks at deep water.

"My name is Garrick," he said. "And I have something to show you before I decide whether to trust a Dunmore."

From his coat he drew a scrap of parchment, scorched at the edges, bearing half a seal in black wax.

Theo went white.

It matched the broken seal on the strongbox that had started everything.
