## 8. The Reforging

The covenant-stone had been beautiful once. Theo could see the ghost of that beauty in what remained—a ring of white granite taller than two men, its surface carved with interlocking spirals that had once caught the moonlight and thrown it back in silver threads. Now the stone stood broken at its crown, split by lightning or by malice, its spirals choked with lichen and the droppings of birds that had no reverence for what had been sworn here. Wind came off the Highmoor crags in long, mournful pulls, carrying the smell of distant smoke. Lowmark was still burning, three valleys away. Theo could smell it on every breath.

They climbed the last stretch of the path with the Codex wrapped against their chest, and Wren moved ahead of them, her grey cloak blending with the granite, her ears—those subtle, wolfish points beneath her hood—turned always into the wind, listening for the thing they both feared most: the sound of inquisitor horns on the road below.

No horns came. Only wind, and the creak of the stone, and the far-off murmur of a war beginning without them.

"The elders sent words for the rite," Wren said, when they stood at last in the ring of broken granite. She unslung the satchel she had carried up from the den-holds and drew out its contents with the care of someone handling relics: a bowl of black iron, a twist of dried rowan, a phial of water drawn from the spring beneath Thornfell where, she said, the first pact had been sworn in blood that was freely given. "Freely given. That's the part the elders said matters most. My grandmother was there the night the covenant broke. She said the old magic doesn't take. It *asks*. And it asks for what the breach cost."

Theo set the Codex on the flat altar-stone at the ring's center. Their hands were steady. That surprised them. They had expected to be shaking by now.

"And what did the breach cost?"

Wren wouldn't look at them. "You already know."

"I want to hear you say it."

"The pact was sworn on blood freely given," Wren said quietly. "Your mother broke it with blood taken against the wolf-folk's will—taken in violence, in the den-holds, the night she came for the Codex. The magic remembers what was spent to break it. It will want the same coin back." Her amber eyes finally lifted. "It will want what you love."

The wind moved through the broken stone and made a sound like a low voice humming an old song.

* * *

They prepared the rite as Wren had been taught, though neither of them had ever seen it performed. Rowan burned in the iron bowl, its smoke thin and bitter. The spring water went into the spirals carved in the altar-stone, filling grooves worn shallow by a century of neglect until the old pattern glimmered whole again, silver-dark in the dying light. The Codex lay open to its final page, where Theo's name sat in ink that had not dried in a hundred years—ink that had waited, Theo understood now, the way a trap waits, or a door.

Theo thought about their mother's letters, folded against their heart. *They gave me a choice,* she had written, in a hand gone shaky at the end. *The covenant or you. I chose you. I have never once regretted it, and I have regretted it every single day of my life, and both of those things are true.* Theo had carried those words up the mountain the way one carries a wound—carefully, because it was theirs, and because it hurt.

The sun was going down behind the western crags, dragging long shadows across the moor. Somewhere below, in the free cities, people were barricading their doors against a war that had not yet reached them. Somewhere in Highmoor, Corren was doing whatever Corren did now—Theo refused to wonder. Somewhere, the people Theo loved were eating supper, arguing, laughing, living their small ordinary lives, entirely unaware that the price of their continuing to do so was being counted out on a mountaintop in rowan smoke.

"Theo." Wren's voice was careful. "Once it starts, it can't be stopped. The elders were clear. The magic takes what it names, and it names what it names, and no one—no one—has ever bargained it down."

"I know."

"Say it back to me. What it will ask."

Theo looked at their hands. Smuggler's hands, scarred at the knuckles, clever with knots and locks. Hands that had been held. That had held.

"Every memory I have of the people I love," they said. "All of them. Completely. As though they had never happened."

Wren's jaw tightened. In the last light her eyes were almost gold. "And you're certain."

"No," Theo said, and smiled, and found the smile was real. "But certain isn't what the rite asks for. It asks for *freely*. That I can do."

* * *

They began when the first star appeared.

Theo spoke the words of the covenant as the Codex gave them—old words, in an older tongue, that Wren whispered along beside them, correcting the shape of Theo's mouth with a patience that was almost tenderness. *Stone to stone. Breath to breath. What was sundered, let it bind.* The rowan smoke rose straight up in windless air, though the wind had not stopped; it bent around them now, as though the mountain itself had turned to listen.

The spring water in the carved spirals began to glow.

It was not a dramatic light. It was the light of the moon on snow, of a lamp in a far window, of the last coal in a dying fire—soft, and patient, and utterly without mercy. It climbed the broken granite in slow veins, and where it passed, the stone remembered itself. Theo watched a crack a century old knit closed like flesh. The split crown of the covenant-stone groaned and settled, and the interlocking spirals blazed silver from crown to root, and the air in the ring grew thick and warm and full of a presence that had no shape but had attention—vast, ancient, and turned wholly upon them.

*Who calls?* It was not a sound. It arrived in Theo's chest like a hand pressing on a door.

"Theo Dunmore," they said. "Covenant-Bearer. I call to reforge what was broken."

*The breach was paid in blood taken. The reforging must be paid in kind.*

"I know."

*You do not,* the presence said, and there was something in it that was almost sorrow. *No one ever does, until the counting.*

The light gathered. It rose off the stone in slow ribbons and hung in the air before Theo's face, and in it, Theo began to see things.

Not visions. *Memories.* Their own, laid out like goods on a smuggler's table.

Their mother's hands teaching them to tie a bowline, the knot forming under her fingers before their eyes. The taste of the honey-cakes she bought from the temple baker on feast days, and the way she always broke the first one and gave them the larger half. Corren's voice, rough and warm, the night he took them off the streets: *You're guild now, little shadow. Nobody's invisible to us.* The smell of the guildhouse kitchen. Merric laughing so hard he fell off the dock into the harbor. The weight of Wren's hand on their shoulder in the pass at Thornfell, when she had every reason to let the inquisitors take them and did not. Their mother's face in the last letter's memory—no, her face in life, tired and fierce at the door of their room, saying *whatever happens, you were worth everything, do you hear me, everything*—

"No," Theo whispered, and the memories kept coming, patient as water rising.

*This is the price,* the presence said. *Every bond of love. Every memory that binds you to another heart. The breach took a people's trust and a mother's choice; the reforging takes what you would choose, so that the choice is made freely and cannot be unmade. Do you offer it?*

Theo stood in the ring of blazing stone with their whole life laid out before them, every person they loved shining in the silver light, and understood at last what their mother had paid, and why she had never regretted it, and had regretted it every day. Both things true. Both things carried.

They thought about running. The thought was small and human and it passed through them like weather. Then they thought about Lowmark burning. About refugees on the passes. About Malvane's wards that would seal the wolf-folk behind the mountains forever, a whole people entombed so that one man need never be afraid. About the cycle—breach, debt, breach, debt—passed down like a smuggled cargo from hand to hand, from their mother's blood to theirs.

It stopped here. Tonight. With them.

"I offer it," Theo said. "Freely. All of it."

*Then bear witness,* the presence said, *and be the door.*

The memories began to leave.

It did not hurt, exactly. It was more like watching a ship sail out of harbor in fog—there, and there, and then a shape, and then nothing. Theo's mother's hands went first, and Theo reached for them and found their own hands empty, and then could not remember what they had been reaching for. Merric's laughter went like a lamp blown out. Corren's voice went, and with it the guildhouse, the kitchen, the smell of bread, the word *home*, which became suddenly a word with a hole in the middle of it. They felt each one go and felt, even as it went, the shape of its absence being written into them—the grief arriving before the love it mourned had fully faded, so that they wept for people they were, even in the weeping, ceasing to know.

Wren caught them when their knees buckled. She held them up, arms locked around them, her voice in their ear saying *I'm here, I'm here, I have you, Theo, I have you*—and Theo heard the name and did not know it, and clung to the voice anyway, because the voice was warm, and warmth was the last thing left.

The light flared.

The covenant-stone rang like a struck bell, one vast clear note that rolled down the mountain and out across the moor and through the passes and into the den-holds beneath Thornfell, where elders a hundred years silent lifted their heads. The wards woke. Theo felt them wake—felt, without knowing how they knew, the mountain's long sleep ending, old doors unsealing, a pact flowing silver and new through stone and root and blood.

Then the light was gone, and the stars were out, and the wind had returned, ordinary and cold.

Theo lay against Wren's shoulder, empty and light, like a strongbox with nothing in it.

"Is it done?" they asked. Their voice sounded strange to them. Everything sounded strange.

Wren was crying, silently, the way her people wept. "It's done," she said. "You did it. Theo—you did it."

"Theo," Theo repeated. "Is that my name?"

Below them, far down in the dark, the beacon-fires of Highmoor began to light, one after another, calling the free cities to war.
