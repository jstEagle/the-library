## 9. The Gates of Highmoor

**Wren**

The army came out of the dawn like a tide of iron, and Wren watched it from the ridge above the Highmoor road with her ears flat and her heart hammering a war-rhythm she had learned in the den-holds before she was old enough to hold a spear.

Malvane's host filled the valley floor as far as sight could reach—pikemen in ranks like planted forests, cavalry wings glinting where the rising sun caught their plates, and behind them, dragged by teams of oxen and groaning slaves, the siege engines. Great trebuchets on wheeled frames. Battering rams housed in roofs of green hide. A tower of timber and iron tall enough to look down from into the city's upper streets.

"Three thousand," said Old Sarn beside her, his muzzle gone grey around the edges. "Maybe more."

"And us?" Wren asked, though she knew.

"Eight hundred wolf-folk. Two thousand of the free cities' levies. Fishermen with billhooks. Smugglers with knives they'd rather use for cutting purses." He snorted. "It should not be close at all."

But it would be close, because of what stood between them and the gates: the covenant-stone's work, newly woken, running through the old ward-lines beneath the earth like blood returning to a numbed limb. Wren had felt it rekindle three nights past—a deep, singing warmth that had made every wolf-folk warrior in the camp stop mid-stride and lift their heads as one. The wards were alive again. The question no one spoke aloud was whether they would answer *them*.

The stranger had done it. The Dunmore. Wren had stood witness at the stone, had held the Codex open while Theo Dunmore paid whatever terrible price the rite demanded—and she had seen the moment the payment was taken. She had seen the light go out of Theo's eyes like a lamp shuttered, leaving the face empty and new, a stranger wearing a savior's skin.

She had wept then, quietly, while the magic sang. She did not fully understand why. She only knew that something precious had been carried up that mountain and left there, and that she alone seemed to grieve it.

Now she crouched at the ridge and watched Malvane's herald ride forward under a white banner, and she thought: *You don't know what he gave up so you could lose today.*

* * *

**Corren**

Guildmaster Corren stood on the wall of Highmoor with a smuggler's cutlass in his fist and thirty years of shame pressing on his shoulders like a wet cloak.

He had sold Theo Dunmore to the inquisitors. He had fed the guild's movements to Malvane's men month after month, name after name, because Corren had done the arithmetic every coward does—the lives of many against the life of one—and because he had always been very good at making the sums come out in his favor.

Then the guildhouse burned. Then the passes filled with refugees instead of gold. Then the world Corren had purchased with his betrayals came for its payment anyway.

And now here he stood, on a wall he had spent a lifetime smuggling goods *under*, commanding a company of gutter-thieves who had somehow become soldiers overnight, all because a person he had sold like cargo had climbed a mountain and bought them all back.

"You think they'll come straight at us?" muttered Vess, one-armed and grim, at his elbow.

"They'll come at the gates first," Corren said. "Test the wards. Then the engines." He looked down at the plain, where the herald's banner had turned back and the horns began to sound. "Pray to whatever you pray to that the mountain remembers whose side it's on."

The first wave hit at midmorning.

They came screaming across the frost-browned fields, ladders and hooks and scaling parties, and Corren watched the front rank reach the invisible line where the old wards ran—and watched the earth itself refuse them. It was not fire. It was not lightning. It was simpler and more terrible than either: the ground simply *ended* their momentum, as if the whole plain had tilted away, as if the air thickened to tar. Men stumbled and fell as though drunk. Ladders leaned and slid from walls that no ladder could bite. And along the battlements, the defenders—wolf-folk and human alike—began to shout, because they could feel it too, the deep hum in the stones under their boots, the mountain waking beneath the city like an old dog lifting its head.

"The wards are holding!" someone cried, and the cheer that went up nearly drowned the horns.

Corren did not cheer. He was watching the trebuchets being assembled beyond bowshot, and doing the arithmetic again—the only kind of sum he had ever been honest about.

* * *

**Captain Ilsa Merrin**

Ilsa Merrin had walked out of Lowmark with four hundred people and arrived at Highmoor with two hundred and sixty, and she had promised herself on the road that she would never count again. Counting was how you learned what refusal cost.

Now she stood on the gatehouse roof with a refugee's spear and a soldier's eye, watching Malvane's engines begin to throw.

The first stone rose in a high lazy arc and fell far short, plowing a furrow in the plain. The second fell closer. The third struck the outer ward-line and shattered—not broke, *shattered*—bursting apart in midair as if it had been thrown against a cliff face that wasn't there. A sound went through the army on the walls, half gasp and half prayer.

But the fourth stone came wrapped in something dark. Ilsa saw it clearly even at distance: a cage of black iron bound in sigils, swinging beneath the missile like a lantern. When it struck the ward-line, the air screamed. Not the stones—the *air*—a high tearing note that made her teeth ache and sent every wolf-folk warrior on the wall to their knees with their ears pressed flat.

"Ward-breakers," snarled Sarn, hauling himself upright, blood trickling from one ear. "He's brought ward-breakers."

The next stone got through. It took the corner off a granary near the east wall, and the dust cloud rose like a grey ghost over the city, and after that the stones came steadily, and the humming song of the wards began, note by note, to fray.

Ilsa found herself looking for the stranger—the quiet one, the Dunmore, who had come down from the mountain two days ago with empty eyes and a book full of blank pages. She spotted them at last near the west bastion, standing very still amid the chaos, watching the fraying light of the wards with an expression Ilsa couldn't read. Grief, maybe. Or the memory of grief, with the reason worn away.

Someone should ask them, Ilsa thought. Someone should ask what they knew, what they'd paid. But when she crossed the bastion to do it, the horns sounded the assault, and there was no more time for questions.

* * *

**Corren**

The breach came at dusk, exactly where Corren had known it would: the east postern, where the ward-line had frayed thinnest and the wall was oldest. Malvane's engineers had spent the whole day feeding that weakness, stone after sigil-wrapped stone, until at last the ancient masonry shrugged off its own enchantment and came down in a slope of rubble wide enough for ten men abreast.

"Company!" Corren roared, and his thieves came running—Vess and her one arm, young Pim with his two knives, old Halvard who'd sworn he'd never fight for any flag but his own purse. Thirty-six of them, forming up in the smoke at the foot of the breach because their guildmaster told them to, which was more loyalty than Corren had ever earned honestly.

"Hold the gap," he said. "Nothing crosses tonight."

"Nothing crosses," Vess repeated, and grinned with all her broken teeth, and then the enemy came down the rubble like a landslide with swords.

What followed was not battle. Battle has maneuver in it, and moments of breath. This was butchery in a smoke-choked throat of stone, hours of it, wave after wave, and Corren fought in the middle of it with a cutlass and a lifetime of sins, and discovered that shame burns hotter than fear. Every man he killed, he thought: *this is for Theo.* Every step he held, he thought: *this is the debt.* He took a spear-graze across the ribs and a sword-cut down his forearm and did not fall, because falling would have meant the arithmetic had won again, and Corren was finished—finally, completely finished—with letting the sums decide.

When the moon rose, the breach was choked with the dead of both armies, and it had not fallen. His company had started at thirty-six. Eleven answered when he called the roll.

From the wall behind him came a sound Corren had never heard in his life: wolf-folk voices raised in the old hunting-song, joined raggedly by human throats that didn't know the words but knew the meaning. On the plain below, Malvane's assault was breaking apart. And through the smoke of the burning engines—someone had fired the trebuchets, wolf-folk raiders slipping out a hidden sally-gate—he saw the usurper's banner wavering, drawing back, drawing *in*, toward the command standard where Malvane himself sat his horse.

Toward the figure walking out of the city gate alone, unhurried, unarmed, with a blank book under one arm.

"No," Corren breathed. "No, no—" He gripped the wall. "Somebody stop them—"

But nobody moved to stop the stranger, because nobody knew why they should. Nobody remembered. Only Wren, sprinting down from the ramparts with her spear abandoned and her voice cracking on a name she alone still carried.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren reached Theo at the edge of the ward-line, where the dying light of the covenant shimmered like heat over a road, and caught their arm.

"Don't." Her voice broke. "Don't walk out there. You don't know what you're going to say. You don't know why you have to be the one to say it."

Theo looked at her with those emptied eyes, polite and puzzled, and said, "I'm sorry—do I know you?"

And Wren, who had lost kin to human hunters and trust to a century of betrayal, who had every reason to let this stranger walk into the arrows and be done with the whole cursed history of Dunmores and covenants—Wren held on.

"You knew me," she said. "That's enough. That has to be enough." She pulled the Codex from under Theo's arm and opened it against her forearm, and the blank page caught the moonlight—and there, faint as breath on glass, new words were surfacing, ink rising like something written long ago finally coming clear:

*Debts written are debts that can be rewritten.*

"I don't know what it means," Wren lied gently, closing the book. "But I know it isn't finished. And neither are you. Come *back* from the wall, Covenant-Bearer. Whatever you paid—it stays paid. Let the rest of us carry the fighting part."

Behind them, above the plain, the battle turned. Whether it was the wards flaring one final time, or the wolf-folk charge that hit Malvane's flank out of the darkness, or simply the weight of everything the free cities had suddenly decided to be—no one afterward could say. They saw the usurper's standard go down. They saw his guard break. By midnight, the army that had come out of the dawn like a tide of iron was flowing back into the mountains it had come from, and Malvane lay dead beneath the walls of the city he had come to claim.

The cheering lasted until morning.

Wren did not join it. She sat on the ramparts with the blank book in her lap and Theo Dunmore beside her, silent among strangers who kept clasping their hand and calling them savior, watching them smile politely at people whose names they had never heard, searching that emptied face for some flicker of return.

Once, near dawn, Theo looked at her sideways and frowned slightly, as if trying to place a dream.

"Did we know each other?" they asked. "Before? It feels like—I keep almost remembering something. Like a word on the tip of my tongue."

Wren looked out at the smoking plain, at the sunrise beginning to bleed red along the mountains her people had guarded for a thousand years, and she made a promise to the morning.

"Yes," she said. "We knew each other. And I'm going to tell you all of it—every story, every road, every name. However long it takes."

She opened the Codex to its first blank page, and picked up a pen.
