## 10. A Blank Page at the End

The bells of Highmoor rang for three days, and Theo did not know why.

They knew the *that* of it, of course. Everyone knew the that. The city had been told a hundred times over, in taverns and temples and from the steps of the guildhall: Malvane had come with his engines and his inquisitors and his banners stitched with iron, and the gates of Highmoor had not broken, because the old wards had woken, because the wolf-folk had come down from Thornfell shoulder to shoulder with human soldiers, because the realms had stood together for the first time in living memory.

But the *why* of it — the shape of their own hand in it — was gone. It had been scooped out of them like the flesh of a fruit, leaving something that held its form but tasted of nothing.

So on the fourth morning, when the crowds found them again, Theo did what they had always done best.

They disappeared.

* * *

It was a smuggler's instinct older than memory, which meant it was the only kind they had left. They knew the backs of streets without knowing why, knew which doorways swallowed footsteps whole, knew the hour when the market guards changed shift and the alley behind the tannery ran empty. Their body remembered everything their mind had surrendered. Walking through Highmoor felt like being haunted by someone familiar.

They went up. That much the body insisted on. Up through the terraced gardens, past the shrines where strangers had left offerings — candles, bread, a child's carved wooden wolf — up the pilgrim road to the covenant-stone above the city, where the air smelled of frost and charred stone and, faintly, impossibly, of pine.

The stone still bore the scorch-marks of the rite. Theo knelt beside it and pressed their palm flat against the surface, the way you'd check a fevered brow.

Nothing. No fire, no voice, no flood of returning. Just cold rock and wind off the mountains, and below them, Highmoor glittering in the morning like a thing worth saving.

"I don't remember you," Theo said aloud to the stone. "I'm told I died here, a little."

The wind said nothing back. But it moved gently through the pines along the ridge, and Theo chose — deliberately, the way you choose to stand rather than kneel — to hear it as an answer.

* * *

They were halfway down the pilgrim road when Wren found them, which was no surprise at all. Wren had a scout's gift for finding people who did not wish to be found, and she had been exercising it relentlessly for four days.

"You're doing the thing," she said, falling into step.

"What thing?"

"The walking-away-quietly thing. Corren says your mother did it too. Vanished into alleys mid-conversation." She paused, and something flickered across her face — the careful look she wore whenever she mentioned the dead woman whose roads Theo's feet still walked. "Sorry. I keep forgetting you don't—"

"No," Theo said. "Tell me anyway."

That had become their arrangement. Wren would begin to apologize for the gap in Theo's life, and Theo would refuse the apology, and Wren would fill the gap instead, story by story, like water poured into a cracked vessel. She had told them about the pass at Wolfjaw, where their mother had once outrun an avalanche. About the cache beneath the lightning-struck oak, and the letters, and the choice she had made. About a girl named Wren who had hated a Dunmore on sight and then, somewhere between Lowmark's burning walls and the covenant-stone, stopped hating them.

"Everyone keeps calling me savior," Theo said as they walked. "There's a mural going up on the temple wall. I saw the sketch. I have very heroic shoulders, apparently. And I don't remember earning any of it. Do you know what that's like? Being thanked every day for something you can't recall doing?"

"Yes," Wren said simply.

Theo looked at her.

"My grandmother carried the last breach," Wren said. "She was there when the wards failed. She carried the grief of it her whole life, and none of us could share it, because we hadn't seen what she'd seen. Carrying something alone is the oldest wound my people have." She glanced sideways at Theo. "I promised you I'd tell you all of it. Every story, every road, every name. I haven't forgotten my promise just because the bells stopped."

* * *

Back in the city, in the small room above the rebuilt guildhouse that no one would explain why Theo had been given, the Codex lay where it had lain since the battle: on the table, closed, patient as a sleeping animal.

Theo opened it.

For four days they had not been able to bring themselves to touch it. Now they turned the pages slowly, one by one, and found them blank — all of them, the prophecies, the names, the dates, the sketch of their own younger face with its sharp smuggler's eyes. A century of written fate, wiped clean as new snow. Whatever power had demanded Theo's memories had apparently taken its ink as well.

Except.

Near the end, on a page that had once held the final prophecy, a single line remained, in the same brown-black script as all the rest, as if it had been too stubborn or too true to erase:

*Debts written are debts that can be rewritten.*

Theo read it five times. Then ten. Then they sat down slowly on the edge of the bed and read it again, and this time they laughed — a short, cracked sound, half sob.

Because they understood it now, or understood it the way you understand a road you've never walked by knowing who walked it before you. Their mother had paid a debt in blood to save an infant. Theo had paid a debt in memory to save two peoples. Debt upon debt upon debt, each generation inheriting the last, each payment larger than the one before. That was the ledger the Codex had kept. That was the arithmetic of the old world.

And the old world's book was blank.

Whatever came next would not be inherited. It would be chosen.

"Wren," Theo called through the window, because she was always somewhere nearby, pretending not to watch over them. "Come read something."

* * *

She came up the stairs two at a time, the way she did everything, and stood over the Codex while Theo pointed at the line. Her ears pricked forward; her tail, which she tried so hard to hold still in the city and never quite managed, gave one slow sweep.

"That's not prophecy," she said.

"No."

"It's instructions."

"It's permission," Theo said. "I think it's permission."

Wren pulled the chair close, sat, and took the pen from where it had rested since the dawn after the battle — since she had first opened the book to a blank page and made her promise. She held it out across the table, nib first, the way you offer a blade handle-first.

"Then let's write," she said. "Not fate. Not debts. Stories."

Theo took the pen. Their hand hesitated over the page — the hand of a smuggler, a liar, an invisible person, trained all their life never to leave evidence — and then, deliberately, they broke their training.

They wrote: *My name is Theo Dunmore.*

Below it, Wren wrote, in her careful, claw-shaped letters: *Her name is Wren of the Thornfall den-holds, and she once saved a Dunmore's life in the snow, and has never once let them forget it.*

Theo laughed again, easier this time, and wrote beneath: *Their mother was named Sera, and she loved them enough to break the world.*

And so it went, line under line under line, two hands trading the pen across the afternoon while the light moved gold down the walls. Some entries were great matters — the terms of the new covenant, drafted properly this time with elders from both sides, so that no generation would inherit another's breach. Some were small and precious: the name of the refugee captain who had held the low gate; the taste of the honey-bread a baker gave Theo free every morning, refusing payment because "saviors eat free"; the fact — which Wren swore to and Theo could only believe — that Theo had once been afraid of wolves, and how absurd that seemed now.

Story by story, the book filled. Not with what they owed, but with what they carried. Not with what fate demanded, but with what love had actually done, witnessed and written down so it could not be taken twice.

When the candle burned low, Wren set down the pen and looked at the pages, dense now with both their hands' writing, and then at Theo.

"There will be gaps," she said quietly. "Years of them. People you loved whose faces you'll never get back. I can't give you those."

"I know."

"But I can give you tomorrow. And the day after. And every story I have, and every road, and if you want—" she stopped, and started again, more carefully, the way she did when something mattered most, "—if you want, I can be there while you build the rest. New memories. Ones nobody takes."

Theo looked at her — at the scout who had pulled them out of the dark of Thornfell, at the enemy-turned-witness-turned-something-that-did-not-yet-have-a-name, at the one person alive who remembered everything Theo had lost and refused to let it stay lost.

Outside, the bells began the evening peal, gentle now, a sound like homecoming rather than victory. Somewhere below, Corren was teaching guild apprentices the new routes — open roads, honest cargo, the passes shared at last. Somewhere beyond the walls, wolf-song rose from the foothills, answered, for the first time in a century, by human voices singing back.

Theo picked up the pen and turned to a fresh page.

"All right," they said. "Start at the beginning. Tell me about the snow."

And Wren smiled, and began.

*Debts written are debts that can be rewritten.* But inheritance, Theo thought, listening to the first story of their new life, is not only what we're owed. It is what we choose to carry forward — and carry, and carry, until carrying becomes its own kind of remembering.

The Codex lay open between them, half-blank still, waiting.

Which was, Theo decided, exactly what a future should look like.
