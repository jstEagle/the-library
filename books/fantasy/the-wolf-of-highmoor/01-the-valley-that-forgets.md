## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The first frost of the season had come in the night, and Marguerite Delacroix woke to a silence so complete it felt like a held breath.

She lay still beneath her quilts, listening to it. Most people, she knew, would have found the silence unbearable—the kind that pressed against the eardrums, that made a person speak aloud just to hear something answer. But Marguerite had been born to this silence, raised inside it like a pearl inside its shell. It was not emptiness to her. It was the sound of the valley doing what the valley had always done: eating.

She rose in the blue-grey dark, dressed in layers of wool, and set water to boil over the peat fire. Through the kitchen window, Highmoor stood black against the lightening sky, its shoulders dusted white, its summit lost in cloud. The mountain did not look like a mountain this morning. It looked like a sleeping animal, vast and patient, and beneath its ribs—beneath three hundred feet of granite and older stone than that—lay the reason for her life.

The gate.

Marguerite drank her tea standing at the window, watching the light come slowly into the valley. It was a beautiful place, and she had long ago stopped being surprised by how few people understood that beauty was not the same as comfort. The valley was shaped like a cupped hand, ringed by ridgelines on every side, with only one pass leading out to the south—and even that pass wound so narrow and so crooked that carters refused it and travelers spoke of it the way sailors spoke of reefs. Nothing came into the valley easily. Nothing left it quickly either. And nothing that depended on magic could survive here at all.

That was the truth of Highmoor, the truth she had nursed alongside her mother's milk and never once thought to question: the valley ate magic. Not violently, not with any drama. It simply drank it, the way dry earth drinks rain, until a witch's fire guttered to candlelight and a conjured blade was only iron and a blessing spoken over a dying man was only breath. Scholars from the free cities had come, centuries ago, to study the phenomenon. None had stayed. There was nothing to study, they wrote in their journals before departing—only absence. A hole in the world's weave.

They had all, every one of them, failed to ask the obvious question. *Why does the valley exist?* Absence does not hold a shape for a thousand years without something holding it.

Marguerite rinsed her cup, wrapped herself in her cloak, and took up her staff from beside the door.

* * *

The path down to the gate was old before the kingdoms were old, and it showed. It descended through the pine forest in long switchbacks, its stones worn smooth and slightly hollowed, as though generations of feet had been drops of water falling in the same places. In some stretches the way was lined with markers—waist-high pillars of grey stone, uncarved, their surfaces glassy with age. Ward-stones, her mother had called them. *You'll know them when one fails,* she'd said. *A living stone is warm. A dead one is just rock.*

Marguerite walked the line of them now, pressing her bare palm to each in turn despite the cold. Warm, warm, warm. At the fourth stone she paused, frowning, and pressed again. It was warm—but faintly, the way a hearth is warm an hour after the fire dies. She stood a moment in the pine-hush, then drew the small knife from her belt and pricked her thumb, pressing the bead of blood to the stone's crown.

Nothing happened.

That was correct, she reminded herself. That was always correct. The wardenship was not a thing of spectacle. Her mother had been very clear about that, or she believed her mother had been very clear about that; the memories of instruction had a strange, secondhand quality now, like words read rather than heard. *Feed them nothing. Ask them nothing. Walk the rounds four times a year, and walk them honestly.* Blood was not a feeding. Blood was only a greeting—a way of saying *I am here, I am the one who walks, I am the same as always.*

Somewhere below, deeper than the roots of the pines, she imagined—or almost imagined—the gate shifting in its sleep.

She continued down.

* * *

The gate chamber had no door. That was the first thing everyone expected it to have, a door, because everything else in the world had doors, but the chamber opened onto the mountainside through a cleft in the rock that narrowed to a slot barely wide enough for a slim woman with a staff. Beyond the slot the passage widened, and the walls began to change: the granite gave way to something darker, shot through with veins of pale mineral that caught the light of her lantern and threw it back silver. The air grew warmer. Not comfortably warm—warm the way skin is warm.

And the silence deepened. Outside, the valley's silence was an absence of sound. Down here it was a presence, a weight, a thing with texture. Marguerite felt it settle over her shoulders like a mantle as she made the last turn, and felt, as she always felt, the peculiar loosening in her chest—the sense of arriving somewhere that knew her.

The chamber itself was round, perhaps forty paces across, and its ceiling arched up beyond her lantern's reach. And there, filling the far wall floor to vault, stood the gate.

It did not look like much. That was the other thing people expected wrongly. They expected a great ring of carved stone, or doors banded in black iron, or a shimmering veil. What Marguerite tended was a wall of darkness—not shadow, which is merely the absence of light, but darkness, which is the absence of everything. It stood flat as poured ink, utterly still, utterly silent, and if she held her lantern close she could see nothing reflected in it. Not flame. Not her own face. Light went toward it and simply stopped, politely, like a guest who knows better than to enter a room uninvited.

Around the gate's edges, set into the living rock, ran the wards: nine rings of script older than any alphabet she knew, cut so fine that her fingertips could barely find their grooves. When she had been a girl she had asked what language it was. Her mother had said, *Ours,* and had seemed startled by her own answer, and had not spoken for the rest of that day.

Marguerite began the rounds.

It took two hours, walking the full circumference of the chamber, pausing at each of the nine anchor-points where the ward-rings crossed. At each one she performed the same small offices: she cleaned grit from the grooves with a brush of boar-bristle; she pressed her palm flat to the stone above the script; she recited, in a voice pitched low, the warden's litany—which was not really a litany at all, only a list. Names. Forty-one names, beginning with Delphine Delacroix, Warden First, and ending, for the time being, with Marguerite Delacroix, Warden Last. She said each name aloud because the list was meant to be said aloud, and because saying them was the closest she ever came to company.

At the fifth anchor-point, she paused longer than usual.

The script there had faded. Not damaged—faded, the way ink fades, the way color fades from cloth hung too long in sun. The grooves were clean; the stone was whole; but the pale luminescence that lived in the cuts, the faint silver breathing-light that she had never once seen absent in thirty years of rounds, had gone thin. Dim. She crouched, brought the lantern near, watched. The light did not pulse. It flickered—barely, at the edge of seeing, the way a star flickers when you look directly at it and stops when you look away.

"Hm," Marguerite said aloud, because the chamber was a good place for talking to oneself. It was, in fact, the only place.

She finished the rounds. At each remaining anchor she looked more carefully than she ever had before, and at the seventh she found the same thinness, subtler still, and at the ninth—the anchor nearest the gate's center-left, the one her mother had called the keystone, though she could not remember whether those had been her mother's words or someone else's—the light was steady and strong as ever. She noted the others in her journal that evening, in the cramped shorthand of the wardens: *fifth and seventh thinned. No cause. Watch.*

Then she banked her fire, and ate, and sat by the window watching the moon rise over Highmoor, and felt the feeling she always felt after rounds, which she had named peace because it needed a name and no other word fit.

* * *

It came to her sometimes, in the evenings, that other people had faces in their pasts.

She thought of this as she mended a sock by the fire, the needle rising and falling. Other people kept portraits. Other people argued with dead fathers in their heads, heard dead mothers' voices scolding them across decades. Marguerite had her mother too—or the shape of her. A tall woman who smelled of pine resin and cold stone. Hands that guided a child's fingers along ward-grooves. A voice that said *walk the rounds honestly* in a tone she could summon perfectly and could not, try as she might, attach to a face. She had tried. On long winter nights she had sat exactly where she sat tonight, eyes closed, reaching back down the years for her mother's face the way a hand reaches into dark water—

—and found only the surface. Only the knowledge that a face had been there. Eyes, presumably. Hair, of some color. A mouth that had said her name, surely, thousands of times, and Marguerite could not remember the sound of it. Not once. Not the name itself, even—she knew she had been called something more than *daughter*, some small private name, a pet name, and it was gone. Gone so completely that she could not even mourn it properly, because mourning requires a shape to grieve for, and she had no shape. Only a hollow where a shape should be, worn so smooth with disuse that it no longer even ached.

She had inherited the wardenship the way one inherits a heartbeat: without ceremony, without memory of a beginning. There had been a day, she supposed, when her mother walked down to the gate and did not walk back up. She could not remember it. She could not remember a funeral, or grief, or even the weather of that year. It was as though her life before the wardenship had been paid out, spent, spent on something—and the spending had been so thorough that she'd never noticed the purse going empty.

The needle slipped. Marguerite pricked her finger, swore softly, and sucked the bead of blood away.

Outside, the wind moved down off Highmoor and combed through the pines, and the valley held its silence around her cottage like a cupped hand, and Marguerite told herself, as she told herself every night, that this was enough. That a warm fire, a mended sock, a duty faithfully kept, was a whole life, and a good one, and that the hollowness behind her breastbone was only the quiet loneliness of anyone who lives alone at the edge of the map.

She believed it, mostly. Belief was a habit here, like the rounds. You walked them honestly and you did not ask questions.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, beneath the mountain, she imagined—or almost imagined—the gate breathing its thin silver light, fifth ring flickering, seventh ring flickering, hungry in some way she had no word for.

Three days later, in the first true blizzard of the winter, something knocked at her door.
