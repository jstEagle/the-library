## 2. A Child at the Door

The storm came down from Highmoor like a debt being collected.

Marguerite had watched it gather all afternoon, a bruise-colored wall crawling over the mountain's western shoulder, swallowing the peaks one by one. By the time the light failed, the wind had found its voice—that low, keening howl that made the shutters rattle and the fire lean sideways in its hearth, as though even flame might be persuaded to flee the valley if it could.

She was mending a net when the knock came.

Three small blows against her door, spaced with a strange, deliberate patience, nothing like the frantic hammering a lost traveler would give. She set down the netting needle and listened. The wind screamed. The knock came again—three more taps, soft as knuckles on a coffin lid.

No one traveled the high road in this weather. No one traveled any road to Highmoor, in any weather, if they could help it. The valley had a reputation. Carters crossed themselves when their routes bent toward it; shepherds spoke of the dead air beneath the mountain, the way sound seemed to fall flat there, the way prayers went unanswered and charms unraveled like rotten thread. *The Valley That Forgets*, the free cities called it, though none of them could say precisely what it forgot, or why.

Marguerite knew. Marguerite kept the knowing, as her mother had before her, and her mother's mother, back through a line of keepers whose faces she could no longer summon. That was the price of the place, though she did not yet know how literally the valley took its payment. She only knew that she had lived alone in the shadow of Highmoor for longer than she cared to count, tending wards she had inherited rather than chosen, and that the loneliness of it had long ago calcified into something she mistook for peace.

She took the iron poker from beside the hearth—it was not a weapon, exactly, but the valley tolerated iron—and opened the door.

The storm poured in. Snow swirled across her floorboards in a white tongue, and standing at the edge of it, half-dissolved in the whirling dark, was a child.

A boy, perhaps eight years old. He wore no coat she recognized, no boots suited to this cold, only a garment of some pale grey weave that seemed to shed the snow rather than gather it, so that he stood dry amid the tempest like a stone in a river. His hair was white—not aged-white but true white, the white of frost ferns on a windowpane—and his eyes were a grey so pale they were nearly the color of the sky behind him. He did not shiver. That was the first wrong thing she noticed, though she would not understand until later just how wrong: no living child stood unshivering in a Highmoor blizzard.

"Come inside," she said, because whatever else he was, he was small, and the night was vast.

He looked at her hand on the door, then up at her face, and something flickered across his features—recognition, or its opposite, the look of someone meeting an expectation. He stepped over her threshold without hesitation, without gratitude, the way one enters a room one has always known.

She closed the door against the storm and the silence it left behind was enormous.

He would not tell her his name, or perhaps he did not have one yet, or perhaps his name was not a thing that fit into words she could hold. He ate the bread and honey she offered him with a careful, studying attention, as though food were a custom he was observing rather than a need he shared. He sat very straight on her bench by the fire, hands folded, watching the flames with an expression she could only call *familiarity*—the way an old woman watches the face of a friend.

"You're not afraid," she said at last. She realized she had been circling this all evening. "Of the valley."

The boy looked up. "Should I be?"

"Everyone is." She wrapped her hands around her cup of cider, needing the warmth. "There's no magic here. Nothing works. Charms die, blessings go quiet, witches sicken if they stay past three nights. The air is—" She stopped, searching. "Dead."

"It isn't dead." He said it simply, without argument, the way one corrects a child's arithmetic. "It's hungry. There's a difference."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind threw itself against the walls and slid away again, defeated.

"And what do you call yourself?" she asked, because asking his name had gotten her nothing, and a change of approach sometimes worked on children. "Where do you come from? There are no villages within two days' walk, and no village would let a child walk out into this."

"I came from under the mountain," he said.

She laughed—a short, startled sound she hadn't made in months—but the boy didn't smile, and the laugh died in her throat like something drowned.

"There's nothing under the mountain but the gate," she said. "And the gate doesn't open."

"I know," he said. "I came through anyway. It was hard. It took me a long time to be enough of myself to fit." He considered this. "Or maybe it took the gate a long time to forget enough of itself to let me slip."

Marguerite set down her cup because her hands had begun, very slightly, to shake.

"You speak our tongue well," she managed. "For a child."

"I speak every tongue." He said it without pride, the way one reports the weather. Then, softer, almost shy for the first time: "But I like this one. It's heavy. Words with weight last longer here."

"What do you mean, *here*?"

Instead of answering, he turned toward the window, toward the wall of whirling white beyond the glass, and lifted one small finger to point—not at the storm, not at the slopes above her cottage, but at the massif itself, the great brooding presence that dominated every horizon of her life, that she had seen every morning of every year and never once thought to name because it needed no name, it was simply *the mountain*, simply *Highmoor*, as constant and unremarkable as the sky.

"Vael-Moruun," the boy said.

The word went through the room like a blade through water.

It was not a word in any tongue she knew, and yet she knew it—instantly, completely, the way one knows the taste of blood. It settled into her bones and rang there. And with it came a flood of things she had no right to know: that the name meant *the Wound That Dreams*, in a language older than the oldest kingdom, older than the free cities, older than the war the gate had been built to end. That her mother had known it. That her mother's mother had spoken it over dying wards with her last breath. That somewhere in the buried strata of Marguerite's own inheritance, in the layers of duty laid down over generations like sediment, the name waited—and that no living mouth had shaped it in three hundred years.

"How do you know that word?" Her voice came out very quiet. Very level. She was proud of how level it was.

The boy turned back to her. The firelight moved in his pale eyes and did not seem to touch them.

"It's my name too," he said. "Part of it. We're named for where we're from, where I come from." He tilted his head, considering her with that unnerving, ancient patience. "You have a name like that as well, Warden. You just don't remember it."

"I remember my name."

"Not that one."

The wind rose to a shriek against the north wall and fell away. In the sudden lull, Marguerite became aware of another sound, or the absence of one—the wards, she thought. The wards beneath the mountain hummed always, a silence-within-silence she had felt at the base of her skull her whole life, as natural to her as her own heartbeat. She felt for them now the way one probes a tooth with the tongue.

They were still there. But they had changed key. Something in their long note had bent, subtly, the way a held string bends when a hand comes near it.

The boy was watching her face. He saw what passed across it, and nodded slowly, as if confirming a suspicion.

"You felt that," he said. "Good. That means you're still the Warden. That means it's not too late—or maybe it means it's exactly late enough." He folded his small hands again and looked into the fire. "I walked a long way to find you. Through the starving places. The gate is hungry, Warden. Has no one told you? A gate that hungers will eventually feed itself. And when it does—"

He stopped. Outside, far off, muffled by miles of storm, something howled that was not wind.

Marguerite stood very still, her heart beating slow and hard, wonder curdling in her chest into something colder and older than fear. The boy—Tam, she would learn, though that too would prove to be only part of the truth—sat calmly in her firelight, snowless and unshivering, wearing a language dead three centuries like a borrowed coat.

"Tomorrow," she heard herself say, "you'll come down to the gate with me."

The boy smiled then, and it was a child's smile, sweet and gap-toothed and utterly at odds with everything else about him.

"I know," he said. "That's why I'm here."
