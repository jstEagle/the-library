## 3. What the Gate Remembers

The storm had spent itself by morning, leaving the valley rinsed and white, the kind of clean that made Marguerite distrust her own eyes. Snow lay on the pines like a debt unpaid, and beneath Highmoor the air had that particular stillness she had known all her life—the silence that was not silence at all but an absence, a held breath, a place where sound went to be forgotten.

She should have taken the boy back down to the low country. She knew this. Every warden's instinct in her said it: send him with the first thaw caravan, let someone else decide what he was. But when she'd suggested it over breakfast—porridge sweetened with the last of the autumn honey—he had only looked at her with those grey eyes, old as slate, and said nothing, and the nothing had been so complete that she'd found herself packing extra bread instead of his boots.

"You'll want to see this," he'd said, when the plates were cleared. Not a request. A translation of something she hadn't yet heard.

So they went down.

* * *

The path to the gate was one her feet knew better than her mind did, which was its own small horror if she let herself think about it. It wound through the black pines along the streambed, past the standing stone her mother had called the Sentinel—a name Marguerite remembered without knowing where the memory came from—and down into the hollow where the mountain's shoulder dipped like a bowed head.

She had made this descent perhaps four hundred times. Seasonal rounds, her mother had called them, though her mother had never explained what the seasons were for. Check the wards. Touch the stones. Listen to the hum. The hum was the whole of it, really: a vibration felt in the teeth more than the ears, a deep and patient note that said *the door is shut, the door is shut, the door is shut*. She had built her life inside that note the way other people built theirs inside church bells.

Today, halfway down the path, she stopped.

The hum was wrong.

Not gone—she would have known instantly if it were gone, the way you know a heartbeat has stopped—but thinned. Ragged. As if someone had taken the great bell of Highmoor and run a crack through it, so that the note caught and stuttered, fraying at the edges into something almost like—

Almost like whispering.

"Tam." She turned. The boy stood three steps behind her, as he had the entire way down, and there was snow in his dark hair and no color at all in his cheeks, and he was smiling. It was not a child's smile. "Tam, stay behind me."

"Why?" he asked, genuinely curious. "It can't reach you here."

"It?"

But he was already walking past her, small boots crunching, heading down into the hollow with the ease of someone returning to a house he'd lived in long ago. And the terrible thing—the thing that made her stomach drop like a stone through ice—was that the valley let him. The dead air, the silence that ate magic and memory and everything bright, parted around him like water around a stone. He walked through the ward-line without a ripple.

Marguerite followed because there was nothing else she could do. Because he was eight years old and alone in the world, and because some cold voice at the base of her skull whispered that whatever waited below already knew he was coming.

* * *

The gate chamber opened out of the mountainside like a throat.

It was older than any masonry she knew—stones fitted without mortar, each one taller than she was, carved with spirals worn nearly smooth by time beyond reckoning. Her mother had called them the Old Work, and had touched them only with the backs of her hands, the way you touch something sleeping. In the center of the chamber, half-sunk in the living rock, stood the gate itself: a doorway with no door, a frame of black stone around an oval of air that shimmered faintly, like heat above a summer road, or like the surface of very deep water seen from underneath.

The wards ran from the gate to the chamber walls in silvered lines, inlaid in the floor like veins. Marguerite had traced them a thousand times. They were hers. They were the whole inheritance of the Wardens of Highmoor: keep the lines unbroken, keep the hum alive, ask nothing else.

Now she knelt at the nearest line and saw what the storm had shown her from the ridge.

Fraying. The silvered metal—ward-metal, her mother's journal called it, though Marguerite had never known where such metal came from or how it was worked—had gone dull in places, and in two places it had simply *ended*, thinning to nothing like thread burned away. Beyond the break, the line did not continue. It just stopped, and the stone around it was pale, bleached, as if the color had been drunk out of it.

"How long?" Tam asked, crouching beside her. He didn't touch the ward. He held his hand above it, palm down, the way you'd test a fever.

"I don't know." Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, too steady, the steadiness of someone standing on a frozen pond listening to it tick. "Seasons. Maybe years. Wards don't fray fast. They fray like—" She searched for the word. "Like rope left in weather."

"Or like a body left unfed," the boy said.

She looked at him. He looked back, patient, terrible, eight years old.

"What do you know about feeding?" she said.

And then, before either of them could answer, before she could decide whether she even wanted an answer, she heard it.

The whispers.

They came through the gate—not from the chamber, not echoing off the stones, but *through*, pressing against the oval of shimmering air the way water presses against a dam. Marguerite had knelt in this chamber hundreds of times. She had heard wind, she had heard the mountain settle, she had heard her own heart in the dead air. Never once, in thirty-one years of keeping, had she heard voices.

They were layered, many-voiced, a murmur like a congregation heard through a cathedral wall. Some of it was language so old it wasn't language anymore, just cadence and grief. But under it, threading through it, running like a bright wire through smoke, was a word repeated. Over and over. Patient as dripping water.

Tam tilted his head, listening, and his smile faded into something softer and far worse: recognition.

"They're calling someone home," he said.

"Calling who?"

He didn't answer right away. The whispers rose a little, and in the dead air of the valley—air that ate every sound, every spell, every bright thing—they came through anyway, which meant they were not sound at all. They were something older than sound. They pressed on the inside of her skull like a thumb on a bruise.

"Home," Tam said again, tasting the word. "That's what they keep saying. *Home. Home. Come home.*" He glanced up at her, and his eyes had gone from slate-grey to something darker, deeper, a well with no bottom. "Warden. Do you know what a prison is?"

"A prison," Marguerite said slowly, "is where you put something so it cannot leave."

"Yes." The boy nodded, grave as a priest. "And what do you call the person who stands outside the door and makes sure it stays shut?"

"A guard," she said. "A keeper. A warden."

"And what do you call the person who stands outside the door and makes sure it stays shut—when no one ever told them why? When no one ever told them there was anything on the other side at all? When they think the humming is just... the mountain?"

The whispers swelled. *Come home. Come home. Come home.*

Marguerite's hands, braced on the pale stone, had begun to shake.

"That's not a guard," Tam finished gently. "That's a prisoner who's been told her cell is the whole world."

* * *

She made herself stand. She made herself walk the full circuit of the wards, because that was what a warden did, because ritual was the last rope holding her above the drop. At each broken line the whispers thickened, and at the worst gap—the one nearest the gate, where a hand's width of ward-metal had simply ceased to exist—the murmur resolved, briefly, horribly, into clarity.

A single voice. Not many-voiced anymore. One speaker, close against the other side of the shimmering air, so near that Marguerite fancied she could feel breath on the back of her neck, though the gate faced away from her.

It said her name.

Not *Marguerite*. Not the name she'd been called in villages, in markets, in the rare letters that came up the pass. It said the other name. The true one. The one her mother had used only twice that Marguerite could remember—once when she was very small, and once at the end, lips cracked and failing, when the old woman had gripped her hand and whispered it like a key being pressed into her palm.

*Ilyen. Ilyen. Ilyen, come home.*

The valley ate magic. Everyone knew that, in the vague way everyone knows things they've never examined. That was why the free cities sent their sick here, their haunted, their mad—that was the story, that the valley's dead air scoured a person clean. Marguerite had believed she tended the thing that did the scouring. A tool. A wall. A mercy.

But the whispers weren't asking the valley to open. They were asking *her*. And the gate, starving, frayed, drinking at the gaps in its own veins, had grown strong enough in its hunger to press one syllable of truth through the silence:

She had never been the wall.

She was what the wall was for.

"Ilyen," Tam repeated softly, using the name like a lamp in a dark room. "That's what they call you. On the other side, that's your name. You had it before you had any other." He looked up at her, and for the first time since she'd found him in the snow, he looked exactly his age—small, and sorry, and afraid for her. "I'm sorry. I thought you knew."

"Thought I—" Her voice broke and she hated it, hated the crack in it, hated the tears that came hot and instant, freezing on her lashes in the chamber's cold. "Thirty-one years. My mother kept it before me, and hers before her, and none of us—none of us ever knew we were *inside*—"

"The knowing is part of the keeping," Tam said. "If the warden knew, the gate wouldn't need to starve. It would just ask. And you'd say yes. You'd say yes, Ilyen, because home is home, and the door would open, and everything on the other side would come through." He wrapped his arms around his knees, making himself small on the pale stone. "So the gate eats the knowing. Eats the memories that would tell you. Your mother's face—you said you couldn't recall it. That's not grief wearing the memory smooth. That's the gate. Feeding."

The whispers rose again, and now they were singing, and the song was beautiful, and that was the worst thing yet, because Marguerite could feel it working on her like warmth on frost. Something in her chest leaned toward the gate. Something old and homesick and buried under thirty-one years of duty stirred and turned toward the sound the way a sunflower turns toward light.

*Come home. Come home. We remember you. Come home.*

She tore her gaze from the shimmering oval and looked instead at the boy—at the child who had walked through her wards like a rumor, who spoke the tongue of the dead kingdoms, who had come out of a snowstorm with no footprints behind him that she could find afterward, though she had looked.

"Tam," she said. "What are you?"

He smiled then, small and sad, and the well-deep eyes closed halfway.

"I'm what leaked through," he said. "When the wards began to fray. I'm the knock before the door opens."

Above them, far above, the morning light crept down the mountain, and somewhere on the southern ridgeline—too far to see, too near to ignore—a thread of woodsmoke rose against the pale sky where no hearth had smoked yesterday.

Someone else had found the valley.

And the gate, hungry, patient, was beginning to hum.
