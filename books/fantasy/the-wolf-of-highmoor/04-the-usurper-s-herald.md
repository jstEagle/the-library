## 4. The Usurper's Herald

The orders had come in a hand that trembled, which Aldric Vane had learned to read as either fear or reverence, and he could not yet decide which.

*Find the door that dreams. It lies beneath the mountain the old maps name Highmoor and the older ones do not name at all. Bring back word of what passes through it, or bring back nothing at all.*

He had copied those words into his own journal twice, once by daylight and once by the light of a guttering candle, and both times the ink had seemed to dry slower than it should, as though the paper were drinking it reluctantly. He told himself this was nothing. He had marched through plague camps and burned villages and a winter in the Greymarch where men ate their boot leather and thanked the Emperor for the privilege. A slow-drying page was not a haunting.

But the orders had been given to him privately, sealed with black wax instead of the army's red, and the quartermaster who handed them over had crossed himself twice before turning away, as if the scroll itself might bite. Aldric had asked no questions. Questions, in the usurper's army, were a currency spent best never at all. He was thirty-one years old, a captain of scouts, the son of a tanner from Corvess who had clawed his way up on a talent for finding things — water in deserts, fords in flooded rivers, gaps in enemy lines that no one else could see. Finding the door that dreams would make him more than a captain. It would make him remembered.

That was the ambition, clean and simple, and he held it up to the light each morning like a coin he was checking for clipping.

They were nine days out from the army's vanguard when the land began to change. The road thinned to a track, the track to a suggestion, and the farms along it stood empty in a way that troubled him more than burned farms would have. Burned farms meant war. These farms meant *departure* — doors left unlatched, tools hung on walls, a cradle in one yard with a blanket still folded over its rail. No smoke, no blood, no sign of struggle. The people here had simply decided, all together and without any visible reason, that they would rather be somewhere else.

His men noticed it too, and did not like it. Scouts were superstitious by trade; you could not walk ahead of an army reading the land's moods and stay entirely sane. Sergeant Broch, a heavyset man who had followed Aldric since the taking of Marren Ford, said the valley ahead smelled wrong. "Like a cellar," he kept saying. "Like something shut up too long." Aldric sniffed the air himself and caught only cold stone and pine, but he had learned long ago to trust Broch's nose over his own certainties.

On the eleventh day they saw the mountain.

It rose out of the morning mist like the shoulder of some sleeping thing, grey and vast, its peak lost in cloud, and the valley below it ran green and quiet between walls of forest that seemed, even at a distance, denser than forests had any right to be. There was a village at the valley's mouth — a scatter of roofs around a stone well — and beyond it, half-hidden in the haze, a single cottage with smoke rising from its chimney. Someone lived there still. One light in an emptied land.

Aldric ordered the company into the treeline and went forward alone to look, as he always did. It was the part of the work he loved best: the slow crawl through wet bracken, the world reduced to what he could see and hear and smell, every other concern burned away. He reached the lip of a rise above the valley floor by midday and lay there a long while, watching.

Smoke from the cottage chimney rose straight up. Not drifting. Straight, as though the air itself refused to move it, and that small wrongness snagged in his mind like a fishhook. Wind moved the trees on the ridges. Wind bent the grass near the valley mouth. But in the deep of the valley, around the cottage and the dark cleft in the mountainside beyond it, the air lay flat and dead, and the smoke climbed like a pillar in a temple.

He wrote it down. *Valley holds its breath.* Then he crawled back to his men, and that night the sickness began.

* * *

It started with the youngest, a boy called Pell who had lied about his age to get his father's sword. He woke the camp vomiting, then shivering, though the night was mild, and by dawn his skin had gone the grey-white of old ash. By the second day three more had joined him, and none of them had fever — that was what unsettled the company's one leech, a grumbling veteran named Odo who had dressed wounds since before Aldric could shave. "No heat in them," Odo said, pressing his palm to Pell's forehead and frowning. "Sickness burns. This is something else. This is like they're being *emptied*."

Emptied. The word sat in Aldric's chest like a swallowed hook.

He moved the camp back two miles and the sick men improved within a day, color creeping back into their faces like dawn into a grey sky. He moved a patrol forward again, closer to the valley mouth, and within hours the same listlessness took them — men who had marched forty leagues without complaint suddenly unable to lift their packs, staring at their own hands as if they'd forgotten what hands were for. Distance, it seemed, was medicine. Whatever lived in that valley wanted them kept away, or wanted something from them that proximity cost.

Odo had a theory. The leech had heard stories in his youth, he said, from a grandmother who came out of the eastern hills — tales of a place where magic pooled like water behind a dam, and the folk who lived below the dam paid for its keeping in years and memories both. "The old kingdoms used to send their mad ones here," Odo said, spitting into the fire. "Their prophets, their sorcerers, the ones who talked to things in the dark. They'd walk them into valleys like this one and the valley would drink whatever made them strange. That's why the farms stand empty, Captain. Nothing grows right where the strangeness has been drunk."

"Superstition," Aldric said, because a captain must say such things aloud, and because the alternative — that his grandmother's-tales leech knew more about their destination than the Emperor's own cartographers — was a thought he could not afford.

But that night he dreamed, and the dream was not superstition.

He stood in a corridor of black stone, and the stones were carved with letters he almost understood, the way one almost understands a voice heard through a wall. At the corridor's end was a door, and the door was not wood or iron but something older, a seam in the world itself, and it was breathing. Slowly. Patiently. The way a sleeping animal breathes, or a man under ether, and with each exhale the door grew thinner and he felt himself drawn toward it not by force but by *recognition*, the way a man is drawn toward a house he lived in as a child and cannot remember living in. He woke with tears on his face and no memory of why, and he lay in the dark listening to his men breathe and feeling, for the first time in eleven years of soldiering, afraid of his own mind.

* * *

In the morning he did what ambitious men do with fear: he converted it into motion.

The sick men stayed with the baggage and Odo, two miles back. He took six of his steadiest — Broch among them — and went down into the valley itself, on foot, in loose order, moving the way he'd been taught to move through country that might be watched. The village at the valley mouth was empty but not abandoned; bread had been left on a table under a cloth, going stale, as though the household meant to return. In the well, when Broch dropped a stone, the water answered with a sound like a struck bell, clear and low and far too resonant, ringing on and on in the dead air until Broch crossed himself and Aldric made him stop throwing stones.

Beyond the village the valley narrowed, and here the trees changed. Pines gave way to birches white as bone, and the birches to bare stone terraces that climbed toward the mountain's root, and cut into the lowest terrace were shapes Aldric had no vocabulary for: rings of standing stones, each taller than a man, each carved top to bottom with the same slow spiraling script. Moss grew on the north faces and nowhere else. The ground between the stones was swept clean, not by wind — the dead air saw to that — but as if by hands. Recently. As if by *hands*.

Someone tended this place. Someone had swept the ground within the week.

And at the center of the ring, set into the mountainside, was the door.

It was smaller than his dream had promised — a dark cleft in the living rock, framed by a lintel of black stone carved with figures whose meaning he could not parse: a woman with her arms spread wide, a child at her feet, a wolf or dog lying curled against her hip. Above the lintel, faint as breath on glass, the rock itself shimmered. Not reflected light. Not heat-haze. A shimmer with no source, hanging in air that did not move, and when Aldric looked directly at it the shimmer brightened, the way a cat's eyes brighten when looked at in the dark.

Broch saw it too. All of them saw it. And all of them, except Aldric, flinched from it.

"Captain," Broch said quietly, "I don't want to be here."

"I know."

"It's not fear. I've been afraid my whole life, this isn't it. It's—" He searched for the word and found only a worse one. "It's like the place knows me. Like it's *counting* me. I can feel it counting, sir, like a merchant counting coins, and I don't want to know what it's weighing me against."

Aldric put his hand on the man's shoulder and felt him trembling, and understood that everything Odo had dismissed and everything Aldric himself had dismissed was standing here in front of them wearing stone. But where Broch felt counted, Aldric felt — called. The shimmer above the lintel warmed as he approached it. His headache, his constant companion of twenty years, faded for the first time since Marren Ford. The ache in his knee from an old arrow wound went quiet. Even the tinnitus left by a powder blast at Corvess fell silent, and in that sudden, terrible quiet he heard the valley's true sound, which was no sound at all: a silence so complete it pressed on the ears like deep water, and inside the silence, faint as memory, something that was almost a voice saying a word he could not catch, over and over, patient as tide.

*Come. Come. Come.*

He did not step through the door. Some instinct older than ambition stopped him — the same instinct that had kept him alive through eleven years of finding things better left unfound. But he stood close enough to touch the lintel, and when he finally drew back, he found that his palm had printed itself faintly in the moss on the black stone, and the print did not fade. Hours later, passing the ring again on their withdrawal, the print was still there, greener than the moss around it, as though the stone had taken nourishment from his touch.

He reported none of this in the dispatch he sent back to the vanguard. Dispatches travel, and are read by many eyes, and Aldric Vane had not survived this long by putting truths on paper. What he wrote was: *Structure located. Valley depopulated. Single inhabitant, cottage at valley center. Recommend force assessment before approach.* What he kept in his journal, in the private cipher his father the tanner had taught him for marking hides, was different, and shorter, and truer:

*The door dreams. Last night I dreamed it back.*

* * *

They made camp that evening on the ridge above the valley, close enough to watch the cottage smoke rise straight as a temple pillar, far enough that the emptiness could not reach them. The sick men were recovering. Odo was grumbling that he'd be glad to see the last of this country. And Aldric sat apart with his map, marking the route the main army would take, and found that his hand kept returning to the same question, circling it the way a hawk circles a field.

The orders said find the door. They did not say what the army was to do with it. Seize it? Study it? Open it? The usurper's chancellery had sent no engineers, no scholars, no priests — only soldiers, and only him. Which meant either that the Emperor's advisors believed the door needed only finding, or that they knew precisely what it was and wanted no witnesses who might understand it.

Ambition whispered the first answer. Unease, that shadow that had walked beside him since the black-wax seal, whispered the second.

Below, in the gathering dusk, a light appeared in the cottage window. Then a second light, moving — someone carrying a lamp from the cottage toward the standing stones. Small and steady, a single flame in all that drowned valley, walking the path he had walked that morning. He watched it reach the stone ring and pause there, and he fancied — though the distance was too great for certainty — that the shimmer above the lintel brightened to meet it, the way one lantern answers another.

Then the light went out, all at once, and the valley below went dark as the inside of a closed fist, and somewhere in that darkness a silence began to press outward, up the slopes, toward his camp, and his horses stirred and stamped and would not settle, and Broch stood watch with his sword drawn and his lips moving in prayers he hadn't said since boyhood.

Aldric wrote one final line in cipher before he slept, and read it three times in the morning, and could not decide whether it was a warning or an invitation:

*She keeps it fed. Find out with what.*
