## 5. Tam's True Name

The storm had passed in the night, but its aftermath lingered in the valley like a held breath. Marguerite woke before dawn out of habit older than memory, and lay listening to the drip of snowmelt from the eaves, and to the other sound — the one that had not stopped since she and the boy came up from the gate chamber two days past.

A whispering. Faint as wind through a keyhole, but it did not come from outside. It came from below, from under the earth, from the direction of Highmoor's roots where the gate sat in its collar of ward-stones like an eye that had finally remembered how to blink.

She rose, banked the fire, and found Tam already awake, sitting cross-legged on the hearthstones with his hands folded in his lap, watching the coals with an expression far too old for his face. He had taken to doing this — sitting perfectly still for hours, as if listening to something on frequencies her ears could barely catch. This morning he turned his head when she stirred, and there was something apologetic in the motion.

"It's louder," he said. Not in the old tongue. In hers. He had learned it with frightening speed, as though language were a coat he'd merely forgotten he owned. "Since we went down there. Since you touched the stones."

"I didn't touch them."

"You breathed on them." He said it gently, the way one corrects a child. "That's enough. They know you now."

Marguerite put water on to boil because her hands wanted occupation. The cottage was small and the boy made it smaller, filling corners with a presence that was somehow larger than his body. She caught herself studying him in the black glass of the window — the dark hair plastered down by melted snow, the fine wrists, the way his shadow fell slightly wrong against the firelight, angled a degree off true.

"Tam." She set two cups out, then hesitated over a third, an old habit for guests who never came. "When you came to my door. Before that. Where were you?"

He looked into his cup as if the answer were written in the tea leaves of nothing at all. Steam rose between them.

"You won't send me away."

"That isn't an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It's a question I'm answering first."

The fire popped. Somewhere beneath them, faint and patient, the whispering continued, like surf against a distant shore.

"I was on the other side," Tam said. "I have always been on the other side. That is — " and here he frowned, searching, a scholar hunting through a burned library, " — that is not quite true either. There is no *always* there. No *before* or *after*, only *near*. Things are near each other, or near the gate, or near you. You are very near, Warden. You always have been. I could feel you through the stones, all the years I was — gathering."

"Gathering."

"A fragment." He said it simply, the way he might have said *rain* or *bread*. "When the wards weaken — and they have been weakening, Marguerite, for years, for decades, they fray like old rope — small things leak through. A smell. A word. A shape of cold. Most of it dissolves in your dead air; your valley eats such things. But some of it pooled. In the low places behind the stones. And some of it — gathered itself. Because that is what pieces do, when they remember being whole."

Marguerite stood very still. The kettle had begun to sing and she did not move toward it.

"What pieces," she said quietly. "Whose?"

"Yours, mostly." He looked up then, and his eyes in the firelight were the color of the light that leaked around a shut door. "Your mother's, a little. The wardens'. Everything the gate has ever taken from the people who kept it — it doesn't destroy what it eats, don't you see? Nothing is destroyed. It only goes *there*. And when the wards thinned, some of it seeped back, the way water finds cracks. And it found the shape of the person who fed it longest. It found the shape of a child, because children are easier. Small. Simple. A fragment given flesh so it could walk out and look at the world with borrowed eyes."

The kettle screamed. Marguerite lifted it from the fire without hearing it, poured water over nothing, over an empty pot, and stood watching the steam rise off bare iron while her mind refused, item by item, to accept what it was being told.

"You're saying you're made of me."

"I'm saying I'm made of what the gate took from you. The parts you paid without knowing. Your laughter at seven years old. The name of your first dog. Whatever your mother sang while she worked — I think I have that one, there's a tune that lives behind my teeth sometimes, and I don't know the words but I know the shape of her mouth making them." His voice cracked, just once, on *her mouth*, and he pressed his lips together until it passed. "I didn't ask to be assembled, Marguerite. I woke up behind the stones already loving you. That's not my fault. But it's true."

"And the rest?" Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice in her own kitchen. "The thing calling from the other side. The voices you translated. *Calling someone home.* If the gate opens — "

"Then everything follows me back." He said it without flinching, which was worse than flinching would have been. "I am a crack, Marguerite. A keyhole-shaped hole in a door. What came through me was a drop. If the door opens, it is the sea."

"How much? How much is *everything*?"

Tam considered. He always considered; he had no gift for the easy lie, perhaps because lies were a thing of the living and he was only mostly that.

"Do you know the old stories? The war before kingdoms. The ones the free cities scrubbed from their histories because remembering gave the memories power?" He waited for her nod. "It wasn't a war between armies. Armies were just what spilled over afterward, like foam on a wave. It was a war between what lived on the other side and what lived here, back when here still had magic in its soil, before your valley learned to drink it. And the other side lost. And the losing did not kill it. Losing only made it patient, and hungry, and *near*."

"The usurper's soldiers," Marguerite said slowly. "The herald. Vane. They're coming for the gate."

"They're coming for a door they think opens onto treasure." Tam's mouth twisted. "Men always think the other side of a wall must be richer than their own. But the gate doesn't open onto treasure. It opens onto everything that ever wanted back in. Every exiled thing. Every eaten memory. Every power that ever learned patience. Your valley starves them, yes — the dead air hurts them, thins them. But the gate has been starving too, and a starving gate pulls harder than a fed one. Blood on the ward-stones will feed it faster than anything. And then—"

He stopped. Outside, the whispering beneath the mountain seemed to rise for a moment, a tide pulling at a seawall, and both of them listened to it, and neither spoke until it settled.

"Then it comes through," Marguerite finished for him. "And the valley can't eat it, because the gate will be open wide enough to feed it as fast as the air drains it."

"Yes."

Marguerite set down the useless kettle. She walked to the window, where the morning was coming up gray and clean over the white slopes of Highmoor, beautiful and dumb and utterly unaware of what slept in its roots. Forty years she had tended this place. Swept the ward-stones. Renewed the chalk lines. Believed herself a guard at a tomb that had long ago finished dying.

And all the while the tomb had been eating her, spoonful by spoonful, and assembling the leavings into a boy with her mother's tune behind his teeth.

"I ought to be afraid of you," she said. It was not an accusation; it was an honest inventory, spoken aloud because honesty was all she had left that the gate hadn't tasted.

"I know." Tam's voice was very small. "Sometimes I'm afraid of me. When I sleep, I dream in languages that bite. Sometimes I wake and I can't remember if I love you or if I only *remember* loving you, if there's a difference, if it matters—" He stopped himself, knuckles white around his cup. "But I walked out of a starving dark place through a crack in the world, and the first thing I saw was your window lit, and the first thing I felt was home. Whatever I'm made of, Marguerite — that part is real. I'd know. I'm made of almost nothing else."

She crossed the room before she knew she meant to and knelt and pulled him against her, this impossible child stitched together out of her own stolen years, and felt his small heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out of a cage, or maybe in. His hair smelled of snow and of something older underneath, something like stone and like the inside of sealed rooms.

"If they come," she said into his hair, "the soldiers, the herald — if they break the wards and force the gate — "

"They'll need you to open it," Tam whispered. "It answers the Warden's blood. That's why they'll keep you alive. That's why they've always needed us alive, every Warden, every generation. We're not the guards, Marguerite. We're the lock."

Below them, beneath the floorboards, beneath the roots of the mountain, the whispering changed. It was no longer formless now. It had found a rhythm, a cadence, almost — almost — a song. And Tam stiffened in her arms, and went pale, and translated before she could ask, his lips shaping the words like a man handling something venomous:

"*The soldiers have reached the pass. They carry iron and old blood. Hurry, little piece. Hurry home. We are so hungry, and you have grown.*"

Far to the north, faint as a scar on the horizon, smoke had begun to rise above the pass — and it did not drift the way smoke should, but leaned steadily toward the mountain, toward the gate, toward them.
