## 6. Soldiers in the Pass

The first sign was not smoke or sound, but birds. Marguerite noticed it on her morning walk to check the upper ward-stones: the valley, which had held its flocks of choughs and snow-finches for as long as she could remember, was suddenly empty of them. They had risen in a single silent cloud at dawn and poured over the shoulders of Highmoor like water finding its level, fleeing something below.

She stood in the frosted stubble of her garden with Tam's mended coat over her arm and listened. The silence that lived in the valley — that dead, humming quiet she had mistaken all her life for peace — felt different now. Thicker. Attentive. As though the mountain itself had turned one stone ear toward the pass.

"They're coming," Tam said behind her. He did not need to say who. For three days he had slept badly, muttering in his old tongue, waking with frost on his breath though the cottage hearth never died. "The wards taste iron. Far off. But coming."

"How far?"

He considered this with a child's terrible gravity. "Two days for the fast ones. The slow ones will come after, and keep coming."

Marguerite looked up at Highmoor's grey shoulder, where the pass threaded through like an old scar. She had spent thirty years believing the war was dead because no one had come to fight it. Now the world was arriving at her door, and it had brought its knives.

* * *

Captain Aldric Vane smelled the valley before he saw it.

The company had climbed for two days through the pass — two days of freezing fog, of men cursing the usurper's name between chattering teeth, of the scouts reporting nothing but rock and goat-tracks. Then, near dusk on the second day, the fog simply ended. It lay behind them like a curtain, and beyond it the air was so clear it hurt, and so still that Aldric could hear his own heartbeat.

"Gods' teeth," breathed Serl, his lieutenant, beside him.

Below them the valley opened like a cupped hand. Green pastures gone gold with winter, a river braiding silver through the middle, a scatter of cottages with woodsmoke rising straight up in windless columns. And above it all, filling half the sky, the mountain: Highmoor, black-browed, wearing its snows like a crown no king had ever dared claim.

It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. But twenty of Aldric's men were already retching by the roadside, and he could not make himself feel anything about it except that it was inconvenient.

The sickness had begun an hour ago, at the edge of the fog. Men had staggered from the saddle, grey-faced, complaining their ears had stopped working — not ringing, they insisted, but *stopped*, as if the sound had been scooped out of them. The horses refused the downward track until whipped. And through it all Aldric alone had felt nothing but a strange, low pull beneath his breastbone, gentle as a hand on a rope, drawing him down toward the valley floor.

Toward the door that dreams.

The orders sat against his ribs in their oilskin wrapping, the usurper's own seal broken once and only once. *Find the valley where magic dies. Find the door. Report. Touch nothing.* The old man in the tower had spoken those last words while staring through Aldric rather than at him, and for the first time in eleven years of service, Aldric understood that his lord was afraid.

"Form them up," he said. "Those who can ride ride. The rest follow on foot or stay in the pass and freeze. We camp at the valley mouth tonight."

Serl wiped his mouth and mounted again, swaying. "And if the locals object?"

"Then we object louder." Aldric turned his horse down the track and felt the pull strengthen with every step, sweet and awful, like walking toward a fire in deep winter. He did not tell anyone that his hands had stopped shaking from the cold. He did not tell anyone that some part of him, some part he did not recognize as his own, already knew the way.

* * *

They came to her door at moonrise — not soldiers, but farmers.

Seven of them, trudging out of the western hills with carts and children and one bony milk-cow, their faces lit orange in her doorway by the fire Tam had kept burning. Their spokesman was a wheelwright named Ostrec whom Marguerite knew distantly, a broad man whose hands shook as he spoke.

"The free cities are done, Warden. Done within the season, maybe less. Corvane has fallen already — you'll not have heard, we barely heard ourselves." He swallowed. "The usurper's armies took the bridge at Marrow Ford without a fight. The League council fled east. There's talk of a last stand at Vell, but Vell has walls of mud and four hundred men. It's over. People just haven't laid down yet."

Behind him, his wife held a sleeping infant wrapped in every blanket they owned, and a girl of perhaps twelve stared past Marguerite's shoulder into the dark, watching for the army that would follow them here.

"Why come to me?" Marguerite asked, though she knew.

Ostrec laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "Because the stories say nothing cursed can follow into your valley, Warden Delacroix. Because your mountain eats magic, and the usurper's pet sorcerers choke like fish on land when they cross your ward-line. Because it's the only ground left on the map that isn't his." He spread his cracked hands. "We're not asking for much. Barn space. Floor space. We'll work for our bread."

Marguerite stood in her doorway a long moment. Thirty years of solitude looked back at her from seven tired faces, and she thought: *this is what I kept it safe for. Not the gate. Them.*

"Bring the cow around back," she said. "There's room in the barn, and I'll need hands cutting peat before the real cold comes. And Ostrec—" she caught his sleeve as he turned, "—soldiers are coming. His soldiers. Two days, maybe less. When they arrive, you keep your people indoors and away from the stones on the mountain path. Do you understand? Whatever you see, whatever you hear—"

"Warden." His eyes were steady and hopeless and grateful all at once. "We heard things singing in the pass as we came over. My wife says it was the wind. It wasn't the wind, was it?"

"No," Marguerite said. "It wasn't the wind."

* * *

Aldric found the first ward-stone by accident, or told himself he had.

He had left the camp at the valley mouth under pretense of scouting, ridden up the shepherd tracks alone, and there it stood: a pillar of grey granite taller than a man, older than any mason's craft he knew, carved over every surface with script that seemed to shift when looked at directly. Snow did not lie on it. Around its base, in a perfect circle, the grass grew green as midsummer while everything else slept brown under frost.

His horse would not approach. He dismounted and walked the last fifty feet himself, and with every step the pull in his chest grew until it was less a feeling than a fact, less a fact than a voice.

*Closer,* something said, in a language he had never learned and somehow knew. *Closer, little echo. You are almost home.*

He put his bare palm against the stone.

Cold went through him like a lance, and then — warmth. A flood of warmth, of *rightness*, images pressing behind his eyes that were not his memories and fit better than any memory ever had: a door standing open in a mountainside, light pouring through it that was not light, and himself walking through, welcomed, *expected*, made whole—

He came back to himself face-down in the frost with blood in his mouth and no idea how long he had lain there. The stone hummed under his cheek like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

When he got back to camp, three men were dead.

No wound, no fever. Serl reported it white-faced: found cold in their bedrolls, eyes open, and — here the man's voice dropped to a whisper — smiling. And the strangest thing, the thing Serl clearly feared to say aloud: since crossing the fog-line, the sentries swore the mountain hummed at night, and each day it hummed a little louder.

"It's the altitude," Aldric said, and did not believe it, and saw that Serl did not believe it either. He looked up at Highmoor's black bulk against the stars and felt the pull in his chest answer it, patient as tide. Somewhere up there was the door. He was meant to find it, report it, touch nothing.

But the voice in the stone had called him *home*, and Aldric Vane had been homeless since he was nine years old, and he was beginning to suspect that the usurper had not sent him here as a scout at all.

* * *

Blood on the ward-stones changed everything.

Marguerite felt it at dawn like a bell tolling inside her skull — the free-city scouts had finally caught the usurper's outriders in the valley mouth, and the skirmish lasted only minutes, but minutes were enough. Four men fell near the lower stones, and their blood soaked into the frozen ground, and the gate drank it.

By the time she reached the nearest ward-stone, running with Tam's small hand locked in hers, the change was visible. The carvings glowed faintly, a dull amber pulse where for thirty years they had shown nothing. The dead air of the valley had acquired a texture, a pressure, like the moment before a storm breaks. And from high on the mountain, thin as thread, she heard it: the whispering. Not words yet. *Almost* words.

"It's stronger," Tam said. He pressed both palms flat against the stone and his eyes went distant, translating something she could not hear. "Every drop makes it stronger. Blood is a kind of remembering, Warden-Mother. That's what my — what the others say. Blood remembers who it belonged to." He looked up at her, and for the first time since he'd arrived at her door in the snow, the boy looked afraid. "The gate is starting to remember being open."

That night she stood in her kitchen among refugees — nineteen souls now, more expected, Ostrec's folk sharing out barley soup with a tinker family who'd come in from the south — and understood that the arithmetic had changed. It was no longer a question of whether the war would come to Highmoor. It was a question of whether the gate would be opened by soldiers, or by hunger, or by time.

Across the room, Tam sat among the refugee children teaching them a clapping game in the old tongue, and their laughter rang against the rafters like small bells, and Marguerite thought: *I promised myself I would never feed it. I promised my mother's ghost.*

From the barn doorway, Ostrec's wife appeared, face grey. "Warden. Riders at the valley mouth. A whole column — banners of the usurper, hundreds strong. And their captain's riding ahead alone. Straight up the mountain path. Alone."

Marguerite closed her eyes. Beneath her feet, faint but unmistakable, the floor hummed — and this time she could have sworn it was not the gate at all, but the mountain itself, purring.

"He can feel it," she whispered. "God help us — the gate has already chosen its next warden."

Tam's clapping game stopped mid-verse.
