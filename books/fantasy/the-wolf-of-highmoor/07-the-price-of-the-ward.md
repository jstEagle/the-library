## 7. The Price of the Ward

The journal had been in the cottage all along.

Marguerite had walked past it a thousand times — the flat iron box beneath the loose flagstone by the hearth, the one her mother had shown her when she was small enough to still ask questions and old enough, apparently, to stop asking them. *When you need it,* she remembered being told, though even that memory was soft at its edges now, like a letter left too long in the rain. She could not recall her mother's voice saying it. Only the fact of having been told, the way one remembers a scar without remembering the wound.

She pried up the flagstone on the third night after Tam's confession, with snow still hissing against the shutters and the boy asleep — if a thing like him slept — in the loft above. The box was cold in a way the hearth-warmed room could not explain, cold as the gate chamber itself, and inside lay a book bound in grey leather gone stiff with age, and beneath the book, a bundle of letters tied with ribbon so faded it might once have been any color at all.

She did not open the letters. Not yet. She opened the journal, and by candlelight, in a hand that began strong and slanted and ended cramped and small as a bird's tracks in snow, she read what the Wardens of Highmoor had never written down until there was no one left to tell.

*The gate does not eat magic,* the first page said, in her mother's writing — or her grandmother's; the earliest entries were older, the ink browned to rust. *Magic is only the smallest thing it eats.*

Marguerite read until the candle guttered, then lit another, and read until dawn came grey through the frost-ferned window and Tam came down the ladder with his strange, unchildlike quiet and stood beside her chair, reading over her shoulder in a language he should not have known but somehow also read plain as milk.

*It hungers for what we hold dearest because that is what burns brightest. A Warden who gives nothing gives nothing to the seal. The wards are not stone. They are remembering. Every ward-stone in that valley is a memory someone chose not to keep.*

*My mother forgot her wedding day to close the western fracture. She forgot my father's face to hold the eastern wall through the long winter. By the end she did not know why she stayed in this valley. She knew only that she must. Duty outlives love. That is how they build us.*

*They* — Marguerite stopped on the word. Read it again. *That is how they build us.* As though Wardens were made, not born. As though the loneliness she had carried all her life, the peace she had mistaken for contentment, the vast quiet where a childhood should have been — as though none of it had been hers at all, but simply the shape left behind after the gate had fed.

"I don't remember her face," Marguerite said aloud. Her voice sounded wrong in the small kitchen, too loud, too alone. "My mother's. I've tried. I know she had hands that smelled of tallow and iron, because someone told me. I know she hummed while she worked. But I couldn't draw her face if you held a knife to me."

Tam said nothing. He climbed into the chair across from her and folded his hands the way old men fold theirs in council, and waited, which was somehow worse than any answer.

Because here was the truth she had been circling since the child arrived: the whispers pressing through the gate had not been random. They had been calling *her*. And if the wards frayed each season, if the silence she tended grew thinner year by year like a garment worn past mending, then the reason was simple arithmetic. There was no one paying anymore. Her mother had emptied herself into these stones. Her grandmother before her, and back and back through the ledger of the dead, each Warden spending down their lives in coin the gate accepted and the world forgot. And Marguerite — Marguerite had inherited an empty purse and a starving god and had never known she was supposed to feed it.

The valley ate magic, yes. It ate everything that leaked from the other side. But the seal itself — the thing that kept the leak small — ran on something else entirely.

Memory. Grief. The currency no one minted twice.

* * *

She went down to the gate chamber alone. Tam had wanted to come; she had told him no, and he had accepted it with that terrible patience of his, standing in the doorway watching her go with eyes the color of deep water under ice.

The descent felt longer than it ever had. The stairs she had climbed since girlhood, the walls sweating cold, the air thickening around her step by step as the valley's deadness closed over her like water over a sinking stone. At the bottom, the gate waited as it always waited: the arch of black stone older than any kingdom, older than the mountain above it perhaps, veined with ward-lines that had once, she understood now, blazed like dawn.

They were nearly dark. Threads of ember-light guttered in the deepest grooves, the last embers of her mother's sacrifice, banked thirty years and burning down to ash. And behind the arch, closer than it had ever been, the whispering.

Not words. Not yet. A pressure, a leaning-in, the way a crowd leans toward a door when a king passes. The other side knew she was there. Perhaps it had always known. Perhaps it had been waiting, patient as winter, for the last coin in the last purse to be spent and nothing offered after.

Marguerite stood before the arch with her mother's journal clutched against her ribs and made herself think clearly, the way she thought about warding-salts and seasonal rounds, because clarity was the only armor she owned.

If the gate fed on memory, then memory given freely might do what memory taken by degrees could not. Her mother's entries suggested it — the fractures her grandmother had sealed in crisis days had held for decades, sealed in desperation, in single great payments rather than the slow bleed of a lifetime. One act. One loss. One bright thing burned whole instead of gutted candle-flame by candle-flame.

And if she did not pay, the gate would take anyway. It would take through the cracks. It had already begun taking — Tam, leaked through like light through a failing shutter. What else had slipped out that she hadn't seen? What else leaned at the door even now?

"Very well," she said to the dark. Her breath did not fog. The valley drank even that.

She searched herself the way a miser searches a house in famine, going shelf to shelf, taking inventory of everything she owned. Her years here — the rounds, the salts, the seasons. Her mother's humming. The smell of tallow and iron. The cottage in snow. Tam asleep in the loft. And underneath all of it, buried so deep she had to dig past strata of ordinary days to reach it:

A summer afternoon. She could not have named the year, but she knew it was early, before the wardenship, maybe her twelfth summer. A river below the high pasture — no, a stream, shallow and quick over brown stones. Someone had been with her. Another girl, she thought, a friend from the lowland farms who came up with her father's wool-cart. They had waded barefoot and dared each other to drink from the coldest pool, and the friend had laughed so hard she'd fallen in, and Marguerite had laughed too, helplessly, until her ribs ached, and the sun had been warm on the water and the world had been —

She could not name the friend. She could not name herself at twelve, except as the girl in the stream. The memory was a room with the furniture gone, but the light was still in it, golden and specific, the laugh still in it, ringing.

It was hers. One of the few things that was purely, provably hers and not the residue of duty.

Marguerite knelt at the base of the central ward-stone and pressed both palms flat against it. The stone was colder than the grave-cold of the chamber, and through it, faintly, she felt the gate attend — the way a dog goes still when it scents meat.

"If this is the price," she whispered, "then let it be paid proper."

She took the memory and she offered it.

There was no ritual her mother had recorded — or perhaps there had been, and the journal had lost those pages too. She simply held the stream in her mind, the brown stones, the drowning laughter, the gold light, and she pushed it out of herself and into the stone the way one pushes a boat off from shore, and for one terrible heartbeat she felt it resist, felt her own mind cling to the light of that afternoon like a hand clinging to a ledge—

Then it let go.

The sound the wards made was not a sound. It was a chord struck somewhere behind the world, and the black veins of the gate flooded with white fire, every line blazing, every guttering ember roaring up to a conflagration of light that threw her shadow huge and wavering against the far wall. The whispering behind the arch cut off — not faded, *cut*, severed mid-breath, and the pressure lifted, and the chamber was silent in a new way, a whole way, the silence of a lock thrown home rather than a room left empty.

Marguerite knelt there in the blaze of her own payment, laughing or crying, she couldn't tell which, watching the wards burn brighter than they had burned in thirty years.

Then she tried to remember the stream.

She knew there had been one. She knew it the way she knew her mother had hummed — a fact with a hole in it, furniture-less, lightless. *I gave something today. I was happy once, in water, in summer, with someone.* The shape remained. Everything warm inside the shape was gone, scooped out clean, spent.

Grief as currency, her mother had written. And grief, Marguerite discovered, kneeling in the white light of the seal she had just refortified, is a strange coin — it buys exactly what it costs, and the buyer remembers only the buying.

She climbed the stairs slowly, holding the rail. In the kitchen the fire had burned low. Tam sat where she had left him, and when he saw her face he stood, and something moved across his not-quite-childish features that looked, for the first time, like fear.

"It worked," she said. Her voice came out steady. "The wards are strong. Stronger than I've ever seen them."

"How much?" he asked softly.

She opened her mouth to say *one memory, only one, a small thing* — and found she could not remember what the thing had been. Could not remember it even now, standing in her own kitchen not an hour after paying it. The gate did not leave receipts.

"That much," she said instead.

Tam looked at her for a long moment, and then at the journal lying open on the table, at the pages of her mother's cramped and fading hand, and he reached out and closed the book gently, the way one closes the eyes of the dead.

"There's more," he said. "In the letters. You haven't read the letters yet, have you?"

* * *

The ribbon crumbled when she touched it. Inside, in ink browner than the journal, in a hand two generations older than her mother's, the first letter began: *To the Warden who comes after — know what you have inherited, and forgive us for the poverty of it.*
