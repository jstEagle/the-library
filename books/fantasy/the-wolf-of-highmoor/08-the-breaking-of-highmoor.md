## 8. The Breaking of Highmoor

The snow had stopped by the time the soldiers came down into the valley, and Marguerite felt their arrival before she saw them—a wrongness in the air, like a held breath, like the moment before lightning finds the ground.

She stood at the cottage window with Tam pressed against her side, watching them descend the pass in a ragged column of grey and iron. Thirty men, perhaps more, their breath pluming white, their boots crunching on the frozen crust. At their head rode a man on a black horse whose coat was frosted silver, and even at this distance she could see that he did not look at the valley the way soldiers looked at a place to be taken.

He looked at it the way a starving man looks at bread.

"They're here," Tam said. He said it in the old tongue first, and then, remembering himself, in hers. His small hand found hers and gripped it hard enough to hurt. "Marguerite. They're here."

"I know."

"The gate is *singing*." His voice cracked on the word. She could feel it too now, faint and terrible, a vibration rising through the floorboards, through her boots, through the bones of her feet—the wards straining against something that wanted through, or something that wanted *in*, and she could no longer tell which was which.

"Listen to me." She knelt so their eyes were level. His face was pale, his dark eyes wide, and he was trembling, but he did not look away from her. Whatever he was—fragment, leak, child given flesh out of the gate's slow hunger—he had been hers since the night the storm delivered him, and she would not let him see her afraid. "Whatever happens down there, you stay behind me. Do you understand? Behind me, always."

"You're going to give it more," he said. Not a question. "You're going to feed it again."

"If I have to."

"It will take too much this time." His lip quivered. "It took your mother's face from you. It took her voice. If you keep feeding it—"

"Then I will forget," Marguerite said quietly, "and the world will not. That is the trade. It has always been the trade."

Below, the column reached the flat ground and began to spread out across the valley floor like spilled ink.

* * *

Aldric Vane had dreamed of this valley for three nights running before he ever saw it, and when he finally stood in it, he understood why.

The orders had called it *the door that dreams*. He had assumed it was poetry, the way usurper's writs were always poetry—grand language draped over ugly work. But standing now before the cleft in Highmoor's flank, where the mountain opened like a throat, he felt the dreams pressing at the edges of his waking mind: towers of green glass, rivers that ran upward, faces without names that smiled at him with unbearable tenderness.

His men felt nothing of the kind. That was the trouble. Sergeant Brohm had vomited blood into the snow an hour ago. Two others had gone deaf near the ward-stones, cupping their ears and shouting. The valley ate magic, the scouts reported, and it seemed to eat other things as well—courage, certainty, the ability to remember why one had marched.

But Vane felt *drawn*. The closer he came to the cleft, the clearer the dreams grew, and beneath them something else: a low, sweet humming, like a mother's voice heard through a wall.

"Captain." Brohm, grey-faced, spat red into the snow. "The men are asking if we turn back."

"We do not turn back." Vane dismounted and handed off his reins. "The Lord Protector wants what's behind that stone, and what the Lord Protector wants, the Lord Protector gets. Form ranks."

The gate chamber lay beyond the cleft—a vaulted hollow in the mountain's heart, its walls carved with spirals worn nearly smooth, its floor set with stones that glowed faintly, like embers under ash. And there, at the center: the door itself. A seam in the world, tall as three men, sealed by lines of light that pulsed slow as a sleeping pulse.

And standing before it, a woman in a warden's grey cloak, with a boy half-hidden behind her skirts.

Vane drew his sword. It sang softly as it cleared the scabbard, and the sound harmonized, horribly, with the hum of the wards.

"Warden," he said. "You'll open the door."

* * *

"No," Marguerite said.

The word echoed in the chamber, small and absolute. She had moved them both to stand directly before the seal, her body between the soldiers and the door, Tam's cold fingers knotted in the fabric of her cloak.

Behind Vane, thirty soldiers filled the chamber mouth, torches guttering in the dead air. Their faces were wrong—sweating, slack, some of them weeping without knowing they wept. The valley was already working on them, pulling the war out of their muscles, the marching, the killing, the reasons. Only Vane stood straight. Only Vane's eyes were clear, and that clarity frightened her more than any madness would have.

"Madam Warden." He stepped closer, sword leveled. "I've read the old reports. I know what leaks through this door when it starves. Dreams. Whispers. Things that unmake armies from the inside. My lord believes such things can be *harnessed*." His jaw tightened. "I believe he is a fool. But he is a fool with ten thousand men at my back, and I am a man who follows orders. Open the door."

"And everything on the other side comes through," Marguerite said. "Everything. You've felt the dreams, Captain. You know what waits."

"I know what waits *here* if I refuse. Ten thousand men will come to this valley, and they will burn your cottage, and they will take the boy apart to learn what he is, and they will pry this door open with fire and steel until it opens whether you will it or no." His voice dropped. "Open it slowly, under your hand, and perhaps what comes through can be met. Met and fought. Refuse, and it comes through in the dark, all at once, with no one ready."

For one mad moment, Marguerite almost admired him. He was afraid—she could see it in the white around his mouth—but he had done the arithmetic honestly, and the arithmetic said *damned either way*.

"There is another option," she said.

"Name it."

"I destroy the gate. All of it. The door, the wards, the hunger. Nothing through, nothing leaking, nothing for your Lord Protector to harness. Ash and silence."

Something flickered across Vane's face—hope? longing? "Can you?"

"No," Tam said suddenly, stepping out from behind her. Marguerite's heart seized. "She can't. Because destroying it means feeding it everything, and—" He stopped himself, eyes going wide, and Marguerite closed her own briefly. *Oh, child. Oh, clever, foolish child.*

Vane's gaze sharpened. "Feeding it what?"

"Nothing," Marguerite said. "He's a child. He says foolish things."

"He says things the door told him." Vane raised his voice. "Take the boy."

Two soldiers started forward—and stopped. Stopped as though they'd struck glass. The ward-light flared along the floor, and the men staggered back, hands to their temples, and one of them sat down abruptly in the snow-melt and began to laugh, high and helpless, at nothing at all.

The chamber went very quiet.

"They cannot touch him," Marguerite said, and her voice shook only a little. "He belongs to the door. And the door—" she laid her palm flat against the seal, and the light leapt beneath her skin, bright as a wound— "*the door knows me.*"

Vane stared at her. Then, slowly, he lowered his sword—not in surrender, but in the manner of a man clearing his hand for worse work.

"Then here is what happens, Warden. I cannot make you open it. So I will simply wait. My men will hold this valley. Winter will do the rest. And every day that passes, that door grows hungrier, and every day, somewhere in the world above, the war grinds on, and people die who might have lived if the things behind that stone had come through to fight for them—or died anyway, if they hadn't. Choose. Starve it or feed it. But choose today."

And the seal, beneath her palm, whispered.

Not words. Not yet. But a pressure, a leaning, like a crowd pressing against a festival gate, and through her hand Marguerite felt the thing she had spent her whole life refusing to feel: the gate was not holding the old powers *out*.

It was holding them *in*. Waiting. Hungry. Calling someone home.

Tam was crying silently, tears freezing on his cheeks, and he looked up at her with her mother's eyes—she realized it only then, only in that moment, that the boy wore the face her memory had lost—and said, in the old tongue, the words she had translated once in the deep of winter:

*They are calling someone home.*

* * *

The fracture came at dusk.

No one decided it; that was the horror of it. Vane had given the order to encamp, his men were driving tent-stakes into frozen ground, and Marguerite stood with her hand still on the seal because she could not make herself let go, and the door simply—*cracked*.

A line of darkness split the light, hair-thin, and through it came a smell like rain on hot stone, like forests before the axe, like a world that had never known a plow. The whispering became voices. The voices became a single voice, vast and patient and terribly gentle:

*Little keeper. You have held us so long. Your mother held us. Her mother. How tired you must be.*

"I'm not tired," Marguerite said aloud, and her voice broke on the lie.

*You are. We can give you rest. We can give you her—your mother, whole and warm, every year the gate took from you returned. Open, little keeper. Come home.*

And Marguerite, who had spent forty years mistaking loneliness for peace, felt the offer slide into the hollow places inside her like water into cracked stone, and knew, with perfect clarity, that she could not hold the door much longer—not because her strength was failing, but because her *reasons* were. Every sacrifice had thinned her. What remained was not enough to want to stay for.

That was when Tam walked past her and stood between her body and the crack.

"No," she breathed.

"You said behind you," the boy said. "But you're falling, Marguerite. I can see it. You're falling *into* it." He turned to face the fissure, small and straight-backed, and spoke in the old tongue, and the mountain itself seemed to lean in to listen. She caught only fragments—*home*, *debt*, *name*—and then he said a word that was clearly a name, his true name, the one no living person should know, and the fissure shuddered.

*Little fragment,* the voice said, tender as rot. *Come home. Be whole.*

"I'm not a fragment," Tam said, in her language, in a child's clear voice. "I'm *hers*. She found me. She fed me. She named me twice—once in yours and once in hers, and the second one counts, because she gave it freely, and free gifts are the oldest law there is." His chin trembled. "So I don't come home. And she doesn't either. Because I'm staying with her, and where I stay, the door stays shut."

The voice laughed. It was not an unkind sound, which made it infinitely worse. *Then the debt falls to the keeper. As it has always fallen. Feed us, little keeper. Feed us, and be emptied.*

"Yes," Marguerite said.

"*No!*" Tam whirled on her, and behind them both, Aldric Vane stood frozen at the chamber's edge, sword hanging forgotten, watching a negotiation older than his kingdom unfold in light and shadow. "Marguerite, it will take *everything*, don't you understand, not just memories—it takes the *why* of you, it takes—"

"I know what it takes." She knelt and took his face in her hands. His tears were hot against her thumbs. "I have known since the journal. Since the first memory I fed it. Listen to me, Tam of the two names. There is a version of me that walks away from this door tonight, and she lives, and she is hollow, and hollow things break, and when she breaks, the door breaks with her. There is no version of this where I keep myself and the world keeps its walls. There is only the question of *how*."

"Then let me—"

"You are the reason," she said simply. "You are the last why I have left. Which means you are the one thing I cannot spend."

She rose. She turned to the fissure, and beyond it she could see them now—shapes moving in a light that was not light, the old powers pressing close, beautiful and terrible, wearing faces like her mother's face, like every face the gate had ever eaten. And she understood, at last, what her mother's journal had been trying to tell her in its fading, circling hand:

The gate did not eat memory. The gate *was* memory—all the memory of the old world, dammed up behind a seal, starving because nothing new was ever given. It did not want to be fed.

It wanted to be *finished*.

"Captain Vane," she said, without turning. "Get your men out of the valley. Now. All of them. Run until the dead air stops tasting of iron."

"Warden—"

"*Now.*"

She heard him go. She heard the boots, the shouts, the panic of men who had seen enough. Good. Let the usurper's army report a valley that resisted them. Let them fear it.

Then Marguerite Delacroix, last Warden of Highmoor, pressed both palms against the fractured seal and stopped pushing back.

She *opened*.

Not the door. Herself. She reached into the gate's hunger the way one reaches into a flood, and offered it everything—her mother's face, her mother's voice, the lullaby half-remembered, the winters of learning the wards, the first time she'd stood alone in this chamber and been afraid, the decision to stay, the years of staying, the loneliness she'd called peace, the peace she'd called enough, the woman she had been before the valley, the woman the valley had made, every morning, every season, every round of the wards, every reason—

The gate drank.

Light poured through the fissure and into her and out of her again, and she felt herself thinning, felt the *why* draining out like heat from cooling iron, and she held on to one thing only, one small burning coal she cupped at the very bottom of her soul: *a boy needs me. A boy needs me. A boy—*

The coal flickered.

The gate took the shape of her mother's smile. It took the sound of her name spoken by someone who loved her. It took the knowledge of why she had ever stayed, and the staying itself, forty years of it, folding away like a letter being closed.

And in the last instant, as the fissure began to knit, as the old powers fell back howling from a door that no longer had a keeper to call home—because the keeper was becoming the door—Tam threw himself against her, arms around her waist, and shouted into the closing light, in the old tongue, in *both* tongues:

"She stays! She stays! She's mine and the valley's and you cannot have her, the debt is paid, the debt is PAID—"

The seal blazed white.

The mountain exhaled.

And Marguerite Delacroix fell into the arms of a child she no longer knew, in a silence deeper than any the valley had ever held, while somewhere far above, a captain rode out of the pass with empty eyes, carrying a report he would never write.

When she opened them again, the chamber was only stone. Cold, dark, ordinary stone.

"Hello," said the boy, wiping his face with his sleeve, smiling through the ruin of his tears. "My name is Tam. I think—I think you take care of me?"
