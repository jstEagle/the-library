## 9. The Woman Who Stayed

*The gate opens like an eye.*

*No—like a mouth. Like the first mouth she ever fed it, when she was young and her mother's hands were still warm over hers on the ward-stones. Feed it, little wren. Feed it what you can spare.*

*She has nothing left to spare. That is the joke of it, the terrible arithmetic of it: she gave one memory in the cottage with the journal open on her knee, and the wards blazed like dawn, and she thought—she thought she could ration herself. A year here. A face there. She had counted her memories like coins, certain there would be enough.*

*But Vane's sword is at Tam's throat, and the seal is fracturing, and something old is pressing its face against the thin place between worlds, and the gate is hungry, so hungry, hungrier than it has ever been because it has been starving for three hundred years and she has been feeding it crumbs.*

*So she gives it everything.*

*Her mother's face goes first. She feels it leave—not pain, exactly, but a kind of unhooking, a door swinging open in a room she will never enter again. Dark hair. A laugh like water over stones. Gone. The wards drink it and flare white, and the thing on the other side recoils, hissing, and Vane shouts something she cannot hear because her ears are filling with the sound of her own life pouring out of her.*

*Her mother's name. Her mother's voice saying her name. The word* Warden *spoken as an inheritance instead of a burden.*

*Gone. Gone. The gate drinks and drinks.*

*She gives it the winters—all of them, every snowfall she has ever stood in, the particular blue of shadowed snow beneath Highmoor. She gives it the summers, the smell of the herb garden, the way the valley light turns gold at the hour when the sheep come down. She gives it her mother's hands. Her mother's death, which she suddenly remembers all at once, complete and terrible, and then does not remember at all.*

*Vane is screaming. Or someone is. The sword-arm falters; she sees that much, sees the captain stagger back from the gate as though struck, his face gone the color of tallow, because he can feel it too—the pull, the vast swallowing attention of the thing she is feeding. It is not eating memories anymore. It is eating* her.

*Her years go. Not their contents—those went first, the small warm things—but the years themselves, the shape of them, the long patient architecture of a life built stone by stone beside a mountain. Forty-one years. Thirty-eight? She cannot say. The number dissolves even as she reaches for it.*

*Tam is crying. She knows this the way you know rain against a window—distantly, without understanding. He is holding her hand. When did he take her hand? His fingers are small and cold and they are the only solid thing left in the world, and she holds onto them because holding is older than memory, older than names, older than the gate itself.*

*Her reasons go last.*

*This is the strange mercy of it: she feels them leaving and they feel like rest. Why did I stay? the gate asks her, in the voice of everything she ever was, and she answers honestly—I stayed because my mother stayed, because the war must not return, because someone must—and the gate takes each answer gently, like a woman lifting sleeping children out of a burning house, and where each reason was there is nothing, a clean white absence, a room swept bare.*

*I stayed because—*

*Because—*

*The seal closes. She feels it close the way you feel a fever break: a shudder through the whole mountain, a sigh in the stones, silence rushing back into the valley like water into a dry bed. The whispers stop. The pressure stops. The old powers on the far side press once, twice, against a door that no longer remembers it was ever a door, and then they are gone, and the gate is only a gate-shaped dark in the rock, and the wards are bright and quiet and fed for three hundred years.*

*Marguerite Delacroix falls to the floor of the gate chamber, and the last thing she thinks, before thought itself runs out, is that she does not know why she is lying down, or whose hand is in hers, or why the boy is crying, or why—*

*Why—*

***

White light. Then grey. Then the low dark beams of a ceiling she almost recognizes.

She wakes in her own bed with snow-melt dripping from the eaves in a rhythm like slow speech, and she lies still for a long time because she does not know what else to do. Her body aches everywhere, deep, as if she has carried something heavy a very great distance. Her mouth tastes of iron. There is a bruise on her palm shaped like a small gripping hand.

A boy is sitting in the chair by the hearth.

He is watching her with an expression she has no name for—old grief worn young, the look of someone who has already done his weeping and now must do the harder thing. He is perhaps eight. His hair is dark and uncombed. There is ash on his sleeves.

"You're awake," he says.

His words are strange to her—not the sense of them, which is simple enough, but the sound, the accent, vowels shaped by a country she could not find on any map. And yet she understands him perfectly, the way you understand your own heartbeat.

"Where am I?" she asks.

The boy's face does something complicated. "Highmoor," he says carefully. "You live here. You're the—" He stops. Starts again. "You keep the valley."

"Keep it," she repeats. The words settle into her like stones into a streambed, finding grooves that were already there. *Keep the valley.* Yes. Yes, that is true, that is true the way fire is hot and winter is long. She does not know how she knows it. She simply does, the way she knows her own hands, which are calloused in patterns that speak of ward-stones and herb-beds and the hauling of water, though she cannot remember earning a single callus.

"There was a battle," the boy says. "Soldiers came. You stopped them." He hesitates. "You paid for it."

"Paid with what?"

"I don't—" His voice cracks. He looks away, into the fire. "I don't know how much you want me to tell you."

Something in her chest turns over, hollow and enormous, like wind through an empty barn. She searches for the feeling's name and finds nothing. She searches for her mother's face and finds a smooth white wall where a face should be. She searches for the reason she lives alone beneath a mountain in a valley the maps forget, and finds—

Finds a boy who needs her.

That is all. That is everything. The knowledge sits in her not as memory but as instinct, as bone: *the boy needs me.* She looks at him—at the ash, at the red-rimmed eyes, at the way he holds himself like something braced against weather—and the knowing deepens until it is the firmest fact in the world.

"What's your name?" she asks.

"Tam."

"Tam." She tries it. It fits in her mouth like a key in a lock she didn't know she owned. "And I'm—"

She waits. Nothing comes. Panic rises, quick and animal, and then the boy says, softly, "Marguerite," and the panic recedes, because the name fits too, the way *keep the valley* fit, the way *Tam* fit. Borrowed knowledge. Secondhand selfhood.

"Marguerite," she agrees. "I'm Marguerite. And you're mine to look after."

"Yes," Tam says, and begins, very quietly, to cry.

She gets up—her body protests, everything protesting—and crosses the room and gathers him in, and he comes to her easily, practiced, as if he has done this before, as if he has held her while she slept more than once. Over his head she looks out the window at the mountain, white and vast and silent under the fading sky, and she feels the watching-thing wake in her, the thing that needs no memory: *a mountain must be watched.* She does not know what watches are for. She knows only that she is the one who keeps them.

Outside, the snow is beginning again.

***

Captain Aldric Vane rode out of the valley three days later with eleven men where he had brought forty, and he did not look back.

His orders had been explicit. Find the door that dreams. Mark its position. Return with coordinates, with sketches, with a full accounting of whatever leaked through it—for the usurper's scholars believed the leak could be widened, weaponized, turned upon the free cities before spring. Vane had imagined glory. He had imagined a barony carved out of the conquest, his name spoken in the same breath as the Emperor's.

What he had found was a woman made of light, giving herself away piece by piece to a darkness that ate her like a meal. What he had found was a child standing between a drawn sword and a breaking world, unafraid, speaking a language that made Vane's teeth ache. What he had found, in the last instant before the mountain shuddered and the light swallowed everything, was a single glimpse through the widening crack—a glimpse of the other side, of what waited there, patient and vast and *aware*—and it had looked back at him, and something in Aldric Vane had quietly, permanently broken.

His men remembered less than he did. They spoke of a skirmish with free-city scouts, of losses, of a valley that made their wounds fester and their dreams run black. They asked him, on the road south, what he would report.

Vane looked at the mountains receding behind them, wrapped in cloud, indistinguishable from a hundred other ranges.

"Nothing," he said. "There's nothing there. Dead valleys, dead passes. We mark it barren and move on."

"Sir, the orders—"

"The orders are wrong." His voice came out flat and final, a door closing. "There is no door. There is nothing in those mountains worth a single soldier's life. Write it down: *barren.*"

They wrote it down. The mapmakers inked the range with the sign for impassable waste, and the usurper's armies swung east toward softer country, and the free cities bought themselves a season they did not know they had been given.

Vane never spoke of the valley again. But some nights, in garrison towns far to the south, he woke with his heart hammering and the taste of iron in his mouth, and he would lie staring at the dark, trying to remember what he had seen through the crack in the world—and failing, always failing, though not, he suspected, because the memory had faded.

Because something had taken it. Something had reached through the closing door and drunk him down to the dregs, and what remained of Aldric Vane was a man with a hole in him shaped exactly like a valley, and he would carry it, silent and unreported, for the rest of his days.

***

In the cottage beneath Highmoor, the woman who stayed sleeps a great deal.

When she is awake she works, because work is in her bones even if nothing else is. She checks the wards—her feet know the path down to the gate chamber, her hands know the stones—and the wards hum their bright silence, and she feels, without understanding it, that they are *full*, that they have been fed, and she is strangely comforted, and does not ask by what.

Tam teaches her things. Small things, patiently offered: the names of her own herbs, the trick to lighting the stubborn stove, the words to a song about a fox and a moon. She learns them the way children learn, fresh and without the ache of remembering, and sometimes she catches him watching her learn with an expression she cannot read—grief and tenderness tangled together like roots.

"Why do you stay with me?" she asks him one evening, over the mending. "A boy your age. Alone with a stranger in a frozen valley. Anyone sane would have gone down to the cities."

Tam considers the fire for a long moment.

"Someone has to remember you," he says at last. "Until you can remember yourself."

She does not understand what he means. But the words land somewhere deep, in a place below memory, and she reaches across the table and touches his hand—the small hand, the cold hand, the hand that fits the bruise on her palm—and outside the window the mountain stands watch under the stars, and the valley holds its breathless, magicless peace, and somewhere far to the east the free cities are burning through the last of autumn, unaware of the season that has been bought for them, or the price.

In the morning, Tam says, there is someone coming up the pass. Refugees, he thinks. Many of them.

And the woman who stayed rises, and banks the fire, and begins, without knowing why, to count the bowls.
