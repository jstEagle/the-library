## 10. Spring at the Edge of the Map

The snow went first from the southern slopes, retreating up the mountain like a tide running out, and Tam watched it go from the cottage door with a bowl of barley porridge cooling in his hands.

Below the valley, where the pass widened into the foothills, smoke rose now from a dozen fires that had not been there in winter. The refugees had come in trickles at first — a shepherd's family, two brothers pulling a handcart of salt fish — and then, as the passes cleared, in streams. They came out of the burning east with nothing but what they carried on their backs, and they came because word had moved faster than any army: *there is a valley under Highmoor where the world goes quiet. There is a valley where it cannot follow you.*

They did not know what *it* was. Most of them had never seen magic do anything worse than light a hearth or cure a fever. But they had seen what came behind it. They had seen the usurper's war-hosts marching under banners stitched with sigils that hurt to look at, and they had heard what happened to towns that stood in the road. So they walked into the dead air of Highmoor Valley and felt their charms go cold and their wards go dark and their healer's gifts fall silent in their hands, and they wept with relief.

Tam understood them. He had been born on the other side of the silence, and the silence was the kindest thing he had ever felt.

He set the porridge down inside the door and went back to watching the path. The woman was still sleeping. She slept more than she used to, and woke gently, like something surfacing through deep water, and every morning she looked around the cottage as if confirming it against some list she could no longer read.

"Tam," she had said to him, on the second morning, before any of this. "That is my name for you? I seem to have always known it."

"You've always known it," he said, which was true, and let her make of it what she would.

She remembered him. That was the mercy in it, or perhaps the design — the gate had taken everything she fed it, and she had fed it everything, but it had left her the shape of her duty the way fire leaves the outline of a burned house. She knew a boy needed tending. She knew the mountain must be watched. She knew the words of the warding when they rose in her throat, though if you asked her where she'd learned them she would frown and say, *they're just words, aren't they? Everyone knows them.* No one knew them. Only she and Tam remained, in all the world, who spoke the tongue of the old kingdoms, and only one of them had been born to it.

* * *

In the third week of spring he began teaching her.

Not the old language — not yet. He started smaller. He started with the things a person needs to be a person: the names of trees, the taste of things, the small catalog of a life.

"That one is alder," he said, walking the streambank with her. "You showed me last summer. You said it likes wet feet."

"Alder," she repeated, and touched the bark, and something in her face settled, the way a picture settles when you hang it straight. "I said that?"

"You say it every year, I think." This was a guess. He was building her a past out of guesses and fragments other people gave him, and he had learned to be careful, because a false memory sat wrong in her the way a stone sits wrong in a shoe. "The woman Maren from the mill remembers you trading her flour for honey, three autumns running. Would you like me to tell you about it?"

"Tell me," Marguerite said, and so he told her: how Maren's husband had died in the fever year and how Marguerite had sat with her through a whole winter of evenings, saying very little, just being a warm body in a cold house. As he told it, he watched her take possession of it — watched her eyes go soft and distant, watched her nod slowly, watched the story stop being his and become hers.

"It sounds like me," she said wonderingly. "Is it like me?"

"It's exactly like you."

It was not, entirely. It was built from what others remembered of her, and from the journal — her mother's journal, which Tam had read cover to cover by lamplight while she slept, learning the woman she had unmade. The journal's later pages were written in a hand that grew plainer and barer year by year, until the last entries were almost childlike: *Wards strong. Fed it the smell of bread. Fed it my sister's laugh. Fed it —* and then a page torn out, and then, at the very end, in letters pressed nearly through the paper: *DO NOT FEED IT THE REASON. KEEP THE REASON. A WARDEN WITHOUT A REASON ONLY WATCHES.*

Marguerite had fed it the reason. Tam knew which memory it was; the gate had shown him, in the last moment before it closed, when everything she was poured past him like a river and he stood in the shallows holding one thing back. He kept it folded inside himself, wrapped in the old language where the valley's silence couldn't reach. Someday — not yet, maybe not ever — he would give it back to her. A warden should choose her own reasons. But a warden without any reasons at all was only a watcher, and Tam refused to let her be only a watcher.

So he taught her spring. He taught her that the first green on the mountain was wild garlic and that she hated onions but loved the smell of rain on dust. He taught her that she sang low, under her breath, when she counted bowls. He taught her the refugees' names as they arrived, and made her repeat them until the valley held forty-one souls and she knew every one.

* * *

By mid-spring the settlement below had walls — rough palisade, more gesture than defense, but the refugees needed to build something and Tam understood that too. Building was its own kind of warding. The smith among them, a broad silent woman named Kettrin who had fled the fall of Marrowgate, set up a forge, and the sound of her hammer carried up the valley all day, ringing off the mountain, and Marguerite said it sounded like the valley was learning to speak again.

They were safe. That was the truth none of them quite dared say aloud. The usurper's armies had rolled over the free cities' outer lands like a wave over sandcastles, and still the valley lay untouched — not hidden, precisely, for travelers found it easily enough, but *uninteresting*, the way a room is uninteresting once the lamp is lit. Magic could not follow the refugees here, and the usurper's power was magic to its bones; his scouts rode to the lip of the pass, felt the dead air press on them like deep water, and turned away without knowing why. Captain Aldric Vane had sent back a report, Tam learned from a trader: *Nothing in the valley worth the army's time. Poor land. Hostile wards, decayed. Recommend bypass.*

Vane himself had come once more, alone, in the early spring, and stood at the edge of the settlement with his helmet under his arm like a man at a funeral. Tam had gone out to meet him. They had looked at each other a long time — the captain who had put his sword to a warden's throat, the fragment-child who had stood between them while the world ended quietly.

"She doesn't remember me," Vane had said.

"No."

"Good." He had looked at the mountain a long moment, jaw working. "What I saw in that chamber — I'll carry it till I die. But I'll carry it alone. My report says there's nothing here." His voice had cracked, just slightly. "There's nothing here. Understand? Tell her—" He had stopped. Started again. "Tell her the valley eats magic. Tell her that's why I'm leaving it alive."

"I'll tell her," Tam had said, though he hadn't, because it would only have been a stone sitting wrong in her shoe.

Vane had ridden out of the valley and not come back, and somewhere far to the south the war went on consuming the map, and here at the edge of the map the snow retreated and the barley came up green.

* * *

On an evening in late spring, when the light lay gold along the stream and the settlement's fires were being lit below, Marguerite sat on the cottage step shelling peas and asked him the question he had been waiting a season for.

"Tam. What was it? What did I give away?"

He kept his hands moving on the pea pods. "Why do you think you gave anything away?"

"Because I'm full of other people's memories like borrowed clothes. Because I know the warding words but not my mother's face. Because—" She stopped, and looked at her hands, and he saw the grief move through her, grief without an object, the cruelest kind. "Because there's a hole in me shaped like something I loved, and I can feel the wind through it."

Tam set down the peas. He reached inside himself, to the folded place, the wrapped place, and held it a moment — her mother's face, laughing, young, the reason she stayed — and then, carefully, he closed his hands around it and kept it still.

"Not yet," he said. "But soon. And when I give it back, you'll understand everything, and you'll have to decide whether to grieve or to be glad. And either way you'll stay, because staying is what you are."

She studied him for a long time — this boy of eight years or eight hundred, who had come through a dying gate wearing a child's skin, and who now sat beside her shelling peas in the gold light. Then she leaned over and rested her head briefly against his shoulder, the way a mother might, the way a friend might, the way someone does when words are too heavy to lift.

"All right," she said. "Not yet. But Tam—"

"Yes?"

"When you teach me things. Teach me the old words too. All of them. If the silence ever fails, someone should remember how to sing it shut again."

Below them, Kettrin's hammer rang. Above them the mountain stood, its hunger fed forever, dreaming whatever mountains dream. And between those two sounds, in the narrow band of quiet where human life goes on regardless, the last speaker of a dead tongue began to teach the first word of it to the woman who stayed:

*Highmoor*, he said, in the language older than kingdoms. *The mountain that keeps.*

And she repeated it, once, twice, until it was hers.
