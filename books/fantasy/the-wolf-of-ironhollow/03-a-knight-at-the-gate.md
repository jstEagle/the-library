## 3. A Knight at the Gate

Felix had learned, over twenty-two years of careful living, that trouble announced itself in small ways. A candle guttering when there was no draft. A dog that would not stop barking at an empty road. The particular quality of silence that fell over the temple courtyard when Brother Anselm was about to ask for someone by name.

So when the bells of the Quiet Flame rang out of sequence—three strokes past midnight, when they should have slept until matins—Felix was already awake in his cell above the scriptorium, already dressed, already listening.

He told himself it was habit. He told himself a great many things like that: that he stayed up late because the copying went faster in the dark hours, that he kept his boots by the door because he was diligent, that he memorized the creak of every stair and the sightline of every window because a good scribe respected order. All of it true in the way a locked door is true. It kept things out. It also, he understood on nights like this one, kept him in.

The third bell finished its stroke and did not fade the way it should have. It hung in the valley air, thin and strange, as though the bronze itself were uncertain. Felix pressed his palm against the cold sill of his window and looked down into Ironhollow.

The valley lay silvered under a waning moon, terraced fields stepping down to the river, the town's slate roofs huddled around the temple spire like children around a hearth. Nothing moved. Nothing ever moved, not really—not beyond what the seasons permitted, not beyond what the Temple sanctioned. That was the whole of the bargain Ironhollow had made with whatever power held its borders: nothing in, nothing out, nothing *changed*. The prayers Felix copied by the thousand went up like smoke and came back as ash, and everyone agreed this was holiness.

Except lately the ash had been answering back.

He pushed the thought down where he kept all the other thoughts—the moving ink, the widow's rain, the way his fingertips still sometimes stung as if he'd gripped something hot—and took the narrow servants' stair down into the scriptorium proper.

The great room smelled of oak gall ink, tallow, and old vellum, a smell so familiar it had stopped registering years ago, the way a man stops hearing his own heartbeat. Moonlight fell through the high lancet windows in pale columns, striping the long copying tables. Forty desks, each with its candle-niche and its slanted board. Felix knew every scratch in every surface. He had spent more of his life bent over these boards than he had spent asleep.

He meant only to look out, to confirm the night was ordinary, to return to bed and let the bell's strangeness be tomorrow's problem for someone whose name wasn't written in the debt-ledger.

Then he saw the gate.

The temple gate stood at the head of the pilgrim road, iron-banded oak twice the height of a man, closed at nightfall and opened at dawn by brothers sworn to the office for life. Felix could see it from the scriptorium's eastern window, framed between two cypress trees. And standing before it—*against* it, her fist still raised from knocking or from pounding—was a figure in armor.

That alone would have been impossible. Pilgrims arrived on foot and on holy days, dusty and bowed, and even then they waited at the outer wall for the wardens to admit them. No one wore steel in Ironhollow. Steel was for the wars in the outer kingdom, the wars that happened to other people, the wars the valley's wards kept out along with everything else.

But the figure was not merely wearing steel. She was *leaning* on the gate, and as Felix watched, her legs simply quit beneath her. She slid down the iron-bound oak in a slow, graceless collapse, and did not get up.

For a long moment Felix stood in the moon-striped dark and did the arithmetic he had been trained to do all his life. *Not my concern.* A stranger at the gate was a matter for the wardens, for Brother Anselm, for anyone whose name carried weight. If Felix reported it, he would be thanked in the flat voice reserved for useful nobodies, and if he touched it—if he *helped*—he would be noticed, and being noticed in Ironhollow was the first step toward owing, and owing was the first step toward ruin. His mother had taught him that with her last breath, more or less. *Be the ink, Lixie, never the page.*

The woman at the gate shifted. Even at this distance he could see the dark smear she left on the oak behind her.

Felix discovered that his feet had already made the decision his mind was still arguing about.

* * *

The postern door near the herb garden stuck in wet weather; Felix knew exactly which shoulder-height spot to hit, and it gave with its usual complaint. He crossed the kitchen yard keeping to the shadow of the wall, heart hammering not with fear, he realized, but with something worse—a thin, shameful exhilaration, like the taste of stolen honey. Twenty-two years of invisibility, and here he was, sneaking through his own cloister at midnight toward a bleeding stranger, feeling *alive*.

The outer wall loomed. Beyond it, the ward-line ran invisible through the pass, the border of the world. Felix had never seen it, no one had, but every child in Ironhollow grew up knowing it the way fish know water: the valley ended where the wards began, and what lay beyond was not their business.

The woman was crumpled at the base of the gate, half in the light of the moon, half out. Up close she was younger than her armor suggested—thirty perhaps, maybe less, with short-cropped brown hair plastered to her forehead with sweat and blood. Her mail shirt was ruined, torn across the ribs, links fused together in a way that made Felix's stomach turn, because metal didn't melt like that without heat beyond any forge he'd ever heard described. Under the mail, her gambeson was black with blood.

She heard him coming—or some part of her did. Her hand snapped to her sword hilt with a speed that belonged to a much healthier body, and then her eyes found him and confusion flickered across a face gray with pain.

"You're a child," she rasped.

"I'm a scribe," Felix said, which was both true and beside the point. "You're bleeding on the temple gate."

Something that might have been laughter cost her a cough. Blood showed on her lips. "Scribe. Good. Write this down." Her eyes were losing focus even as she spoke. "'Maren Vale, Knight of the Order of the—'" Another cough. "Of the—damn it. Write down that I almost made it."

"Made it where?" Felix crouched beside her, close enough now to see the wound properly. The mail had been torn open below her breastplate, and beneath the padding there was a gash long as his forearm, deep enough that he could see the glisten of things he preferred not to name. She should not have been walking. He honestly did not understand how she was alive. "You need a healer."

"The healers in this valley are your temple." Her hand found the front of his robe and gripped with surprising strength, dragging him closer. Her breath was hot against his face, fevered. "And your temple will kill me. Do you understand me? They'll open my satchel, they'll find it, and they'll burn me and everything I'm carrying and probably half your pretty valley with it. So you can fetch them—" she gestured weakly at the gate, at the whole sleeping edifice of the Quiet Flame, "—or you can help me, and one of us dies tonight either way, but at least I'll die owing a stranger instead of a god."

Every instinct Felix owned screamed at him to pull away. This was how it started. This was how debt started—with a favor, with a kindness, with a moment of weakness in a dark courtyard. He could hear his mother's voice, hear Anselm's sermons, hear the ledger-pages turning in his sleep.

Instead he heard himself say: "Why would your own temple burn you?"

"Not mine." Her grip loosened. "Crown inquisitors. There are four of them. They crossed the pass behind me." Her eyes drifted toward the darkness beyond the wall, toward the invisible line where the world ended. "They'll reach the wards by morning. And when they do—" She frowned, as if the next part hurt worse than the wound. "When they do, I don't know what happens. Nobody knows. The wards haven't been tested in a hundred years."

Felix looked at her—at the melted mail, at the blood, at the sword she hadn't managed to draw—and felt the ground shift under the settled architecture of his life. Inquisitors. Crown soldiers at the border of a valley that had not seen a soldier since his grandfather's grandfather. The bells ringing wrong. The prayers answering back.

Everything was already changing. The question was only whether it changed while Felix stood safely aside, or whether he stood in the middle of it.

"Can you walk?" he asked.

Her laugh again, weaker. "Ask me something easy."

"There's a door," Felix said. "Forty paces. Can you make forty paces?"

"For forty paces," Ser Maren Vale said, "I can make myself do anything."

She couldn't, quite. Felix got her arm over his shoulders and took her weight, and she lasted perhaps thirty of the forty paces before her legs went again, and after that it was mostly dragging, the two of them staggering and sliding along the inner wall, leaving a trail any novice could follow at dawn. Twice she bit down hard enough on her own sound of pain that Felix heard her teeth click. At the postern by the herb garden she passed out entirely, and Felix, who had never in his life lifted anything heavier than a stack of ledgers, hauled a knight in full ruined mail through a doorway built for carrying cabbages.

He left the trail. There was nothing to be done about it. But the night was moonless in the yard's lee, and rain had fallen at dusk—the widow's answered rain, said a small voice in his head, *the prayer that shouldn't have worked*—and the packed earth had gone soft. Small mercies. Or large ones. Or answers.

The undercroft beneath the scriptorium had once stored the temple's wine. Then a reforming prior had declared drink an enemy of clarity, and the racks had come out, and the room had become a graveyard of broken furniture and condemned records—ledgers too damaged to read and too sacred to burn, stacked in drifts against stone walls that sweated cold. Felix had spent three unhappy months of his novitiate cataloguing it. He knew the dry corner behind the collapsed shelving unit. He knew the draft that came through the old vent shaft, just enough to keep a wounded woman breathing.

He got her onto his own folded cloak, cut the ruined mail off her with the scribe's knife he used for trimming quills—it took an absurdly long time, and he ruined the knife—and did what little he could. Clean linen from the binding-room. Boiled water. Pressure on the wound, which made her wake with a sound he would remember for years. Wine from the flask he kept against the winter damp, which she drank like a woman dying of thirst.

"You're going to sew me up with bookbinding thread, aren't you," she muttered.

"It's linen. It's strong."

"It's *sacred* linen, isn't it. From your altar cupboard." But she was smiling, or trying to, gray lips stretched over clenched teeth. "Do it, then. I've been stitched by worse men with better thread."

He stitched her. His hands, trained to a precision most surgeons would envy, did not shake, which surprised him. She passed out twice and cursed him awake both times. When it was done—ugly work, but holding—he sat back on his heels among the condemned ledgers and looked at what he had done.

A stranger lay hidden beneath the scriptorium of the Temple of the Quiet Flame. By dawn, someone would find the blood-trail in the yard, or the missing knife, or the empty altar-cupboard, and questions would travel upward as questions always did in the temple, from novice to brother to elder, and somewhere at the top of that ladder a man with a ledger would write Felix Oyelaran's name in it, and his debt—which had taken his entire life to climb within sight of—would grow.

And beyond the wall, in the dark, four crown inquisitors were walking toward the wards of Ironhollow, carrying whatever it was Maren Vale had bled to keep from them.

"What's in the satchel?" Felix asked quietly.

She was mostly unconscious, but the word reached her somewhere deep. Her hand crawled across the stones, found the leather bag she'd refused to let go of even while he dragged her, and pulled it against her chest.

"Tell me," Felix said. "If I'm going to be damned for this, I want to know what for."

One eye opened. Fever-bright, fixed on him with unsettling steadiness.

"A dragon-scale," she whispered. "Sealed with the king's own blood. And a message that has to reach someone the scale chooses." Her eye moved over Felix's face, over his ink-stained fingers, and something in her expression flickered—recognition, or its beginning—but she was too far gone to finish the thought. "It hasn't chosen anyone yet," she murmured. "It hasn't warmed for anyone in a year. Not for me. Not for anyone."

She slept. Felix sat in the dark among the dead records, listening to her breathe and to the rain starting again outside—the second rain in a season that had known none—and after a while, because his hands needed doing and his mind needed quiet, he picked up the satchel she would not release, worked it gently from her grip, and looked inside.

On top of a bundle wrapped in oilcloth lay a scale the size of his palm. It should have been dull, dead thing, a relic. Instead it threw back the faint light of his shuttered lamp like a coal remembering fire, shifting through colors he had no names for—and as Felix's fingers hovered over it, the scale *warmed*.

He snatched his hand back. The warmth lingered in the air where his hand had been, faint and unmistakable, the way a hearthstone holds heat after the fire dies.

Ser Maren Vale breathed slow and deep in the dark, and did not see.

Felix wrapped the satchel closed with hands that were, at last, trembling, and told himself—as he had told himself a thousand times—that he had imagined it. That he was tired. That the world was orderly, and the valley was silent, and he was the ink, never the page.

Outside, far off toward the pass, something howled. It might have been wind.

It was not wind.
