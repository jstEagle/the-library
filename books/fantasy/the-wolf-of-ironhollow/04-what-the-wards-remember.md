## 4. What the Wards Remember

The undercroft smelled of dust and old tallow and, faintly, of fear.

Maren Vale had known many kinds of dark in her thirty years—the black under a helm at midday, the dark behind her eyelids when she prayed for sleep that would not come—but this was a new one: the close, dry dark beneath a temple of a god whose whole doctrine was silence. Above her, through the floorboards, she could hear the muffled shuffling of feet, the scrape of chairs, the occasional cough of an elder clearing his throat. The Quiet Flame's faithful went about their business like people trying not to wake something. She supposed they were. Everyone in this valley walked as though the world were holding its breath.

She sat with her back against a shelf of ledgers older than the kingdom, her sword across her knees because setting it down felt like surrender, and waited for the scribe to come back.

Felix Oyelaran. That was the name stitched into the collar of his robe, half worn away by washing. He had found her at the gate half-dead from fever and inquisitors both, and instead of raising the alarm he had looked at her for a long moment—looked at her the way men look at a cliff edge, measuring whether to fall—and then he had helped her down off her horse and hidden her horse in the woodshed and brought her here, into the cold belly of the very temple whose hounds were hunting her.

She did not trust him. She wanted to, badly, the way a drowning woman wants to trust any hand at all, but wanting was how knights got killed. Wanting was how she had failed Aldric.

She flexed her burned hand in the dark. The dragon-scale lay wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack, and even through three layers of leather and linen she could feel it now, faintly warm, like a stone kept near a hearth. It had never been warm before Ironhollow.

Footsteps on the stair. Light, quick, careful—the tread of someone who had spent his life being unheard. Maren's hand found her hilt out of pure habit, then released it. A knight who drew on a boy carrying bread was no knight at all.

He came down the last steps with a covered lantern held low, and the light caught his face first: young, maybe twenty-two, with ink stains worked so deep into the creases of his fingers they looked like bruises, and eyes that flinched from hers and then made themselves come back. She knew that look too. It was the look of a man who had practiced invisibility until it became a discipline, and who resented, somewhere far down, how good he had gotten at it.

"You shouldn't be here after terce," he whispered. "The elders walk the scriptorium floor until vespers. I told them I was inventorying the sealed records." He set down a basket: bread, hard cheese, a clay jug sloshing with small beer. His hands shook. "You're bleeding again."

"It stopped."

"It stopped *into* your bandage. That's not stopping, that's pooling." He knelt beside her before she could object, and there was something almost funny in it—a scribe, a boy who copied prayers for a living, unwinding a knight's dressing with the brisk tenderness of a camp surgeon. The wound across her ribs had closed ugly. He looked at it without flinching, which surprised her more than anything else he'd done. "This was a blade?"

"A hook. Inquisitors favor hooks. They say it's harder to run with a hooked man." She let him work. "You're good at that."

"Copying prayers teaches you patience. Patience is most of medicine. The rest is not panicking." He tied off the bandage, and only then did his composure crack a little; he sat back on his heels and looked at her, really looked, and asked the question she had been waiting three days for him to ask. "Why are they hunting you? Truly. Not whatever story you gave me at the gate."

Maren leaned her head back against the ledger shelf. Somewhere above them, an elder coughed twice and moved on.

"If I tell you," she said, "you can't unknow it. And you live inside the thing I'd be telling you about. You understand? This valley—" she gestured vaguely upward, at the wards she could feel like a pressure behind her eyes ever since crossing the border, a silence so total it ached, "—this valley runs on not-knowing."

"Felix Oyelaran," he said quietly, "has been running on not-knowing since the day he was born. Tell me anyway."

* * *

She told him about the king first, because it was the part she could tell without her voice breaking.

"I was sworn shield to Aldric Vane. Third of his name. I stood at his right shoulder for nine years. I held the door when the ambassadors screamed at each other, I carried his standard at Redford Bridge, I—" She stopped. Breathed. The scribe watched her with those flinching, returning eyes. "A year ago last midwinter, the king stopped sleeping. Then he stopped eating in public. Then, one morning at council, he opened his mouth to answer the Lord Treasurer, and what came out was his voice, every cadence of it perfect, but it wasn't *him*. I can't explain better than that. Words are mine, I know words—I've heard Aldric angry, grieving, drunk, terrified. This was none of them. It was a performance of him. Flawless and empty, like a coin struck from lead and gilded over."

Felix's pen-hand twitched, an unconscious motion, as if reaching for ink to write the feeling down.

"The physicians said melancholia. The priests said a wandering spirit. The Queen Mother said grief for his father and dosed him with poppy until he drooled at state dinners." Maren's jaw ached; she realized she was clenching it. "I said nothing. That's my confession, scribe. I saw it and I said nothing, because saying it aloud meant losing everything—my oath, my rank, my head. I told myself a king's strangeness was a king's own affair. For four months I told myself that while the thing wearing my king sold border forts to our enemies and signed away the forest charters and smiled at crowds with a smile that never reached past the teeth."

"And then?"

"And then I found the letters." Her voice dropped. "Hidden in his dispatch case, in cipher—his old campaign cipher, the one only his shield-knights knew. Written in his true hand, shaky, getting worse month by month. *I am still here. I am under the ice. Do not trust what wears me. Find the fire.* Over and over, in different forms. He was drowning, Felix, and writing messages in bottles from underneath, hoping anyone brave or stupid enough would read them." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I read them. And I was stupid enough to act, and clever enough to get caught doing it, and the only reason I'm alive is that the scale was in my boot when they took my sword."

"The scale." His gaze went to her pack. "You said royal blood was used to seal it."

"Aldric's own, drawn the night before midwinter, before—" Before the thing got its grip fully set, she didn't say. "It's a key and a message both. Dragon-scale doesn't exist anymore, do you understand? There hasn't been a living dragon in the outer kingdom in two hundred years. Whatever the king knew, whoever told him, he sealed it in the last relic of the last fire in the world and trusted it to me." She dragged the pack over and began unwrapping the oilcloth. "And now I have to show you why I crossed a warded valley full of people who hate magic to bring it here."

* * *

The scale was the size of her spread palm, dark red shading to black at the edges, veined with lines of dull gold like cooling lava. In the lantern light it should have gleamed. Instead it seemed to drink the light and hold it, the way coals hold heat after the fire dies.

"This is Emberfall's," she said. "Last of the dragons. The king learned of her the way he learned everything important—from documents no one else reads. Old land grants. Ward-charters." She watched the scribe's face carefully as she said the word. "Do you know what a ward-charter is, Felix?"

"No."

"Neither did I, and I was the king's shield. They're not taught. They're not *allowed* to be taught. But I spent a year reading everything Aldric left me, and here is what I know." She laid the scale flat on the flagstones between them. "Three hundred years ago, this valley wasn't a sanctuary. It was a prison. Something was bound here—something that eats worship, that grows fat on belief, that wore kings before Aldric and will wear kings after him if no one stops it. Your ancestors built the wards that keep it starved and asleep. Seven families. Seven bloodlines woven into seven anchor-stones, and the valley's silence is the lock, and the price of the lock is that nothing magical can live here—not god, not dragon, not answered prayer."

"That's nonsense," Felix said automatically. "Magic isn't real. The elders teach—"

"The elders teach that magic isn't real *because* the ward makes it true here. That's the genius of it. The lie that enforces itself." Maren leaned forward. "But locks fail, Felix. And a week ago, in a village called Marrow's Bend on your southern edge, a widow prayed for rain and woke to find her fields drowned. Two days ago, a child dead of fever breathed again. I know because I've been following the miracles backward across three counties, and every one of them points *here*. The wards are failing. The prisoner is waking up. And it has already found one door out."

She turned the scale over. On its underside, scratched fine as spiderwork, was a line of old script she had traced a hundred times without being able to read—and beside it, a sigil: seven interlocked rings around a single vertical stroke.

"Seven anchors," she said. "One of them is failing faster than the rest. The scale can feel it—it's been warm since I crossed your border, warmer every day, like a compass needle straining toward—"

Felix reached out and touched the scale.

* * *

Later, Maren would remember the sound more than the sight: a low hum, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, rising through the flagstones, through the shelves, through the bones of the building itself. The light in the scale's golden veins kindled all at once, gold flooding red flooding white, and heat rolled off it in a visible shimmer, and Felix Oyelaran snatched his hand back with a cry and fell against the shelving, clutching his fingers, and every loose scrap of paper in the undercroft lifted an inch off its surface and hung there, trembling, before drifting down like snow.

Silence. The ordinary, engineered silence of Ironhollow.

"What," Felix whispered, "was that."

Maren stared at him. At his hand, where the marks of the scales' veins had printed themselves briefly across his palm in glowing lines, already fading. At his face, where terror and something else—something hungrier, more frightened than terror—fought openly for the first time since she'd met him.

"That," she said slowly, "is what I came here to learn. Because the sigil on the underside—the seven rings and the stroke—" She picked up the scale, careful of its fading warmth, and turned it toward him. "I finally found a text that named it. It's not just the seal of the founders. It's a family mark. The stroke is a stylus, Felix. A scribe's pen. The anchor-bloodline of Ironhollow, the one the whole lattice hangs from, the line that was never supposed to leave the valley—" She watched him understand, watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out. "Your family founded the wards. Your blood is the keystone. And I don't think the scale warmed up because the wards are failing."

Her voice gentled, though gentleness felt obscene given what she was handing him.

"I think it warmed up because you're here."

Felix looked at his own hand as if it belonged to a stranger. When he spoke, his voice was thin and steady in the way of a man walking a ledge.

"No. No—you've made a mistake. We're debtors. We copy prayers. My father copied prayers, and his mother, and we've never—we're *nothing*, that's the entire point of us, we're meant to be nothing—" He stopped. Swallowed. "If what you're saying is true, then my whole life—"

"Has been a cage built around you specifically. Yes."

"Then the temple debt—"

"Is a chain with a very old link." Maren rose, joints protesting, and stood over him where he sat among the settled papers like a man among ashes. "I won't pretend to soften it. If I'm right, the thing wearing my king has been sending prayers into this valley like fishhooks, waiting for the wards to thin enough for one to be *answered*—waiting to draw out whoever the answer would matter to most. It knows the keystone line survives. It may already know your name." She offered him her unburned hand. "So. Now you can't unknow it either, scribe. The question is what you do tomorrow morning, when the elders ask you what you found in the sealed records."

Felix did not take her hand. He was looking at the scale, at the dying gold in its veins, and Maren saw in his face the exact expression she had seen once before—on Aldric, in the last letter, the shaky true handwriting that said *find the fire*.

"They'll notice," he said at last. "The ink on the widow's petition. It moved. I'm the only one who checked the archive copies, and I logged the discrepancy, and Brother Osric reads every log I file." He laughed, short and hollow. "I have spent twenty-two years learning how to be invisible. And in one night I've become the most visible thing in this valley."

"Then you have until vespers," Maren said, "to decide who you are when they see you."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the temple bell began to ring—and beneath it, so soft that Maren might have imagined it, she heard another sound: a long, slow exhalation, like something vast turning over in its sleep, and the candle flames in the undercroft all bent sideways at once, though no door had opened.

Felix's head came up sharply.

"That," he whispered, "doesn't happen here. Nothing *moves* here."

The bell rang on. And somewhere above them, unhurried footsteps began to descend the stairs.
