## 6. The Color of the World

Felix had copied ten thousand prayers in his life, and not one of them had prepared him for the sky.

It was the first thing. Before the pain, before the fear, before Maren's hand hauling him upright in wet grass while he vomited up everything Ironhollow had ever fed him—it was the sky. In the valley, the heavens were a gray lid, a ceiling of pewter and ash that never quite changed and never quite mattered. Scribes learned not to look up; there was nothing up there but weather, and weather was the temple's business, not theirs.

But here, past the ward-line, past the standing stones with their worn-off names, the sky went on forever, and it was *blue*—not gray-blue, not the blue of ink diluted to stretch a budget, but blue like a shout, blue like something alive that had been waiting all this time for someone to notice it. Clouds moved. Actually moved, dragging their shadows across hills so green they looked wet-painted. And the light—Felix kept turning his hand over in it, watching it fall on his knuckles like warm water.

"It hits you like this for everyone," Maren said. She was crouched nearby, rewrapping her bandages, watching him with something between sympathy and amusement. "First hour outside. I cried into a ditch when I was nine."

"I'm not crying."

"Your eyes are leaking, scribe."

"That's the wind."

"There is no wind." There wasn't. Felix wiped his face with his sleeve and decided, with the last of his dignity, to stop talking about it.

The pain came next, and it did not stop coming. Crossing the ward-line had felt like being flayed by light—every nerve in him singing a note he hadn't known his body could hold—and even now, hours later, magic sat in him like swallowed lightning. He could feel it in *everything*. That was the horror of it, and the beauty too, and he could not separate the two no matter how he tried. The grass under his boots hummed. The stones in the old road murmured to each other in voices like distant bells. When Maren handed him a strip of dried meat, he felt the animal it had been, faint and sad, and had to close his eyes and breathe before he could eat.

"You'll learn to turn it down," she said. "Like closing a shutter. The gift's a window; you don't have to stand at it all day."

"And if I don't want the window at all?"

She snorted. "Then you should have stayed home."

Home. The word landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for the splash and heard nothing—just the long dark falling, and falling. He thought of the scriptorium, of his desk by the north window, of the precise rows of his own handwriting marching across vellum like soldiers who'd never once disobeyed. He thought of Brother Osric's face as the temple guards dragged Felix out—Osric, who had looked *away*, who had said the word *heretic* like spitting, who had been Felix's only friend since they were boys copying penance psalms side by side.

He had spent thirty years making himself small enough to fit inside Ironhollow. It turned out he had never fit at all. The valley had simply been holding its breath around him, the way a cage holds a bird it hasn't measured yet.

They walked. The world kept happening to him.

By afternoon he had catalogued, in the neat mental hand of a lifelong copyist, forty-one colors that had no name in any manuscript he'd ever touched. The temple inks came in black, red, and a poverty-grade brown; the valley itself came in green, brown, and gray. But here a field of some flowering weed burned violet-orange at the edges where the sun struck it, and a hawk's shadow passed over ground the color of hammered copper, and Maren's battered cloak, which he had taken for gray, turned out to be dyed a deep bruised plum that only showed itself in honest light. He kept wanting to stop and mix pigments that didn't exist, to write down what he saw, and there was no time and no vellum and no one who would believe him anyway.

And the noise. Ironhollow was quiet the way tombs are quiet—not peaceful, just emptied. Here the world was *loud*. Insects droning in chords. A river arguing with its own rocks somewhere below the ridge. Birds screaming insults at each other across whole valleys. His ears, tuned to the scratch of quills and the murmur of lecterns, ached with it—but it was a good ache, the ache of a muscle remembering use.

Once, crossing a meadow, he stopped dead because the flowers turned to watch him pass.

Not all of them. Just a patch of tall white blossoms near the path, their heads swiveling slowly, politely, like congregation members tracking a preacher. Felix stood very still. The flowers waited very still. Somewhere in their stems he felt a drowsy, vegetable attention, neither kind nor cruel, simply *aware*.

"Maren."

"I see them. Keep walking. They're only curious." She glanced back. "Most things out here are only curious. It's the ones that aren't that you learn about fast."

"What do they want?"

"To know what you are." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't match her voice. "Which, incidentally, is the same question everyone else is going to ask. So decide your answer soon."

Felix walked on. Behind him, he felt the flowers lose interest, one by one, their attention sliding off him like rain off wax. He found he couldn't decide whether that was a relief or an insult.

* *

They reached the eyrie on the second evening, or what Maren insisted was an eyrie and Felix's eyes insisted was a wound in the mountainside.

The road had been climbing all day through country gone quietly wrong—trees leaning at angles trees shouldn't lean, streams running briefly uphill before thinking better of it, a farmhouse whose roof tiles had been arranged, by nobody human, into patterns that hurt to look at directly. Magic pooled and eddied here like water finding old channels. Felix had stopped flinching at it around midday. Now he just felt it, the way you feel weather coming in a healed bone.

The eyrie had been carved—or grown—into a granite shoulder of the mountain: terraces of black stone spiraling upward, arches wide enough for wings, stairways broad as roads ascending in sweeps meant for creatures the size of churches. Most of it had fallen. Great slabs lay tumbled down the slope, furred with moss, their carvings worn to suggestion. Whatever dragons had needed from architecture, humans had built it grandly and then abandoned it, and the mountain was patiently eating the ruin back.

Smoke rose from the highest terrace. Thin, gray, domestic smoke—the first ordinary thing Felix had seen all day.

"Someone lives here?" he whispered.

"One someone. Maybe two, if you count the dragon, and you should count the dragon." Maren started up the ruined stair, moving carefully on her bad leg. "Her name is Emberfall. She's dying, scribe. Has been dying for years—dragons die slowly, like empires. Wren keeps her fire fed and her scales oiled and pretends neither of them is grieving. Try not to say anything stupid."

"What would I say that's stupid?"

"You're a temple scribe approaching a dragon-keeper. Everything you say will be stupid. Lead with silence."

Felix, who had survived thirty years in Ironhollow largely by leading with silence, took this advice to heart.

The top terrace opened onto a courtyard half-swallowed by the mountain: broken columns, a dry fountain carved in the shape of coiled serpents, and against the far wall—a wall that was less wall than cliff-face—an opening like a cathedral door. Warm light breathed out of it, amber and slow, pulsing very slightly, like the light of a banked hearth that was also somehow breathing.

A girl stood in front of it with a drawn knife.

She was perhaps twelve, maybe younger—hard to tell, because everything about her was angles and wariness, a creature assembled entirely out of points. Bare feet, cracked and black with soot. Clothes made of patched leather and dragon-scale scraps that caught the light like oil on water. Hair the color of wood ash cut raggedly at her jaw. And her eyes—her eyes were molten, gold shot through with red, and they fixed on Felix the instant he stepped onto the terrace with an intensity that made his skin prickle.

"That's far enough," she said. Her voice was low and rough, a voice that had talked more to dragons than to people. "What do you want."

"We're passing through," Maren said, hands open and empty. "Wren. It's Ser Maren Vale. We met—"

"I remember." The girl—Wren—didn't take her eyes off Felix. "You came in spring. You smelled of blood and lying then too."

"That's fair," Maren admitted.

"Not you." Wren's chin jerked toward Felix. "*Him.* What is he?"

"He's Felix. He's a scribe. He's—"

"He's *wrong*." Wren took a step forward, knife low but ready, and Felix felt the strangest sensation: heat, rising off her like heat off a forge, though no fire burned anywhere on the terrace except the breathing glow behind her. "He walks like a mouse and he smells like—" Her nostrils flared. Her eyes widened slightly. "Like *ash*. Like burnt paper. Like—"

She stopped. Her gaze dropped to the satchel at Felix's side, where the dragon-scale lay wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with royal blood, the thing Maren had carried half across the world and nearly died carrying. The scale Maren had pressed into his hands at the ward-line, saying *you hold it, it liked you better.*

Wren stared at the satchel the way a drowning woman stares at shore.

"Open it," she whispered.

"Wren—"

"*Open it.*"

Maren sighed the sigh of a woman renegotiating with fate. "Felix. Show her."

His fingers weren't quite steady as he unwrapped the oilcloth. The scale within was the size of his spread hand, deep red-black, veined with gold, and warm—always warm, ever since it had flared at his touch in the undercroft, ever since it had sung against his palm like a struck bell the night they crossed the ward-line. He held it up.

Wren crossed the terrace in three strides. She stopped just short of him, trembling, knife forgotten, and Felix watched her expression do something complicated—hunger and grief and recognition fighting openly across her face.

"She's been asking about this," Wren said hoarsely. "For three days she's been asking. Calling. She doesn't call anymore, she's too weak, but she's been *calling*—" Her hand rose, hovered an inch from the scale without touching it. "Where did you get this."

"A knight brought it out of the capital," Felix said quietly. "Sealed with the king's blood. It was meant for the dragon-keepers, I think. For help."

"The king." Wren spat the word like a seed. "Kings came here once, when I was small. They smelled like metal and fear." Her eyes came back up to his, molten and searching. "Why does she call *you*? Not the knight with her sword-arm and her blood-oaths. You. The mouse who smells of burnt paper."

"I don't know."

"Liar."

"Yes," Felix admitted. "But an honest liar. I genuinely don't know. It warmed when I touched it. It sings sometimes, at night, when I'm holding it. I assumed it was dying the way everything dies."

Wren's breath caught. For a long moment she just looked at him, and Felix had the uncomfortable sense of being read—not his face but his *interior*, every hidden line of him, the way he'd read manuscripts. Then she stepped back, knife vanishing into her belt, and jerked her head toward the breathing doorway.

"She wants to see you. Both of you. Don't touch anything. Don't touch *me*." A pause. The ghost of something almost like humor flickered through the ferocity, quick as a fish under ice. "I bite."

Behind her, the amber light breathed again, slow and vast, and Felix felt it against his skin like a hand laid flat on his chest—ancient, exhausted, and unmistakably, terribly *aware of him*.

He followed the dragon-keeper's orphan into the dark, carrying the scale like a candle flame cupped against wind, and the last thing he heard from the open sky behind him was the evening birds going silent, all at once, as though the whole mountainside were holding its breath.
