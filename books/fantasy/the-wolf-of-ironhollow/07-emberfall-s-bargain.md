## 7. Emberfall's Bargain

Wren smelled the stranger before she saw him.

Not the way the valley folk would smell him—soap and wool and fear—but the way *she* smelled people, with the deep old senses Emberfall had woken in her blood when she was small enough to sleep curled against a dragon's ribs. The man who had climbed the broken stair to the eyrie carried ink under his skin. Not on it, not on his stained fingers where a scribe's stains ought to live, but *under*, threaded through him like veins of ore in gray stone. And beneath the ink, fainter, older, something that made the fine hairs along her arms stand up and hum.

He smelled like the wards.

She did not like him.

Wren crouched in the shadow of the collapsed archway at the eyrie's mouth, knees drawn up, knife loose in her grip—not to use, just to hold, because holding it made thinking easier—and watched him come up the last stretch of stair behind the knight. Ser Maren moved like something that had been broken and badly soldered, favoring her left side, her eyes never still. But the scribe—Felix, Maren kept calling him, in the tone of a woman dragging a reluctant cat on a leash—the scribe looked around at the ruined eyrie with an expression Wren recognized and hated: awe. Soft, wondering, city-soft awe, as if ruin were a beautiful thing instead of just what happened when everything else was gone.

"Stay close," Maren murmured. "And whatever you see, don't run."

"People keep saying that," Felix said. "It doesn't help."

Wren let them take three more steps into the great hollow throat of the eyrie before she dropped down onto the rubble ahead of them, landing soft as ash.

Felix made a sound like a swallowed yelp. Maren's hand went to her sword hilt and stopped there, hovering—she'd been told too, then. Good. Someone had manners.

"You're standing in her bed," Wren said.

The scribe looked down. He was, in fairness, standing in a depression the size of a farm cart, ringed by fused stone where dragonfire had melted sand into glass over centuries of sleeping. He stepped back very quickly. "I'm sorry. We didn't—there was no one here."

"There's never anyone here," Wren said. "That's the point. This is hers. Everything you're looking at is hers." She jerked her chin upward, toward the dark vault above them, where the soot-black beams of the old roosts still hung like the ribs of some enormous drowned thing. "You came all this way to beg something from her. So you'll wait until she wakes, and you'll ask properly, or I'll throw you off the stair myself and save her the trouble."

Maren exhaled slowly through her nose. "Wren. This is Felix Oyelaran. He's the one we told you about."

"I know what he smells like," Wren said, and watched the two of them exchange one of those glances grown-ups thought children couldn't read. She was fifteen. She could read anything. She could read Emberfall's moods from the rhythm of her breathing through forty feet of rock, and these two were easier than breathing.

The scribe rubbed his arms like he was cold. "What do I smell like?" he asked, and his voice cracked a little on it, which surprised her. Grown men weren't supposed to be honest about being frightened.

"Old," Wren said. "Like a door nobody's opened in a long time." She turned before he could ask anything else, because Emberfall's breathing had changed—she'd felt it shift while they talked, the long tide-pull of her sleep going shallow—and Wren did not want strangers present for the waking. It was private. All of this was private, and now the whole world was climbing their stair.

* * *

Emberfall woke the way winter ends: not all at once but in slow concessions.

First one eye, gold-shot with green, opening beneath the shelf of her brow ridge. Then the long uncoiling of her neck, joints crackling like green wood in a fire, her scales scraping stone with a sound Wren had heard every morning of her remembered life and would hear in her dreams until the day she died. She was smaller than the stories said dragons should be. Hunger had eaten her down over the years, and grief had eaten what hunger missed, until what remained was an ancient creature folded into the hollow of her own bones like a letter no one would ever deliver again.

But her eyes were the same. Her eyes had always been the same.

*Strangers,* Emberfall said, in the voice that was not a voice—that arrived inside Wren's skull warm and vast and slightly amused, the way sunlight arrives through a window. *In my eyrie. How novel. The last time someone climbed my stair uninvited, I ate him.*

"He knows about the scale," Wren said aloud, because Emberfall could hear her thoughts if she wished but preferred speech; she said spoken words were a courtesy, and courtesies mattered more the older you got. "He touched it. It burned him."

*Did it.* The great head swung around, slow as a millstone, until one molten eye fixed on Felix Oyelaran, who stood at the edge of the firelight doing an admirable job of not fainting. Wren gave him points for that. Not many. Some. *Come closer, little scribe. You have walked a long way carrying a question you haven't dared ask.*

"I—" Felix glanced at Maren, who said nothing, which Wren approved of. Let him do it himself. "I don't even know what questions are real anymore. A prayer got answered in my valley. That's not supposed to be possible. Ser Maren says the wards are failing. She says—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "She says my family built them."

*She says true,* Emberfall said, and the warmth went out of her voice, leaving something old and level and terribly tired underneath. *Sit down, child of Ironhollow. What I have to tell you is longer than your legs want to stand through.*

Wren fetched the flat stone she used as a stool near the fire-pit and shoved it at him harder than strictly necessary. He sat. She planted herself between him and Emberfall, arms crossed, and told herself firmly that she was guarding her mother and not hiding behind her, and knew perfectly well that she was lying.

*Before there was your kingdom,* Emberfall began, *there were gods in the world like weather. Some of them kind. Most of them simply indifferent. And one of them was hungry in a way the others could not tolerate. Vhalor, the old pantheon named it, though names were the least of what it devoured—it ate worship, it ate memory, it ate the shape of a person and wore the shape afterward like a coat. Your gods could not kill it. Killing leaves nothing, and Vhalor was very good at becoming nothing. So they imprisoned it instead. They poured it into a valley and sealed the valley, and the seal required an anchor—something mortal, patient, and bound.*

Felix's face had gone the color of the ash on the floor. "Bound how?"

*Blood,* said Emberfall. *Your blood. The first wardens of Ironhollow were your ancestors. They agreed to be the lock, and their descendants after them, generation upon generation, each life nailed into the mechanism of the prison. The temple's debts, the copying of prayers, the careful smallness of every life in that valley—all of it scaffolding. Maintenance. You were not kept poor and invisible by cruelty, little scribe. You were kept poor and invisible by design. A lock does not need to be happy. It only needs to be turned.*

"That can't be right," Felix whispered. It wasn't protest, exactly. It was the sound of a man setting down something heavy to check whether it was heavy. "The prayers. The answered prayers. If the wards held—"

*They are failing,* Emberfall said. *Because Vhalor has found another door. Your king stopped being your king a year ago. Something else wears him, and through his crown it drinks the worship of a whole nation, rebuilding itself body by body, belief by belief. It answered prayers in your valley the way a fisherman churns water—to draw the anchor out of the lock. To draw* you *out. And you came. As it knew you would, because you are your ancestors' child: the kind who walks toward the breaking thing instead of away.*

Silence filled the eyrie then, the huge ringing silence of high empty places. Wren watched the scribe's hands. They were shaking. Ink-stained fingers pressed flat against his knees, pressing hard, as if he could pin himself to the world by friction alone.

"So the king," Felix said at last, very carefully, "can be freed?"

*Burned clean,* Emberfall corrected. *Fire is the one thing Vhalor cannot wear. Fire consumes the shape rather than preserving it. My fire. The last of it in the world.*

Wren's whole body went cold.

She knew. Of course she knew—she had watched Emberfall gutter for years, had learned to read the dimming of her scales the way other children learned letters. But knowing a thing and hearing it offered away were different animals entirely, and the second one had teeth.

"No," Wren said.

*Wren—*

"No." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept talking anyway. "You don't get to—you've barely got enough to make it through the winter. I've counted. I sit up at night and I count your breaths, I know exactly how much of you is left, and you're not spending it. Not on him. Not on a king we've never met, not on some stupid valley full of people who'd burn us both if they knew what I was—"

*Little spark.* The great head lowered, and one vast eye came level with Wren's face, close enough that she could see herself reflected in it, small and furious and already crying, which was the worst part. *My flame goes out regardless. Tonight or next spring, it makes no difference to the sum. The only question is whether it goes out as heat on the air, or as something that matters.*

"It matters to me," Wren said, barely above a whisper. "Doesn't that count? Can't it matter to me?"

The eye closed briefly. When it opened again, Emberfall turned back to Felix, and Wren understood that she had been given her answer, and that the answer was no, and that her mother loved her exactly enough not to pretend otherwise.

*There is a condition,* Emberfall said. *Hear me fully before you speak, scribe. I will give you my last flame. It will burn Vhalor out of your king, if you carry it truly and spend it rightly. But fire is not a gift that travels lightly. It must be carried in a vessel, and the only vessel equal to it is a bloodline already shaped by binding—yours. I will pour what remains of me into you. It will mark you. Your eyes will change. Your shadow will glow in the dark like banked coals. Iron will grow hot in your hand and no temple will ever again mistake you for a servant. You will be known, everywhere, forever, by anyone with eyes—and everything that wants the last fire in the world will know precisely where it sleeps. There is no putting it down once it is lit. Do you understand? This is not a burden you may set aside when it grows heavy. It is a marriage. Till death, and past it.*

Wren stared at the scribe and hated him in advance for saying yes. She could already see the yes forming in him, moving up through his chest into his throat, and she wanted to scream at him to say no, to be a coward, to be small and invisible the way he'd been his whole life, because cowards kept their mothers alive.

But Felix Oyelaran didn't say yes. He sat very still for a long moment, staring into the dead fire-pit, and when he finally spoke his voice was rough and quiet and honest.

"My whole life," he said, "everyone told me what I owed. The temple put a number on me before I could write my own name. Every prayer I copied was somebody telling me I was less than the debt." He looked up, and his eyes found Wren's for a moment—found hers, not Emberfall's, and something in that steadied her whether she wanted it to or not. "This is the first time anyone's asked me to choose something. Even something terrible. Especially something terrible."

*Then choose,* said Emberfall, gently, *but choose tomorrow. Flame spent in haste burns crooked. Rest tonight. Eat what the girl gives you—she'll pretend it's an insult, but she'll give you the larger portion.*

"I will not," Wren said wetly, and went to do exactly that.

* * *

Night came down over the eyrie like a lid.

Wren couldn't sleep. She lay in her usual hollow against Emberfall's foreleg, listening to the great slow bellows of her breathing, counting the spaces between breaths the way she always did, except tonight the numbers felt like coins slipping through a hole in her pocket.

After a while she gave up, wrapped her blanket around her shoulders, and climbed the inner ramp to the ledge where the scribe sat keeping watch. He'd pulled his cloak tight and sat with the dragon-scale in his open palm, turning it over and over. In the starlight it gleamed like a chip of frozen sunset.

"She talks in your head," he said quietly, without looking up. "Is it always like that? Warm?"

"When she wants it to be." Wren sat down a careful arm's length away, knife across her knees out of pure habit. "When she's angry it's like standing too near an oven door."

"She loves you." It wasn't a question. He said it the way people said things they envied.

"She's dying," Wren said, because she was not in the mood to be comforted. "Tomorrow she's going to pour herself into a stranger and go out, and everyone's going to call it noble, and none of them will have been the one who brushed her scales every night for ten years. None of them will know that she hums when she's falling asleep, or that she pretends to hate the mountain goats I bring her and then eats four." Her voice was coming apart and she let it. "So if you waste it—if you carry her carelessly, if you spend her on something stupid—I will follow you to the end of the world and I will make you regret every day you're marked. Do you understand me?"

Felix looked at her then, really looked, and to her fury his eyes were wet too. "I understand you," he said. "I'll carry her like she's the only holy thing left." He paused. Closed his fist around the scale. "Maybe she won't die, Wren. Maybe fire that's carried well is still fire."

"Don't lie to me," Wren said. "Everyone lies to children. Don't be everyone."

He was silent a moment. "Then I won't promise anything except this: she won't be wasted."

From below, rising through the stone and the dark, came a sound neither of them had heard in years—a low, cracked, rumbling vibration that Wren felt in her teeth before she understood it. Emberfall was humming. The falling-asleep song. Whether she'd meant them to hear it or not, Wren couldn't say.

They sat together on the ledge in silence, the orphan and the scribe, listening to a dragon sing herself toward the morning when she would stop.

And far to the south, Wren did not yet know it, but the road to the capital lay open and waiting, and the thing wearing the king's crown had already begun to dream of fire.
