## 8. Road of Relics

Felix had copied four hundred and eleven prayers about roads in his years at the scriptorium—pilgrims begging for safe passage, merchants praying against bandits, soldiers asking to come home along them—and not one of those prayers had mentioned that a road was mostly mud, and that mud got into everything, and that Wren thought this was the funniest thing in the world.

"Your boot," she said, pointing, delighted, from her perch atop Maren's saddlebag. "It's gone. It's just a brown lump now."

"I'm aware."

"You walk like a heron. A heron with one leg. A heron that lost a bet."

Maren snorted without breaking stride. Three days out of the eyrie, the knight had stopped limping and started humming—low, tuneless, half under her breath, as if she'd only just remembered her body was allowed to make sounds that weren't orders or apologies. She wore no colors now; she'd traded her tabard to a tinker for dried fish and a hat with a feather in it, which made her look less like a fugitive knight of the outer kingdom and more like a woman who had lost several arguments with weather. The dragon-scale rode wrapped in oilcloth against Felix's chest, warm as a sleeping bird.

They were following the old processional way south toward Vhalor's Reach—the capital, though no one who lived outside its shadow called it anything but the Reach—through country Felix had never seen except as ink. He knew these hills from a tithe ledger, 120 households, barley and flax. He knew them now as green shoulders rising into mist, as hawks riding thermals, as a smell of crushed fennel where Wren trampled off the path to catch lizards. The valley had taught him that the world was words on vellum. The world kept correcting him.

The scale had grown warmer each day since Emberfall's bargain. Not painfully. More like a hand resting on his sternum, patient, reminding him it was there. Some mornings he woke with his palm already pressed flat over the oilcloth, as if he'd been holding it in his sleep, keeping watch over it—or it over him.

He did not know how to be someone things happened to. In Ironhollow he had been a function: quill, inkpot, debt. Here, children in the first village they passed had run up to touch his sleeve because Wren told them he was a dragonscribe, a word she invented on the spot with total confidence, and an old man had wept when Felix could not bless him. "You're god-touched," the man said, gripping his wrist. "I can see it. You carry something." Felix had wanted to say *you're mistaken, I copy prayers, I don't answer them,* but the lie would not form properly in his mouth anymore. That was the trouble with leaving the valley. Words started meaning what they meant.

* * *

The hedge-witch found them rather than the reverse, which Maren later insisted was the correct order of things.

They had sheltered at dusk in the ribs of a ruined shrine—a roadside altar to some nameless god of the old pantheon, roof fallen, walls waist-high, the god's face worn to a smooth suggestion of kindness. Felix was attempting to cook. This went as expected. The pot smoked; Wren heckled; Maren ate a charred onion with the stoicism of a soldier accepting wounds.

"There's a trick to it," said a voice from the dark beyond the shrine wall. "You let the fire die down first. You all cook like you're angry at it."

A woman stepped through the gap in the stones as if the gap had been built for her: small, gray-streaked hair braided with rowan berries, eyes the color of pond water, a walking staff crowned with a bundle of drying herbs. She looked at each of them in turn, unhurried, the way Felix checked a page for errors.

"Maren Vale," she said. "Deserted after the Battle of the Fords, where your king stood on the ridge and did not speak even when his own guard died around him. And a child who smells of ash and grief. And—" Her gaze settled on Felix and stayed there. Her breath changed. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor lad. You're carrying a fire that hasn't lit yet."

Wren was on her feet with a knife before the woman finished speaking. Maren's hand found her sword hilt. But Felix, absurdly, heard himself say: "How do you cook it, then?"

The witch laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and crouched by his miserable fire, pushing coals aside with her stick. "Sela," she said. "And you let the flame get small and steady. Big flames burn the outside and leave the middle raw. Same as most things." She glanced up at him. "Same as gods."

She stayed the night. She would not say how she'd known they were coming—"the birds gossip, and so do I"—and she would not say why she'd been living alone in the hills for nine years, except once, near midnight, staring into the embers: "I healed a lordling's daughter when the temples said let her die. They called it theft. Taking what belongs to the gods' judgment." She turned her hands over, palms scarred with old burns. "So I took my craft and walked until the road ran out. Been talking to shrines ever since. They're better company than people. Quieter."

"You talk to dead gods," Wren said, with neither mockery nor pity, just the blunt curiosity of a child raised by a dragon.

"I listen to them. Different thing." Sela looked at Felix then, and her pond-water eyes went strange and far away. "They've been restless lately. All of them. Like dogs before a storm. Something old is waking up wearing new clothes, and every shrine I've sat with has been trying to tell me the same word for a month now." She hesitated. "*Anchor.*"

The scale against Felix's chest pulsed once, warm as a heartbeat. Nobody spoke for a long time.

In the morning she packed her bundle, looked at the road south, and said, "Well. I can't very well let you three cook yourselves to death unattended." Just like that. As if belonging were a thing you simply picked up and carried, like a pot.

* * *

They gained two more before the river, and Felix never quite learned how.

The mute giant came first. They heard him before they saw him—the ground reporting his footsteps minutes ahead—and Maren drew steel until a shape resolved out of the rain: enormous, seven feet if he was an inch, wrapped in sacking and rope, hauling a cart with one hand. On the cart lay a broken statue, a god's torso, marble arms missing. The giant set the cart down carefully, looked at them with wide dark eyes full of something Felix recognized instantly because he had worn it his whole life—the look of someone waiting to be told they shouldn't be here—and extended one huge hand, palm up, holding three perfect wild pears.

His name, Sela worked out through signs and patience, was Bramble—not his true name, which he would not give, because names could be used. His village had put him out when his silence began, at the same hour their shrine god's statue cracked down the face. He'd been carrying pieces of fallen gods to high places ever since, so they could see the sky. He could not speak, but he hummed while he worked, low and resonant, and Wren discovered within a day that he would teach her songs with his hands, tapping rhythms on logs and knees, and the two of them communicated in a language of gesture and laughter that excluded everyone else entirely and made Felix ache in a way he couldn't name.

The defrocked inquisitor they nearly killed twice.

He appeared on the fourth evening, sitting calmly at the edge of their firelight with his hands visible and empty, a lean gray man in traveling clothes with the faded brand of the Order burned out on his wrist—an inverted mark, flesh cut away, the inquisitors' answer to desertion. "Dagny Holt," he said. "Formerly Second Voice of the Inquisition of the Reach. I've been following you for six days. If I wanted you taken, you'd have been taken."

Maren's blade was at his throat before Felix finished standing. "Give me one reason."

"The same reason you deserted, Ser Vale. I saw the king's eyes at the autumn court." Holt didn't move, didn't blink. "Something looked out of them that wasn't him. I reported it. My superiors thanked me and promoted me, which is how I learned the Order serves whatever wears the crown rather than the crown itself. So I burned my oath-mark and left. And now the Order's finest are on the road behind you, and I'm the only person alive who knows their protocols, their cipher, and their current route." He smiled thinly. "That's my reason."

"He could be leading us into a net," Maren said that night, when Holt slept apart from the fire, as he insisted.

"He could be," Sela agreed, sharpening her herb-knife. "Or he could be a man who lost everything and is hoping we'll be cruel enough to need him. Both sorts end up on this road eventually. God-touched, god-cursed, god-forsaken—it's all the same road."

"And what does that make us?" Felix asked quietly.

"A congregation," Sela said, and grinned at his expression. "Don't make that face, scribe. You've been reading scripture your whole life. You must have noticed most of the miracles happen to people in the middle of nowhere with nothing left to lose."

* * *

It was around the fires, in those weeks on the road, that Felix learned the thing the scriptorium had never taught him: that a family could assemble itself out of strangers the way a river assembles itself out of rain—no plan, no permission, just gravity and persistence.

He learned that Bramble was gentle with small creatures and terrified of bridges, and that nobody teased him about either. He learned that Sela snored like a landslide and swore like a dockworker and knew a folk cure for every ailment including homesickness, though hers involved bitter tea and honesty and therefore nobody requested it twice. He learned that Holt kept meticulous records in a cipher of his own invention, logging their route and supplies with the discipline of the order that had cast him out, and that when Felix asked him why, he said, "Because someday someone will need to know exactly what happened here, and it might as well be written correctly," and Felix understood him completely and immediately.

He learned Wren. That was the slowest learning and the deepest. She had spent her whole life as one half of a pair—dragon and girl—and the shape of her grief was still forming, still sharp-edged. Some nights she slept pressed against the wrapped scale in Felix's pack without asking, and he pretended to sleep so she wouldn't have to explain. Some days she was bright as struck flint, teaching Bramble lizard-catching, inventing insults, naming constellations after people she disliked (*that one's the Temple Elder Corvus, because he's cold and pointy*). Other days she went silent and watched the southern horizon where Emberfall was guttering alone in the eyrie, and no one disturbed her, and Maren always, those nights, sat a little closer to her bedroll than strictly necessary.

One evening, cooking rice—correctly now; Felix had mastered letting the fire go small and steady—he caught himself laughing. Actually laughing, at something Wren had done with a frog and Sela's hat, and the sound coming out of him was so unfamiliar that he stopped mid-breath, startled, as if he'd heard a stranger in the room. Wren noticed. Of course she noticed.

"You laughed," she said accusingly.

"No, I didn't."

"You did. Your whole face moved. I saw it."

"It won't happen again."

But it kept happening. Small treasons against the boy he'd been: singing badly with Bramble's drumming palms; arguing theology with Holt until midnight and discovering he *had* opinions, real ones, that had been waiting years for permission; falling asleep mid-sentence while Sela explained the difference between wards and promises and waking at dawn with a blanket over him that he hadn't fetched.

Debt, he thought, watching sparks climb toward the stars. He had spent twenty-three years believing debt was the load-bearing structure of the universe—that everything owed everything, that love itself was just credit extended between desperate parties. But this—these five people who owed him nothing, whom he owed nothing, gathered around a fire because they chose to be—this ran on some other currency entirely. He didn't have a word for it yet. He suspected the temple's lexicons didn't contain one.

He was almost—almost—happy. And happiness, he was learning, was the most expensive thing he owned. Someone was always watching for it.

* * *

He felt them before anyone else did, on the twelfth night past the river.

Not saw them. Felt them—a wrongness in the dark south of their camp, like a blank space in a familiar text. He'd been copying again, at Sela's urging, keeping a private record of the scale's behavior, the old god-shrines they passed, the dreams that had begun visiting him nightly (a throne of iron roots; a voice like many mouths saying *come home, little lock*). His quill stopped moving. His head lifted.

"What?" Maren said. She trusted his instincts now, which frightened him more than the instincts themselves.

"Inquisitors," Felix said. "South ridge. Four—no. Five. They've been paralleling us since the ford."

Holt rose silently and was beside him in three strides, peering into the dark. "You can't know that."

"They don't pray," Felix said, and heard how mad it sounded, and said it anyway. "Every living thing out there prays, even animals, even trees, it's just—noise, background, like insects. There's a patch of quiet moving along the ridge, and quiet shaped like people, walking when we walk and stopping when we stop. I grew up inside a ward. I know what warded silence feels like from the outside now." His hand drifted to his chest, to the scale. "They're not attacking. They're herding."

Holt was quiet a long moment. "That's Third Voice doctrine," he said finally. "You don't take a marked quarry in open country. You press them onto a road you control." His jaw tightened. "There's exactly one road ahead that fits their pattern. The ferry crossing at Graywater. Narrow ground. No cover."

"Then we don't take the ferry," Maren said.

"We take the ferry," Felix said.

They all looked at him. Even Wren, curled awake against the pack, her eyes reflecting firelight.

"They'll herd us regardless, and every other crossing adds days, and Emberfall doesn't have days," Felix said. His voice sounded calmer than he felt; somewhere inside, the boy who had survived Ironhollow by being invisible was screaming, and the man he was becoming put a hand on the boy's shoulder and said *not tonight.* "We cross at Graywater. We just don't cross the way they expect. Holt—you said you know their protocols. Tell me everything about how Third Voice runs an ambush. Every signal, every formation, every habit. Because for the first time in my life—" and here he smiled, and it wasn't entirely a kind smile, "—I'm the one holding the cipher key."

Beyond the firelight, on the dark southern ridge, the patch of silence stopped moving.

And then, deliberately, unmistakably, it began to walk toward them.
