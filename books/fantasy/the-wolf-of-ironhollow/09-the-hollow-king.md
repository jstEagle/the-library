## 9. The Hollow King

The king woke in the dark behind his own eyes, as he did every morning, and counted what was left of himself.

*Three hundred and sixty-one days.* He had lost track once, early on, and the terror of that lost week still lived in him like a splinter of glass. So now he counted. Every morning, before the thing wearing his body opened his eyes and smiled with his mouth, Aldric of House Veyran, ninth of his name, sovereign of the outer kingdom, lay in the cellar of his own skull and counted days like a miser counting coins that could be stolen at any moment.

*Three hundred and sixty-two.*

Today there would be a council session. Today he might get a sentence.

He had learned the architecture of his prison the way a drowning man learns the shape of the water. There were shallows near the top—moments when the god's attention turned outward, toward worship, toward the slow feeding it drew from ten thousand bowed heads—and in those shallows Aldric could rise close enough to the surface to taste air. To see through his own eyes for a handful of heartbeats. To move a finger. Once, gloriously, to cough during a coronation anniversary mass, which had caused three bishops to faint and one to declare it a miracle.

It was not a miracle. It was a man drowning who had managed to splash.

Below the shallows, the water went dark and deep, and things moved in it that he refused to look at directly. The god—*Vhalor*, though the name was never spoken aloud anymore, only worn like a garment—had eaten most of what made Aldric a king. His memories of his father went first, devoured in great greedy mouthfuls during the first month. Then his mother's face. Then, cruelest of all, the sound of his own laughter, which he could remember having but could no longer remember *hearing*. What remained was small and stubborn: his name, his counting, and a fury so old and cold it had become less an emotion than an organ, beating quietly somewhere beneath everything else.

They think a hollowed king feels nothing, Aldric thought, drifting up through the murk as the god's attention shifted toward morning prayer. They think it is like sleep.

It is not like sleep. It is like being buried alive in your own house and hearing the furniture moved.

***

The council convened at the tenth bell. Aldric surfaced just long enough to see the chamber—the long table of black ironwood, the banners of the nine provinces, the faces of men and women he had appointed and could no longer trust because he could not know which ones suspected, which ones had been replaced, which ones merely flattered the thing on the throne because flattery was safer than sight.

"—the harvest tithes from the eastern marches," Chancellor Breven was saying, "continue to fall short of projection, Majesty. The drought in Ironhollow's neighbor valleys has—"

Aldric felt the god lean forward through his hands, felt his own fingers lace together atop the armrests with a grace he had never possessed. When the voice came out of his throat it was his voice, and that remained the worst of it. Not a rasp, not a stranger's timbre. *His.* Warm. Reasonable. The voice of a man who had once settled a border dispute by sharing bread with both sides.

"Ironhollow," the god said through him, tasting the word. "Tell me of Ironhollow."

Breven blinked. "A warded valley, Majesty. Temple lands. Quiet Flame. Nothing of strategic value—the wards keep all magic out, and little enough goes in. A backwater of scribes and sheep."

"A backwater." The god smiled Aldric's smile. "And yet its prayers have begun to be answered."

Aldric went very still in the deep water below. *Answered prayers.* He had heard fragments of this over the past weeks, rumors surfacing through council reports like drowned bodies: rain where rain should not fall, a child breathing again, crops healing overnight in a valley that magic could not touch. He had assumed the reports were fever dreams, or the god's own theater. But the way the thing wearing him leaned into those words now—not with surprise but with *anticipation*, the way a fisherman leans when the line finally sings—

*You want something from that valley,* Aldric thought, pressing himself against the inside of the moment, straining to see. *Something is there. Something you've been waiting for.*

"Send no soldiers," the god said. "No inquisitors. Let the valley be. If anything stirs there... let it come to us."

*Us.* As if it were plural. As if it already knew what was coming, and was simply setting the table.

The council session dissolved into numbers after that, and Aldric sank, furious and helpless, watching through the gray water as his hands signed a decree he could not read.

***

But the god slept—or whatever passed for sleep in a thing that had been imprisoned for centuries and wore a king the way a hermit crab wears a stolen shell—and in the sleeping hours, Aldric worked.

He worked in ink.

This was the discovery that had saved his sanity, in the second month, when despair had grown teeth: the god despised documents. It tolerated them the way it tolerated food it did not enjoy—necessary, distasteful, beneath its attention. State papers bored it. Ledgers, petitions, decrees, marginalia—it skimmed them with Aldric's educated eyes and retained nothing, because what use has eternity for bookkeeping?

But Aldric had been raised by archivists. His grandfather had taught him cipher before he taught him to ride. And every night, when the thing wearing him retired to bed and its grip slackened like a fist going loose, Aldric rose through the layers of himself to the shallow water, took up the pen his body still held, and wrote.

He could not write openly, of course. The first attempt—a plain declaration scrawled across a tax memorandum—had nearly destroyed him. The god had felt the dissonance, had *turned around* inside his skull like something vast rotating in a cave, and the punishment had been a full day of being held under while it peeled away another year of his memories, slowly, letting him feel each one go. He had lost the smell of his wife's hair that day. He no longer remembered he'd had a wife, except that some mornings his left hand ached for a ring that wasn't there, and he had learned to read that ache like a text.

So instead he wrote in the old court cipher—his grandfather's cipher, the one every Veyran learned at seven and was expected to abandon by twelve, so childish and obsolete that no inquisitor alive would recognize it. And he hid it where no one looks: in the margins of mundane state papers. A flourish on a customs report. An abbreviation in a grain ledger. The spacing of words in a boundary dispute resolution, off by half a finger-width in a pattern only a trained scribe would catch.

*I am not the king you see. I am beneath. The thing wears me. Count the letters. Third, first, fourth.*

He had been leaving these for months. Hundreds of them, scattered through the archive like seeds thrown into a field he would never see harvested. He had no idea whether anyone had ever noticed. That was the particular torture of it—writing rescue notes and dropping them into the sea, with no way of knowing if the sea was empty.

Tonight, the god had ordered a proclamation drafted: a festival of thanksgiving, nationwide, celebrating "the king's renewed vigor." Aldric surfaced in the shallow water as the draft scribe's hand moved across vellum, and in the margin, between two flourishes, he wrote:

*Do not celebrate. Do not kneel. Kneeling feeds it. I am still here. Find the scribes. Trust the ink.*

Then, because he could not help himself, because dignity was the last coin he had and he refused to stop spending it: he added the royal seal's private countersign, the one only true kings knew, the mark that proved the message came from the hand and not the mask.

Let them doubt. Let them call it forgery, madness, trickery of the enemy. Somewhere in this kingdom there was one archivist, one clerk, one stubborn reader of margins who would count the letters and know.

There had to be.

***

On the ninety-seventh night of the thanksgiving preparations, the god brought home something new, and Aldric understood that the endgame had begun.

He surfaced at dusk to find himself standing at a window overlooking the inner courtyard, watching a procession enter the gates—inquisitors, a dozen of them, escorting prisoners. His eyes found the prisoners before the god could stop him, and what he saw made him claw upward through the water with all his remaining strength.

A knight. Tall, mud-caked, armor scorched, hands bound. Her face was ruined by exhaustion but unmistakable even after a year: Ser Maren Vale, who had commanded his personal guard, who had been on duty the night it happened, who had vanished afterward and been declared traitor by the thing wearing his crown.

*Maren.* She had gotten away. All this time she had been free, and now—

Beside her walked a boy, perhaps sixteen, in travel-stained clothes that marked him as no nobleman. He carried himself like a scribe—shoulders slightly stooped, ink stains ghosting his fingers even from this distance—and he looked up at the palace with an expression Aldric recognized with a jolt like lightning: the expression of a drowning man seeing the surface.

And clutched against the boy's chest, wrapped in cloth, was something that *burned*. Not visibly—but Aldric felt it the way one feels a hearth from across a room, a warmth with weight and will behind it, radiating a pressure that made even the deep water around him tremble. The god felt it too. Aldric felt the vast attention snap toward the courtyard like a predator's head turning, felt hunger and caution war in the thing's mind, and heard it whisper through his lips, so softly the guard beside him didn't hear:

"The anchor comes to me at last."

*Anchor.* The word struck Aldric like a physical blow. The wards. The valley. Whatever that boy carried, whatever the boy *was*, it was the thread the whole web hung from—and the god had lured him here, had spent months answering prayers in a warded backwater like a fisherman chumming waters, all to draw this one frightened scribe across the threshold.

*Run,* Aldric screamed into the water, knowing the boy couldn't hear him, knowing the scream reached nothing but the walls of his own cage. *Whatever they told you, whatever bargain you think you're walking into—run.*

But the gates closed behind the prisoners with a sound like a ledger closing, and the boy walked on, and Aldric sank.

That night, working in the margins while the god dreamed its slow hungry dreams, Aldric wrote faster than he had ever written. He filled the margins of a routine grain shipment schedule with cipher, his hand shaking:

*The boy is the key. The knight knows more than she says. The fire they carry can burn me out—it is the ONLY thing that can. Protect the boy. Protect the fire. If you are reading this, you are the last chance, and I am sorry, truly sorry, for what it will cost you.*

He hesitated, pen hovering. Then, in the smallest hand he could manage, he added the final line, the one he had been saving for a year without knowing why:

*A king's debt is still a debt. I intend to pay mine.*

He blotted the ink, set down the pen, and sank back into the deep water as the god stirred, waking, stretching itself through his limbs like a man donning a beloved coat.

Tomorrow there would be audiences. Tomorrow his voice would pronounce mercies and judgments that were neither. Tomorrow the boy with ink-stained fingers would be brought before the throne, and the thing wearing Aldric would study him with patient, ancient hunger, and Aldric would watch from the bottom of his own eyes, counting.

*Three hundred and eighty-nine days.*

But tonight, in the dark behind his own eyes, Aldric permitted himself one thing he had not permitted in a year. He allowed himself to believe that the message would be found. That somewhere in the archives, a pair of tired eyes was moving across a grain ledger right now, slowing, stopping, beginning to count the letters.

*Drown quietly,* he told himself, folding his hands in the dark like a man at prayer. *Drown quietly, and leave the water marked.*

Somewhere above him, muffled by flesh and silence, the last dragon's fire was entering his palace—and the god wearing his face had no idea what was written in its margins.
