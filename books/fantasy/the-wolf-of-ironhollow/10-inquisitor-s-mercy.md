## 10. Inquisitor's Mercy

The trap closed at dusk, in the ruin of a wayshrine where the road bent toward the capital, and Maren Vale knew it before the first crossbow string sang.

She had spent twenty years learning the grammar of ambush—the held breath of birdsong, the way horses go quiet when men are hiding from them, the particular quality of silence that means *armed and waiting*. She heard it now in the hush around the shrine, felt it in her teeth like the pressure before a storm. She turned to Felix, who was kneeling by their cold fire, feeding it the last of a dead man's fence posts.

"Get behind me," she said. "Both of you."

Felix looked up, ink-stained fingers frozen over the satchel of copied prayers he still carried everywhere, as though those papers could save anyone. "What—"

The arrows came with the last light.

Maren got her shield up—she'd bought it cheap in a river town, its paint flaking, but the wood was sound—and two bolts struck it with a sound like knuckles on a door. A third took her through the meat of her left shoulder, punching through mail that had been made to stop swords, not quarrels fired at thirty paces by men who knew their work.

She went down on one knee. Through the shrine's broken arch she saw them come out of the treeline: twelve figures in grey-and-white, faces masked to the eyes, the sunburst sigil of the Temple's inquisition stitched at every breast. They moved like water finding a channel. No shouting. No glory. Just the patient, professional closing of a circle.

*They waited for dark*, Maren thought, dragging Wren down beside her. *They wanted us tired.*

"Felix." Her voice came out steadier than her shoulder deserved. "The back wall. There's a gap behind the fallen stone—I scouted it this morning. Take the girl and run."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are, because I'm ordering you, and unlike your temple elders I've earned the right to be obeyed."

He didn't move. Of course he didn't. Three weeks on the road and the scribe had developed a spine somewhere between the hedge-witch's cottage and the giant's ferry, and it was going to get him killed. He stood there with his hands half-raised, palms out, the universal gesture of a man who had never thrown a punch in his life, and said, "There's someone you should talk to first."

"Talk? They're inquisitors, not diplomats—"

"Look at the leader."

Maren looked. The lead figure had stopped at the edge of the shrine, crossbow lowered—not slung, just angled down, the muzzle discipline of a soldier rather than an executioner. The others held position in a crescent, but the leader walked forward alone, boots crunching on old mosaic tile, and pulled back his hood.

Familiar eyes. Familiar face. Softer than she remembered from the scriptorium's doorway, softer than the man who had named Felix a heretic with his own mouth, but unmistakable all the same.

"Brother Osric," Felix breathed. It wasn't surprise, exactly. It was something older and more wounded than surprise—a wound reopened to check whether it had healed. "You."

"Hello, Felix." Osric's voice carried across the ruined shrine, quiet and level, a voice built for reading scripture aloud. "You look well, considering."

"Considering you sold me to the temple?" Felix took a step forward and Maren grabbed his sleeve; the crossbows tracked him instantly, twelve little deaths waiting on a word. "Considering you stood in front of Elder Corvane and told him I'd hidden a fugitive under the scriptorium? I bled for that, Osric. My debt doubled. My name—"

"Your name is worth more than you know," Osric said, "which is why I did what I did. Put the bow down, Dannel." The nearest crossbowman hesitated, then lowered his weapon. "All of you. Down."

The circle wavered. These were temple soldiers, Maren saw now—young, well-fed, frightened in the way men are frightened when they've been told they hunt monsters and have started to suspect the monster hunts back. One of them, a woman with a scarred jaw, kept her crossbow up and said, "Brother, the writ says—"

"The writ says bring them alive," Osric said. "I'm aware. I wrote three drafts of it."

That landed strangely. Maren's hand found her sword hilt anyway. Twenty years of court politics had taught her that the most dangerous lies were the ones wrapped in concessions.

"Lower it, Sister Yeva," Osric said, without heat, without turning. "Or explain to the chapter why you disobeyed a direct order in front of witnesses."

The crossbow came down. Slowly.

*

They sat in the shrine while the last light died, and Maren did not put away her sword, and Osric did not ask her to. His soldiers withdrew to the road—within earshot, deliberately, so no one could later claim the parley had been secret—and the four of them arranged themselves around the dead fire like pieces on a board: Maren with her back to the wall and her blade across her knees, Felix and Wren on one side, Osric alone on the other with his hood down and his hands visible, folded, patient.

Up close, the man had changed since Ironhollow. Thinner. There were shadows under his eyes that hadn't been carved by travel alone, and when he moved his right arm he did it carefully, guarding something beneath the robe. Maren catalogued these things the way she catalogued everything: wounds, weapons, exits. But she also watched his face when he looked at Felix, and what she saw there was not the flat cruelty of a true believer. It was guilt. Old guilt, worn smooth, the kind a man carries so long he stops feeling its edges.

"Say it plainly," Maren said. "You betrayed him once. Convince me you didn't betray him twice."

Osric nodded slowly, as if she'd asked the only reasonable question in the world. "You were at Court, Ser Vale. You understand how confessions work."

"I understand how they're extracted."

"Then hear mine freely given." He drew a breath. "Three years ago I found a ledger in the temple archive. Not the debt ledgers—the real ones, sealed under Elder Corvane's floor. Records going back four hundred years. Payments received, Ser Vale. From the crown. For keeping the valley silent."

Maren's shoulder throbbed. She ignored it. "Silent how?"

"Wards need tending. Tending needs payment. And the temple discovered long ago that a valley full of people who can't do magic makes very good neighbors to a throne that doesn't want witnesses." His gaze flicked to Felix. "Your family built the wards. The temple has been paid to maintain them—or to let them rot, when the payments came with instructions. And a year ago the instructions changed. Corvane began receiving letters in a hand none of the elders recognized. Orders we were told came from the palace. I went to the capital to trace them."

"And?" Felix asked.

"And the trail led to a room in the palace where the king's own correspondence is written by clerks who never see his face. I got as far as a sealed antechamber before the temple's friends in the guard found me." He touched his right arm, the guarded one, and Maren understood: they had hurt him, and he had gone back to Ironhollow afterward and smiled at Corvane and waited. "So I made myself useful to them instead. Useful men see everything. And when the order came down to retrieve you—" his eyes met Felix's, and did not flinch— "I volunteered."

"To capture me."

"To reach you before men who would have burned you at the roadside and called it mercy." Osric spread his hands. "Check my soldiers' orders. Alive. Unhurt. Delivered to a waystation outside Vellin where, I promise you, no temple cell exists. I have spent a year being the thing I hate, Felix, because the thing I hate is the only door into the room where this kingdom is being strangled. You have every right to despise me for it. Do it after we survive."

The silence stretched. Wren, who had been crouched against the wall with her knees drawn up and the dragon-scale clutched to her chest, spoke for the first time.

"He's telling the truth or he's very good at lying," she said. "Emberfall says liars smell like cold iron. He smells like ink and fear." A pause. "Mostly ink."

Maren almost laughed. Almost.

But it was Felix who broke it, and Maren watched him do it—watched the scribe who had spent his whole life invisible look at the friend who had broken him open and choose, visibly, deliberately, to set the break rather than avenge it.

"The waystation at Vellin," Felix said quietly. "If you're lying, I'll know. I copied the temple's route schedules for six years, Osric. I know which stations exist."

Something in Osric's face loosened—relief, maybe, or grief at being believed by someone he'd wronged. "You always were the best of us," he said.

"Don't," Felix said. "Not yet. Walk with us. Earn it."

*

They made camp an hour on, in a hollow off the road where the hedge-witch's wards—chalk lines renewed each night—faintly warmed the air. Maren sat apart, cleaning the arrow wound, and it was Osric who came to her, carrying clean linen and a flask of temple spirits, and knelt without asking permission.

"May I?"

"It's your shoulder if you slip."

His hands were steady. Scribe's hands, ink-stained even now, gentle in the way of men used to handling fragile things. "Ser Vale," he said, working the linen beneath the torn mail, "may I say something impertinent?"

"You may attempt it."

"I read your trial record. Before I left the capital archives, I read everything about the king's household I could find." He tied off the bandage, neat as a manuscript margin. "You were Aldric's shield-companion for nine years. You were on the wall the night the thing took him, and you were dismissed from your post eleven days later for drunkenness on duty. The record notes you confessed."

Maren stared at the fire. The chalk-wards crackled softly, a sound like distant rain.

"I confessed because it was true," she said finally. "I was drunk. That's the part no one disputes. What the record doesn't note—" her voice came out lower than she intended— "is why. The night before, the king summoned me at midnight and asked me, very calmly, whether I thought a man could be replaced. Whether the people around him would notice, if he were. He laughed after. Said it was a riddle from his boyhood. And I drank because I couldn't shake the feeling that my king had just told me he was drowning, and asked me for help in the only language he had left, and I'd answered with a joke and a cup."

Osric said nothing. He was good at that, she was learning—the silence of a man who listens the way scribes write: completely.

"Eleven days later he stopped speaking in his own voice entirely. And I was gone, and I told myself for a year that there was nothing I could have seen, nothing I could have done." She looked at him. "But that's the lie I live in, Brother Osric. I saw the drowning and called it a riddle. Everything since—the road, the girl, the dragon, all of it—is me trying to answer a question a year too late."

"Yes," Osric said quietly. "I know the shape of that lie. Mine is that I protected the temple for thirty years because I loved it, when the truth is I was afraid of it." He rose, brushing ash from his robes. "Perhaps that's what trust is, Ser Vale. Two people admitting the shape of their lies to each other, and choosing to stand in the same direction anyway."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then she held out the flask.

"One drink," she said. "And if you ever cite my trial record again, I'll break your other arm."

Osric smiled—a small, tired, real smile—and drank.

Across the hollow, Felix sat awake beside Wren, showing her the letters of the common alphabet by firelight, the dragon-scale glowing faintly warm between them. Watching them, Maren felt the strange, unaccustomed weight of something she had not carried since the night the king stopped being her king: the sense of having people. Fragile, wounded, untrustworthy people, each holding a piece of a truth none of them could carry whole.

It was Wren who lifted her head first, eyes going wide and dark, the scale blazing suddenly against her chest.

"Maren," she whispered. "Emberfall says the sky is wrong. Something is coming from the city. Something that isn't Osric's soldiers." The child's voice cracked. "It's coming fast."
