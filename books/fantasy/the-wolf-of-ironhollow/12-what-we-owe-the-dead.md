## 12. What We Owe the Dead

The fire was going out.

Wren had known it the way you know a storm is coming before the clouds gather—some old animal knowledge sitting in her chest, patient and certain. Emberfall's light had been the color of forge-coal when Wren was small, a red so deep it seemed to come from inside the stone of the eyrie itself. Then it had gone the color of embers banked for the night. Now it was the color of the last coals in a hearth no one tended: orange fringed with grey, flickering at the edges as though the wind through the broken vaulting had opinions about whether the dragon should keep burning at all.

Wren sat on the warm stones before her, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, and refused to look away. Looking away felt like agreement.

"You're staring again," Emberfall said. Her voice had changed too. It used to roll through the eyrie like distant thunder deciding where to fall; now it was smoke and paper, beautiful still, but thin. Thin the way an old cloak is thin—you only notice when the cold gets through. "Little wren. Staring will not feed a fire."

"Neither will talking." Wren's voice came out sharper than she meant. That happened a lot lately. Everything in her came out sharp, like she'd been whittled down to edges. "So stop talking about dying."

The dragon's eye was the size of Wren's whole head, and when Emberfall turned it toward her, the world turned with it. There was gold in that eye, and patience older than the valley, older than the wards, older than the names of gods. And there was something else now, something new: a tenderness so vast it frightened Wren more than the dimming did.

"I am not going to die," Emberfall said. "I am going to *leave*. There is a difference, and you are old enough now to learn it, though I confess I had hoped to teach you gentler things first."

"There's nothing gentler left."

"No," the dragon agreed, and the word fell between them like a stone into a well. "There isn't."

* * *

Wren had been four years old, maybe five—she didn't know, exactly; the keeper who'd left her in the high pass hadn't bothered with dates—when she first understood what Emberfall was. Not a beast, not a mountain that breathed. A person. A person with a name, and griefs, and a memory so long it had worn grooves in her, the way water wears stone.

She remembered being carried against scales warm as sun-baked rock. She remembered learning to sleep in the curve of the dragon's foreleg, in the hollow the keeper had called the cradle, which had been worn smooth by centuries of children who were never Wren. She remembered songs—Emberfall hummed when she thought Wren was asleep, low notes that moved through the bones of the eyrie and came back changed, and Wren would lie awake cataloguing them, hoarding them the way other children hoarded river-stones.

She had never once, in all those years, imagined a morning without the humming.

Now she lay awake in the cradle—too big for it now, her legs hanging over the edge—and listened to the silence where the humming should be, and hated everything: the silence, the dimming, the strangers downstairs arguing in low voices about kings and gods, the stranger with the burning blood who looked at her scale like he knew its weight, the whole wide colored world that had let this happen.

Because that was the worst part, the part that sat in her stomach like swallowed ice: the world had done this. The world outside the valley, with its wars and its worships and its hungry god wearing a king's face—that world had spent Emberfall. Every year of her long slow burning, spent holding something closed, or holding something back, or simply outlasting everything else until she alone remained, and remaining alone is just another way of dying slowly.

Wren pressed her face into the smooth stone of the cradle and did not cry, because crying was agreeing, and she had decided not to agree.

* * *

In the morning—or what passed for morning in the ruined eyrie, a grey seeping through the shattered roof—Emberfall called her down.

Not with words. With the song.

It was one note, held long, and it went through Wren like a hand through water. She was down the spiral stair before she was properly awake, barefoot on cold stone, heart hammering, telling herself it was nothing, it was just a summons, dragons summoned things all the time—

But the note had been shaped wrong. She knew every one of Emberfall's notes the way she knew her own heartbeat, and this one was shaped like an ending. Like the last page of a story, folded open.

The great head lay low among the stones. Emberfall's scales, which had always thrown back light even in darkness—even dying darkness—had gone matte, ash-grey shot through with veins of dull red, like a coal remembering fire rather than having any. Her breath moved the air in slow tides. When she spoke, each word cost something visible, some small collapse deep in the vast body.

"You have brought me the scribe-blood," she said. "Good. Sit. Both of you. What I have left to give cannot be given twice."

Felix stood at the mouth of the great hall, hovering the way he hovered, hands half-raised like a man afraid of touching anything. Maren leaned against a fallen pillar behind him, arms crossed, watching with the flat careful expression she wore instead of feelings. Wren dropped onto the warm stone beside the dragon's jaw and glared at Felix as if the whole thing were his fault. Some of it was. He had a face like a door someone had forgotten to lock.

"Wren," Emberfall said.

"No."

"I have not asked anything yet."

"You're going to ask me to say goodbye. I heard the note. I'm not stupid."

The dragon's breath stirred Wren's hair, warm and smelling of hot iron and, faintly, of the high snow-passes where she had hatched a thousand years ago. "No, little one. Goodbyes are for creatures who end. I am asking you to learn a song."

"A song." Wren's laugh cracked in the middle. "You're dying and you want to teach me *songs*."

"I am dying," Emberfall said, with terrible gentleness, "and I want to teach you the only thing I have ever made that outlives me. Listen."

And she sang.

* * *

It was not like her other humming. Those had been comfort-sounds, lullabies, the idle music of a mind too large for silence. This was architecture. This was a building made of sound, and Wren could feel its walls rising around her as the notes went on—low first, deeper than hearing, felt in the teeth and the floor and the wet dark of the lungs; then higher, threading through, bright as sparks off a grindstone; and under all of it a pulse, a rhythm like a heart that had learned to beat outside a body.

"The first fire was sung," Emberfall said, between phrases, her voice fraying at the edges of each note. "Before your temples, before your wards, before your gods put names on and called themselves creators. Fire does not begin. Fire is *carried*. From the first burning thing to the next, hand to hand, throat to throat, age to age. When a flame has sung long enough—when it has loved enough—it learns the way across. The way from one vessel into another."

Wren's hands had gone cold. "Across to what?"

"To whoever carries the song." The great eye turned to her, and in it Wren saw reflected a tiny version of herself, small and fierce and utterly unready. "I have carried my flame for longer than your kind has kept written words. I am nearly empty. But a flame does not go out, little wren. Not truly. It goes *elsewhere*. If there is a throat that knows the song. If there is a chest willing to hold it."

"No." Wren was on her feet without deciding to stand. "No. You are not putting your fire in me. I've seen what carrying things does to people. Look at him—" she jabbed a finger at Felix, who flinched, "—his whole life was carrying something he never chose. Look at the king. Look at *everyone*. Everyone who carries something big ends up hollowed out or nailed down or dead, and I am not—I am not going to be a *vessel*, I'm a person, I'm *your* person, I'm not a lamp—"

"Wren."

"—and you can't dress it up in a song! You can't make it beautiful! You're just trying to make me do the burying before you're even dead, so it's tidy, so it's *convenient*—"

"*Wren.*"

The note stopped her. Not loud—the opposite of loud. Emberfall poured the whole remainder of herself into a single tone so pure and so sad that Wren's anger broke apart against it like surf on a sea-cliff, and she stood there shaking, fists clenched, tears finally coming because they had beaten her, they had gotten past the decision not to cry.

"I am not making you a vessel," the dragon said. "Do you think I do not know what vessels suffer? I watched the god-makers make one of my clutchmate. I watched what wore him afterward. No." A long, labored breath, a tide going out. "A vessel is filled by force, by another's purpose. I am offering you a *hearth*. A hearth is chosen. It holds the fire not because it must, but because the fire is welcome, and the ones who come in from the cold are welcome, and the warmth belongs to everyone who sits beside it. That is what I am offering. Not a cage. A home that walks."

"And if I say no?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, wind moved through the broken vaulting, and loose slate ticked down onto stone.

"Then the song dies with me," Emberfall said simply. "The last flame gutters, and the dark that follows is only dark—not cruel, not hungry, merely empty. And you will grieve, and heal, and live, and I will have lost nothing by your refusal, because your life was never mine to spend. That is what love is, little wren. It is the one debt that is paid by never being collected."

"That's not fair," Wren whispered. "You can't say things like that. You can't say things like that and then die. It's cheating."

"It is entirely cheating," Emberfall agreed. "I have had a thousand years to perfect it."

Despite everything—a sob tore out of Wren, ugly and wet, and she crossed the space and pressed both palms flat against the dragon's cheek, against scales gone cool as evening stone. "Teach me the rest tomorrow," she begged. "You always said tomorrow. Teach me the rest tomorrow and the day after and the day after that, and I'll learn it so slow you'll be ancient, you'll be *older*, we have time—"

"We do not." The great eye closed, opened. "But hear me, Wren-of-the-eyrie, daughter of no one and everyone: I am not teaching you this song so that you lose me. I am teaching it so that you never do. Every note you carry is a morning I am still humming. You will lie awake some night years from now, and you will think you hear it—and you will not be imagining it. It will be me. Being early. Practicing."

Wren laughed and sobbed at once, a horrible tangled sound. "That doesn't even make sense."

"Most true things don't." The dragon's wing shifted, folding closer around the girl like a wall against the whole indifferent world. "We begin again at dawn. All the verses. And you will complain, and get them wrong, and stamp your feet, and I will correct you, and it will be the best work of my very long life."

Maren had turned away, her shoulders rigid, doing whatever it was she did instead of weeping. Felix hadn't moved at all. He was staring at Emberfall with an expression Wren couldn't read—hunger and horror in equal measure, the look of a man seeing the shape of his own future in someone else's ending.

* * *

They began again at dawn. And the next dawn. And the one after that.

Wren learned the way she learned everything—badly first, then furiously, then all at once. She got the third verse wrong eleven times and stamped her feet exactly as predicted. She got the seventh verse wrong once and never again, because the seventh verse was the one about leaving, and something in her refused to sing it incorrectly, as if precision might somehow argue with the truth of it.

Each day Emberfall dimmed a little more. Each day the verses came a little harder to her, and Wren found herself singing parts *back* to the dragon, feeding the melody upstream, and Emberfall would close her eyes and listen as though drinking after a long thirst.

On the fourth dawn, the dragon did not call her with the song.

Wren woke already running.

Emberfall lay among her own greyness like a hill of cooling slag, breath a shallow tide, but her eye found Wren and held her, and the voice that came out was barely a whisper of smoke. "One verse remains. The last one. I saved it—" a rattling pause— "because it is not mine to teach. It is yours. The last verse has no notes, little one. It is only this: *when the moment comes, mean it.* The song carries what the singer carries. Nothing more. Nothing less. Carry me well, and I will burn forever. Carry me in fear, and I will gutter. That is the whole of it. That was always the whole of it."

"I mean everything," Wren said fiercely. "I have never meant anything in my whole life as much as I mean this."

"I know," said Emberfall. "That is why it took me a thousand years to find you."

The eye closed. The tide of breath slowed.

And far away—so far it might have been imagination, might have been hope, might have been a promise already traveling toward her across the whole width of the world—Wren heard, faint and unmistakable, the first note of the song.

Humming. In her own chest.

Below, in the ruins, Felix burst up the stair two steps at a time, wild-eyed, the dragon-scale in his fist blazing like a brand. "Wren—the scale—it's burning—something's coming, something's *found* us—"

Wren rose, and the hum in her chest rose with her, and she did not look back at the grey hill that had been her mother, because looking back was agreement, and she had decided—she was *deciding*—that endings were just doors she hadn't learned to sing open yet.

"How fast can you run?" she said.
