## 13. Palace of Whispers

The palace of Aldric the Devout had a hundred gates, and every one of them was watched. It had one door that was not.

Felix found it the way he found everything—by reading. Three days in the capital's undercity, hunched over borrowed ledgers in the hedge-witch's smoke-dark shop, and the answer had been waiting in a two-hundred-year-old record of guild dues: *the Scriptorium of the Crown pays no gate-toll, for the king's word travels by no man's hand but a scribe's.*

The scriptors' guild had its own entrance. A plain door in the eastern wall, beneath a carved lintel worn smooth by the shoulders of generations of men and women who had learned, as Felix had learned, that the surest way to be invisible in a kingdom was to be the one holding the pen.

"You're certain about this," Maren said. She stood with her back to the wall of the tanner's row, hood up, and even hooded she looked like what she was—a sword someone had tried to sheathe in a peasant's cloak. Her eyes kept moving. Gate. Rooftop. The inquisitor's patrol turning the corner by the chandlery, white hoods bright as bone in the morning light.

"No," Felix said. "But I'm certain about the alternative, and it's worse."

Wren crouched at his knee in the shadow of a rain barrel, and Felix had to look away from her, because looking at her too long made the plan feel like what it was: a gamble with a child's life folded into the stake. She had Emberfall's scale on a cord around her neck—no, not the scale, that was his; she had a *tooth*, a dragon's tooth no bigger than his thumb, and she kept touching it the way other children touched a mother's hand.

"Say it again," Wren said. "What you're going to be."

"A scribe," Felix said. "A journeyman of the Ironhollow scriptorium, come to the capital to petition the guild for standing. Name of Felix Corvane. Ink-stained, dull, slightly desperate. The kind of man no one remembers by supper."

"And me?"

"You're my sister. You're bored. You're going to sit by the guildhouse door and be small and forgotten, which I know you hate, and I'm sorry."

Wren's chin came up. "I'm not small."

"You're *strategically* sized," Felix said, and something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go, once she'd finished learning how. "If I'm not out by the second bell after midday, you run. You don't argue, you don't wait, you run to Maren and you don't stop running until you're behind the witch's wards. Swear it."

The girl looked at him with those too-old eyes, the ones that had watched a dragon die by inches for years, and Felix understood that she was deciding whether to let him have this lie. She was deciding whether loving someone meant pretending his plans were good.

"Second bell," she said at last. "Swear it on the fire."

"I swear it on the fire."

* * *

The guildhouse swallowed him whole, and it was the easiest thing Felix had ever done, and that frightened him more than any inquisitor.

Because it was *home*. That was the obscenity of it. The smell of size and lampblack, the scratch of a hundred nibs in a hall lit by high clerestory windows, the soft institutional murmur of men reading aloud to catch errors—it was Ironhollow's scriptorium with better light and worse air, and his body knew it the way a dog knows the shape of its own kennel. His feet found the journeyman's bench. His hands found the correct posture, spine straight, wrists loose, the posture ten thousand hours of temple drudgery had carved into him.

A guild examiner, a woman with ink to her elbows and spectacles that magnified her eyes into twin startled moons, looked at his petition letter, looked at his hands, looked at the way he'd automatically squared the blotter before sitting down.

"Ironhollow," she said. "The Quiet Flame's scriptorium. We don't get many of you out here. Valley folk don't travel."

"Valley folk don't have much to travel *for*," Felix said, and pitched his voice into the exact register of a man whose life has been small and intends to keep it that way. "I want standing. A wage. A bed that isn't in a dormitory with forty other men who snore in canon."

She snorted. It was the right sound. Somewhere behind it, he knew, was the machinery of the guild's own quiet politics—journeyman slots, patronage, the eternal war over the good lamp-oil—and he had already begun, in the way of scribes everywhere, to map the factions by the ink they used. Three men at the far bench used the cheap tallow-black of the counting house. Two used temple violet, which was interesting, because the temple was supposed to have no presence here. And the examiner herself used a fine iron-gall with a thread of oak-gall blue that Felix had seen exactly once before: in the ledgers of the crown's privy chamber, in the sample book Maren had stolen from a dead inquisitor.

*The examiner is the crown's ear in the guild.* He filed it away, and bowed his head, and became, thoroughly, Felix Corvane, journeyman, dull as ditchwater.

They tested him, of course. They always test. A fair hand, a passage of old law to transcribe, a psalm in the temple shorthand that only valley-trained scribes used. He did all of it at the speed of a man who is competent but not remarkable, because remarkable gets remembered, and remembered gets *looked at*, and being looked at was the one thing his whole life had trained him to avoid.

By the second bell he had what he'd come for, and it wasn't guild standing. It was the roster.

Every scribe in the palace was assigned by the guild, and every assignment was posted on the guild board in a hand that changed weekly. Felix read it the way other men read battlefields. The privy chamber: four scribes, all senior, all temple-violet. The exchequer: six. The king's *personal correspondence*: one name, alone, in a hand that had been rewritten three times in the past year, each time more hastily.

*Seren of the Lower Scriptorium. Assigned by direct warrant of the Lord Inquisitor. No substitution permitted.*

One scribe for a king's correspondence. No substitution. A warrant, not a request.

Felix copied the roster into his memory—truly into his memory, the old valley discipline of the *inked mind*, where you built a room in your head and hung every fact on a hook—and then he made his mistake on purpose. He let the examiner catch him looking at the board.

"Everyone wants the palace postings," she said, coming up behind him. "Everyone wants to be the one who writes the king's words." Her magnified eyes were unreadable. "You'd be surprised how few want to keep them."

"Keep them?"

"Archive them. File them. The palace archive is undermanned." She said it lightly, but her hand had come to rest on the board, covering the name *Seren* as though the ink might smear. "A valley man like you would find it quiet work. Familiar."

*She's offering me the archive.* Felix's heart did something complicated. Either the crown's ear had just tried to recruit him into the one room in the palace where every document in the kingdom came to die—which was either a trap or a miracle—or she was something else entirely, something that wanted a stranger poking through the king's papers, and he could not tell which, and he had until the second bell to decide.

"I'd be honored," Felix said.

* * *

The palace archive was in the undercroft of the eastern range, and it was exactly as advertised: quiet, undermanned, and full of dead men's paper.

It was also, Felix realized within the first hour, *lying to him*.

He felt it the way he'd learned to feel it in Ironhollow—the wrongness in a ledger that balances too neatly, the petition that answers a question no one asked. He'd spent his life copying prayers, and prayers have a grain, like wood, and when a document is written to be read a certain way you can feel the grain running the wrong direction.

The archive's catalog said the king's correspondence for the past year was filed under *R* for *Routine*. Felix pulled the first box with hands that had gone very steady, the way they did when the temple elders walked past his bench, and he read.

The king's letters were bland. Perfectly bland. Blessings on the harvest, thanks to allied lords, confirmations of appointments. Every one of them in a fine, confident, slightly ornamental hand.

Every one of them wrong.

Because Felix had spent three weeks on the road with Ser Maren Vale, and Maren had talked about Aldric the way you talk about a brother you failed—not his policies, his *hand*. "He wrote letters to the widows of every man who died in his service," she'd said once, staring into a campfire. "Every one, in his own hand, no matter how many. He said a king's grief should cost him something. His letters were ugly. He pressed too hard and blotted them and he never let anyone fix it. He said the ugliness was the honesty."

These letters were beautiful. These letters were the hand of something that had never grieved in its life.

Felix sat in the lamplight of the archive with a box of the king's beautiful, lying correspondence, and he thought: *the thing wearing the king writes like this, but the king—*

The king was still writing.

He found the first one by accident, or told himself it was an accident, in the waste-basket of the privy chamber drafts that had been sent down for pulping. A scrap, half-burned at the edge, in a hand that pressed too hard and blotted:

*count the bells that do not ring*

He nearly wept, right there among the dead men's paper. He didn't. He was a scribe, and scribes weep on their own time. But his hands shook as he smoothed the scrap flat, and he understood that he was holding the first thread of a trail, and that a drowning man had tied it off hoping someone, someday, would wade in after him.

*Count the bells that do not ring.*

He went back to the archive and stopped reading the king's letters and started reading *around* them—the marginalia, the filing slips, the archivist's marks. And there, in the official record of the palace bells, a discrepancy so small only a man who had spent his life noticing ink would ever see it: the bell schedule listed nine bells of the evening peal. The physical tower, per the mason's survey of forty years ago, had housings for ten.

One bell that did not ring. One bell that had been silenced.

* * *

The tenth bell tower was a stump above the chapel roof, sealed with a cap of lead, and getting to it required every skill Felix had never known he was weaponizing.

He did it with paper. That was the joke of it, the beautiful, bitter joke: he forged a work order. Twenty minutes at a guild bench with the right iron-gall and the right hand—the examiner's hand, learned from a requisition slip she'd discarded—and he was the bearer of an authorized inspection of the chapel roof lead, and two bored guards waved him up a ladder with nothing but a lantern and a coil of his own patience.

The bell chamber was dark and smelled of pigeon and old bronze. The bell itself hung silent, its clapper bound in cloth, and around the head of the bell, in an engraving so fine it could only be read by touch, ran a line of text.

Felix ran his fingers over it. Old script. Valley script—the liturgical hand of the Quiet Flame, the hand his own ancestors had invented, the hand he'd been copying since he was six years old.

*What is bound in blood let blood unbind. The anchor sleeps where the debt was written.*

He knelt there in the dark with his heart slamming, and the pieces began to move, and they moved with the terrible inevitability of a ledger finally balancing.

*The anchor sleeps where the debt was written.* Where was the debt written? Where had his family's debt been written, maintained, copied, renewed for nine generations? In the temple rolls of Ironhollow. In the *debt-book*.

And Aldric's scraps—the waste-basket trail, a drowning man's coded messages—had led here, to this bell, to this sentence. The king had known. The hollowed king, buried alive inside his own body, had spent a year hiding a message in the one place his captor never looked: in the boring, beautiful, official paper that no one reads.

*The hollowing ritual requires a blood-anchor.* That was what Maren's stolen documents had said, what the defrocked inquisitor Halvard had half-translated before the inquisitors caught up to them. The god wearing the crown needed an anchor of the founding bloodline to hold its shape, the way a sail needs a mast. And there was only one founding bloodline left in the world.

Felix's.

*He's been protecting me.* The thought arrived whole, and it broke something open in Felix's chest. A year of coded messages, a silenced bell, scraps in a wastebasket—a king drowning in his own skull, spending what remained of himself not on escape, not on screaming for help, but on making sure that the last of the bloodline, some unknown valley scribe, would never be found. Aldric had hidden the anchor's identity the way you hide a child: not by fighting the thing that hunts, but by being so quietly, so painstakingly *boring* that the hunter looks elsewhere.

The king had never met him. The king had spent his own mind to shield a stranger.

Felix pressed his forehead against the cold bronze of the silenced bell, and for a moment he let himself feel all of it—the rage and the grief and the unbearable, unearned kindness of it—and then he did what scribes do. He took out his notebook, and he copied the engraving, and he added it to the inked mind, hung on its hook.

And that was when he saw the last scrap, wedged in the joint of the bell-frame where a desperate hand had pushed it years ago—no, months, the paper was still white. Fresh. *Recent.*

In the king's ugly, honest hand:

*If you found this, you are the one I have been hiding. I am sorry. Do not come for me. The ritual needs you alive and willing. Stay willing to be nothing, and you stay free. — A.*

Felix read it three times. The lantern guttered. Somewhere below, the palace bells began to mark the hour, nine bells of the evening peal, and the tenth hung silent above his head, and Felix Oyelaran—Felix Corvane, journeyman, dull as ditchwater, invisible as ink—understood that the second bell after midday had come and gone long ago, that Wren would be running to Maren right now with his failure, that the plan had worked too well and found too much.

And he understood one thing more, the thing that turned his blood to iron: *stay willing to be nothing, and you stay free.*

The king had offered him the one exit. The one door out. Be nothing. Stay hidden. Live.

Felix folded the scrap with the care of a man handling a relic, and put it next to his heart, and began to climb down toward the palace that held a god and a drowning king, knowing exactly what he was going to do instead.

He was going to be *something*.
