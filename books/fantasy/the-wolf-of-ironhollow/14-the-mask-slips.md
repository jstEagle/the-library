## 14. The Mask Slips

Aldric had learned to count in the dark.

Not hours—hours meant nothing anymore—but heartbeats. His own. The only rhythm in the whole of his body that still answered to him and not to the thing coiled around his spine like a second skeleton. Four beats to a breath. Sixteen breaths to a verse. And verses—he had verses. Verses were how a king who could no longer speak in his own voice had learned to write.

He counted them now, in the prison behind his own eyes, while his hands—*his* hands, moving with a grace that was not his own, signing decrees in a script that was not his own—sealed another day of Vhalor's slow coronation.

Four hundred and eleven days since the god had come for him.

He knew because he had counted every one.

---

The hollowing had not begun with pain. That was the cruelty no one warned you about. It began with *convenience*. A morning when the prayer at dawn felt slightly farther away, as if heard through water. A night when he dreamed of his father's face and woke unable to recall whether the man's beard had been grey or black. Small losses. Forgivable losses. The kind any tired king might misplace between one war council and the next.

By the time he understood what was being taken, it had already taken so much that grief itself felt borrowed—grief was a memory, and memories were what Vhalor ate first.

Aldric remembered the moment of clarity the way other men remembered battles. He had been alone in the shrine beneath the east wing, kneeling before an altar to the Quiet Flame that had gone cold years ago, cold the way everything in this palace had gone cold, and he had thought: *I cannot remember my mother's voice.*

And something inside him had laughed.

It was not his laugh. It came from below his ribs, from deeper than marrow, from whatever place Vhalor had made its nest, and it was warm and patient and terribly fond—the laugh of a host delighted by a guest's belated understanding. *Now you see,* the laugh said, without words, without sound, only pressure and warmth like sunlight through amber. *Now you know what I am eating.*

Aldric had knelt on that cold floor until his knees went numb, and somewhere in those hours he made the decision that had kept him alive—not alive, precisely; *intact*—ever since.

If the god ate memories, then memories could be spent.

---

His hands finished the decree. He watched them do it from the small dark room at the center of himself, the room he had built out of will and repetition, the room where he counted heartbeats. The decree ordered the execution of three farmers in the western provinces who had been heard praying to dead gods. Vhalor loved executions of the faithful. They were meals. Every death offered up in terror fed the thing wearing him, and every feeding made Aldric's cage smaller.

*Three farmers,* he thought, pressing each name into himself like a brand. *Hale of Westmarch. The brothers Odo and Tam. Their mothers' faces—I don't have their mothers' faces. I never saw them. Remember that you never saw them, so that when the guilt comes you can tell it apart from the theft.*

This was the discipline. This was the whole of his kingdom now, this dark room and its ledgers. He kept accounts the way a starving man keeps crumbs: what was his, what was taken, what was spent. He had lost his mother's voice on day sixty-two. His wedding day on day ninety-eight, all of it, Elenna's face gone so thoroughly that he now mourned a stranger he knew only from portraits—which was perhaps why Vhalor had left the portraits hanging. It liked him to grieve things it had already digested. Grief without substance was just obedience wearing mourning clothes.

But some things he had defended. Some things he had paid dearly to keep, hoarding them in the deepest drawer of the dark room, wrapped in verses, buried under false memories he had deliberately cultivated—false trails, decoy recollections of a childhood that never happened, planted like thornbushes around the few true things remaining:

That the ritual which hollowed him required an anchor.

That the anchor was blood.

That there was one last anchor in the world, and her line ran through a valley where prayers went unanswered, and her name—

No.

Even now he would not shape it. Even here, in the locked room, he kept it as a shape without a sound, a door without a handle. A scribe. He allowed himself that much. There was a scribe in Ironhollow who copied prayers he did not believe in, and the god wanted him, and the god did not yet know that the king knew.

Aldric had spent two years learning the exact dimensions of what Vhalor did not know.

---

* *

They came for him at dusk, or what the palace called dusk—the windows had long since been enchanted to show a sky that never quite matched the real one, gold when the court needed warmth, bruise-purple when Vhalor wished the petitioners subdued. Tonight the sky outside the glass was the color of a healing wound, and Aldric stood at the map table in the war room, watching hands that were his and not his move carved armies across a country they were bleeding dry.

Then the pressure changed.

He had learned to read Vhalor's attention the way sailors read weather. Most days the god's awareness spread through him thin and diffuse, a fog with a thousand concerns: the tribute caravans, the inquisitors' reports, the taste of ten thousand worshippers murmuring his new name. But sometimes the fog *condensed*. Drew tight. Became an eye.

The eye was open now. And it was looking south.

*Ironhollow,* Aldric thought, and made himself feel nothing, because the god could taste feeling the way a snake tastes heat. *You feel nothing. You are a king reviewing maps. You have always been a king reviewing maps.*

The pressure slid along the inside of his skull, deliberate, tasting. Somewhere beyond his control his hand set down the army piece it held—too gently, too precisely—and turned toward the window.

Through the wounded-colored glass he watched his own reflection, and the reflection watched him back, and for a moment—a heartbeat, four beats, sixteen breaths—he saw past it. Saw the thing wearing his face the way one sees a lantern behind paper: Vhalor's attention blazing through his eyes, vast and old and hungry, older than the pantheon that had chained it, patient as rot.

*Something moved,* the god said. Not aloud. Never aloud. Words would have been a mercy; words could be refused. Vhalor spoke in pressures, in tides. *In the valley that does not answer. A prayer was answered.*

*A miracle,* Aldric's mouth said, in his voice but not his cadence. He hated how well the god had learned his cadence. *The people are pleased. Pilgrims are already asking after the road.*

*The wards are failing.* The tide pressed closer. Aldric felt it probing along the walls of the dark room, patient fingers testing every seam. *Wards do not fail, little king. Wards are *unmade*. Someone unmade them. Someone with the right blood.*

*I am no student of valley genealogies.*

*No.* The amusement rolled through him like a stone through a bell. *You are a student of very little these days. You sleep so much. You dream so strangely. You leave such curious... marginalia.*

The world went white.

Not pain—pain he could have borne, pain was a language he had grown fluent in. This was *search*. Vhalor reached into him the way a man reaches into a sack of grain, plunging both hands deep and turning everything over, and Aldric felt his memories churn past the god's attention like coins in a beggar's cup: his coronation, spilled out and examined and dropped; his father's funeral, handled, weighed, discarded; the hunt in the northern passes when he was nineteen, the boar, the broken leg, the shepherd boy who'd carried him down the mountain—

The shepherd boy. The god lingered there. Turned the boy over. Found nothing. Moved on.

*Decoys,* Aldric reminded himself, from very far away, from the bottom of the dark room, counting. Four beats. Sixteen breaths. *Let him sift the decoys. Let him find the shepherd, the false tutor, the invented sister. Let him grow bored. Boredom is a wall. Boredom has held for two years.*

But this time the god did not grow bored.

This time the tide withdrew slowly, reluctantly, the way a hand withdraws from a purse it suspects is not empty, and the pressure gathered itself into words again, soft as ash falling:

*There is a knight.*

The dark room shuddered.

*Maren Vale. My inquisitors chased her to the valley's edge and lost her, which should be impossible, and is impossible, unless someone inside the valley helped her. A scribe's undercroft, they tell me. A hiding place among the prayer-records. How quaint.* A pause, and Aldric felt the full weight of the god's attention settle on him like snowfall, silent and crushing. *You knew her. Before. She swore her oaths to you. She wept when you stopped speaking in your own voice—yes, I know about that too, little king. I let you keep your tears because they salted the meat.*

*She is a traitor to the crown,* his mouth said. *When she is found, she will hang.*

*She will,* Vhalor agreed pleasantly, *and so will whoever taught her that kings can be rescued. Unless—" and the tide rose, gentle, almost tender, the tenderness that was worse than any violence— "unless there is nothing left in this body worth rescuing. I wonder, Aldric. I wonder how much of you is still in there. I wonder what a drowning man clutches. I wonder what he would burn to hide."

* *

That night, in the dark room, Aldric opened the deepest drawer.

He had built the room out of a single afternoon he remembered perfectly: the day he was nine years old and his mother had shown him the royal ledgers and told him, *A kingdom survives on what it writes down, not what it remembers. Memory dies with the man. Ink doesn't.* He had built the room from that sentence. He had furnished it with everything he most needed to survive: the ritual's structure, the truth of the blood-anchor, the shape of a plan he had been threading through state documents for a year—coded messages in tax rolls and border decrees, invisible to everyone except, he prayed, someone with a scribe's eye and a traitor's heart.

The messages were compromised now. He understood that. Maren Vale had reached the valley and been found; the trail she followed would lead Vhalor's servants to the same documents, and once the god suspected the code existed, it would tear every ledger in the palace apart page by page. The messages had done their work or failed to, and either way they were spent.

But the messages pointed to the anchor. And the anchor's identity—if Vhalor learned it before the fire came—was lost. Lost forever. The god would take the last anchor, wear them as it wore him, and there would be nothing left in the world that could burn it out.

So Aldric sat in the dark room, in the prison at the center of his own skull, and took inventory like a bankrupt king pawning his crown.

What remained that was truly his?

His mother's voice: gone, day sixty-two.

Elenna: gone, all of her, day ninety-eight. He had spent her saving the ritual's structure, trading her memory piece by piece across a hundred desperate nights while the god slept its shallow, greedy sleep—her laugh for the binding-words, her hands for the sequence of the rite, her voice saying his name for the knowledge of where the anchor's blood must be spilled. He had spent his wife to buy a stranger's salvation and he had never once regretted it, and he could not remember her face, and the not-remembering was the price, and the price had been right.

His children. He had no children. He checked, as he checked every week, with the particular dread of a man confirming a wound hasn't reopened. No children. Vhalor had eaten that hope before it ever bore fruit, and there was nothing left even to mourn.

The shepherd boy. The boar hunt. Nineteen years old and the rain coming down sideways and a farm boy carrying a prince on his back, laughing, telling him the names of stars in a dialect the court had forbidden. Aldric held it up and looked at it for a long time.

It was true. It was the truest thing he had left.

And it was worthless—or rather, it was worth exactly one thing: it could be traded. A memory that vivid, that beloved, that *specific*, could be woven over the deepest drawer like a blanket. If Vhalor searched him again—and the god would search again, tomorrow, the next day, hungry now, suspicious now—it would find the shepherd boy glowing there like a coal and would believe, would *want* to believe, that this was the treasure the drowning man clutched. Kings cling to their childhoods. It was plausible. Plausibility was armor.

But a blanket must be stitched from something, and the only thread strong enough was the memory itself.

*Burn it,* he told himself. *Burn the boy. Burn the rain and the stars and the laughter. Spend it.*

He hesitated.

In the dark room, in the dark, Aldric Montrose—no; the king had no surname anymore; Vhalor had filed it off early, the way one files a dog's teeth—Aldric, then, the king, the remnant, the account-keeper, knelt in the ruin of himself and understood with perfect clarity what he was about to do, and understood also that this was the last hesitation he would ever be permitted. After tonight there would be less of him. After tonight the room would be barer, colder, emptier, and he would go on living in it, counting heartbeats, keeping ledgers, guarding a name he could no longer pronounce for a person he could no longer picture.

Was it worth it? A stranger. A scribe who copied prayers he didn't believe in. A nobody from a valley of nobodies, whose entire inheritance was a chain.

*Elenna,* he thought, reaching for her and finding, as always, only the shape of the absence, a portrait-shaped hole in the world. *I spent you for this. I will spend everything for this. What else is a king, in the end, but the last one standing between his people and the dark?*

He picked up the shepherd boy. He held him once more—the rain, the stars, the laughing, the terrible kindness of strangers—and he said goodbye the way one says goodbye to the dead, completely, without reservation, because half-goodbyes curdle into hauntings.

Then he burned him, and wove the ashes over the deepest drawer, and felt the last warm room in his childhood go dark forever.

Somewhere far above, in a body that no longer fully belonged to him, the king sighed in his sleep, and the sound was small and human and utterly genuine, and Vhalor, listening, heard only a tired old man dreaming of nothing at all.

* *

Three nights later the summons came.

Aldric knew before the herald finished speaking. He had felt the tide change at dawn—had felt the god rise through him like floodwater, exultant, *fed*, and knew that somewhere in the city or the palace or the pages of a ledger, something had been found. A message decoded. A trail recovered. Perhaps even the scribe himself, already in chains, already being carried toward the throne room where Aldric would be forced to sit and watch and feel nothing, feel nothing, *feel nothing*—

The throne room doors opened. He walked them, his legs, his stride, the god's poise draped over his shoulders like a stolen cloak. Courtiers bowed. The bruise-light fell through the high windows. On the throne—on *his* throne—he settled, and folded his hands, and waited with the perfect patience of a man who has already sold everything he owns.

Vhalor's attention swept the hall, and then turned inward, and spoke, and for the first time in four hundred and eleven days, the god sounded uncertain:

*The messages speak of an anchor. The trail leads to Ironhollow. But every document ends at the same blank—the name is nowhere, the blood-line is nowhere. Either your scribes failed you, little king...*

The tide paused. Tested the air.

*...or you burned it out of yourself. And if you burned it, Aldric—if you spent your own memory to hide a name—then the name matters. Then the name is the whole war.*

The god laughed, low and delighted, and the throne room heard its king laugh and did not know why the sound made their skins crawl.

*Very well,* Vhalor said, rising through Aldric's throat, wearing his voice like a glove. "Bring me the valley," it told the hall, in the king's voice, with the king's smile. "Every record. Every register. Every prayer-book and parish roll. We will read Ironhollow down to its foundations, and when we find the name that isn't written anywhere—"

Inside, in the dark room, in the cold, Aldric counted his heartbeats and held the ashes of his childhood around the last secret in the world, and prayed—to no god, to the quiet flame, to the stranger-scribe he could no longer fully remember why he was saving—that whatever was coming would arrive before the ledgers did.

"—we will know it," the god finished, "by what burns."
