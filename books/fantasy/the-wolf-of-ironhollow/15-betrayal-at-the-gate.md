## 15. Betrayal at the Gate

Maren Vale had learned to read a battlefield by its silences, and the silence around the shrine-town of Candlemere was the wrong kind.

It wasn't the hush of a place at rest. It was the held breath of a place waiting—streets swept too clean, shutters closed but not barred, smoke rising straight and thin from chimneys where no supper should be cooking yet. The old god-shrine at the town's heart, cracked and moss-eaten like all the others they'd passed since leaving the capital's undercity, stood open to the sky. Someone had lit candles in it. Dozens of them. Their flames didn't move.

"Wind's from the east," Wren said quietly beside her. The girl had gone still in that particular way of hers, head cocked, listening to something Maren couldn't hear—the dragon-song, maybe, or whatever Emberfall's fading fire whispered to the child it had raised. "The flames are standing wrong."

"I see it."

"They're not wrong like magic-wrong." Wren's fingers found the strap of her satchel, where Emberfall's last scale hung wrapped in wool against her chest. "They're wrong like *obedient*."

Felix came up the road behind them with Osric, the two of them deep in the low-voiced argument about scripture and wards that had become their strange ritual over the weeks on the road—a monk who'd left his temple to undermine it, and a debtor-scribe who'd left it because it tried to bury him, circling each other through theology like two dogs deciding whether to fight or sleep curled together. Maren had stopped pretending she wasn't fond of watching it.

"—the Litany of Severance doesn't even appear in the Ironhollow canon before the third century," Osric was saying. "Which means either your ancestors invented it later to justify the debt, or—"

"Or it was always there and the temple burned the earlier copies." Felix's voice had an edge Maren recognized now, the one he got when the valley's arithmetic of debt touched a bruise. "You of all people should believe that possible."

"I believe it entirely. That's what worries me."

"Hold," Maren said.

They stopped. The candle-flames stood like little soldiers. At the far end of the market square, before the shrine, a table had been set out—white cloth, silver ewers, chairs enough for six. A banquet laid for ghosts in the middle of an abandoned town.

And seated at the table, hands folded, wearing the grey traveling habit of a Temple envoy instead of her inquisitor's black, sat Mother Kestrel of the Order of the Quiet Flame.

Maren knew the name. Every knight of the outer kingdom knew it—Kestrel, who had burned four hedge-covens in the Saltmarsh years without trial; Kestrel, whose signature appeared on the writ that had sent Maren running from the capital with a royal seal pressed into her palm and a king's last sane whisper in her ear: *find the anchor*. She was small, silver-haired, with a face like a kindly grandmother and eyes that had never once been kind.

Beside her stood two figures in plain robes, faces shadowed, and—Maren's hand went to her sword—one more person she knew far better than she wished to.

Brother Osric made a sound like he'd been struck.

"No," he said. "No, that isn't—I *left*—"

"You did," Felix said softly. He had gone very pale, but his voice was steady, and Maren saw him do the thing he always did when fear came: he reached for ink and order, cataloguing. "Which is exactly why she's here. You can't betray someone who's already gone. But you can be used as proof that the traitor's friends are liars too." He glanced sideways. "I'm sorry. I know that's not what you want to hear right now."

"It's not—" Osric's jaw worked. "I need you to understand, whatever happens down there. Whatever you see. I need you to remember that I—"

A bell rang somewhere beneath the town. Once. The candle-flames all bent east at once, toward the shrine, like grass under wind, and straightened again.

Mother Kestrel rose from her chair and smiled up the empty street at them, though they were still forty yards off and half-hidden. Her voice carried without effort, warm as a hearth and just as contained.

"Scribe Oyelaran. Knight Vale. Child of the eyrie. And Brother Osric, prodigal and much-missed." She spread her hands. "Please. Sit. We've come a long way to offer you a fair bargain, and I'm told the valley folk appreciate fair bargains above all things."

"The valley folk," Felix said as they descended into the square—he wouldn't hang back, Maren realized; hiding had never once worked for him, so he'd learned to walk forward instead, "were taught that the fairest bargain is the one you don't make with someone holding your ledger hostage."

"A clever tongue. The temple wasted you." Kestrel gestured, and one of the hooded figures poured wine into six cups. "Then let me be plain, as befits a soldier's errand. The Temple of the Quiet Flame, in council assembled, offers Felix Oyelaran full pardon. All debts struck. The record of heresy expunged. Restoration of name, of standing, of the scriptorium desk he was never permitted to keep warm."

Maren watched Felix's face do something complicated. Twenty-two years of debt, of invisibility, of copying other people's prayers by lamplight while his own went unanswered—and here was the whole weight of it offered back to him, lifted, forgiven, *free*.

"In exchange," Kestrel continued, "he surrenders the dragon-scale."

"And if he refuses?"

"Then the pardon is withdrawn, the heresy stands, and we take the scale anyway." Kestrel smiled again. "But I am instructed to say—by my own superiors, mind, which should tell you how serious this is—that we would very much prefer the gentle way."

"*Instructed*," Osric said suddenly, stepping forward. His voice cracked and steadied. "Mother. Forgive me. But the Quiet Flame's envoys do not take instruction. They give it. Who gave you yours?"

For the first time, Mother Kestrel's smile flickered—not with guilt, Maren thought, but with the strain of someone maintaining a mask under bright light.

"Brother Osric. You were a promising student. Grief makes us dramatic. Come home."

"I spent nine years copying ledgers in that temple," Osric said, and his voice dropped low, and Maren heard the thing underneath it now, the thing she'd half-guessed on the road: "Nine years learning which entries were altered, which prayers were sealed unread, which petitions went into the furnace instead of the archive. I left because I found a letter in the Founder's hand describing the ward-anchor bloodline in detail—and then found every subsequent record of that bloodline systematically destroyed. You didn't send anyone after me when I fled. Do you know why? Because I was supposed to find Felix first. I was bait, Mother. I've *always* been bait."

The square went very quiet.

"That's a lie," said one of the hooded figures, and threw back its cowl.

Maren's sword cleared its scabbard before her mind caught up—because the face beneath was wrong, smooth as poured wax, features arranged rather than grown, and the eyes were the gold of banked fire. The second figure's cowl fell too. Wax-faced, gold-eyed, identical.

Vhalor's agents. Not coerced temple. *Worn* temple.

"There it is," Felix breathed. "There's the coercion."

Mother Kestrel hadn't moved. She looked at the wax-faces with an expression Maren couldn't parse—until it resolved, horribly, into recognition. Into *familiarity*.

"You," Kestrel said quietly. "You were in the council chamber. You were wearing Chancellor Deverel when I took my orders."

One of the gold-eyed things inclined its head graciously. "We wear what is useful, Mother. As will you."

And Mother Kestrel of the Quiet Flame—burner of covens, signer of writs, the most feared inquisitor of the age—drew a knife and put it to her own throat.

Not her own. Maren understood a heartbeat later, as the woman's hand moved with a smoothness that wasn't hers, her face slack above the blade. *Worn.* How long? Since the council chamber. Perhaps longer. Perhaps everything Kestrel had ever done had been done by the thing inside her—

"Run," said Osric.

The world broke.

* * *

Later, Maren would try to assemble the fight into order and fail. Memory came back to her in shards: the bell tolling again and again beneath the ground; the candle-flames all bending toward Felix like compass needles; Kestrel's worn body moving faster than any human body should, knife flashing, and Maren meeting her blade-on and feeling the wrongness travel up her arm—the strength in it was borrowed, secondhand, a dead woman's muscle driven by a hungry god.

"Felix, the scale!" Wren screamed, and tore it from her satchel herself, and the moment the wool fell away the whole square *changed*—heat rolling out from the girl's cupped hands, the obedient candles guttering, the wax-faces beginning, horribly, to soften and run.

The gold-eyed things turned from Felix to Wren with a single motion, like one creature sharing two bodies.

"The vessel is not required," one said. "Only the fire. Take the child."

Everything after that was Maren failing.

She cut one agent down—it came apart like tallow, screaming in a chorus of voices, hers among them, Deverel's among them, Aldric's among them, and she would hear that chorus in her sleep for the rest of her life—and turned for Wren and found the second one already there, already gripping the girl's wrist, and Osric between them with a prayer-book raised like a shield, shouting words in the old canon that blazed white along their pages.

The words bought three seconds. Three seconds in which the thing wearing Kestrel's body drove its knife past Maren's guard and into her shoulder, in which Felix grabbed for Wren's other arm and missed, in which the world tilted—

—and then heat. Enormous, sourceless heat, and every flame in the square leapt ten feet high, and the wax-thing holding Wren recoiled, its face blistering, and in that opening Wren did the bravest and most terrible thing Maren had ever seen a child do.

She stopped fighting it.

She let go of Felix's hand. She let go of the scale. She looked at him—looked at all of them—with those feral eyrie eyes, wide and furious and wet, and said, "Take it to Emberfall's fire. Promise me. *Promise*—" and then the shadows behind her opened like a mouth and took her, and the square was empty of everything except candle-smoke, spilled wine, and the sound of Maren Vale screaming a promise into air that no longer held the girl.

* * *

They regrouped—or what passed for regrouping—in the shell of the old shrine, because it was the only place the gold-eyed things' presence seemed to dim, some residue of the old gods' imprisonment clinging to the stones. Maren sat against the altar with her shoulder bound, Felix pressing a cloth to the wound with hands that shook only a little, and Osric knelt in the center of the floor with his forehead nearly touching the flags, saying nothing at all.

"She's alive," Felix said finally. Not gently—there was no gentleness available to any of them—but firmly, the way you state a fact to hold a roof up. "They need the fire, and the fire lives in her now. They won't harm what they need. That's not comfort, Maren. That's tactics. Hold onto the tactics."

"She's twelve years old."

"Yes."

"She let herself be taken. She *chose* it, so we could run, and I ran, I—"

"You held the line so Osric could break their working. We both did our part. Hers was hardest." Felix tied off the bandage. His eyes were dry, which Maren suspected meant the opposite of what it usually meant. "We get her back. That's the whole plan. Everything else is steps."

Osric lifted his head. His face was wrecked, tear-tracked, older by a decade. "I told you," he said hoarsely. "In the road. I told you to remember, whatever you saw down there. Because they'll use me again, Felix. Today proved it—they wore Kestrel, they'll wear me, they'll make you watch me say and do things I'd die before choosing. So I'm saying it now, while it's still my own mouth: I am with you. To the end of this. And I am sorry. I'm sorry I was bait without knowing it. I'm sorry I brought her to that square by existing." His voice broke entirely. "I'm sorry I was too slow."

Felix crossed the floor and pulled the monk up by the shoulders and held him, hard, the way men hold each other when words have failed past repair. Maren looked away to give them privacy and found herself looking at the shrine wall instead—at the old carvings there, gods with wolf heads and crown-hands and burning wings, chained in stone by smaller figures whose wrists bore marks she now recognized.

Ledger-marks. Debt-marks. Scribe's knots.

Her shoulder throbbed in time with her heart. Somewhere north, a king was drowning inside his own body, kept alive by coded messages in state documents, waiting for people brave enough to follow the trail. Somewhere south, a dying dragon's fire was guttering toward nothing. And somewhere in between, in whatever dark place Vhalor kept its stolen things, a twelve-year-old girl was sitting alone with a god's voice in her ears, and the only weapon she carried was a song.

Maren Vale had failed a king once. She had sworn, running from the capital with his seal hot in her fist, that she would not fail twice.

"We move tonight," she said. "North. Through the Godswatch passes—if they wanted us dead they'd have taken all of us, which means they want us *coming*, which means every mile is a trap. Good." She pushed herself upright, shoulder screaming, and picked up her sword. "Let them set the table. This time we bring the fire."

Behind them, unnoticed, the last of the obedient candles in the square guttered out—all except one, which burned steadily on, its flame shaped, if you looked too long, like a small girl standing with her arms crossed, waiting to be found.

Felix saw it. He said nothing.

He picked up the scale, wrapped it in wool, and walked north into the dark.
