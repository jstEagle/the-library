## 17. The Fire-Song

Wren had never been inside a building that swallowed sound before.

The eyrie had been all echo — every word she'd ever spoken bounced off the stone ribs of the mountain and came back to her twice, so that even alone she was never truly alone. Emberfall's cavern had been the same, vast and ringing, a place where a child's voice could fill a cathedral of rock. But the throne room of Aldric's palace ate everything. Her footsteps vanished. Her breathing vanished. The marble drank it all, and what remained was a silence so complete it felt like being buried alive in velvet.

She hated it. She wanted to claw the walls until they bled echo.

*Steady,* said the ember in her chest, small and banked and stubborn. *You are not buried. You are carried.*

Wren pressed her palm flat against the warmth beneath her sternum and kept walking.

They had brought her here in chains, which was stupid of them, because chains were for holding bodies and Wren's body had never been the point of her. The inquisitors who marched her down the long white corridor had been gentle about it — too gentle, apologetic in the way people are gentle with something they've been told is holy or cursed — but gentleness didn't change the arithmetic. Felix had walked into this place wearing a scribe's gray robes and a face like a closed ledger, and Vhalor had taken him. Maren and Osric were somewhere below, bleeding at the gates. And Wren, twelve years old and four feet eleven inches, was the only person left carrying a fire that could unmake a god.

Emberfall had told her once: *The smallest vessels hold the hottest coals. It is why hearths are built low.*

Wren walked toward the throne.

* * *

The thing on the throne was not looking at her when she entered. It was looking at Felix.

He knelt on the marble steps below the dais, his scribe's robe torn at one shoulder, his hands bound in front of him with black cord that smoked faintly where it touched his skin. His head was bowed. His lips were moving — not praying, Wren realized, but counting. He counted when he was frightened. He had done it in the eyrie the first night, lying awake under the dragon's ribs: *one, two, three*, the numbers of things he could see, because numbers stayed where you put them.

Vhalor stood over him.

It wore King Aldric's body the way a hand wears a glove, but the glove was failing at the fingertips. The king's face was handsome still, in a worn coin-sort of way, but the eyes were wrong — deep as wells and just as cold, and around their rims the skin had gone gray as old ash. When Vhalor moved, the movement was smooth in a way no living body moved, gliding rather than walking, and the shadows in the great hall bent toward it slightly, like grass leaning into wind.

Wren knew what it was to be looked through. She had been a vessel since before she could remember; Emberfall had found her in the ashes of the eyrie, a squalling infant left as tribute by people who thought dragons still took offerings. All her life she had been *the keeper's orphan*, *the fire-child*, *the little vessel* — named for what she could carry instead of who she was.

So she recognized the expression on Felix's bowed face. And she recognized, better than anyone in this marble tomb could have, what it meant to be standing where a god wanted you standing.

"Here is the last of them," Vhalor said. Its voice was Aldric's voice, but layered, like a bell rung underwater. "The dragon-keeper's whelp. I smelled the old fire on her from a hundred miles." The well-eyes turned to Wren, and the whole room seemed to tilt toward them. "Come closer, child. Let me see what the last dragon spent herself hiding."

Wren did not come closer. She stopped at the base of the dais, planted her bare feet — they'd taken her boots, the fools, as if boots held magic — and did the thing Emberfall had taught her to do before anything else.

She breathed.

In through the nose, four counts. Hold, seven. Out through the mouth, eight. The rhythm of a creature whose lungs were bellows big enough to warm a valley. *When you are afraid,* Emberfall had rumbled, drowsy and enormous, her chin curled around Wren's whole body like a wall made of love, *breathe the way I breathe. Fear is just fire looking for a chimney.*

"What are you doing?" Vhalor asked. For the first time, something moved behind the well-eyes. Something that might have been uncertainty.

"Singing," Wren said.

And she sang.

* * *

The fire-song was not music, exactly. Or it was music the way a heartbeat is music — a rhythm older than melody, a pattern pressed into the world before there were ears to hear it. Emberfall had taught it to her over three winters, syllable by syllable, in the dark of the dying eyrie, and Wren had thought she was learning a lullaby. A keepsake. A last gift from a mother who was running out of time to give gifts.

She had not understood until now that it was a key.

The first note came out of her small and cracked, because she was crying and hadn't noticed. That was fine. Emberfall had said the song worked best when it was true, and nothing was truer than this: that Wren was terrified, that she missed her dragon with an ache that lived in her teeth, that she would burn this whole marble room to ash if it would buy one more hour curled against Emberfall's warm flank listening to the slow thunder of her heart.

*Ash-of-the-first-morning, breath-before-the-naming—*

The syllables were in no language Wren knew. They weren't meant to be known. They were meant to be *remembered*, and something in the deepest cellar of Wren's blood remembered them perfectly, the way her lungs remembered air.

The temperature of the room changed.

It wasn't heat — not yet. It was more subtle and more terrible than that. Every shadow in the throne room leaned away from Wren, the way they leaned toward Vhalor, and the candles guttered upright, flames standing tall and straight as soldiers called to attention. The black cord smoking around Felix's wrists went dark and dead. On the throne, the god wearing a king went very, very still.

"Stop," Vhalor said.

Wren did not stop.

*Scale-that-was-shield, wing-that-was-roof, heart-that-was-home—*

She was singing to Emberfall. She understood that now, in the way you understand things mid-fall. This song was not a weapon. It was not a spell. It was an *address*. It was a letter written in the oldest grammar of the world, addressed to the last living creature who could read it, and it said: *I am here. I am yours. Come home to me.*

Across the world — across mountains Wren had never seen and valleys whose names she didn't know — something heard her.

She felt it arrive the way you feel a storm change before the clouds do: a pressure shift, a prickle along the arms, a hush in the marrow. The ember in her chest, the little banked coal Emberfall had pressed into her keeping weeks ago in the ruins of the eyrie, opened like an eye.

And then it *answered*.

* * *

Later — much later, in the years when children asked her what it felt like — Wren would say it felt like being remembered. Not remembered by someone, but remembered by fire itself, by the first fire that ever burned in the dark before anything had a name. The flame that answered Emberfall's song did not come into her the way water comes into a cup. It came the way dawn comes into a valley: everywhere at once, inevitable, total.

Light poured up out of her chest.

It was not red. Everyone expected red, orange, the colors of hearth and forge. But the fire that ignited in Wren Oyelaran-nothing-dragonkeeper-everything was white at its heart and gold at its edges and at its outermost rim it burned the color of dawn on snow — a color that has no name because it exists only for the few seconds the world permits it. The light filled the throne room. It filled the corridors. It poured out the high windows and, miles away, sent sentries at the gates stumbling back from the walls, shading their eyes, certain the sun had come to the wrong part of the sky.

The chains fell off her wrists not broken but *unmade*, returned to the iron ore they had always meant to be.

On the dais, Vhalor recoiled. The god-thing's composure tore like wet paper. What showed underneath was vast and starving and suddenly, unmistakably, *afraid* — a darkness so old it predated light, flinching from a twelve-year-old girl wreathed in dawn.

"*No*," Vhalor said, and the king's voice cracked down the middle, and underneath the crack another voice sounded, deeper, wronger, a voice like a landslide in a tomb. "*That fire is DEAD. I unmade the last of them. I drank the eyries dry. There is NOTHING LEFT TO BURN.*"

"You're right," Wren said.

Her voice came out doubled. Herself, and something vaster speaking through her, kindly, without anger. The fire did not need her to shout. Fire is never loud. Fire is simply everywhere it decides to be.

"She's dead," Wren said. "She's been dying since the winter I was nine. She gave me the song because she knew she wouldn't last to see the burning. She gave me the coal because a fire doesn't die, it *changes address*." Tears ran freely down her face now, and where they fell they hissed into steam, and she didn't care. "You didn't kill her, you great hungry dark. You just made her patient."

And far away — past the capital's smoke, past the hills, past the ward-line of a hidden valley where a scriptorium stood quiet under wards held now by consent instead of curse — a dragon older than kingdoms lifted her head for the last time.

* * *

Emberfall died singing.

She was alone, and she was not alone. In her chest, where a furnace had burned for six thousand years, there was almost nothing left — a guttering candle-end, the last coin of a treasury spent buying time for the world. But the song reached her the way spring reaches a frozen river, and she gathered what remained, every scrap, every spark, the embers of her first morning and the ash of her last evening, and she sent it all down the road the song had opened.

She sent it to her daughter.

Not by blood. Blood was a human vanity. By something older and better: by choice, repeated ten thousand times over twelve years of cold nights and warm ones. By the chin curved around a sleeping child. By the lullabies hummed in a language of coals. You do not need to birth a daughter. You only need to decide, again and again, that she is yours — and Emberfall had decided so thoroughly and for so long that the universe itself had given up arguing.

As the last of her flame left her — pouring out across the world in a river of dawn-colored light, singing home to the child who sang to her — Emberfall did what her kind had always done at the end.

She sang back.

Her voice was thin now, thinner than a hatchling's, a thread of sound where there had once been thunder. But the throne room heard it anyway. Wren heard it. Felix, still kneeling on the steps with his heart hammering out a count that had finally, mercifully, stopped mattering, heard it. Even Vhalor heard it, and cringed from it, because there is no sound in any world more terrible to a devouring dark than a dying fire that is not afraid.

*Little coal,* the thread of sound said. *Little spark. Little daughter of my choosing.*

"I'm here," Wren wept, the fire roaring gently through her, around her, *of* her now, woven through her blood the way it had once been woven through a dragon's. "I'm here, I'm here, Mama, I'm here—"

*Carry it warm,* Emberfall sang, and her voice was going, fraying like a banner in wind, and she sang faster, spending her final seconds the way she had spent everything: generously. *Carry it kind. Burn only what deserves the burning, and let the rest grow green in your light. I was tired, little one. Six thousand years is a long watch. You have released me — do you understand? You have not lost me. You have RELEASED me—*

The voice thinned to a whisper.

To a sigh.

To silence.

* * *

In the eyrie, in the high cold ruins where a dragon had kept faith with an empty valley for longer than most nations had existed, the great body settled at last onto its breastbone, wings folding with a sound like a mountain exhaling. The scales dimmed from ember-red to the gray of ordinary stone. The enormous eyes, which had seen the raising of the wards and the crowning of forty kings and a small girl's first wobbly steps, closed with the unhurried peace of a creature who has finished her work and knows it was good.

Where she lay, the stone did not grow cold.

It never would. Travelers would come to that eyrie for a thousand years and find a dragon-shaped hollow in the rock, warm as fresh bread, and they would leave offerings there — not out of fear, but out of gratitude. Flowers, mostly. Children's drawings. Once, memorably, a very good meat pie.

But that was later. Now, in the throne room of a stolen king, a girl stood wrapped in white-gold fire, sobbing and blazing at once, while the last words of the last dragon faded from the air like warmth fading from a bed someone has just left.

And Wren — Wren who had been an orphan, a vessel, a keeper's burden, a thing carried — stood up straight inside the fire that now lived in her chest, and wiped her face with both hands, and looked at the god on the throne.

Vhalor had retreated to the top of the dais. The king's body shook. The well-eyes were wide, and in their depths, for the first time in a year, something else surfaced — something small and drowning and desperately human, a man's consciousness clawing for a moment of daylight.

Aldric's mouth moved. Not Vhalor's voice — the king's own, rusty and cracked and real:

"The girl — tell the girl — the anchor — *the blood-anchor must choose*—"

Then the dark slammed shut over him like water closing, and Vhalor's layered voice hissed out between the king's clenched teeth:

"Burn me, then, little coal. Burn, and take the kingdom's rotting husk with it. I have worn worse deaths than fire."

"No," Wren said quietly. The fire in her chest banked itself, obedient, patient, waiting. It did not need to be spent here, wildly, wastefully. It needed to be spent *precisely*, and there was exactly one thing in this room worth its whole cost. She turned to Felix — still kneeling, still bound in dead cord, still counting under his breath — and held out her hand, and the dawn-colored light ran down her arm and pooled in her small palm like a cupped sunrise.

"It's your turn," she said. "Get up. We're going to finish what she started."

Felix looked at her hand. Then, slowly, he stopped counting.
