## 19. Unbinding

**Maren**

The gates of the palace had been built to keep armies out. No one had ever imagined they would need to be held from the inside.

Maren Vale stood on the broad stone steps beneath the Gate of Kings, her back to the doors, her sword drawn, and watched the plaza fill with torchlight. The inquisitors came first—two hundred of them, maybe more, their white-and-gold livery gone gray with ash and haste, their chains of office swinging like nooses as they advanced. Behind them came the temple guard, and behind them the frightened, furious crowd that Vhalor's priests had whipped into a frenzy with three days of sermons about heresy and dragon-cults and the desecration of holy ground.

"They're not going to stop," Osric said beside her. He had traded his inquisitor's whites for a plain soldier's gambeson, but he still moved like a man who had spent his life giving orders in rooms where obedience was mandatory. His hands were steady. His voice was not quite. "Two hundred against two. They know it. They're coming anyway."

"Then we make it expensive," Maren said.

She had failed this king once. She had stood outside a door in this same palace a year ago, heard the silence behind it, told herself the king was only tired, and walked away. Every mile since—the fugitive years, the wound in her side that never quite closed, the dragon-scale she had carried across half a kingdom like a guilty conscience made solid—all of it had been the long road back to this door. She would not walk away from it again.

Osric glanced at her. In the torchlight his face looked older than it had in Ironhollow's scriptorium, when he had been a soft-spoken brother with ink-stained fingers and a betrayal in his mouth. She understood him better now. He had sold Felix to the temple to buy time for the temple's conscience, and then he had walked out of the valley wearing an inquisitor's chain so he could rot the order out from inside. It had nearly worked. It had also very nearly gotten them all killed at the gate of Vhalor's own making. Trust between them was still a bridge under construction, planks missing, ropes frayed—but tonight they stood on it together, and that was more than either of them had managed a month ago.

"The shrines?" Maren asked.

"Bramblewick lit the first one ten minutes ago." Osric tilted his head toward the sound rising from the undercity—a low, thrumming chorus, half hymn and half drumbeat, rolling up through the palace foundations like water finding cracks in a dam. "The hedge-witch says the old god-stones remember how to burn. The mute one is carrying her between them on his shoulders. By now there should be fires under half the district."

The ground confirmed it. A shudder ran through the flagstones, faint but unmistakable, and somewhere below them something ancient exhaled.

Above, in the throne room, Felix Oyelaran was walking into the mouth of a god.

*Hold the door,* Maren thought, not a prayer, because she had stopped believing in prayers in any temple's language, but a promise, aimed upward through stone. *We'll hold the door.*

The first rank of inquisitors broke into a run.

* * *

**Felix**

The throne room was cold.

Felix had expected fire. He had expected the reek of sulfur, heat like a forge, the theatrical grandeur of every hell the temple's illuminated manuscripts had ever depicted. Instead there was only cold—the clean, patient cold of a crypt—and light that came from everywhere and nowhere, silver-white, sourceless, casting no shadows at all. Without shadows the room looked wrong, flattened, like a drawing of a room rather than a room itself.

And King Aldric sat upon the throne, and did not cast one either.

Felix had seen the king once before, from a distance, at a festival in the outer kingdom—a big man, loud, laughing, famous for drinking with farmers. What sat on the throne now wore Aldric's body the way a glove fits a hand: perfectly, and with nothing inside it. The face was handsome still, composed, almost kind. The eyes were the problem. They were the color of frost over deep water, and they did not blink often enough, and when they fixed on Felix they fixed completely, the way a hawk's do.

"You came," the thing said, in a voice like Aldric's voice played slightly too slow. "I wondered if you would. You are such a careful creature, Felix Oyelaran. So quiet. So small. I have been watching you copy prayers since before you could write your own name."

Felix's disguise—a junior scribe's gray robe, a courier's satchel, a forged seal—was already ruined; Vhalor had known him the moment he crossed the threshold. That was fine. The disguise had never been for the god. It had been for the guards, and the guards were elsewhere, chasing smoke and hymns in the dark beneath the palace. Everything was proceeding exactly as badly as planned, which was to say, exactly as planned.

"I'm here to settle my accounts," Felix said. His voice only shook a little. "You've read my ledger. You know what I owe."

"I know everything you owe." The king's mouth curved. "That is my whole nature, little scribe. I am the collector. I was the collector before your gods had names, and I will be the collector after their bones are dust. Debts are the only honest prayer. Everything else is begging."

"Then let's be honest." Felix walked forward, one step at a time, up the long aisle between pillars that had been carved with the faces of dead kings and defaced by something that had eaten the faces' eyes. "You pulled me out of Ironhollow. You answered the widow's rain-prayer. You let a dead child breathe. You spent miracles like coin to buy my attention, because you needed a blood-anchor and mine is the last blood that holds the lock. So here is my offer." He stopped, ten paces from the throne, and opened his arms. "Take me. Bind yourself to me. My blood will hold you faster than any crown, any kingdom, any worship. You can wear me instead of him."

The frost-colored eyes narrowed—not with suspicion, Felix realized, but with pleasure. That was the worst part. The thing was pleased. It had wanted this. Perhaps it had always wanted this; perhaps every answered prayer in Ironhollow had been a fisherman's lure, and Felix had been swimming toward the hook since the night the ink moved on a widow's petition.

"You understand," Vhalor said, rising from the throne, "that I will accept."

"I counted on it."

"And you understand that I will take everything. Not just your service. Your memories. Your name. Your griefs—I have plans for your griefs, they are excellent building material. I will live in you the way you lived in that valley: invisible, indispensable, owned." The god descended the dais steps, and each footfall made the sourceless light flicker like a candle in wind. "Do you know why your ancestors built the wards, Felix? Not to keep me out. To keep me *in*. This whole kingdom is my prison, and your family has been my jailers, and jailers are never free. You are offering me a bigger cell. Why should I not simply refuse, and keep the king?"

"Because the king is dying," Felix said, "and dead flesh doesn't hold a god. And because Emberfall's fire is coming, and you can feel it—you've felt it since Wren began to sing. You want out of this body before it burns. I'm the door. Doors don't wait."

For three heartbeats, the throne room was silent except for the thrumming hymn rising through the floor, growing louder, closer, the old shrines burning brighter under the city. Then Vhalor smiled with Aldric's handsome mouth, and reached out, and took Felix's wrist the way a man takes a tool.

"Bound, then," the god said. "Let us begin with what you owe."

The world went white.

* * *

It began gently, which Felix would later understand was the cruelest part.

He was nine years old again, standing in the scriptorium, and Brother Anselm was holding up a page he had copied wrong—a single transposed word in a funeral rite—and the whole room of older boys was laughing, and the shame of it went through him like a splinter entering flesh. He remembered wanting to disappear. He remembered succeeding. He remembered learning, year by year, to make himself so small, so precise, so useful, that no one ever looked at him closely enough to laugh again.

*There,* said Vhalor, from everywhere. *There is the first debt. Shame. Do you know what shame weighs, little scribe? I weigh everything.*

The memory tore loose—not faded, *ripped*, the way a bandage comes off hair and skin together—and Felix felt the absence of it like a hole in his chest. And then the next one, and the next. His mother's face, going soft and grainy because he had not thought of it in years, and Vhalor seizing even that, holding it up, turning it, saying *you forgot her, you owed her remembrance and you defaulted.* His first lie. His last kindness. The night he hid Maren in the undercroft and lay awake cataloguing all the ways it would cost him. The moment he betrayed nothing and still felt like a traitor, because invisibility is its own sin, and Vhalor read sins the way Felix read ink—fluently, hungrily, line by line.

Every debt he had ever carried was dragged into the light and weighed and found wanting. The copper he had once stolen from the offering box—he was eleven, he was hungry, he had put most of it back, and none of that mattered. The prayer he had copied without believing. The friend he had not defended. The years he had spent being safe while other people were not. Vhalor recited them all aloud, in Aldric's slow voice, with the relish of a moneylender reading terms, and with each recitation Felix felt something being taken—not the memory itself, not yet, but the *ownership* of it. The god was foreclosing. Room by room, floor by floor, the house of Felix Oyelaran was passing into other hands.

*Fight,* said a voice that was not Vhalor's. It came faintly, through the white noise, through the cold—Wren's voice, distorted by distance and stone, singing the fire-song somewhere far above or below, he could not tell. *Felix. Hold on. Hold on.*

*I'm holding,* he tried to say, and discovered he had no mouth anymore, only the idea of one.

This was the price. He had known it would be. Maren had warned him in the eyrie's ruins; Emberfall had said it plainly through Wren's trembling translation: *the unbinding is paid for in full view, and the payer must remain conscious for all of it.* A god could only be burned out of a vessel it was forced to leave, and it could only be forced to leave if something it valued more than the vessel was offered in exchange—and what Vhalor valued was ownership itself, total, itemized, complete. So Felix had offered himself as collateral, and now the audit had begun, and the auditor was merciless, and the only way through was endurance. Not cleverness. Not courage, exactly. Endurance—the oldest virtue, the least celebrated, the one no ballad was ever written about. Standing still while everything was taken, and still standing.

*Your debts,* Vhalor murmured, almost tenderly, *are so beautifully kept. You wrote them all down yourself, didn't you? All those ledgers. All those confessions disguised as prayers. You made my work so easy.*

Felix had nothing left to answer with. So he did the only thing left. In the whiteness, in the cold, in the place where his name used to be, he began to count.

One. The widow's rain on the roof of the scriptorium, the sound of it, the impossible gift of it.

Two. Maren's hand closing around his wrist the night they crossed the ward-line, dragging him into a world that hurt and was worth it.

Three. Wren's face the first time she laughed at something he said, astonished at herself.

Four. Osric, weeping, saying *I'm sorry* in a way that finally meant it.

Five. Color. Six. Campfires. Seven. The smell of Emberfall's smoke, which smelled like cedar and thunder.

He counted, and Vhalor took, and Felix gave, and gave, and gave—and somewhere in the giving, the god began, very slightly, to slow. Because a debt can only be collected from a debtor who exists, and Felix was spending himself deliberately, converting ownership into offering, and an offering freely given is the one thing a collector cannot repossess. Vhalor noticed. Gods notice everything. But noticing and stopping are different arts, and by the time the frost-eyed thing understood what Felix was doing—understood that it was not plundering a man but accepting a ransom, and that the ransom was consuming itself as it was paid—the fire was already moving through the walls.

* * *

**Maren**

They had held the gate for forty minutes, and it had cost them everything except the gate.

Maren's left arm hung useless, the shield-strap snapped, her shoulder a mess of bruised bone. Blood ran from a cut above her eye into her vision, and she kept wiping it with a sleeve that had no dry cloth left on it anywhere. Beside her, Osric fought like a man who had spent twenty years studying violence precisely so he could recognize the moment it became necessary, economical and terrible, his blade doing in one motion what hers did in three. Between them, on the steps, lay eleven inquisitors who had chosen poorly, and around them a semicircle of living ones who had stopped advancing and started merely watching, uncertain, because the hymns from the undercity had grown loud enough now that the stones themselves seemed to be singing, and men did not easily charge into a building that was audibly on fire from beneath.

"It's working," Osric said. "Can you feel it?"

She could. The old shrines burning below weren't just a distraction—they were a summons. Every god the kingdom had buried was answering, and their answering had weight, and the weight pressed up through the palace floors, and Vhalor's perfect sourceless light was guttering overhead like a lamp in a draft. Somewhere in the last few minutes the frost-eyed composure on the throne room's far side of the building had cracked; the guards had been recalled; the priests had fled; and the crowd in the plaza, which had come to watch heretics burn, was instead watching the palace glow from underneath like a lantern, and beginning, very quietly, in ones and twos, to pray to the old names. Not loudly. Not yet. But aloud.

"He's paying attention to Felix now," Osric said. "All of it. Every scrap of his attention. Which means—" he paused, and Maren saw him swallow whatever he was about to say next, because saying it felt too much like hope and hope was a luxury soldiers learned to distrust. "Which means it might be working."

Maren looked up at the dark windows of the throne room, high above the Gate of Kings, and thought about a boy she had dragged out of a warded valley against his will, a boy who had flinched from loud voices and hidden in margins and apologized for existing. He was up there right now, alone, doing the bravest thing she had ever heard of, and she could not help him, and no one in the history of the world would ever know unless they survived to tell it.

"Hold," she said, to Osric, to herself, to the bleeding night. "Whatever happens up there—we hold."

* * *

**Felix**

The counting had reached ninety-one when the whiteness began to change color.

At first Felix thought it was him failing—his mind going, the way Aldric's mind had gone, burning its own furniture for warmth. But the new color was warm. Amber. Cedar-smoke. It came up through the floor of the white place the way the hymns had come up through the floor of the palace, and where it touched, the cold retreated, and the things Vhalor had taken began to *glow* instead of grieve—his mother's face returning to him bright rather than grainy, the laughing boys in the scriptorium fading to a story he had survived rather than a wound he carried.

*What is happening,* Vhalor demanded, and for the first time the god's voice sounded frightened. *What are you doing. What is this fire. Where is it coming from. Answer me.*

"You know where it's coming from," Felix said—or thought, or was, or whatever remained of saying when a person was mostly light. "You've felt it since the eyrie. You just didn't believe a dying dragon and a feral child could matter. You priced everything, collector. You just never learned what things cost."

The amber rose. It found the holes Vhalor had torn in him and filled them—not erased them, filled them, the way floodwater fills a channel, and Felix understood suddenly that this had been the design all along, Emberfall's design, the bargain he had accepted in the ruins: the god would take and take until there was nothing left to take but the space itself, and then the fire would pour into the space, and a vessel full of fire has no room left for anything else. He was not enduring the collection. He was *emptying on purpose*, and every loss was a room prepared.

Ninety-two. Ninety-three. The numbers were getting harder to hold. His edges were dissolving. He could feel himself becoming less a person than a point of view, a witness watching its own unmaking, and there was a terror in that which no ledger had ever recorded, because it was not a debt—it was the thing that incurred debts, going out like a tide.

But the tide was going out *toward* something. Far off in the amber, closer every moment, he could hear singing—Wren's fire-song, unmistakable now, no longer distant, and beneath it a vast slow heartbeat that could only be Emberfall's last flame crossing the world to reach the child who had carried its song. And beyond both, dim and drowned and fighting, another presence: a man's mind, ragged, nearly spent, pressing outward against the thing that wore him. King Aldric, surfacing one last time, spending the final coins of himself.

*Now,* said the drowning king, in a voice that was barely a voice, a message coded in pure desperation after a year of coding messages in ink. *Scribe. Whatever you are going to do. Now.*

Felix opened what remained of his hands.

The amber came through them like sunrise through a window, and the white place shattered—

—and the last thing he saw clearly, before everything blurred into fire and blood and the smell of cedar and the sound of a child singing in a throne room full of falling frost, was Vhalor's face. Frost-eyed no longer. The eyes had gone the color of embers about to collapse, orange-black, desperate, *small*, a god discovering at the end of its long accounting that some debts cannot be collected because the debtor has already given everything away, and what you cannot collect, you cannot own, and what you cannot own—

The fire reached him.

The throne room screamed.

* * *

Outside the Gate of Kings, Maren felt it pass through her like a bell struck inside her ribs—the whole palace ringing, every stone, every shrine below, every buried god answering at once—and the light in the high windows turned from silver to gold to a red so deep it was nearly black, and the crowd in the plaza dropped to its knees, not from command but from awe, and Osric grabbed her broken arm and shouted her name and she could not hear him over the sound, which was not a scream after all.

Up close, from inside, it might have been a scream.

From the steps of the Gate of Kings, in the smoke and the singing and the falling ash, it sounded almost exactly like a door unlocking.

And through the great doors, down the aisle, across the scorched and shadowless floor, a figure was walking—small, gray-robed, haloed in ember-light, carrying something in cupped hands that glowed like a coal wrapped in skin. Behind him, rising slowly from the throne, blinking, weeping, hollowed out and human and utterly undone, came a big man with a king's face and a stranger's bewildered grace, whispering a name over and over as though tasting it for the first time in a year:

*Aldric. My name is Aldric.*

The figure in gray kept walking. Toward the doors. Toward Maren. Toward the child's song still hanging in the air like heat lightning.

Toward the last flame—and the choice it would demand.
