## 20. The Last Flame

The throne room of Aldric's fathers had been built to make a man feel small.

Felix had copied that truth once, in his old life, in a prayer-book margin he was never supposed to annotate: *the ceiling is heaven and the floor is dust, and between them the king is a thread.* He had written it as an observation about architecture. Standing here now, at the center of a circle of blackened god-shrine light, with Vhalor's attention fixed on him like a needle through cloth, he understood it as prophecy. The thread was him. The thread was always going to be him.

He knelt on the flagstones where the ritual demanded, palms open, wrists bare. His scribe's disguise lay discarded behind him—a gray robe folded neatly, because even now, even here, some habits refused to die. Order mattered to him. Order had been the only thing the valley ever gave him for free.

Vhalor wore the king like a glove worn so long its shape had become invisible. The thing beneath the crown did not look monstrous. That was the worst of it. It looked *regal*. It looked like what a king should be—broad-shouldered, calm-eyed, radiating a stillness that Felix recognized now as hunger wearing patience. When it spoke, it used Aldric's voice, and the voice was beautiful, and every word of beauty in it was stolen.

"You came," Vhalor said, and there was something almost tender in it, the way a fisherman might admire the fish. "A year I scattered prayers into your little valley like seed. A year I waited for one of you to *notice*." Its gaze slid over Felix's kneeling body, taking inventory. "The others prayed. You *read*. Of course it would be you."

Felix had prepared for this moment the only way a scribe knows how: by memorizing everything. Every line of the unbinding rite he'd reconstructed from Aldric's coded messages, every phrase of counter-binding Emberfall's fire-song implied, every word Wren had sung into his ear three nights ago until the melody lived behind his teeth like a second heartbeat. He could not fight this thing. He could not out-pray it. But he could *copy* it—he could take what Vhalor intended and write it differently, in blood instead of ink, in choice instead of curse.

That was the whole gambit. It fit inside a single sentence, and he had carried it across a kingdom like a coal cupped in both hands:

*A binding willingly accepted cannot be used against the bound.*

Emberfall had told him that, or Wren had told him what Emberfall told her, in the dragon's last lucid hour: *the old ones chained your family to the wards without asking. A chain and a vow are made of the same iron. Only one of them can be melted down and reforged.*

"Kneel there," Vhalor said, circling him slowly, its shadow pooling and stretching wrong, reaching toward corners no shadow should find. "You know what you are. The last anchor. Your grandmothers' grandmothers poured their blood into the valley's stones and called it duty, and their children called it debt, and you—" the voice softened, cruel in its gentleness, "—you called it nothing at all. Invisible. Unremarked. I chose you *because* of that, little scribe. An anchor no one sees holds best."

"I know," Felix said. His voice did not shake. He had spent thirty years practicing not shaking. "I've read your letters. All of them. The answered prayers were yours. The widow's rain. The child who breathed again." He lifted his chin. "You wrote to me in miracles because you thought I couldn't be reached any other way."

"And were you?"

"No." Felix let the lie sit there for exactly as long as it needed to—the length of one breath, the length of a scribe's hesitation before committing ink—and then he took it back, because the ritual required honesty the way a lock required a key. "Yes. I was reached. I'm here. Do what you came to do."

Vhalor smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile did not reach eyes that held no king at all.

It began.

There was no thunder. There was no light. What there was, was *attention*—a pressure like the whole sky leaning on one point of Felix's skull, and then the tearing began, gentle at first, the way a page turns. Vhalor entered him the way water enters paper. Not violently. Inevitably. Felix felt his memories laid flat and read like documents: the scriptorium's cold mornings, the taste of cheap ink, Brother Osric's laugh before the betrayal-that-wasn't, Maren's bloodied hand pulling him through the ward-line, Wren's fierce thin arms around the scale. Every sin. Every debt. Every small cowardice he had ever committed in the name of staying safe—the prayers he'd copied without faith, the widow he hadn't warned, the years he'd spent being *careful* when careful was just another word for complicit.

Vhalor read them all aloud, inside his own head, in his own voice.

*You owed the Temple four hundred crowns and paid them in invisibility. You owed your mother a grave-side visit and never went. You owed the dead child of Millbrook a warning and gave her silence. You owe, you owe, you owe—*

"Yes," Felix said, and wept, and did not stop the ritual, because the ritual required him to mean it. "All true. Enter the ledger. I keep excellent accounts."

And somewhere in the tearing, in the awful intimacy of being *known*, Felix found the thing he had come to find: the seam. Every binding has a seam. The wards of Ironhollow had one—his ancestors' blood, poured unwilling, had left a scar in the working, a place where the iron remembered it had once been free metal. Vhalor's hold on him was built the same way, from the same old craft, the same hungry gods' arithmetic: *what is taken can be kept.*

But what is *given*—

Wren's voice cut through the throne room like a blade through wet silk.

She stood in the shattered doorway, small and terrible, lit from within. Not metaphorically. Lit. From. Within. The fire-song had done what Emberfall promised it would do from across the dying miles of the world: the last flame of the last dragon had heard the song its keeper's orphan sang, and it had crossed the dark between them, and it had answered, and it lived now in the chest of a twelve-year-old girl who was crying and singing at the same time and refusing to do anything so reasonable as choose between them.

"*An-sael voru, an-sael keth,*" she sang, and the old words meant *I carry you, I am carried,* and the throne room's black shrine-light flinched from her like a beast from a brand.

Vhalor turned from Felix—turned its attention, that needle of sky-weight—and Felix felt the pressure lift just enough, and he knew, with a scribe's cold clarity, that this was the moment the plan would live or die. Because Vhalor had a choice now, too: finish consuming the anchor, or turn and crush the flame. It could not do both quickly. Hunger always believes it has time.

It turned toward Wren.

Which was the mistake. Which was the *only* move, and Felix had counted on it the way you count on a river running downhill. Because Wren was not alone. Wren had never been alone since the night she'd walked into their campfire circle holding a dragon-scale and a grudge, and the people around her—Maren Vale with her broken-and-mended sword, Osric with his inquisitor's seals turned inside out, the hedge-witch Sable, the mute giant Brannoc whose silence was itself a kind of prayer—they had all put themselves between the girl and the door, and they did it now, wordlessly, the way family does.

Maren's voice rang off the vaulting: "*For the king we failed!*" And Osric's answer, ragged but steady: "*For the brother I sold!*"—and the gates held, and the undercity's old shrines blazed distraction through the palace foundations, and none of it was enough to win, and all of it was enough to buy.

To buy *this*: the three heartbeats during which Vhalor's attention was split, and Felix, kneeling in the wreck of his own opened self, spoke the words he had rewritten.

Not the binding as it was given to his bloodline—not the chain. The vow.

*"I take this fire into my blood of my own will. I set it in the place my ancestors bled. What was taken from them, I give back to myself. Let the lock become a key. Let the key become a door. Let the door open only from within."*

He said it in the old tongue, which he had taught himself from ward-stone rubbings in the scriptorium's forbidden drawer, because of course he had; of course the invisible man had spent his invisible years learning the language of his own cage. The words came out of him like blood comes out, warm and unstoppable, and the air above the flagstones changed texture, thickened, began to *wait*.

Because a vessel had been declared. The ritual demanded one. Vhalor had planned to pour itself deeper into Felix and wear him as it wore the king. But a declaration of willing binding outranked a declaration of theft—it was the first law of the old craft, older than Vhalor's imprisonment, older than the pantheon that had done the imprisoning—and the working paused, suspended, listening, like ink on a page waiting to see which way the pen will move.

Then Wren stopped singing.

She didn't stop because she was defeated. She stopped because the song had reached its final verse, the verse Emberfall had saved for last, the verse that was less a lyric than an instruction: *when the fire finds its bearer, let the bearer speak its name.*

Wren looked at Felix across the burning throne room, and her eyes were full of dragon-light, and she said, hoarse and furious and absolutely certain: "*Now. Take it now, or I will, and I don't know what it'll make me, and I don't care—take it NOW—*"

Felix opened his hands wider.

* * *

What happened next happened faster than thought and slower than forever, which is the only way to describe the moments in which a life changes its fundamental nature.

The flame came to him. Not as fire comes to a house—leaping, devouring, indifferent. As fire comes to a wick. As warmth comes to a hand held out in winter. It crossed the space between Wren's chest and Felix's open palms in a ribbon of white-gold light, and it passed *through* the place where Vhalor still clung to him like smoke clinging to cloth, and where it passed, the smoke burned.

Felix felt the god tear.

He felt it the way you feel a thorn come out of flesh you'd forgotten was wounded—sudden, precise, agonizing, clean. Vhalor had woven itself through him through the king through the year of hollowing through the century of imprisonment through the thousand years of the valley's wards, one continuous thread of theft, and the dragon-fire ran down that thread like a spark down a fuse, and everywhere it touched, it *unmade*. Not destroyed—unmade. Returned. The stolen years poured backward out of Aldric's body. The stolen worship drained out of the black shrines. The stolen peace of Ironhollow, the stolen blood of Felix's ancestors, the stolen childhood of a boy who had been told his whole life that he owed a debt no one would itemize—all of it caught in the flame and rendered down to its true value, which was *nothing*, which had always been nothing, because you cannot owe what was taken from you.

Vhalor screamed.

It screamed with a thousand voices—the voices of every prayer it had ever answered falsely, every miracle it had ever minted as bait, every soul it had ever hollowed—and the scream shook ash from the rafters of the palace of Aldric's fathers, and then the scream *thinned*, and then the scream was only one voice, and then the voice was only a shape, and then the shape was only a hunger with nothing left to eat, and then—

Then it was gone. Burned out of the world the way fever burns out of a body: leaving weakness, leaving sweat, leaving the astonished quiet of survival.

King Aldric fell forward onto the flagstones.

Felix caught him. He did not decide to catch him; his arms simply moved, the way they moved to steady a page in wind. And the man in his arms was lighter than a king should be—lighter by the weight of a god, lighter by the weight of a year, lighter by the weight of everything Vhalor had added to him to make him seem more than human—and he was *moving*, twitching, breathing in ragged gulps like a diver surfacing, and his eyes opened, and they were gray, and they were ordinary, and they were the most beautiful eyes Felix had ever seen, because they were only eyes. No hunger behind them. No patience. No god.

"I—" Aldric said, and stopped, and tried again, and his voice was rusted, ruined, real. "I wrote to you. In the ledgers. Did anyone—did anyone ever read—"

"I read them," Felix said. "Every one. I came as fast as ink allows."

The king's face crumpled. Not in defeat—in *relief*, the pure undiluted relief of a man who has been drowning for a year in his own body and has just, finally, felt air. He wept. He wept the way the freed weep, messily, gracelessly, without dignity, which is the only honest way to weep, and Felix held him while he did it, a scribe cradling a king, and neither of them was anything in that moment but two men who had survived the same cage from opposite sides of its bars.

"I burned my own memories," Aldric whispered, when the tears slowed enough for words. "To hide you. To hide the anchor. I thought—if I forgot who needed protecting, maybe it couldn't find—" His breath caught. "Was it worth it? Am I—am I still *me*? Is anything left?"

Felix looked at him honestly, because the moment demanded honesty and he had spent a lifetime withholding it and today, of all days, the account had come due.

"Some of you burned," he said. "Some of you survived. You'll have to meet what's left, and I think—I think you'll find you like him. He's mortal. He's flawed. He cries easily." He smiled, faintly, through the fire settling into his own veins. "In my experience, that's the mark of someone worth following."

* * *

Afterward—after the ash settled, after Maren and Osric came through the doors with soot on their faces and tears they pretended weren't there, after Sable bound Wren's singed fingertips and Brannoc finally, *finally*, spoke, one word, "*home*," in a voice like a bell cracking—after all of that, there remained the matter of the fire.

It was still in Felix. It had not left with Vhalor. The flame, having burned out the thief, had kept its appointment with the vessel, and the vessel was him, and it sat in his blood now the way a coal sits in a banked hearth: patient, warm, alive. He could feel it when he pressed a hand to his sternum. A second pulse. Slower than his own. Older.

Wren stood in front of him, small and ash-streaked, staring at his chest as if she could see through skin to the light she'd carried and given away.

"It's not—" she started, and faltered, and her voice cracked down the middle the way it had cracked all through the fire-song, grief and triumph fighting for the same breath. "Emberfall's *dead*. She died singing. She died *while I was singing*, Felix, she heard the whole song and then she was just—cold, she was just a mountain that used to be my mother, and the fire came here, and I thought—I thought if I gave it to you then she'd really be gone, there'd be nothing, no ember, no *anything*—" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furiously, like a child refusing to cry and crying anyway. "Is she still in it? Is she in *you*?"

Felix considered lying to her. He had a lifetime's skill at it. Then he considered the last year—Maren's honesty, Osric's costly truth, the whole long road of learning that secrets are just debts with better handwriting—and he told her the truth instead.

"When it's quiet," he said, pressing his palm flat over the second pulse, "I hear something. Under the fire. Like a heartbeat inside the heartbeat. Like someone humming." He met her eyes. "I think she's in it. I think she's in it the way a song is in the singer. Not *alive*. But not gone, either. Carried."

Wren put her forehead against his chest, right over the flame, and stayed there a long time, and Felix held very still, because you do not disturb a child listening to the echo of her mother.

When she finally stepped back, she was different. Not healed—nothing was healed; healing was a road, not a door—but *settled*, the way ash settles after burning, and ash, Felix knew better than anyone, ash is what remains when something has been fully, honestly spent.

Above them, in the smoking silence of the throne room, King Aldric got to his feet. Slowly. Badly. Holding Maren's arm, gripping it the way drowning men grip driftwood, and when he stood fully upright for the first time in a year as *himself*, he swayed, and laughed—a broken, startled, human laugh—and said to the empty room, to the ashes, to the four people and one giant and one witch and one knight and one defrocked inquisitor and one scribe-on-fire who had torn his kingdom back for him:

"Well." He wiped his eyes. "I appear to owe everyone everything."

Maren, standing beside him with her sword finally, truly sheathed, said quietly: "We'll discuss terms."

And Felix felt the fire turn over in his blood like a sleeper turning in bed, and thought: *yes. Terms. Accounts. Ledgers.* He was, after all, still a scribe. Some things survived even dragon-fire.

Outside the palace windows, dawn was coming up over a capital that had worshipped a lie for a year and did not yet know it was free—and the first thing the new day would ask of them, Felix already knew, was not celebration.

It was reckoning.
