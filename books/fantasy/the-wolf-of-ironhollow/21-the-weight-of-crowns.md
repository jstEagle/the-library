## 21. The Weight of Crowns

The bells did not ring.

Maren had expected them to—had imagined, in the fevered hours after the burning, that the whole city would erupt in celebration, that the people would pour into the streets singing Aldric's name, that there would be feasts and fireworks and the great bronze bells of the Citadel thundering out a new age. That was how it happened in the old romances she'd read as a girl, curled by her father's forge-fire: the dragon slain, the king restored, the kingdom saved, and everyone living happily until the last page.

But when dawn came on the second morning, the bells hung silent in their towers, and the streets were quiet in the wrong way. Not the hush of joy. The hush of a city holding its breath.

Maren stood at a window in the east wing of the palace—a room they'd given her without asking whether she wanted one, because apparently a knight who helped burn a god out of a king was owed quarters now, even if that same knight had been a fugitive with a bounty on her head for three years—and watched the capital wake below her.

There were people in the streets. That much was true. But they moved strangely, in small knots and clusters, stopping at corners to speak in low voices, looking up at the palace with expressions Maren couldn't name from this height. Grief, maybe. Or the particular blankness of people whose world has been rearranged overnight and who have not yet found where to put their hands.

*A year,* she thought. *A year of worship.*

That was the thing no one had said plainly yet, though everyone was thinking it. The kingdom had not been conquered. It had been *devoted*. Every festival held in Vhalor's honor—the Hollow King's festivals, they'd called them, though no one had known what hollow meant then. Every tithe redirected to the new temples springing up like mold across the city. Every prayer whispered to the god beneath the crown by farmers hoping for rain and mothers hoping for sons returned from war. A whole nation had knelt, willingly, gladly, to the thing that had eaten its king alive.

And now the king was back, and the kneeling was over, and nobody knew what to do with their knees.

A knock at the door. Soft, hesitant—the knock of someone who wasn't sure of their welcome.

"Come in," Maren said, and turned.

Osric stood in the doorway, still in his inquisitor's grays, though he'd torn off the insignia sometime in the last two days; the shoulder of his coat bore only a pale rectangle where the silver flame had been stitched. He looked older than he had in Ironhollow. Or perhaps Maren had simply never looked at him properly before, back when he was only Felix's childhood friend and therefore, in her private accounting, suspect.

"The king asks for you," Osric said. "If you're willing."

"Is he eating?"

Osric blinked. "I—that's the second time you've asked."

"Because he's spent a year being worn like a coat, and the first thing a body forgets, when it's not its own anymore, is hunger." Maren took her sword belt from the chair—it felt strange to buckle it on now, strange and necessary both—and followed him into the corridor. "So. Is he?"

"A little. Felix sits with him at meals. Doesn't talk much. Just... sits." Osric's mouth twitched toward something that wasn't quite a smile. "Wren tried to bring him a roasted pigeon she'd caught on the roof. Alive. He thanked her very seriously and asked if it might be cooked first."

Despite everything, something in Maren's chest loosened. "And what did Wren say?"

"She said dragons eat theirs raw, so he should be grateful for the consideration."

*The child is learning to be a person,* Maren thought. *Slowly. Like ice melting.* Two days ago Wren had screamed herself hoarse over Emberfall's death—Maren had heard it from three streets away, that sound, grief with wings on it—and since then the girl had gone quiet as ash, orbiting Felix like a moth that had lost its flame and chosen the next nearest light. She was sleeping in the corridor outside his door. She was eating, when reminded. She had not yet cried again, and Maren, who had buried enough people to know the shape of things, understood that the second grief was coming—the real one, the one that arrived once survival stopped being urgent—and dreaded it the way you dread weather you cannot stop.

They walked through corridors that still bore the marks of the unbinding. Servants had scrubbed the scorch-marks from the throne room floor, but here in the private wing the damage remained, raw and honest: a doorframe blackened where Vhalor's rage had poured through it, a tapestry reduced to its warp threads, a stretch of wall where the plaster had simply *sublimated*, leaving bare stone shaped like a handprint the size of a shield.

"The masons want to start repairs," Osric said, following her gaze. "The king told them to wait."

"Why?"

"He said—" Osric paused, choosing words carefully, the way he'd learned to do in the temple's halls. "He said the scars should stay visible long enough for him to understand them. Then they could go."

*An honest king,* Maren thought, and the thought sat strangely in her chest, half hope and half dread. Honesty in a ruler was like a naked blade in a market crowd—admirable, dangerous, and likely to cut someone before the day was out.

***

Aldric received her in a small room, not a grand one—a library, or what had been a library before Vhalor's year. Whole shelves stood empty, their books burned in the purges when the god had ordered every text of the old pantheon fed to the flames. What remained was mostly legal codes and tax ledgers, and the king had clearly been reading them, because they lay open across the table in drifts, annotated in a tired but steady hand.

He looked *diminished*. There was no kinder word for it. The man Maren had sworn to six years ago had been broad and loud, a king who filled doorways and laughed with his mouth open and slapped shoulders hard enough to bruise. The man sitting at the table was thinner—not just in body but in some essential way, as if Vhalor had taken more than flesh during the wearing. His hair had gone gray at the temples. His hands, wrapped around a cup of broth he seemed to be drinking mostly out of duty, trembled faintly.

When he saw her, he set down the cup and rose—rose fully, formally, and bowed his head.

"Ser Maren Vale," he said. "Knight of the Realm. Which I am told you ceased to be, by my own order, three years ago."

"You don't have to—"

"I do." His voice was quiet but it stopped her like a hand on the chest. This was the king's voice, she realized—the real one, un-worn, and it carried the same weight it always had, just held differently now, the way a man carries water after a drought: carefully, knowing what it costs. "I remember everything, Ser Maren. That is my curse and my penance both. I remember the tribunal where I condemned you. I remember *watching myself* condemn you—I remember standing behind my own eyes while that thing used my voice to call you traitor, and oath-breaker, and worse, and I remember the part of me that screamed, and the part of me that knew no one could hear it." His jaw tightened. "You were right about the queen's death. You were right about the border garrisons. You were right about everything, and I let them strip your title and hunt you across three kingdoms rather than admit my own court had gone rotten around me. So yes. I have to say this, and you will stand there and listen to it, because you earned the listening."

Silence. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobblestones.

"Majesty," Maren managed finally, "you were not yourself."

"I was precisely myself. That's the horror of it. Vhalor didn't make me anything I wasn't already—I was proud, and slow to trust, and too fond of my own certainty. It merely removed the parts of me that hesitated." He came around the table, and up close she could see the fine tremor in him, the effort it cost him simply to remain upright and present in his own skin. "I am offering you restoration. Full honors. Your title, your lands, your place at court—everything that was taken, returned with interest, and publicly, before the assembled nobles, so that every soul who jeered at your exile must now bow to you. Say the word and it will be done by week's end."

It was everything she had dreamed of, once. In the worst nights of her flight—in ditches and stolen barns and the borderlands where bounty hunters called her name like a curse—she had rehearsed exactly this scene. The restoration. The apology. The nobles' faces. She had built the fantasy so carefully, stone by stone, that it had become load-bearing: the single wall she leaned against when everything else fell.

And now it was offered, freely, sincerely, by a king who meant every word—

—and it weighed nothing at all.

"No," Maren said.

Aldric blinked. Beside the door, Osric went very still.

"Not like that." She made herself hold the king's eyes. "Your Majesty, I spent three years wanting my honor back. And then I met a scribe who'd spent his whole life paying a debt that was designed never to end, and a child raised by a dying dragon, and a hedge-witch who sleeps in hedgerows because no village will have her, and a giant who hasn't spoken since soldiers killed his family, and an inquisitor who tore off his own insignia." Her voice roughened. "Do you know what I learned? Honor isn't something they can give you. It's a coin minted by other people, and it spends according to *their* ledger, not yours. If you restore me tomorrow, I'm honorable because the crown says so. And if the crown says otherwise next year—as crowns do—then I'm nothing again. I've been nothing by decree before, Majesty. I won't be anything by decree again."

"Then what will you be?" Aldric asked, and there was no offense in it—only genuine curiosity, the question of a man relearning how to ask.

"Purpose." The word came out of her before she'd fully chosen it, and once spoken, it was obviously, completely true. "Give me work, not rank. There are thousands of people in this city who prayed to Vhalor believing they prayed to you—or to the gods, or to anyone. Farmers who tithed their harvests. Mothers who named children after it. Whole villages that built shrines with their own hands. They're going to wake up every morning for years knowing they knelt to a devouring god, and shame makes monsters of ordinary folk faster than cruelty ever does. Someone has to go to them—not with inquisitors, not with fire, but with the truth: *you were deceived, not damned.*" She swallowed. "Someone has to tell them the king remembers what it was like to kneel."

Aldric stared at her for a long moment. Then he laughed—a short, cracked, startled laugh, the first she'd heard from him that belonged entirely to him.

"A knight who refuses a dukedom to become a traveling confessor." He shook his head slowly. "Very well, Ser Vale. Purpose it is. I'll have letters drawn up—no, wait." He glanced at Osric, and something wry crossed his ruined face. "Is the scribe available? I find I trust his handwriting more than any chancellor's."

"He's with Wren," Osric said. "But I'll copy it fair, Majesty, if you'll dictate. It seems I'm still good for ink-work after all."

"And I'll ride with the letters," Maren said. "Every province. Every shrine. Starting with the ones closest to Ironhollow."

At the valley's name, all three of them went quiet for a moment. The wards had held—Felix's fire-bound blood kept them alive—but the valley beyond them had changed, everyone knew, in ways that wouldn't be visible for a generation. A place built to be a prison, learning to be a home.

"Then we understand each other," Aldric said softly. "Go and tell my kingdom it was lied to. Gods know I can't—I can barely walk the length of this room." He looked down at his trembling hands, and for a moment the mask of kingship slipped entirely, and Maren saw underneath: a man exhausted past the edge of endurance, holding himself together out of sheer stubbornness and the memory of who he used to be. "Tell them the king sends his shame and his thanks in equal measure. They'll believe you. I'm not sure they'd believe me."

"They will," Maren said. "In time. That's what purpose is for."

***

She found the others at sunset, on the palace's south terrace, where the ruins of the old god-shrines were visible in the city below—Osric's allies had ignited them during the battle, and though the fires were long out, the shrines themselves stood gutted, their domes cracked open to the sky like broken eggs.

Felix sat on the terrace steps with Wren asleep against his shoulder, her hand fisted in his sleeve even in sleep, as if letting go might cost her the only solid thing left in the world. His eyes were closed, but Maren knew he wasn't sleeping. Nobody was really sleeping yet. The fire in his blood had settled into something quieter these two days—a banked hearth rather than a blaze—but she'd seen him flinch twice when a servant dropped a tray, seen him press his palm flat against his sternum as though listening for something inside.

Hedge-witch Sera sat cross-legged nearby, grinding herbs that smelled of valerian and smoke. Bromm the mute giant lay along the terrace wall like a felled tree, watching the shattered shrines with an expression Maren had learned to read as contentment. And Osric came out last, carrying a tray of food that nobody had asked for, and set it down among them with the air of a man performing a small ritual of normalcy.

For a while, nobody spoke. The sun went down over a wounded capital, and the first stars came out above the broken domes.

"Wren asked me today," Felix said at last, quietly, so as not to wake her, "what happens to a god's prayers. When the god dies." His hand rested absently on the girl's hair. "All those people praying to Vhalor for a year. Where did it all go? Did it just—vanish?"

"What did you tell her?" Sera asked.

"That prayers are like seeds. Most of them land nowhere. Some of them land somewhere terrible. But a few—" he looked out at the darkening city, at the windows beginning to glow, one by one, as people lit candles against the night— "a few land somewhere that answers back. And you can't control which. All you can do is keep praying anyway, to something worth the risk."

Bromm made a low sound—not speech, but agreement, in the register only his found family had learned to hear.

Maren sat down on the steps beside Felix, sword clinking softly against stone. Below them the city breathed, uncertain and alive. Above them the stars turned, indifferent and ancient, the same stars that had watched the old gods rise and fall and imprison one of their own beneath a valley, and had watched a copying-scribe carry fire in his veins and choose, freely, what his ancestors had been forced to bear.

"So," Felix said, leaning his head briefly against her shoulder—the gesture of a brother, or something closer than rank allowed words for. "Terms."

"Terms," Maren agreed. "The king rides north in a month to see the valley for himself. I ride ahead with the letters. Osric stays to run the scriptorium. Sera and Bromm—" she glanced at them, and Sera smiled, and Bromm nodded once— "go wherever we go, apparently, because none of us remembers how to be alone anymore."

"And Wren?"

Wren stirred against Felix's shoulder but did not wake. In her sleep her lips moved faintly, shaping notes without sound—the fire-song, still practicing itself in her dreams, Emberfall's inheritance humming in the smallest member of their company.

Felix looked down at her, and then out at the city, and then at the distant dark line of hills to the north where a hidden valley waited, its wards glowing faintly now—everyone swore they could see it on clear nights—with a warmth that hadn't been there before. Firelight, seen from a hundred miles away.

"She comes with us," Felix said simply. "Wherever the fire leads."

Maren nodded, and reached for the bread Osric had brought, and broke it, and handed pieces around the circle—first to Felix, then to sleeping Wren's free hand, which closed around it reflexively, the way children hold things they intend to keep.

Below them, in the cracked-open shrine at the foot of the hill, a candle flickered alight—unlit by any human hand—one small flame standing upright in the ruin, steady as a vow, burning gold instead of Vhalor's hungry red.

Sera saw it first. She said nothing. She only touched Bromm's arm, and pointed, and the whole company went still, watching the little fire burn in the broken house of a dead god.

"Well," Maren said softly, into the strange quiet after war. "That's either an omen or an invitation."

Felix's hand drifted to his chest, where the fire slept. When he spoke, his voice was thoughtful, and very slightly afraid.

"It's a *question*. And I think it's waiting for our answer."
