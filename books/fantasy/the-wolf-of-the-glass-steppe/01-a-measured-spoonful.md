## 1. A Measured Spoonful

The hemlock was in the honey.

I knew it before I touched the spoon, the way a musician knows a wrong note before the orchestra finishes the phrase. The glaze on the king's quail should have smelled of thyme and burnt sugar and the particular smugness of a head chef who has been complimented twice this month. Instead there was a green note underneath, faint as a lie told well—parsley, said the smell, but parsley does not sit at the back of the throat like that. Parsley does not promise numbness.

Hemlock, then. My fourteenth.

I stood at the tasting table in the corner of the palace kitchens with the silver spoon in my hand and let the moment stretch, because there is a discipline to this work that no one appreciates. Anyone can swallow poison. Children do it by accident every year. The craft is in the waiting—in knowing exactly how much to take, and when, and what to watch for afterward, and how to write it all down later in a hand so small and dull that no one reading it would ever guess what the words cost.

One measured spoonful of the honeyed quail. Not a bite more. The dose matters; the dose is everything. A full serving might kill me before I could name the poison, and a crumb too small might hide its nature entirely, and either failure would be a waste of a perfectly good evening.

I swallowed.

The kitchen went on around me the way it always did, which is to say: loudly, badly, and without any awareness of me whatsoever. Forty cooks and scullions and spit-boys under one vaulted ceiling, and not one of them looked at the woman by the tasting table. That is the first thing you must understand about being a royal food-taster in Vhaloria: we are not people. We are instruments. A knife is not thanked for cutting. A scale is not pitied for bearing weight. And a taster is not looked at, because looking at a taster means remembering what she is for, and remembering what she is for means imagining the king dead, and imagining the king dead in the palace kitchens is a conversation with the floor scrubbers and then, if you are unlucky, with the headsman.

Invisibility is not something that happens to you. It is a skill, like immunity. You build it a grain at a time, over years, until it holds your weight.

I set down the spoon and began the count.

* * *

The first thing hemlock does—and this one was good hemlock, old-fashioned, root-cut, none of your modern distillations—is nothing. That is its genius and its horror. Arsenic announces itself. Belladonna paints your world pretty within the hour. But hemlock is patient. Hemlock lets you finish your sentence.

So while it did its nothing, I did mine.

I walked the length of the kitchens the way I do every evening, unhurried, a gray woman in a gray apron, checking the things a taster checks. The salt cellar had been locked since morning; the seal unbroken. The wine had been drawn from the cask in front of the steward, who watched it the way a miser watches coin. The water came from the deep spring, which no one poisons anymore because the last three people who tried were caught by the spring itself—or so the story goes—and were pulled down into the dark, which everyone knows is impossible, because the valley of Vhaloria's capital is the most magic-dead place in the known world.

That word again. *Dead.* They say it comfortably here, the way you'd say a room is drafty. Magic cannot follow you into the valley. Magic curdles at the boundary stones and slides off the mountains like rain off glass. It is why the capital is where it is, why the kings built here after the Unmaking, why the whole country huddles inside this one quiet pocket of the world like a tongue in a closed mouth. Nothing answers prayers here. Nothing whispers in wells. Nothing moves unless it is pushed.

I have lived inside that stillness for twenty years, since I was seven years old, and I will tell you honestly: I do not know if I find it restful or suffocating. Both, probably. Both at once, the way a well-worn coat is both.

The second stage of hemlock arrives in the legs.

It came on schedule, somewhere between the bread station and the roasting spits—a softness in my calves, a faint sweet heaviness, as though my ankles had been packed with warm sand. I leaned against the flour shelf and catalogued it. *Onset: four minutes. Ascending, symmetrical.* A cook brushed past me with a tray of custards and said "mind yourself," not out of kindness but because I was in her path, and even her annoyance was a kind of comfort. Annoyance means you've been seen enough to be annoyed with. It's nearly intimacy, in my profession.

By minute six the heaviness reached my knees. This is the part where an untasted victim starts to feel afraid, and fear makes them breathe fast, and fast breathing spreads the alkaloids quicker, and the whole ugly business accelerates. So I did the opposite. I slowed my breath to four counts in, six counts out. I thought about arithmetic. I thought about the recipe for the almond torte I'd been memorizing off the pastry mistress that week—not because I'd ever bake it, but because a mind needs a room to stand in while the body burns.

Minute eight: the knees finished, the hips beginning. There is a sensation specific to hemlock that I have never found a better word for than *pouring*—as though the legs are vessels being slowly filled from the feet up with something heavier than blood. My hands stayed clean. My hands always stay clean longest, which is fortunate, because a taster who drops her spoon in front of witnesses has questions asked about her.

Minute ten: I could no longer trust my legs, so I stopped using them. There is a stool behind the tasting table, placed there years ago by a taster named Maren who died at fifty-one with twenty-two poisons in her ledger and a pension the crown never paid out. I sat down with the smoothness of someone simply tired, and no one noticed, because no one ever notices.

And here is the thing I want you to understand, the thing I have never written in any ledger, the thing I am only telling you now because a book has no steward and no headsman: I sat down on that stool, paralyzed from the waist up by degrees, in a kitchen full of people who would not notice if I died, and I felt—

Pride.

There. I've said it. Pride. The ugly, private, magnificent pride of a body that survives everything. Every taster has it, though we'd all deny it under torture, which some of us have technically undergone. It is not pride in the king, whom I have seen perhaps nine times, always at a distance, always chewing. It is not pride in Vhaloria, which sold me to the palace for eleven crowns and a forgiveness of debt when I was seven. It is pride in the body itself—in this one stubborn, methodical animal that has taken fourteen poisons and filed them all away like a clerk who keeps her own records. Somewhere in my blood there is a library now. Foxglove, arsenic, belladonna, aconite, the white amanita they put in the soup the winter I turned nineteen, ergot, two kinds of snake venom, mercury, and now hemlock, welcome, take your seat, the others saved you a chair.

No one else in this kingdom owns anything so complete. Kings own countries. I own my survival. I know which of us sleeps better.

* * *

The paralysis peaked at minute fourteen and began its retreat at minute nineteen, exactly as hemlock does when the dose is honest. By half past the hour I could stand, and by the time the supper cart went up to the king's apartments I was walking beside it with my ledger under my arm, gray as dishwater, invisible as ever.

At the door of the private stair, the captain of the guard took my report. He did not look at me. He looked at the ledger, which is the correct order of operations.

"Well?" he said.

"Clean," I said.

He raised his eyebrows. He was new—three weeks in the post—and he had not yet learned that the taster's report is delivered in one word whenever possible. "You're certain? Cooks have been nervous lately. There was talk of—"

"Clean," I said again, and he waved the cart through, because a captain who argues with the taster's ledger is a captain who has imagined the king dead, and see above regarding conversations with the floor scrubbers.

Was it a lie? No. It was a translation. The ledger says *clean*; my private book, hidden behind the loose brick in my cell, says otherwise. Tonight's entry reads, in my smallest hand: *Fourteenth. Hemlock, root-cut, in honey glaze, quail. Onset 4 min. Full presentation 14 min. Recovery 19 min. Dose generous—intended for a larger body than mine, or intended twice.* That last phrase is the important one. A single dose of hemlock kills a man once. A dose sized for two killings suggests the poisoner wanted insurance—which suggests the poisoner knows the king sometimes has his meals tasted twice, or knows the king is large, or is simply careless.

Careless poisoners are rare. Careful ones are common. And a careful poisoner who salts the honey with hemlock on a Tuesday, with no feast, no foreign delegation, no anniversary of any grudge I can find in the court calendar—that is not carelessness. That is patience. Someone is testing the kitchens the way I test poisons: a little at a time, learning what gets through.

I climbed down to my cell below the pantry level—the tasters' cells are always below the food, a piece of symbolism the architects surely intended—and I wrote out the full entry by candlelight, and I ate my own supper, which tonight was barley porridge and a heel of bread, both of which I tasted for myself out of habit, though no one has ever bothered to poison me. Why would they? Poisoning a taster is like burning down the executioner's house. Everyone knows what lives there.

The candle burned low. The valley's stillness pressed in around my little room the way it presses around everything here—complete, attentive, empty. In other countries, I'm told, the night has texture. Witches' lights on the fens, saints in the roadside shrines that sometimes move their eyes, prayers that go somewhere. Here, nothing. Here, the dark is just dark, and a prayer is just breath shaped like wanting, and I have spent twenty years telling myself that this emptiness is safety, which it is, which it absolutely is.

But I will confess one more thing to this page, since we've come this far.

When I catalogued the hemlock tonight—lying on my cot, legs pouring, heart steady—I felt, beneath the pride, beneath the professional satisfaction of a clean identification, something I did not expect. Something small and cold, like a fish turning over in deep water.

I felt *watched.*

Not by the poisoner. Not by the captain or the cooks or the king. Watched the way a valley watches—which is to say, not at all, because valleys cannot watch, because magic is dead here, because the boundary stones hold and the gods are a hundred years gone and the only thing that has ever answered a prayer in Vhaloria is silence.

Except that tonight, for the first time in twenty years of silence, the silence felt less like absence and more like attention. Less like an empty room and more like a held breath.

I told myself it was the hemlock. Residues do strange things; Maren's ledger mentions it; the fourth stage of many toxins is a mild paranoia that passes by morning. I blew out my candle. I lay down. I recited the almond torte recipe until I slept.

And in the kitchen above my head, long after midnight, a scullery maid with burned hands knelt down at the great hearth to sweep the ash—and instead of sweeping, she prayed.

I did not hear it. I was asleep, and the walls between us are thick, and besides, no one hears prayers in the valley. Nothing has answered one in a hundred years.

Nothing was supposed to.
