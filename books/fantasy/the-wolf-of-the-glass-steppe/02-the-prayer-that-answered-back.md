## 2. The Prayer That Answered Back

I wake to the smell of burnt sugar and something underneath it that has no name.

That's the first wrong thing. The palace kitchens have forty-one identifiable smells at any hour—tallow and onions and wet slate and the vinegar we use to cut the fish smell out of the cutting blocks—and I know all of them the way other people know their mother's face. A nameless smell is not a smell. It is a hole shaped like a smell.

The second wrong thing is the quiet. Not the ordinary quiet of the valley, which I have lived inside for nineteen years and which I could describe to you the way a blind man describes a wall: a stillness with weight, a hush like held breath, the particular silence of a place where nothing *listens*. This is different. This is the quiet after a bell has been struck. The air above my cot in the tasters' dormitory feels struck. Ringing without sound.

I lie still and catalog, because cataloging is what I do instead of panicking. Pulse: elevated but regular. Mouth: dry. There is a taste on my tongue I did not put there—cold, faintly mineral, like licking the inside of a well bucket—and I have not eaten anything since supper, since the hemlock in the honeyed quail, fourteenth poison, fully metabolized by now. So either the hemlock has done something hemlock does not do, or something in this building has changed while I slept.

I get up. I dress. I go upstairs toward the kitchens, and I tell myself it's because the night ovens need banking and someone forgot, which is even true, if you want it to be.

* * *

The scullery maid's name is Tamsin and she is perhaps sixteen and I have never once spoken to her, because speaking to people is how you stop being invisible, and invisibility is the only armor I own. She works the low end of the kitchen—the pots, the ash, the grease trap—and she has burned hands. Everyone who works the low end has burned hands; the great hearth takes its tax from whoever tends it. But Tamsin's are bad. I've noticed them the way I notice everything about hands, because hands matter to me professionally. Blistered across both palms, the left wrist scalded shiny-pink where a kettle went over two weeks ago. She'd wrapped them in rag strips and kept working, the way the low end always does, because the palace does not pay for pain and does not excuse it either.

It is past midnight when I come up through the service stair, and the kitchen is nearly empty. One banked oven ticking as it cools. Two candles left burning at the long prep table because Cook believes candles left unlit invite mice, which is superstition, but cheap superstition. And Tamsin, kneeling at the great hearth with her broom forgotten against the stone, her burned hands lifted in front of her face, saying words.

I stop in the shadow of the doorway. Old habit. You can stand in that doorway for an hour and no one will see you; I've tested it.

She isn't praying the way the priests pray. Palace prayer is performance—loud, cadenced, addressed to gods who stopped listening before my grandmother was born, recited so the king's piety can be recorded in the margins of official documents. What Tamsin is doing has no form at all. It's just a girl whispering into ash, the way children talk to things they've been told don't exist. I catch fragments. *Please. I know nobody does this anymore, but please.* Her burned fingers curled inward like leaves in frost. *My gran said you used to listen. Gran's dead now, so she can't tell me if she lied.*

And here is what I should have done: I should have coughed. Cleared my throat, let my boots ring on the flagstones, broken the moment into ordinary pieces—a maid, a hearth, a silly half-heard mumble to dead gods. That is what the old me, the careful me, the me who survived fourteen poisons by never once being interesting, would have done.

Instead I stood in the dark and listened, because I wanted to know what a prayer sounds like when nothing answers it.

Something answered it.

* * *

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last thing I'll have left after tonight, and because if I ever have to describe it to anyone—which I won't, which I mustn't—I want the account to hold up under examination. I am a professional observer. My whole life depends on noticing what no one else notices and reporting none of it. So: the facts.

Fact one: the two candles at the prep table guttered. Not the way candles gutter when a door opens—not a lean, not a flutter, but a *bend*, both flames leaning hard toward the hearth, toward Tamsin, as if pulled on strings. And the light coming off them was blue. Not the blue edge a wick gets when it's drowning in its own wax. Blue the way deep ice is blue, blue the way the underside of a dead man's fingernails is blue. For three heartbeats the whole kitchen stood in underwater light, and every shadow fell the wrong way.

Fact two: the smell got worse—or better, depending on your appetite. Burnt sugar and under it that nameless thing, and now I had a word hovering near it and refusing to land. It smelled like a country. Like snowmelt over iron, like bread baked in a wood I've never seen, like cold air off a lake at the exact minute the sun clears the ridge. It smelled like a homecoming I have never had and grieved anyway, which makes no sense, and I am reporting it anyway, because those were the terms I set.

Fact three: Tamsin's hands healed.

I watched it happen from twenty feet away in bad light and I would stake my life on it, and in this profession staking your life on an observation is not a figure of speech. The blisters did not fade gradually. They *closed*, the way a mouth closes, the raw skin drawing smooth from the center outward, the scalded shine on her wrist sinking back into unmarked flesh in the space of a breath. She made a sound—a small, punched-out sound, half gasp and half laugh—and turned her palms over and over in the blue light, looking at them, touching the unbroken skin with fingers that had been ruined since midwinter.

Then the candles righted themselves. The light went yellow and ordinary. The nameless smell thinned, lingered, and was gone, leaving behind only the burnt sugar and the tick of the cooling oven.

Tamsin knelt there in the sudden ordinariness, holding her own hands like someone else's hands, and began, very quietly, to cry.

* * *

Here is what everyone in Vhaloria learns before they learn their letters: the gods are gone. Not dead—dead implies a body, a grave, an ending you could point to. *Unmade.* A hundred years ago, give or take, the old country of Vhaloria was erased—not conquered, erased—and the gods went with it, or it went with the gods; the histories disagree, and the histories are written by the winners, and the winners wrote the erasure itself. What remains is the valley, and the valley is safe, and the safety has a texture. Magic cannot follow you here. Prayers cannot be answered here. The old powers press against the valley's borders like weather against glass, and the glass holds, and has held for a hundred years, and every child grows up inside that holding without ever knowing it's remarkable, the way fish presumably do not find water interesting.

We still say the prayers because the king likes them said. We still keep the feast days because feasts are feasts. But no one expects anything. Expectation died with the old country. You might as well expect the moon to answer.

So when the candles bent blue and a girl's ruined hands closed up like a book being shut gently, I did not think *miracle*. I thought, in this order:

One. Containment breach.

Two. Is anyone else awake?

Three. Did it see me?

I stood in that doorway with my heart going like a fist on a locked door and understood, with the clarity the hemlock sometimes lends me, that I had spent nineteen years learning to survive everything that can enter a body through the mouth, and I had trained for exactly nothing that enters through the ribs.

Because that's where it had gone. While Tamsin cried over her new hands, something had walked through the open door of her prayer and looked around the room, and part of it had snagged on me—on the taster crouched in the dark, on whatever it is inside me that has been steeping in poison for two decades like tea grown too strong. I felt it as a hook. A small, patient hook set just behind my sternum, in the place where dread lives, and it did not pull. It didn't need to. It had simply *fastened*, the way a leech fastens, quietly, with an interest that is worse than hunger.

I know hooks. I grew up around fishermen on the river quarter before the palace bought me. A hook is baited for a specific fish. Whatever had come through Tamsin's prayer had found, in that dark kitchen, exactly the thing it had been fishing for, and the thing was me.

* * *

Tamsin gathered herself eventually. Wiped her face on her apron, looked at her palms one more time, folded them closed like keeping something, took up her broom, and swept the ash with hands that would never blister again. She hummed while she worked. It was a tune I didn't know, and I know every song sung in these kitchens; songs travel down the service stair faster than gossip. The melody had an odd shape to it, falling where songs usually rise, and it sounded—this will seem mad—it sounded *translated*. As though she were humming something remembered in another language.

She finished. She banked the hearth. She left by the servants' door, humming, and the kitchen settled into its ordinary dark, and I stayed in the doorway a long while, doing sums.

Sum one: the valley holds. That is doctrine, that is history, that is the entire architecture of the peace. If the valley held, then nothing gets in. Therefore either the doctrine is wrong, or the valley has developed a flaw, or—

Or the thing that answered Tamsin did not come *in*. Or it was already here, asleep somewhere in the stone, for a hundred years, waiting for someone stupid enough and sad enough to kneel at a hearth and ask.

Sum two: Tamsin's hands. Healing magic, in the valley, is like fire underwater. Unless the healing wasn't magic. Unless it was something older and more local than the categories allow. I turn it over and over and cannot make it fit any container I own.

Sum three—and this is the sum I keep arriving at no matter how I try to arrange the numbers—*me*. The hook behind my ribs. Why me? I am the least magical creature in this building. The valley's deadness and my own deadness-to-attention are practically the same material; I am a walking extension of the border. Nothing should snag on me. Nothing should *want* me.

Unless wanting isn't the right verb. Unless what fastened onto me behind the sternum wasn't hunger but recognition. Something looked around that kitchen and saw a girl whose body is a museum of swallowed harms—hemlock, arsenic, foxglove, fourteen poisons and counting, a woman built entirely out of small survivings—and recognized, perhaps, a country that was also swallowed. Also survived, wrongly, incompletely. Also sitting inside someone else's walls, unquiet.

I don't believe in intuition. Intuition is pattern-matching wearing a shawl. But every pattern I own is pointing at one conclusion, and the conclusion is: something has started again, and it knows my shape.

* * *

By the time the sky goes grey, I have made my decisions, and they are the decisions of a coward, and I am at peace with that; cowards live longest, that's rather the point of us.

Decision one: I saw nothing. Candles gutter, girls heal slowly, prayers are noise. If anyone asks, I was banking ovens. I have been invisible for nineteen years and I intend to remain so until I die old and dull and full of antidotes.

Decision two: I will watch Tamsin. From a distance. Without her knowledge. Not because I care about her—I don't, caring is a luxury item and I've never earned the wages—but because whatever touched her touched through her, and I want to know if it comes back, and if it comes back, whether it looks for me.

Decision three, and this one I write down, which already breaks rule one, which tells you how badly the night shook me: in the back pages of Maren's ledger—the dead taster's ledger I inherited, the one with the recipes and the symptom lists and the margins full of a stranger's handwriting—I record the date, the hour, the blue of the candles matched against a swatch of dyestuff, and the taste on my tongue, which by morning I could still name: snowmelt over iron.

A flavor from nowhere. In a valley where nowhere is not supposed to exist.

I close the ledger. Behind my ribs, the hook sits quiet and patient, and I understand suddenly that it is not waiting for me to move.

It is waiting for me to be hungry.

Above my head, faint through the floorboards, someone begins to hum a tune that falls where songs should rise.
