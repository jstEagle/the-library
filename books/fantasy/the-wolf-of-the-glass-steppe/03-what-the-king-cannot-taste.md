## 3. What the King Cannot Taste

The trouble began with the pears.

Not the poison in the pears—there wasn't one, that night, and I checked twice, because a taster who checks twice is a taster who lives to retire. The trouble was what the pears tasted of *besides* themselves: poached in white wine, as they always were on the second Thursday of the month, served to His Majesty Aurelian III with a spoon of clotted cream and a dusting of cinnamon. I took my measured bite from the serving dish before it went up, as I had taken a measured bite every second Thursday for six years.

And the pear tasted of snow.

Not cold. Not the clean chill of the palace ice-house, which has its own particular mineral sweetness. Snow—the way I imagined snow tasting, anyway, having never eaten any. Something vast and silent and old. And underneath it, faint as a watermark, iron. Blood-iron, blade-iron, the iron taste that sits at the back of your tongue when you've bitten your cheek and pretend you haven't.

I swallowed. I waited. I catalogued.

Tongue: tingling, but not the hemlock tingle, not the foxglove numbness. Pulse: steady. Hands: steady, which surprised me. Vision: clear, except—

Except for half a heartbeat, the banquet hall's candles had looked blue at their cores. The way they'd looked three nights ago, when Marta the scullery maid prayed at the hearth and something *answered*, and her scalded hand healed smooth as a child's while the rest of us stood very still and pretended not to see.

I did not report the pears.

Understand what that means. A royal food-taster reports everything. A bitter note, a bruised flavor, an aftertaste of tin—all of it goes into the log, because the log is the whole architecture of my survival. The king trusts me because my honesty is boring. Boring is armor. The day I report something unreportable—*Your Majesty, your dessert tastes like a country that was erased before either of us was born*—is the day someone important looks directly at me and asks a follow-up question.

Attention is death for a taster. Not the quick death; I have antidotes for most of those. The slow kind. The kind where the priests take an interest, and the alchemists want samples, and eventually someone decides that a woman whose body tolerates fourteen poisons might be more useful opened up than standing up.

So I wrote in the official log: *Poached pears, white wine, clotted cream, cinnamon. No adverse effect.*

And then I went back to my cell—a room with a bed, a shelf, and a lock on the outside—and lay awake listening to the valley's silence.

The Vhalorian Valley is quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Magic doesn't work here; everyone knows it, no one discusses it much, because discussing it means admitting there's such a thing to be dead to. Wards fail at the boundary line. Charms go dull. Prayers fall like stones. It's why the palace was built here, three hundred years ago, in the crater of whatever happened to make this place so thoroughly *nothing*. Nothing gets in. Nothing answers.

Except something had answered Marta. And now something was seasoning the king's supper.

* * *

It got worse by degrees, which is how the truly dangerous things always arrive.

Friday's venison carried a ghost of smoked salt—not valley salt, not sea salt, but something darker, cured over fires I couldn't name. Saturday's bread tasted briefly, at the very edge of each bite, of woodsmoke and rye grown in colder soil than ours. Sunday the king's honeyed wine went down my throat and came out the other side of my attention smelling of pine resin and, impossibly, of *feasting*—of a long table, of many voices, of a room full of people eating together somewhere that no longer existed.

I know how that sounds. I stood in the tasting alcove off the royal kitchen with my eyes closed, holding a spoon of wine, and I could have sworn I heard a room I'd never been in. Laughter in a language I didn't speak. A woman's voice saying something warm and short, the kind of thing said a thousand times over a thousand dinners until it stops being words and becomes the sound of home.

I opened my eyes. The alcove was stone and shadow and the smell of yesterday's stock. The wine was wine. The king drank his portion an hour later and complained only that it was a touch sweet.

No one else tasted it. I made sure to find this out without asking—asking is attention. I watched the other servants eat from the same pots. Cooks licked spoons and grimaced about nothing. The under-steward sipped the wine and pronounced it adequate. Whatever was haunting the king's table, it was haunting it *for me*.

Or through me.

I didn't let myself finish that thought for two more days. Then I stopped not letting myself think it, because a taster who lies to herself will eventually misjudge a dose, and misjudged doses are how tasters die.

Something answered Marta's prayer. Now something was speaking to me in flavors. And I was the only palate in the valley trained finely enough to hear it.

* * *

On the ninth day I did the first dishonest thing of my career, if you don't count the log entries, which were honest in wording and false in completeness, which is the only kind of lie I'd ever permitted myself.

I stole a book.

Not stolen, precisely. Requisitioned without paperwork. There's a shelf of ruined ledgers in the counting room—water-damaged, ink-bled, destined for the pulping barrel. I took the least destroyed one: a merchant's recipe compendium, forty years dead, its early pages still legible. Recipes are the perfect camouflage. No one questions a servant writing down recipes. Servants are supposed to hoard recipes the way squirrels hoard acorns; it's considered charming.

That night, by candle stub, I began a second record.

*Day nine. King's supper: roast capon, buttered roots, spiced plums.* And beneath it, in a shorthand of my own devising—because even a hidden ledger deserves a locked door—*ghost notes: smoked salt, pine resin, iron beneath plum. Blue at candle-core for 2 heartbeats. No symptoms. No symptoms is the wrong word. Symptoms of nothing I have been poisoned with.*

I sat back and looked at what I'd written and felt the crack run through the whole careful structure of my life.

Here is what no one tells you about invisibility: it works beautifully right up until you need to be seen, and then you discover you've spent years practicing your own disappearance, and the skill doesn't reverse. I had built myself into the walls of this palace. I knew which floorboards announced visitors, which servants reported gossip to which masters, which hours the priests walked the corridors. I could stand in a doorway and be furniture. For eleven years—seven as a scullery girl, four as taster—that had been the whole of my ambition. Survive. Remain unnoticed. Keep my body mine.

Now I owned a secret book full of impossible flavors, and keeping it meant becoming a person with something to hide beyond my own existence. A person with a *project*. A person the god—or whatever it was—had chosen for reasons I couldn't fathom, and chosen visibly enough that pretending otherwise was starting to feel less like caution and more like lying flat in a field while something circles overhead.

I blew out the candle and told myself: one more week. One more week of logging, watching, waiting. If it stays small, it dies or it bores itself and moves on. Everything in the palace eventually bores itself and moves on. That's the other thing about being invisible—you learn that even gods, if they exist, have short attention spans for furniture.

The week did not stay small.

* * *

Wednesday. The king's birthday feast, moved up a fortnight because the astrologers insisted the omens favored it—which should have been my first warning, because astrologers insisting on anything usually means the omens said the opposite.

Twelve courses. I tasted all twelve in the antechamber, working methodically down the line while the under-steward called them out and the page recorded my verdicts. Quail in honey. Eel pie. A soup of pale beans that the cook swore by and everyone else endured. Cheese from the high pastures. Small sweet cakes shaped like moons.

The moon cakes were the twelfth course, and they were the ones that broke me open.

I bit. Almond, rosewater, honey—correct, all correct, no toxin, my body confirmed it with its usual mute efficiency. But underneath, rising through the almond like a body through water:

Snow. Iron. Pine smoke. And now, clearer than ever before, *bread*—a dark dense loaf, rye and caraway, torn warm, and a voice, a specific voice, a grandmother's voice, saying a blessing over it in a language with too many soft consonants, and the blessing wasn't words to me but it was *meaning*, it was meaning the way heat is meaning to a frozen hand, and I understood without understanding that I was tasting a kitchen. An entire kitchen. A kitchen that had been fed by hands, loved by noses, remembered by no one, because everyone who remembered it had been—

Had been what? Unmade. Erased. The Glass Steppe, the histories call it, though the histories give it half a paragraph: *the eastern nation of Vhaloria was destroyed in the Godfall a century past; the valley of its ruin was settled by Vhaloria's conquerors; no survivors of note.*

No survivors of note. And yet here was its bread in my mouth, its salt-smoke in my sinuses, its grandmother's blessing lodged behind my sternum like a fishbone.

My hand shook. Just the one hand, just once. In eleven years of professional tasting, my hand had never shaken. I set down the moon cake with great care, as if it were sleeping, and said, "All twelve pass," in my flat gray voice, and the page wrote it down, and the courses went up, and the king ate his birthday meal and laughed at the jugglers and lived, which was, technically, my entire job.

Then I went to my cell and wrote for two hours.

Not log entries. Not clinical catalogue. I wrote the bread. I wrote it as best I could—rye flour cut with barley, caraway seed, a sour starter fed daily, baked on a stone that had seen a thousand loaves, torn, never cut, because cutting was for strangers and tearing was for family. I didn't know how I knew these things. That was the point. That was the horror and the fascination braided together so tightly I couldn't tell which strand was which: the flavors came with *knowledge attached*, knowledge I had never been given and couldn't have earned, arriving in my mouth like letters addressed to someone else that only I could read.

By the time the candle guttered out I had filled six pages. Six pages of a dead country's cooking, recorded in a dead language of measurements I invented on the spot, in a stolen book hidden inside the wall cavity behind my shelf, in a valley where nothing magical was supposed to be able to happen.

I lay in the dark and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

Marta prayed. Something answered. Nine days later, the king's food began tasting of a country that died a hundred years ago, and it tasted that way *only to me*—to the one person in the palace whose palate was sharp enough to catch a whisper, whose body was a laboratory of swallowed dangers, whose whole life was a practice in taking things into herself and surviving them.

A vessel, some part of me whispered, using a priest's word I'd heard only in passing and never expected to apply to myself. You swallow things. Things are learning to speak through what you swallow.

"Shut up," I told the dark, quietly, because even my defiance had learned to keep its volume down.

But the dark didn't shut up, and neither did the flavors. Over the following days they grew bolder. The kitchen's morning porridge carried a thread of that dark bread. The well water, on one unforgettable occasion, arrived at my cup tasting faintly of melted snow and river stones from a river that was now a mirror-flat plain of glass. Once—I would swear this on any altar you like, though I no longer believed altars held anything—the steam rising off the soup kettle bent, just slightly, against the draft, and formed for a moment the shape of a doorway.

I kept writing. Every ghost note, every blue-cored candle, every half-heard blessing. My little stolen book fattened. And with every entry, the crack in my invisibility widened, because here is the thing about secrets: they are weight, and weight changes how you walk. I caught myself moving differently. Watching doors. Flinching when the priest Father Ossen passed me in the corridor and paused—just a beat, just a flicker of those pale eyes—and said, "You're Fairweather, yes? The taster." Not a question he needed answered. A question he wanted me to hear him ask.

"Yes, father."

"Eat well," he said, smiling, and moved on.

Eat well. I turned the phrase over for the rest of the day like a stone with something under it. Priests bless; it's their trade; perhaps it meant nothing. But Father Ossen was the one who had examined Marta after the hearth-fire miracle, the one who had asked her, gently, terribly, whether she had *invited* anything in. And now he knew my name. Tasters don't have names, not to men like him. We have functions.

That night I added a final entry to the hidden book, and my hand was steady again, and that steadiness frightened me more than the shaking had, because it meant I was adapting. Means to an end. The taster's creed. Even to this.

*Day nineteen. Ghost notes persisting and strengthening. Bread, snow, iron, pine smoke, door-shape in steam. Subject: self. Conclusion: I am no longer only tasting the king's food. Something is tasting me.*

I read it back twice. It sounded mad. I kept it anyway, because the alternative was to decide I was mad, and madness, unlike poisoning, has no antidote I can brew.

* * *

On the twenty-second day, two things happened before noon.

The first was small. Sef—the orphan boy who scrubbed the tasting alcove floors, seven years old, ears too big for his head, the kind of child the palace absorbs and forgets in the same motion—stopped scrubbing as I passed and looked up at me and said, in a voice not entirely his own, or perhaps entirely his own for the first time:

"You taste it too."

I froze. "Taste what?"

He shrugged, small shoulders under an enormous cast-off apron, and went back to his floor. But as I walked away I heard him humming—four notes, descending, the melody of that grandmother's blessing, the one from the bread, the one in the language with too many soft consonants.

The second thing was louder. The bells.

Not the dinner bells, not the alarm bells. The observatory bell, high in the astronomer-priests' tower, which rang, according to palace lore, only when the sky itself required comment. I had never heard it. Neither, judging by the faces pouring into the corridors, had anyone else currently living.

Servants clustered at windows. Cooks abandoned stockpots. Somewhere below, the under-steward was shouting for calm and achieving the opposite. And through the kitchen's high east window I saw the astronomers crossing the inner court in their gray robes, moving fast, heads together, carrying instruments wrapped in wool like wounded birds.

I returned to my alcove and my ledger and my gray invisible life, and I wrote one line:

*Day twenty-two. The sky is making announcements. Whatever is speaking to me in flavors is about to start speaking to everyone.*

Behind my ribs, faint but unmistakable, something tightened. Like a hook finding purchase. Like an invitation being prepared.

Like a door, far away, beginning to remember that it used to open.
