## 4. The Cartographer of Nowhere

They let Ilya Vosk keep his pens. It was the single mercy of his confinement, and he suspected it was not a mercy at all but an experiment: give the madman his instruments and see what he draws.

Eleven years in the under-archive of the Royal Survey, three floors below the map room where his charts had once hung in gilded frames. The cell was a converted records vault, which meant stone walls, one grate for air, one slot for food, and shelving that still smelled of the vellum that had been taken away from him. They left him paper instead. Cheap paper, pulpy, that drank ink wrong and buckled with humidity. He had learned to love its flaws. Flaws were honest.

Every morning a survey clerk descended with a slate of official charts — the borders of Vhaloria as the crown insisted they had never existed — and every morning Ilya copied them out in his small, exact hand, because copying was his sentence and he was, whatever else they said of him, obedient to the discipline of the line.

And every night, alone, he watched the copies redraw themselves.

It began always the same way: a coastline breathing. The northern shore of the Sea of Glass, which on the king's charts was a smooth and empty curve, would develop a harbor by candlelight. A river would remember itself, wandering back into a valley that officially contained no water. Village names surfaced like drowned men rising — Kelmoryn, Serevan, Ostrava-of-the-Reeds — written in an alphabet the clerks called nonsense and Ilya could read as easily as his own name, because it was his own name, or nearly; Vosk was a Vhalorian ending, worn smooth by two generations of hiding.

He had stopped reporting it after the third year. Reporting got him a hearing before the Survey Tribunal, and the Tribunal asked him questions in the gentle voice reserved for men they intended to declare mad, and madness in a cartographer meant the end of everything except the cell he already occupied. So he kept the redrawings to himself. He kept them the way a man keeps a second heartbeat — privately, faithfully, without any expectation anyone else would ever feel it.

The truth, which he had confessed exactly once, eleven years ago, in a bright room full of men who loved him and then did not: he had not mapped Vhaloria from the archives.

There were no archives. That was rather the point of the unmaking. A century ago the kingdom had erased its eastern neighbor so thoroughly that even the erasure had been erased; the histories mentioned a "disputed steppe," the songs mentioned nothing at all, and the oldest surviving atlas showed a blank space with a marginal note in a dead clerk's hand: *here be nothing.* The crown's position, maintained across four reigns with the patience only bureaucracy can achieve, was that there had never been anything to erase.

Ilya had mapped it anyway — every harbor, every road, the terraced vineyards above Kelmoryn, the twelve-stepped temple at Serevan with its moon-counting frieze — because he remembered it.

Not from books. From being four years old and standing on his mother's hip while she argued with a fishmonger in a language that later, in exile, she refused to speak again. From the smell of her kitchen, which no recipe in Vhaloria's successor provinces produced. From a smuggler's cart, at night, looking backward at a country whose lights went out behind them all at once, all together, like a table being cleared.

He had told the Tribunal it was research. He had told them the details came from fragmentary sources, oral tradition, inference. He had lied badly, because lying requires imagination and he had spent his whole life trading in the opposite. And when the Lord Cartographer held up Ilya's chart of the Sea of Glass and asked, softly, *how does a man know the sound a drowned bell makes*, Ilya had made the mistake of answering honestly:

*I've heard it.*

Eleven years. He had learned the archive's sounds the way he'd once learned coastlines: the drip in the northeast corner that froze in winter; the boots of the day-clerks, two hundred steps overhead, a soft percussion through stone; the hour when the lamp-oil man came, bringing with him the smell of tallow and, faintly, from the kitchens far above, roasted meat and honey — the smell of the king's supper, seasoned and tasted and carried up through the palace's veins.

Some nights he drew anyway. Not the official charts. He drew Vhaloria from memory, over and over, refining, correcting against the nightly redrawings as though the paper were a correspondent writing letters from a country he'd lost. The maps kept adding things he didn't remember. A bridge at Ostrava that hadn't been there when he was four. A thirteenth step on the temple frieze. Small revisions, patient, like a place putting itself back together in the dark.

His mother had taught him a word for that, before she stopped speaking the language. He didn't use it anymore. Words like that got men cells.

* * *

On the morning it changed, the day-clerk was late.

Ilya noticed because the light through the air-grate — a knife-thin stripe of grey that crossed his floor between the eighth and ninth hours — arrived without its usual accompaniment of footsteps. The archive was silent in a way it almost never was. Even the drip seemed to be listening.

Then he heard different footsteps. Slower. Lighter. Descending the iron stair with the particular care of someone who did not want to be announced.

He put down his pen. Eleven years had taught him the archive's population the way a lighthouse keeper knows ships: clerks, the oil man, twice-yearly inspectors, the rat-catcher with his terriers. Nobody came down slowly unless they were afraid of being heard, and nobody who belonged here was afraid of being heard.

A figure paused at the foot of the stairs, at the edge of the lamplight. Slim. Kitchen-grey clothes, the undyed wool of the lower service. An apron folded over one arm like a shield. Hair pinned back with the severe practicality of someone who had decided invisibility was a career.

He knew her. Not by name — the palace had ten thousand servants and their names were weather to him, felt but never recorded — but by type. He'd spent years among the kitchen staff during his disgrace hearings, taking his meals with the scullery hands because disgraced officers ate below stairs. The tasters sat apart even there. A separate table, a separate silence. People looked through them deliberately, the way you look through a window you don't want to admit is a mirror.

This one had the look. But she wasn't looking through him now. She was looking at him the way he hadn't been looked at in eleven years: directly, with a question in it.

"You're late," Ilya said, because the silence needed breaking and honesty was his incurable condition. "Whoever you are. The day-clerk comes at the eighth hour."

"I'm not the clerk." Her voice was low, level, pitched not to carry. "I came down with the linen carts. No one counts linen."

"No," he agreed. "No one counts linen. It's the perfect crime." He stood, slowly — his knees had opinions about stone floors now — and gestured at the shelves beyond the bars. "You're in the under-archive of the Royal Survey. Everything down here is either wrong or forbidden, and most of it is both. If you're looking for something to steal, the wine inventories are worth nothing and the border surveys are worth your life."

"I'm not stealing." She came closer, into the light, and he saw her properly for the first time: young, but with the finished quality of someone who had been old since childhood. Pale in a way he recognized — the pallor of the taster's trade, which eats the color out of you one measured spoonful at a time. Her eyes went past him, past the bars, to the papers spread on his table.

To the papers that were not the official chart he'd copied that morning.

He turned. His heart did something embarrassing.

Overnight, while he slept, his private map of the Sea of Glass had redrawn itself again. The harbor of Kelmoryn had grown a second breakwater. A road he'd never drawn ran inland from the shore toward the terraced hills. And along the margin, in that alphabet the crown called nonsense, in ink he did not own and had not touched, a single new line had appeared:

*Ask her why she watches.*

He looked back at the woman. She was staring at the margin. At the words. Her lips moved slightly, the way lips move when a language is arriving somewhere deep and unwelcome.

"You can read it," Ilya said. It wasn't a question. Nothing about her face allowed questions.

"No," she said. Then, quieter, with the flatness of a professional delivering a clinical finding: "Yes. I don't know how. It tastes—" She stopped herself, visibly appalled at her own mouth.

"Tastes," Ilya repeated.

"Like snow and iron." She said it the way people confess to crimes. "Everything I taste lately tastes like snow and iron. The king's supper. Water. My own— " She shut her jaw. Recalibrated. He watched her do it and thought, absurdly, *she catalogs herself the way I catalog coastlines.*

"My name is Ilya Vosk," he said. "Royal cartographer. Formerly. Currently the Survey's resident lunatic, eleven years served of a life sentence for accurate work." He held out his hand through the bars, an antique gesture, and after a moment she took it. Her grip was cool and dry and careful, the grip of someone accustomed to handling dangerous things in measured amounts.

"Xanthe," she said. Just the name. No house, no station, no surname. A name like a locked door.

"Well, Xanthe-with-no-surname." He withdrew his hand and tapped the margin of the map. "You should understand what you're looking at, because I've had eleven years to understand it and it hasn't helped me sleep. This chart is supposed to show forty leagues of empty steppe east of the border. There has never been anything there. That is official policy, ratified by four kings, enforced by the Tribunal, and believed by everyone in this palace except me, the rats, and apparently this ink."

"And the ink disagrees," she said.

"The ink insists." He laughed, and it came out rusty, a hinge unused for years. "Every night it corrects my corrections. I copy the official blank and the paper fills itself in. Harbors. Roads. Names. It's been teaching me things I don't remember, Xanthe-with-no-surname, and I *lived* there. Or — near there. Before." Before the lights went out all at once, like a table being cleared. He didn't say it. Some sentences you keep.

She was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register — lower, faster, the voice of someone stepping off a ledge she'd been circling for weeks.

"The food tastes like a country. Not this country. I taste the king's meals — that's my work — and for a month now every dish arrives pre-seasoned with a place that doesn't exist. Snow and iron. Woodsmoke and something sweet I can't name. A spice like cinnamon's older sister. And last week I started seeing it. Kitchens. People eating. Mid-meal, mid-life, mid-argument — whole tables of them, going about their suppers as if they hadn't been erased a century ago." Her eyes flicked to the map again, to the harbor, the hills. "As if someone forgot to tell *them*."

Ilya sat down slowly on his stool. Eleven years. Eleven years of redrawing margins and Tribunal hearings and the specific loneliness of the man who remembers a country that officially never existed — and now here stood a stranger in kitchen wool, trembling slightly, describing his homeland's dinner tables from the inside.

"What's your poison of choice?" he asked. It sounded insane even to him. "Forgive me. I mean — you taste for the king. What do they dose you with?"

"Hemlock, currently. Fourteenth." She frowned. "Why does that matter?"

"Because hemlock opens doors," Ilya said. "In Vhalorian practice. The old physicians used microdoses for vision-work — the temple at Serevan kept a whole pharmacopoeia of it, poisons as prayers, the body poisoned until the soul could see through the walls." He stopped. Realized what he'd said. *Soul could see through the walls* — that was the kind of phrase the Tribunal had circled in red ink eleven years ago.

But Xanthe wasn't circling anything. Xanthe had gone very still, in the manner of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

"There's more," she said finally. "There's a maid in the kitchens who prayed at the hearth, and something answered. First answered prayer in a hundred years. And there's an astronomer-priest saying the moon has begun a thirteenth turn — a turn that belongs to no calendar. A turn somebody is owed." She looked up at him, and her eyes were dry and frightened and absolutely steady. "I came down here because the archives have one forbidden subject, Master Vosk, and it's yours. I want to know why a country that never existed is setting my tongue on fire."

For eleven years Ilya Vosk had waited for someone to ask him why the maps kept redrawing themselves. He had rehearsed the answer in ten thousand solitary hours — orderly, evidentiary, sane-sounding. He opened his mouth to deliver it.

What came out instead, in a voice that cracked down the middle like old vellum, was the truth:

"Because it isn't gone. Because you can't unmake a country, you can only make it wait. And something — someone — has decided the waiting is over." He gripped the bars. "The maps are letters, Xanthe. Someone is writing home. And if your tongue tastes it too, then whoever's writing—"

Above them, muffled through three floors of stone, a bell began to ring. Not the hour-bell. The other one. The one Ilya had heard only once before, eleven years ago, the day they took his charts down from the walls.

Xanthe's face went white. "That's the palace," she said. "Something's happening upstairs."

The ink on the table moved — both of them saw it, a fresh line surfacing in the margin like a fish rising — and Ilya read it aloud, because reading it aloud was the only thing he could do:

"*Thirteen moons. Twelve gone.*"

* * *

She left the way she'd come, quick and silent, promising nothing, promising everything. Ilya stood at his bars long after the linen-cart footsteps faded, holding the map that wouldn't stay blank, and found that the thing growing in his chest was unfamiliar enough that it took him several minutes to name it.

Hope. Bitter, rusted, eleven-years-drowned hope, hauling itself up out of the dark and clearing its throat.

He sat down, took up his pen, and for the first time in eleven years wrote a letter back.
