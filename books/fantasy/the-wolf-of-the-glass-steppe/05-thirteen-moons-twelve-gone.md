## 5. Thirteen Moons, Twelve Gone

The announcement came at noon, which Xanthe found offensive on principle. Catastrophe should keep decent hours.

She was in the kitchens when the bells began—wrong bells, the deep ones from the Astral Tower that most servants heard perhaps twice a decade, at coronations and royal deaths. The pastry line stopped mid-fold. The scullery boys froze with their arms in gray water. Somewhere above them, the bells kept tolling, thirteen strokes, and then, after a pause long enough to doubt yourself, one more.

Fourteen.

"Count them again," said Marla the undercook, flour to her elbows.

The bells obliged. Thirteen, pause, fourteen. Then silence, as if the tower itself had realized its mistake and was embarrassed by it.

Xanthe kept her head down over the quail she was portioning and thought: *there it is.*

She had known for six days. She had known since the night the moon had risen a finger's width off its charted path and the king's wine had tasted of cold smoke, and she had said nothing, because saying nothing was the whole of her craft. But knowing a thing privately and hearing it proclaimed by an astronomer-priest from the steps of the Great Hall were different animals. One was a secret you could hold like a coin in a closed fist. The other was weather.

"They're saying the moon's turned backward," whispered Pell, the youngest scullery boy, to no one in particular.

"It hasn't turned backward," snapped Marla. "It's gone *forward*. Onto a thirteenth turn. A turn that isn't on any calendar."

"Calendars have twelve turns because moons have twelve turns," said Pell.

"Then explain the fourteenth bell," said Marla, and went back to her pastry with the violence of a woman who wanted very much to be elsewhere.

Xanthe finished the quail. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; that was the first thing they'd trained into her at seven years old, before they'd trained anything else. *A taster's hands do not shake, because a shaking hand spills, and a spilled dose tells the poisoner you know.* She wrapped the portions, set them aside for the evening service, and let the kitchen's panic wash around her like water around a stone.

Invisible. Unremarked. That was armor, and armor did not rattle.

But behind her ribs, in the place where the answered prayer had hooked itself a week ago, something shifted its weight.

* * *

The astronomer-priest's name was Ossian Vell, and he delivered his proclamation to a full court that afternoon, which meant the kitchens heard every word of it third-hand by supper, embellished past recognition and then corrected back toward horror.

The true version, as Xanthe pieced it together from three separate tellings, was this: the moon had completed its twelfth turn of the year on schedule, exactly as it had for a hundred years, exactly as it had since the Reckoning. And then it had not stopped. It had hung where the empty sky should have been and begun—slowly, measurably, undeniably—to turn again. A thirteenth turn. A turn belonging to no calendar ever kept in Vhaloria or the valley both, because calendars were made by men, and men had only ever counted twelve.

Old Ossian Vell, who was ninety if he was a day and had spent his life measuring a sky that never surprised him, had reportedly wept at his instruments. He had gone to the king and said words that were already being repeated in whispers through every corridor:

*"There is a turn the sky owes. There is a debt written in the old prayers. The moon has begun to pay it."*

The old gods are owed, he'd said. The turn is theirs.

And the king—who was not a stupid man, whatever else could be said of him—had ordered the temples opened for the first time in a century. All of them. Even the sealed ones.

Even the one beneath the palace.

*That* detail arrived at the kitchens around midnight, carried by a laundress who had a cousin among the masons. The sealed temple below the east wing, the one the oldest servants crossed themselves about and the youngest pretended not to fear—the king had sent masons down with crowbars, and the masons had come back up inside an hour, white as meal, refusing to say what they'd seen except to one another, over and over, when they thought no one listened:

*It's warm. It's warm down there. Nothing down there should be warm.*

Xanthe sat on her narrow bed in the tasters' quarters—a small stone room she had occupied for nineteen years, so plain it might have been a cell, so familiar it might have been nothing at all—and turned this over.

The valley was magic-dead. Everyone knew this the way everyone knew water ran downhill. Whatever power had once moved through the world had broken against the valley's borders and drained away, and that deadness was why the palace stood here, why the kings had built their seat in this particular fold of hills a century ago. Poison worked here. Steel worked here. Prayers went nowhere.

Prayers had gone nowhere.

She lay back and stared at the ceiling. Behind her ribs, the hook shifted again—not painfully, not yet, but with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

"You're not real," she told it quietly. "Nothing answers here."

The hook said nothing, which she chose to find reassuring.

* * *

Her poisons began showing her kitchens on the fourth day after the bells.

It happened at the evening tasting, and afterward she would take the ritual apart piece by piece, hunting for the moment it went wrong, and never find it, because there wasn't one. That was the vertigo of it. There was no wrong moment. There was only the ordinary ceremony of survival, and then the world tilted sideways through the floor of it.

Hemlock, fifteenth poison, measured into the mulled wine. She knew the taste better than her own name now: green and sweetish, a meadow pretending to be harmless. She sipped, held it on her tongue as protocol demanded, swallowed, and waited for the familiar litany of symptoms to begin their cataloguing.

Numbness at the lips. Check. A faint coldness climbing the throat. Check.

And then the coldness kept climbing, past her jaw, past her eyes, and the kitchen walls went out like snuffed candles.

She was standing somewhere else.

A kitchen—but not hers. Longer, lower, with a hearth wide enough to stand in, black bread cooling on a plank, and a pot of something venison-dark breathing steam into lamplight. A woman stirred the pot with a wooden spoon worn smooth as riverstone, singing under her breath in a language Xanthe did not know and understood perfectly anyway, the way you understand a lullaby without translation. Three children set a table with more bowls than the household could plausibly need—one extra, always one extra, set at the head where no one sat—and the woman sang, and outside the little window the snow came down over fields Xanthe had never seen but recognized the way you recognize a face from a story told before you could remember being told it.

Someone said a word that meant *enough*, that meant *plenty*, that meant *may there always be more than we need*—a blessing over food, over a table, over the extra bowl—and the vision folded up like paper and Xanthe was back in the palace kitchen, both hands flat on the worktable, heart going like a hammer on a door.

"Taster?" Marla was frowning at her. "You've gone gray. Did the wine turn?"

"The wine is fine," Xanthe said. Her voice came out level. Nineteen years of practice put it there. "Slight dizziness. I skipped breakfast."

Marla grunted and returned to her sauces, satisfied. Dizziness was a known hazard of the trade. Every taster collected them.

Xanthe straightened. Wiped her hands. Finished the tasting, logged the hemlock in the ledger in her small precise hand, and noted, in the column reserved for symptoms: *mild cephalic disturbance, transient.*

What she did not note: the kitchen. The woman. The song. The bowl set for someone absent, at a table where the family had clearly, habitually, for generations, expected someone to return.

She did not note it in the ledger. But that night, after the lamps were out, she took the old recipe book—the one she kept hidden under a loose floorboard, the one in which, for weeks now, she had been recording impossible flavors in code—and she wrote by feel in the dark:

*Venison, dark broth, juniper and black bread. A song I don't know the words to but know the tune. An extra bowl. Always an extra bowl.*

And beneath it, because her hand seemed to want to write it and she had stopped arguing with her hand several days ago:

*They are mid-meal. Whoever they are, wherever that is, they were eating when it happened, and some part of them is still eating. Still setting the extra bowl. Still waiting.*

She sat with that for a long time in the dark.

Her poisons had one job. They entered her body, her body catalogued them, her body survived them, and in exchange for this daily murder she was permitted to remain in the only place she had ever lived, doing the only thing she had ever been worth. The system was ugly but it was *hers*, and it had never once lied to her. Poison made you numb, or blind, or sick, or dead. Poison did not make you *homesick* for a country that had been unmade before your grandmother was born.

Except now it did. Now every dose was a window, and every window showed the same country, and the country was full of kitchens, and the kitchens were full of people eating their last meals at tables they did not know were last, and the hook behind her ribs pulled a little tighter each time, like a fisherman pleased with the catch.

*You are being haunted,* she told herself, with clinical calm. *By your own survival skill. Note the irony. Log it. Move on.*

She almost managed it, too.

* * *

On the seventh day after the bells, the visions stopped being hers alone.

She was tasting the king's breakfast—foxglove in the preserves, sixteenth poison, a gentle one, mostly a formality—when the second window opened, and this time she saw not a stranger's kitchen but the same one, closer, the woman turning from her pot to look directly at Xanthe across a century of snow. The woman's mouth moved. No sound crossed the distance. But Xanthe read her lips the way you read a language you didn't know you'd learned:

*Set the bowl.*

Xanthe came back to herself kneeling on the flagstones of the royal dining chamber, the tasting spoon still in her hand, the chamberlain staring down at her with an expression she had never once seen directed at herself in nineteen years of perfect invisibility.

Concern.

"Xanthe." He crouched. He knew her name; that alone was alarming. "You fainted."

"Transient dizziness," she said. "I logged it."

"You're logging faintings now?"

"I log everything. That's the job."

He studied her a moment too long, and she watched him arrive at the edge of a thought and step back from it—the thought being that the palace's tasters had all been reporting strange flavors lately, snow and iron and woodsmoke in dishes cooked from ordinary valley stores, and that the priest had been asking questions, and that a girl who tasted a dead country's cooking in the king's supper might be exactly the kind of vessel the temple-sealers downstairs kept muttering about.

He stepped back from the thought. This time.

"Rest," he said. "Eat something. And Xanthe—" he paused at the door, "—if the food tastes strange to you again. Report it."

"Yes," said Xanthe, who reported nothing, because reporting meant attention, and attention was death for a taster.

But lying on her cot that night, hand pressed flat against the hook-ache behind her ribs, she admitted what she had been not-admitting for seven days: invisibility was failing her. The armor had a crack in it shaped exactly like her, and things on the other side were looking through.

The moon rose off-schedule outside her window, fat and wrong, climbing through its thirteenth turn with the slow confidence of a debt being collected. Below, in the sealed dark beneath the east wing, something warm waited in a cold temple. Above, in her chest, something patient waited behind her ribs.

Two hooks. Or one hook with two ends. She was no longer certain there was a difference.

She got up, pried loose the floorboard, and added a final line to the recipe book:

*Sixteenth poison: foxglove. Vision: same kitchen, same woman. She spoke. She said: set the bowl. I am starting to think these aren't hauntings. I am starting to think they're invitations.*

Then she blew out the lamp, and in the dark she heard—clearly, impossibly, from the direction of the sealed temple two floors down and through solid stone—a sound no palace had made in a hundred years.

Someone, down there, was setting a table.
