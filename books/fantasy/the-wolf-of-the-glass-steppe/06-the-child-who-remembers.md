## 6. The Child Who Remembers

The scullery was the warmest place in the palace and the least watched, which made it the best place in the world to hide a child. Xanthe had learned that years ago, back when she'd been small enough to hide herself. The orphans who worked the kitchens slept in shifts on straw pallets behind the copper boilers, and nobody counted them. Nobody counted them because counting them would mean admitting they existed, and admitting they existed would mean feeding them properly.

She went down at midnight, when the fires were banked low and the last of the night's dishes sat drying in racks like rows of pale teeth. She told herself she was checking on the maid—Marta, the one whose burned hand had healed itself at the hearth, the one whose prayer had been answered by something old and hungry. Marta had not come to work that evening. In a palace, an absence was either nothing or it was everything.

What she found instead was a boy.

He was curled on his side on the third pallet from the wall, maybe eight years old, thin as a whisk, his hands too big for him the way hands get when a body is growing faster than its food supply. His name, if anyone had bothered to ask, was Sef. Nobody had bothered to ask. He'd been in the kitchens eleven months, delivered by a farmer's cart along with a crate of turnips, and he'd stayed the way a stray stays: quietly, usefully, without being noticed enough to be thrown out.

He was talking in his sleep.

Xanthe stopped three steps into the room. The words coming out of him were soft and slurred the way sleep-talk always is, but they weren't Vhalorian court speech, weren't the valley's common tongue, weren't anything she knew. And Xanthe knew languages the way other people knew songs—picked up in passing, stored without effort. The palace ran on six tongues and she could taste her way through all of them. This was none of them.

It took her a moment to understand why the sound of it made her skin prickle, and then she did understand, and wished she didn't: the language was wrong for this world. It had the cadence of the flavors—the snow-and-iron taste that had been creeping into the king's food, the taste of a country that no longer existed. The boy was speaking Vhalorian. Fluently. In his sleep. A language with no living teachers, spoken by a child who had never left the valley.

She crouched beside the pallet, careful not to touch him, and listened.

*"...and the table was set for nine,"* he murmured, in that impossible tongue, *"but only eight came, and grandmother said the ninth plate stays anyway, you always set the ninth plate, you never know who is walking home."*

Xanthe's breath went shallow. Not because the words frightened her. Because she recognized them. Three nights ago, in the vision that came up through the hemlock like smoke through floorboards, she had stood in a kitchen exactly like the one the boy was describing—a kitchen with a ninth plate set at a table for eight—and she had heard a woman say those exact words. *You never know who is walking home.*

Not similar. Exact. The same voice, coming out of a sleeping child's mouth.

She should have walked away. Every instinct she had honed over twenty years of surviving said walk away: invisible people do not kneel beside miracles, invisible people do not touch the things the gods are using. Attention was death. She had built her whole life on that arithmetic.

But the hook behind her ribs—the one that had gone in the night Marta's prayer was answered, the one she'd pretended for weeks was indigestion—pulled. Gently. Like a fisherman testing a line.

She reached out and touched the boy's shoulder.

His eyes opened. Not all the way—half-lidded, dream-heavy—but open, and focused on her with a clarity that no sleeping child should have. And then he spoke again, awake now, still in Vhalorian, and this time she understood him without understanding how:

*"You came. I drew your door but you didn't come, so I kept drawing it."*

"I don't—" Xanthe started, in the valley tongue, and heard her own voice come out strange, flattened, as though she were speaking through cloth.

The boy frowned at her, patient the way children are patient with adults who are slow. Then he rolled over, dug beneath his pallet with the practiced efficiency of someone who hides things for a living, and produced a scrap of paper folded into eighths, soft as cloth from handling.

He unfolded it and held it out to her.

It was a drawing. Charcoal, probably stolen from the pastry section where the cook sharpened sticks for marking loaves. A doorway. An arched stone doorframe, drawn with the obsessive precision of a child who has looked at something until he memorized it—every stone courses marked, every joint shaded, and around the frame, radiating outward like heat shimmer, dozens of fine lines that might have been light or might have been the strokes of a word written so large it became weather.

And inside the doorway: a hearth. Fire drawn in quick confident strokes. A kettle on a hook. A table beyond, seen through the door, set with plates—one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine.

Xanthe stared at it and felt the floor tilt.

Because she knew this door. She had been seeing it for two weeks, every time the poisons took her under—hemlock, foxglove, the bitter blue extract she sipped each dawn to keep her blood a hostile country to anything foreign. The visions had started as flavors and become places, and the place they always ended was this: a stone archway, firelight spilling through it, and on the far side a country that had been unmade a hundred years ago, whole and warm and waiting. She had assumed the door was in Vhaloria. Of course it was in Vhaloria. Everything about the visions was Vhalorian—the kitchens, the markets, the snow on iron roofs, the smell of bread she had no name for.

But the boy had drawn it from the other side. She could tell by the perspective. He had drawn it the way you draw something standing in front of it, here, in this world—and the hearth inside the door was not a Vhalorian hearth. It was a palace hearth. She knew the shape of that firebox; she'd warmed her hands over a dozen just like it. The kettle hook was palace ironwork. The stones of the arch were the same gray granite as the scullery wall three feet behind him.

The door wasn't in Vhaloria.

The door was here. In the kitchens. Under the palace. Maybe ten yards from where she knelt.

"Where did you see this?" she asked, and her voice cracked in the middle of the question like an egg.

The boy looked at her for a long moment. Then he pointed—not at the wall, not at the hearth, but at his own chest. And then, after a pause, at hers.

* * *

Sef did not remember arriving at the palace. This was the first thing he told her, once he'd decided to talk—which took three nights of her bringing him bread ends and dried apple and sitting on the floor beside his pallet while the kitchen slept, saying nothing, asking nothing, until trust arrived the way it does with feral things: suddenly, completely, and on its own schedule.

He remembered a farm. He remembered a woman who sang while she cooked, and a language that made his mouth feel right when he spoke it, and a table with a ninth plate. He remembered a night of white light and a sound like the sky clearing its throat, and then he remembered nothing for a long time, and then he was on a cart with turnips, and everyone in the valley spoke the flat heavy tongue that made his mouth feel like a borrowed coat.

"You speak it in your sleep," Xanthe said. "Every night?"

"Most nights." He shrugged, a small sharp motion. "Grandmother says dreams are where the kept things live."

"Who is grandmother?"

"The one who sings." He said it simply, without confusion, and Xanthe understood that he meant a dead woman—or a woman from a country that was dead, which amounted to the same thing and also did not—and that this distinction troubled him not at all. "She talks to me when I'm asleep. She says the door is almost ready. She says someone has to open it from the waking side, and it can't be her, because she's on the wrong side of the flour."

"The wrong side of the flour."

"That's what she says." He smiled, gap-toothed, entirely unbothered. "I think it means she's dead. But she doesn't say it like that, so I don't say it like that either."

Xanthe sat with that. Around them the scullery ticked and settled, copper cooling, banked fires breathing. Somewhere above them the palace slept its magic-dead sleep, safe and sealed and certain of itself, and here in the warm dark a child was taking dictation from the unmade dead.

"Why me?" she asked finally. "You said I came. You said you drew my door. Why mine?"

Sef considered her with those too-serious eyes. "You taste things," he said.

"How do you know that?"

"Grandmother knows. Grandmother says the taster swallows the country a little bit at a time, and a swallowed thing can be carried." He tapped the folded drawing against his knee. "She says most people are walls. You're a door that learned to be a wall. Doors remember."

The hook behind her ribs pulled hard enough that she put her hand flat against her sternum, an unconscious gesture, and the boy watched her do it and nodded as if she'd confirmed something on a list.

"It hurts?" he asked.

"Like a stitch. Like a fishhook. It comes and goes."

"It'll pull harder before the moon turns." He said it the way he'd say *it'll rain before harvest*. "Grandmother says the thirteenth moon is the hinge. She says the god has been polite for a hundred years and politeness runs out."

"Sef." She leaned closer, keeping her voice under the sound of the cooling fires. "The god. What does grandmother call it?"

"Vhal." He pronounced it with a care that transformed it—two syllables that in any other mouth would be nothing, and in his were a bell rung once in an empty church. "Vhal the Host. Vhal Who Sets the Table. He fed everybody, grandmother says, before the other gods got jealous and unmade the pantry." He paused. "He's the one pulling behind your ribs. He does that when he wants something carried. Grandmother says he's very gentle about it. She says that's the worst part."

* * *

On the fourth night, Xanthe brought him honey cake stolen from the king's own dessert service, and Sef laughed for the first time since she'd known him—a startling, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years—and told her his real name, or the name that fit his real mouth: Sevvel. *Sef* was what the kitchen called him, a flattening, a translation, the way the valley flattened everything it absorbed. Sevvel. He said it twice, tasting it, and then said he hadn't said it aloud in eleven months because there was no one to say it to.

Two invisible people, recognizing each other across a scullery floor. She had been sold to the palace at seven. He had been carried in at seven, or near enough. Both of them had learned the same first lesson: survive by being unnoticeable, by being furniture, by being a flavor no one thinks to spit out. She had built a fortress out of invisibility. He had built a smaller one, and kept a window in it, and through the window came a singing dead woman and a hungry courteous god and a door drawn in charcoal that matched, stroke for stroke, the archway that haunted her poisoned sleep.

That night she let herself be tasted by the hemlock—skipped the antidote draught, lay down on her narrow taster's cot, and let the vision come all the way in for the first time instead of fighting it to the surface.

She walked through the door.

Not toward it. Through it. The stone passed over her skin like cool water, and on the other side was Vhaloria: a market street under a violet dusk, stalls lit with oil lamps, a baker pulling loaves, children running chalk games between the cobbles. Whole. Warm. Utterly real. And everywhere, threaded through it, the sense of a held breath—a country paused mid-motion, waiting a hundred years for someone to finish the sentence that had cut it off.

At the end of the street stood a figure. Tall, robed in colors she had no names for, face kind and unreadable, holding out a plate the way a host holds out a plate: with courtesy, with patience, with a hunger underneath so vast it made the sky look shallow.

*You swallow well,* the god said, in a voice like a cellar full of wine. *Come. There is a table. There has always been a table.*

Behind her ribs, the hook pulled.

She woke with salt on her lips and Sef's drawing clutched in her hand, though she had no memory of fetching it, and outside the narrow window the moon hung over the palace roofs—swollen, wrong-colored, a hair past full and climbing toward a fullness no calendar owned.

Thirteen moons. Twelve gone.

The thirteenth was nearly here, and somewhere below her cot, in the warm dark of the scullery, a child slept and spoke fluent Vhalorian to the dead, and a door waited in a hearth, and a god who set tables was running out of patience.

In the morning, the palace priest would come down to inspect the kitchens. Xanthe didn't know that yet.

But the god did.
