## 7. Poison as Prayer

The priest came down to the kitchens at dawn, which was itself an omen. Father Anselm had not descended below the second floor in eleven years; the palace kitchens were, in his view, a place where holiness went to be rendered into fat.

He arrived with two temple guards and a censer, and he walked the long stone aisle between the ovens like a man inspecting a stable for rot.

Xanthe saw him first from behind the stockpots, where she was skimming broth with the particular stillness that had kept her alive for twenty years. She catalogued him the way she catalogued everything: height, weight, the tremor in his left hand, the smell of incense masking something underneath — sweat, fear, and under that, faintly, snow and iron.

He'd been tasting it too, then. Whatever the king ate at supper, the priest tasted in his prayers.

"Which of you," Anselm said, not loudly, "has been praying to the old gods?"

No one answered. Thirty cooks and scullions became very interested in their work. The broth steamed. Somewhere a kettle ticked as it cooled.

"Because something has been *answering*," the priest said, "and I would very much like to know what you have been feeding it."

Xanthe kept skimming. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady — that was the whole architecture of her survival, hands that did not shake, eyes that did not lift, a face so unremarkable that guards forgot her at checkpoints and stewards wrote her name wrong on the rosters. *Fairweather.* Even her surname was a shrug.

But Father Anselm stopped walking three feet from her, and turned, and looked at her the way she had spent a lifetime teaching people not to.

"You," he said.

She set down the ladle. "Father."

"You taste the king's food."

"Every dish."

"And how is your health?"

It was such an unexpected question that for one full second she answered honestly. "Excellent."

"Yes," said Anselm softly. "That's what I thought." He lifted the censer, and the smoke came out wrong — it bent toward her, curling against the draft like water finding a drain. The nearest cook dropped a pan. "Come here, child. Stand where I can see you."

Every instinct Xanthe owned screamed at her to be small. She had been small for twenty years. Smallness was armor; invisibility was a craft she had practiced longer than any poison. But the smoke was bending toward her ribs, toward the hook behind them, and she understood with a cold clarity that hiding had just become impossible — that whatever lived inside her now was too loud to be quieted by posture.

She stepped out from behind the stockpots.

Anselm circled her slowly. He did not touch her. He looked at her mouth, of all things, then at her hands, then pressed his thumb briefly against her wrist and went pale.

"When did they start?" he asked.

"When did what start."

"The visions."

The kitchen had gone silent enough to hear the ovens breathe. Xanthe weighed her options the way she weighed doses: this much truth, no more. "I see kitchens," she said. "Kitchens that aren't ours. People eating. A language I don't know."

"Vhalorian," said Anselm, and crossed himself with a hand that shook. "You see Vhaloria."

"It doesn't exist."

"No," the priest agreed. "And yet here you stand, full of it. Child, do you know what you are?" He didn't wait for her answer. He turned to the guards with something close to joy, which was the worst thing Xanthe had seen all morning. "Send word to His Majesty. We have found a vessel. A clean vessel — a body trained to hold what would kill any other. God's mercy, look at it. Twenty years of poison, and it stands upright and *skims broth*."

"I'm not a vessel," Xanthe said. "I'm a taster."

"You were both," said Anselm. "You simply hadn't been told about the second half of your employment."

They took her that afternoon. Not to a cell — to a room. That was the palace's way: everything upholstered, everything polite. They moved her to a chamber in the east wing with a bolted shutter, a soft bed, and a guard outside the door who was instructed to let in only Father Anselm and the king's steward. There was even a tray of food, which she refused on principle, and which sat there smelling of snow and iron until a servant removed it untouched.

Through the bolted shutter, in slivers, she watched the moon rise.

She lay on the bed and did the arithmetic she should have done years ago.

Here was the thing about invisibility that no one tells you: it only works while nothing wants you. For twenty years she had been safe because she was worthless — a tool, a function, a girl-shaped spoon. The moment she became valuable, every wall she had built became a cage built to spec. Staying hadn't been safety. Staying had been the dangerous choice all along, and she had made it daily, with every measured spoonful, telling herself that a body which survived everything must surely survive this too.

But she had never survived being *wanted* before. No immunity covered it.

Behind her ribs, the hook shifted, patient as tide.

*You could open,* it did not say, in a voice that was not a voice, because gods who set tables had manners. *You could stop fighting the flavor.*

She got up and pressed her eye to the shutter's gap. Below, across the courtyard, the scullery door stood open to the evening air. Sef would be asleep in the warm dark by now, or pretending to sleep, listening with that terrible fluent ear.

And deeper in — past the scullery, down the archive stairs, behind a door the palace had locked and forgotten — a man who drew maps that redrew themselves.

Xanthe sat back on the bed and, for the first time in her adult life, planned something irreversible.

* * *

The lock on her door took four minutes.

Not because she was clever — because the palace was lazy. Tasters were not escape risks; tasters were furniture, and furniture does not pick locks. The guard outside was armed with a pike and boredom, and boredom, Xanthe knew from twenty years of study, is the most poisonable substance in any palace. She waited until his stomach growled — she had heard it growl twice already, marking his habits the way she marked symptoms — and then she knocked softly and told him the priest had ordered her brought to the chapel for evening rites, and when he opened the door to escort her, she hit him in the throat with the edge of her hand, precisely, clinically, the way she dosed.

She felt briefly, absurdly proud of herself. Fourteenth poison, fifteenth skill.

Then she ran.

The palace at moonrise was all long shadows and sleeping stone, and she moved through it along routes no one maps because no one important walks them: the servants' stair behind the buttery, the passage through the linen store, the low arch into the scullery where the embers of the hearth still breathed orange. The same hearth. Of course the same hearth. She did not let herself look at it too long.

Sef was exactly where she'd known he'd be, curled in the warmth by the ash bin, and he woke the instant she touched his shoulder — kitchen children sleep light — and looked up at her without surprise, as if he'd dreamed this part already.

"They're going to use me," Xanthe whispered. "The priest. The king. As a door."

Sef considered this with the gravity of the very young and the very old. "You shouldn't be a door," he said. "Doors get broken."

"That's roughly my thinking. Can you run?"

"In my sleep I can run all night."

"Then stay awake and do it anyway."

She took his hand. It was the first time she had ever taken anyone's hand on purpose, for reasons other than steadying a cup or receiving a document, and the strangeness of it nearly undid her more than the running did.

The archive stairs were below the record rooms, and the lock there was real iron, old work, but Ilya Vosk had been eleven years behind that door and had told her once, in the flat voice of a man describing his own execution, exactly where the palace's attention had rusted through. She had come down two nights ago with a stolen file wrapped in her apron and worked at it in the dark while he watched through the grate and said nothing, because men who map erased countries understand patience.

Tonight the file found its groove. Tonight the lock turned.

Ilya stepped out into torchlight looking less like a freed prisoner than a man stepping off a ship after a long voyage — blinking, unsteady, taking inventory of a world that had changed shape. He was thinner than his charts suggested. His hands, those precise instruments, were shaking.

"You're the taster," he said. "The one who watches."

"I'm the one leaving," Xanthe said. "Are you coming?"

"Where?"

"Out. Over the boundary. To find the door before the god finds me."

Something moved through his face — hope, that bitter old tenant, coming home after eleven years and finding the house unlocked. "You'll die out there," he said. "Your immunities — some of them aren't real, taster. Some of them were the valley keeping you alive, not your body. Cross the line and—"

"Stay here," Xanthe said, "and become a doorway. I've done the arithmetic, cartographer. Both answers kill. Only one of them is mine."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached back into his cell and came out with a leather tube strapped under his cot — his charts, his prayers, his whole confiscated country — and slung it across his back.

"Sef," he said, crouching to the child's level with the care of a man handling a live coal. "Do you remember me? From the kitchen. I draw maps."

"You draw our house," Sef said.

Ilya's breath caught audibly. He did not ask which house. He stood, and looked at Xanthe, and nodded once, and that was the whole ceremony.

* * *

The scullery gate opened onto the kitchen garden, and the garden onto the cliff path, and the cliff path down toward the valley floor where the boundary waited — a line no one drew, that everyone obeyed, where the magic-dead stillness ended and the world began again.

They went fast and quiet, three shapes in the dark: a woman whose body was a pharmacy, a man carrying his homeland in a tube, a child who spoke to the dead in his sleep. Behind them, faintly, a bell began to ring — not the chapel bell, not yet the alarm, but some third bell Xanthe didn't know, deep-toned, patient, ringing from a tower that she was fairly sure had no bells in it.

Behind her ribs, the hook pulled — harder now, offended.

*Vessel,* said the voice that was not a voice. *Where are you going with my door?*

"Not yours," Xanthe said aloud, and Sef squeezed her hand, and Ilya lengthened his stride without asking why she was talking to the air.

At the bottom of the path the valley opened silver under the climbing thirteenth moon, and far ahead, where the fields gave out and the fused sand began, the Glass Steppe caught the moonlight and threw it back flat and white — a mirror lying face-up on the world, reflecting not the night sky above it but something older, warmer, lit: windows, rooftops, a country mid-meal.

Xanthe stopped at the last row of the kitchen garden and looked back once at the palace, black and solid and safe, the only home her body had ever known, the place that had fed her poisons like a mother feeds milk.

Then she looked down at Sef's hand in hers, and forward at the shining plain, and felt the terror and the exhilaration arrive together in her chest like twin flavors of the same draught — the first irreversible act of her life, still on her tongue, still dissolving.

"Whatever happens," she said, "nobody tastes anything first."

And the three of them stepped off the garden's edge, onto the sand that remembered, as somewhere behind them the third bell rang louder, and the door it guarded swung open in the dark.
