## 8. The Edge of the Valley

I had spent eleven years drawing lines for a living, so you would think I'd have known better than to trust one.

Every border I ever inked was a lie agreed upon. Rivers wander. Mountains are matters of opinion. The line between Vhaloria and the empire that ate it existed only because a cartographer with steady hands and a coward's heart decided it should—and I say that with authority, because I was that cartographer, or his ghost wearing his hands.

But the valley's edge was different. The valley's edge was honest.

You could see it from half a mile off, which was the first strange thing, because nothing else about that night had been visible. We had walked since moonrise through orchards gone wild and fields gone fallow, and the dark had been the ordinary kind—the kind that hides things from you. Then the trees simply stopped, all at once, in a line so straight it looked ruled, and beyond them the land opened into moonlit grass that bent in a wind I couldn't feel on my face. And above that grass, the stars were wrong.

Not missing. Wrong. On our side of the line they burned white, the way stars had burned my whole life, steady as nails hammered into black cloth. Past the boundary they flickered—each one stuttering like a candle in a draft, like something breathing.

"Stop." Xanthe said it quietly, but Sef stopped as if she'd shouted, and so did I, which told me more about her than any confession had.

She stood at the edge of the orchard with the boy's hand in hers and studied the line the way she must have studied the king's supper: cataloguing, weighing, deciding what could kill her tonight. She had been doing it since the scullery gate. Since before that, if I'm honest—she had been tasting her own escape the way she tasted everything, one measured spoonful at a time, checking it for poison.

I knew what waited past that line because I'd mapped its rumor a dozen times. Every chart in the royal archive carried the same annotation at the valley's edge, written by hands long dead: *HIC FINIS MAGICAE*—here magic ends—as if you could end a thing with Latin. As if you could fence it out like wolves. For a hundred years the valley had sat inside its dead zone like a coin at the bottom of a well, and everything old and hungry in the world had pressed against that stillness and found it closed to them.

Including, until three nights ago, the gods.

"They can't follow us across," Sef said. He wasn't asking. Children who talk in their sleep in dead languages develop a certain authority about such things. "Vhal can't cross."

"No," Xanthe agreed.

"But we can cross back," he said. "If we want to. Anyone can walk in. It's only walking *out* that—" He stopped, frowning at his own knowledge, at the place in him it came from. He was nine. He shouldn't have known the grammar of boundaries. His mouth kept moving anyway, translating for a country that no longer existed. "It's not fair the other way. Doors always open inward."

I wrote that down. Old habit. My fingers were already stiff with cold and I had no ink, so I scratched it into the soft bark of the last apple tree with a pin from my cloak—*doors open inward*—because some part of me had understood, standing in my cell for eleven years, that the maps were never the work. The notes were the work. The margins were where the truth went to hide.

Xanthe watched me do it. She didn't ask why. That was another thing I was learning about her: she didn't ask questions she could taste the answer to.

"You're hesitating," I said.

"I'm assessing."

"Those aren't different words."

"They are to me." She crouched suddenly, and I thought she was going to be sick—she'd been gray around the mouth since midnight—but instead she pulled a small tin from her bodice, opened it, and touched one fingertip to her tongue. Whatever she tasted there made her close her eyes.

"What is it?" Sef asked.

"My pulse." She held up the fingertip. A grain of powder, pale as salt. "Ash-of-foxglove. I take it every third day to slow my heart against cardiac poisons. If I miss a dose my body forgets the rhythm." She looked out at the flickering stars. "That's the problem none of you understand. None of my immunities are mine. They're borrowed. Half of them were never chemistry at all—they were the valley. The stillness holding my blood steady, holding the toxins quiet, holding *me* quiet. Out there—" she nodded at the grass, at the breathing stars, "—out there the stillness ends. And everything I've swallowed for thirty years wakes up."

The wind moved the grass beyond the line. None of it reached us. Even the air respected the border.

"And if you don't cross?" I asked, though I knew. We all knew. Behind us, far back toward the palace, the sky had a color in it that wasn't weather—a pressure, a gathering, the thirteenth moon dragging its reflection across the world like a net. The god was patient the way hunger is patient. Staying meant being found, and being found meant becoming a doorway, and I had seen what a doorway looks like after the god has finished using it. There were sketches in my confiscated notebooks. I had drawn them myself, from memory, before I understood whose memory.

Xanthe stood. She brushed the dirt from her knees with two precise strokes, the gesture of a woman tidying herself for an execution or a wedding, and I realized those might be the same gesture.

"I've been poisoned every day for thirty years," she said. "On purpose. By my own hand, on the king's order, because a body that survives everything is worth more than a body that belongs anywhere." Her voice stayed level. That was the terrible thing about her; the level voice never broke, it just got quieter, and you had to lean in to hear the breaking. "Do you know what I felt when the maid prayed and the burn healed? Nothing. Everyone in that kitchen felt the old power come back like a hook behind the ribs, and I felt *nothing*, because the valley's deadness lives in me too. I am the most protected person in Vhaloria and the least alive. So." She stepped forward to the line, right to the edge of it, close enough that her boot toe nearly touched the last blade of orchard grass. "The cost of safety has finally come due. I'd like to see the bill."

"That's not funny," Sef said.

"I wasn't joking."

"I know. That's how I know it's not funny."

She almost smiled. I saw it happen and un-happen, a tide going out. Then she looked back at us—at the boy, then at me, and there was something in her face I recognized from mirrors in worse years: the expression of someone checking whether the people watching them will remember them kindly.

"If my heart stops over there," she said, "don't drag me back across. Whatever comes out of me when the stillness leaves—it isn't me anymore. Promise me."

"Don't make him promise that," Sef said sharply. "Don't make anyone promise that."

"Sef—"

"He'll promise wrong. He'll promise and then do the other thing. Grown-ups always do the other thing." The boy's chin was up and his eyes were wet and furious, and he sounded, in that moment, exactly like the grandmother he had never met. "You don't get to order your own dying. You're the one who cooks. Cooks don't get to leave the table."

Silence. Somewhere behind us, very far away, thunder rolled without clouds—or something that wanted us to think it was thunder.

Xanthe looked at him for a long time. Then she took her boot toe and drew it once through the boundary line itself, disturbing the grass, breaking the rule, and stepped back.

"All right," she said. "Nobody promises anything. We cross together, and whatever happens happens to all three of us at once, so no one has to be brave alone. That's my final term. Take it or walk home to the king."

"It's a terrible term," I said. "It means if you die, I die too."

"Yes."

"Fine." Eleven years in a cell teaches you to recognize a good bargain when it arrives wearing bad terms. "Fine. Together."

Sef put one hand in hers and one in mine. His palms were damp and small and gripped like a drowning man's, and I thought: *this is what I fled Vhaloria without. This is the weight I was smuggled out of the country too light.* Not this boy specifically—he hadn't been born—but this. Hands. Someone to carry across a line.

"On three," Xanthe said.

We went on four, because none of us trusted each other enough yet to go on three, and that seemed right somehow. That seemed like the beginning of trust rather than the absence of it.

* * *

The crossing took less than a second and lasted approximately forever.

There was no wall, no shimmer, no sound. One stride I was in the valley's held breath and the next I was out of it, and the difference hit me like falling into a river in April—I wasn't even the one crossing, I just felt the shock radiating off the other two.

For Xanthe, it was otherwise.

She made it three steps onto the open grass before her knees went. I caught her under the arms and lowered her down, and what came out of her was not a scream. It was worse. It was a sound like a woman remembering every meal she had ever survived, all at once, in alphabetical order.

Because that's what happened. Everything woke up.

I knelt beside her on the glass-cold grass and watched thirty years of poison come due in the space of ten minutes. Her skin flushed and paled and flushed again, chasing colors like a man chasing a thief. Her pupils blew wide, then pinned to points. Sweat stood on her forehead and froze there in beads. And under her skin—Sef saw it first, pointed with a child's terrible clarity—things *moved*. Shadows in her veins, shifting, restless, waking from their long chemical sleep now that the valley's dead palm was lifted off them. Magic-adjacent, she'd called some of her immunities. Borrowed. Now the lenders had come to collect, and the debt was being paid out through her body in full public view.

"Hemlock," she gasped. Listing. Cataloguing. Even now. "That's hemlock waking. Cold in the teeth. And that's—ah—that's warrowroot, that burns low, that sits in the belly like a coal. And that one I never learned the name of, the king's father's taster died of it, they gave me the immunity by—" her back arched, "—by *other means*—"

"Stop talking," Sef said. "Save your breath."

"Talking is triage." Another shudder went through her, head to heel, and her teeth clicked. "If I name them they stay ingredients. If I don't name them they become... weather."

So she named them. All of them. Lying on frozen grass under flickering stars while a disgraced mapmaker held her shoulders and a kitchen orphan counted her breaths aloud—fourteen poisons, fifteen, twenty, names I didn't know and some I suspect had no names outside the palace kitchens—she recited her own body like a menu, and slowly, slowly, the convulsions spaced themselves out.

And underneath the naming, underneath the shaking, something else was happening. I felt it before I understood it, because I was born on the other side of this line and my bones remembered the grammar even when my mind had lost the vocabulary. The old power was in the ground here. In the grass, in the wind, in the stuttering stars. And it was touching her—not the way the god's hook had touched her, not a hook at all, more like water finding a dry channel, running into every crack that thirty years of swallowing had carved in her, filling her up.

When she finally opened her eyes, they were wet, and she was laughing. Quietly. Horribly. The laugh of a woman reading her own bill and finding it itemized correctly.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, I can *taste the air*. Do you know what the air tastes like out here? It tastes like the first course of something enormous."

"That's not comforting," Sef said.

"It isn't meant to be. It's meant to be accurate." She let me help her upright. Her legs held, barely. She swayed, caught herself, and stood there trembling in the wind she could finally feel on her face, and I watched her take inventory of her own ruin with the same clinical calm she'd used on the king's supper, and I understood that we had not freed her by bringing her here.

We had *unmade* her. Taster, vessel, weapon, ghost—all the safe shapes of her, dissolved in one stride. What was left was a woman standing in the open, unprotected, tasting everything, belonging nowhere, alive in a way that clearly hurt.

She turned to look back at the valley once. Just once. The orchard stood black and silent behind the invisible line, the palace somewhere beyond it with its candles and its kneeling servants and its thirteenth moon rising over the towers, and I saw her take it in the way you memorize a house you've sold.

Then Sef tugged her sleeve and pointed east, across the open dark, and we all looked.

Far out on the plain—too far to be real, too clear to be imagined—glass caught the moonlight and threw it back. Miles of it. A horizon that gleamed like a frozen sea, flat and bright and waiting, reflecting stars that weren't in the sky above it.

"The Steppe," I said. My voice came out rusty. "That's it. That's where they unmade it."

Xanthe wiped her face with both hands, once, efficiently, the gesture of a woman closing a ledger.

"Then we walk," she said, and took the first step.

Behind us, far away, the false thunder rolled again—and this time, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind across the boundary that was supposed to keep everything out, came the smell of roasting meat, and honey, and a dinner being set for three.
