## 9. The Glass Steppe

The valley ended the way a sentence ends—not with a wall, not with a gate, but with a period. One step there was grass and wind and Ilya's muttered complaints about his boots; the next step was glass.

I felt it through my soles before I saw it: a smoothness so complete it seemed to hum against my skin, like standing on the surface of a frozen lake that had never known a crack. We had crossed the boundary at dawn, three days ago, and I had spent those days learning how much of my body had been built on lies. Now we stood at the edge of the place where Vhaloria had been unmade, and the ground did not welcome us. It simply acknowledged us, the way a mirror acknowledges a face.

"Gods," Ilya said quietly. He hadn't sworn in three days of walking. He saved his swearing for moments that deserved it, which told me something about what he was looking at.

I looked too.

The steppe stretched to every horizon at once—a plain of fused sand, pale as bone ash in the morning light, flat as poured wax. Not dunes. Not stone. Glass, or something older than glass, fused by whatever force had erased a nation from the world and left only its footprint behind. And in that glass—

In that glass was Vhaloria.

Not ruins. Not bones. The country itself, whole and living beneath our feet, reflected with a clarity no water had ever managed. Rooftops of blue tile. Orchards heavy with fruit that cast shadows even though the sky above held nothing but pale morning. A river running silver between fields, barges drifting down it, laundry strung from balconies snapping in a breeze I could not feel on my own skin. Smoke rising from chimneys. People—small and distant and utterly indifferent to us—walking streets that existed nowhere except below.

"They're moving," Sef said.

He had gone still in the way he went still when one of his sleeping memories woke up inside him with his eyes open. He crouched, pressed both palms flat to the glass, and his face did something complicated—joy and grief fighting for the same real estate.

"That's Maro Street," he said. "The baker puts the walnut loaves out at this hour. You can smell them if you stand near the fountain."

I couldn't smell anything. But I looked where he pointed, down through the impossible depth of the reflection, and there was a fountain, and near the fountain a bakery window, and in the window a tray of dark loaves, and beside the tray an old woman arranging them with flour-dusted hands.

"She can't see us," Ilya said. It wasn't a question. He'd been a cartographer long enough to know the difference between a map and a country, and everything in his voice said he understood, before any of us said it aloud, exactly what this place was.

"No," Sef agreed. "She's not *there* anymore." He said it the way children say terrible things, plainly, without flinching, because they haven't yet learned that flinching is expected. "But she's not gone either."

We walked out onto the sky of a dead nation.

* * *

There was no road, because roads are for places that have somewhere to go. There was only the direction Sef pointed, and the strange etiquette of walking on a reflection without disturbing it.

That last part proved unnecessary. Our boots made no sound. No mark. When I glanced back after an hour, the steppe behind us showed nothing—no footprints, no sign that three small lives had crossed it. We were not guests here. We were not even trespassers. We were simply irrelevant, ghosts passing over a portrait of the living.

I hated it more than I can easily explain.

I had spent twenty years making myself unremarkable—the taster who moved through kitchens like furniture, whose name servants forgot and kings never learned. Invisibility had been my craft, my armor, my single great talent. And now the land itself had rendered me invisible, and I discovered that invisibility chosen and invisibility imposed are different animals entirely. One is a shield. The other is being told, by an entire country's absence, that you do not weigh enough to leave a trace.

"Tell me about her," I said to Sef, because silence was worse. "The baker."

He walked with his hands in his pockets, kicking at nothing. "Her name's Tamsin. She lost two sons in the war Vhaloria didn't fight." He frowned, correcting himself mid-thought, the words coming from somewhere deeper than his own understanding. "No—the war Vhaloria wasn't allowed to fight. She kept baking anyway. Grandmother said grief needs somewhere to put its hands."

"Your grandmother," Ilya said carefully, "was Vhalorian."

"Everyone says that like it's strange." Sef shrugged. "It's just true. Like saying someone's tall."

It was not just true. It was impossible. Vhaloria had been erased a hundred years ago—unmade, the histories said, though none of them explained what unmaking meant, because the ones who ordered it had also burned the explanations. No teachers survived. No speakers. The language lived nowhere, in no mouth, in no book. Except in the mouth of an eight-year-old orphan who slept in palace sculleries and dreamed in a dead tongue, and in the memory of a disgraced mapmaker who had confessed, two nights ago by a fire, that his charts were not research but recollection—that he had been born under these reflected rooftops and smuggled out before he could walk.

Two people carrying a country between them, neither old enough to have earned the burden.

"You're limping," I noticed, watching Ilya.

"I'm favoring my left boot. There's a distinction, and it matters to me personally." But he let me take his arm, which was its own kind of confession. Three days past the boundary, and the cartographer—who had mapped half the world from prison cells and archives—needed steadying on perfectly flat ground. None of us said what we were all thinking: that flatness this total is its own kind of vertigo. That a horizon with nothing on it makes you doubt your own eyes.

By noon—or what I judged to be noon, since the sun here moved strangely, hesitating at its zenith as if reluctant to commit—the reflection changed.

* * *

The rooftops gave way to open country, and the open country gave way to a city. And the city was burning.

Sef stopped walking so abruptly that I nearly went past him. Ilya caught my elbow; I caught my breath. Below us, rendered in perfect detail across ten miles of glass, Vhaloria's capital stood wrapped in flame—not the honest orange of ordinary fire but a white heat, a heat that ate color, that turned stone to smoke and smoke to nothing. Streets emptied. Towers leaned and folded. And everywhere, everywhere, people running—not toward the flames but away from something we could not see, faces tilted upward toward a sky that held—

"The thirteenth moon," Ilya whispered.

It hung above the burning city in the reflection, vast and close, far larger than any moon has a right to be, and it was wrong in a way my mind kept trying to correct and failing. Too many phases at once. Full and crescent and new, all visible simultaneously, like a coin spinning so fast it shows all its faces. Underneath it, the white fire moved like something alive. Like something feeding.

"It's not a memory," Sef said. His voice had gone thin. "Memories don't move."

He was right. That was the horror of it, the thing my taster's mind—trained to notice what doesn't fit—took several full minutes to accept. The baker had been still in the way paintings are still, alive but fixed. This was happening. Or it happened once and refused to stop happening, replaying itself across the glass the way a wound replays its hurting.

A hundred years, and Vhaloria was still falling.

"We should move," Ilya said. "We shouldn't watch this."

"We should watch this," Sef said, with a child's merciless clarity. "Nobody else remembers it. If we look away, then nobody's looking at all."

So we watched. All three of us, standing on the sky while a country died underneath it, again, forever. I found myself cataloguing details with the clinical calm I used for poisons—the angle of a collapsing bridge, the way the white light pooled in low streets like liquid—which is how I survive things, which is how I have always survived things, and which suddenly felt like the most obscene skill I owned. Somewhere in the middle of it, my hand found Sef's shoulder and stayed there. I don't know which of us that was for.

When the reflection finally dimmed—when the white fire guttered down to embers and the embers to darkness, and the dark city lay quiet under its wrong moon—I realized my cheeks were wet.

"Grief for a home none of us had," Ilya said softly. He was crying too, and not hiding it. "That's what this place does. It's not asking permission. It's just handing it to you."

"Maybe it's not doing anything," I said. "Maybe we're finally somewhere honest."

* * *

We camped that night on the glass, because there was nowhere else. Ilya rigged a shelter from oilcloth and cartographer's stubbornness. Sef slept the moment he stopped moving, the deep sleep of children and soldiers, and within minutes he began speaking Vhalorian in low, fluent murmurs—conversational, domestic, a boy talking to someone about bread or weather or love. I listened without understanding a word and understood all of it anyway.

I sat up instead of sleeping. Partly watchfulness—we were exposed on an endless plain, and a god with a hook behind my ribs knew roughly where we were. Partly because sleep, lately, had become unreliable territory. Since the boundary, my body had been purging itself of twenty years of deliberate poisoning, and what came up came with fragments: hemlock dreams, foxglove colors, tastes I couldn't name arriving on my tongue like messages.

Tonight the taste arrived while I was fully awake.

Snow and iron. The same flavors that had haunted the king's supper weeks ago, the first crack in my careful invisibility—but stronger now, closer to their source, and underneath them something else: woodsmoke, caraway, honey darkened with spice, and a warmth I could only call *kitchen*. A specific kitchen. I could see it with my eyes closed: low ceiling, copper pots, a table worn pale at two spots where generations of elbows had rested. A woman humming off-key. Bread proving under cloth.

I opened my eyes and looked down.

Directly beneath me, forty feet of glass away, the kitchen glowed. My kitchen. Not mine—never mine, I had been sold to a palace at seven years old and had never once cooked in a room that belonged to me—but the kitchen from the vision, warm and lit and occupied. The woman at the table looked up, straight at me, through a hundred years and a country's death, and she smiled, and she gestured at the empty chair across from her the way you gesture at family.

Then the reflection rippled, and she was gone, and I was alone on the cold glass with the taste of someone's home-cooking dissolving on my tongue like an apology.

I did not cry. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger of my life: I did not cry. But I sat very still for a long time, holding my own arms, grieving—with full knowledge of how ridiculous it was—for a kitchen I had invented, in a country that had been murdered before my grandmother was born, for a chair offered by a woman who had never existed in my lifetime and would never exist in any lifetime I touched.

This, I thought, is what Vhal did to the survivors. Not fire. Not flood. Just this: homesickness with no home attached, grief with no grave, a hunger that no meal in the living world could answer. A god who starves a century of people and then waits politely for them to come looking for the table.

Behind my ribs, the hook shifted—gently, almost kindly, the way a host adjusts a guest's cushion.

*I can give it to you*, said no voice at all, which is how the truest voices speak. *You know I can. Sit down. Eat.*

"Not yet," I said aloud, and Sef stirred in his sleep, and the night went on.

* * *

Dawn on the steppe was a lie I stopped believing around the second day. The sun rose, technically, but the light fell on glass that gave nothing back except Vhaloria, eternally morning in the valleys, eternally harvest in the fields, eternally smoke over the capital at the horizon of every glance. Walking across it was like walking through someone else's photo album while they narrated from beyond death.

On the third day of the crossing, Sef began navigating in earnest.

Up until then he'd pointed us generally west and we'd followed, trusting the child's compass of inherited memory. But that morning he climbed a swelling in the glass—there was no swell, the steppe was flat, and yet he climbed it—and stood shading his eyes, and said, "The river forks ahead. We want the north bank. There's a ferryman."

"There's no river," Ilya said. "I've seen no river in three days."

Sef looked at him with pity. "It's under us. Everything's under us. You keep forgetting we're *on top* of the country instead of inside it, and honestly, Uncle Ilya, you're supposed to be the map person."

Uncle Ilya. Neither of them remarked on it. Both of them heard it. I watched the cartographer turn away quickly and pretend to study the horizonless horizon, and I added one more item to the growing inventory of things we were becoming to each other without anyone's permission.

The river fork appeared an hour later—two silver threads converging in the reflection, and at the fork, exactly where Sef said, a ferryman poling his barge through waters that existed only below. North bank. South bank. A village on the north side with a name I couldn't read and Sef pronounced perfectly.

"How far to the door?" I asked him, because someone had to say it. "The door in your drawings. Are we close?"

Sef chewed his lip. The certainty drained out of him slowly, the way it always did when the waking world asked questions his sleeping self answered easily. "I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "The door isn't a place here. Grandma said—" his brow furrowed, pulling words up from a well too deep for him— "Grandma said the door isn't where you go. It's where you're from. So we're not getting closer to it. We're getting further."

Ilya and I exchanged a look over the boy's head. *Further.* We were three days hard-marched onto the Glass Steppe, fleeing a god and a king, following a vision and a child's dream, and the destination might be behind us. Might be the palace kitchens themselves, the hearth where a maid's prayer had cracked the world open. Ilya had said as much, that night of his confession, mapping it out in spilled salt: *the door was never in Vhaloria. Maps point home, Xanthe. That's all maps ever do.*

But we couldn't go back yet. Not across that much glass with a god's patience wearing thin and a moon filling early. And there was something else—something I hadn't said aloud, because saying it might make it true:

Smoke. On the southern edge of the reflection, where no reflected chimney should be, thin gray smoke rose into the actual air. Real smoke. A fire lit by living hands, in the middle of a plain where nothing lived.

Someone was out here. Someone had refused to leave when Vhaloria was unmade, or had found a way to stay, or had come looking—like us—for a country that only existed downward.

Sef saw it at the same moment I did. His whole face changed, the way a locked house changes when someone inside lights a lamp.

"Xanthe," he whispered. "Somebody's cooking."

And God forgive me—after everything, after the burning city and the offered chair and the taste of snow and iron—I was hungry.

We turned south, toward the smoke, walking faster than caution advised, three orphans of one kind or another crossing the sky of a dead nation toward the smell of live fire. Behind us, far behind, the glass ticked. Once. Twice. Like ice settling on a pond. Like something enormous beginning to shift its weight.

Neither of us looked back. We had learned that much about the steppe already: whatever it wanted to show you, it could show you better if you watched.
