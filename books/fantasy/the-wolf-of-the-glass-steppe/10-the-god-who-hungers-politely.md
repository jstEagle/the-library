## 10. The God Who Hungers Politely

The table found them before the god did.

One moment they were walking the glass at dusk—Sef asleep on Ilya's back, Xanthe counting her own heartbeats to keep from counting the cracks spreading behind them—and the next there was linen underfoot instead of mirror. A long table, white cloth, silver that caught a light coming from no sky Xanthe could name. Chairs enough for thirty. Food steaming on every plate: roasted birds glazed dark, bread split and buttered, a soup the color of late honey.

Xanthe's body did what it had been trained to do for twenty years. It catalogued.

Roasted quail—hemlock glaze on three plates, none of it fresh. Bread—safe, or as safe as anything could be out here. Soup—she leaned close without touching, breathed it in. Snow. Iron. Underneath both, something warm and brown that made her eyes sting before she understood why: barley browned in butter, caraway, a bone broth simmered all day by someone who loved whoever was coming home.

She knew that soup. She had never eaten it. She had tasted its memory forty times since the moon turned, floating up through her poisons like sediment through water.

"Vhalorian," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on the second syllable.

"That is correct."

He was simply there when he hadn't been a heartbeat before—a man at the head of the table, standing with his hands folded like a host waiting for guests to finish admiring the room. He looked ordinary, which was the most frightening thing about him. Middle-aged, broad, gray at the temples. An apron, of all things, flour dusting one sleeve. He had the face of someone who cooked for a living and was good at it.

"You must be hungry," he said. "You've walked two days on my steppe and I haven't offered you so much as water. My mother would have been ashamed of me." He smiled, rueful, human. "Sit. Please. Everything here is safe. I would not poison you."

*No,* thought Xanthe, *you'd only ask me to poison myself.*

Ilya set Sef down gently against one of the chairs—the boy didn't wake—and put himself between the god and Xanthe with the stiff formality of a man who had once stood guard over a king's maps and knew exactly how little his body mattered as a barrier.

"You're him," Ilya said. "Vhal."

"I am." The god inclined his head. "And you are Ilya Vosk. The cartographer." Something moved across the host's face—not menace. Tenderness. "Your charts were beautiful. Did anyone ever tell you? Eleven years in a cell, and still your lines came out true. I answered every one of them, you know. Every stroke of ink was a small prayer, and I heard all of them."

Xanthe watched Ilya go very still. She had seen him furious, seen him bitter, seen him laugh once, badly, at his own joke about borders. She had never seen him look like this—as if someone had reached into his chest and turned over everything stored there, carefully, respectfully, the way you turn the pages of a book that belongs to someone else.

"You said—" His voice came out wrong. He started again. "In my letters to the archive. The marginal notes. Those weren't—"

"I read them all," Vhal said gently. "You wrote *let them see it again.* So I am showing them. Sit down, cartographer. Eat. You've earned a meal in the country you drew."

*Don't,* Xanthe wanted to say. *It's a hook dressed as hospitality.* But she knew—Illa knew—that hooks work best on people who want to be caught. And she watched him want it. Watched eleven years of disgrace reach toward one chair at one table in a country that existed nowhere but in his memory.

Sef woke, took one look at the food, and climbed into a chair without a word. Children know when a table is real, whatever else is false about it. He picked up a spoon and looked at Xanthe.

"Taste first?" he asked. It had become their ritual on the road. She checked; he ate.

She pulled out the chair beside him and sat, because refusing outright might end this too soon, and because—she admitted it with clinical honesty, the way she admitted everything to herself—she was curious, and curiosity had always outrun her dread.

The quail was perfect. She tasted hemlock in the glaze, faint, ceremonial, a cook's signature rather than a weapon—Vhalorians had flavored their feast birds with it, she now knew, the way some houses used rosemary—and her trained body dismissed it without a flicker. The bread was still warm. The soup—

The soup undid her.

Not the flavor. The flavor was exactly what her visions had promised. It was the *context*. Someone had made it. Someone had stood at a stove an hour ago, or a century ago, and skimmed the fat and salted it twice and thought, *this will be good for them.* Every meal of Xanthe's life had arrived with a question folded inside it: *is this the one?* This soup arrived asking nothing. It was just food, offered freely, by someone who wanted her fed.

She ate until her throat tightened, then stopped, because crying into the broth seemed like giving something away.

* * *

From the head of the table, Vhal watched the taster eat, and permitted himself, briefly, the shape of joy.

He had worn many bodies in the century since his country was unmade around him. He had been weather, mostly. Wind with opinions. Once, memorably, a well. But he liked being a host best. Hosting was the oldest work of his people: feed the stranger, learn the stranger, make the stranger family. And these three were strangers the way a key is a stranger to a lock—shaped, all along, for a purpose they didn't yet know they carried.

The child was already half-his. Slept in Vhalorian, dreamed in Vhalorian, remembered kitchens he'd never stood in. The cartographer had spent eleven years drawing prayers. And the taster—

Ah, the taster.

She interested him most, because she had done the impossible thing without knowing why. A hundred years of swallowed grief, dish by dish, poison by poison, until her body had become a ledger of everything his country had lost. She had practiced mourning so long she could taste it. She was the fullest vessel ever to walk out of the valley, and she had built herself that way out of fear, discipline, and a hunger to belong somewhere that had never once fed her honestly.

He could give her that. That was the offer, and it was not a trick, and this was the tragedy of it: none of it was a trick. The table was real. The rest was real. The belonging was real. All he asked was the door, and the door needed her hand, and after the door opened she would have a country, a name, a table of her own forever.

That everyone currently living in the valley would become ingredients—well. Countries are expensive. He had learned politeness precisely because hunger, unpolished, frightens people.

"The soup," he said to her, "was my sister's recipe. She browned the barley first. Everyone else in Vhaloria boiled it straight, and she insisted—" he smiled, "—that patience was a flavor."

"It is," Xanthe said quietly. "It's the strongest one."

They looked at each other across the length of the table, hostess and guest, taster and tasted, each recognizing the other's craft.

"You're wondering what this costs," Vhal said.

"I'm a taster," Xanthe said. "Wondering what things cost is the whole profession."

"Then let me be honest, as honest as I have been with your bread." He spread his hands over the table like a man presenting a finished dish. "I want the thirteenth moon. I want my country back. Not as reflection—as *supper*, warm, lit, alive. And to open the way between what was unmade and what was left, I need a living key. Someone who has swallowed a nation's worth of loss and kept walking." His gaze rested on her, kind as lamplight. "There has never been anyone else like you, Xanthe Fairweather. There will never be anyone else at all, if the moon turns full before the door opens."

"And if I say yes?"

"Then you come home. All of you. The child remembers this place; his memories will fit him again. The cartographer's maps stop being treason and start being true. And you—" he leaned forward, and his voice dropped to the register cooks use for secrets, "—you eat a meal that no one has poisoned, in a house where no one needs you to die first. You sit at a table where your seat was saved before you were born. You will never be invisible again. Is that not what invisibility was protecting you from wanting?"

The words went in clean, the way the best toxins do—no burn, no bitterness, nothing to warn the tongue. Xanthe sat with her hands flat on the linen and felt the hook behind her ribs tighten, and hated that it wasn't pain. It felt like being *wanted*. Precisely, specifically, by name. Twenty years of being furniture, and here was a god saying her name like a place setting.

She was still finding her answer when Ilya found his.

"No."

* * *

Ilya had rehearsed refusals for eleven years. In the archive cell, pacing eight steps and turning, he had composed speeches to the king, to the tribunal, to the anonymous clerks who burned his charts. Elegant refusals. Devastating ones.

What came out now was none of those. It was just the word, flat, dropped on the table like a stone into soup.

"She doesn't answer for me," Vhal said pleasantly. "But I'll hear you out. Guests' objections are a kind of seasoning."

"You're feeding her the thing she's starving for and calling it generosity." Ilya stood. His knees were bad from the cell years and they shook, and he let them shake visibly, because he wanted the god to see the cost of standing up. "You want her to open a door that puts your country back on top of mine. Mine. The valley. Three million people who never unmade anything, eating their supper tonight, kneeling to pray to you because you've made forgetting feel holy." He pointed at the boy. Sef had stopped eating, spoon halfway down, watching with the terrible attentiveness of children at adult disasters. "You've already got your hooks in him. Don't dress it up as a dinner party."

"It *is* a dinner party," Vhal said, and for one flickering instant the apron was a shroud and the linen was grave-cloth and the steam off the dishes was the breath of ten thousand throats, and then it was a kitchen again, warm, forgiving. "Grief hosts beautifully, cartographer. You'd know. Your maps wept."

"My maps told the truth," Ilya said. "Here's mine, free of charge, no ink required: she says no."

"I haven't asked her yet."

"You don't have to." Ilya turned to Xanthe, and everything in his face—the bitterness, the irony, the armor he'd worn since his arrest—all of it was gone, leaving something younger and more frightened underneath. "Listen to me. I know what he's offering because he offered it to me first. Eleven years. Every night in that cell, the walls smelled like my grandmother's kitchen. Every chart I drew came back more beautiful than I'd drawn it. He was courting me the whole time, and I *knew*, and I still almost—" His voice broke. He steadied it. "The kindest thing he does is the worst thing he does. Kindness is the bait. It's always the bait."

Vhal sighed, and the sigh moved through the food, cooling every dish a degree.

"You wound me, Ilya Vosk. I have been patient for a hundred years. I have waited through twelve moons of your slow little lives with my hands folded." He rose. The light that came from no sky dimmed as he did, politely, the way a host dims lamps when the evening ends earlier than hoped. "But you're right about one thing. I don't need her answer tonight."

He lifted the soup tureen and refilled Sef's bowl himself, tenderly, a man serving a child.

"I need it before the moon fills. Take the road. Rest when you find the village—you'll find it; I've left it where you can. Eat well. Sleep warm." He smiled at Xanthe last, longest, and it was the smile she would carry the rest of the way across the steppe, worse than any threat, because it was genuinely fond. "The door isn't going anywhere, taster. Neither are you. Grief keeps. I should know—I'm made of it."

The table stayed. The linen, the silver, the untouched second course—all of it stayed, sitting absurdly on the mirrored plain, a dining room abandoned mid-evening on the corpse of a country. Only the host was gone.

Sef picked up his spoon again, looked at Xanthe, and asked the question she couldn't:

"If the food's real, and the wanting-to-help is real... how do we know saying no is right?"

Xanthe looked at the soup. Barley browned in butter. Caraway. Patience as a flavor. Her body, traitorous, trained, honest, told her it was the best thing she had ever tasted.

Behind them, far off across the glass, the moon was rising into its thirteenth night—and it was fuller than it had any right to be, four nights early, swelling like something swallowing.
