## 11. What Immunity Costs

The fever came for her at dusk, an hour after they crossed the boundary line, and it came the way a debt collector comes: politely at first, then all at once.

They had made camp in the lee of a leaning stone on the steppe's near edge, where the glass began in earnest—a plain of fused sand stretching west until it married the sky. Ilya had built a fire from scrub-grass and dung, because there was nothing else to burn, and Sef had arranged their provisions with the grave seriousness of a quartermaster: hard bread, dried lamb, a skin of water that would not last.

Xanthe sat down to rest for a moment and did not get up again.

It started as cold. Not the ordinary cold of evening on open ground—she knew that cold, respected it, dressed for it—but a cold rising *out* of her, as if her bones had been storing winter for twenty years and had chosen tonight to spend it. Her teeth began to chatter. Then her skin caught fire, both at once, the classic signature of aconite withdrawal, except she hadn't taken aconite in three years, or she thought she hadn't, except apparently her body had been keeping accounts all along.

"Xanthe?" Sef's voice, small and careful. "You've gone grey."

"Grey is fine," she said. "Grey is my natural color."

Then the shaking took her whole body, and lying became not a choice but a verdict.

She heard Ilya swear in cartographer's shorthand—the language of men who describe disasters precisely—and felt his hands under her shoulders, dragging her bedroll into place. She heard him say something about the boundary, about what crossing meant, about how he'd tried to warn her, and she wanted to tell him that warnings were useless against arithmetic, that she had swallowed poison every single day for nineteen years on the theory that a body could be taught anything, and now her body was teaching her back.

The purging came after midnight.

Her stomach surrendered first everything she'd eaten, then everything she hadn't. Bitter waves of it, and each wave carried a flavor she recognized the way you recognize a face in a crowd of strangers: hemlock, sweet and green as new peas. Foxglove. Belladonna, dark as church. The arsenic they'd disguised in almond milk when she was nine, which had taken her a week to stop tasting in her sleep. Wolfsbane, which she'd been given at eleven and which had nearly ended the experiment, and the taster-master's voice saying over her retching body: *again tomorrow.*

Nineteen years of doses, and her body was returning them all, dish by dish, like a guest returning gifts from a house she no longer wished to visit.

Somewhere in the middle of it she stopped being embarrassed and started being frightened, because her heart had developed opinions—it stuttered, raced, paused at length as though considering whether to continue—and because her hands, her reliable hands, the hands that could measure two grains' difference between poisons by feel alone, had gone clumsy and strange, fingers she didn't recognize.

"She needs water," Sef said. His voice had changed. He sounded older than ten. He sounded like someone who had done this before, in some other life, for some other sick person, in a kitchen she kept seeing behind her eyelids.

"There's barely enough," Ilya said.

"She needs it more than we do." A pause. "I'll drink less."

"You'll drink your share," Xanthe managed, "or I'll haunt you specifically," and Sef laughed, wet-eyed, and held the skin to her lips, tilting it with the practiced care of a boy who understood exactly how much a sick person could take without losing it.

She lost it anyway. Most of it.

* * *

Time went strange. She surfaced and sank. When she surfaced, there was always Sef.

He had made a compress from their one cloth soaked in water wrung from the grass—he'd learned it somewhere, he said, he didn't know where, the knowledge just lived in his hands—and laid it across her forehead. He counted her breaths aloud in Vhalorian when he thought she couldn't hear, low and rhythmic, a lullaby-shaped thing that might have been a prayer. He told her about the stars, pointing out constellations with names no living teacher had given him. He talked so she wouldn't be alone inside the fever, and once, when she surfaced enough to focus, she saw that Ilya was asleep sitting up and Sef was still awake, still watching, still counting.

"You should sleep," she whispered.

"I slept already."

"Liar."

"I slept *already*," he insisted, with the dignity of a boy defending his honor, and she was too weak to press it.

In one of the deep hours she dreamed—not visions this time, not Vhaloria, just memory, her own, dredged up raw:

A kitchen. Not the palace kitchen. A smaller one, with a crooked chimney and a smell of onions and tallow, and a woman kneeling in front of a girl of seven, straightening her collar with hands that smelled of lye.

*"Be good," the woman said. "Eat what they give you. Don't cry where they can see."*

*"Mama—"*

*"Don't." The woman's hands tightened on her shoulders. "Don't call me that. They don't buy crying children."*

And then the palace gate, and the tasting-master's office, and a man writing her name wrong in a ledger—*Fairweather*, a name invented on the spot, a name belonging to nobody—and the first spoonful, honey with a grain of something bitter in it, and the tasting-master watching her not die with the satisfaction of a man watching money earn interest.

She woke weeping, which was its own humiliation, and found Sef's hand wrapped around hers.

"I saw," he said simply. "When you talk in fever. You talk in Vhalorian too, sometimes. Your accent's terrible."

Despite everything—a laugh, cracked and real.

"My mother sold me," she said. It came out flat, factual, a recipe read aloud. "I was seven. There were six of us and the year was bad. She walked me to the palace gate herself and told me not to cry where they could see, and I have been following instructions ever since." She stared at the fire's low coals. "Nobody chooses to swallow poison for a living, Sef. I chose nothing. I chose to survive choosing nothing. And then I called the surviving a skill, and the skill an armor, and I wore it so long I forgot it was welded on."

Sef considered this with the gravity he gave everything.

"I remember a grandmother," he said. "I never met her. She died before I was born, or maybe she didn't exist, or maybe—" he shrugged, the shrug of a child carrying a country. "But when you were sleeping you cooked things. Out loud. You gave measurements. In Vhalorian. For dumplings with nettles in them." He looked at her. "I know that recipe. I've always known that recipe. Nobody taught me."

"The country remembers through us," Xanthe said. "That's what the god is doing. That's what any of this is."

"No," said Sef, with sudden fierce certainty. "The god *takes*. This—" he pressed her hand harder, small fingers over her ruined knuckles, "—this is ours. We're remembering *each other*. That's different."

She had no answer to that. She was a woman who catalogued symptoms for a living, and she catalogued this one: warmth, spreading from the hand outward, unexplained, untreatable, possibly fatal.

* * *

By the third day the worst had passed, and what remained was worse in a quieter way.

She was empty. That was the only word for it. Nineteen years of daily doses had built walls in her blood, tolerances stacked on tolerances, and the crossing had dissolved them all, and now she could taste everything. The bread tasted of wheat and dust and the sweat of whoever milled it. The water tasted of the skin, of leather and fear. The smoke tasted of dung and distance and Ilya's guilt. She lay in the bedroll feeling like a peeled fruit, every surface exposed, taking in the world at full strength for the first time since childhood.

"Do you know what I miss?" she asked Ilya, who was redrawing the boundary behind them from memory, checking and rechecking their position. "I miss not tasting things. I spent my whole life training myself to notice everything—every off note, every bitterness, every grain of wrongness—and I thought the noticing was power." She turned her head. The sky over the glass was enormous, indifferent, beautiful. "Noticing is just pain with good posture."

Ilya set down his pencil. "Why did you do it? Truly. The tasting. You could have run at any point before now. People do."

"Run where?" She laughed, and it hurt. "I was property, Ilya. Property doesn't run; it gets resold. And besides—" she searched for it, the true answer, the one beneath the clever ones, "—if I was going to be poisoned anyway, I preferred to be the one holding the spoon. That's all ambition ever was, for me. Choosing which hand hurts you."

"And now?"

"Now my hand shakes." She lifted it: it trembled, faintly, honestly. "And I'm alive anyway. Isn't that the joke? I survived the armor coming off. All those years I thought the poisons were keeping me safe, and it turns out I was keeping *them* safe—bottled up, tolerated, manageable. Out here they've got nothing to protect. There's just me. Undercooked and overexposed." She smiled, and found the smile mostly real. "Turns out that's survivable too. Nobody told me that."

Sef returned from his scout of the glass edge, walking carefully, and crouched by the fire. He had that look he got—the far look, the borrowed-memory look—and when he spoke it was half to them and half to someone a century dead.

"There's a village," he said. "Out there. On the glass. People stayed when it happened. They live inside the reflection." He blinked, and was a boy again, puzzled at his own mouth. "I don't know how I know that. But I know it like I know the dumpling recipe. And Xanthe—" he turned to her, urgent now, "—you need to eat. Real food. Cooked food. Your body emptied itself out and the steppe is watching and hungry things notice emptiness."

"Hungry things," Xanthe repeated. "Meaning the polite one."

"Meaning the one who sets tables." Sef's eyes were old for a moment. "He notices people who've been poured empty. Empty vessels are easier to fill."

Ilya and Xanthe exchanged a glance over the boy's head.

"Then we move today," Xanthe said, and pushed herself upright on arms that shook, and found—to her genuine surprise—that they held.
