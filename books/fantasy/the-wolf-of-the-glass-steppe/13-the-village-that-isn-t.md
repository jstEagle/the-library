## 13. The Village That Isn't

The glass changed before we saw the village.

I noticed it first in my feet. For three days the Steppe had hummed a low, grinding note through our boot soles—the sound of a dead country being walked on, like treading the lid of something enormous that hadn't finished dying. Then, near dusk, the humming stopped. The glass went quiet under us, and quiet, after three days of noise, felt worse.

"Stop," I said.

Ilya stopped. Sef, who had been walking with his eyes closed for an hour—navigating, he said, by the taste of bread he couldn't describe—opened his eyes and said, "There's smoke."

There was no smoke. There was no anything. The Steppe stretched out ahead of us, mirror-flat to the horizon, reflecting a sky gone the color of old pewter. But Sef was staring at a point perhaps half a mile distant with the absolute certainty of a child pointing at a dog everyone else insists isn't there.

"It's cooking," he said. "Onion and lamb. And—cumin? No." His face screwed up. "It's a word I don't have. It smells like a word I don't have."

Ilya crouched beside him. "Show me where."

Sef pointed again, and this time Ilya saw it too—or saw enough. His breath went out of him in a long, careful stream, the way it did when he was trying not to hope about something. "There," he murmured. "Look at the reflection."

I looked down instead of ahead, and I understood why my feet had gone quiet.

The glass had always shown us the wrong sky—a Vhalorian sky, blue and cloud-streaked, hanging beneath our boots like a second country upside down. But here the reflection deepened. Beneath the inverted sky there were rooftops. Chimneys. A smear of woodsmoke rising from cookfires that cast light upward into a world that no longer contained them. A village, whole and lit, living inside the mirror of the Steppe as fish live inside water.

"They didn't leave," Ilya said quietly. "When it happened. When the unmaking came through—they stood their ground, and the ground went to glass around them, and they—" He stopped, because cartographers run out of words at the edges of things, and this was past every edge he'd ever drawn.

Sef took my hand. He'd been doing that more since the fever, since I'd told him about being sold at seven and he'd decided, with the grave finality children reserve for such decisions, that we belonged to each other. "They're having dinner," he said. "We should knock."

"You can't knock on a reflection," Ilya said.

"You can if you're polite," Sef said, and started walking toward the point where the smoke wasn't.

* * *

The door appeared because Sef knocked.

That is the truest sentence I know how to write, and I hate how it sounds, but I was there. He walked to the edge of the deepened reflection, crouched, and rapped his knuckles twice against the glass—gently, the way you knock at a neighbor's door when you've come to borrow flour—and the surface rippled outward from his fist like pond water, and then there was a stairway going down into lamplight and the smell of onion and lamb and the word Sef didn't have.

I put out an arm to stop him. Habit. In the palace, doors that opened for children were doors that swallowed children.

"Xanthe," Ilya said softly. "If they wanted us dead, the Steppe has had three days of chances."

"The Steppe isn't them."

"No," he agreed. "But nothing out here has tried to kill us yet except the god, and he did it with a dinner table." He looked at me. "Your call. You're the one who tastes things."

Which was unfair and exactly right. I tasted things. I tasted intentions in the set of a cook's shoulders; I tasted lies in the sweetness of a sauce; I had spent twenty years reading poison out of the air of rooms. So I stood at the top of those stairs and I read what rose up out of them: lamb fat and cumin and woodsmoke and, underneath, something older and stranger—grief, cooked slow, the way you cook grief when you've had a hundred years to practice.

No malice. That much I could swear to. But grief can be its own kind of trap. Grief can hold a door open forever and never let you leave.

"We eat standing up," I said. "We keep our boots on. We leave before the moon clears the horizon."

Sef grinned and went down the stairs like the answer to a question nobody had asked him yet.

* * *

The village was called Tarn, which is Vhalorian for *the place where the river used to turn*, and the woman who met us at the bottom of the stairs was called Maret, and she was one hundred and six years old and had been thirty-six when the world above her went to glass.

She was small and brown and quick, with flour to her elbows and eyes the color of tea left steeping too long, and when she saw Sef she made a sound I will hear until I die—a short, broken-off sound, half gasp and half prayer, the sound of a woman seeing a language come back to her in the body of a child.

"You speak it," she said to him. Not a question. Her hand hovered near his cheek without landing, the way you hover near a fire you've learned burns. "Where did you get it? There's no one left to teach it. There's no one left."

"I dream it," Sef said, in Vhalorian, in a boy's accent, in a dead grandmother's cadence, and Maret sat down right there on the street, on the cobblestones, and wept into her flour-white hands while the whole village came out of its houses to look at us.

They came out slowly. That's what I remember most—the slowness. A hundred years inside a reflection teaches you patience, or maybe it just teaches you that everything worth having arrives slowly or not at all. They gathered in the lane between the cook-smoke and the lamplight, forty or fifty of them, in clothes two generations out of fashion, and they looked at us the way starving people look at a table being set.

An old man touched Ilya's sleeve. "You're Vosk," he said. "The mapmaker. We've seen your charts in the reflection. They redraw themselves, did you know? Every few years your lines move a little closer to the truth." He smiled with most of his teeth. "We thought you were praying."

"I was," Ilya said, and his voice cracked on it.

And then Maret stood up, wiped her face, and did the thing that frightened me more than anything the god had done: she said, "You'll stay for supper."

Not *will you*. Not a question with room inside it. She said it the way the palace kitchens had said *you'll taste the king's supper* for twenty years—an instruction wrapped in kindness, a role handed to me before I could ask what it would cost.

* * *

I want to be honest about the meal, because honesty is the only immunity I have left.

They fed us in a long low house with a ceiling of packed earth and roots growing through it—roots that shouldn't grow under glass, roots that grew anyway, because the village lived inside the reflection and the reflection remembered being soil. Forty people at long tables. Lamb with prunes and that unnameable spice. Flatbread blistered from a clay oven. A soup of lentils and charred onion that Sef ate three bowls of and then, halfway through the fourth, stopped, spoon in the air, and said in a small voice: "My grandmother made this. I never had a grandmother. But she made this."

Maret reached across and folded her hands over his. "Then she's in the recipe," she said. "That's what recipes are. That's all they ever were."

And I sat there with a bowl in front of me, steam rising into my face, and I did the thing I have done at every meal since I was seven years old.

I waited.

Twenty years of waiting. Twenty years of the courtly little pause while the king's chamberlain watched me not die. Twenty years of counting heartbeats, cataloguing numbness in the fingertips, noting the precise shade of my own gums in a polished spoon. I had eaten ten thousand meals and never once—not once—eaten one. The food went into my mouth and became data. Sweetness: no bitterness at the root of the tongue. Heat: capsaicin, harmless. Texture: correct. *Safe*, I would conclude, and the king would eat, and I would remain invisible, which was the entire architecture of my life.

Maret was watching me. Of course she was. A hundred and six years old, thirty-six years of Vhaloria in her bones, and she read me across the table like I was a label on a jar.

"You're tasting for poison," she said.

The tables near us went quiet. Not hostile—attentive. A hundred years of a dead nation, and still the instinct to hush when someone names a hard thing.

"Habit," I said.

"What do you find?"

"Nothing," I said. "Nothing dangerous. That's the problem." I looked down at the lentils. "I don't know how to eat food that hasn't been tested. I am the test. I have always been the test. If I'm not tasting for someone—if no one needs me to survive this bite—I don't—" My voice did something humiliating. I steadied it. "I don't entirely know what the food is for."

Maret nodded slowly, as if I'd asked her a real question and she intended to give it a real answer. She ladled more soup into my bowl herself, deliberately, looking at me the whole time.

"This soup was my mother's," she said. "Her mother made it before her. When the unmaking came, I was stirring a pot of it. I refused to leave the pot. That sounds mad—it was mad—but the pot was my mother's pot, and I thought, they cannot take the country if I keep the pot." She laughed, wet-eyed. "So the god took everything else and left me the pot, and I have cooked in it for a hundred years, and every person in this room has eaten from it, and no one has ever once wondered whether it might kill them."

"That's because you're kind," Sef said.

"It's because we're already dead," Maret said, gently, terribly. "That's what a reflection is, girl. We are the part of Vhaloria that wouldn't finish dying. Every meal down here is a meal for ghosts. And you—" she tapped the table once, sharply, "—you are the first living mouth this soup has touched in a century. Do you understand? Your fear of it is the most alive thing this table has seen since the glass came down."

I looked at the bowl. Lentils, charred onion, the unnameable spice that Sef finally named, halfway through his fourth helping, as *sorrow-root*—a word that existed nowhere but in a dead grandmother's kitchen and in a hundred-year-old woman's garden under the world.

I ate.

Not as a test. Not as data. I ate the way I imagine people eat when they are simply hungry among others who are also simply hungry, and the soup was good—salt and smoke and earth and the faint sweet ache of sorrow-root—and somewhere in the middle of the bowl I realized my hands had stopped cataloguing and my eyes were doing something embarrassing again, and Sef leaned against my arm as though leaning on walls was a thing he'd decided I now was.

No one wanted me to die for this meal. No one needed me to live for it either. The food was just food, offered because offering it was the whole of what these people had left, and I could not remember the last time—or any time—that anyone had given me anything without wanting a survival report in return.

I distrusted it completely. I ate it anyway. Those two facts coexisted in me the way the village coexisted inside the glass, and I understood, sitting there, that this was going to be my condition from now on: belonging offered, and distrusted, and accepted anyway, one spoonful at a time.

* * *

Afterward, the villagers showed us where we'd sleep—a real offer, soft beds, a roof of remembered earth—and Ilya went off with the old man who knew his charts, two cartographers of nowhere bent over a table of impossible maps. Sef fell asleep mid-sentence in Maret's kitchen with sorrow-root on his breath.

I stayed at the long table. I have always stayed at the table after; that's the taster's hour, the quiet audit, the inventory of symptoms. But there were no symptoms. There was only fullness, strange and total, and the sound of forty ghosts washing dishes in a village that wasn't.

Maret brought me tea and sat down across from me, and for a while neither of us spoke.

"You'll go on tomorrow," she said at last. Not a question. These people didn't waste questions on things they already knew. "To the door."

"If there is a door."

"There is a door." She turned her cup in her hands. "And the god is getting impatient. You've felt it. The glass has been singing at night this week—have you heard it? A high note, under everything. He's pulling. He's tired of waiting, and when a hungry god gets tired of waiting, girl, he doesn't send invitations anymore. He starts setting the table himself." She looked up at me, and her tea-colored eyes were very clear. "The moon is coming early. Ask your mapmaker. He'll have seen it in the charts. The thirteenth turn doesn't wait for calendars. It waits for permission—and somebody out here is about to give it."

Above us, muffled by a hundred feet of glass and memory, the sky over the Steppe began, very faintly, to sing.
