## 14. The Turn of the Moon

The moon came early.

Xanthe knew it before anyone told her, because she felt it in her teeth—a pressure like a spoonful of something too sweet, too thick, held too long on the tongue. She woke in the guest-house the villagers had lent them, in a bed that smelled of rosemary and old smoke, and looked through the window at a sky that was wrong.

The thirteenth moon hung fat and silver over the steppe, three days from fullness by any honest count. But it was swelling as they watched—Sef beside her at the window, Ilya behind them both, all three drawn from sleep without knowing why—and its light did not fall so much as *pour*, pooling on the glass plain like milk on a table.

"That's not right," Sef said, in Vhalorian first and then, remembering, in the valley tongue. "Grandmother said the moon keeps its appointments."

"Your grandmother," Ilya said quietly, "said a great many things that stopped being true a century ago."

Outside, the village was already stirring. Doors opened along the crooked lane; people stepped out into that silver flood without shields or cloaks, faces tipped upward, and Xanthe saw that some of them were smiling and some were weeping and most were doing both at once, which was a thing she had only ever seen at funerals and coronations.

She dressed fast. Old habit: when the palace kitchens went wrong at night, you got there before the shouting started, because a taster who arrived second was a taster who had time to be blamed.

*You're not their taster,* she reminded herself, lacing her boots. *You're not anything's anything anymore.*

It didn't help. It never helped. She had spent twenty years learning to move toward danger with a spoon in her hand, and apparently the training outlived the job.

* * *

By the time they reached the village square, the reflection had begun to rise.

That was the only word for it. The Glass Steppe had always shown them Vhaloria—the dead country mirrored beneath their feet, streets and orchards and lit windows, a nation preserved like a fly in amber. But now the mirror was tilting. Xanthe felt it in her inner ear before she saw it, a slow seasick lean, and then she saw it plainly: the reflected rooftops were climbing. The glass underfoot had become a pane angled against the sky, and beyond it, upside down and getting less upside down by the hour, Vhaloria was coming up to meet them.

"They'll drown us," said a woman near the well. Mira—Xanthe knew her name now, knew most of their names, because the villagers had made a point of teaching them, the way you teach someone you intend to keep. "The whole country, turned over on top of us like a bowl."

"Not drown," said old Tam, who had been a boy when the unmaking came. His voice was steady, but his hands around his walking stick were not. "Vhaloria doesn't fall on things. It *receives*. That was always the trouble with it. It receives, and receives, and cannot tell the difference between a gift and a grave."

"The god is pulling it up," Ilya said. He had his charts spread on the well's lip, weighted with stones, though everyone present could have redrawn them from memory by now. "Look at the angle of the light. He's not waiting for fullness. He's dragging the turn forward."

"Why?" Sef asked.

And because no one else would answer a child honestly, Xanthe did. "Because we've been here too long. Because he offered us dinner and we walked away from his table. Because he's been patient for a hundred years and patience is the one poison he never learned to hold." She paused. "And because he thinks we know where the door is."

"Do we?" Mira asked.

The question landed in the square like a dropped knife. Everyone looked at each other. Everyone looked at Xanthe.

"We know it isn't here," she said, and hated how thin it sounded. "We know it isn't on the steppe. Ilya's maps say—"

Ilya's face did something complicated. Xanthe had watched this man lie to a royal tribunal with a serene expression and had admired him for it. Now he couldn't manage serenity at all. He opened his mouth, closed it, and finally said, "My maps say what my maps say. Ask me again when there's less moonlight."

Which was, Xanthe thought, the worst possible thing he could have said. Because it told every person in that square exactly what he'd told her two nights ago by the fire, in a voice low enough that Sef wouldn't hear: *the door was never in Vhaloria. It's back in the valley. In the palace. In the kitchen hearth.*

The villagers didn't need the words. They read the silence, and the silence told them the door was far away, behind them, across a cracking plain, inside the fortress of the king whose food-taster stood among them. And still—they began to come to her. Not all at once. First Tam, leaning on his stick. Then Mira with her son half-asleep against her hip. Then others, a handful, then a dozen, forming a loose ring around her that was not quite a crowd and not quite a congregation but was, unmistakably, a *plea*.

"Taster," Tam said. The old honorific, the one that in Vhalorian meant more than food—it meant *one who takes the bitterness so others need not.* "You've swallowed poisons all your life. Swallow this last one. Open the door. Let him have his key and let the rest of us go home."

"I can't open it. I don't even know—"

"You dreamed it." A young man Xanthe didn't know, a fisherman once, maybe, in a country that had no coast anymore. "Mira says you dreamed the hearth. You dreamed the fire with two shadows. We all feel it off you—you carry his attention like a lamp carries flame. Open the door, and the moon turns, and the waiting ends."

"The waiting ends either way," Ilya said sharply. "Open or shut, when that moon peaks, something happens. The question is what, and to whom."

But they weren't listening to Ilya. They were looking at Xanthe with a hope so clean and so enormous that it made her want to be sick, and she recognized the feeling from the other side—from twenty years of watching kings and courtiers look at her cup and trust it, trust *her*, with their lives, when all she'd ever wanted was to be small enough that no one would ever look at her at all.

This was what she had fled invisibility to escape, and it had found her anyway. Weight. Being needed. Being *counted on*, which was just another word for being trapped, and the trap this time wasn't a palace kitchen but an entire village of patient ghosts-in-exile, holding their breath, waiting for her to save them.

"I need to think," she said.

"There's no time to—" 

"*I need to think.*"

Her voice cracked across the square harder than she'd intended, and the ring of villagers drew back a half-step—not afraid, exactly. Respectful. Which was worse.

* * *

They gave her the well-house, which was empty and dark and smelled of rope and cold stone, and she sat on an overturned bucket and put her head in her hands and did the one thing she knew how to do with a problem: she tasted it.

Because that was the secret nobody understood about tasting. It wasn't swallowing. Anyone could swallow. Tasting was *attention*—holding a thing on the tongue of your mind, rolling it, finding the notes underneath the notes. Hemlock tasted of honey if you were careless. Almond if you were unlucky. But attentive, it tasted of itself: green and cold and certain.

So she tasted the village's hope. And underneath the sweetness of it—*save us, end the waiting, take us home*—she found the bitter note. The one they were all refusing to taste.

If the door opened and Vhaloria returned—if the reflection finished rising and settled over the real world like a lid—what happened to the valley? To the king's kitchens and the scullery maids and the girl who prayed at the hearth? To Ilya's pardon, Sef's future, the million small lives that existed on the far side of the boundary line?

A country does not come home gently. A country comes home the way water comes home: into every crack, over every sill, and it does not ask which rooms you were using.

*He knows,* she thought. *Vhal knows. He's not lying about wanting his people home. He's just not telling them what home costs.*

And the worst part—the part that sat in her chest like a stone swallowed wrong—was that they might say yes anyway. If she told them. If she laid out the truth like a dish and made them taste it. Some of them would choose the drowning. People always did. She had seen courtiers drink what they knew was poisoned because the alternative was a life they couldn't bear.

She heard footsteps on the well-house steps, and didn't look up until Sef sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"Ilya's drawing," he said. He held out a scrap of chart-paper. On it, in Ilya's precise hand: a sketch of the rising reflection, annotated, with a single line circled twice. *"Rate of ascent doubles at true fullness. At false fullness—"*

"At false fullness, what?"

"He crossed it out." Sef's voice was very small. "Then he wrote it again smaller. Then he crossed it out again. I don't think he wants me to see it."

Xanthe took the paper. Held it to the strip of moonlight under the door.

At false fullness, the glass breaks.

Not the reflection. Not Vhaloria. The *steppe*. The plain they were standing on—the only road home, the only floor under several hundred people who had refused to leave for a hundred years—was going to crack apart under the weight of a country pulling itself upright through it.

She sat with that for a long moment. Then she laughed, short and awful, the laugh of a woman who has just identified the flavor in the soup.

"Of course," she said. "Of course he doesn't have to choose. He just has to make *us* choose fast. Rush the dish, and the diner swallows without tasting."

Sef looked up at her. "Is that what kings do?"

"That's what *everyone* does when they want something badly enough." She stood, and her knees ached, and she realized she'd been sitting on that bucket for an hour. "Come on. Find Ilya. Wake whoever leads this place—Tam, Mira, whoever will stand up and listen. And Sef—" she crouched, caught his chin, made herself gentle, "—when they beg me again, and they will, I'm going to say no. I need you to remember that I said no *for* them, not *to* them. Can you carry that for me?"

He considered this with the terrible seriousness of children considering adult nonsense and occasionally finding the real thing buried in it.

"Yes," he said. "Can I carry the lantern too?"

"Yes," she said, laughing despite everything. "You may carry the lantern."

* * *

They gathered in the square again as the false moon climbed, and Xanthe stood on the well's lip because there was nowhere else, and told them.

She told them about the cost. She told them about the valley, the boundary, the way a returning country floods. She used the words *drowning* and *lid* and *crack* and she did not soften any of them, because softening flavors for frightened people was how tasters got killed. She watched their faces as she spoke, cataloguing symptoms the way she'd once catalogued numbness and ringing ears, and she saw the hope crack in some of them the way the glass was beginning to crack beneath them.

Because it was beginning. Even now. A sound rolled under the square—deep, tectonic, a note too low for comfort—and a seam of light opened along the lane's cobbles, running away northward toward the horizon like a vein of lightning pinned under the world.

Tam listened to all of it with his eyes closed. When she finished, he opened them.

"You're saying the door is in the palace," he said. "In the kitchens. In the hearth where the maid prayed."

"Yes."

"And you're saying if we wait for the moon, the ground eats us, and if we run for the valley, the god follows us, and either way we end the hundred years of waiting."

"Yes."

"Then what are we waiting for?" And the old man smiled, and it was the smile of someone who had already grieved everything grieavble and found, on the far side of grief, a kind of terrible cheer. "Taster. We didn't stay on this glass because we hoped to live here. We stayed because someone had to keep watch. Someone had to remember. You—" he pointed the stick at her, and his arm shook, and he pointed it anyway, "—you cooked for us. You fed us our own memories. You kept watch better than we did. So we'll walk. All of us. Behind you, ahead of you, around you—we'll walk to the valley and knock on that hearth ourselves if we have to. But you'll lead, because you're the one the god watches, and I am too old to outrun a country."

The square murmured assent. Mira shifted her sleeping son. Somewhere in the crowd someone began, softly, to sing—a Vhalorian work song, a marching song, the rhythm of it unmistakably the rhythm of feet on a long road.

Xanthe stood on the well lip and felt the weight settle onto her shoulders, and it was heavier than any crown, heavier than any poison, and it fit worse than both.

Behind Tam's shoulder, past the singing villagers, past the cracking glass, the thirteenth moon poured its light down on the rising reflection—and high above the tilted roofs of dead Vhaloria, almost lost in the silver, she saw the shape of a man at an impossible window, raising a glass to her.

*To your health,* the gesture said, courteous as ever, patient as hunger.

She turned away from it, jumped down from the well, and took the first step east, toward the valley, toward the hearth, toward the door she had promised herself she would open and the choice she had already begun to suspect she would refuse.

"Keep them moving," she told Ilya. "And Ilya—whatever happens when the glass goes—I need you to promise me you won't stop mapping. Not even then."

"Especially then," he said, and unrolled a fresh chart as the village began, at last, to walk.
