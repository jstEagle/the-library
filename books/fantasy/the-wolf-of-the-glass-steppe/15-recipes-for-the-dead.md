## 15. Recipes for the Dead

The village had a name once. I could feel it hovering at the edge of my tongue like a flavor I'd tasted and forgotten—something with soft vowels, something that would have sounded like bread rising if bread could speak.

"You can call it what we call it," said the woman who had let us in, a broad-shouldered grandmother type named Maret who moved through her kitchen like weather. "We call it Enough."

"Enough of what?"

She smiled at me with half her face. The other half was busy looking at nothing. "Enough of waiting."

I should have distrusted her immediately. I distrusted everyone who fed me; that was the whole architecture of my life. But she put me to work before I could build any walls, pressing a knife into my hand and pointing at onions, and there is no suspicion in the world that survives an onion.

Sef sat on a stool by the fire, swinging his legs, watching the villagers come and go. There were maybe thirty of them. They moved strangely—not slow exactly, but careful, like people walking on a frozen pond who have forgotten how thick the ice is supposed to be. They spoke Vhalorian among themselves, but haltingly, the way you speak a language you learned from a dream. Words came out rounded off at the ends. Whole nouns went missing mid-sentence, and they'd gesture at the space where the noun used to be, patient as people waiting for a late guest.

"They're forgetting," Sef said quietly, when the kitchen emptied for a moment.

"I know."

"Not like forgetting. Like—" he frowned, hunting. His Vhalorian was better than theirs. His Vhalorian was terrifyingly good, fluent in his sleep, stumbling awake. "Like someone is eating the words out of their mouths. From the inside."

Ilya, who had claimed the corner nearest the door out of eleven years of habit, looked up from the chart he was redrawing on the tabletop with a wet finger. He said nothing. He'd been saying nothing all day, in the loud way he said nothing when a thought was forming that he didn't want to say out loud yet.

Maret set a pot of water on to boil and asked me what I knew about cooking.

"Everything," I said. "And nothing. I know what things taste like. I don't know what they're supposed to taste like."

"What's the difference?"

"The difference is whether anyone ever told you *good*."

She laughed, and then stopped laughing, and pressed her palm flat against her chest the way people do when something hurts behind the sternum. "Say that again," she said. "Slowly."

I said it again. And I watched thirty years of training do what they were built to do: I watched her face while she listened, and I read her like a symptom.

Her eyes had gone bright and unfocused. Her breathing had changed. She was tasting something. Not my words—the thing underneath them.

"You've eaten Vhalorian food," I said.

"I—" She shook her head slowly. "I don't remember eating. That's the trouble. None of us remember eating. We remember being hungry for something we can't name, and then the hunger stops mattering, and then—" she gestured at the careful way they all walked, the missing nouns, the rounded-off sentences. "Then you get like us."

*The god has been feeding on their forgetting.*

I'd thought it in the night, lying awake on a pallet that smelled of wool and old grief, and I'd dismissed it as fever-talk. But here was Maret, starving in a full kitchen, and here was I, standing in that kitchen with a hundred flavors of a dead country stacked behind my teeth like coins.

Because that was the thing I hadn't told them. That was the thing I'd been carrying since the valley: every poison I'd ever swallowed to save the king's life had turned around somewhere on the Glass Steppe and started showing me dinners. Vhalorian dinners. A kitchen with copper pots hung from antlers. A soup the color of late summer, made from something golden and ribbed. Bread baked on hot stones with seeds pressed into the crust in patterns—I knew the patterns were prayers but not which prayers. A honey cake cut into thirteen slices for twelve guests, because you always set a place for the one who couldn't come.

I had catalogued them the way I catalogued symptoms. Clinical calm. Fourteen poisons, forty visions, one dead country's worth of recipes, filed away in the recipe book I'd stolen from the palace—a book that now lived against my ribs under my coat, warm as a second heartbeat.

I hadn't cooked any of it. Cooking felt too much like answering.

But Maret stood in front of me with hunger she couldn't name, and Sef watched me from his stool with his chin propped on his fists, and outside the window the glass steppe reflected a sky that wasn't ours, and somewhere past the horizon a polite god was getting impatient with our pace.

"Maret," I said. "Do you have golden squash? Ribbed. Small. And barley, dark barley, and—do you keep bees?"

* * *

They had bees. Of course they had bees; the village kept everything it had carried out of Vhaloria a century ago, packed in cellars and cupboards like a household waiting for its owners to come back. Squash from seeds saved across a hundred autumns, each generation planting a country they couldn't name. Barley in sealed crocks. Honey so dark it was nearly black, with a bitterness underneath the sweet that made the back of your throat ache.

"That bitterness," I said, tasting it off the tip of one finger. "What flower?"

Nobody knew. Nobody remembered. Maret shrugged with her whole body, helpless and apologetic, and something in me went hard and bright.

Fine. Then I would be the remembering. It was the one job I'd trained for without knowing it: I had spent my entire life learning to identify what was in a dish by what it did to me. Name the toxin by the numbness. Name the alkaloid by the tremor. I had turned my own body into an instrument of detection—and now I would turn it into an instrument of memory instead.

I cooked the way I tasted: precisely, fearlessly, and alone at first, while the village gathered in doorways drawn by smells none of them recognized and all of them needed.

The soup first. Golden squash, dark barley, the bitter honey stirred in at the end off the heat, and—I closed my eyes and let the vision come, let it wash over me the way the hemlock-visions did, clinical calm, cataloguing—a handful of dried leaves crumbled fine. Sage, but not sage. Wilder. A mountain herb that grew on the steppe's edge before the steppe was glass. I found something close enough growing in Maret's windowsill pots, a weed she pulled and threw away, and the smell of it bruised between my fingers made three people in the doorway sit down abruptly, like their knees had been untied.

"It needs salt," I said. "Sea salt. And it needs to be served with an apology."

"An apology?" Maret asked.

"To whoever's place is set and empty." Thirteen slices for twelve guests. "That's the custom. You cook for the one who couldn't come, and you apologize that the food is only this good and not what they deserved."

Silence in the kitchen. Then Maret, very quietly, in rounded-off Vhalorian: "*Zhava.* Grandmother." She pressed her hand to her mouth. "I set her place. Every year. I set her place and I didn't—I couldn't remember why the extra bowl. I thought it was waste. I thought I was being sentimental."

I ladled the soup into bowls. My hands were steady. My hands were always steady; that was the point of me. But my heart was going like a rabbit's, because I could see it happening, the thing I'd hoped for and dreaded in equal measure:

They ate. And they remembered.

Not gently. Not like a gift. It hit them like the poisons hit me—symptom by symptom, a wave of heat, a tightening of the throat. An old man began to cry into his soup without changing expression, tears just falling steadily as he chewed. Two sisters gripped each other's wrists across the table and spoke rapid, fluent, whole-nouned Vhalorian, arguing about something, joyfully, furiously, the argument itself clearly a memory being re-lit. Maret ate three spoonfuls and then set down her spoon and said, in a voice like a struck bell: "*My name isn't Maret.*"

Everyone looked up.

"It's Marazhet. Marazhet Ovanna Kell. I'm the third daughter of the house of Kell, and we kept the western beacon, and I—" she laughed, wet and astonished, "—I have a *name*, and it has *ends* on it."

*He's been feeding on their forgetting,* Sef had said. Well. Then tonight we were spoiling his meal.

* * *

By midnight the whole village had eaten, and the village had become a different place—or rather, it had become the same place, the one it had been refusing to stop being for a hundred years. People were dragging things out of cellars: banners, instruments, a wedding chest with names carved inside the lid. They were teaching each other songs where the missing verses turned out to have been hiding in everyone's mouths all along, waiting for permission.

And I stood at the hearth and cooked dish after dish, chasing the visions, translating flavor into food, and with every bowl I served I felt myself being emptied out and filled up at once. Because here was the thing I hadn't planned for. Here was the cost nobody warns you about when you spend your life surviving other people's meals:

Every dish I cooked for them, I tasted. Cook's privilege, or curse. A spoonful of each, off the stirring spoon, the way I'd tasted the king's supper for thirty years.

Only this time nobody wanted me to die for it. This time every spoonful was mine, and it went down into a body already saturated with fourteen poisons and one dead nation's grief, and it *hurt*. Not in the throat. In the place behind my ribs where the god had set his hook. Each authentic flavor—that was the word my mind kept using, *authentic*, as if the visions were forgeries and these were real—each one pulled the hook tighter, like a fisherman testing his line.

You're doing my work for me, the tightness said. You're gathering them up for me. All these memories, all this *Vhaloria*, concentrated in one room, ripe for harvest.

Maybe. Maybe that was true. Ilya caught my eye across the kitchen at one point, and his look said he was having the same thought, and neither of us said it out loud, because there were children dancing.

Let him think that, I decided, stirring the next pot. Let him think the cook is his kitchen girl. A taster learns one lesson above all others: never show the poison until it's already swallowed.

* * *

Near dawn, the crowd thinned and the fire burned low, and I found Sef asleep sitting up on his stool, which was how he slept best, he'd told me—upright, like a boy waiting for orders in a kitchen that wasn't kind. I woke him the gentle way, with smell instead of touch. The last dish was cooling on the board beside him: small round cakes, fried in butter, dusted with sugar and crushed nuts. I'd seen them in a vision a dozen times, always the same scene—a low kitchen, morning light, flour handprints on a child's smock, a woman singing off-key, and the cakes, always the cakes, turning gold in the pan while the song stumbled and restarted.

Sef opened his eyes. The smell reached him. And I watched a boy's face come apart.

"No," he said. "No, that's—" He was shaking. "That's *baba's*. That's my grandmother's kitchen. That's her song, she sang it wrong, she always sang it wrong, she forgot the words and made up ones about *goats*—" And then he was crying, huge silent sobs that shook his whole small body, and I did the only correct thing, the thing no palace ever did for either of us: I picked him up. He was seven when they sold me; he couldn't have been more than eight now, and light as a bundle of kindling, and he clung to me with his face in my shoulder and grieved for a grandmother he had never met in a language only the two of us and thirty villagers alive could speak.

"She's not gone," he said finally, muffled. "Xanthe, she's not gone, she's just—*folded up*. Like a map. The cakes unfold her." He pulled back and looked at me with eyes swollen and absolutely certain. "You have to teach me to make them. So I can unfold her whenever I want."

"First lesson," I said, and my voice cracked, and I let it. "Flour goes everywhere. That's not a mistake. That's the recipe."

He laughed through the last of the crying, and took up the rolling pin like a sword.

* * *

It was full daylight when Ilya touched my elbow. He'd been at the table all night with his charts, working by candlelight, and when I came over I saw why he'd been silent so long.

He'd laid the maps side by side—the official charts from the archive, his prison-era sketches, and the new one he'd been drawing since we crossed the boundary. The redrawn lines matched. Perfectly. But that wasn't the thing.

The thing was that the new map had changed overnight. While we slept. The coastline of the reflection had crept inland by a finger's width, and where the village of Enough sat, there was now a mark he hadn't drawn: a small circle, precise as a seal.

"That's the dinner table," I said. My mouth had gone dry. I knew it before I knew it, the way I knew hemlock in honey. "The impossible table. He's marked where he set his table for us."

"And look where the coast is heading." Ilya's finger traced the encroaching line. Past the village. Toward the valley. Toward home. "He's not waiting anymore, Xanthe. He's *plating*."

Behind us, the villagers were singing with their whole restored throats, and Sef was laughing, covered in flour, and the thirteenth moon hung pale and early in the morning sky like a coin someone had bitten to test and found wanting.

I looked at my hands. They smelled of butter and wild herbs and a country's worth of grief.

Good, I thought at the hook behind my ribs. Eat well. Because the next course is mine, and I have spent thirty years learning exactly how much poison a body can hold.

I tied my apron tighter and went back to the fire.
