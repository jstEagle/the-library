## 17. Running Before the Thirteenth Moon

The glass began to sing before it began to crack.

I had never heard anything like it—a high, sweet keening that came up through our boots and into our teeth, as if the whole steppe were a struck wineglass and someone had run a wet finger around its rim. Sef heard it first. He was asleep against Ilya's pack when the sound woke him, and he sat up with his eyes already wide, already fixed on the horizon behind us.

"It's early," he said. "The moon. It's not supposed to be full yet."

He was right. We had three nights left by my count—three nights of the thirteenth turn still climbing—and I had built our whole retreat around that arithmetic. But when I turned and looked south, where Vhaloria's reflection pooled in the sky like ink poured into milk, the moon hanging above it was round. Whole. Fat and white and wrong, the way a coin is wrong when you find one from a country that no longer mints them.

"Vhal is hurrying," I said.

Ilya didn't answer. He was standing very still, the way he used to stand in his cell when the wardens came down the corridor—stillness as armor, stillness as listening. Eleven years in a cage teaches a man to read footsteps through stone. Now he was reading the steppe.

"The reflection," he said quietly. "Look at the edge of it."

The edge of it was moving. That was the thing I couldn't make my eyes accept at first—the reflected country, the ghost-Vhaloria that lay across the sky of the steppe wherever we walked, had always held its position, patient as a painting. Now its borders were creeping outward. Rolling toward us like weather. And inside that rolling edge, I could see movement: rooftops assembling themselves out of nothing, streets unspooling, windows lighting one by one with a warm gold light that made my chest ache in a place I didn't know I had.

"He's not chasing us," I said. "He's *arriving*. Over everything."

"Then we run," Ilya said.

* * *

We ran.

There is a particular humiliation in running when you are forty-one years old and have spent your life cultivating invisibility. Invisibility does not require speed. It requires smallness, quiet, the talent of being overlooked—and I had been so good at it for so long. Now I was pounding across a mirror-flat plain under an impossible moon, my lungs burning with air that tasted of snow and iron, and every part of me that had ever wanted to be unseen was screaming that this was the opposite of survival.

But I had a boy on my back, or would soon, and a map burning a hole in Ilya's coat, and a door waiting in a hearth four hundred miles away. So I ran.

The glass sang under us. Ahead, the village of the Unforgotten was already breaking apart—or rather, being taken. The reflection had reached it. I could see the village's little houses flickering now, doubled, tripled, each one existing simultaneously as weathered timber and as some idealized version of itself with fresh paint and smoking chimneys. The villagers stood in their doorways watching their refuge be eaten by memory.

Marta was waiting for us at the village's eastern edge, where the ground rose into the low ridge they called the Causeway—a spine of thicker glass that ran east toward the valley, the only path across the deep fissures that the cracking had opened overnight. She was an old woman who had once fed me a meal that no one wanted me to die for, and she was shouting orders to her people with the crisp authority of someone who had been preparing for this day for a hundred years without admitting it.

"You're too slow," she told us, which was true. "The bridge won't hold open long once we let go of it."

"Let go of it?" Ilya panted.

"The Causeway stays solid because we walk it. Every dawn, every dusk, the whole village walks it end to end, singing. That's what keeps it glass instead of memory." She pointed back at her village, where the reflection was thickening like frost forming on a window. "When Vhal pulls the whole country over, there will be no more walking. No more singing. The bridge becomes a street in Vhaloria, and streets don't cross out of dreams." She gripped my arm with fingers surprisingly strong. "So here is what we're doing. We walk the bridge one last time—all of us, everyone, together—and we hold it open behind you until the crack takes it. You don't stop. You don't look back. Do you understand me, taster?"

"No," I said honestly. "Why? Why give up your refuge? Your homes? Everything?"

Marta smiled, and there was something in it that I recognized—the same strange pride I had carried all my life in the palace kitchens, the pride of a body that survives everything.

"A refuge isn't a home," she said. "A refuge is where you wait. And we have waited long enough." She turned and raised her voice to carry across the flickering village. "Everyone to the Causeway! Bring nothing! We are going to walk our grandmother's road one more time!"

* * *

They sang as they walked.

It was a Vhalorian road-song—I knew it from my visions, from the flavors of it, honey and rye and woodsmoke—and hearing it come out of eighty throats at once did something to the glass beneath our feet. The singing steadied it. Where the villagers stepped, the cracks sealed; where they passed, the reflections thinned and the real world showed through: grey sky, pale sun, the honest emptiness of a dead plain.

We went ahead of them. Marta had insisted—the child first, then the old woman, then the cartographer. "You three are the errand," she said. "We are only the door holding itself open."

For two hours it worked. The Causeway ran straight and true, a dark spine over a sea of shattered mirror, and behind us the song followed like a lamp.

Then Sef fell.

* * *

He had been running well—better than well, better than a nine-year-old should, with the stubborn mechanical rhythm of a child who has decided not to complain. But children are not machines, and the thirteenth moon was pulling at him harder than at any of us. He remembered this country. The closer Vhal's reflection came, the more of Sef belonged to it, and I watched him stumble twice, three times, each fall longer, each recovery slower.

On the fourth stumble he didn't get up.

I reached him in four strides. He was on his hands and knees on the glass, shaking, and when I rolled him over his eyes were half-closed and unfocused.

"I can hear her kitchen," he whispered. "Grandmama's kitchen. The kettle. Xanthe, I can hear the kettle, and if I go to sleep I'll be there, I'll be *home*, and—" His face crumpled. "Is that so bad? Is it so bad to want to just stop running?"

Behind him, far to the south, the reflected country rolled closer. I could see its streets now, its lit windows, and I understood exactly what he felt, because I had spent my whole life wanting a table of my own, and here was a god offering one to a child who had never eaten a meal that wasn't stolen or leftover or poisoned-adjacent.

"Yes," I said. "It's that bad. Because it isn't yours. It's bait, Sef. A hook dresses itself up as a home."

"But—"

"Hold on to me."

I got him onto my back. He weighed almost nothing—that was its own grief, the specific weightlessness of a palace orphan—and I knotted his arms around my collar the way I'd knot a sack of flour, and I stood up, and I ran.

I am not a strong woman. I want to be clear about that. My strength had always been the other kind—the kind that holds still while hemlock does its work, the kind that watches and records and says nothing. Carrying a child at a dead run across cracking glass was the single most physical act of my entire life, and my body informed me of this immediately and at length. My lungs filled with broken glass. My heart tried to climb out through my throat. Every poison I had ever swallowed seemed to wake up at once and hold a meeting in my legs.

But Ilya took one arm over his shoulders, and the old woman's people were singing behind us, and the door was ahead, and so I ran anyway.

* * *

The cracks found us at the halfway mark.

They came up through the glass like black lightning frozen mid-strike—fissures that hadn't existed an hour before, splitting the Causeway into islands. Ahead of us, a gap yawned six feet wide, and below it there was no depth at all, only reflection: the gap was full of sky, full of Vhaloria, full of a warm golden afternoon in a country that had died before I was born. Falling into it would not have been falling. It would have been *arriving*.

"We jump," Ilya said. He was gray-faced, breathing in ragged pulls. "Or we stop. Those are the options."

"Then jump." I shifted Sef higher on my back. His cheek was hot against my neck. "I've survived worse than arithmetic."

"That's not arithmetic, that's physics."

"I've survived worse than physics, too. I once ate the king's entire birthday feast on a dare from the master of the household, and three of the dishes were attempts on his life."

Something cracked in Ilya's face—not the glass; his composure. It might have been the first real laugh of his life. It lasted half a second, and then he backed up ten paces, took my hand, and we ran at the gap together.

I remember the moment in the air with terrible clarity. Sef's arms tightening around my throat. The reflected country rising up beneath me like a face rising toward the surface of water—streets, kettles, a kitchen window with geraniums in it, someone's hands kneading bread, all of it close enough to touch, close enough to want. For one suspended instant I understood completely why people walk into the reflection. It looks like mercy. It looks like everything.

Then my boots hit glass on the far side, hard enough to jar my teeth, and we kept running.

Behind us, the singing faltered.

* * *

I looked back. I shouldn't have. Marta told me not to look back, and I looked back.

The reflection had reached the rear of the column of villagers. They were still walking, still singing, but the last dozen of them were translucent now, flickering, their bodies doubling and redoubling as Vhal's arriving country claimed them. And Marta—Marta was at the very end, on purpose, because of course she was, because someone has to be last, and she had chosen it the way I had once chosen to drink poisons daily: deliberately, clinically, with her eyes open.

She saw me looking. She smiled. She mouthed something at me across half a mile of singing glass, and though I couldn't hear the words, I knew them, because I had thought them myself a thousand times in a palace kitchen:

*Don't waste it.*

Then the crack took the Causeway behind her—a great white fracture racing up the spine of the bridge like a fuse—and the last I saw of the village of the Unforgotten was eighty people dissolving into the warm gold light of a country reborn in a dead sky, still walking, still singing, holding the door open until the very last possible instant.

Sef began to cry against my shoulder. I think I did too. I honestly couldn't tell you; there wasn't time to check.

* * *

The rest of the Causeway was a gamble played at a sprint.

The fractures spread faster than we ran—we could see them branching ahead of us, feel them shivering through our boot-soles—but the villagers' song lingered in the glass the way heat lingers in a hearthstone after the fire dies. Where the singing had passed most recently, the glass remembered being solid. So we ran the ghost of their song, island to island, gap to gap, Ilya calling the jumps and me making them with a child on my back and no breath left for doubt.

Once, the glass gave way under Ilya entirely.

He dropped to his waist into a fissure and for one horrible second the reflection rose around him like floodwater—and I saw it reach for him, saw Vhaloria offer him the one thing it knew he wanted. I saw his face change. Eleven years in a cell, and here was his mother's country saying *come home, Ilya, you mapped me faithfully, come home.* His hand stopped reaching for mine.

"Sef," I gasped. "Say something in Vhalorian."

And the boy, half-conscious, feverish, dreaming, murmured a phrase into my ear—something soft and domestic, a grandmother's phrase, a *come eat, the food is getting cold* phrase—and Ilya flinched as if struck, and his hand shot up and closed around mine, and we hauled him out of the country that loved him, because that is the trick of it, that is the whole trick: the trap doesn't need to lie to you. It only needs to love you slightly more than you love yourself.

We ran. We jumped. The moon climbed.

* * *

By the time the Causeway ended, the thirteenth moon was directly overhead, and it was no longer pretending.

It had grown. It hung swollen and near, filling a quarter of the sky, and its light fell on the steppe not white but gold—the exact gold of Vhaloria's lit windows—and everywhere it touched, the glass softened. The whole plain was becoming liquid memory. Behind us, the reflected country had swallowed the horizon entire, and it advanced now not as weather but as tide, streets and steeples and kitchen smoke rolling northward at the speed of a slow walk, patient, inevitable, courteous even in pursuit.

But ahead—ahead was the ridge, the boundary, the thin grey line where the valley began and magic ended. And beyond it, impossibly, blessedly ordinary: scrub grass, cold wind, the smell of nothing magical whatsoever. The valley's dead air had never smelled so much like safety.

"The door," Sef whispered against my neck. He was burning with fever now, his body caught between two countries. "Xanthe, the door. It's in the hearth. I can see it. Grandmama's hearth, the palace hearth, they're the same hearth, they were always—"

"I know," I said. "Hold on. We're nearly home."

Home. I heard the word leave my own mouth and marveled at it. Four hundred miles behind us lay a palace where I had been sold at seven years old, where I had drunk poison every evening for thirty years, where attention meant death and belonging meant danger. And I had just called it home, and meant it, because home was wherever Ilya was arguing about maps and Sef was stealing pastries, and both of those things were waiting on the other side of that ridge.

Ilya reached it first. He stopped at the line—the invisible line where the valley's silence began—and turned back to look at me, at the tide of gold memory rolling up the plain behind us, at the boy on my back.

"It'll be close," he said. "The moon peaks tonight. The door opens tonight. Can you—"

"Move," I said.

I crossed the boundary at a stumbling run, and the valley's magic-dead air closed over me like cool water, and the god's pull went suddenly quiet in my ribs—the hook behind them loosening, easing, unable to follow past the line. I staggered, caught myself on Ilya's arm, and turned to look back one last time.

The golden tide stood at the ridge's foot. It did not cross. It gathered there, pressing against the valley's silence like the sea against a seawall, and in its depths I could see him—Vhal, the courteous host, the last of the old gods—standing in a lit doorway in the middle of his arriving country, watching us with the patience of someone who knows the door he wants is already inside.

He inclined his head to me. Almost politely. Then he raised one hand, and the moon overhead brightened, and brightened, and began, very gently, to descend.

"How long do we have?" I asked.

Ilya consulted the sky with a cartographer's eye, and his voice, when it came, was very calm, the way voices get when there is nothing left to gain from panic.

"If we push through the night," he said, "we reach the palace by moonrise tomorrow. If we don't—" He looked at the descending moon. "Vhaloria comes to us instead."

I tightened my grip on Sef, who was muttering kitchen words in a dead language against my shoulder, and started down into the valley at a run.

"Then we don't stop," I said.
