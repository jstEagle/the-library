## 18. The Hearth Where It Began

They came back into the valley the way thieves come home—over the wall, through the herb garden, down the scullery stair that Xanthe had climbed a thousand times with trays she was not allowed to drop.

The valley had changed. She felt it before she saw it: the stillness was gone. For thirty years the air inside the boundary had been dead water, magic-proof, poison-friendly, safe. Now it hummed like a struck bowl. The moon overhead was wrong—too large, too low, wearing a faint second ring of light, as if the thirteenth turn had pulled another moon out of hiding just to watch.

"It's peaking," Ilya whispered. He had his final map folded against his chest like a breastplate. "We have maybe an hour. Less."

Sef's hand was in hers. The boy had not spoken since the steppe began to crack behind them. He didn't need to; his grip said everything—*don't let go, don't let go*—and she didn't.

The kitchens were lit.

That was the first wrong thing. At this hour the palace kitchens should have been banked coals and sleeping cats. Instead every lamp burned blue, and the long room was full of people kneeling on the flagstones—cooks, scullery maids, the old man who turned the spit, the girl who had once prayed at this hearth and been answered. They knelt facing the great fireplace, foreheads to the stone, and they were all praying aloud, a low tide of voices:

*"Vhal who was unmade. Vhal who waits. Open us the door, feed us the feast—"*

"They've been at it since moonrise," Ilya murmured. "The whole palace is awake. Look at the windows."

Xanthe looked. Above the kitchen stairs, the palace rose against the sky, and every window of it glowed that same blue. Hundreds of candles. Hundreds of prayers. The king himself would be on his knees by now; she could almost taste it—the whole country swallowing the same sweetness, the same snow-and-iron flavor, and calling it grace.

"The hearth," Sef said. His voice came out cracked. He pointed.

The great fireplace stood open like a mouth.

Not fire—though there was light—but a doorway where the flames should be, tall as two men, its edges shimmering like heat-haze. And through it—

Through it was evening in a country that did not exist.

Xanthe had seen Vhaloria only in visions, in fragments: a kitchen mid-meal, hands breaking bread, a woman laughing over a pot. But this was whole. This was *lit*. A street of golden stone under a warm sky, lanterns coming on one by one, doors standing open because no one in Vhaloria had ever needed to close them. Someone was singing, far off, a song with no words she knew but a tune her body recognized the way it recognized hunger. The smell came through the door too—woodsmoke and roasting meat and something sweet with honey in it—and it was the most terrible thing she had ever smelled, because it smelled like *home*, and she had never had one to compare it to.

"Gods," Ilya breathed. He stepped forward and stopped himself with visible effort, hand pressed flat to his own chest. "It's real. It's actually—I mapped it from memory and I got the lanterns wrong. There are more lanterns."

"Don't," Xanthe said softly. "Don't look too long. It shows you what you're missing. That's how it hunts."

But she was looking too long herself. She could feel the hook behind her ribs—not pulling now, but *cradling*, gentle as a hand under a chin.

The kneeling servants never noticed them. Prayer had made them blind to everything but the door.

Then the god spoke, and he did not speak from the doorway. He spoke from everywhere—from the blue lamps, from the flagstones, from the space just behind her teeth.

*You came back.*

Xanthe's knees nearly went. She locked them. "I never left anything here worth coming back for."

*You left yourself.* The voice was warm. That was the horror of it; after the storm on the steppe, after the cracking glass, she had expected thunder, and instead it was the voice of someone setting a place for you at a table you thought you'd been banned from. *Come. Stand in the doorway. You don't have to cross. Just stand where the warmth can reach you.*

"I know your recipes," she said. "Hospitality first. Flattery second. Then the ask."

A sound that might have been laughter moved through the lamps. *You tasted me across a hundred miles of glass. Of course you know my recipes.*

She walked toward the hearth anyway. Ilya caught her sleeve—"Xanthe"—and she shook him off gently, and took Sef's hand again instead, because if she was going to walk into temptation she wanted her family where she could count them.

Up close, the doorway's warmth was unbearable in the best way—a bath after years of cold, bread when you'd forgotten eating was pleasure. Through it, a woman at a table looked up and smiled at her. Not a vision. Not a reflection. A person, with flour on her wrists, and she smiled at Xanthe the way you smile at someone expected.

"There is no one there," Xanthe said. Her voice shook. "There's nothing there. It's a hundred-year-old echo."

*Is it?* The god was quiet a moment. *And what are you?*

The question went in like a needle finding a vein.

*Here is what I offer,* Vhal said. *Not riddles. A home—you have slept in a pantry and a prison cell and a dead nation's sky, and never once in a room that was yours. A name—Xanthe Fairweather, a taster's name, a tool's name, given to you by the people who sold you. In Vhaloria names are earned, and yours is waiting. And a table.* The warmth deepened. *A table of your own. No one dies for your food. No one tests it first. No one watches you eat to see if you flinch. You cook, and people eat, and live, and thank you. Every night. Forever.*

Her eyes were burning. She hated that. Thirty years of clinical calm, fourteenth poison, fifteenth, cataloguing symptoms while her heart slowed itself on command—and one sentence about tables and she was coming apart like cheap thread.

"You'd consume the valley," she managed. "I tasted it. On the steppe. Your return isn't a return, it's a meal. This whole country is the thirteenth course."

*The valley unmade my country,* Vhal said, and for the first time the warmth frayed, showed the grief underneath, vast and old and starving. *Why should it not be unmade in turn?*

"Because it's full of people like her." Xanthe nodded at the scullery maid, still kneeling, still praying, a burn-scar healed a year ago and never questioned. "People who never unmade anything. People who just want to eat their supper."

*Then stay,* the god said simply. *Cross alone. Leave the valley to its fate. You owe it nothing—it fed you poison and called it a living.*

And there it was. The real trap, laid so carefully she'd walked into it days ago without noticing: not the offer, but the permission. The god offering her the one thing she wanted with the one price she could almost justify paying.

Behind her, Sef's grip tightened. Small fingers. Flour still under the nails from the village, from the night they'd cooked the dead back into remembering.

"No," Xanthe said.

*You haven't heard the whole—*

"No." Louder now, and the blue lamps guttered. "You want a key. You want grief enough to open a country-sized lock. And I have swallowed more than anyone alive—poisons, yes, but also every meal I ever tasted for a man who'd let me die without noting it in the ledger. I am *full* of it, Vhal. I know exactly what I'm carrying." She stepped closer to the doorway, into the heat, close enough to see the flour on the smiling woman's wrists, close enough to see that the woman's smile did not reach her eyes—that it couldn't, because the woman was memory, and memory only knows how to repeat itself. "But I learned something on your steppe. Everything I ever swallowed, I survived by deciding what it became inside me. Poison became immunity. Grief became—"

She stopped. Because she hadn't finished deciding yet. That was tomorrow's work, or tonight's, or the next five minutes'.

"—became mine," she finished. "So no. I won't cross alone. I won't cross at all. And I'm not giving you the key."

The doorway flickered. For one instant the golden street showed its bones: glass, fused sand, the reflection of a thing long dead, and the smiling woman was a smear of heat-light with no face at all.

Then the god gathered himself, vast and patient and terribly kind, and said:

*Then we will do this the old way. The way between a host and a guest who refuses the feast.* The lamps dimmed to embers. Around them, the kneeling servants lifted their heads, eyes blue as the flame. *You will swallow the moonlight, little taster, or the moonlight will swallow everything you love. Choose your dish.*

Above the hearth, through the high window, the thirteenth moon slid fully into peak—and its light poured down the chimney like water finding a drain, straight toward the open door, straight toward her.

Xanthe opened her mouth.

And tasted starlight.
