## 19. The Taster's Choice

The feast stretched the length of the great hall that did not exist in any palace I had ever served.

Vhal had set it with a host's careful hands: long table of pale wood, candles burning the color of honey held to light, chairs enough for everyone who had ever died hungry in Vhaloria and then some. The dishes came without servants carrying them—they simply arrived, steaming, as if the kitchen were just through a door we could not see. Which, I supposed, it was.

I knew every dish on that table.

Not by name—I did not know their names, not truly, though Sef murmured some under his breath like a rosary—but by taste. By the ghost-flavors that had been leaking into the king's supper for months. The quail glazed in something darker than honey. The dark bread split and steaming. A soup the color of late autumn with a skin of butter melting across it. A custard scented with a flower I had never been able to name until now, because the flower had been unmade along with everything else.

*Pirin.* Sef had named it once, in his sleep.

"Sit," Vhal said, drawing out my chair himself. He wore his courtesy like livery—the long coat, the neat beard, the smile of a man who has practiced hospitality for a century with no one to practice it on. "You've crossed my country on an empty stomach. That would shame me in any age."

Ilya stood at my shoulder, arms crossed, radiating the specific fury of a man who has already refused this dinner once and cannot believe he must attend it anyway. Sef sat where Vhal pointed him, because Sef was nine and there was bread, and even the end of the world can wait for bread.

I sat.

Because I wanted to know what he was serving me. Because tasting things was the only language I had ever been fluent in. Because—and here was the thought I had been circling since the hearth, since the door opened in ordinary palace fire and showed us a country lit and warm and *waiting*—because I had begun to understand what I was for.

"You're staring at the food the way you stare at everything," Ilya said quietly. "Like it might be lying."

"Food always lies," I said. "That's why they hire tasters."

Vhal laughed, a warm sound, genuinely amused, and poured wine into three cups. The fourth place at the table stayed empty. I noticed it the way I noticed everything: the fourth setting, the chair drawn out but unoccupied, the plate laid with the same dark bread. A place kept for someone who was coming or who had never left.

"For your grandmother?" I asked Sef.

He shook his head slowly, staring down the length of the table. "For everybody," he said. "It's for everybody."

* * *

Let me tell you what I understood, sitting in that impossible hall while the thirteenth moon pressed against the ceiling of the world like a thumb testing fruit for ripeness.

The door needed a key. Not a lockpick, not a spell—a key, living, walking, *willing*. Vhal had said as much at our first dinner, courteous as a knife wrapped in linen: *the country needs a living key.* And all across the steppe I had wondered what shape such a key took, what bloodline, what blessing, what terrible luck.

Now, looking at the food, I knew.

A key is something made to fit a lock. And a lock is a wound shaped like a door. To open Vhaloria—not to visit it, not to map it, but to bring it *back*, whole, into the world—you had to have swallowed it. All of it. Every flavor of its loss, every poison of its grief, held in the body without dying, held so long the grief became chemistry, became tolerance, became *you*.

Dish by dish. Poison by poison.

Fourteen toxins in training. Hemlock in the honeyed quail. Arsenic in the almond milk. Belladonna in the wine, foxglove in the broth. And then the fifteenth poison, the one nobody dosed me with: a hundred years of a dead country bleeding through into the king's supper, snow and iron and pirin-blossom, and me swallowing that too, night after night, cataloguing it, keeping it, *building immunity to a nation's death* the way I'd built immunity to everything else.

No one had trained me for that. No one had to. It was the same skill. It had always been the same skill.

"You know," Vhal said, watching me watch the food. He didn't touch his own plate. Hosts never eat; I'd learned that in palaces too. "Of all the souls who walked my steppe this season, you are the only one who never once asked why. The cartographer asks why constantly. The child asks why beautifully. You simply kept walking, and tasting, and knowing."

"Knowing what?"

"That you were being seasoned." He smiled. "I apologize for the word. But it is accurate. Grief is a marinade, Xanthe Fairweather. Time is the salt. And you—" he gestured at me with genuine admiration, "—you have spent thirty years making yourself edible to a dead country."

The candle flames leaned, all together, like a crowd turning its head.

"And if I say yes," I said. "If I take the key's work. What happens to me?"

"What happens to any door that becomes a wall?" Vhal spread his hands. "You stop being passage and become threshold. You stand between. It is holy work. It is *needed* work." His voice went soft, and the softness was the most honest thing he had offered yet. "You have never been needed before. Don't pretend you haven't noticed. Don't pretend you don't want it."

He was right. That was the obscenity of it. He was right the way good bait is right.

I reached for the cup of wine.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about immunity: it does not mean the poison isn't there. It means you've agreed to carry it. My body was a ledger of everything the valley had ever tried to do to me, every entry balanced, every debt paid in small daily sips. Tasting wasn't bravery. It was bookkeeping.

So when I lifted the wine, I tasted it the way I tasted everything—professionally, completely, hunting the lie.

And found it.

Under the black cherry and the smoke and the pirin, under the gorgeous architecture of a cuisine rebuilt from a dead nation's memory, there was a flavor I knew from the wrong side of the spoon. I knew it from the kitchens, from the nights I caught assassins' work in time, from the one dish in my career that had killed a fellow taster before I could knock the fork from her hand.

It was the taste of something that eats.

Not spoils. Not turns. *Eats.* A sweetness with a hunger inside it, the way rot blooms sweet. I set the cup down and looked at the whole table again—the bread, the soup, the quail, the custard—and saw it everywhere now that I'd found the note. Every dish carried it, faint as a watermark. Every dish was delicious the way a trap is comfortable.

"It's lovely," I said. "The feast. It's the best thing I've ever been offered."

"I know," Vhal said.

"So I'll ask the question I should have asked at the first dinner." I turned the cup slowly on the wood. "What does it cost? Not me. I know what I cost, I'm the cheapest thing at this table. The *return*. When Vhaloria comes back—when the reflection comes over the valley, over the real—what happens to everything that's standing where your country used to be?"

Sef stopped chewing.

The silence had texture. I felt Vhal consider lying, and I felt him decide against it, and I will grant him this: whatever else he was, he had the honesty of a well-made poison, which kills you cleanly and tells you the dose.

"The valley is built on Vhaloria's grave," he said. "When a country returns, it returns to its own borders. Its own footprint. Everything within that footprint is either reclaimed—" the pause was gentle, surgical, "—or consumed as material. Soil remembers. Stone remembers. People are more difficult. People are mostly water, and water belongs to whoever claims it first."

"The village," Ilya said hoarsely. "The people on the steppe—"

"Will come home. They are the point of all this."

"And the palace," I said. "The kitchens. The scullery maid who prayed. The other maids. The cook who slips me extra bread because she thinks I'm too thin." I heard my own voice go flat and precise, the voice I used for reporting symptoms, which meant I was in more danger than I had ever been in my life. "The orphanage row. The tanners' street. The whole city, the whole valley—every soul inside the old border."

"Yes," Vhal said.

"And the boy."

"He is Vhalorian. He would come home. He would be—"

"He's nine," I said. "He's nine years old and he was born in a stable loft in a valley he thinks is the whole world, and you're going to render him down for soil?"

"He would *live*—"

"Everyone lives," I said. "That's the problem. Everyone lives somewhere, and somewhere is always somebody else's somewhere. You've been starving politely for a hundred years and I understand it, I do, I have been hungry my entire life and I still know better than to eat my neighbor's plate."

I pushed back my chair. The sound of wood on stone rang down the hall like a dropped bell.

Ilya had gone very still beside me. Sef had both hands flat on the table, knuckles white, eyes huge, looking from Vhal to me and back with the terrible arithmetic children do faster than adults—he had a grandmother's kitchen waiting through that door and a cook's extra bread waiting behind it, and he had just understood, at nine years old, that he could only keep one.

"Don't," Vhal said, and for the first time the courtesy cracked, and underneath it was not rage. Underneath it was a hundred years of empty tables, and it was worse than rage. "Xanthe. You of all of them. You were *made* for this. Sold at seven, dosed since childhood, hollowed out and filled up again—no one will miss you. No one has ever missed you. I am offering you the one thing the valley never did: a reason you exist."

And there it was. The true poison. Not in the wine. In the words.

I stood in that impossible hall with my hand half-extended toward a future where I mattered, where I was key and keeper and chosen, where thirty years of swallowed horror finally meant something—and I did the only professional thing left to do.

I tasted the moment itself, the way I tasted everything, hunting the lie.

Found it immediately. Found the rot-sweetness blooming under the offer, the same note as in the wine: *you matter, and the price is everyone.* The oldest poison in the world. Kings drank it daily. I had spent my life catching it in their cups.

"No one misses me," I agreed quietly. "But I miss *them*. Turns out that counts. Turns out it's the whole recipe."

I looked down the table at the empty fourth place, set for everybody, kept for a country that could not understand why its dinner guests kept refusing to come home.

Then I looked past Vhal's shoulder, through the tall windows of a hall that did not exist, at the sky above the Glass Steppe—where the moon hung swollen and early and full, a thirteenth turn pressing down like a thumb on a bruise, and beneath it the mirror-flat plain had begun, very faintly, very far away, to glow. Not reflecting the moon anymore.

Reflecting *through* it. Light pushing up from underneath, from a country rising in its sleep, and along the horizon the glass was singing a note I felt in my teeth, in my scars, in every poison I had ever balanced in my blood—

and the first crack ran across the steppe like a sentence being written.
