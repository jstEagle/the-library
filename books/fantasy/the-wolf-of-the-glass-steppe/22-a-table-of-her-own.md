## 22. A Table of Her Own

The bread rose wrong.

I stood in my kitchen at the valley's edge—*my* kitchen, the words still strange after a year—and watched the dough climb the bowl's rim with the slow confidence of something that had never once been poisoned, and I thought: *good*. Let it rise wrong. Let it be ugly. Ugly bread is honest bread.

There had been a time when I could taste a lie in a loaf before it finished baking. Hemlock wore honey like a borrowed coat; arsenic flirted with almonds; nightshade pretended to be nothing at all, which was how you knew to fear it most. Fourteen years in the palace kitchens taught me that every flavor was a confession if you listened long enough.

Now the only thing my bread confessed was that I had used too much rye and not enough patience.

Outside the window, the Glass Steppe caught the late sun and threw it back in long amber sheets, all the way to the horizon. From here—at this distance, at this angle—it looked almost gentle. A mirror laid flat against the world. It did not look like the place where a country had died, or where a god had gone hungry, or where I had carried a sleeping child across cracking glass while the thirteenth moon chased us like weather.

Distance does that. Distance is its own kind of mercy.

I punched down the dough and started again, because the first loaf was for practice and the second was for the table, and tonight there would be a table. Three chairs. Three plates. The good ones—Ilya had traded a chart of the southern passes to a potter in Millbrook for them, glazed the blue of a sky reflected in glass, because he said we should eat off the color of the place that made us. Sef said they were too pretty to use. I said that was exactly why we'd use them. Pretty things die of disuse faster than ugly ones. I know. I spent fourteen years being useful and invisible, and invisibility nearly killed me more thoroughly than any poison ever managed.

The kettle sang. I poured the water over the dried vervain—the valley grows it wild now along the boundary line, where my stillness lives in the ground—and thought about spoons.

A year ago, a spoon was a weapon I aimed at myself twice a day. A measured spoonful, swallowed with clinical calm while a king ate his supper three rooms away, trusting that my body would tell him what his could not survive learning. I catalogued my symptoms like weather reports. Numbness at the lips, quarter-hour onset. Sweating, mild. Vision graying at the edges but returning. *Survivable*, I'd write in my recipe book, in a hand so small it was practically an apology. *Serve with confidence.*

No one ever thanked me. That wasn't the arrangement. The arrangement was simpler than gratitude: I tasted, the king lived, and I remained too valuable—and too strange—to notice. Invisibility as armor. I built it plate by plate, dose by dose, until even I couldn't see me.

Then the old gods answered a scullery maid's prayer at a kitchen hearth, and everything I had armored myself against turned out to be the smaller danger.

I set the table while the second loaf baked. Three places, set properly—knife on the right, spoon beside it, cloth napkins folded into shapes Sef had learned from a woman in the village-that-wasn't, who remembered folding them from a life none of the rest of us had lived. Sef folded his napkins into little sleeping birds. He said Vhalorian children used to do it so their grandmothers' spirits could rest in them overnight. Whether that was true or whether he'd invented it, I didn't ask. Some things you let a child keep either way.

* * *

Ilya arrived at dusk, the way he always did, announcing himself by argument rather than knock.

"—and if you'd just *look* at the coastal survey instead of assuming—" His voice carried through the door before his key found the lock, addressed to someone who wasn't there yet, which meant he'd been rehearsing. Eleven years in a cell will teach a man to hold conversations with himself; a year of freedom hasn't fully cured him of it.

The door opened. He stood there with a map tube under one arm and windburn across his cheekbones, silver threading deeper into his black hair than it had been last spring, looking at me like he was checking—still, after all this time—that I was real.

"You're arguing with the door," I said.

"I'm arguing with the *survey office*, who happen to share your habit of not listening." He came in, kissed my temple—he still hesitated half a heartbeat first, every time, as if asking permission he already had—and set the tube on the table with the reverence other men reserved for relics. "Coastal survey. Final edition."

"The memorial charts."

"Printed Thursday." He unrolled a corner, just a corner, the way he always did, saving the reveal. "Two hundred copies. Every school in the valley gets one. Vhaloria drawn in gray ink—not red, never red again—labeled plainly: *Here a country stood. Here its people cooked, argued, sang, and were. Remember it as food, not as hunger.*"

I touched the ink. It was still faintly warm from the press, or I imagined it was. A year ago these maps were prayers that a god answered; now they were eulogies that no one prayed over, and that was the whole difference between a haunting and a history. Ilya had redrawn every coastline of the drowned nation from memory—his memory, smuggled out of Vhaloria in a child's skull the way Sef's grandmother's kitchen had been smuggled into Sef's sleep—but he drew them now to keep the dead *dead*. Resting, not waiting.

"They're beautiful," I said.

"They're accurate," he said, which was Ilya for *thank you*, and then, quieter, looking around my kitchen at the rising bread and the blue-glazed plates and the napkin-birds: "It smells like a home in here."

"It is one."

He nodded slowly, the way he'd nod at terrain he'd finally, after years of surveying, agreed to trust.

* * *

Sef arrived the way Sef always arrived: through the window.

Not because the door was locked. Because he was twelve, and because the boundary-keeper's cottage sat at the edge of the valley where the wall was low and the plum tree leaned over it, and because he had decided sometime in the past year that arriving normally was for people without personality.

"Evening, Xanthe!" he called from the sill, upside-down, cheeks full.

"If those are Millbrook pastries—"

"They're *liberated* Millbrook pastries." He dropped into the kitchen, scattering crumbs, and produced a paper bag with the flourish of a conjurer. "Baker Vess says hello and also that she counted six missing and I'm to bring her a jar of your vervain honey as reparations, which frankly is theft on her part because honey takes a whole season."

"A jar it is." I took the bag. Inside: six honeyed buns, still warm, glazed gold. My mouth knew what they were before my mind caught up—honeyed pastry, the ghost of quail-and-hemlock suppers, the flavor of a king's table where I had eaten nothing for fourteen years except measured spoonfuls of dying.

I ate one standing up, right there, crumbs and all, and Sef watched me do it with the solemn satisfaction of a boy witnessing a ritual he'd personally invented.

"Good?" he asked.

"Good," I said. And then, because a year of being someone new has taught me to say the truer thing underneath: "It tastes like nobody wants me to die for eating it. Still gets me every time."

Sef grinned and stole a second bun for himself, and I let him, because some thefts are load-bearing.

* * *

We ate late, because the bread wanted its full proof and the stew wanted its full simmering and none of us wanted anything sooner. Lamb and root vegetables and the wild herbs that grow along my stretch of the boundary—thyme gone feral, marjoram escaped from some dead garden and thriving in the magic-dead soil like a refugee who'd stopped looking back. I cooked the way I cook now: generously, imprecisely, tasting as I go not for poison but for pleasure, adjusting salt with my fingers instead of measuring spoons.

Measured spoonfuls are behind me. I own exactly one measuring spoon and I use it to level the tea.

Over dinner, Ilya told us about the eastern villages petitioning for a copy of the memorial atlas, and about the astronomer-priests—who had, in the year since the thirteenth moon set for the last time, quietly rewritten their entire calendar to include a fourteenth turn they called *the Keeper's Turn*, which everyone understood to mean mine and no one said aloud. Sef told us about his tutor, a patient widow named Marta who was teaching him letters and arithmetic and the vital skill of apologizing to Baker Vess convincingly. He told us he'd spoken Vhalorian in his sleep only once this whole month, and when Marta's daughter asked what he'd said, he'd checked with me first, and I'd told him the truth: it was probably a recipe.

Grandmothers' kitchens persist in recipes. That is how countries survive being unmade—not through doors and moons and hungry gods, but through hands remembering what to do with flour.

After dinner, Sef washed the dishes, badly, singing. Ilya spread the coastal survey across the cleared table and walked me through it coast by coast, and his voice went soft in the places where the memory was softest—a harbor town shaped like a cupped hand, a lighthouse whose keeper had been his mother's cousin—and I listened the way you listen to a man reading his own eulogy and finding it kind.

And then the moon came up.

We all three drifted outside without discussing it. It's become the custom. On the anniversary nights—the night the maid prayed, the night we crossed the boundary, the night the door closed—we stand at the valley's edge and watch the sky together, the way survivors of a shipwreck watch the sea.

The thirteenth moon rose over the Glass Steppe, fat and white and utterly ordinary.

That was the miracle of it. That was the whole miracle, and I have never needed a larger one. A year ago that moon belonged to no calendar, was owed to a starving god, dragged a dead country's reflection across the living world like a tide pulling at a wound. Now it was just a moon. It lit the steppe silver, and the steppe reflected it back, and somewhere under that glass a nation slept the way nations should sleep—in the past tense, dreamed of in recipes and gray-ink maps and napkin-birds, and nowhere else.

Sef leaned his head against my arm. Ilya stood close on my other side, his shoulder touching mine, and neither of them said anything, because we are people who have learned that the important things mostly go unsaid at the exact moment they're most true.

I felt it then—the feeling I get on these nights, the one the astronomer-priests named me for. The stillness. The valley's magic-dead quiet lives in me now, has lived in me since the night I swallowed the thirteenth moon's light the way I'd once swallowed hemlock: deliberately, completely, dish by dish, grief by grief. I am the boundary now. Nothing old and hungry crosses me. Sometimes, far out on the steppe, I feel things test the line—a whisper of prayer, a curl of old wanting—and I feel them slide off me like rain off glass, and I stand there, and I remain.

It is the strangest work in the world, being a wall. But I was trained for it, in a way. Fourteen years of swallowing what could kill others. I simply changed what I was keeping safe.

"Xanthe," Sef said, around a yawn. "Next anniversary, can we have the honeyed quail? The real kind. Without the hemlock."

"There was never a real kind," I said. "But yes."

"And can Ilya make the flatbread? The one from the harbor town?"

"He'll pretend it's difficult and then make it perfectly," I said, and Ilya, who pretends nothing is ever sentimental, said, "Obviously," and Sef laughed, and the moon climbed, and the bread for tomorrow rose in the warm kitchen behind us, ugly and honest and entirely ours.

The price of survival, I thought, watching my two impossible people argue softly about whether flatbread requires a specific pan. Paid in full. Paid in poison and glass and a god's hunger and a body remade. Paid the way I pay for everything—

One measured spoonful at a time.

And worth it. Every swallow. Worth it.
