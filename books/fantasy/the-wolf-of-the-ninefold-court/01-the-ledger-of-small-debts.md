## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candles in the counting room were tallow, and they burned the way everything in the Ninefold Court burned: slowly, obediently, without ever quite being allowed to finish.

Petra Reyes trimmed the third wick with their thumbnail, watched the flame steady, and returned to the ledger open before them. It was a small book, bound in grey calf-hide gone soft at the corners from handling — *Ledger of Small Debts*, said the gilt on its spine, worn nearly to nothing because no one but Petra ever opened it. The great ledgers of the Court lived in the vault below, behind glass and oath and lock: the Ledger of Sovereign Trades, the Ledger of Names, the Nine Books themselves, which no clerk below the rank of master was permitted to touch. But the small debts came to Petra. The small debts always came at night.

Outside, rain worked at the city of Corvane with the patience of an accountant. It had been falling since dusk, finding every seam in the slate roofs, filling the gutters along Sable Street until they ran loud as little rivers. Inside, the counting room was dry and warm and smelled of candle smoke, ink, and the faint green scent of memory-glass — that smell like a garden kept underwater, which clung to everything in the building and which Petra had long ago stopped noticing.

Long ago. They turned the phrase over as they wrote, the nib scratching. Long ago was a dangerous thing to say in this house. Everything here was measured against everything else, and time itself was stock in trade.

*One first kiss,* Petra wrote, in the small even hand that twenty years of practice had ground into them like pigment into oil, *appraised at four marks, twelve coppers. Quality: high. Provenance: freely sold. Condition: intact, weeping lightly at the edges.*

Across the table, under a bell jar, sat the kiss itself.

It looked like nothing much — a curl of light the size of a plum seed, gold going soft at the center to the color of late afternoon through a window. It shifted when the candleflame shifted, leaning toward warmth the way a sleeping person leans toward a body beside them. Widow Maren Osta had carried it sixty-one years. She had come up the service stair just past the ninth hour, dripping, her boots leaving crescents of wet on the stone, and she had set it on the counter between them with both hands, the way one sets down something breakable, or something already broken.

"It's all I have left of him," she'd said. "And I can't pay the rent with a feeling."

"No," Petra had agreed. "You can't."

They had weighed it while she watched. They had named the price while she watched. She had not wept, or if she had, she had done it the way the well-bred do, quietly, above the nose, her chin lifted as though daring the room to notice. Sixty-one years of a man's mouth against hers, and she traded it for coin enough to last the winter, and she walked back out into the rain without looking at the bell jar, because looking would have made it worse and not-looking was a skill the poor learned early.

Petra had logged the trade, sealed the glass, and felt — nothing. That was the craft of it. That was the whole profession, distilled: you learned to feel nothing, cleanly, on schedule, the way a butcher learns to keep his knife steady. The Court did not employ clerks who trembled. The Court employed clerks who balanced.

*Balance carried forward,* Petra wrote, and ruled the line beneath it straight and true.

* * *

At the second hour past midnight, the accounts of grief required settling.

These were not small debts, exactly, but they were not large ones either — the middle country of sorrow where most trades lived. Grief moved through Corvane the way water moved through the aqueducts: drawn off at the high houses, where the rich bought other people's mourning by the ounce because their own had run thin; pooled in the low quarters, where families sold what they could not afford to carry; and passing always, always, through the Ninefold Court, which took its tithe of everything that flowed.

Petra opened the grey ledger's sister volume — blue calf-hide, *Accounts of Traded Grief* — and began the night's reconciliation.

Three lots received from the Harrow Street collection house. Two lots dispatched upward, to buyers whose names appeared only as sigils, because grief of good vintage was a gentleman's purchase and gentlemen did not sign their names to what they drank. One lot returned, damaged: a mother's grief for a drowned son, sold and resold so many times it had gone threadbare, its edges fraying into something that was barely sorrow anymore, just a grey ache shaped like a boy. Returned lots went to the incinerator. The Court did not deal in damaged goods, and the Court did not believe in charity, and there was no drawer anywhere in the building marked *give these back to someone who might still want them.*

Petra recorded the destruction. The nib did not waver.

This was the thing about themselves that Petra Reyes understood best, the thing they had built their whole life around the way a mason builds a wall around a spring: they were precise. Not cold, precisely — precision only looked like coldness from the outside, the way a locked door looks like a wall. They could hold a widow's first kiss in a glass bell and feel the exact weight of what it was and what it cost, and they could write the number down, and the number would be fair. Fairer than the market demanded. There were clerks in the Court who shaved appraisals, who bought low from desperate sellers and booked the difference as diligence, and Petra had reported two of them in their years here, calmly, with citations, and slept afterward like a child.

Because that was the rule. Not *the* Rule — the Court's Rule, the one carved over the service door in letters old enough to predate the current dynasty:

**THE COURT TRADES IN WHAT IS GIVEN. IT TAKES NOTHING THAT IS NOT SOLD TO IT FREELY.**

Every clerk swore it on entry, hand on the Nine Books, voice steady. Most forgot it within a year, or bent it, or discovered the hundred comfortable loopholes that the masters pretended not to see. Petra had never bent it. Not once. Twenty-two years in the house, from apprentice to senior appraiser of the night desk, and not once had Petra taken, coerced, forged, or touched a memory that had not been laid on the counter willingly, price agreed, seal pressed, witnessed and entered.

It was, Petra knew, a small pride. A narrow virtue. The masters wore masks of silver and spoke of destiny, and the great ledgers held trades that had unmade marriages and ended feuds and, rumor said, once erased a war — and here was Petra, proud of keeping honest books in a house that was honest only in the way a knife is honest. But the pride was real anyway. It fit the hand. It was the one thing they owned outright.

The rain thickened against the windows. Petra dipped the pen, and wrote, and did not think about how quiet the room was, or how the quiet had a shape now after all these years, and the shape was exactly the size of a life spent among other people's feelings, cataloguing warmth by the ounce, owning none of it.

Loneliness was not in the ledgers. There was no column for it. Petra checked, sometimes, the way one checks a locked drawer knowing it will be empty.

* * *

Near the fourth hour, footsteps on the service stair.

Petra knew every footfall the house produced — the masters' measured tread, the porters' shuffle, the particular drag of Old Hesk the vault-warden's bad knee. These steps matched none of them. They were uneven, hesitant, and stopped twice before reaching the door, as though the climber kept forgetting why they had come, or kept hoping to change their mind.

The knock, when it came, was three taps. Soft. Almost apologetic.

"Closed until dawn," Petra called, without rising. "Trades resume at the sixth bell. If it's urgent, the day desk at Sablegate handles—"

The knock came again. Same three taps. And beneath it, a voice — a young voice, cracked and small and soaked through with something worse than rain — saying words that Petra's ear caught and their mind refused, for one full second, to accept.

Not Vessic. Not old Imperial. Not any of the trade-tongues or gutter-pidgins a night clerk learns across two decades of desperate sellers. This language was older than the shapes of the words suggested, older than the grammar underneath them; it moved the way riverbeds move, in curves too deep for the eye. And yet —

Petra stood. The candle flames leaned.

*I know that word,* they thought, absurdly. *That word means "door."*

They had never learned it. No one alive had learned it; the archivists' lists of dead tongues did not go back far enough to include whatever this was. But it rose in Petra anyway, surfacing through twenty-two careful years of controlled distance like a drowned thing rising through dark water — fragments, only fragments, *door*, and *stay*, and a word for a kind of bread baked in ashes that made Petra's mouth flood with a taste they had never eaten.

The rule. The Court trades in what is given. It takes nothing that is not sold freely.

Whatever stood on the other side of that door was not selling. Whatever stood there had knocked the way people knock when they are asking, not trading — and asking was outside the ledger, outside the columns, outside everything Petra Reyes had spent a lifetime keeping balanced and closed.

The rule said: do not open for what is not offered.

Petra crossed the counting room, lifted the bar, and broke their own perfect record in the space of a heartbeat — because the rule governed trades, and this was not a trade, and some small stubborn part of them had already decided that opening a door was not the same as taking.

The child on the step was maybe nine years old, barefoot, caked in mud to the knees as though it had walked out of a riverbed rather than down Sable Street. Rain ran off it and steamed faintly, though the night was cold. In one fist it clutched a coin — Petra saw it only for a moment, a flash of metal stamped with a design no mint in Corvane had ever struck — and when the child looked up, its eyes were the color of the light inside a memory-glass.

It spoke again. And this time, unaccountably, horribly, wonderfully, Petra understood almost every word.

*"I've been walking a long time,"* the child said, in a language older than kingdoms, *"and the door was supposed to be here."*

Behind Petra, down in the vault, something in the Nine Books turned over in its sleep.
