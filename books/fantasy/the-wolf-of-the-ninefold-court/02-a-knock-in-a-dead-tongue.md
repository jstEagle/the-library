## 2. A Knock in a Dead Tongue

The rain came to Corveth that night the way debts come to debtors: slowly at first, then all at once, and then it would not stop.

Petra Reyes heard it before they saw it — a hiss against the leaded glass of the ledger room, soft as sand poured through an hourglass, and beneath it the deeper sound of the Ninefold Court settling into its bones. The guildhouse was old in the way mountains are old; it creaked and groaned and spoke to itself in the dark, and after eleven years of service Petra had learned which sounds meant nothing and which ones meant someone was walking where they should not be.

This was a nothing-sound. Rain. The building breathing. Somewhere below, the furnaces that kept the memory-vaults at their precise and terrible temperature ticking over like patient hearts.

Petra dipped the pen again and wrote: *One grief, maternal, well-worn. Appraised at three marks. Sold.*

The ink took cleanly. Good ink always took cleanly on nights like this, when the air was wet enough to keep it from drying at the nib. Bad nights were the dry ones, when every word had to be coaxed out of the pen like a confession from a proud man.

It was past the second bell of morning. The widow's first kiss — appraised that afternoon, a small bright thing, tasting of cider and nerves — had already been decanted into its glass cradle two floors down. Tomorrow some merchant's son would buy it, press it into his own mouth behind his eyes, and believe for the rest of his life that he had been kissed once, long ago, by a girl under a blossoming tree, and that he had never known what became of her.

That was the trade. That was all the Court did, when you boiled away the incense and the masks and the marble: we bought what people could not bear to carry, and sold it to people who wished they had carried it themselves. Grief for joy. Joy for grief. First loves for last regrets. The ledgers balanced, always, because the Court's founders had built balance into the very stone, and if one side of the scale grew too heavy the whole guildhouse would lean, subtly, imperceptibly, until you found yourself walking uphill in your own corridors.

Petra kept the small-debts ledger. It was considered humble work — kisses, lullabies, the smell of a dead father's coat, a child's belief in something kinder than the world proved to be. The great clerks handled the grand trades: whole marriages, decades of love, the memories of kings purchased by the men who would replace them. But Petra had never wanted the grand trades. Grand trades required grand detachment, and Petra's detachment, though genuine, was a modest domestic thing, like a good lock on a modest door. It worked. It held. It did not need to be magnificent.

*Never open a ledger you weren't assigned.* That was the rule. Not the only rule — the Court had rules the way a wolf has teeth — but the one that mattered, the one Master Ossory himself had set as a test on Petra's first day, tapping one gray-gloved finger on the shelf of forbidden folios. *The ledgers remember who reads them,* Ossory had said, in that voice like dry paper turning. *And they tell us.*

Eleven years. Not once. Petra took a certain pride in that, the way a tightrope walker takes pride in the wire, though a walker with any honesty admits there are nights the wire is all there is.

They blew out the lamp, squared the ledger on its shelf, and went down through the sleeping guildhouse toward the clerk's cell they rented from the Court at a rate calculated, with typical precision, to leave exactly enough for bread, coal, and the occasional cup of wine that made the wire easier to walk.

The service door was at the bottom of the scullery stair, a slab of black oak older than the city around it, banded in iron gone soft with centuries of hands. It opened onto the mews lane, where the night-soil carts came and the deliveries of ice came and, twice a year, the anonymous wagons came with their cargo of sealed jars that no one asked about. Petra passed it every day without looking at it. A door is a door.

The knock came at the third bell.

Three strikes, spaced wrong — not the rhythm of a fist but of something smaller, knuckles perhaps, or the heel of a palm, striking once, waiting, striking twice more as if unsure the door still existed between blows.

Petra stood at the foot of the scullery stair with the candle raised and listened. Rain. The house breathing. Then the knock again, weaker now, and this time followed by a sound that undid all Petra's careful training in a single heartbeat:

A child crying. Quietly. The way children cry when they have learned that crying loudly accomplishes nothing.

Every rule Petra had ever kept said: *light no lamp, open no door, wake the watchman, let the watchman deal with it.* The Court did not reward kindness. Kindness was an irregularity, and irregularities in the ledgers got people audited, and audits ended with men in gray gloves carrying jars down into the vaults.

Petra lit the lamp anyway.

The bolt was cold under their thumb. The hinges made no sound at all, which should have been the first warning — everything in the Ninefold Court sounded, everything groaned or ticked or whispered, and a door that moved silently was a door that had been waiting.

The child fell more than stepped across the threshold.

Mud — real mud, river mud, the gray-brown silt of the Vell, though the Vell ran nowhere near the mews lane — sheeted the child from bare feet to chin. Hair plastered flat. Lips blue-tinged. Eyes so dark they seemed less colored than *deep*, as though the child's face had been painted by someone who understood shadows better than light. Maybe seven years old. Maybe younger; hunger and cold edit age downward. Clad in a rough smock of a weave Petra had never seen, dyed a color that wasn't quite any color, the green-gray of water over stones.

The child looked up at Petra, and stopped crying instantly, the way a clock stops when you lift its pendulum.

"You're late," the child said.

The words were nonsense. Petra knew that immediately, the way one knows a coin is counterfeit — not through analysis but through a lifetime of handling the true article. The language was none of the nine tongues of Corveth, none of the merchant pidgins, none of the high speech of the northern courts or the low burr of the docks. The vowels came from somewhere else entirely. The consonants were shaped like things that had not been spoken aloud in a very long time.

No living scholar knew this language. Petra knew this the way everyone in Corveth knew it, because it was one of those facts that sat in the civic blood alongside the color of the sky: there had been, once, a country called Vhalen, east beyond the salt flats, and it had been destroyed so completely — by war, by plague, by the slow spite of geography, the histories disagreed and the histories were mostly blank anyway — that even its language had died unrecorded. Scholars wrote papers about the *absence*. There was a chair at the University called the Chair of Vhalenic Studies, and its occupant spent his career studying a country the way one studies a hole in cloth.

So the words were nonsense. And yet —

"You're late," the child had said, and Petra had *understood*, the way you understand a shout from another room: not the words but the meaning arriving whole. Late. As in *I have been knocking. As in I walked farther than you know. As in the door should have been open already.*

"I don't—" Petra began, and heard their own voice come out strange, half a register lower, the accent of it wrong, vowels rounding in a mouth that had never been theirs. "—know what you're saying."

Which was true. And also a lie. Both at once, cleanly, the way a ledger can show two columns that sum to the same total.

The child tilted their head. Mud dripped from their jaw onto the flagstones, each drop landing with a small final sound, like punctuation.

"Late," the child said again, slower, and pointed one small muddy finger back out into the rain, back the way they had come, and Petra felt — not thought, *felt*, the way one feels a floorboard give — a stretch of distance that had not been traveled by feet. Felt salt wind. Felt a horizon flat as a held breath. Felt, absurdly, homesickness, thick and specific, for a place with bells in it. Bells and harvest smoke and a tower with nine sisters standing around it, and the smell of grain drying, and a word for the moment when the sun clears the hills that has no equivalent in any tongue Petra owned—

Petra put one hand against the doorframe.

The feeling passed, or was passed back, or was taken away — it left the way a tide leaves, dragging at the ankles. The child watched them with those depthless eyes and did not seem surprised. That was the worst part. The child looked at Petra the way a clerk looks at a ledger that has finally, after long searching, been found.

"You *are* late," the child said, and this time the nonsense-word landed differently, softer, almost kind, and Petra understood *late* to mean something closer to *kept waiting*, closer to *the world forgot and you didn't*.

"Come inside," Petra heard themself say. Rule-breaking number two in the space of a minute. The rules were falling off them like wet clothes. "Before someone sees."

*

The child ate like winter itself was being fed. Bread, dripping, the heel of hard cheese, a cup of watered wine that Petra watered further and the child drank without comment and without blinking, which told Petra something about what the child had been drinking lately. They sat on the scullery bench while the fire Petra had dared to build cracked softly in the grate, steaming the mud on the child's skin into a smell of river and iron and something faintly sweet underneath, like cut grain.

"Name," Petra tried, touching their own chest. "Petra."

The child considered this with grave attention, as though translating across a greater distance than the width of a kitchen.

"Name," the child agreed, and touched their own chest, and said a word.

The word was four syllables and none of them survived the journey from the child's mouth to Petra's ears intact. It was like trying to hold water in a net. Petra caught maybe the shape of it, the melody, a rising third and a falling close — and then, unbidden, unwelcome, from somewhere that was absolutely nowhere in Petra's own remembered life, rose the knowledge that names in that tongue weren't spoken so much as *rung*, that a name was a small bell struck inside the listener, and that this particular name meant something like *all-of-us-at-once* or *the-many-in-one* or possibly, in its oldest usage, *harvest*.

"That's not a name," Petra said. "That's a census."

The child blinked. Did not understand the words. Understood the tone perfectly, and smiled — the first smile, small and crooked and mud-framed, and it went through Petra like a knife through wet paper, because it was such an ordinary smile, such a *human* smile, worn smooth by use, the smile of a child who has been smiled at and knows the shape to make back.

Then the child held out their hand, palm up, the way one offers a thing for inspection, or payment.

On the muddy palm lay a coin.

*

Petra had appraised ten thousand objects in eleven years. Coins most of all; coins were the Court's least glamorous currency and its most constant, because memory-traders needed to be paid and paid discreetly, and there was no discretion like a common coin. Petra knew the mints of nine kingdoms by touch alone. Knew the clipped edges of Corveth copper, the false silver from the southern presses that rang a quarter-tone flat, the gold of the eastern empire that was always, always slightly too heavy, because emperors cannot help advertising.

This coin was none of them.

It was silver, or had been. Now it was the color of old bone. It bore no king's profile, no eagle, no crossed swords. On one face: a tower. Nine towers, rather, arranged in a ring around a central spire, rendered in lines so fine they might have been drawn with a single hair — and the perspective of the engraving was impossible, showing every tower face-on at once, the way things appear in dreams. On the other face: not a number, not a date, but a single character in a script of interlocking curves, and Petra's mind supplied its meaning before Petra could stop it, the meaning rising up like a bubble through dark water:

*One bell. All voices.*

"Where," Petra said quietly, "did you get this?"

The child closed their fingers over the coin and drew it back against their chest — not greedily. Protectively. The way one protects a thing that has already cost everything it can cost. And they said a sentence in the dead tongue, a full sentence, several clauses long, and Petra understood perhaps one word in five, and the one word in five was enough to turn the scullery cold:

The word that meant, as near as Petra's haunted mind could render it: *they are coming*.

Or possibly *it is coming*. Or *we are coming*. The grammar refused to sit still. But underneath the grammar, underneath the translation, the child's face said the rest plainly enough, the way children's faces do: fear, held very carefully, the way one holds a full cup while walking.

Outside, the rain went on falling on Corveth, indifferent, ancient, falling on roofs and gutters and the mews lane and the salt flats far to the east where no rain ever fell at all.

And beneath the rain, from somewhere above — from the upper floors where the masters slept behind their masks, or from the vaults below where the furnaces ticked, Petra could not tell which, could not tell *where*, only that it came from everywhere in the guildhouse at once like a change in pressure — came a sound.

Faint. Almost nothing. The sound of a very large building paying attention.

The child heard it too. The child went rigid, the coin disappearing into their fist, and looked up at Petra with an expression of terrible recognition — the look of a hunted thing that knows the hunters' footsteps, knows them the way prey knows them, in the marrow, before the ears.

"They're coming," the child said, in the language of a country that had never existed, and this time Petra understood every word.
