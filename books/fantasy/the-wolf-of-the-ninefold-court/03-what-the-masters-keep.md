## 3. What the Masters Keep

The summons came at the hour when even the lamps grow tired.

Petra knew it was a summons and not an invitation by the shape of it: a slip of grey vellum slid beneath the ledger-room door without footsteps to announce it, sealed with the Court's sigil — nine interlocking rings around a hollow center. No courier in the Ninefold Court walked that quietly. Either the paper had delivered itself, or someone had wanted Petra to believe it had. Both possibilities sat equally ill in the stomach.

The instruction was brief, as instructions from above always were. *Attend. The Concourse. Bring nothing you would grieve to lose.*

Petra read it twice, then set it down on the great oak desk beside the open ledger of the evening's accounts — the widow's first kiss, appraised at three silver marks; two measures of grief traded from a shipwright for a season of untroubled sleep; the small, steady commerce of other people's pasts. Petra closed the ledger, squared its corners out of habit, and looked once at the door behind which, in the linen press, something very small breathed very slowly.

They had told no one about the child. Not yet. Not the night-wardens, not the archivists, not the quiet brotherhood of clerks who kept the Court's books and asked no questions because asking questions was how clerks stopped being clerks. When the child had appeared at the service door — mud to the knees, speaking that impossible language that had somehow opened doors inside Petra's own skull — Petra had made a decision so quickly it had not felt like a decision at all. They had brought the child inside. Hidden them among the linens. Given them bread and salt water, the old remedy for shock, though where Petra had learned that remedy they could not say. It had simply been *there*, the way the fragments of the dead tongue were there, surfacing like drowned things rising.

*Vhalen.* Even now the word moved through Petra's mind like a fish under ice. They did not know what it meant. They only knew that when the child said it, pressing one small hand flat against Petra's chest as if checking whether anything still beat beneath the wool and ink-stains, Petra had understood it the way one understands one's own name — completely, and without being able to explain why.

Now the masters wanted an audience. And the child slept in a linen press four floors below the Concourse, smelling of river mud and something older than mud, something like rain on stone that had never known rain before.

Petra locked the ledger room, pocketed both keys, and climbed.

* * *

No one entered the Concourse by choice. That was the first thing every clerk learned, and the last thing most of them ever said aloud about it: the stair up to the masters' hall seemed to climb itself, drawing visitors upward the way deep water draws things down. By the time Petra reached the top their legs ached with the effort of resistance, though they had not consciously resisted at all.

The Concourse had no right to be as large as it was. The guildhouse occupied a narrow wedge between a chandler's yard and a defunct counting-house, seven floors in all, cramped and crooked as a spine. But the hall at its crown stretched farther than the building could contain, its ceiling lost in a darkness that was not merely absence of light but a kind of patient, listening dark. Nine thrones stood upon a dais of black glass, arranged in a crescent, and in each throne sat a figure robed and masked.

Nine masks. Petra had seen them perhaps six times in eleven years of service, always from across some ceremonial distance, always briefly enough to convince oneself afterward that they had dreamed it. Each mask was white as bleached bone and smooth as an egg, featureless except at the eyes — and the eyes were not holes. They were windows, or wells, or something worse: depths in which light moved of its own accord, slow and colored, like weather seen from very far away. No two masks showed the same weather. One flickered with the green-gold shimmer of wheat under wind. Another churned with storm-grey and the cold iron gleam of winter rivers. A third held a color Petra had no name for, a blue so old it seemed to predate the word *blue*, and looking into it too long made the back of Petra's teeth ache.

The masters did not wear faces. They wore memories. Everyone in the Court knew this much and pretended otherwise — that somewhere in the deep vaults below, the founding treasures of the guild were kept, and that the nine who ruled wore pieces of that treasure against their skin, the way kings wear crowns, the way wounds wear scars.

"Ledger-Keeper Reyes."

The voice came from the center throne, and it took Petra a moment to understand that the speaker had risen — that the tall figure descending the dais steps with unhurried grace was Master Ossory, whose mask burned with a low amber light, honey-colored and warm, warm the way a hearth is warm, the way a fever is warm. Ossory alone ever came down from the dais to meet the servants. He claimed it was courtesy. Everyone understood it was a demonstration: that he could come close, and that closeness cost him nothing.

"Master." Petra bowed precisely the correct depth — deep enough for respect, shallow enough to preserve the fiction that clerks were colleagues rather than property. "You summoned."

"We summoned." Ossory's voice was dry silk over gravel, a voice built for reading last wills and testaments. "And yet we find ourselves curious, Ledger-Keeper, why our summoning was necessary at all. You have something of ours."

The lamps along the walls — there were no lamps along the walls; Petra's eyes insisted there were lamps, guttering tallow flames in iron sconces, and refused all correction — dimmed slightly, as if the hall itself were leaning in.

"I keep many things," Petra said. "It's my work. Nothing in my keeping is anyone's but the Court's."

"Ah." Ossory tilted his mask, and the amber light within it swirled, and for one vertiginous moment Petra felt the sensation of being *appraised* — truly appraised, weighed and valued and priced, the way they themselves had spent eleven years pricing other people's moments. It was a hideous feeling. It explained everything about the masters' patience. "Yes. Diligence is your ornament, isn't it? Eleven years without a single discrepancy. Without opening a ledger you weren't assigned. Do you know what a rarity that is, Reyes? In eleven years, the temptation has never once —"

"Never," Petra said. Too fast. Too flat.

The silence that followed lasted exactly long enough for Petra to hear their own pulse in their ears. Somewhere above the unreachable ceiling, something shifted — a vast weight resettling, or the building remembering it was only a building after all.

Ossory laughed. It was a gentle sound, almost kind, and it frightened Petra more than anger would have. "Good. Hold to that. A clerk without discipline is a candle in wind. Now." He turned, robes whispering, and gestured toward the dais, toward the eight seated figures whose eye-wells had not blinked because they had no lids to blink. "Bring your report. All of it. Tonight a thing came to our service door that does not appear in any register of this city, this kingdom, or any kingdom extant. A child. Mud-marked. Speaking in tongues that predate tongues." The amber light flared. "Our wardens are thorough, Ledger-Keeper, but records can be... incomplete. Tell us what you saw."

*Lie,* said something in Petra, in a voice that was not quite theirs — or was theirs, older and truer than the careful clerk they had built themselves into. *Lie now.*

But Petra had never lied to the Court. Eleven years. And lying, they discovered in that moment, was a skill like any other, and they had never practiced, and their first attempt would be performed before nine immortal witnesses.

"The child spoke," Petra said. "I heard fragments. I couldn't parse them. The wardens drove it off toward the river gate — I assumed it drowned or fled, either way it's gone. I logged the incident." Every word true individually, which was the only trick Petra knew: truth, arranged dishonestly. "There was little to report."

"You logged it," Ossory repeated softly.

"In the incident ledger. Third entry."

"Third entry." Ossory descended another step, and another, until he stood close enough that Petra could feel warmth radiating off him — actual warmth, hearth-heat, though the hall was cold as a crypt. "Then you won't mind if we check."

* * *

What Petra had actually written in the incident ledger, three hours earlier, with hands that had only begun to shake after the pen was put down:

*Entry the third. At the hour of the flood-tide, a vagrant child presented at the service door. Species of disturbance: unknown tongue, unintelligible. Disposition: driven off, eastward, toward the water. No further note warranted.*

*No further note warranted.* Six words that Petra had chosen with the same care they gave to appraising a widow's kiss, because those six words were the entire architecture of the lie. Everything else was true. The child had spoken. The words had been unintelligible — to anyone else. To Petra they had been half a hymn, half a lullaby, half a list of names, and the fractions did not sum correctly, and Petra had written *unintelligible* while their own blood whispered translations.

Ossory read the entry standing over Petra's shoulder, which was itself a violation of every protocol the Court maintained about hierarchy and distance, and Petra understood that the violation was the message: *your rules do not apply up here; they barely apply anywhere.*

"Mm," Ossory said. "Unintelligible."

"Yes, master."

"And yet." He straightened. Beneath the hem of his robe, his feet made no sound on the black glass. "And yet the child was found this morning, before dawn, in the chandlers' lane, and it was not driven off, eastward or elsewhere. It was taken in. Fed. Sheltered." The amber eyes turned. "By persons unknown. Our wardens lost it between your ledger room and the street. Curious, that a mud-soaked foundling should navigate our halls better than men who have walked them thirty years."

"Children are quick," Petra said. "Small spaces suit them."

"Do they." Ossory considered this. Around the crescent of the dais, the eight seated figures had begun, very slowly, to lean forward — or perhaps the hall was tilting, or perhaps distance simply meant less here than it should. "Reyes. You understand what the child is."

"No, master."

"No?" The warmth of Ossory's attention intensified, unbearable, like standing at an open oven. "Then let me educate you, since education is clearly wasted on your betters tonight. There was once a country, Ledger-Keeper. Before the maps you've copied, before the kingdoms you've catalogued, before the language you think in. It was called Vhalen. It was beautiful, and it was proud, and it made a mistake that proud places make: it trusted its neighbors. Nine of them came against it, and when the armies failed — Vhalen's armies were never beaten, mark you, not once — the nine tried another war entirely. A war of memory. They unmade Vhalen the way you'd burn a library: page by page, mind by mind, until no one remembered it had existed, including the people who lived there. Including, in time, the victors themselves. We wrote the absence into every map and every chronicle and then we forgot that we had written it. That is the deepest kind of victory, Reyes. A crime with no witness, not even the criminal."

He paused, and the amber light in his mask guttered like a candle in a draft.

"But a country is not a book. Books stay burned. A country leaves residue. Rain remembers falling. Bells remember ringing. And memory, denied every vessel — every person, every stone, every grave — will eventually build itself a vessel." His voice softened into something almost reverent. "Last night, one washed up at our door. The last loose thread of Vhalen, knotted into flesh. Everything that country was, compressed into something small enough to carry. Small enough," he added, and here the warmth turned by degrees colder, the way gold turns cold, "to *process*."

The word went through Petra like a hook through soft flesh.

"Process," Petra repeated.

"It must be decanted," Ossory said, as reasonably as a man discussing wine. "A vessel that size, holding that much — it will rupture within days, perhaps hours. Everything inside will spill and dissipate and be gone forever, wasted. But poured properly, preserved in the old ways, it can be kept. Studied. Drawn upon. A country, Reyes, in a bottle. Imagine the inventory." He smiled; the mask did not, but the voice smiled, and that was worse. "Take us to it."

Every drop of moisture left Petra's mouth at once. "To — I don't know where it is, master. I told you. Driven off. East—"

"Reyes." Ossory's tone did not change at all, which was how Petra knew they were already dead if they kept talking this way. "Eleven years without opening a wrong ledger. Do you imagine we don't know what that costs you? What you're owed? We have watched you balance grief for strangers with the patience of a saint and go home to rooms with one cup in the cupboard. We know you, Ledger-Keeper. We know you better than you know yourself, because parts of you are filed downstairs." He leaned in, and the amber light filled Petra's whole vision, honey and fever. "So I will ask plainly, and you will answer plainly, because the arithmetic of this night is very simple: tell us where the child is hidden, or we will find it ourselves, and then we will ask what role you played in conceiving it — and the answers to that question are extracted, not given."

* * *

Afterwards, Petra would never be able to say which impulse won. Perhaps neither. Perhaps it was only the oldest reflex in the body — the one that makes a hand close around a falling thing before the mind agrees.

"The linen press," they heard themselves say. "Ledger room. Behind the third shelf."

Because the alternative was the vault. Because Ossory had already turned away, satisfied, signaling with two fingers, and the hall's doors were opening downward instead of outward, and Petra understood with sickening clarity that they were going to be shown the processing regardless — that obedience had purchased not the child's safety but only Petra's presence at its unmaking. Unless. Unless the child was not where Petra had said. Unless the linen press held blankets and dust and nothing else, because sometime in the last hour — during the climb, during the summons, during the long cold walk — the child had done what mud-soaked foundlings apparently did in these halls, and slipped away through spaces too small for wardens, following some instinct older than the guild itself.

Petra had checked the press before leaving. Hadn't they? They had locked the door. The keys were in their pocket. They could feel them, sharp-edged, real.

Or could they. In this hall, where lamps hung from walls that had no lamps, certainty itself was merchandise.

* * *

The vault lay beneath the guildhouse, beneath the cellars, beneath the forgotten underlevels that appeared on no plan Petra had ever catalogued — down a spiral stair so old its steps were worn into shallow bowls, lit by glass globes full of pale liquid light that might once have been someone's wedding day. Ossory led. Two wardens flanked Petra, close enough that their shoulders brushed. Eight masters remained above, or did not; the geometry of the Concourse had stopped meaning anything.

The last door was iron, banded in bronze, and it opened at Ossory's touch with a sound like a sigh released after centuries.

Inside was cold. Not winter-cold. *Absence*-cold, the cold of places where warmth had been removed rather than never present. And the shelves —

The shelves rose into darkness on every side, hundreds of them, thousands, and every shelf held glass.

Glass vessels of every shape: jars and flasks, bells and spheres, long thin vials like the ones apothecaries use for poisons, great rounded carboys like ship's ballast. And in each one, light. Not the liquid glow of the stair-lamps — living light, moving light, light that had texture and season and weather inside it. Petra stood in the aisle and watched a jar the size of a fist fill and refill with the particular gold of late afternoon on water. Watched a tall flask hold what could only be someone's mother's voice, swirling faintly, shaped like breath on a window. Watched, in a vessel like a birdcage of blown glass, the unmistakable green-gold shimmer of wheat moving under wind, and felt their chest tighten around a homesickness for a field they had never stood in.

Memories. Decanted. Labeled in a script that predated the kingdoms, that predated — Petra's stomach dropped — that matched, stroke for stroke, the marks the child had traced in spilled flour on the ledger-room floor.

"This is what we keep," Ossory said, walking the aisles like a man walking his orchard. His voice had gone tender, and the tenderness was the most obscene thing Petra had ever heard. "Not stolen goods, whatever the street says. Rescues. Every one of these was dying — fading in some failing head, drowning in some dying elder, scattered at some senseless death. We catch them. We keep them safe. Forever, if we choose." He lifted a small sphere, cradled it, and within it snow fell eternally on a village square where a girl was eternally laughing. "Somewhere out there is a woman who sold us her childhood to pay a debt. She sleeps better for lighter. And here, look — it snows whenever I want it to."

He set the sphere back with a click that echoed down the endless aisles.

"Your child will join them by morning. Decanted gently — I promise you gentleness, Reyes; we are not monsters, we are *curators*. And in exchange, the Court will owe you a debt beyond reckoning. Name it later. Anything in this room. Anything in the world." The amber mask inclined. "You've earned a withdrawal."

And Petra, standing in the cold among ten thousand rescued, imprisoned yesterdays, understood suddenly and completely that they had just lied to the most dangerous beings alive — that somewhere above them the linen press was empty or full, that the child was caught or free, and that whichever it was, Petra had stepped off the edge of their own life and was now falling, and the only question left was what they would still be holding when they landed.

Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, a bell began to ring — an alarm, thin and urgent.

Ossory's head came up sharply.

"That bell," he said, very quietly, "is the ledger room."
