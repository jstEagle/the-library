## 4. The Child Who Remembers Countries

The ledger room of the Ninefold Court smelled the way all old paper smells — like a forest that had agreed to forget being alive. Petra knew every inch of it by dark: the iron shelves bolted floor to ceiling, the ledgers bound in grey calfskin and chained at their spines, the single lamp whose flame burned a color found nowhere else in the world, a green so old it had gone almost gold. They had worked here eleven years. In eleven years they had never once opened a ledger they were not assigned to open.

They told themselves, as they barred the door behind them with hands that shook only slightly, that this was not the same thing. The child was not a ledger.

The child stood in the corner where the shadow of the shelving pooled deepest, dripping river-mud onto a floor that had never in its history been permitted to hold a stain. Someone would notice that mud by morning. Petra looked at it and thought, absurdly, *I will have to sweep*, and then thought, just as absurdly, *I have never once swept this room*, and understood that something in their life had already tipped past the horizon line, quietly, the way a boat tips when you are looking at the water instead of your own feet.

"Sit," Petra said. "Don't touch anything."

The child sat on the floor itself, cross-legged, unbothered, as though floors were exactly what floors were for. Up close, in the green-gold light, they resolved into particulars that made no comfortable sense. The child was perhaps eight years old, or perhaps sixty; the face kept deciding between the two and settling on neither. Mud had dried in the creases of their knuckles. Their eyes were the brown of water over old wood, and they did not blink as often as eyes should. When they did blink, Petra felt a faint pressure behind their own sternum, like a bell rung somewhere below hearing.

"You understand me," Petra said. "Some of it. Don't you."

The child tilted their head. Then they spoke — that language again, the one that predated every kingdom, the one three scholars of the Athenaeum had wept over for want of even a syllable — but this time Petra heard it differently. Not as noise with meaning hidden inside it. As meaning wearing noise like a coat worn too long, threadbare at the elbows.

*Where,* the child said. Or perhaps: *When.* The word came up through Petra's own chest, surfacing from a place they had never been, and they tasted salt and cut grain on the back of the tongue as they heard it.

"I don't know how I know that word," Petra said aloud, because saying things aloud kept them true. "I've never been anywhere. I was born in the harbor district. I've appraised nine hundred memories and kept none of them. That is the whole of my life."

The child reached out one muddy hand and laid it, gently, on the toe of Petra's boot.

* * *

Petra had spent eleven years handling memories secondhand. They could tell grief from guilt by weight alone through the glass. They had once held a vial containing the last living recollection of a drowned fisherman's daughter and read its appraisal notes without their voice changing once. Distance was the whole of their craft. Distance was the wall they had built around their life, stone by stone, and called it discipline, and called it pride.

The child's hand broke the wall in under a breath.

There was no transition. One moment Petra was standing in the ledger room with wet boot leather against their toes; the next they were kneeling in a field of wheat so tall it brushed an adult's chin, under a sun that was *wrong* — warmer than warmth, a gold that meant something, that carried a name the way people carry names — while ten thousand stalks moved together in a wind that smelled of bread not yet baked. Somewhere beyond the field, bells. Not church bells. Bells with no god attached, rung for the pure administrative pleasure of announcing that the harvest had come in, that the country was fed, that another year had been folded safely into the granaries of the world. People were singing off-key on purpose, the way harvest singers do, and the wrongness of the notes was part of the song.

A woman turned toward Petra and laughed, and her teeth had a chip in the left incisor, and she said a word —

— and Petra was back in the ledger room, on their knees now, both hands pressed flat to the cold flagstones, breathing like a man hauled up from deep water.

The child watched them with terrible patience.

"What," Petra managed, "was that."

The child pointed down. At the floor. At, apparently, the world.

"No." Petra sat back on their heels. Their heart was still running harvest-rhythm. "No one remembers that. There is no such country. I balance the Court's accounts against the histories of nine kingdoms, and there has never been a country with — with bells like that, with wheat like that. It isn't in any ledger. It isn't in any map."

The child's mouth did something that was almost a smile but arrived at grief first. They held out their hand again, palm up. An offering. A question.

Every trained instinct Petra owned said *do not*. Every instinct they had spent eleven years sharpening said that memory was debt, that debt was danger, that the only safe way through the Ninefold Court was to owe nothing and carry nothing and feel nothing that could be weighed. And beneath those instincts, quieter, lonelier, older: eleven years of appraising other people's first kisses and harvest days and dead mothers' voices, cataloguing joy at arm's length, going home each night to rooms that contained nothing anyone would ever want to steal.

Petra took the child's hand.

* * *

The palace had nine towers, and each tower rang its own hour, and the hours argued.

That was the first thing, and it was such a small thing, and it undid Petra more thoroughly than the wheat field had. Nine towers of white stone veined with something blue that caught the light and held it a half-second too long, and from each tower a bell, and the bells were tuned a quarter-tone apart so that on the hour the whole city heard a chord — a slightly sour, stubborn, beloved chord that every citizen of the country complained about and no citizen of the country would have changed for any treasure. Children were taught to sing along with the clash of it. Old men claimed you could set your mood by which tower you listened to. A blind baker two streets from the palace had composed, over forty years, a private theory about why the ninth tower ran late, and his theory was wrong, and he told it to everyone, and everyone let him, because it was his.

*I know that,* Petra thought, inside the memory, inside the crowd flowing across a bridge over a slow green river. *I know the baker. I have never met him and I know he keeps his flour under lock because his apprentice steals it for love-charms.* The knowledge arrived the way your own name arrives — no learning in it at all, just possession.

The flashes came faster after that, the child no longer able or willing to ration them:

A winter market where the stalls sold nothing but memories — small ones, cheap ones, the taste of a specific snowfall traded for a copper — and a girl of six counted out her coins with enormous ceremony to buy her grandmother's memory of a dance fifty years dead, so the grandmother could have it back before spring.

A funeral where no one wept, because in this country the dead were remembered aloud, formally, by everyone who had ever known them, in shifts, for a year and a day, until the remembering wore smooth as river stone and became less a wound than a shape the living fit their hands around.

A night sky with a ninth star that appeared on no chart Petra had ever balanced accounts against, low over the northern hills, red as a banked coal, that the astronomers of the nine towers had named, variously, depending on the century, the Ember, the Warden, and — in the oldest records, the records written in that same dead tongue — *the Door That Watches.*

And underneath all of it, everywhere, woven through everything like warp through weft: the feeling of the country itself. Not geography. *Consent.* Millions of lives agreeing, daily, without ever discussing it, to be a single continuous thing — to keep the bells sour, to keep the baker's theory alive, to remember the dead in shifts. Vhalen was not a place. Vhalen was an agreement so vast and so old that no one inside it could see it, the way the eye cannot see itself.

Then gray.

It happened mid-flash. Petra was standing in the harvest field again — the woman with the chipped tooth laughing beside them — and the gold went out of the sun like a coin wiped clean. Not darkness. *Absence.* The wheat stayed upright but stopped meaning anything. The bells rang on and the sound passed through Petra without catching, the way light passes through glass. Faces continued moving, mouths opening on words, and the words fell off them like ash. The woman turned to laugh at Petra and there was nothing behind her eyes where the laugh had been — not emptiness, which is at least a shape, but *unwrittenness*, a page before ink, and Petra screamed, or tried to, and had no mouth in the memory to scream with —

— and was back. Flagstones. Lamp. The child's small hand still holding theirs, grip surprisingly strong, and the child crying.

Not sobbing. Crying the way stone weeps groundwater — silently, continuously, from everywhere at once. Tears cutting clean tracks down the mud on their face.

"They took it out of the middle," Petra whispered. Their voice sounded borrowed. "Mid-harvest. Mid-*word*. You didn't end. You were *interrupted*."

The child nodded, once, and the nod carried the weight of a nation.

Petra sat down properly on the cold floor beside the child, because their legs had resigned without consulting them. They looked at the small mud-streaked figure in the lamplight and finally said the thing that had been assembling itself in them since the wheat field, since the bells, since the baker with his locked flour.

"You're not a child who remembers a country."

The child looked at them.

"You *are* the country. What's left of it. Compressed." Petra's hands were shaking again; they let them. "You're what didn't get eaten. All of it — the bells, the baker, the harvests, the funerals — packed down into one body small enough to walk through a door. To *run* through a door." They laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "The Court trades in stolen memories. I have spent eleven years weighing stolen moments of ordinary lives in little glass bottles. And you are — you are an entire *country* of ordinary lives, walking around in the mud, and the masters put you in a cell and sent for Ossory, and I —"

They stopped. Dread arrived then, punctual as the sour ninth-hour chord, and settled over the wonder like a lid.

Because Petra knew what the Court did with valuable stock. They knew what the vault below was for. They had seen the decanting racks, the rows upon rows of glass, the long shelves where a person's whole remembered childhood could sit in a dozen vials waiting for a buyer rich enough to want someone else's mother. And they understood — with the flat, sick certainty of a clerk reading a column that finally sums — that a compressed nation would not be kept in one bottle. It would be *portioned*. Sold by the harvest. By the funeral rite. By tower. The richest men in nine kingdoms would drink Vhalen a thimbleful at a time and never know they were drinking a country, and the masters would call it the find of the age, and the ledgers would balance beautifully, and the bells would ring in no one's chest ever again.

"They're going to pour you out," Petra said. "Vial by vial. Until there's nothing left to pour."

The child wiped their face with a muddy sleeve, leaving the tracks wider rather than cleaner, and looked at Petra with those unblinking brown-water eyes. Then they lifted their hand — not palm-up this time, not offering — and pointed, slowly and deliberately, at the chained ledgers on the iron shelves. At the grey calfskin spines. At the oldest shelf, the one no clerk was assigned to, the one whose chains bore locks that matched none of the keys hung in the wardens' hall.

Petra followed the pointing finger and felt the hair rise on their arms.

"The founding ledgers," they said. "Nobody opens those. Nobody is *permitted* to know what's in those."

The child kept pointing.

Somewhere far above them, muffled by stone and distance, a bell began to toll — not the Court's hour-bell, but the summons bell, the one that called the nine masked masters to conclave, the one that had not sounded in Petra's eleven years of service. Its note slid down through the building's bones and shivered the lamp flame.

The child lowered their hand and, in Petra's own language, learned sometime in the last hour from God knew where, said four words in a voice like a small bell cracking:

"They are coming down."
