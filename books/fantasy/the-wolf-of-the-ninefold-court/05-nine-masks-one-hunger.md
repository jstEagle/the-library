## 5. Nine Masks, One Hunger

Master Ossory rose before dawn, as he had for four hundred years, because hunger kept its own hours.

The hunger was not in his belly. He had not needed bread since the reign of the second Aldemar king, though he still ate on feast days out of sentiment and to keep the clerks from wondering. No — this hunger lived behind his eyes, in the warm hollow where a man keeps his oldest recollections. It woke with him each morning like a dog that knows the hour of its feeding, and it would not be quiet until he had gone down into the dark place beneath the Court and put his lips, so to speak, to the country's throat.

He dressed slowly. His hands were old men's hands by any honest measure — spotted, veined, knuckles swollen like the roots of an olive tree — but they did not shake, and they never had. That was the first gift. The others came after.

In the antechamber outside his chambers hung a mirror taller than two men, framed in black walnut carved with vines that no longer grew anywhere in the world. Ossory did not look at it. He had not looked at himself in the true glass in three centuries, and he recommended the practice to every serious person he met: a face is a rumor; a mask is a fact.

He took the mask down instead.

It was called the Shepherd's Face, though it had not always borne that name. Before it was a mask it had been a morning — one specific morning, in a country that no map admitted to — the last morning Vhalen ever had, seen through the eyes of a shepherd boy on a hill above the river Vessa. The memory had been cut whole from that boy, who had been paid in bread and then, when the ledgers required it, in nothing at all. In those days the craft was young and the cutting was crude; the boy's mother had screamed for eleven days afterward without once knowing why. Ossory had been there. Ossory had held her hand. He remembered — how could he not? — the exact temperature of her fingers going cold.

Now the morning hung inside the lacquered shell like light caught in amber, and when Ossory lifted it to his face, the world changed.

* * *

Here is what the masters of the Ninefold Court know, and what no clerk among them suspects, and what no ledger records:

That the Court was not founded to trade in memories. Trading was the means, not the end. A guild that buys and sells the past must, above all things, accumulate it — and accumulation was the founding purpose, the sacred purpose, set down before the guildhall had walls or a name.

That Vhalen existed. Not *had existed*. Existed — present tense, subjunctive mood, hidden in the grammar of things the way a splinter hides in the palm. Nine founders went into that green country with knives of doctrine and came out carrying its mornings in their fists, and where a thing has been taken out completely enough, the world obligingly forgets there was ever a space for it. Maps redrew themselves. Songs lost their middle verses and no singer noticed the gap. Wives who had married Vhaleni husbands woke widowed and could not say of what. The erasure was the masterpiece. The theft was merely the paint.

And that each founder took one piece — one memory, vast and deep as a lake, cut from the country itself rather than from any single soul — and wore it. Not carried. *Worn.* For a memory does not die in a keeping-glass the way a moth dies in a jar; but neither does it live. It waits. And a mind pressed against waiting glass for long enough learns to drink through the wall.

Ossory settled the Shepherd's Face over his own and felt the familiar cold, then the warmth, then the *opening*, like a door unbarred somewhere behind his sternum.

The morning poured into him.

Grass wet to the knee. River-smell, willow-smell, woodsmoke from the valley where the baker's ovens were already lit because in Vhalen the bakers rose in the dark, believing — the boy had believed, and so now Ossory believed, four hundred years stale and no less sweet — that the day deserved to be greeted by someone awake. Bell-song from the nine towers, staggered and overlapping, each tower ringing a different hour of the same moment because the Vhaleni had never been able to agree on time and had made a music of their disagreement. And over all of it the sun coming up along the eastern hills like something being forgiven.

Ossory stood very still and let it feed him.

This was the secret arithmetic: a memory worn daily does not fade. It *ripens*. Every wearing laid another year of longing over the original like varnish, until the shepherd boy's simple sunrise had become a cathedral of feeling, inexhaustible, and drinking from it each dawn kept nine bodies alive past all natural term. The founders had meant to share the country among themselves forever. They had not anticipated — none of them, not even clever Veyra with her mathematics — that immortality purchased this way would require the country to remain *gone*. A restored Vhalen would be a Vhalen with its mornings back. And a morning returned to its rightful owner is a morning no longer yours to drink.

So the child could not be permitted. So the child would be processed.

Ossory straightened the mask's chin-strap, which had grown stiff as cartilage over the decades until it was less a strap than a second jaw, and went down to convene his brothers and sisters in appetite.

* * *

The council chamber sat at the heart of the guildhall, ringed by the nine vault-stairs, and it was deliberately beautiful. Ossory had designed it himself, in the century when design had still given him pleasure: a round room floored in pale ashwood, walled in stone the color of oatmeal, with nine high windows that caught the light of whichever hour mattered least. No table. Tables encouraged documents, and documents were how empires died. The masters met standing, in a circle, like conspirators, because that was what they were and honesty about it had kept them alive while louder villains rotted.

They assembled one by one as the sky went from iron to pearl.

Veyra arrived first, as always, wearing the Mathematician's Face — a memory of the night Vhalen's astronomers calculated the return of a comet and wept in the observatory because the universe had kept its promise. She stood with her arms folded inside her gray sleeves, and behind her mask her eyes did sums. They had done sums for four hundred years. Once, Ossory suspected, she had counted other things: children, perhaps, or kindnesses. But the comet-memory rewarded counting, and the woman had followed her crown's inclination until the inclination was indistinguishable from the self. This was not tragedy. Ossory had long ago stopped filing such things under tragedy. A tool shapes the hand that holds it; that is simply what tools are *for*.

Brannoc came next in the Fisherman's Mask — a dawn haul, nets heavy, the sea giving — and he was humming. Brannoc hummed always now; the memory had filled him with tide-rhythm until his own pulse had learned the tune. Then Ilse in the Midwife's Mask, whose founding moment was a birth in a flooded farmhouse, a child pulled gasping into lamplight while the river climbed the stairs; she drew strength from that first cry the way other women drew it from wine. Then the twins, Corvo and Corvessa, sharing between them the memory of a masked carnival, two halves of one night, and consequently unable to be in a room without finishing each other's silences. Then Maren of the Harvest Mask, broad and slow and patient as a field. Then quiet Sef, the youngest-worn, whose memory was a funeral — the only master crowned with grief rather than joy, and the most dangerous of them for it, because grief drinks deeper than gladness. And last, gliding in on his cane of yellowed bone, ancient Aldric, the First Founder, wearing the Gatekeeper's Mask: the memory of the closing of Vhalen's borders, the last day anything could enter or leave.

Nine faces. Nine mornings, nights, hours, cut from a living land and nailed to living skulls.

"Brothers," Ossory said. "Sisters." He spread his old hands. "We have a leak."

He told them about the child. Mud to the waist at the service door, speaking the dead tongue — not scholarly reconstructions of it, not the careful syllables Petra Reyes had fumbled toward in the ledger room, but the *living stream of it*, the language as it had sounded on the tongues of the drowned. Speaking it the way rain speaks. Clutching a coin minted nowhere, struck in some mint that had been erased so thoroughly that even the coin's metal had forgotten its alloy.

"A foundling?" said Brannoc, still humming faintly, off-key in the way of tides.

"A remainder," said Veyra. Her voice was dry as chalk dust. "Do the arithmetic. We took nine. Nine is not everything. A country is not divisible into nine clean parts; there are fractions left over, sweepings, crumbs under the knife. Four hundred years of crumbs, cohering." She turned her masked face toward Ossory. "Or worse. Coalescing. A compression. All the ordinary people we didn't specifically harvest — the millers, the gossips, the children too small to have memorable mornings — folded down into one small body and pushed out ahead of us like a debt collector."

"The tongue," said Ilse softly. From behind the Midwife's Mask her voice had the particular gentleness of women who talk to laboring mothers, and Ossory had never once trusted it. "Petra Reyes understood fragments of it. Our meticulous, incorruptible little bookkeeper."

"Reyes understands nothing," Ossory said. "Reyes has never broken a rule. I checked the personal file myself, twice, last night. Twenty-two years of service and not one unauthorized opening. Do you know how rare that is? How *beautiful*? Most clerks peek within a year. Curiosity is a hunger, and we are a pantry. But Reyes—" he smiled inside the mask, where no one could see it and everyone somehow felt it— "Reyes has starved beautifully. There is a soul you could build a vault around."

"And yet the child knocked on *their* door," said Sef, in his grave-dry voice. "Of all the doors in the Court. Doors choose, Ossory. Ask any ghost."

"Coincidence chooses more often," said Ossory. "But I take your point, brother. Which is why I've ordered processing moved up. Tonight, after the auction. Decant below, standard procedure, full extraction." He let the words sit a moment. "I want the extraction witnessed. All nine. If this creature is what Veyra says — a compressed remainder, a whole people in miniature — then what comes out of it may not fit in one glass. It may need to be... distributed."

Silence, of the good kind. He could feel them tasting it. Four hundred years of partnership had taught him the flavor of each silence: Veyra's silence was calculating mass and yield. Brannoc's was already at sea. Ilse's was remembering a birth, and finding the metaphor pleasing. Even Aldric, leaning on his bone cane in the Gatekeeper's Mask, gave a small nod that meant *the border holds*.

"There is one complication," Ossory added, mildly, the way a man mentions weather. "Reyes lied to me."

He described it: the ledger-room interview, the clerk's steady answers, and then the small, seamless falsehood — a claim that the child had been placed in holding cell nine, when in truth Ossory's own counterspies had watched Reyes carry the mud-soaked bundle down toward the archive levels, where no holding cells existed at all. He described it with admiration, actually, because it deserved admiration. The lie had been well-made. Unadorned. Delivered with the flat competence of a professional reading figures aloud. Twenty-two years of perfect obedience ending in one flawless act of treason — that was not corruption. That was *conversion*. Something had gotten into the bookkeeper, and Ossory thought he knew what: the same thing that got into everyone eventually, given proximity. Vhalen leaked. It had leaked for four hundred years through every crack they'd failed to seal, and it leaked most of all through pity.

"What do you want done about the clerk?" asked Maren, slow as threshing.

"Nothing. Yet." Ossory folded his hands. "A clerk who lies once is a clerk who can lie again — and a liar with access to our ledgers is worth ten loyal ones, provided the leash is short and the collar invisible. Besides." He let the pause stretch, luxuriating in it, because he was an old gourmet and this was his favorite course. "I have read Reyes's intake file. Orphaned at six. Mother dead of marsh-fever in front of the child. Father never recorded. Raised by the guild, fed by the guild, *made* by the guild. People like that don't betray institutions, friends. They betray *people*. Find the right person to stand between Reyes and the institution, and the leash ties itself."

"You mean the child," said Veyra.

"I mean," said Ossory, "that tonight, after the auction, we take the child below and open it like a letter. And I think our incorruptible bookkeeper should be invited to watch. Attendance builds loyalty. And if it doesn't—" the smile again, private as a coin under a floorboard— "then we will learn exactly what kind of thief we are keeping in the house. Either answer profits us. That, sisters and brothers, is what we call good bookkeeping."

Corvo laughed, or Corvessa did, and the other finished it.

* * *

Alone afterward — the others drifting out to their duties, their feedings, their long patient days of being adored by the city that never knew what fed them — Ossory went down one stair, and then another, into the vault proper.

He walked the rows of keeping-glass with his fingertips trailing, the way other men trail fingers along a picket fence in a neighborhood they love. Shelf upon shelf upon shelf, descending farther than any clerk had clearance to see. Here a widow's first kiss, glowing faint rose. There a deserter's last look at his home village, banked blue. Griefs in green glass. Joys in gold. The working inventory of the trade, the visible wealth of the Ninefold Court, and beneath it — floors down, past locks that answered to nine masks and no keys — the true collection.

The deep shelves. Where the country's surplus slept.

He stopped at the row they called the Orchard, though no sign named it so. Glass after glass of ordinary Vhalen: a woman singing to dough as it rose. Two boys stealing apples in an orchard that had fed a nation. An old man teaching his granddaughter the names of clouds in a language with forty words for the ones that promised rain. None of these were founding memories. None could be worn. They were simply — *hers*. All of hers. Everything they hadn't needed for their crowns, harvested anyway because the harvest was the holiness and leaving anything behind had seemed, in those early triumphant years, almost blasphemous.

Ossory stood before the apple-thieves' glass a long while. Inside, the afternoon hung motionless: dappled light, stolen fruit, laughter mid-burst. Four hundred years and the laughter had not finished landing. Sometimes he thought that was the cruelest thing they had done — not the taking, but the *preserving*. A memory locked in glass is a scream held at its highest note forever. The boys in there were not at peace. They were not anywhere. They were *about to*, eternally, and Ossory drank a sip of their joy each spring equinox and thanked them for it, and meant the thanks, and knew the thanks was filth, and drank anyway.

Hunger forgives everything. That was the first lesson of the Court, older than the masks, older than the name. Hunger forgives, and hunger remembers, and hunger keeps better books than any clerk.

He was turning to go when he felt it — faint, far up through nine floors of stone, like a bell rung in a neighboring town.

The child. Awake, somewhere in the archive dark where the bookkeeper was hiding it, and *remembering*. He could taste it even at this distance: not one flavor but hundreds braided together, bread and bells and rain on nine towers, a whole country's worth of small mortal mornings compressed into one small furious flame. It flickered against his hunger the way a candle flickers against a hungry mouth.

Soon, he promised it silently. Tonight. After the auction.

He climbed back toward the daylight, mask snug against his face, the shepherd's sunrise warm in his chest, and began composing the invitation he would send up to the ledger room — courteous, formal, impossible to refuse:

*Master Reyes is requested to attend the processing.*

Let the bookkeeper come and watch what the Court truly was. Let them stand in the vault and see what became of countries. Either the sight would break them — in which case, well. Glass broke. It was practically the guild motto. Or the sight would teach them, the way it had once taught nine idealists in a green country's doorway, that some hungers are too large to apologize for.

Ossory reached the top of the stairs and paused, one old hand on the rail, savoring the morning that was not his and never had been and would be his until the stars went out.

Below him, faint and stubborn, the child went on remembering.
