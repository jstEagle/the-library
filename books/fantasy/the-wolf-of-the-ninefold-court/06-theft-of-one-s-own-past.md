## 6. Theft of One's Own Past

The plan, such as it was, came to Petra in the hour before dawn, in the ledger room, with the child asleep against a stack of unbound folios like a cat that had decided the warmth of paper was as good as any hearth.

The child could not stay here. That was no longer a suspicion but an arithmetic. Master Ossory had asked — had *asked*, gently, the way one asks after a widow's grief before naming a price for it — whether the mud-soaked foundling had been seen on the premises. Petra had said no. The lie sat in their chest like a swallowed stone, and every time they passed a colleague in the corridor they felt it shift, threatening to rise.

So: the child must be moved. And moving a child through the Ninefold Court was not like moving a child through anywhere else, because the Court did not watch with eyes. It watched with ledgers. Every soul who entered the service stair left a mark in the attendance rolls; every room recorded its visitors the way a riverbed records water. You could hide from a person. You could not hide from a book.

Unless you gave the book something else to read.

Petra sat with their chin on their folded hands and thought about cover stories. A false entry in the rolls would be caught — the archivists reconciled the night-books against the day-books each morning, and discrepancies rang like cracked bells. A forged pass would be worse; forgery was a hanging offense even for clerks, and the Court's idea of mercy was a clean decanting. What was needed was not a lie but a substitution. Not *no one entered the lower galleries* but *someone else did.*

Someone whose presence there would raise no questions. Someone ordinary. Someone whose absence from their own bed for two hours would mean nothing at all.

Someone like Maren Ostrey, third-rank clerk of the Grief Accounts, who slept three nights in seven in the cot behind the tally-room because her husband's memory had been sold to pay his debts and she could no longer bear the shape he'd left in their bed.

Petra closed their eyes.

They had known what they were considering from the first moment of the thought, and they had let the thought continue anyway, which was its own kind of confession. There was only one way to make Maren Ostrey walk the lower galleries in Petra's stead without her knowing or remembering it, and that way was theft.

Not appraisal. Not trade. Theft.

In eleven years of service, Petra Reyes had touched ten thousand memories and kept none of them. They had held a widow's first kiss up to the light and pronounced its color honestly; they had weighed griefs on scales calibrated by masters dead two hundred years; they had watched people walk out of the Court lighter than they came in and called it mercy, called it service, called it a fair price. They had never once taken what was not offered. It was the line they had drawn so early and defended so quietly that they had stopped seeing it as a line at all — it was simply the shape of them, the way a river's shape is the banks that hold it.

And now they were going to put their hands into another human being's past and take out whatever fit.

The child stirred against the folios. In sleep its face went smooth and young in a way it never managed awake, where its eyes were old in a manner no child's should be — old the way ruins are old, old the way a field is old after the fire has passed through it. Petra watched the small chest rise and fall and thought: *I am doing this for you.* And then, honestly, because they had always been honest with themselves if with no one else: *and I am doing this because Ossory looked at you the way a man looks at the last bottle of a vintage, and someone has to move faster than hunger does.*

It did not make the theft smaller. But it made it possible to stand up and begin.

* * *

The taking of a memory by consent was a ritual, and rituals were armor. The client reclined in a padded chair; the clerk spoke the litany of valuation; silver needles hummed at the temples; the memory rose out of the client like cream rising in milk, and the clerk drew it off into glass with hands that never trembled, because trembling was unprofessional and unprofessional clerks were decanted down to useful size. Petra had performed this a thousand times. Their hands had never trembled.

There was no ritual for the other thing. The Court wrote none because the Court assumed none would be needed — who steals from inside a vault? So Petra had to build their own rite out of spare parts, alone, at the fourth bell of the morning, in the tally-room where Maren Ostrey slept her three nights in seven.

She lay on her side under a wool blanket gone gray at the folds, breathing slowly. The tally-room smelled of ink, cold tallow, and the particular dust of abacuses — beeswax and old wood and somewhere beneath it, faintly, the iron tang of the counting frames' wire. A single lamp burned low. On the shelf above her cot sat a chipped cup, a hairbrush with three strands still caught in it, and a small framed silhouette cut from black paper: a man's profile, sharp-nosed, smiling slightly, done by some street artist in a market Petra had never visited. Her husband. Or rather, the shadow her husband had left behind when the Court took everything else.

*Don't look at it,* Petra told themselves, and looked at it, and understood that looking was part of the cost, that the cost had already begun.

They knelt beside the cot and took out the kit they had assembled over three sleepless nights: needles borrowed from the decanting gallery, a blank vial, a spool of fine copper wire, and — this was the piece they were most ashamed of, though shame had only begun to keep its accounts with them — a copy of the intake litany, so that their mouth could say the words even if the words were lies now, even if saying them over a sleeping woman made the litany itself into something broken.

"You're owed a valuation," Petra whispered. "That's all this is. I'm valuing what I'm about to do to you."

Maren breathed. The lamp guttered and steadied.

The needles went in at the temples, and Petra's hands, which had never once trembled across ten thousand honest appraisals, shook now like a novice's. They had to close their eyes and breathe twice before the copper wire would seat properly. Then, because there was nothing else to do, because stopping halfway was worse than any completion, they began to speak.

"State your name for the record." Silence answered, and Petra answered it themselves: "Ostrey, Maren. Third rank. No consents on file." The words tasted of wax. "This extraction is conducted without—" and here their voice failed, actually failed, a small dry snap in the throat, and they started again. "Without knowledge of the subject. For purposes undisclosed. Duration: minimal. Take: one."

*One.* As if limiting the number made it a kind of restraint. As if a thief who stole a single coin were a different animal from a thief who emptied the house.

Petra laid two fingers against Maren's brow, the way they would have for any extraction, and opened themselves to the surface of her sleeping mind.

Memories do not lie still. Even in sleep they drift, rise, settle, like silt in slow water. Petra had always experienced the client-side of an extraction as a kind of lantern-light over a pond: shapes passing below, colors, the occasional flash of a fish turning. With a waking client you saw the day's sediment — errands, grievances, small hopes. With a sleeper you saw deeper strata.

He should have chosen quickly. Taken the nearest bright thing and gone. That was the plan: a single vivid memory, recent enough to serve as a decoy entry in the night-rolls, dated and stamped in Maren's own cognitive signature so the archivists would read *clerk Ostrey, lower galleries, Tuesday bell* and think nothing of it. In and out in four minutes.

But the pond under his fingers was full of her husband.

He was everywhere in her sleep — not as one memory but as weather, as the temperature of the whole water. Petra moved through the drifting strata trying to find a neutral bright thing, a market day, a good loaf, anything, and everywhere they turned there he was: Tomas laughing with flour on his forearms; Tomas sick in winter and Maren sixteen years old and terrified and certain; Tomas teaching her numbers on a slate because she'd been born to figures the way other people are born to song; Tomas's hands; Tomas's voice saying her name the way people say the names of harbors they've sailed home to. The Court had taken him eight years ago, cleanly, lawfully, at a fair assessed price, and what remained was this: a silhouette in black paper, three hairs in a brush, and a mind that returned to him every night like a tongue returning to the socket of a missing tooth.

*Take the nearest bright thing,* Petra commanded themselves, and their fingers, treacherous, gentle, drifted instead toward the brightest thing in the whole dark water, the one memory that glowed through all the others like a lamp under ice—

—and they took it.

There was no resistance. That was the worst of it, afterward, the thing Petra returned to at odd hours like a sore tooth of the conscience: there was no resistance at all. Maren's sleeping mind parted around the extraction the way water parts around a hull, and the memory rose into the needle and down the copper and into the waiting vial, and it was beautiful, it was *terribly* beautiful, Petra could see it glowing in the glass even before they sealed the wax: a wedding morning, small and poor and sunlit. Maren at nineteen in a borrowed dress the wrong shade of blue, laughing at herself. Tomas barefoot because his boots had gone to the cobbler and the ceremony couldn't wait. Rain expected all day and sun breaking through anyway, the whole street smelling of wet stone and frying bread, neighbors leaning from windows, and Tomas saying — the words were clear in the glass, luminous, preserved perfectly, the way the Court preserves things, the way it preserves *everything* it takes — Tomas saying, *"Whatever else happens, we'll have been happy once, and no one can unpick the stitching of that."*

No one can unpick the stitching of that.

Petra sat back on their heels in the cold tally-room with a stolen wedding morning sealed in glass in their hands, and understood that they had just proven Tomas Ostrey wrong.

The wax seal went on. The needles came out. Maren turned over in her sleep and murmured something — a name, half of a name, *To-* — and settled, and did not wake, and Petra knelt there in the lamplight holding the brightest hour of her life and felt nothing at all for three long breaths, which frightened them more than guilt would have.

Then guilt arrived.

It did not come gradually. It came the way floodwater comes through a door left open — all at once, everywhere, with weight behind it. Petra had imagined, insofar as they had let themselves imagine the feeling at all, that a first theft would feel like transgression: hot, thrilling, edged with fear. Instead it felt like *accounting*. It felt like reading a column of figures and finding the sum correct and wishing devoutly that the sum were wrong. Eight years Maren had survived on the interest of that morning — on the knowledge, unreachable but certain, that happiness had happened to her once and could not be retroactively undone. Petra had just withdrawn the principal. Somewhere ahead of her, in a future Petra could not see, was a morning when Maren Ostrey would reach for the certainty and find the drawer empty, and would not know what was missing, only that something had been, and the not-knowing would be the crueler wound.

That was the design of the Court, wasn't it. That was what the Court *sold*: not memories, but holes shaped like them, and the customer never knew what had filled the space where the hole now lived.

Petra had been the Court's hands for eleven years and had never once understood it from the inside until this moment, kneeling on cold boards, holding glass.

They hid the vial in the false bottom of their ledger-case, beneath the appraisal forms, and it burned against their hip all the way down the corridor like a coal sewn into their coat.

* * *

By the seventh bell, the substitution was made. The night-rolls now recorded Clerk Ostrey descending to the lower galleries at the fifth bell, on a routine errand of re-shelving that Petra had fabricated and then, with a skill that curdled their stomach to recognize in themselves, anchored in the extracted memory's cognitive signature — a thread of Maren woven through the false entry so finely that reconciliation would pass over it as smoothly as a hand over sound cloth. The child, bundled in a reefer coat two sizes too large, its coin minted-nowhere tucked safe in its fist, could now be moved through the Court's blind spots wearing Maren Ostrey's borrowed ghost.

"It's wrong," Petra told the child, quietly, in the ledger room, while outside the window the harbor fog thinned toward gray daylight. "What I did. I want you to know that I know it's wrong. Whatever you are — whoever you remember being — I want one person in this whole rotten building to say plainly that it was wrong."

The child looked up at them with those ruin-old eyes. It reached out and touched Petra's wrist, and for a moment Petra braced for the flood of Vhalen — bells, harvests, nine towers — but what came instead was small and strange and almost tender: an impression of *weighing*. Of hands holding grain in a market in a country that no longer existed, judging the measure, being fair. An approval, ancient and simple, from people who had believed a fair measure was a holy thing.

Then the child frowned, and its gaze went past Petra's shoulder, toward the door, and its whole small body went rigid as a snared bird.

Down the corridor, muffled by walls and fog, someone had begun to cry.

Not loudly. Not the crying of a child or a mourner. A low, continuous, bewildered weeping, the sound of a person weeping without knowing why, the sound grief makes when it has lost even its own address. It grew closer, unhurried, terrible. Petra knew the footfalls that accompanied it. Heavy tread, uneven, favoring the left knee. Third-rank clerk of the Grief Accounts, coming up from the tally-room where she had slept without dreams for the first time in eight years and woken with her pillow soaked and her heart hollowed and no word in her mouth except a name she could no longer attach to a face.

Petra stood very still, the child's cold hand tight around their fingers, and listened to Maren Ostrey weep her way down the corridor past the ledger room door, and did not go to her, and did not open the door, and added the sound to the ledger they were apparently keeping now behind their ribs — the new one, the private one, the one written in red.

When the weeping had faded, the child tugged Petra's sleeve and pointed — not at the door, not at the stairs, but down. Down, past the cellars, past the vaults, toward the forgotten levels where the Court's oldest debts were buried.

And in the child's feral tongue, in words that surfaced in Petra's mind unbidden, translated by memories they had never lived, it said:

*"We have to go now. The wolf has smelled the door."*
