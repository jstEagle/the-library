## 9. Hunted by Your Own Face

There is a discipline to running that no one teaches you, because no one expects a clerk to need it.

You do not run the way soldiers run, all lungs and panic. You run the way I once balanced a ledger against an auditor's clock: in segments. One corridor at a time. One breath measured against one stride. The body wants to spend everything at once; the mind must ration it like coin. I had spent nineteen years learning not to feel things at full price, and it turned out that grief and terror are both currencies, and frugality transfers.

The child ran ahead of me through the underlevels, small feet slapping wet stone, and did not once look back to see if I followed. That was the first thing I noticed, and the first thing that frightened me more than the hunters behind us: the child was not afraid of being left. The child had been left before. The child had been *left behind by an entire country*, and had learned what every abandoned thing learns — that looking back is a luxury, and forward is the only direction with air in it.

"Petrásha," my mother's voice had said, and I had put one foot in front of the other and run anyway, and her voice had followed me down the drowned stair like water finding its level.

*Little clerk.*

She had never called me that when she was alive. She had called me *moy zvyozdochka* — my little star — in the river-tongue of the harbor districts, a language I had not heard spoken aloud in two decades and could still recite in dreams. So where had *little clerk* come from? Where had *Petrásha* come from?

I knew the answer before I finished asking the question, and the answer made the next three corridors very cold indeed.

The Court did not build its trackers from nothing. Nothing in the Ninefold Court is built from nothing; that is our first law and our last hypocrisy. An echo must be *sourced*. And the richest source of a person's weakness, the deepest well of usable material, is not the memory of the target themselves.

It is the memories other people kept of them.

Somewhere below the guildhall, in a vault I had inventoried but never truly read, there was a ledger entry in my own careful hand recording the acquisition of a dead woman's remembered voice. Purchased from — I could not recall. A neighbor. A priest. Someone my mother had been kind to, or cruel to, someone who had carried her around inside their skull for years and finally sold her by the ounce. And the Court had decanted her, filtered her, wrapped her in oilcloth and hunter's flesh, and aimed her at me like an arrow fletched with my own childhood.

They had not copied her out of *my* head. They hadn't needed to. My mother lived on in dozens of minds, the way the dead do — distributed, diluted, half-remembered — and the Court simply gathered the versions and stitched them together, and the result walked, and spoke, and knew the pet name of a child she had died before it learned to keep ledgers.

I understood, running, that this was the true cruelty of the echoes. Not that they looked like the dead. That they were assembled from love itself — from every small act of remembering anyone had ever done for free — and turned into instruments.

*You're a long way from home.*

Home. The word snagged in me like a fishhook and I made myself cut the line and keep swimming.

* * *

We surfaced in the gallery of hanging lamps — the long vaulted hall where the Court stores the memories too damaged to sell, each one sealed in smoked glass and hung from hooks like cured meat. The child stopped there, abruptly, and held up one hand, and I nearly fell over them.

"Listen," the child said, in the old tongue, and then, with visible effort, dragging the words up from somewhere deep: "Listen. Floor."

I listened. Beneath us, faint as a pulse under a wrist, came footsteps. Not behind. Below. More than one set, moving with the unhurried confidence of things that have never once been late for anything.

"They split," the child said. "Some went the fast way. Some went the—" the child's face screwed up, hunting for the word, and found it in my language instead: "—the *true* way. Through walls that remember being doors."

"Walls that—"

"The Court is built on Vhalen." The child said it flatly, the way you'd say *the house is built on rock*, and something in my spine went cold and stayed cold. "Your guild didn't choose the ground. The ground chose you. Every wall here remembers being a wall of mine. The hunters walk the old ways. I know the old ways." A pause, and then, quieter, almost apologetic: "So do they. Because they are wearing pieces of me to find me."

I looked at the child — at this scrap of mud and stubbornness that had walked out of a salt flat into my service door — and I finally understood what Master Ossory's hunters were carrying under their gray wrappings. Not weapons. Not faces, only.

They were carrying *home*. Pieces of a country, worn like lanterns, and the light they cast fell only on one thing in all the world: the child fleeing through the dark ahead of them.

"We can't outrun that," I said. "You can't outrun your own—"

"No." The child crouched, pressed a palm flat to the flagstones, and closed their eyes. "But I can lie to it."

* * *

What the child did next, I will describe as best I can, though description fails it, the way a ledger fails to hold a life.

The child knelt on the stones of the lamp gallery and began to speak — not to me, not to the hunters, but to the floor. To the walls. In the old tongue, low and rhythmic, the cadence of it less like speech than like tending: the sound of someone walking a row of plants, touching each leaf. And the stones *answered*. I felt it through my boots — a shiver, a settling, the faint groan of masonry remembering a different shape. The air in the gallery thickened until breathing felt like drinking.

"What are you doing?"

"Telling the walls a story about tonight." The child's eyes were still shut. "A story where we went the other way. Down the flooded stair. The walls will believe it, because the walls want to be useful again. Walls miss their work, Petrásha-of-the-ledgers. Doors especially. A door that hasn't opened in nine hundred years dreams about it."

"You told them a false report."

"I told them a *kind* story." The child opened their eyes, and for one moment they looked far older than any child, older than the guild, older than the lie of maps. "It's what my country did at the end, too. We told ourselves a story so the fear would go somewhere else for a little while." The child stood, swayed; caught my sleeve; steadied. "It costs. Come. While the walls still believe me."

We went the way the child chose — not away from the footsteps but *across* them, along a ledge above a dry aqueduct, single file, in darkness broken only by the child's uncanny certainty of where the edge was. Twice the child stopped me with a hand on my chest, and twice, seconds later, something poured past below us: gray oilcloth, wrapped faces, moving with terrible patience, heads tilted toward a sound that wasn't there anymore.

And the third time —

The third time, the footsteps stopped directly beneath us. And a voice rose out of the dark of the aqueduct, unhurried, warm, familiar in the specific awful way that only one voice in the world can be.

"Petrásha."

My mother.

Not shouted. Not projected. Spoken the way she had spoken through my bedroom door when I was small and feigning sleep: gently, with perfect knowledge that I was awake.

"I know you can hear me, starling. I know how you breathe when you're pretending. You got it from me."

I put my hand over my own mouth. It did not help. Grief does not live in the mouth.

"The men who dressed me this morning were very polite," the voice went on, drifting up through stone, conversational, terrible. "They asked me questions about you all day. What you looked like at six. Whether you sucked your thumb. Whether you cried easy. I told them everything I know, which is everything, because I am made of what everyone remembers, and everyone remembered *you*." A pause, and when it resumed the warmth had not changed at all, and that was the worst thing, the unforgivable thing: "Do you know what I remembered most? Not your face. Your face I got from a dozen people and none of them agreed. What I remembered was the sound of your pen. Scratching. All night. Such a diligent child. Such a *careful* child. Counting everything so nothing could ever surprise you."

Below me, the wrapped figure stood motionless in the dry channel, face tilted up, though it could not possibly have seen me through the dark. It didn't need to see. It had me by the throat already, by a grip older than either of us.

"Come down, starling. Put the little ghost down. Give it back to the men in masks, and come home, and I'll make the rice with the burnt bottom you liked, and you can pretend to be asleep and I'll pretend to believe you. We'll do it forever. That's all they're offering, my love. Forever, with the burnt rice."

And here is the thing I will not be able to explain even to myself, afterward, writing whatever I write with whatever remains of me:

For eleven full seconds — I counted; counting is my prayer — I wanted it.

Not the trap. Not the masters. I wanted *her voice saying my name*, and I wanted it with a hunger so total it made the Court's whole economy suddenly, horribly legible. This is what they sell upstairs. This is what the widow bought back, what the grieving trade away, what sits in smoked glass in the gallery of hanging lamps. Not memories. *Doses of the dead.* And I understood in those eleven seconds that the Ninefold Court had never really been a guild of thieves at all. It was a church of addiction with a ledger, and I had been its most devout bookkeeper, and my whole careful neutral life had been nothing but a man standing in a pharmacy telling himself he wasn't thirsty.

The child's hand found mine in the dark. Small. Cold. Steady.

"That isn't her," the child whispered. Not unkindly. With the authority of someone who knew exactly what it was to be copied, worn, used as a lantern. "That is what other people kept of her. Other people loved her badly and briefly and they sold it. But you—" the child squeezed my fingers, "—you kept yours. Didn't you. You never sold a piece of her. That's why it hurts. That's why it *works*."

"Yes," I breathed.

"So carry it yourself. Don't let them carry it for you. If she walks, let her walk in *your* memory, where she's yours. Not theirs."

Eleven seconds. Then twelve. Then I took my hand off my mouth, and I leaned over the edge of the aqueduct into the dark, and I said — quietly, in the harbor-tongue I had not spoken in twenty years, the language of burnt rice and scratched pens:

"Goodbye, Mama. Sleep."

And I walked away from the voice of my dead mother while it called my name, and I did not stop walking, and I did not look back, because I had learned it from the best — from a child who had been left behind by a country and knew that forward was the only direction with air in it.

Behind us, after a long moment, the footsteps resumed. But slower now. Confused. Hunting a story the walls no longer believed.

* * *

We came up at last through a hatch into the disused counting room on the guild's north flank, the one where they store the ledgers of dissolved chapters, and I barred the door behind us and allowed myself, for the first time in two days, to sit down on the floor like a person rather than stand like a function.

The child sat beside me. For a while neither of us spoke. Dust moved in the light from the high window like snow that had lost its nerve.

"It will use different voices now," the child said eventually. "It has used the mother. It will try the others. Everyone you've wronged. Everyone you've loved. The Court keeps excellent records, Petrásha-of-the-ledgers. You wrote them yourself." A pause, delicately cruel, entirely without malice — the child did not yet fully grasp cruelty, having been on its receiving end at civilizational scale. "How many names are in your ledgers?"

I thought about it honestly, because the child deserved honesty and because lying to this child was beginning to feel like lying to weather.

"Thousands," I said. "Tens of thousands. Every appraisal. Every debt. Every name attached to every memory that ever passed across my desk." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and cracked. "I thought keeping accounts was the honest work. I thought I was the clean one. The whole Court bleeds and I'm the one who just... writes it down."

"And now?"

"Now I know what the ink is for." I looked at my hands. Clerk's hands, ink-stained, precise, and for nine hundred years the same hands had written down the same crime in the same neat columns, and the crime had been *everything*. "Now the question is whether I'm the kind of thief who steals to eat, or the kind who steals to give back. I intend to find out."

The child considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate. Then reached into the ragged fold of its clothing and produced, of all things, the coin — the coin minted nowhere, the one it had clutched the night it knocked on my door — and pressed it into my palm and folded my fingers over it.

"Payment," the child said gravely. "In advance. For the rest."

The metal was warm. It should not have been warm. Nothing in that room was warm. But the coin lay in my hand like a small sleeping animal, and when I looked closely — closer than I had ever dared look at it — I saw that the face stamped on it was not a king's, not any king's. It was a door. A door standing free in open country, and around its rim, in letters older than every kingdom, the same word repeated nine times.

I couldn't read it. But the child watched me trace it, and smiled for the first time since I'd known them, and the smile was the saddest and most beautiful thing I had seen in nineteen years of failing to forget.

"It says *Vhalen*," the child said. "All the way around. Both sides. Everything we minted, we minted with the name, so that even when we were gone, we'd still be *in circulation*." The child's eyes lifted to mine. "Someone has to spend us back."

Which was when the door — barred, bolted, locked, the door I had personally secured — opened without a sound, and Master Ossory stepped through it alone, unmasked for the first time, holding no weapon at all, only a chair, which he set down facing us, and sat, and folded his hands, and smiled at me the way a man smiles at an account he has been auditing for centuries and has finally decided to close.

"Sit, Petrásha," he said pleasantly. "Let's talk about what you're *worth*."

He knows. He knows, and he came alone, and he brought a chair — and some part of me, the part that spent nineteen years balancing other people's griefs, understood that the most dangerous thing in the Ninefold Court was never the hunters in their oilcloth.

It was an offer.
