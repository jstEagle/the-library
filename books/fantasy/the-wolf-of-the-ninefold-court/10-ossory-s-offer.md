## 10. Ossory's Offer

They did not bind me when they took me. That was the first insult.

I woke on a chair of black oak in a room with no windows, my wrists resting on the arms as though I had chosen to sit there, as though I had walked in of my own accord and asked to be received. The ropes lay coiled on the floor beside me, neat as a housewife's knitting, and Master Ossory sat across from me in the only other chair, his mask of pale horn tilted at an angle of courteous attention, the way a physician listens to a chest.

"You slept nine hours," he said. "You needed it. Fugitives never sleep properly. It makes them stupid, and you are not stupid, Petra Reyes. You were never stupid. That was always the tragedy of you."

The room smelled of tallow and cold stone and, beneath it, faintly, of the glass vault below — that clean, mineral scent like a riverbed after flood, the smell of memory in storage. I knew the room. I had carried ledgers through its antechamber a hundred times, had never once been invited past the door. Now I was its only occupant, and the door was somewhere behind me, and I did not turn to look at it because looking would have been a kind of begging.

"You have questions," Ossory said.

"I have an escape to plan."

"Of course." He said it the way one says *of course it's raining*, with no disappointment at all, and I understood that he had heard every defiance a captive could produce in three hundred years and had catalogued them all in one of those beautiful, terrible ledgers of his. "But first, tea. You'll find it's your kind — the grey mountain leaf, bitter, no honey. We keep a record of such things. It is, after all, what we do."

A servant I never saw poured tea I never watched being poured. The cup appeared at my elbow. I did not drink it, and Ossory did not insist, and that too was part of the architecture of the room: everything offered, nothing forced, so that every refusal would feel, eventually, like my own idea wearing the wrong coat.

"Where is the child?" I asked.

"Safe. Fed. Sleeping, I should think, though sleep is a complicated word for what it does." He folded his hands. His rings caught the lamplight — nine rings, one for each mask, though he wore only the horn one tonight, and I found myself counting the bare fingers the way one counts the missing teeth in an old dog's mouth. "You want to know if we are hurting it. You want me to say no, so that you can disbelieve me, and feel the clean pain of that. Shall I spare you? We are not hurting it, Petra. We are *feeding* it. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole history of the world."

* * *

He rose and went to the wall, and I saw then that the wall was not a wall. It was a shelf, floor to ceiling, and on the shelf stood jars — not the great decanting glass of the vault below but small vessels, vials and locket-glass and one stoppered inkwell, each labeled in the clerk's hand I knew better than my own. Because it *was* my hand. My own script, from my own apprenticeship, labeling vessels I had never filled.

"Do you know what these are?" Ossory asked.

I did not answer. He had not expected me to.

"Yours," he said. "Or rather — the Court's, which for most of your life has amounted to the same thing. The first time you wept over a ledger entry and could not say why — that is here. The year you were nine and your mother sang in a language she claimed was nonsense, and you dreamed for a week of bells — here. The winter you almost left the Court, packed a bag, got as far as the harbor stairs, and turned back—" he touched a squat jar of greenish glass with one ringed finger, the gesture almost tender, "—here. You thought you turned back because you were prudent. You turned back because we asked you to. A small withdrawal. The part of you that could bear to leave. We've kept it in excellent condition. Would you like to see it?"

"You're lying."

"Am I?" He lifted the green jar and held it to the lamp, and inside, in the amber suspension, something moved — not light, not smoke, but the *shape* of a decision, the way you can see the shape of a fish under ice. I felt it. That was the obscenity of it. I felt the jar the way one feels a pulled tooth: a hollow, an ache, a wrongness in the architecture of my mouth. Somewhere in me there was a Petra-shaped hole exactly that size, and it had been there so long I had mistaken it for my personality.

"Put it down," I said, and my voice came out small.

He set it back on the shelf, precisely where it had stood, aligning the label with the others. "You see the difficulty," he said gently. "You came to us at seven years old, an orphan of the river districts, and you have spent twenty-two years in our keeping. Every kindness we showed you, we showed with one hand and harvested with the other. Not out of cruelty. Out of *economy*. A clerk who feels nothing balances books beautifully. A clerk who feels everything balances them beautifully and then burns the building down. We curated you, Petra. You are the finest instrument this Court has produced in a century, and you are only now learning to read your own label."

The tea had gone cold. I drank it anyway. I don't know why. Perhaps because it was mine — the one thing in the room that had been given without being taken from somewhere else first.

* * *

"You think we are monsters," Ossory said, resuming his chair. "I would like to disabuse you of that, not because I mind the accusation but because it will make you stupid, and I have plans for your intelligence."

"Plans."

"Nine masks, Petra. Nine founding memories of Vhalen, worn like crowns by nine men and women who have not aged since the erasure. Do you know what a founding memory is? Not a memory *of* founding — a memory that *does* the founding. The last sunrise, kept in a cistern by a warden who has forgotten his own name but not his duty. The first law, spoken in a tongue that predates every kingdom — the tongue your feral child screams in its sleep. The harvest that never failed. The bell that called the dead home. Nine of them, and between them, the whole weight of a country, and we hold that weight, and from it we draw—" he spread his hands, and for a moment the lamplight bent toward him, subtly, the way water bends toward a drain, "—everything. Youth. Certainty. The power to walk into a memory and edit it. You have seen us decant grief into glass and sell it by the dram. Where do you imagine the grief comes from? We are not merchants, Petra. We are the mouth of a river."

"And the child."

"The child is the spillage. When we — when the nine founders, before they were masters, when they were nine frightened scholars with a working and a grievance — when they consumed Vhalen, they were careful. They took the great things. The monuments, the laws, the loves that made chronicles. But a country is mostly *small* things. Bread. Arguments. The way a grandmother hums while she shells peas. You cannot wear those as a crown. They would not hold a shape. So the small things — all of them, every one — compressed themselves into a single vessel and walked out of the erasure on two small legs." He paused. "It has been walking for four hundred years, Petra. Toward the Court. Toward us. Toward the only nine people left alive who remember what it is. Do you understand? It wasn't lost. It was *coming home*."

"To be eaten."

"To be *integrated*." His voice did not rise, but something behind it did, a pressure like deep water. "You have seen what happens to a memory left loose. It frays. It forgets itself. The child is forgetting itself hour by hour — you've seen it, you've held its hand, you've felt it reaching for words that were there yesterday. In the vault, in the glass, in the keeping of the nine, it would persist. Whole. Forever. Is that not worth the price? Is permanence not worth anything?"

"It isn't permanence," I said. "It's taxidermy."

The horn mask regarded me for a long moment. Then Ossory laughed — a real laugh, brief and startled out of him, and it was worse than any anger, because for one unguarded second there was a man inside the mask, a man who had been a frightened scholar once, four hundred years ago, and who had made his bargain and never once been allowed to stop paying its installments.

"Taxidermy," he repeated, savoring it. "Yes. You'll do."

"Do for what?"

And then he told me, and this is the part I have turned over every night since, the part I keep trying to file under *manipulation* and cannot, because it was offered too plainly, with too much of the truth attached.

* * *

"Vess is dying," he said.

Master Vess. The silver mask, the eldest of the nine, the one whose voice in convocation sounded like a page turning. I had seen her perhaps a dozen times in twenty-two years, always at a distance, always at the head of the long table.

"The founding memory she wears is failing — no. That is imprecise, and I promised you truth. The founding memory she wears is being *reclaimed*. Something is taking the nine back, one by one, and we do not know what. A hermit in a cistern who should have been a warden and became a worshipper. A door in a salt flat that our maps refuse to hold. A child that should have been a vessel and became a *person*, which is the one thing a vessel must never become. And a clerk." He leaned forward. The lamp threw his mask's shadow huge against the shelf of my stolen self. "A clerk who never once opened a ledger she wasn't supposed to, until she did. Do you know what that means to me, Petra? It means the curation failed. It means there is something in you we could not jar. Something that grew in the dark of you, unrecorded, unowned. I have spent three hundred years trying to buy or bottle every part of every person in this building, and you have grown a *wilderness* in the one acre I never thought to fence, and I find I am not angry. I am *fascinated*."

"Vess's mask will pass to a successor. It must. If the nine become eight, the weight redistributes, and eight cannot carry what nine were made to carry — the memories begin to wake, to leak, to *remember themselves out loud*, and the erasure unravels, and everything the founders built, everything the Court *is*, comes down in a single night. So. A successor." He gestured, and it took me a moment to understand that the gesture was at me.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the offer."

"I've heard enough of it to know the shape."

"Humor me. I have been alive four hundred years; let me have my recitations." He stood, and walked the length of the shelf, touching each vessel as he passed, a priest walking his reliquary. "You would take the silver mask. You would take Vess's founding memory — the first law of Vhalen, *the law of names*, the statute that says a thing truly named cannot be erased — and you would wear it, and it would keep you. Not immortal — I will not lie to you, we are not immortal, we are *preserved*, which is a different and more difficult thing. You would not age. You would not sicken. You would not forget, ever again, anything, because forgetting would no longer be something that happens to you but something you *do*. Every grief, kept. Every love, kept. Your mother's voice, not fading the way it fades now, not already half-gone, but whole and permanent and yours to open like a book on any night you choose."

My mother. He said it and I felt the hook go in, felt it sink past the place where I kept my judgment, down into the place where I kept the sound of her singing in a language she claimed was nonsense.

"You have lost so much already," Ossory said, softly now, and this was the temptation wearing its truest face — not grandeur, not power, but *rescue*. "You have lost it to us, to the trade, to the slow tax of living, and you have spent your whole life pretending you never had it, because a clerk who pretends she has nothing cannot be robbed. I am offering you the end of pretending. A self polished and permanent. A Petra Reyes with no holes, no jars, no green glass full of the winter you almost left. Everything you are, kept. Forever. And the child kept too — fed, whole, unfraying — because you would be there, inside the nine, to insist upon it. You could be its warden, Petra. Its advocate at the table where its fate is decided. Refuse, and it goes to the vault under the others' hands. Accept, and it goes to the vault under *yours*." He let that sit. He was very good at letting things sit. "You cannot save it from the Court. You can only choose which hands the Court uses. That is not cynicism. That is the minutes of the last four hundred meetings."

The room was very quiet. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below us, I thought I heard the vault — the faint, continuous sound I had never been able to describe, like a crowd heard through a wall, like a country heard through glass.

"And if I refuse the mask?" I asked. "What happens to me?"

"Nothing," he said, and smiled behind the horn. "Nothing at all. That is the other thing I promised you truth about. If you refuse, we will not kill you, we will not punish you, we will not even pursue you with any great energy — the hunt has already cost us an echo and two wardens and the Court is eating itself, as you will have noticed. If you refuse, we simply wait. Because the child is forgetting itself hour by hour, Petra, and you cannot outrun an hourglass. In six days it will not remember your face. In ten it will not remember Vhalen. In twelve it will be an empty vessel, and we will decant it without cruelty and without haste, the way one harvests a field that has ripened on its own. You don't have to lose, clerk. You only have to be *slow*."

He set a key on the table between us. An iron key, old-fashioned, heavy.

"The door is unlocked," he said. "It has been unlocked since you woke. Go back to your child. Hold its hand. Count the hours. And when you have counted enough of them — when the arithmetic of mercy finally balances on your side of the ledger — come to the convocation stair, and ask for the silver mask." He paused at the door, and did not look back, and his voice came to me the way the vault-sound came through walls. "We keep a record of such things, Petra. It is, after all, what we do. But I confess — for the first time in three hundred years — I do not know which way you will be entered."

The door closed behind him. The key sat on the table, catching the lamplight, patient as a debt.

I did not touch it for a long time. I sat in the black oak chair in the room with no windows, in the smell of tallow and cold stone and kept memory, and I thought about my mother's voice, whole and permanent, mine to open like a book. I thought about a green jar on a shelf, and the winter it held, and the hole in me its shape.

Then I stood, and took the key, because a locked door is only ever an invitation to find the wall beside it — and because I had counted, in the dark behind my eyes, and the arithmetic was already done.

Six days. The child was forgetting itself hour by hour, and Ossory had just told me the one thing I needed more than the silver mask or my mother's voice or my own name kept forever in glass:

He had told me the hourglass was real. And hourglasses, whatever the masters believed, can be turned over — if you can reach the table where they stand.

I put the key in my sleeve, and went to find the child, and to teach it, before it forgot, the one word in its dead tongue I had understood from the very first night at the service door.

The word for *door*.
