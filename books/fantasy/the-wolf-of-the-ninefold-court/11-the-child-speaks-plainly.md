## 11. The Child Speaks Plainly

I found the child where I had left it, which was the first miracle of that night: in a city of shifting stairways and hunters who wore my own face, the one thing that stayed put was a small mud-colored shape curled behind the ledger room's false shelving, knees drawn up to its chin, hands pressed flat over its ears as though it could press the world out by force.

It looked up when my shadow crossed the lamp.

Its eyes were wrong. Not cruel-wrong — *empty*-wrong, like windows in a house whose family has gone. Six days ago they had held harvests. Four days ago they had held bells. Now they held only a gray attention, an animal wariness, and beneath it something flickering that could not decide whether it was a person or a weather.

"Vhalen," I said softly, using the name Ossory had given me like a knife turned handle-first. The child flinched at the sound of its own country. Then I crouched, slow, hands open, and said the other word. The first word. The one that had come out of its mouth on the night of the rain, in a tongue older than every kingdom, and had surfaced in my mouth like water rising through stone.

"*Doreth.*"

The child went very still.

Not the stillness of fear. The stillness of a struck bell — the whole body ringing with a single syllable. Its lips moved around the word again, shaping it, tasting it, and I watched something happen in its face that I will spend whatever remains of my life trying to describe and never succeed: a door opening inside a house whose owner had already begun packing.

"*Doreth,*" it repeated. And then, in broken, rusting Common, each word dragged up like a nail from old timber: "*Door. Yes. Door is — is closing. Door is me.*"

"I know," I said. "We have to go now."

It nodded. It took my hand. And we ran.

* * *

I will not pretend the escape was clever. There was nothing left in me that night but the animal arithmetic of fugitives — how many breaths before the next patrol, how many doors between us and the underlevels, how much of myself I could afford to forget if forgetting bought us another hour. The Court was tearing itself apart above us; I could hear it through the floors, mask against mask, two masters' voices raised in registers no human throat should own. Ossory's purge had begun. Every corridor smelled of ozone and old parchment burning, because when masters fight, they fight with memory, and spent memories fall out of the air like ash.

Twice the echoes found us.

The first was the ferryman whose grief I had once balanced into neat columns, back in my ledger days, before ledgers meant anything but numbers to me. He stood at the mouth of the second stair with his lantern raised and his face smooth as poured wax, and he said in my mother's voice — they always borrowed her voice, the Court had owned it for years and used it like a tool — *"Petra. Come home."*

I walked past him. That is all I can report. I walked past him while my chest tore along a seam I hadn't known was there, and the child tugged my hand and said, in its crumbling Common, "*That is not her. She is glass. You are not glass.*"

The second echo was worse, and I will not speak of it except to say that it knew about the winter of my twelfth year, and it used what it knew, and I am still carrying the bruise of it somewhere memory cannot reach.

Then we were down. Down past the drowned cistern where the warden's grief still hung in the water like oil; down past the stair that appears only when unobserved, which observed us now with a kind of shy reluctance and let itself exist anyway, because even architecture, I think, grows tired of serving monsters. We came at last to a low vaulted room where the salt of old Vhalen seeped through the mortar and glittered on the walls, and there was no pursuit, and no light but the child's skin, which had begun faintly to shine — the way a coal shines just before it goes out, or just after being breathed upon. I have not decided which.

And there, in the dark that smelled of salt and centuries, the child sat down against the wall, pulled its knees up, looked at me with those emptying eyes — and spoke.

* * *

Not fragments this time. Not the dead tongue, though it threaded through everything like gold wire through cloth. Plain speech, whole sentences, an accent like no country's because it was every country's at once — the accent of a language spoken by everyone who has ever been ordinary.

"You keep asking what I am," it said. "You ask with your face, even when your mouth asks nothing. So. Listen. I will say it while I still know how to say."

I sat down across from it. My legs simply stopped holding me. I remember thinking, absurdly, of ledgers — of the moment before you enter a figure so large it changes every column beneath it.

"There was a country," the child said. "You have heard its name now, so you think you know this part. But you do not know the part where nine hundred thousand people woke on the last morning and did not know they were last. The harvest came in. The bells rang for evening service. In the palace of nine towers the king's steward counted spoons, because stewards count spoons in every country until the end of the world. And then the nine came over the border with their masks, and they did not burn anything, Petra. Burning would have been mercy. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be grieved.

"They drank us."

The child's small hands opened, palms up, as though showing me the emptiness of them.

"A memory, taken whole, does not leave a hole. It leaves a *smoothness*. A place where a thing was, polished flat. They took the harvest, so Vhalen had never grown anything. They took the bells, so our god had never had a voice. They took the roads, the songs, the names of rivers, my grandmother's way of salting fish, a boy called Iven who was going to be a bridge-builder, a woman called Maretta who kept bees and sang off-key — they took and took and took, nine hundred thousand lives folded down and down and down, compressed the way snow becomes ice becomes glacier, until all of it — all of us — fit inside—" 

It touched its own chest.

"—this."

The lamplight of its skin guttered. For a moment its face was many faces: an old man's around the eyes, a girl's around the mouth, a farmer's brow, a scholar's tired lids. Then it was only a child again, mud-colored, exhausted, sitting in a salt-smelling dark.

"I am not a person," it said. "Do not make the mistake of loving me as one, because it will hurt you more than it hurts me, and I will not be able to carry that too. I am everyone. The baker is in here. The beekeeper is in here. Iven the bridge-builder is in here, and he has been trying to get out for three hundred years, and every hour he gets quieter. Do you understand what forgetting feels like from the inside? It does not feel like darkness. It feels like *narrowing*. Like a road that was ten lanes becoming one lane becoming a footpath becoming a deer trail becoming — nothing. The deer trail is already gone. Some nights the footpath flickers. When you touched me in the ledger room, you held open the footpath for a little while, and Iven got to see stars, and he wept, and I wept, and you thought you were dreaming."

I discovered I was crying. It seemed important to note this without shame, the way one notes the weather.

"The masters wear nine of us," the child went on, softer now. "The nine largest. The founding ones — the sunrise, the law, the river-covenant, the bell-founding, the rest. Those are the keystone memories. Take them away and the rest of us — the nine hundred thousand little lives — have nothing to hang from. We are washing hanging on a line, and the line is coming down, peg by peg." It looked at its own hands, turning them over as though checking whether they still existed. "Six days ago I could recite the census. Yesterday I lost the eastern provinces. This morning I reached for my grandmother — *our* grandmother, everyone's grandmother, she is the strongest thread in me — and found only the shape of where she had been. Hour by hour, Petra. Your Ossory told you true. The hourglass runs."

"How long?"

"I don't know. No one knows. That is the cruelty of it — the erasure was written so that no clock could measure it. But I can feel the width of me. This morning I was a nation. Tonight I am a crowd. By tomorrow I may be a family. After that—" It smiled, and the smile was Maretta the beekeeper's smile, worn briefly by a dying country like a borrowed coat. "After that, a stranger. After that, silence. And the terrible thing, Petra — the thing I need you to understand, because you are the only hand I have left — is that silence would be *fine*. Silence would be easy. Forgetting does not fight. It is only the remembering that hurts, and I am running out of strength to hurt."

"Then don't fight alone," I said.

The child looked at me for a long time. Somewhere far above us, muffled through forty feet of stone and salt, the Court screamed and burned and ate its own tail, and none of it mattered, none of it, compared to the small wet sound a dying country made when it let itself hope.

"You found the word," it said at last. "*Doreth.* Door. How did you find it? You were born after. You never lived any of this."

"I don't know," I admitted. "The words come up from somewhere. Like I'd drunk the water once, long ago."

The child's eyes widened — and for the first time since I'd known it, the widening was not grief. It was wonder, sharp and sudden, the wonder of a scholar finding a page out of order.

"Everyone in Vhalen drank the water," it whispered. "The river-covenant. The third founding memory — the one Ossory wears behind his silver mask. Everyone who ever lived there carried a little of the river in them, forever, even in exile, even in blood three generations thin. It was the covenant's whole purpose: that Vhalen could be destroyed but never fully *lost*, because some of it would always be dissolved in the people who remembered drinking." The child rose to its knees, gripping my sleeve, suddenly fierce, suddenly luminous. "Petra. You are not a stranger who learned our word. You are *downstream of us*. Someone in your line — your mother's line, your mother's mother's — stood on Vhalen's soil. The river is in you. That is why the echoes use her voice. That is why Ossory wants you wearing a mask. You are not just our thief, our ledger-keeper, our hand. You are *proof*. You are the covenant walking around in a clerk's coat, three hundred years late, still keeping the books."

I thought of my mother. Of the lullaby she hummed in a scale that fit no music I ever heard afterward, the one the neighbors called odd, the one I had traded to the Court at nineteen because grief makes us stupid and they made it easy. A song in a dead tongue. A song from a country that had never existed.

She had known. Not with her mind — with her water, her blood, her river. She had carried a whole nation's goodnight in her throat and sung it over my cradle, and I had sold it for thirty crowns and a clean conscience.

"I'm going to take it back," I heard myself say. "The song. Her. All of it. Every piece they hold."

"You cannot take back what they have eaten—"

"I can trade," I said, and the coldness in my own voice frightened me, because it was not my coldness, it was the ledger's coldness, the Court's own arithmetic turned home at last. "Memory for memory. That is how their whole machine works, isn't it? Everything in that guild is a debt. Well. I am done keeping their books. From tonight I am calling in mine."

The child studied me — nine hundred thousand faces studying me through one small pair of failing eyes — and something in its expression shifted, settled, became decision.

"Then hear the last of it," it said, "while I can still say it plain. The door at the border — *doreth* — it takes nine keys, and the keys are the founding memories themselves, set into the locks like teeth into a jaw. You have one. The warden's sunrise. Eight remain: seven in the Court's keeping, and the ninth—"

It stopped.

Its skin flickered — not dimming this time but *stuttering*, light and dark, light and dark, like a candle in wind, like a signal, like a heart missing beats. It pressed both hands to its temples and made a sound I will hear for the rest of my unremembering life: not a cry, but the sound of a vast library losing a shelf.

"The ninth," it gasped, "the ninth is — the river — the covenant — Ossory wears the masks but he *keeps* the ninth apart, he keeps it in — in —"

"In where?" I gripped its shoulders. "In *where*?"

The stuttering slowed. Steadied. And when the child lifted its head, its eyes had gone strange — clear and deep and terribly calm, and for one uncanny moment I understood that I was no longer speaking to a compressed nation or a frightened child, but to someone specific. One voice, surfacing from the many like a swimmer breaking water. An old voice. Dry and precise and familiar in a way that made the hair rise on my arms, because I had heard that cadence read aloud in the ledger room every audit-day of my adult life.

*"In his ledger,"* said the voice of Master Ossory's own discarded first self — the man he had been before the mask, kept like a pressed flower inside the memory he guarded best. *"He keeps the ninth founding memory inside his own account book. Page one. He has never trusted glass. And, clerk — he is coming down the stair behind you, and he has brought the hourglass, and he means to turn it himself."*

Above us, faint but unmistakable, came the measured tread of feet on salt-crusted steps.

And the child's light went out.
