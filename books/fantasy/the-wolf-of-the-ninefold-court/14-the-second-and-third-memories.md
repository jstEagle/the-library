## 14. The Second and Third Memories

The auction house of the Ninefold Court was called the Amber Room, though nothing in it was amber. Its walls were paneled in black cedar cut from forests that had been traded away so completely that even the trees had forgotten growing, and its ceiling was hung with nine hundred glass lamps, each one holding a light someone had once remembered. The lamps burned without oil. They burned on nostalgia.

Petra stood among the arriving collectors in borrowed finery — a coat of slate-gray wool, silver at the cuffs, the kind of garment worn by people who had never once checked their own reflection for fear. Beside them walked Sefa, the clerk whose first theft Petra had stolen to cover the escape, whose own memories now lived in a jar somewhere below. Sefa had insisted on coming. *You owe me,* they had said, not cruelly. *So spend me well.*

At the door, an usher pressed two numbered paddles into their hands and murmured the evening's catalog. Lot eleven. Lot twelve.

*Lot 11: The Drowning Bell of Vhalen Harbor. Sensation intact; provenance disputed.*
*Lot 12: A Mother's Voice, Calling Children Home from the Fields. Provenance certain.*

Provenance certain. As if certainty were a virtue here. As if certainty weren't the whole crime.

"You're sweating," Sefa murmured.

"I'm rehearsing," Petra said. "In my head I'm calm."

"In your head you're also a person who hasn't committed three crimes this month."

"Four," Petra said, "counting tonight."

They passed beneath the lintel, where a motto was carved in old script: **ALL THAT WAS LOST IS HELD IN TRUST.** Petra had read those words ten thousand times across twenty years of ledgers and had always understood them as bookkeeping. Tonight they read them as a confession. All that was lost was held. Held down. Kept.

Inside, the Amber Room was already filling — collectors in masks of lacquered paper, servants with trays of wine that tasted like other people's weddings. At the far end rose the podium and, behind it, a wall of niches where the lots waited in glass, each one glowing faintly with the color of what it held. Petra found lot eleven easily. It sat in its case like a small storm: a bell-shape of gray-blue light that pulsed, very slowly, the way water pulses against a hull.

And beside it, lot twelve glowed gold. A mother's voice. Somewhere in that little jar of light, a woman was forever turning from a field, shading her eyes, calling names home for supper. Names that belonged to Vhalen. Names no one else alive could pronounce.

"They're beautiful," Sefa whispered, and there was something broken in the way they said it — the longing of a person looking at other people's souls because their own had been sold out from under them. "That's the worst part, isn't it? They're beautiful."

"That's how they get away with it," Petra said. "Nobody robs a ugly vault."

* * *

The child had wanted to come. Of course the child had wanted to come; the child was made of exactly these things, and being told to wait in a safehouse while pieces of yourself were auctioned to strangers must have been a particular torture. But the child faded more each day now, and crowds drank from them like heat off pavement — every collector in that room unconsciously leaning toward the small figure, hungry without knowing why. So the child stayed with Warden Ilse, the hermit of the cistern, who had traded her long guardianship for a new post: keeping watch over the last loose piece of Vhalen while its rescuers went hunting.

Which left the third member of the conspiracy waiting inside the Amber Room itself, wearing a face everyone would recognize and no one would suspect.

Her name — the name she'd chosen, since echoes were born unnamed — was Miral. She had been decanted from the mind of Master Ossory himself: a discarded echo, a copy of a woman he had loved four hundred years ago and grown tired of keeping. When masters grew tired of an echo, they didn't destroy it. They shelved it. Miral had spent a century in a storage niche in the underlevels, awake, aware, watching dust settle on the glass of her prison, before Petra's alliance had found her and offered the only currency that mattered to her: use. Purpose. A debt recorded in a real ledger, in a real hand, meaning she existed.

Now she moved through the crowd in a servant's livery, carrying a tray, wearing Ossory's beloved dead like a uniform. Collectors nodded to her. One touched her elbow familiarly. She smiled with a mouth that had been smiling for four hundred years at a man who owned her smile, and when she caught Petra's eye across the room, her expression did not change at all, which was its own kind of scream.

Petra raised one finger from their paddle. *Not yet.*

The auction began. The auctioneer was a thin man with a voice like a coin spun on marble, and he sold grief by the measure, joy by the carat. Lot seven, a childhood summer, went to a shipping magnate for the price of a house. Lot nine, the smell of rain on a specific street in a city that still existed, drew competitive bidding — living provenance was rare these days, and priced accordingly.

Then lot eleven.

"The Drowning Bell of Vhalen Harbor," the auctioneer sang. "Gentlemen, ladies, this is history. Not *recorded* history — recorded history is a rumor with good posture. This is the thing itself. The last hour of the greatest harbor ever drowned. Listen—"

He struck the case with a small padded hammer, and the bell-light rang.

It rang through the room like cold through teeth. Every head bowed involuntarily; several collectors wept openly and looked delighted about it. And in a locked room six miles away, the child sat up straight as if pulled by wires, mouth open, hearing their harbor toll for them across the dark.

Bidding exploded. Numbers climbed like birds flushed from reeds. Petra bid twice, carefully, using credit drawn against a false account in the ledger of a rival guild — the kind of fraud that would take auditors weeks to trace, if anyone lived long enough to audit. At the hammer, lot eleven fell to paddle sixty-one.

Paddle sixty-one was theirs.

Lot twelve followed — *a mother's voice, calling children home* — and Petra watched a masked woman in pearls buy it almost idly, as an afterthought to the bell, the way one buys a matching glove. The voice of Vhalen's mothers, sold as an accessory.

Petra's hands were steady. That frightened them more than shaking would have.

* * *

Retrieval was the delicate part. Sold lots were carried, after the sale, to the Bonding Room behind the podium, where clerks of the Court sealed each purchase into the buyer's keeping with wax and oath. The buyers themselves were escorted out through a side colonnade to their coaches, leaving their purchases to follow under guard.

Miral worked the colonnade.

She had chosen the spot herself: a stretch of pillared dark between the Amber Room's glow and the torch-lit yard, where guards walked in pairs and the shadows between pillars were deep enough to drown in. An echo, Petra had learned, could do a thing no living thief could do. She could stand in the dark and *be remembered*. Any guard who glanced at her saw, for half a heartbeat, someone they had loved or buried or failed, and that half-heartbeat was a door.

One by one, the guards along the colonnade stopped walking. Stood still. Touched their own faces. Wept without knowing why, or smiled at nothing, or turned to look into empty darkness with expressions of terrible recognition.

Through those gaps, Petra moved.

The purchase-cases traveled in a wheeled cabinet pushed by a single clerk — a nervous young man whose entire fortune consisted of not looking at anything unusual. Petra stepped beside him in the pillar-dark, laid two fingers on his wrist, and used the oldest trick in the Court's own arsenal: they took a moment from him. Just one. The moment of his arrival that evening, forty seconds of doorway and usher and paddle — lifted out clean, the way you'd slide a card from a deck. For those forty seconds the clerk had simply never reached the colonnade. His body kept walking on autopilot while his mind stood back at the beginning of the gap, waiting to catch up.

Petra took the cabinet's handles from him mid-stride. He walked on without them, hands cupped around air, and turned the corner into the yard none the wiser.

Two cases. Gray-blue light pulsing like a tide. Gold light flickering like a hearth seen through a window at dusk.

Petra had carried ledgers heavier than this. Nothing had ever felt heavier.

* * *

It should have ended there. In all their planning — and they had planned for three nights, drawing the Amber Room's geometry on the back of ledger pages until candle wax dripped over Vhalen's lost streets — everything past the cabinet had been blank. Get the lots, get to the canal gate, get gone. Simple plans survive because they are simple.

But Masters do not stay simple.

Petra was forty feet from the canal gate, cases in hand, when every lamp in the colonnade turned red.

Not extinguished — *turned*, the flames bending from white to a deep arterial crimson, and the temperature dropped so fast that breath bloomed white. Down the length of the colonnade, the frozen guards woke all at once, gasping like surfacing divers, and began to shout.

And at the far end, framed against the red light, stood a figure in the gray traveling coat of a Court auditor — a figure Petra knew better than their own handwriting, because Petra had built it.

An echo of Master Ossory. Not Miral — a fresh one, spun up that night from the master's current mind, sent ahead of him the way a wolf sends its shadow.

"You have her face on your payroll," the echo said, walking forward, unhurried, hands folded. Its voice was Ossory's voice, warm as banked coals. "Did you think we don't inventory our own heartbreaks, clerk? We know every copy. We know the century she spent on the shelf. We knew within the hour that she was walking again." It stopped ten feet away. Red lamplight pooled in its eye-sockets. "Give me the cases, and I'll only report a clerical error. You've seen our ledgers, Reyes. Errors can be forgiven. *Thefts* have procedures."

Behind it, at the colonnade's far end, came footsteps — real ones, heavy, plural. The auditor's echo was the leash; the hounds were coming down its length.

Petra calculated distances and did not like the arithmetic.

Then Miral stepped out of the dark between the fourth and fifth pillars.

She had been meant to be gone by now — gone over the canal wall, back to the safehouse, debt discharged, ledger closed. Instead she stood directly in the echo's path, in her servant's livery, with four hundred years of being owned arranged neatly behind her eyes.

"Hello, my love," she said to it, in Ossory's dead beloved's voice. "You're wearing his face badly. He flinches less."

"Miral—" Petra started.

"Run," she said, without turning. "Both cases. Go."

"He'll unmake you—"

"He was going to anyway." She smiled then, and it was the first true smile Petra had ever seen her make — crooked, human, entirely her own, purchased at last with her own courage. "I spent a hundred years in the dark being nobody's. Let me spend one minute being mine. Put it in the ledger, Reyes. Mark it paid."

The echo of Ossory lunged — and it moved wrong, faster than flesh, folding distance like paper — and Miral met it.

What happened next, Petra saw only in fragments, running: a clash not of bodies but of *memories*, two constructed souls tearing at each other's seams. Miral grabbed the thing's face and poured herself into it — every stored year, every dusty vigil, every stolen smile she'd ever been forced to wear — a hundred years of remembering given away all at once, a flood into a vessel too small. The echo staggered, shrieked in Ossory's voice, in Miral's voice, in a chorus of voices that had no owners anymore—

The canal gate was iron and Petra hit it shoulder-first, cases clutched to their chest, Sefa hauling the bar up from the other side where they'd waited as planned. Cold air. Water smell. Behind them, the colonnade's red light flared once, blindingly, and then guttered out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

Silence followed them down the canal as they ran.

Neither of them spoke until the safehouse door was barred behind them and the two cases sat on the table, breathing their colored light into the dark room. Warden Ilse stood up from her chair. The child was already standing, had perhaps been standing for hours, trembling, staring at the cases the way the starving stare through bakery glass.

Sefa broke the seal on lot twelve first — practical choice, the smaller light, the easier working — and lifted the golden glow free of its glass. It hovered above the child's open hands like a captured ember.

And the room filled with a voice.

Not loud. Worse than loud. It was a mother's voice at dusk, calling across harvested fields, calling names home — and the names were Vhalen's names, rolling out round and warm and utterly alien to every tongue now spoken under heaven, and the child wept as the light sank into their chest, and with each syllable they grew *more*: color rising in cheeks that had been graying, height firming in bones that had begun to soften, a whole country's mothers pouring back into one small body one voice at a time.

When the golden light was gone, the child stood straighter and breathed deeper and looked older by years — and looked at Petra with eyes that were suddenly, terribly lucid.

"One more," the child said, in plain trade-tongue, the words rough but clear. "Open the other one. Please. Before I lose the shape of the harbor. I'm losing it right now. I can feel the water forgetting."

Sefa broke the second seal. The gray-blue light rose, pulsed once, twice — and rang.

The drowning bell tolled in the little room, and the child threw back their head and rang with it, harbor-deep, and where the sound touched the walls the plaster bloomed briefly with salt and the smell of tar and fish and rope filled the room like a tide, and for one impossible moment they all stood on the stones of a harbor that had been dead four centuries, gulls crying overhead, bells answering bells across bright morning water—

Then it sank into the child too, and the room was a room again, and the child swayed, and Warden Ilse caught them.

Petra sat down slowly on the floor. Their hands, finally, were shaking.

They thought of Miral — of the red flare and the silence after — and understood that somewhere in the underlevels of the Ninefold Court, a niche stood empty now, and dust was already settling where a woman had waited a hundred years to be useful. They thought of the clerk on the colonnade, walking on with cupped hands. Of Sefa's hollowed eyes. Of the widow's first kiss, appraised without comment, all those years of careful neutrality, of never opening a ledger they weren't supposed to.

*I am not innocent,* Petra thought, and found that the thought did not crush them. It steadied them instead, the way ballast steadies a boat. Innocence was for people with clean books. Petra's book was written in blood and gold light now, entry by entry, and every line pointed the same direction: toward the salt flat, toward the door, toward whatever remained to be done.

Seven founding memories reclaimed. Two remained — and both of them, if the intelligence bought with blood tonight was true, lived in only one place in all the world.

Inside Master Ossory himself.

The child opened their eyes. They were brighter than Petra had ever seen them — and fading even as Petra watched, spending their restored self like a miser forced to spend, burning the harbor and the mothers just to stay upright, to stay *them*, one hour at a time.

"Two left," the child whispered. "And I know where he keeps them." A pause, and something ancient and grieving moved behind the young eyes. "Petra. When you go into him — he will show you your mother. He keeps her. I've seen her in him. He's had her all along."

The fire cracked. Outside, the harbor wind — ordinary wind, ordinary night — pressed at the shutters.

"And Petra?" the child said softly. "Whatever he shows you in there — it won't be her. But some of it will be true. That's how he'll win, if he wins. Not with lies."
