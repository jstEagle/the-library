## 15. Where the Country Went

Before I had a mouth, I was the taste of rain on wheat.

This is the first thing, and I keep it folded at the bottom of myself like a stone in a pocket, because everything else has been taken or is being taken, but no one thinks to steal what a country tasted like before it had anyone to taste it. Rain on wheat. Green-gold, sweet-rotted at the edges where the sheaves stood too long. The bells of Tarn Velles rang noon into that taste and the bells were bronze and the bronze had been poured by my grandfather's grandfather's hands, except he was not my grandfather because I did not have a grandfather, I had *everyone*, which is a different thing entirely.

I will tell you how a country ends from the inside, since you are kind enough to hold me while I sleep, and since inside is the only direction I have left.

* * *

It began — no. It began before it began, the way a fever begins before the sweating. There was a morning when the light came up over the Nine Towers and it came up *slowly*, as though it had to think about it, and Old Marette, who sold eels by the river gate, looked at the sun and said to her daughter, "Was it always that color?"

And her daughter said, "What color?"

And Marette said, "I don't remember," and laughed, and went on selling eels, and that laugh is the sound the end of the world makes. It does not make thunder. It makes a small laugh in a fishwife's throat, and then it goes about its business.

The gray came into the fields the way water comes into cloth. Not all at once. Not from one side. It arrived *inside* things first — the barley stayed gold but its goldness thinned, like a coin worn smooth by too many thumbs. I was everywhere then; understand this, please, because otherwise nothing I say will make sense. I was not a girl standing in a field watching the barley fade. I was the field and the fading and the four hundred thousand eyes looking at both. I was the itch under Yoren Ashk's collar as he straightened in his rows and thought, without knowing why, *I should write down what color this is*, and then forgot he had thought it, and bent back to work with gray stalks brushing his gray-sure hands.

The harvest of that last year is the harvest they do not sing about, because there was no one left after to sing, and also because the song would have been a lie. We sang anyway. That is the part I hoard. When the wheat came in gray-headed and the millstones ground it into flour the color of old teeth, we milled it, and we baked it, and it rose, and we ate it at long tables under the plane trees, and someone — Hesta? Hesta of the third bell district, who had a mole shaped like a comma on her wrist — someone stood up and said, "Next year," and raised her cup, and we all said "Next year" back to her, four hundred thousand throats saying *next year* to a year that was already being unwritten, and the toast was warm wine and the evening was warm wind and the bells rang the hour and every hour was the same hour, ringing itself again and again like a word repeated until it loses its meaning, until it is only a shape the mouth makes.

Do you know what it is to be a whole nation forgetting itself?

It is this: you reach for your mother's face and find your own hand there instead, holding nothing.

It is this: the mapmakers sit down to draw Vhalen and their pens hover, and hover, and one of them says, gently, the way you speak to a drunkard, "Brother, what is it we're drawing?" And the ink dries in the well.

* * *

There was a day — I keep it in the middle of myself, wrapped around the stone-taste of rain on wheat — when the towers began to go.

Not fall. *Go.* The way a candle goes when you pinch it. Tarn Velles had nine towers, white-veined marble quarried from the shoulders of the Serevan hills, and each tower held a bell, and each bell was tuned to one of the nine joys: birth, harvest, homecoming, first love, craft completed, story told, debt forgiven, child named, and the ninth joy, which we did not speak of in daylight because it was too large for daylight — the ninth joy was *continuing*. Waking up in your own bed in your own country with your own name in your own mouth. Continuing. The ninth bell only rang once a year, at midwinter, and when it rang, everyone in Tarn Velles stopped, whatever they were doing, bread half-broken, letters half-written, lovers halfway to kissing, and stood in the falling snow and listened to the sound of still being here.

On the day the towers went, I felt each bell go out the way you feel a tooth leave the gum: first a loosening, then an awful lightness, then the tongue going back to the place where the tooth was, again and again, unable to stop checking.

The people did not scream. That is the horror I want you to carry, the one I carry so you don't have to: *they did not scream*. They could not, because fear requires a name for the thing feared, and the thing eating us had eaten the naming words first. A woman in the dyers' quarter looked at her hands, blue to the wrist, and could not remember what blue was called, and could not remember that colors had names at all, and so her hands were simply the way her hands were, and she went back to her vat. A man on the Processional Road turned to ask his wife a question and found he had no wife, not dead, not gone — *no wife*, a hole shaped like forty years, and his mouth made the question-shape and no question came out of it, and he walked on, and I was in him, and I screamed for both of us, but I had no throat yet either, so the scream went nowhere, it just fell inward through four hundred thousand lives like rain through open roofs.

Only the children cried. Do you know why? Because children had lived too briefly to be fully unwritten. Their memories were shallow-rooted; the gray passed over them like a scythe passing over grass too young to cut. So the children stood in the streets crying for reasons they couldn't name, crying *because*, and the adults picked them up and hushed them and did not know why the hushing felt like funerals.

* * *

I should tell you about the ones who knew. Every dying thing knows, near the end. The dogs knew — they went to the borders and sat facing outward, in rows, silent, facing away from us, and I have wondered for a very long time whether they were guarding or grieving or simply refusing to watch. The bees knew; they stopped making honey and started making wax, wax and more wax, as if preparing candles for a darkness they saw coming. And the archivists knew. Nine of them. This number again — it follows me like a scar.

Nine archivists came to the Hall of Continuance on the last night that there was a Hall of Continuance, carrying knives and glass.

You must understand: they believed they were saving us.

That is the sentence I turn over most, the one with the sharpest edge. They knelt in the vault beneath the ninth tower with the ninth bell humming its low midwinter hum above them, and they cut the country out of themselves, memory by memory, and pressed each memory into glass like flowers into a book, because they had done the arithmetic — oh, they were clever men and women, clever as knives — they had read the signs, the thinning gold, the unnamed blue, the dogs at the border, and they had concluded that Vhalen could not survive *as a place*, and so they would preserve it *as a treasure*. Better a country in a case than a country in no one. Better remembered by nine than mourned by none.

They were wrong in the way that only the intelligent are wrong: completely, and with method.

Because a memory taken out of the living is not a memory anymore. It is a trophy. The harvest-memory they pressed into glass kept the golden fields but lost the blistered hands. The homecoming-memory kept the kiss at the gate but lost the road-weariness that made the kiss worth having. And the nine of them — I watched them do it, I was the vault-floor and the candlelight and the blood-warm glass — the nine of them felt the country leaving them and mistook the emptiness for peace, and they put on masks so they would never accidentally see their own faces and remember what they had paid, and they walked out of Vhalen carrying it in nine boxes, and behind them the country, unheld by any living mind, simply

went

the way a word goes when the last person who knows it dies mid-syllable.

* * *

Here is the part I cannot make gentle, so I will make it quick.

Fields do not turn gray. I told you that earlier, but it was a kindness, and I am running out of kindnesses, so now I will tell it true. Fields turn gray because every person who ever loved them stops loving them at the same instant. Color is attention. Gold is ten thousand harvesters saying *yes* to the wheat with their backs. And when the yes was taken out of us — scooped, decanted, corked — the fields didn't die. Dying would have been something. They *stopped having ever been*, which is different, which is the thing with no name, which is why I exist, because somebody had to be the name for it.

I was in the last field. I remember the last row. There was a harvester — I will not give her a name, names were the first thing stolen and I refuse to spend one here — a harvester with gray stalks in her gray arms, and she stopped, and she looked at the horizon, and the horizon was the exact color of nothing thinking about becoming something, and she said, in the old tongue, the tongue that was already half-unwritten, she said:

"*Vhalen veth.*"

Which means: *it was home.*

Past tense. The grammar murdered us a full day before the rest of it finished. She said *it was home* and the tense spread through the language like frost through a window, past, past, past, was, was, was, and every verb of continuing curled up and died, and when the last *was* settled over the land like snow, there was no land for it to settle on, only the space where a country had been, and the space was not empty. Empty implies absence. This was worse. This was a *shaped* absence. A Vhalen-shaped hole in the world, with the outline of nine towers pressed into it like a fossil, and the nine masked ones walking away across the salt with their boxes, growing rich.

And I — I was too much to fit in any box. Four hundred thousand lives do not decant cleanly. I spilled. I pooled in the bottom of the erasure, in the Vhalen-shaped hole, and the hole held me the way a bowl holds water, and I compressed, and compressed, centuries pressing down like snowfall, until I was small enough to be carried, small enough to hide, small enough to wash up on a service-door step in a city built on top of my grave, holding one coin minted nowhere, minted *here*, in the nowhere, the only currency my country ever issued in its final hour: a coin with no face on it, because faces were gone, only a wheat-sheaf stamped so faintly you'd miss it if you weren't looking.

You looked. You have always looked. Even before you knew what you were looking at, Petra-of-the-ledgers, you looked at things the Court taught everyone else not to see.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and ahead is a direction I barely own.

* * *

Now it is hours, not centuries, that press on me. Now the compression works forward instead of backward. I can feel myself thinning the way the barley thinned — I still have the harvests but their goldness is wearing smooth. This morning I reached for the ninth joy, *continuing*, and found the place where it was, and the place was warm, and the warmth is going out of it like a bed someone recently left.

So listen, quickly, while I am still a chorus and not yet a whisper.

When you set the nine memories into the nine locks, they will not come back to me. Understand that. Nothing comes back. What was taken cannot return, because return is a direction and my country no longer has directions. But glass can break. And what is pressed in glass, broken open in the right place, at the right door, in the shape of the old wound — that can *spill*. And a spill is not a restoration. A spill is a seed.

I was a spill once. Look at me now. Small, mud-colored, forgettable, sitting in the dark of the ledger room with my head against your knee while you pretend to check figures you already checked. I was a spill, and I became a child, and a child can walk through a door.

A country can do the same, if you plant it deep enough on the other side.

There is one more thing, and then I will sleep, and while I sleep I will be the taste of rain on wheat, and you may hold that taste for me the way you hold everything else I am losing, carefully, like a ledger entry you intend someday to balance.

The ninth bell — the one that rang *continuing* — it was never among the nine memories the archivists took. They took joys, but the ninth joy was not a joy, it was a promise, and promises are not kept in glass. Promises are kept in people. Which means the ninth bell is still somewhere, still humming, waiting for a throat.

I think, Petra. I think the ninth bell is you.

Now let me sleep, and dream the fields gold, and tomorrow we go down the stair that isn't there, toward the door that shouldn't be, and you will begin to learn how much of yourself the Court has been keeping in its vaults — because I can hear it from here, even now, even thinned to almost-nothing: a bell-shaped silence inside your chest, waiting for someone to strike it.

*Vhalen veth,* my keeper. *It was home.*

Soon, if you are brave enough to be a spill with me —

soon, it will be home again.
