## 16. The Door Out of Nowhere

The child's guidance was failing the way a candle fails — not all at once, but in stutters, in long dark intervals between flames.

For three days we had walked north out of the Court's territory, through country that grew stranger the farther we went from ledgers and masks. First the wine hills, where Petra Reyes had once appraised a vintner's memory of his dead wife's laugh and had not thought about it since; then the pine forests where the roads thinned to deer paths and then to suggestions of paths; then a high moor where the wind spoke in no language at all, only pressure and cold. The child rode on my back when its legs gave out, its arms looped loose around my neck, and it hummed. It hummed constantly now. Not melodies — I had learned they were not melodies. They were inventories. A list of bells set to rhythm. A census of doors, sung. Each hour the songs grew shorter, as if whole verses of the world were being struck from the score.

"Tell me what you're losing," I asked once, at dusk on the moor.

The child considered this with the grave patience it brought to everything. "The smell of bread," it said. "I had it this morning. There was a woman in Vhalen whose bread smelled like rain coming off hot stone, and everyone in her street timed their days by her ovens." Its brow furrowed, an old person's gesture in a young face. "This afternoon I remembered the street but not the woman. Tonight—" it held up its hands, turned them over, as though the answer might be written on the palms— "tonight I have the street and a smell like rain coming off hot stone, and I do not know anymore which one came first."

We had three memories sewn into the lining of my coat: the last sunrise, warm even through the wool, so that my left side was never quite cold; the harvest-bell, which chimed faintly whenever I turned too quickly, like a conscience; the coronation oath, silent and heavy as a river stone, which I had taken from an auction house in a night that still visited me in dreams. Three locks' worth. Six missing. And the child forgetting itself by the hour, thinning like smoke, its edges going translucent at dusk — I had seen the moor grass through its wrist twice now, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would not be a cruelty dressed as honesty.

On the fourth day the green country ended.

It did not end the way countries end, with a river or a customs post or a change in the architecture of fences. It ended the way a sentence ends when someone has scraped the words off the page. One step we were in scrub pine and gorse; the next, white. Salt. A salt flat stretching to every horizon, blinding under a high pale sun, cracked into hexagons like the skin of something ancient and sleeping. No birds. No insects. The silence had texture; it pressed against the ears like water.

The child slid from my back and stood very still.

"It's here," it whispered. "Underneath. Underneath underneath underneath." And then it began to walk — not hesitantly, the way it walked everywhere else, but with sudden terrible purpose, dragging me by two fingers, its small hand burning cold against mine.

* * *

The flat went on for hours. Salt got into everything — my boots, the seams of the coat, the creases of my palms, so that for days afterward everything I touched tasted faintly of the sea's ghost. The sun did not seem to move. My shadow lay beneath my feet like a puddle. Twice I checked our position against the crude map I'd copied from the archives of the Erased — the ledger where the founding crime was recorded in plain sight, the borders of Vhalen inked and then scratched out and then inked again by nine different hands across four centuries, each generation of masters re-committing and re-concealing the theft. According to those maps, we were walking through the middle of a country. According to the world, we were walking through nothing at all.

"The maps are right," the child said, reading my thoughts the way it sometimes did, effortlessly and without malice, the way you might read a sign posted at eye level. "They're just maps of the floor. We're walking on the ceiling."

"I don't understand."

"You will," it said, and smiled — and for a moment its face was not a child's face at all but the face of a place, if a place could look at you, if a place could be fond of you.

Near what should have been late afternoon — the sun disagreed — the child stopped so abruptly I nearly walked past it. It stood pointing with one arm straight as a surveyor's rod.

At first I saw nothing. Then the light shifted — or I shifted, some fraction of a degree, the way you shift when a stair isn't there under your foot — and the horizon resolved.

A door.

Standing free. Standing alone in the middle of the salt, upright without wall or frame or building, a door in the middle of nowhere the way a question stands in the middle of a conversation. It was perhaps eight feet tall, dark wood gone silver-gray with age, carved along its lintel with nine towers. Nine towers — I knew them before I knew them, knew them the way I had known fragments of the dead tongue the night the child first knocked at the service door. The palace of nine towers. The child's earliest gift to me, surfacing in my own mind uninvited, memories I had never lived wearing my memories like stolen coats.

Around the door, in a rough circle perhaps fifty paces across, the salt was different. Greener. Not green with plant life — there was nothing growing — but green the way glass is green, deep in its substance, as if the ground itself remembered being a field. Within that circle the air moved. Outside it, nothing had moved all day. Inside it there was a breeze, gentle and warm, carrying a smell I could not name and could not stop breathing: cut hay, woodsmoke, rain on dust, bread. The composite smell of a whole country, distilled to its essence and leaking, drop by drop, under a door.

"Oh," I said. It was all I had. Fourteen years in the Ninefold Court, appraising griefs and kisses and the last words of dying men, trained to name the worth of anything in an instant — and the entire vocabulary of my profession collapsed into *oh*.

The child ran for it. Ran across the green circle laughing — actually laughing, the first time I had heard it laugh, a sound like a bell and a brook arguing — and threw both arms around the door and pressed its cheek to the wood the way you'd press your cheek to a mother's chest.

And I saw the locks.

Nine of them, set down the door's edge in a vertical line, each different, each ancient: a padlock black as bog iron; a hasp of woven bronze like braided hair; a ring-and-staple fused shut with something that glittered dully, like dried blood mixed with glass; a lock shaped like a bird with folded wings; others half-corroded beyond identifying. Eight visible. I counted twice, hoping I had miscounted, hoping the ninth was hidden, decorative, already open.

Eight locks hung closed. The ninth — the lowest, smallest, simplest, a plain iron latch-lock worn smooth as a river pebble — hung open, swinging gently in the door's private breeze.

"One," the child said softly. It touched the open lock with one finger, tenderly, the way you'd touch a scar. "The kitchen door of the palace. Someone got out. One person, right at the end, when everything else was—" it searched, failed, settled— "*folded*. They couldn't take the whole country, so they took the doors. And they locked them from their side. But one lock—" the finger trembled— "one lock somebody opened from inside, and the person who came out carried enough of us to make me. And then they died, and I was in the mud for a long time, and then I knocked on your door."

I crouched beside it. "Three memories," I said. "That's three locks. Which ones?"

The child pointed without looking, eyes still on the open latch. "The sunrise. The bell. The oath." Its voice flattened. "But Petra. The other six aren't lost. You know that. I can feel them from here, like teeth through a swollen jaw."

I knew. I had known since the archive, since I read the founders' own hand admitting what they had done. Six founding memories remained, and six masked masters still wore them — wore them the way crowns are worn, fused to their skulls behind lacquer and ritual, drawing their long unnatural lives from a country only they remembered existing. Ossory among them, with whatever he wore beneath his mask — I had felt its weight once, passing him on the stairs, a gravity like standing near a well.

"They'll come," I said. "When we use the three we have, they'll feel it. Every lock we open, they get weaker, and they will know exactly why."

"Yes," said the child simply. It said it the way children say *yes, the medicine tastes bad*, accepting the necessary without negotiation.

* * *

We camped that night within the green circle, because outside it the cold was absolute and inside it the air held the warmth of an endless late summer evening. I built no fire — nothing burned here, the salt seeing to that — and we ate hard bread and dried apples while the strange local dusk came on, violet and gold, colors bleeding up out of the ground rather than down from the sky. Above us the stars came out wrong. I recognized none of the constellations. Overhead was not the sky I knew; overhead, I understood slowly, was the sky of Vhalen, preserved in the bubble of the door's remembering, the last piece of heaven that still belonged to the erased.

The child slept against my shoulder. In sleep it flickered. I watched its outline soften and sharpen, soften and sharpen, like breath on glass, like a lamp guttering. Each time it steadied itself, I felt a faint shudder pass through the small body — the effort of remaining, the daily labor of being one person when you were the compressed residue of millions.

I sat with the coat across my knees and felt the three memories through the lining, and I made an appraisal, because appraising was the one thing I knew how to do. Three locks we could open. Six we could not, yet. Behind us, two hundred miles south, the Ninefold Court was tearing itself apart — I had heard it in the rumors that chased us through the last market town, whispers of masters defying masters, of a purge in the vault levels, of Master Ossory walking his halls at all hours like a wolf pacing a shrinking cage. Chaos was opportunity; I had built a career on that arithmetic. But chaos also meant desperate men, and desperate men with centuries of hoarded power do not negotiate. They hunt.

And somewhere behind us — I had felt it since the moor, a prickling between my shoulder blades that was not wind — something was following. Not the echoes; those I would have recognized, the weaponized nostalgia of faces I had loved. This was quieter. This was patient. This was the feeling of a ledger being balanced, of a debt being tallied, of a man in a mask doing sums.

The child stirred. "You're afraid," it murmured, not fully awake.

"I'm a clerk," I said. "Fear is just interest accruing on hope. I'm keeping track of it."

Its mouth curved. Then, eyes still closed, it said the thing I had been not-saying to myself for three days:

"When we open the three locks tomorrow, the door will crack. Just a little. Just enough to see through." Its hand found the open ninth lock and rested there. "And Petra — through the crack, I'll be able to hear them. All of them. Everyone I am. And I don't know if I'll want to keep walking afterward, or if I'll want to stay at the crack forever, listening." The eyes opened then, dark and bottomless and terribly tired. "So promise me you'll count. Whatever happens. Promise me you'll keep the ledger."

Somewhere out on the salt flat, far off in the direction of the world we had fled — the world of maps that agreed with each other — a light appeared. Small. Moving. Steady. Not searching, the way searchers search, in sweeps and circles.

Coming straight toward us.

I watched it, and put my hand on the child's hair, and said, "I promise. Now sleep. Tomorrow we find out what kind of key a memory is."

The light grew. It did not hurry. It had all the patience in the world, and it was not stopping.
