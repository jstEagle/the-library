## 17. Masks Off

### I.

Master Ossory had not removed his mask in three hundred years, and so he was the only one of the nine who felt the crack before he saw it.

It began as a sound — a fine, hairline singing at the edge of his hearing, like ice on a winter pond, like a wineglass touched by a wet finger. He was in his counting room above the Hall of Ledgers, doing what he always did in the hour before dawn: counting. Not money. Money was for clerks. Ossory counted memories. He kept them in his head the way a miser keeps coins in a mattress, turning each one over nightly, feeling its weight and wear, confirming that nothing had been shaved from its edges.

*The bells of Vhalen,* he thought, and there they were: nine bells in a tower of white stone, and the sound of them rolling down over terraced fields, over a river the color of hammered tin, over the faces of people who —

The bell-tower flickered.

He sat very still. The candle did not flicker; the *tower* did. The white stone went briefly gray at its crown, the way bread goes stale from the outside in, and when he reached for the memory again it answered him a half-beat late, like a servant who has begun to resent his master.

Ossory rose from his chair and pressed both palms flat against the ledger desk until the wood bit into his skin. Around him, the counting room performed its small eternal theater: scales weighing nothing, quills writing nothing, a fire burning without heat, all of it conjured long ago out of stolen wonder and maintained by habit. Habit was the mortar of the Ninefold Court. Habit and hunger.

Someone had taken a founding memory.

Not lost it — taken. There was a difference, and the difference had a taste: copper, salt, and underneath it something almost sweet, like the breath of a child who has been running. The last sunrise of Vhalen had been kept in the drowned cistern beneath the old aqueduct for as long as there had been a Court to keep it, tended by wardens who were themselves little more than memories given chores. It was gone. And gone with it was the light.

He could feel the absence the way an amputee feels weather. Every founding memory the masters wore was a limb of a country, and the country had begun to favor itself again — to pull its own weight back through whatever hands held it. Somewhere in the city, a clerk named Reyes was walking around with a piece of dawn in their pocket.

Ossory poured himself wine he would not drink and considered, with the patience of a man who had eaten centuries, how best to eat this problem too.

Then the second bell-tower flickered, and he understood that it was not going to be so simple.

### II.

Master Ysolt kept her founding memory folded into her left hand.

That was the difference between her and the others, and she had never told any of them. Where Ossory wore his like a crown and Master Corvane wore his like armor and poor hollowed Halvard wore his like a pacifier clutched against nightmares, Ysolt had spent two hundred years holding hers the way you hold a bird — firmly enough that it cannot fly, gently enough that it does not die. Hers was a market morning. Fish on ice. A woman laughing at her own haggling. A city gate opening at first light because Vhalen trusted its mornings.

She could feel it dying in her palm.

She stood at the window of her apartments in the Court's east range, watching the harbor fog come in off the gray water, and she opened her fingers one at a time, and the market morning lay there in her hand like a moth, its colors gone to ash at the edges.

Three memories reclaimed already. She knew it the way they all knew it now — not through report but through rot. The masks had been beautiful once, or so the founders claimed in the minutes no one had ever been permitted to read. Nine masks carved from the wood of Vhalen's nine towers, each holding one ninth of a nation's soul. Wear the mask, feed on the remembering, live past death. What the minutes did not say — what Ysolt had worked out alone, over decades, in the privacy of her own cowardice — was that the arrangement required all nine parts. The country had been cut into nine pieces precisely so that no single master could hold the whole of it. But a country is not a coin. A country wants to be whole. Cut anything living into nine pieces and the pieces will strain toward one another across whatever distance you put between them, quietly, forever, like lovers separated at a border.

Take away one piece and the other eight begin to grieve.

Ysolt closed her hand. The market morning fluttered weakly against her palm, and she felt something she had not felt since her initiation, when Ossory had held her face between his hands and told her, kindly, that she would never be hungry again: she felt pity. For a memory. For herself. For the woman at the fish stall who had never known she was destined to become a snack for immortals.

Below her window, in the courtyard, torches were being lit. She counted them. Twelve. Then eighteen. Ossory was mustering hunters, and he was not being quiet about it, which meant he wanted the others to see. Which meant he suspected the others.

Which meant it had already begun.

She took up her pen, and on the back of a debt notice — the Court's paper, the Court's ink, let him choke on the irony — she wrote four words in a cipher only two other people alive could read:

*The wolf is starving.*

She sealed it, gave it to a courier she had owned for thirty years, body and debts, and watched from the window as he vanished into the fog toward the west range, where Master Corvane kept his rooms and his knives and his increasingly desperate collection of purchased youth.

Then Ysolt did something no master of the Ninefold Court had done in living memory, which was easy, since masters of the Ninefold Court did not have living memories, not really, only rented ones.

She took off her mask.

Without it her face was ordinary — a broad, lined, middle-aged face belonging to a woman who might have sold bread in any city on any coast. She had forgotten she looked like this. She had forgotten she had a face under the wood. She wept a little, standing there in the fog-light, holding a dying market morning and wearing her own expression, and then she blew out her candles and went downstairs to commit treason.

### III.

Corvane received Ysolt's message at the same moment he received Ossory's summons, and the comedy of it nearly made him laugh out loud, which would have been fatal.

He read them side by side in his workroom, among his jars. Corvane collected echoes the way other men collected wines — he had copies of everyone he had ever envied, everyone he had ever feared, everyone he had ever loved badly. They hung in his cellar like cured hams, dreaming their borrowed dreams, and twice a year he brought one up and had a conversation with it and pretended afterward that this was not the loneliest thing a man could do.

Ossory's summons said: *Attend the convocation. Bring your mask.*

Ysolt's note said: *The wolf is starving.*

Starving wolves, Corvane knew, do not convene. Starving wolves cull the pack. And Corvane had done arithmetic of his own lately, late at night, with his mask off — another secret, another sin — because his founding memory was a funeral. That was what he had been given at initiation, the piece of Vhalen assigned to him: a state funeral, banners, a closed coffin, ten thousand mourners. For two hundred years he had assumed the grandeur of it meant importance. Only recently had he understood what it actually was.

Vhalen's founders had divided their country's soul into nine pieces, and one of those pieces was grief. Not triumph, not glory, not the coronation or the founding law. *Grief.* Because grief is the densest thing a people carry. Because you can feed longest on sorrow.

And because whoever chose the division had known — had *planned* — that a day would come when the pieces would want to go home.

"Convocation," Corvane said aloud, to the jar nearest his elbow, which contained an echo of his brother, drowned forty years before Corvane ever joined the Court. The echo turned slowly in its brine, patient as fish. "He'll ask who helped the clerk. He'll ask each of us to swear it wasn't us. And whichever of us swears most beautifully—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. His brother's glass face smiled at him with the smile of a man who has been dead too long to be shocked by anything.

There were, Corvane calculated, exactly three ways out of the convocation chamber: as master, as meal, or as smoke. He intended to be smoke. But smoke needs a wind, and the only wind blowing in the Ninefold Court tonight was a clerk with a satchel full of sunrise and a feral child made of everything the masters had ever eaten.

So Corvane did the second-worst thing he had ever done, after the first-worst thing, which had been joining.

He wrote back: *I'm yours. Name the price.*

And then he burned his copy of the Court's oldest map — the one with the blank space where a country used to be, the absence drawn in gold leaf like a wound dressed in silk — and packed his jars, all of them, even the ones that screamed.

### IV.

They convened at moonrise, the seven who remained loyal, in the Round Chamber where the Court had been founded, beneath the dome painted with nine towers that no longer existed anywhere but overhead.

Ossory sat them in a circle and spoke to them for an hour about vigilance, about the thief in their midst, about the sanctity of the great work, and as he spoke he watched their hands. Masks hide faces beautifully, but hands confess everything, and Ossory had three hundred years of practice reading confession.

Halvard's hands were shaking. Halvard's hands had been shaking for weeks, ever since the fourth memory — his, a wedding, humble and sunlit — had begun to fray at the edges like cloth worried by moths. Halvard was the weakest of them, the newest, barely a century old, recruited from a plague city where he had sold false cures to the dying. He had never been strong enough for what he'd been given. Grief, triumph, dawn, a market morning, a funeral — these could be carried. But a *wedding*. All that hope, all that ordinary human tenderness, packed into a man who had built his life on selling lies to corpses-to-be. Halvard had been drowning in borrowed joy for a hundred years, and now the joy was leaving him, and Ossory could see the terrible relief underneath the terror on his trembling hands.

*He wants to give it back,* Ossory realized. *He wants to fail.*

Which made Halvard not a traitor but something worse: a leak.

"Brothers," Ossory said, and his voice through the carved wood of his mask was warm as a banked fire, "the thief has taken three of our treasures. I do not intend to mourn the fourth. So let us make certain there is no fourth."

He rose. The others rose. Seven masks caught the candlelight, seven pairs of hands folded in imitation of calm.

"Halvard," Ossory said gently. "Come here."

Later — in the years Halvard did not have — he would try to remember whether he had seen it coming, and he would conclude that yes, of course, he had seen it coming from the moment Ossory said his name in that warm voice, and that he had walked forward anyway, because obedience was the deepest thing they had ever carved into him, deeper than the wedding, deeper than his own name.

He crossed the circle. Ossory laid one hand on the back of his neck, tenderly, the way you steady a child at prayer.

"You're tired," Ossory said.

"I'm tired," Halvard agreed, weeping inside the mask.

"Give it back, then."

What happened next happened in silence, which was the Court's way; violence among masters had long ago been refined past noise. Ossory did not tear the memory from him. He *unmade the keeping* — reached through the mask into the place where Halvard stored his ninth of Vhalen, and simply stopped holding the door shut. The wedding came out of Halvard like water out of a cracked jug. It poured through the chamber, and for one impossible moment every master in the Round Chamber was standing in a sunlit field watching two strangers promise each other forever, smelling cut grass and beeswax, hearing an old priest say the old words — and then Ossory gathered it, all of it, every thread, and swallowed.

The candles guttered. The dome overhead seemed, briefly, less like paint.

Halvard collapsed the way furniture collapses when the rope is cut — all at once, without dignity. And here was the thing none of the six remaining masters would ever say aloud: for a single instant, looking down at the empty mask rolling on the floor and the shriveled man inside it, they had all felt *envy*. Because Halvard was finished. Halvard was free. Whatever was left of him would be swept out with the morning ash, and it would never again have to hold a country's happiness in hands too small for it.

Ossory straightened, adjusted his sleeves, and addressed the six survivors in the tone of a man concluding routine business.

"The clerk and the child will surface," he said. "Thieves always do. When they do, I will take back what is ours, and the Court will be nine again." He paused. "Six," he corrected himself, without apology. "Six is enough. Six has always been enough. We were greedy with nine."

Under his mask, in the dark, Master Ossory smiled with teeth he had bought from a dead man.

He did not yet know that two chairs' worth of loyalty had already left the room — that Corvane's seat held a man composing alibis while his heart signed surrender papers, and that Ysolt's place was filled by a body whose mind was three streets away and moving fast, carrying a dying market morning toward the one person in the city who might know how to keep such things alive.

He did not know. But the dome knew. Painted towers have long memories, and all night they leaned inward, listening, the way towers lean when the ground beneath them has begun to shift.

### V.

In the ledger room, Petra woke to the smell of smoke that wasn't there.

The child was sitting upright in the nest of ledgers, eyes wide open, and the air around them shimmered faintly, the way air shimmers over a road in high summer. Petra had learned the child's signs by now: the stillness that meant listening, the sudden brightness that meant remembering, the grayness creeping through the irises that meant forgetting, God help them both, hour by hour, more and more.

"What is it?" Petra whispered. "What do you feel?"

The child's mouth worked. Words came slowly these days, worn smooth by use, and Petra had to lean close to catch them.

"Bells," the child said. "A bell fell."

Petra waited. The child's fingers moved against the floorboards, tapping out a rhythm Petra almost recognized — a peal pattern, change-ringing, the kind of thing bell-ringers practice for years — except the sequence was wrong, truncated, missing a voice. Eight bells ringing a pattern written for nine.

"Something broke," the child said. Something in the child's voice had changed since the cistern, since the sunrise, since the thefts began. Firmer. Sadder. More like a person and less like a weather system. "One of the — the ones who ate us. He couldn't hold his piece anymore. It went back into—" The child pressed both hands over their own heart. "Into me. Into us. I can feel a wedding, Petra. I can feel a whole wedding. Grass and beeswax and an old man saying the words." Tears slid down the small face without disturbing its expression at all. "But it's mixed up with screaming. The man who kept it. He's still screaming somewhere. Empty now. Like me. Like I'll be."

Petra sat back on their heels and did the arithmetic they had been avoiding for days. Three memories recovered. Six still held — and now, apparently, consolidated further into fewer and crueller hands. The Court was eating itself, which was opportunity, which was the only door through chaos they would ever get. And somewhere above them, hunters were mustering by torchlight, eighteen strong, while masters defected and masters devoured and the whole ancient machine of the Ninefold Court groaned on its foundations like a ship taking water.

"We move tonight," Petra said.

The child nodded — and then stopped, and tilted their head, and their face went strange.

"There's someone in the stairwell," they said.

Petra's hand found the knife they had stolen along with everything else. Below them, on the stairs that appeared only when unobserved, footsteps were climbing. Two sets. Unhurried. One heavy, one light.

"They're not hunting us," the child said, frowning at some perception Petra had no organ for. "One of them smells like a funeral. And the other one..." The child looked up, and their gray-flecked eyes were wide with something Petra had never seen in them before.

Hope.

"The other one smells like a market morning," the child said. "Like fish on ice. Like a woman laughing at her own haggling." The child gripped Petra's sleeve. "She's bringing ours back, Petra. One of them is bringing ours *back*."

The knock, when it came, was soft. Three raps, and then — unmistakably, deliberately — a fourth, tapped out in the rhythm of a peal pattern missing a voice.

Petra looked at the child. The child looked at Petra. Somewhere far below, the Court's foundations shifted another inch toward ruin, and the stairs that appear only when unobserved observed them both, waiting to be climbed.

Petra opened the door.
