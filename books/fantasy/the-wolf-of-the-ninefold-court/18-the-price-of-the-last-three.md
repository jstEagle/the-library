## 18. The Price of the Last Three

The tide came in over the salt flat at dusk, and with it came the smell of the Court's defeat.

Petra stood at the mouth of the cistern stair with the ledger of debts open across their forearms — the new one, the honest one, inked in their own hand — and counted what remained. Six memories recovered. Six locks turned at a door that stood alone in the white waste like a tooth pulled from some enormous jaw. Six green seams of light bleeding around its edges into the coming dark.

Three memories left. Three locks still shut.

And all three of them lived inside Master Ossory.

"You're certain," said Wren, the clerk whose first theft Petra had stolen in another life, three chapters and a lifetime ago. She stood with her back to the wind, arms crossed, her face still carrying that raw, unfinished look of someone who had woken weeping without knowing why and had decided, since then, to know why everything. "Certain as you're certain about anything."

"The echo saw it before it burned." Petra's voice came out flat, which was how they knew they were frightened. Flatness was what fear sounded like when it had been trained for thirty years to hold a pen steady. "The last thing it sent back through the bond was Ossory's mask coming off, and underneath —" They stopped. Swallowed. "Underneath there wasn't a face. There was a harvest festival. A bell tower. A wedding on a river barge. He ate them whole, Wren. He didn't wear the founding memories like crowns. The others did. He *swallowed* his, centuries ago, and let them root."

The hermit warden sat on the lowest step of the stair, wrapped in his coat of grey felt gone stiff with salt, watching the door with the expression of a man watching an old wound refuse to close. He had guarded Vhalen's last sunrise in a drowned cistern for longer than most kingdoms had existed, and he had not forgiven Petra for taking it. He had come anyway.

"He'll be strong," the warden said. His voice was gravel soaked in brine. "The other eight wore their memories. Wearing is a leash. Swallowing is a marriage. You don't take a thing out of a marriage by picking its pocket."

"No," Petra agreed. "You trade for it."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the cistern stair, because the child had taught them that the borderlands of erased countries rewarded those who kept small fires, though no one could have said why. The flames burned slightly wrong here — a half-beat behind their own heat, as if the light were remembering fire secondhand — and by that doubled glow Petra laid out the plan they had been turning over since the echo died, since before the echo died, if they were honest, since the moment in the vault when they had first lied to Master Ossory's smooth voice and felt something in their chest unclench like a fist opening.

Ossory was holed up in the Court's auction house, or what remained of it after the masters turned on each other. Two had defected — Master Ilenne had simply walked out of the conclave and vanished into the city wearing her founding memory like a woman carrying stolen silver, and Master Corvane had sold his to a collector before the purge began, which told Petra everything about how he valued his own immortality. One was dead. Ossory had unmade him in the council chamber, and the sound of it, according to the debtor-allies who had witnessed the flight from the Court, had been the sound of a book being slammed shut on a scream.

That left five masks still loyal, and one wolf.

"He'll expect negotiation," Petra said. "He offered me a seat among the nine once. He thinks everyone has a price because he bought his own a long time ago and never noticed the receipt." They turned the page of the new ledger. Their hand did not shake, and they hated how much effort the steadiness cost. "So we negotiate. And while he's talking — while he's showing me how much of my past is already his property, and he will show me, he showed me once and men like him never tire of their best trick — I go in."

"Go in where?" Wren asked, though her voice said she already knew.

"Into him." Petra looked up. The firelight moved wrong across their hands. "A memory isn't a coin. It can't just be cut out of a man who's fused himself to it. But memory trades for memory — that's the oldest law of the Court, older than the nine founders, older than the ledgers. Every exchange leaves both parties changed. If I enter his mind and offer him something he wants badly enough, the founding memories loosen. Grief loosens them. Longing loosens them. He's been holding a country hostage inside his own skull for four hundred years, and four hundred years is long enough to get lonely even for a wolf."

"And what do you have," said the warden, not unkindly, "that a man would trade a country for?"

Petra closed the ledger.

"Myself," they said. "Everything I am. My mother's voice. My name. My first theft. The taste of the tea I drink every morning in the ledger room. The rule I never broke, and the pride I took in never breaking it. He wants selves, Wren. Polished ones. Permanent ones. He collects people the way I used to collect debts — carefully, at arm's length, telling myself I wasn't touching anything." They laughed, and it came out wrong, too short. "I'm the only person alive who has spent thirty years keeping perfect accounts of other people's losses. I am, God help me, *qualified*."

The silence that followed had texture. It had the weight of the salt air and the wrong-burning fire and the six seams of green light at the edge of hearing, because the door had begun to make a sound now, low and continuous, like a held breath, like a country waiting behind a wall with its ear pressed to the wood.

Wren spoke first. "There are other ways—"

"There aren't."

"—slower ways. We could starve him out. The masks are failing, you said so yourself. Each memory we pull back weakens—"

"Weakened wolves bite hardest." Petra shook their head. "And every day we wait, the child forgets more. You've seen it. Yesterday she couldn't remember the name of the river. This morning she couldn't remember there was a river. She is a nation compressed into one small body and she is going out hour by hour like a lamp running dry, and somewhere behind that door there are fields that won't come back unless—" Their throat closed. They made themselves finish. "Unless all of her comes home."

The child herself sat apart from the fire, as she always did, knees drawn up, watching the door with eyes that had seen a country die from the inside. When Petra glanced at her, she tilted her head — that birdlike, ancient gesture — and said, in plain speech now, in words that cost her visible effort:

"You will come out smaller."

"I know."

"You will not know why you are smaller."

"I know that too."

She considered this with the terrible patience of the very young and the very old. Then: "In Vhalen we had a word for someone who gives pieces of themselves so a place can live again." Her brow furrowed; the word slipped away even as she reached for it; her hands curled into fists of frustration against her knees. "We had a word," she repeated, quieter. "I had it an hour ago."

"Then I'll earn it twice," Petra said. "Once from you, once from whoever you become."

* * *

They went to the auction house alone, because that was part of the price and everyone understood it. A delegation would read as war. A single clerk with a ledger under one arm would read as surrender, and Ossory had spent four centuries believing in nothing so completely as he believed in other people's surrender.

The building rose out of the salt mist like a ribcage picked clean. Its great doors stood open — an invitation, obviously, and Petra walked through it with their heart going like a trapped bird and their face arranged into the mild neutrality that had carried them through thirty years of appraising widows' kisses and balancing accounts of traded grief.

Inside, the auction floor was lit by a single chandelier of glass decanters, each one holding a curl of colored light: a memory awaiting sale, or a memory already sold and left empty, Petra couldn't tell anymore, and understood suddenly that the distinction had never mattered here.

Master Ossory sat at the appraiser's table.

He was thinner than Petra remembered. That was the first shock — that the wolf could be diminished visibly, that the purges and defections and the slow hemorrhaging of his brotherhood had carved lines into a face that had once seemed poured from wax and certainty. His mask lay beside him on the table, face-up, empty. Without it he was merely an old man with extraordinary eyes, and the extraordinary eyes were the second shock, because they were *tired*, and tiredness was a crack, and cracks were doors.

"Clerk Reyes." He spread his hands, and the sleeves of his robe fell back from wrists gone papery. "Come to balance the account at last?"

"I've come to make an offer."

"Ah." Something moved behind the tiredness — appetite, maybe, or hope, and Petra hated that they couldn't tell the difference and hated more that they'd need to learn. "Sit, then. Let us do business the old way. You were always the best of them at the old way. Careful. Neutral. Never once opened a ledger you weren't supposed to." He smiled, and the smile had rust in it. "I've wondered, these past months, what it cost you to break the rule. I've wondered whether you even know yet."

"I know what things cost," Petra said. They sat. They opened the ledger. "That's rather the point of me."

Outside, far off across the flats, the door breathed its held breath. Somewhere above them both, in whatever country kept the sky, stars were coming out over a nation that did not exist, shining down equally on its executioner and its accountant.

Petra turned to a blank page, dipped their pen, and wrote, in a clear hand, the only entry that had ever mattered:

*One mind, entered willingly. Payment: to be determined.*

"Now," they said, looking up into Ossory's hungry, hollowed, four-hundred-year-old eyes, "show me what you want. And I'll show you what I'm willing to lose."

Ossory leaned forward, and his shadow on the wall unfolded like a wing, and somewhere very deep beneath the auction house, beneath the salt, beneath the world, three memories stirred in their long sleep and began — softly, sweetly, terribly — to sing.
