## 19. Inside the Wolf

The way in is through a wound.

That is how the old texts describe it — the ones Petra was never meant to read, the marginalia in ledgers three hundred years dead: *to enter a mind that hoards memory, enter as memory enters, through the newest grief.* And Ossory's newest grief, they had learned in the long terrible hour of preparation, was the crack running through his own mask, hairline-fine, where the second and third reclaimed memories had torn free of him like teeth pulled from a jaw.

So Petra sits across from the hollowed master in the vault beneath the Court, takes his ruined hands in theirs, and does not think of doors or stairs or escape. They think of grief. They let it rise — not their own, which they have spent so carefully over the years, but the shape of one, a vessel's shape — and they lean forward and press their forehead to the split in the lacquered mask, the way a kiss is pressed to a wound, the way a ledger is pressed shut.

The world folds like paper.

* * *

Petra falls upward.

There is no other word for it. They fall, and the falling carries them higher, deeper, into a dark that is not empty but *full* — full the way an archive is full, the way a throat is full before it speaks. Light comes from below them, from everywhere, a pale amber glow like lamplight through honey, and when they finally stop moving they are standing on a road made of days.

Not paved with them. Made of them. The road beneath Petra's boots is a summer market day, and they can feel it: the press of crowds, the smell of frying fish, a child crying two streets over about a dropped sweet. The road stretches ahead into amber haze and behind them into amber haze, and branching off it, in every direction, run other roads — thousands of them, tens of thousands, crossing and braiding and splitting again until the whole sky is a canopy of roads, all of them glowing faintly, all of them humming with the lives pressed into their surfaces.

Ossory's mind. His hoard. Four centuries of taken remembrance laid down like cobblestones, and he has walked on them every day of his long life.

"Mind the seams."

The voice comes from everywhere at once, warm as mulled wine, and Petra does not flinch because flinching is a debt paid too easily. Ossory coalesces out of the haze beside them — not masked here, never masked here, this is the one place he wears his own face, and it is worse than the mask. It is a kind face. A grandfather's face, seamed and patient, with eyes the color of tea left too long in the pot.

"You came," he says, and there is genuine pleasure in it, which is the most frightening thing yet.

"You invited me."

"I invited you to die politely in my parlor." He gestures, and the amber light bends toward a structure rising ahead on the main road — a house, vast beyond reason, its walls built of framed moments the way a normal house is built of bricks. "But here you are, walking my roads instead. Do you know what you're standing on?"

"A market day."

"Vhalen's last market day." He says it the way a man names a vintage. "I took it myself, four hundred years ago. The whole afternoon. Every vendor, every argument over the price of eels, every stolen glance between the cooper's daughter and the ferryman's son. I keep it underfoot so I can feel it when I walk. Come inside. We have business, you and I, and I would rather do it sitting down."

* * *

The house has no door. The wall simply opens, the way a memory opens, and they step into a hall whose ceiling is a night sky dense with stars that Petra slowly understands are not stars. Each point of light is a memory, hung and catalogued, and there are more of them than there are stars over any real sky. More than there have been nights since the Court was founded.

They sit. The chairs are upholstered in someone's wedding day; Petra can feel the nervous joy of it through the fabric and makes themselves stop feeling it.

"Three memories," Ossory says. He holds up three fingers, and in each fingertip a light burns — red-gold, deep as forge-coals. The founding memories, fused into him, the last three locks of Vhalen's door. "You've come to bargain. Good. I respect bargains. I've been making them since before your language settled into its bones. So let us be honest with each other, clerk, because honesty in a mindscape is cheaper than it ever is outside."

"Name your price."

"My price is everything you are." He says it gently, without menace, which somehow strips the words of any mercy. "But we'll begin smaller. That's what bargains are — small doors into large rooms." He waves a hand, and from the star-field above, one light detaches itself and drifts down between them, growing, taking shape.

It becomes a kitchen. A small kitchen, narrow, with a window over the sink that looks out on a brick airshaft. A woman stands at the counter with her back turned, singing under her breath, off-key, some harbor song. Her hands are quick and capable. She has flour to the wrists.

Petra's breath stops.

They know that kitchen. They know the particular squeak of the third floorboard. They know that if she turns around she will have flour on her cheekbone, just there, and she will wipe it with the back of her wrist and say *you're up early, little ledger* —

"Don't," Petra says.

"She's yours." Ossory turns the memory slowly, letting the light catch it. "Or rather, she was yours, and then she died, and grief began to eat her out of you the way damp eats a book. You sold me pieces of her yourself, over the years. Small ones. The sound of her laugh, the year you were nineteen. The way she said your name when she was angry. You needed coin; the Court pays well for first-rate material." He smiles his grandfather smile. "But this — the whole of her. The kitchen. The singing. Everything I bought and everything you kept. This is the largest single holding in your account."

"What do you want for it?" Petra hears themselves ask, and despises the question even as it leaves their mouth, because they already know the answer, because the answer is the only currency this place accepts.

"The first lock." The coal-light in Ossory's smallest finger pulses. "Give me back the memory of Vhalen's bells — the harvest bells, the ones the child showed you — and I will give your mother back to you entire. Not resurrected. Better than resurrected. *Permanent.* Kept in glass, kept perfect, hers forever, and yours whenever you choose to sit inside her kitchen and hear her sing."

The kitchen hums. The woman sings. Somewhere in the walls of this impossible house, four hundred years of stolen afternoons hold their breath.

And Petra — careful, neutral, never-once-broken Petra — feels the temptation go through them like cold water through cloth, total and instant, soaking every fiber.

"No," they say.

The word costs more than anything they have ever spent.

* * *

The bargain does not end there. That is the trap of the wolf's house: no is only the opening move.

Ossory walks them through his halls for what might be hours or might be years — time moves like syrup here, pooling in the low places — and at every turning he offers something. Not crudely. Never crudely. He is a master appraiser, and he appraises Petra the way they once appraised a widow's first kiss: with attention, with respect, with a professional's understanding of exactly what a thing is worth to the one who holds it.

He shows them their name. Not the name on the Court's rolls — their true name, the one their mother used, the one they traded away at twenty-two to cover a bad quarter's accounts, so long ago they had almost stopped missing it. Here it hangs in a case like a moth specimen, still faintly fluttering. *Yours,* he says. *Take it back. All it costs is the second lock.*

He shows them their first theft — the clerk Maren's memory of her daughter's first steps, stolen in chapter and verse to buy passage for the child, the theft that taught Petra what they were capable of. Here it is preserved in amber, pristine, and Ossory turns it lovingly. *This one isn't even yours to want,* he says, almost teasing. *And yet look how it shines in you. Guilt is the best preservative. Give me the third lock, and I will release it back to her. She wakes whole. Your sin unmade.*

He shows them their loneliness. He shows it to them as a room, a small grey room they have carried since childhood, and he says, *I can fill this room, clerk. I have filled mine. Four hundred years, and I have never once been lonely, because I am everyone. That is what the Court offers, in the end. That is what the masks are. You could be everyone too.*

And Petra walks, and refuses, and refuses, and each refusal takes something out of them that they cannot name and cannot get back, because refusing inside a mind is itself a transaction, and the wolf keeps the change.

* * *

It is the child who saves them, though the child is miles away and sleeping.

Petra knows this because somewhere past the hundredth offered corridor, they find themselves standing in a part of the house they were not led to — a wing that is older than the rest, where the roads underfoot are not Vhalen's days but something else. Here the memories are not displayed. They are *caged*. And one of the cages holds a small mud-soaked figure, seen from the outside, from above, from a master's height: the child as Ossory first saw them, at his service door, and around that image a lattice of intent so old and smooth with handling that it gleams.

Petra touches the lattice and feels what Ossory feels when he thinks of the child.

Hunger. But underneath the hunger, worn thin as watered silk, something else. Something he has been standing on for four centuries the way he stands on market days:

*Awe.*

Because the child is the last loose thread of the only country he has ever truly loved, and even a wolf knows the difference between prey and the last wild thing.

And in that instant of touching his awe, Petra understands the shape of the bargain he cannot name, and they understand, too, why he brought them here instead of simply crushing them — why a master with all the power in the world opens his own mind to a thief.

He wants to be talked out of it.

* * *

"You're tired," Petra says.

They are back in the great hall, or a hall very like it; the house rearranges itself when Ossory's attention wanders. The grandfather face goes very still.

"Careful, clerk."

"Four hundred years." Petra walks the length of the hall, boots silent on someone's remembered wedding day. "Four hundred years wearing other people's afternoons under your feet. Do you know what I see when I look at your stars? I'm a ledger-keeper. I count. And I count a man who has been *full* for four centuries and has not been fed once." They turn. "You don't want the locks back. If you wanted them back you'd have taken them from me in the waking world; you're stronger there. You want someone to take them *away*."

The tea-colored eyes narrow. For a moment the kindly face flickers, and beneath it there is something vast and tired and hungry as winter, and Petra understands they are balanced on the edge of being unmade.

Then Ossory laughs. It is a terrible, honest sound, like a building settling.

"You audited me," he says wonderingly. "In my own house. No one has audited me in three hundred years." He sinks into his chair of wedding days. "Very well. Very well. But a bargain must still be struck, clerk, because that is the law of this place and I did not write it — the mind trades only in what the trader owns. You want my three coals. I cannot give them freely; nothing here moves freely. So: pay me."

"With what?"

"With what you carry that I do not." His voice drops, and the hunger in it is suddenly naked, ancient, the hunger of a thing that has eaten everything except the one meal that matters. "Your memories are small, yes. Cheap stock. A mother, a name, a theft. But they are *yours*, earned, unworn — and I have lived so long on other people's living that I have forgotten what it tastes like to own a single day honestly." He spreads his hands. "Three coals. Three payments. Your mother's kitchen for the first. Your name for the second. Your first theft — and the guilt that preserves it — for the third. Fair rates. Ledger rates."

"And then I walk out of here with nothing."

"Then you walk out of here as the person who remains," Ossory says softly, "when everything remembered is spent. Which is either no one at all—" the coals pulse in his fingertips, red as the door's green opposite, "—or the only person there ever really was. I have wondered which, for four hundred years. Consider it my final curiosity. Pay me, and set me down, and open your door, and perhaps we will both learn the answer together."

* * *

Petra stands in the hall of stars and takes inventory, the way they have done every night of their working life. Ledger open. Accounts balanced.

A mother at a sink, singing.

A name spoken soft, *little ledger*, in a kitchen that smells of flour.

A theft, and the guilt that has kept it warm all these years, the guilt that taught them they were capable of harm and therefore capable of choice.

Three things. Is that a self? They think of the widow from their first night in this story, the one who traded her first kiss and walked away lighter, smiling, saying *it wasn't doing me any good anyway.* They had pitied her then. They understand her now. Some things are not diminished by being given. Some things are only finished.

And some things remain. The rule they never broke — broken now, but chosen. The hand that counts. Whatever is left when the counting stops.

"First payment," Petra says, and reaches into their own chest, and takes out the kitchen.

It comes away warm. The woman sings on inside it, off-key, unbothered, forever at her counter in the moment before she turns around. Petra carries it to Ossory and sets it in his palms like bread.

The first coal gutters and goes dark, and the bell-memory of Vhalen slides free of him, ringing faintly, and hangs in the air between them like a held note.

"Second payment." The name. They speak it aloud one last time — it sounds like home, it sounds like a door closing — and give it away, and feel the strange lightness of becoming unnamed, a person reduced to function and choice, *clerk, thief, hand.* The second coal dies. The harvest memory rises, gold and heavy with grain-smell.

"Third payment." This one hurts most, because it is the one they are proudest and most ashamed of, the theft that made them who they are, and they give it up along with its guilt and feel something tear loose in them that they suspect was load-bearing.

The third coal goes out.

And Ossory — hollowed, emptied of his crowns, holding a stranger's kitchen against his chest like a man clutching warmth in a flood — begins, very quietly, to weep.

"It's so *small*," he whispers, turning the memory over. "One afternoon. One room. One woman. It fits in two hands." He looks up, and his tea-colored eyes are clear for the first time in centuries, clear and lost. "How did I ever mistake the whole sky for enough?"

"I don't know," Petra says honestly. "But the bells are ringing. Can you hear them?"

He can. Outside the house, across the endless amber roads, Vhalen's memories are gathering — the sunrise and the bells and the harvest, circling, calling to each other across the dark like birds finding a flock — and the mindscape is coming apart around them, walls dissolving into morning, the star-field raining gentle light.

"Go," Ossory says. He does not stand. He sits in his chair of weddings, cradling a kitchen, a king dethroned by arithmetic. "Wake, clerk. Take them to the door. And when you set the last lock—" his voice frays, "—put your hand over the child's. Someone should touch this country kindly, going in."

The world folds like paper.

* * *

Petra opens their eyes in the vault beneath the Court, forehead still pressed to the cracked mask, and finds three lights hovering in the air before them — sunrise-red, bell-silver, harvest-gold — waiting, patient as coins in a till.

Behind them, in the doorway of the vault, stands the child, awake now, luminous, holding out both small hands.

"Hurry," the child says, in plain words at last. "The door won't stay honest much longer."

And far above, through nine floors of stone, comes the sound of the Court tearing itself apart — shouting, shattering glass, and one voice raised in fury that Petra knows better now than their own lost name.

Ossory's brothers have noticed their brother's crown has gone dark.
