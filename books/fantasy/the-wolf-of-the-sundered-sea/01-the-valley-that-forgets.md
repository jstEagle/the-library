## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley of Hollowmere kept its secrets the way a miser keeps coins: close, counted, and never spent. Sable Galloway had lived inside its borders for twenty-six years, and in all that time she had never once seen the fog at the northern pass lift high enough to show what lay beyond. She had never needed to. The valley gave her everything a person could want—bread that rose, water that ran sweet, neighbors who waved from their gardens—and asked only one thing in return: that she bring nothing in.

No charms. No blessings. No magic of any kind.

Hollowmere was a hole in the world's fabric, a place where the old power simply could not follow. The founders had chosen it for exactly that reason, three hundred years ago, when the purges first began. They had built their cottages on ground where a witch's fire would not light and a seer's sight went blind, and they had raised their children to be ordinary on purpose, ordinary as a shield, ordinary as armor. Sable's mother had taught her the catechism before she could read: *We are plain folk. We keep no gifts. We owe the crown nothing but our taxes and our silence.*

Sable had obeyed. Mostly.

She was a cartographer by trade, and a good one—good enough that merchants from the low country paid her in silver for maps of roads she'd never walked, drawn from the testimony of travelers who passed through Hollowmere's single inn and described what they'd seen. She had a steady hand and an honest eye. Her coastlines were praised in towns she would never visit. And every night, after the lamp was trimmed and the shutters latched, she sat down at her desk and drew maps of a place that did not exist.

She called it the Sundered Sea, because that was the name that came with the dreams, whole and unbidden, the way a song comes back to you after twenty years. In the dreams she walked streets of pale stone beside a harbor full of ships with ribbed sails, and the water beyond the breakwater was green-gold and warm, and the people spoke a language she somehow understood without knowing how. She dreamed of a palace with towers like folded hands. She dreamed of a library where the shelves went up into darkness and the books breathed when you touched them. She dreamed of drowning—not frightening, never frightening, more like being poured into something vast and patient—and woke each morning with salt drying on her lips.

Then she would grind her ink, wet her brush, and draw it all down before it faded: the curve of the harbor, the names of districts she'd never heard spoken aloud, the little blue arrows marking currents in a sea that had been erased from every atlas in the kingdom. She kept these maps in a locked chest beneath her floorboards, beneath the loose stone under the hearth, because she was not a fool. A woman who drew places out of dreams was either mad or gifted, and both kinds burned.

So she hid them, and she hid the habit, and she told herself—as she told herself every morning, pressing her palm flat against the floorboard until the wood warmed—that the dreams meant nothing. That drawing them was harmless. That Hollowmere held, and would go on holding, and the fog at the pass would stay thick forever.

It was a good story. She had told it so long she almost believed it.

* * *

The proclamation came on a Tuesday, nailed to the post outside the tithe barn before anyone was awake to see who'd done the nailing.

Sable read it twice, standing in the cold with her breath steaming, while behind her the village went about its morning as if the paper were just another tax notice. It was written in the crown's formal hand, all flourishes and iron:

*By order of His Majesty, let it be known throughout the realm: those who bear the old gift, who traffic in charms or portents or the unquiet arts, are hereby declared enemies of the peace. Let them be sought, named, and brought to judgment. Let no household shelter them. Reward is offered for true report.*

Below the words, stamped in red wax, was the sigil of the Office of the Hunt: a closed eye above crossed keys.

Someone behind her made a small sound. Sable turned. It was Maren Ostler, the cooper's wife, her arms full of kindling, staring at the proclamation the way one stares at a snake in the pantry.

"They'll not find anything here," Maren said, too loudly, to no one in particular. "There's nothing here to find."

"No," Sable agreed. "Nothing at all."

They looked at each other for half a heartbeat longer than was comfortable, and then Maren hurried off toward her house and Sable walked home the long way, past the millpond, telling herself the same thing everyone in Hollowmere would be telling themselves for days: *we are plain folk, we keep no gifts.* The valley held. The valley had always held.

But that night she did not sleep well, and when the dream came anyway—the harbor, the ribbed sails, the patient green water—she woke with her heart hammering and lay in the dark listening to the wind come down off the fells, and for the first time in her life the wind sounded less like weather and more like footsteps.

* * *

Tam Ostler was taken eleven days later.

Not Tam the cooper—his wife Maren had nothing to fear—but Tam's brother, who lived alone at the far end of Mill Lane and kept bees and had once, years ago, been heard to say that he could tell when a storm was coming a full day before the clouds showed. Everyone had laughed at the time. Old Tam and his weather-sense. A joke, a quirk, the sort of thing valleys collect the way attics collect dust.

The hunters did not laugh.

They came at dawn, four of them, riding gray horses through the fog that was supposed to stop such men at the border. Sable watched from her window as they dismounted in front of the beekeeper's cottage, unhurried, professional, their boots crunching the frost. Their leader wore a long coat the color of wet slate and moved like a man walking through his own kitchen. He did not knock. He did not need to. The door opened before he reached it, which meant someone inside had already given him leave, or already given him Tam.

They brought him out barefoot. That detail lodged in Sable like a splinter: barefoot, in the frost, his hands bound with cord rather than iron, as if iron were too dignified for what he was now. He did not struggle. He looked small between the horses, a thin man in a nightshirt, fifty years of quiet living reduced to a bundle to be transported. As they led him past the well, he turned his head and looked directly at Sable's window—or so it seemed; the light was bad, the glass wavy—and his mouth moved around words she couldn't hear.

She pressed her hand flat against the cold pane and did not move until the riders and their prisoner had gone up the lane and into the fog, and the fog had closed behind them like water over a stone.

* * *

That evening the village gathered in the meetinghouse, because that was what villages did, and the rector read the proclamation aloud a second time in his best mourning voice, and then everyone talked about everything except the thing that mattered.

"The valley protects us," said Aldous Wren, the miller, in the tone of a man reciting scripture. "It has always protected us. Tam's trouble was Tam's own—he should have kept his mouth shut about storms thirty years ago."

"Of course," murmured his wife.

"Of course," said the rector. "Though it is curious." He smoothed the proclamation on the lectern. "Curious that they found him. Curious that the fog let them through."

A silence went around the room like a draft.

"The fog lets in what the fog lets in," said Aldous Wren, too quickly.

But Sable saw the rector's eyes move across the congregation, unhurried, counting, the way a shepherd counts sheep when he suspects the wolf has already eaten one. And she thought: *he knows the protection is failing. He knows, and he said it aloud in front of everyone, because he wants us to start watching each other.* Because that was how hunts worked. They didn't need many hunters. They needed neighbors.

She left early, pleading a headache, and walked home through streets where doors closed softly as she passed and curtains shifted in windows, and she understood that from tonight forward, every person in Hollowmere would be a small, frightened government, issuing verdicts in whispers.

At home she lit no lamp. She knelt at the hearth, worked the loose stone free, and lifted out the chest, and sat with it in her lap in the dark, feeling the weight of ten years of nights.

She should burn them. Every map, every dream, every impossible coastline. Feed the lot to the stove and be plain again, be safe, be nobody. The thought arrived with real force, and for a moment her hands actually shook with the wanting of it.

But underneath the wanting was the other thing, the older thing, the thing she had never once said aloud even to herself: *these are not my dreams.* She had known it for years the way you know a scar is there without looking at it. The hands in the dreams were older than hers. The grief in them was worn smooth with age. Somewhere, sometime, someone had walked those pale streets and loved that green-gold water and drowned in it gladly, and whatever was left of them had washed downstream through the generations and pooled in Sable Galloway, and if she burned the maps she would be burning the last living record of a country the crown had murdered twice—once in fact, and once in memory.

She put the chest back under the floor. She pressed the stone flat. Then she sat at her dark window until midnight, watching the fog bank at the head of the valley, and she noticed—for the first time, though perhaps it had been true for weeks—that the fog was thinner than it used to be. Thinner, and moving. Not lying against the fells like a wall anymore, but drifting, restless, the way fog drifts when something behind it is pushing.

In the morning there was a knock at her door.

She knew before she opened it. Some animal part of her had known since the day the proclamation went up, and now it stood in her chest with its ears flat. Through the wood came a voice she recognized from the well-side, level and unhurried, a voice that belonged to a man walking through his own kitchen:

"Mistress Galloway. Captain Vane, Office of the Hunt. A moment of your time."

Sable looked down at her own hands—at the fine ink stains in the creases, the cartographer's marks no scrubbing ever fully lifted—and thought, absurdly, of the sea in her dreams, green-gold and patient, waiting.

She opened the door.
