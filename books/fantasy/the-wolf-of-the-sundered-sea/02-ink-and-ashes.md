## 2. Ink and Ashes

Captain Aldric Vane was not what she had expected, though she could not have said what she had expected instead. Perhaps a man built like the proclamations—all iron and threat. But he was lean, grey at the temples, with a face that might have belonged to a schoolmaster or a surveyor, and eyes the flat brown of a winter river. He stood in her doorway with his hands loose at his sides, as though he had come to borrow a cup of sugar.

Two hunters flanked him on the step. They were what she had expected: broad, silent, their collars stitched with the crown's white antler.

"Mistress Galloway," Vane said again. His gaze went past her shoulder into the house, unhurried, cataloguing. "May we come in?"

It was not a question in any way that mattered. Sable stepped back, and they filled her small house the way cold fills a room through a broken window—gradually, then all at once.

She had prepared for this, or told herself she had. The night after the proclamation went up on the well-house door, she had sat at her worktable until the candle drowned in its own wax, deciding what could stay and what must go. The safe maps stayed: Hollowmere itself, the valley roads, the shepherd paths up to the high pasture, all of it dull and true and harmless. Those she left pinned where any honest cartographer's work would hang.

The others—the dreams, the impossible coastlines, the drowned country that rose green-gold behind her closed eyes—she had carried out to the fire pit three nights running, until only one remained.

"You keep a tidy house," Vane said. He did not touch anything. He walked slowly along the wall where her finished maps hung, reading them the way other men read letters, and something in the set of his shoulders made Sable think of a man listening for a sound under a floor.

"I'm a mapmaker," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "For the valley. Surveys, boundaries, deeds."

"So I understand." He stopped before her map of the mill road and studied it. "Fine hand. Very fine." He turned his head slightly, not quite looking at her. "You've worked for the crown office in Corvane?"

"Once or twice. Copies."

"Copies." He let the word sit a moment, the way a man sets down a stone to see if it rings hollow. Then he moved on.

* * *

They searched the way water searches—without hurry, without malice, finding every crack. One hunter took the loft; the other went through her chest of drawing instruments with hands gloved in soft leather, lifting each pen, each rule, each brass weight, holding it to the light as if ink itself might confess.

Sable stood by the hearth and kept her breathing even. Above the mantel hung the last of them.

She had meant to burn it with the others. She had meant to, three separate nights, and three separate nights she had stood at the fire pit with it rolled in oilcloth and found she could not open her fingers. It was the best thing she had ever drawn. That was the truth of it, ugly as any confession: when she unrolled that map, the Sundered Sea lay there whole—not guessed at, not dreamed, but *known*, every bay and headland drawn with the certainty of a woman remembering her own face. She had told herself she kept it because destroying it would feel like a lie, and a cartographer did not lie on paper. But standing now in her own kitchen with the hunt walking through it, she admitted the simpler truth. She had kept it because it was beautiful, and because some stubborn animal part of her refused to believe a thing so true could be so dangerous.

Vane looked up at it.

He stood beneath it for a long time without moving, and the house went very quiet around him, the way a forest goes quiet. Sable watched his face and saw nothing move in it at all, which frightened her more than anger would have.

"A strange subject," he said finally. "For a valley cartographer."

"It's an old piece. Commissioned years ago. I don't recall by whom." The lie came smoothly, and she hated how smoothly.

"No," Vane agreed mildly. "I don't imagine you do."

He reached up and took it down from its nail. He held it rolled, not opening it, and looked at her over it. "Where is your fire?"

"The pit. Out back."

"Burn it," he said.

The words dropped into her like stones into still water. She heard herself say, "It's worth money—"

"Burn it, Mistress Galloway. Here, where I can watch you do it. If it's what you say—a commission, a curiosity—then it's ash either way, and no loss but coin." His voice never rose. "And if it isn't what you say, then I'd rather see it burn than take it back to my superiors. Do you understand me?"

She understood him perfectly, and the understanding was worse than a shout would have been. This man was offering her a rope while telling her he knew exactly what it was tied to.

She took the map from him. Her hands did not shake; she noted that from somewhere far away, the way one notes the weather. She carried it out the back door into the grey morning, and the two hunters followed, and Vane followed them, and the whole procession had the terrible ordinariness of a funeral.

The fire pit still held the bones of her other burnings—curls of vellum gone grey, a scrap of coastline that had survived as a black-edged crescent. She knelt, fed in kindling, struck flint until the tinder caught. The morning was damp and the fire fought her, and some small mad part of her hoped it would win.

Then it caught, and she fed it the map.

She fed it corner first, and the flame ran up the western shore like a lit fuse. She watched the Sea of Glass go, the harbor city whose name lived in her mouth but nowhere in the world, the long green-gold coast that had risen behind her eyelids every night since childhood. The heat pushed against her cheeks. Ink curled and bloomed dark, then brightened, then was nothing.

Vane stood beside her with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the fire with the attention of a man committing something to memory.

"You grieve it," he said quietly.

"It was good work."

"Yes," he said. "It is."

* * *

Inside again, she thought it was finished. The hunters were closing their cases; the loft ladder creaked under boots going up a final time. Vane stood at her worktable, turning her brass scale-weight over in his fingers, and Sable allowed herself the first breath she had taken since the knock.

Then he said, without looking up: "Show me your arms, Mistress Galloway."

The breath stopped halfway.

"The Office of the Hunt requires inspection of all persons named in the district rolls. Arms, throat, and hairline. It takes a moment." Now he looked up, and his winter-river eyes were patient and bottomless. "Or you can refuse, and refusal will be noted, and my men will assist you. Their assistance is less gentle than mine."

Her blood had gone thin and ringing. She thought of Tam Merrilees, who mended shoes and sang off-key, taken from his forge two days past with his wife screaming in the lane—for what, for a trick of light in his hands, for sparks that bent wrong. She thought of the fire pit smoking behind the house.

She rolled up her sleeves.

Left first, ordinary skin, freckled at the forearm where the sun reached it through the workroom window. Then right, slowly, hating the slowness, hating that she could not make her hands hurry.

The birthmark began below the inside of her elbow.

Everyone in Hollowmere knew she had it; it was the sort of thing a valley knows about its people. A birthmark shaped like nothing much, a smudge, a spill of color too faint to name. Children had asked. She had always said she didn't know what it looked like, and this was nearly true, because it changed. Not hour to hour—no one could have seen that—but across years, across seasons, across sleeps. Her mother had called it her wandering stain and laughed about it. Her mother had also, Sable remembered suddenly and painfully, stopped asking about it after Sable turned twelve, and had gone quiet in a new way, and had died before Sable thought to ask why.

In the firelight it was showing lines.

Faint ones, pale blue-green beneath the skin like channels seen through shallow water—a curve that could be a coastline, a branching that could be a river delta, small precise marks that could be nothing at all or could be the same script she dreamed in, the writing that appeared on the maps she drew half-asleep and woke unable to read.

Vane went very still.

She felt his stillness the way one feels a held breath in a dark room. He crossed to her—three steps, no more—and took her wrist, not hard, turning her arm toward the window's grey light. His fingers were cold. He did not speak for so long that she heard the fire tick in the next room and the wind working at the eaves.

When he spoke, it was barely aloud, and it was not to her.

"That's Carreth," he said. "The harbor bend. That's—" He stopped. His thumb moved over the mark as if checking whether it was wet ink, and Sable flinched, and he let go at once, stepping back as though the touch had scalded *him*. "Where did you get this?"

"It's a birthmark. I was born with it."

"People are not born with maps of places that don't exist."

The silence stretched between them, and in it Sable made the mistake—or perhaps it was the only honest act of her life—of not lying. She said nothing at all, and her silence said everything, and Vane read it the way he had read her maps.

He looked older suddenly. Tired in a way that had nothing to do with the morning. Behind him, one of his hunters came to the foot of the stairs and waited, sensing something, saying nothing.

"My men will report what they saw here today," Vane said at last, and his voice had rebuilt itself, brick by brick, into the schoolmaster's calm. "A cartographer burning old commissions. Nothing else. District rolls will show Mistress Galloway inspected and passed." He picked his gloves up from the table and drew them on, finger by finger, not looking at her. "You should know what that costs me to say. And you should know that I will be back."

"Why?" The word tore out of her rougher than she intended. "Why pass me? Why hesitate? What is it you think you recognize?"

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"When I was a boy," he said, "there was a woman in our village who drew that same coastline on the frost of windows. They took her in the winter. I was made to watch." The latch clicked. "I have spent twenty years learning not to remember her face, Mistress Galloway. And this morning you opened your door wearing her map."

Then he was gone, down her path, into the lane, his hunters falling in behind him, and Sable stood alone in the wreckage-smell of burnt ink with her sleeve still rolled up and the coastline moving slowly, patiently, beneath her skin like something swimming home.

That night she did not sleep. She sat with her back against the cold hearthstone and watched the mark by candlelight, and near dawn—exhausted, terrified, unable to stop herself—she dipped a pen and began, on fresh vellum, to copy it line for line.

She was three strokes in when she understood what she was drawing, and her hand went still over the paper.

It was not a map of the coast.

It was a map *out* of it.
