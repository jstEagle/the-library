## 3. The Bargain of the Hunter

Aldric Vane had learned long ago that the hardest part of the work was not the killing. It was the waiting after.

He sat in the taproom of the Drowned Bell with his back to the wall and his hands wrapped around a cup of small beer he had no intention of drinking, watching Sable Galloway across the room the way he'd once watched wolves at the tree line. She had not touched her supper. She sat very straight, very still, in the manner of people who have decided that if the end is coming they will meet it on their feet. Her sleeves were pulled down over her wrists. Over the mark.

The mark. He had seen it only for a heartbeat—when she'd dropped the candle, when the firelight had swung wild across her forearm—and in that heartbeat something old and drowned in him had turned over like a body rising from lake water.

He knew that symbol. He had drawn it himself, as a boy, in the ash of a hearth that no longer existed, in a country that no longer existed, before the crown taught him what happened to children who drew such things.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm assessing."

"That's a kinder word for it." She finally lifted her gaze, and her eyes were gray as slate under rain. "Are you going to turn me in properly this time? Or do we sit here all night pretending you didn't see?"

* * *

They had taken her neighbor two days ago. Old Marta, who kept bees and whose gift was so small it amounted to little more than knowing when it would rain—the kind of magic that hid inside a person like a splinter, too shallow to matter, too deep to dig out without bleeding them. The hunters had come for her anyway, because the crown's new edict did not distinguish between splinters and swords. Vane had stood in the square and watched them bind her wrists with red cord, and Marta had looked at him—not at the others, at *him*—with an expression he would carry into whatever grave was waiting for him.

She hadn't cursed him. That was the worst of it. She had looked almost sorry for him.

Now he set down his cup and crossed the taproom, and the innkeep found somewhere else to be, which was sensible of him. Vane lowered himself into the chair opposite Sable Galloway and said, quietly, "Show me your arm."

"No."

"It's not a request I'm making as a hunter."

"Then what are you making it as?"

He had prepared answers for this. Lies, mostly—he was good at lies; they had purchased his life twice over—but sitting there under her flat gray stare, he found the prepared words crumbled like wet paper. What came out instead was the truth, or close enough to it to burn on the way out.

"As a man who used to draw that same symbol in the ashes of a house that burned down before you were born."

Something shifted in her face. Not fear. Recognition's cousin—the look of someone hearing their own dream spoken aloud by a stranger.

"The Sundered Sea," she said.

"You know the name."

"I've dreamed it since I was a child." Her hand moved unconsciously toward her wrist and stopped. "Coastlines. A city on cliffs where the gulls never land. Rivers that ran backward at the equinox. I thought—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "I thought everyone dreamed places. I thought it was just how minds wandered."

"And then you started drawing them."

"How did you—"

"Because I did too." He kept his voice low, level, a hunter's voice, trained to carry no further than the ear it was aimed at. "Until I was nine years old. Then men in gray coats came to my village and asked my mother which of her children saw things that weren't there. And my mother—who loved us, understand, who loved us the way mothers do, desperately and stupidly—told them it was me. Because I was the oldest, and because she thought—" His throat closed briefly. He drank, though the beer tasted of nothing. "She thought one child could be saved by giving them the other. And she was right. That's the joke of it. She was exactly right."

Sable said nothing. The fire popped in the grate. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked, and both of them flinched in the same instant, a small synchronized animal motion, and that too was its own kind of confession.

"What happened?" she asked finally.

"I was given a choice, at eleven years old. They don't kill children with the gift—they're not monsters, whatever else they are. They put you in a white house by the sea and they teach you until the gift drowns. Most of us drown with it. But some of us—" He turned his own wrist over. There was nothing there now, no mark, only a pale scar like a fish hook gone sideways, where they had cut the sigil out of him at fourteen, awake, because the surgeons said pain helped the unlearning. "Some of us learn to hunt. It's the trade they offer. Your freedom for your service. Your survival for theirs. You walk into houses like yours was, Galloway, and you drag out people like your neighbor, and every year the debt gets smaller and the ledger never reaches zero."

"You bought your life," she said. No accusation in it, which was somehow worse than accusation. Just the flat cartographer's precision, naming a feature of the terrain. *Here be the thing you did.*

"I bought my life," he agreed. "With other lives. Thirty-one of them that I counted, before I stopped counting. And the crown thinks I'm in Hollowmere doing crown work. Which brings me," he said, "to why we're having this conversation instead of why you're already in a wagon."

* * *

He laid it out for her the way he'd laid out campaigns, because that was the only language left to him. The valley was failing. He'd known it since the first night, when his compass needle had trembled at the tree line like a frightened bird—the enchantment that kept magic out of Hollowmere was thinning, fraying, and when it went, the edict would come through it like water through a cracked dam. Every gifted soul in the valley, and there were more than the census admitted, would be found. He could slow the search. He could not stop it.

"There's a place," he said. "East of the White Mile, past the salt flats, where the maps go wrong. Where every map goes wrong—I've carried six different surveys through that country and each one lied differently. The old records call it nothing. The older ones call it the door out of the Sundered Sea."

Her breath caught. He watched her hand press flat against the table, steadying itself against something invisible.

"The country fell," she said slowly. "Everyone knows it fell. Famine, war, the sea taking it—no one agrees how, but it fell."

"Everyone knows a story. The stories disagree because the truth was erased. Professionally erased, Galloway. By people like me. But erasure leaves seams, and seams can be followed." He leaned forward. "I want you to take me there."

"Why you? Why does a hunter need a door out of a dead country?"

Because his dreams had changed, he did not say. Because for three years the white house by the sea had been replaced in his sleep by a frozen coastline and a city rebuilding itself out of fog, and he woke with salt on his lips and a word in his mouth—*Carreth*—that belonged to no living tongue. Because the crown's new masters had begun asking questions about him that had nothing to do with witch-hunting, and he had seen the ledgers, and knew that hunters who outlived their usefulness were entered in them like anyone else.

"Because I'm dying either way," he said instead. "Slowly here, quickly there—it hardly matters. But you—" He tapped the table between them. "You have a map on your skin that moves when you sleep. I've served the Office of Erasure for nineteen years and I have never seen anything like it. Whatever you are, Galloway, you're the key to something. And keys get used. Better it's me than them."

"And what do I get?"

"Your death." He let the word sit. "I'll write it. Fever, taken quick, poor girl, burned with the others—a clean death, well witnessed. The crown loves paperwork; it's how they forgive themselves. Once you're dead on record, no hunter comes for you. You walk out of history the way the Sundered Sea walked out of it, and no one follows, because no one hunts ghosts."

"And in exchange you want me to guide you through a winter waste to a door that may not exist, using a map I can't read while awake, pursued by men with your training and none of your regrets." She almost smiled. It was not a warm expression. "You're asking me to trust the man who held the torch at Marta's arrest."

"I'm asking you to trust the man who loosened the knots on her cords before the wagon left." He held her stare. "It bought her perhaps a mile. Perhaps nothing. But I did it, and you'll never know if I've done worse, and neither will I. That's the shape of what I am. Take it or leave it."

For a long moment she was silent, and he watched her do what cartographers do: measure, weigh, chart the coasts of a decision. When she spoke, her voice had gone quiet and hard as frost forming.

"My grandmother's name was Isolde. She came to this valley before I was born and never spoke of where from. She died drawing—ink still on her fingers. And lately, when I sleep, I dream in *her* handwriting." She pushed back her sleeve.

In the tavern's dim light the birthmark showed dark against her forearm: interlocking lines, a coastline folded into a sigil, shifting faintly even now, like something swimming beneath ice.

"This isn't mine alone," she said. "Whatever leads to that door, it leads through her. And I want to know why she ran. So yes, hunter. I'll guide you."

Vane exhaled a breath he felt he'd been holding for nineteen years.

"But we leave tonight," she added, standing, pulling her sleeve down, "before you find a reason to be the man they made you. And Vane—if you betray me on that road, I won't need magic to end you. I'll just leave you a map home. And you'll follow it. We both know you will."

Outside the Drowned Bell, the snow had begun to fall—early, heavy, wrong for the season—and far off beyond the eastern hills, where no road should run, the air above the trees shimmered faintly silver, as though the horizon had been drawn there recently, and by an unsteady hand.
