## 4. Crossing the White Mile

We left Hollowmere in the hour the valley pretended not to see—the deep blue dark between the last lamp going out and the first cock crowing, when even the dogs sleep as though ashamed of it.

I had packed nothing but what a mapmaker needs: ink frozen in its horn, three quills wrapped in oilcloth, a knife for sharpening them and another kind of knife entirely, bread gone hard as slate, and my grandmother's atlas sewn into the lining of my coat, where it lay against my ribs like a second heartbeat I had spent my whole life pretending not to feel. Vane carried his own weight in iron—manacles, a brand still smelling faintly of other people's skin, papers stamped with the crown's seal that would open gates or close graves depending on how he held them. He did not apologize for any of it. That was the thing about Aldric Vane: he wore his sins the way other men wore scars, without flinching, without explaining, and somehow that was worse than if he'd wept.

The road out of Hollowmere ran along the river, under alders black as burnt spoons. Frost furred every branch. My breath came out in small white ghosts and I kept thinking: *these are the last ghosts this valley will ever get from me.*

"You're crying," Vane said. He didn't look at me when he said it. He never looked at me when he said things like that; he watched the road instead, the way a man watches a wound on someone else's body.

"The cold does it," I said.

"It doesn't," he said, and let it go.

Behind us, Hollowmere lay in its cup of hills like water in a cupped hand. All my life I had believed the valley loved us—that was the story mothers told, the story I had told myself while I inked coastlines that could not exist: *the valley keeps us, the valley hides us, magic cannot cross our stones.* I had built my whole quiet life inside that belief the way a snail builds inside its shell, and I had never once asked what a shell is for. A shell is for the thing that hunts you. And a shell only holds until something cracks it.

We heard the crack at the ford.

It was not a sound so much as an absence—a silence with edges, like glass breaking inward. The river stopped mid-rush. I mean that literally: the water stood up for one impossible moment, a gray ribbon hanging in the air, and then it fell all at once with a sound like applause, and beyond it the hills shuddered. Every bird in the alders went up together, a black rag torn loose against the stars.

Vane's hand closed on my arm. "What was that?"

"That was home," I said. "That was the valley forgetting how to protect us."

Because I understood it then, standing in the shallows with my boots filling with ice-water and my heart hammering somewhere up around my collarbone. The enchantment hadn't been broken by the witch-hunters. It had been *worn out*—a candle burned to its socket—and whatever had fed it for two hundred years, some bargain made by frightened founders whose names no one remembered, had finally come due. Hollowmere was naked now. By morning there would be hunters in it, going door to door, and Mrs. Ellery's daughter would not be the last child they dragged into the square.

"Run," I said, and for once Aldric Vane did not argue with me.

* * *

Beyond the ford the land changed the way weather changes—not gradually but all at once, one step mild and the next step winter. We climbed out of the river valley onto a high moor the maps called the White Mile, because in hard winters the snow never left it, and tonight the snow was falling upward, which should have been the first warning.

I felt my gift come back before I understood what was happening to me.

It came back like blood returning to a numb limb—all pins and needles and then agony. Twenty-six years of holding still, of thinking flat thoughts, of pressing my dreams down into paper where they couldn't hurt anyone, and now every dream I'd ever suppressed stood up inside me at once and stretched. I went down on my knees in the snow. The world rang like a struck bell. I could taste colors. I could hear the moor breathing, a slow tidal sound beneath the wind, as though the ground itself were asleep and dreaming, and I knew—I knew with sick certainty—that it was dreaming *me*.

"Sable." Vane crouched over me, and snow gathered on his shoulders like ash. "What's wrong? What do you need?"

"Nothing," I gasped. "Everything. It's back. All of it." I pressed my palms against my eyes. "You don't understand what it's like, having a door in you that's been locked your whole life, and then the lock just—"

"Gives way."

He said it too quietly. When I looked up, he wasn't watching the moor. He was watching me with an expression I had never seen on him, something old and hollowed-out, and I realized he did understand, that he had been a locked door once himself and had chosen, in the end, to become the lock instead.

"Get up," he said roughly. "Whatever comes, we move through it. That's all. We move."

But the terrain had already begun to lie.

I saw it first as a wrongness in the path. The road we followed bent—there was no other word for it. One moment we were walking north by the stars, the next the road curved gently, courteously, eastward, as though it had remembered an appointment. And I knew the direction it bent toward, because I had dreamed that direction ten thousand times: a harbor town with green copper roofs and gulls the color of old silver, a lighthouse burning with a flame that gave off cold instead of heat. I had never seen it. I had drawn it anyway, over and over, in margins and ledgers, until my hands knew it better than my own face.

"It's following me," I whispered. "The road. It's bending toward what I remember."

"You've never been here."

"I know."

"Then whose memory—"

"*Mine,*" I said, and the word came out strange, doubled, as though someone were saying it with me, slightly behind my voice, slightly ahead of it. "Or mine and someone else's. Vane, I have to tell you something I should have told you in the valley. I don't think these are dreams. I think they're inheritances."

The snow fell upward. The road curved. Somewhere far off across the moor, I heard bells—harbor bells, tide bells, bells from a country that had been dead for two hundred years, ringing as though they'd only just been waiting for someone to walk close enough to listen.

* * *

By the time the sky went gray, I had stopped trusting my own feet.

Every landmark was almost-right. There was a stone bridge over a frozen stream, and I knew before we reached it that its third arch had fallen, because in my dreams the third arch had always fallen—and it had, rubble tumbled in the ice exactly as I'd pictured it, except the rubble was fresh, the mortar pale and unweathered, as if the bridge had broken *this morning*, on schedule, for my benefit. There was a stand of birches I recognized from a margin-sketch I'd made at fourteen, bored during a sermon. They stood in precisely that pattern, seven trees, one leaning—but the bark was smooth as new paper and when I touched it, warm.

The land was being *remembered into shape*. Around us, mile by mile, the world was rebuilding itself out of the only archive that survived the Sundered Sea's erasure: me.

"Do you feel that?" Vane asked. He had stopped walking. His breath hung around him in rags.

"Yes."

"No." He turned slowly, scanning the horizon. "Not the land. The *absence* of it. Everything here is almost real. Like a room furnished from description. Like—" He stopped, jaw working. "Like a place rebuilt from testimony. From accounts. From *maps*." His eyes found mine, and there was fear in them, and worse than fear: recognition. "This is what I helped them do, Sable. This is what's left. A country made of secondhand memory, waiting for someone to finish the sentence."

"And if I finish it?" I asked. "What happens then?"

"I don't know." For once, the hunter admitted ignorance without flinching. "But I know what happens if the crown reaches that answer before you do. They won't restore anything. They'll burn what remains and salt the ash, and they'll use your gift to find every last thread of it."

Ahead of us, the moor ended. Not gradually—no polite slope down into the lowlands. It simply stopped, a clean edge like a page torn out, and beyond it lay a landscape that did not belong to any living memory: terraced fields under snow no one had planted, orchards of black trees heavy with frost-fruit, a road of white paving stones running straight as a ruled line toward a horizon where something enormous glimmered, sea-colored, sea-shaped, impossible.

The drowned coastline. Surfacing. Because I was near enough now, and dreaming hard enough, to pull it up.

"Gods," Vane breathed. He crossed himself the way men do who don't believe and are afraid anyway. "That's Carreth. That's the capital. That can't be Carreth, it's been gone since before my grandfather—"

"It isn't Carreth yet," I said. My voice sounded far away to me, layered, borrowed. "It's the shape Carreth leaves in the world. And it's pulling."

The pull was in my chest, in the ink-stained bones of my hands, in the birthmark at my wrist which had begun, very softly, to glow beneath my glove—a thin gold line tracing itself like a signature, like a map remembering its author. I pulled the glove off and looked at it and did not flinch, though every instinct of my hidden life screamed at me to hide it, cover it, be small, be safe, be nothing.

No more hiding. The valley was behind us and it was already ash in everything but fact. Whatever I was, I had carried it out into the open world, and the open world had begun rearranging itself to meet it.

"We rest at dawn," Vane said at last, and his voice was steady, which cost him something; I could hear what it cost. "Then we walk toward that light. And Sable—" he hesitated, and for the first time since the night he'd spared me, Aldric Vane looked unsure of his own next words. "When we reach the city, there are people in it. Or things shaped like people. Things that died badly when the country fell, and have waited a long time for someone to blame." He met my eyes. "They'll know my face."

"Will they?" I asked quietly. "Whose face will they see?"

He didn't answer. He turned up his collar and started down the white road, and I followed, my mark glowing at my wrist like a small stubborn star, drawing the drowned kingdom up around us out of snow and grief—one remembered street at a time—while somewhere behind us, on the moor, the crown's riders found our tracks in the snow and turned, unhurried, toward the light.
