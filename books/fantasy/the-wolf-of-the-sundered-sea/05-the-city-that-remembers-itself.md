## 5. The City That Remembers Itself

We smelled Carreth before we saw it.

That was the first wrongness. A city three hundred years drowned should smell of nothing but stone and salt-rot, but the wind coming off the plain carried woodsmoke, and beneath it something sweeter—baking bread, tallow candles, the green bite of a fish market at dawn. Smells that needed people. Smells that needed *living*.

Sable reined in the borrowed horse and pressed her gloved hand flat against her sternum, where her heart had been hammering since the pass.

"Do you smell it too?" she asked.

Vane rode half a length ahead, the way he always did, as if distance from her were a courtesy he could still offer. He didn't turn. "I smell a city."

"I smell a *kitchen*."

He was quiet for a moment. Snow came down around them in fat, unhurried flakes, and each one landed without melting, as though even the weather here had forgotten what warmth was supposed to do. Then he said, in the flat voice he used for confessions he hadn't made yet: "Then it's already begun."

* * *

They crested the last ridge at dusk, and Sable forgot how to breathe.

Carreth lay below them in the bowl of a frozen valley—or the idea of Carreth did, because it was two cities at once, laid over each other like double-exposed ink. There were the ruins: black ribs of walls, a harbor basin filled with grey ice where the sea should have been, towers snapped off at the waist like broken fingers. And there, moving through the ruin like light through water, was the other city—the remembered one. Gabled roofs that cast no shadow. Windows glowing with candlelight that illuminated nothing. A street of cobbles running straight through the middle of a collapsed wall, both equally real, neither quite touching.

And people. Figures in the streets. Not ghosts, not exactly—they had weight, they left prints in the snow that vanished only slowly—but they moved with the terrible smoothness of things being *remembered* rather than lived. A woman shook out a rug on a balcony that was half rubble. Two boys chased a dog down a lane that ended in nothing, turned, and ran back, laughing, and their laughter reached Sable on the ridge clear as bells.

"They're not real," Vane said beside her. His voice had gone strange.

"No," Sable said. "But they're *true*. There's a difference." She had spent her whole life drawing places that didn't exist; she knew the difference intimately. A lie could be accurate. She had never drawn anything this accurate by accident.

The city was building itself out of her dreams. Out of the maps she'd burned in Hollowmere, maybe, or out of whatever well those maps had come from—and now that she stood inside its reach, she could feel the pull of it, gentle and enormous, like a tide deciding to take her. The ache behind her eyes that had begun at the White Mile deepened into something almost sweet.

"It's using you," Vane said.

"It's *remembering through me*." She kicked her horse forward before he could argue. "There's a difference in that, too."

* * *

They came down into Carreth as the false dusk settled, and the city received them the way a host receives guests who are late to a funeral.

The streets knew Sable. That was the horror of it. She turned left at a fountain she had never seen awake and knew the fountain would be there; she cut through an alley between a chandler's shop and a house with blue shutters, and the chandler's shop smelled of honey and wick-trimmings, and the blue shutters were peeling in exactly the way she'd have painted them. Her feet found cobbles her boots had never touched. Somewhere under her skin, her grandmother's memories were laying roads for her, and Sable walked them like a woman sleepwalking through her own house.

Vane, she noticed, was having a different experience.

The citizens saw him. That was the trouble. They streamed past Sable with barely a glance—another face, another daughter of Carreth come home—but when their eyes found Vane, they stopped. They stared. And their faces changed.

An old man selling chestnuts from a brazier that gave off no heat looked up at Vane and said, warmly, terribly, "*Aldric.* You're tall. Your mother would be proud." And then his smile faltered, flickered, like a candle in a draft, and he said in a different voice entirely, older and colder: "You're tall. You were small, the day you left. You were small and you cried."

Vane walked faster.

A woman hanging washing from a line strung between a standing wall and a fallen one called down to him: "Lieutenant! Lieutenant Vane! Is it true about the eastern villages? Is it true the crown is—" and then the sentence unraveled mid-air, because the woman's memory did not contain how that sentence ended, and neither, Sable realized with a slow cold turning in her gut, did the woman know she'd been asking a man who would have known the answer better than anyone alive.

"Vane," Sable said quietly, catching his sleeve at the mouth of the great square. "What did you do?"

"Later."

"There is no later. Look at them. They *know* you. This city remembers you, Aldric, and it remembers you *wrong*, and I want to know why."

For a moment she thought he would pull away. His jaw worked under the stubble; his eyes went to the square, where a fountain played water that fell upward, gently, into the sky. Ghosts—citizens—whatever they were—had begun to gather at the square's edges, not menacingly, just *looking*, the way villagers look at a wolf that has come down among the sheep wearing a collar.

"The palace archive," he said finally. "Where your answers are. Where mine are too, if there's any mercy in this place. Walk."

* * *

The palace of Carreth rose over the frozen harbor like a drowning man's last gesture, half ruin and half memory, its great dome patched with candlelit ghost-stone. Inside, the halls were lit by sconces that burned without fuel, and the air tasted of dust and myrrh and, faintly, of the sea—which had not touched this coast in three centuries.

It was in the long gallery above the throne room that the ghosts finally cornered him.

They came out of the walls the way frost comes, all at once and everywhere. A dozen of them, then more, citizens in the dress of the old kingdom, and they did not reach for weapons because they had none, and did not need any. They simply stood in front of Vane, blocking the gallery, and looked at him with the patient grief of people who have waited a very long time to be understood.

"You wore our colors," said a soldier, still in the rotted finery of the Sundered Sea guard, his breastplate translucent. "You sat at our tables. You learned our names so you could give them up."

"I was a child," Vane said. His voice cracked on the second word.

"You were nineteen," said an old woman, and her voice was kind, which was worse. "Old enough to choose. You chose warm rooms and a full belly and a crown's pardon, and you drew them the coastline, Aldric. You drew them every harbor, every landing, every place a ship could slip away in the night. And when they came—not to fight us, we could have fought them, we'd have died fighting, and dying would have been *ours*—when they came with ink instead of soldiers, you handed them the pen."

Sable stood very still. The cold in the gallery was not winter-cold. It was the cold of a truth arriving.

"Vane," she said. "Tell me they're lying."

He didn't look at her. He looked at the ghosts, and his face—the face she had crossed a cursed winter beside, the face that had hesitated over her in a Hollowmere kitchen with mercy in it—was gray and old.

"The kingdom wasn't conquered," he said hoarsely. "Not at the end. It was *erased*. There was a bargain—I don't know the shape of it yet, that's in the archive, that's what we came for—but the crown didn't take the Sundered Sea. It *unwrote* it. And someone had to show them where to begin the unwriting. Someone who knew the harbors." He swallowed. "Someone who owed them."

"You bought your life with a country," Sable said. The words came out of her steady and cold and she barely recognized them as her own.

"I bought my life with a *map*." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "Do you understand the joke of it, cartographer? You draw places back into the world. I drew one out. We're the same trade. We've been walking side by side across a winter and we're the same trade."

The ghosts began to move closer, and the candlelight bent toward them like iron to a lodestone, and somewhere far below, in the frozen harbor, the ice groaned—a long, deep sound, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"We showed you the door," the old woman said softly. "Now show us the way back."

And beneath Sable's feet, deep under the palace, under the ice, under three hundred years of deliberate forgetting, something answered—a pulse, faint and rhythmic, like a heartbeat kept waiting in a locked room.

The archive doors, at the end of the gallery, swung open on their own.

* * *

Inside, the archive stretched farther than the palace could hold, shelves receding into dark that the ghost-light couldn't reach. Dust hung motionless in the air, undisturbed since the world forgot this place. On a reading table in the center of the vast room, as though set there minutes ago, lay a single ledger bound in grey leather, its cover stamped with a symbol Sable knew better than her own name.

She had worn it on her skin since birth.

Vane saw it too. She heard his breath catch—the same breath, she realized, that had caught in her kitchen in Hollowmere, the recognition he had never explained.

"That mark," she said, not turning. "On my arm. You knew it. You've known since the first night."

"It was on the treaty," he whispered. "The surrender-unwriting. The last signature on it. I was in the room, Sable. I was nineteen years old, holding the inkwell, and an old woman signed away a kingdom with that symbol, and she looked at me—she *looked* at me, like she was leaving something with me, like she knew—" His voice failed.

Sable reached out and opened the ledger.

The first page held a map of the Sundered Sea, complete and luminous, drawn in a hand identical to her own.

Beneath it, in faded brown ink, a single line:

*If you are reading this, granddaughter, then the bargain is failing, and you must finish what I could not.*
