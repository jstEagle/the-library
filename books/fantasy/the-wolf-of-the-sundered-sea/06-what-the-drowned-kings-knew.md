## 6. What the Drowned Kings Knew

The archive of Carreth did not want to be found.

Sable felt it as she climbed the palace's spine—a resistance in the stone, a reluctance, like a body flinching from a touch it had learned to expect. The stairs wound upward through the ruined tower in a slow spiral, each step furred with frost that was not quite frost. It crunched beneath her boots and then, when she looked back, the prints were already filling with a pale, granular light, as though the tower were healing itself over her passage. Erasing her.

*It remembers itself,* Vane had said at the city's edge, his voice strange. *It just doesn't remember us.*

She had left him below, in the great hall where the ghosts gathered thickest, because he could not climb past the third landing. The city would not carry him higher. Whatever Carreth remembered of Aldric Vane, it remembered with its teeth bared, and the stairwell had simply—refused him, the steps going soft and treacherous as river mud until he'd retreated, jaw tight, saying nothing. She had not offered to stay behind with him. She was not sure yet what he was to her: guide, jailer, betrayer, the man whose hands had burned her neighbor's cottage while hers had drawn the flames' reflection in ink. All of those things could be true at once. The valley had taught her that much—nothing was only one thing, not even safety.

So she climbed alone, into the cold that breathed like something sleeping.

The archive door was ironwood banded in silver gone black, and across its face ran a script she knew without knowing how. Her hand rose before her mind gave permission, fingers tracing the characters the way she traced coastlines in her dreams—the flowing, salt-worn letters of the Sundered Sea, the language that existed nowhere in any book she'd ever been allowed to touch. The words said:

*WE GAVE WHAT WE WERE TO KEEP WHO WE LOVED.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, worn nearly smooth by centuries of touched grief:

*ENTER ONLY IF YOU CAN BEAR IT.*

Sable stood a long time in the dark of the stairwell. Wind moved somewhere above her, through broken clerestories, making a sound like the sea she had never seen but had drawn ten thousand times. She thought of Hollowmere, of the valley that forgot on purpose, of her mother's face going carefully blank whenever Sable asked about her grandmother. She thought of the proclamation nailed to the meetinghouse door, and Maren Ostler's screams, and the smell of burning that had followed her into every dream since.

*I can bear it,* she told the door. *I've been bearing it my whole life without knowing what.*

The lock was not a lock. It was a question asked in pressure, and when she pressed her palm flat against the ironwood, the mark on her forearm—the birthmark that shifted when she slept, the map written in her skin—warmed and answered, and the door swung inward on silence.

* * *

Inside, the dark was total for three heartbeats. Then the archive lit itself.

Not torchlight. Not lamplight. The shelves themselves began to glow along their edges, a faint blue-white luminescence like moonrise on snow, and Sable saw that she stood in a round chamber walled entirely with books—but these were not books as Hollowmere understood them. They were slabs of slate and sheets of vellum and cylinders of sealed glass, and coils of copper wire strung with beads, and one entire wall of small clay tablets stacked like loaves. A country's memory, kept in whatever would hold. And everywhere, everywhere, maps.

They hung from the ceiling on wires. They lay open on reading tables of black wood. They covered the floor in places, weighted down with stones, and Sable walked among them slowly, afraid to breathe too hard, seeing her own secret work reflected back at her from every surface—coastlines she had drawn blind in the night, harbors she had dreamed, the long curve of the Sundered Sea rendered here in faded inks by hands long dead. One map near the center table bore a signature she recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water.

*S. Galloway.*

Her own name. In a hand steadier than hers, older than hers, but the same hand—the same stubborn way of doubling the stroke on the *l*, the same little hook on the terminal *y*. She stared at it until her eyes stung. The date column beside the signature held numbers in a calendar no kingdom currently used.

"Not mine," she whispered, and her breath hung in the glowing air like a small ghost. "But mine."

The last cartographer of the Sundered Sea. The woman her mother would not speak of. Whose name had been given to Sable like an inheritance nobody wanted—and whose memories had been seeping into her granddaughter's sleep ever since, drop by drop, dream by dream, a lifetime of drowned geography poured into a child in a valley that forbade magic. Because paper burns, and skin burns, but blood keeps what it is given.

Sable put her hand over her mouth and stood very still, and let herself understand it all at once, the way ice lets go of a branch all at once. She had never been drawing fantasies. She had been remembering. Every night since childhood, her grandmother had been showing her the country, patiently, lovingly, the way you teach someone a song they will need to sing alone.

* * *

The truth was in the center of the room, on a lectern of whalebone under a dome of glass so old it had begun to sag at the corners like cooling wax.

Inside lay a single document—not vellum but something stranger, a membrane of beaten kelp-fiber that glowed faintly from within, lettered in the high royal hand. Sable lifted the glass away and read, and the reading took less time than the understanding, though both together took everything she had.

It was a treaty. It was a confession. It was a prayer. It was all three, and it began:

*In the famine-winter, when the sea froze to the horizon and the grain ships could not come, we the Crown and Council of the Sundered Sea did make this bargain—*

The terms were laid out in language so plain it hurt. There had been no war. No conquest, no invasion, no burning fleet. There had been hunger—three years of it, a winter that would not end, children dying in the fish-smoke houses, the granaries empty, the king's own daughters gray-lipped and hollow-eyed. And into that winter had come an offer, from powers the treaty named only as *the Ones Below the Ice*, terms spoken through oracles and written in this membrane that could not burn.

The Sundered Sea could be spared. Its people—every soul, down to the beggars on the wharves and the babes unborn—would be carried out, fed, sheltered, settled in lands far away where the famine could not follow. In exchange, the country itself would cease. Not fall. Not be conquered. *Cease.* Removed from the world's memory and the world's maps, folded up like a chart, its coastline unmade, its cities put away somewhere between one moment and the next. The land would be held in trust, frozen beneath a sea that was no longer there, waiting.

And the price of taking it back—the price of opening the door, whenever any descendant should find it—was written on the final page in letters that seemed to darken as she read them, as if the words drank light:

*For a thing erased may be restored only by an equal erasure. What was traded for survival must be traded again for return. Let whoever opens the door give up what we gave up keeping: their loves, their remembrances, the faces and voices of everyone who ever mattered to them. This is the toll. We paid it once in a country. Whoever comes after must pay it in themselves.*

Survival was the original sin. Survival was the debt still accruing interest, four generations deep.

Sable set the membrane down with hands that would not stop shaking. The chamber glowed around her, patient, full of the dead country's careful records—harvest ledgers, tide tables, letters between sisters, a child's first attempt at drawing a gull—and every shelf, every tablet, every coiled wire of beads was a voice saying: *we were real, we were real, we were real.* The Ones Below the Ice had let them keep their archives inside the fold, had let the country remember itself even as the world forgot it. That was the cruelty of it, or the mercy; Sable could not tell which. A whole nation entombed with its own memory, able to know exactly what it had lost, forever.

And outside, in the world, witch-hunters burned cartographers' daughters for lesser knowledge than this.

She thought of Maren Ostler, who could coax blisters closed with a song. Of the old ferryman who'd been taken the year before, quietly, no proclamation then, just an empty chair by the river. Of her mother, pressing Sable's hands flat against her apron and saying, *never let them see, never let them see,* and meaning: I am giving you a life half-sized because a whole one will get you killed. The valley had never been sanctuary. It had been a hiding place, and hiding places are graves you agree to walk around in.

Her grandmother had known. Had drawn the maps, had passed the memory down like contraband, had trusted a granddaughter not yet born to finish the work—to carry the country out of the fold and open the door, whatever the door cost. *Enter only if you can bear it.* Not the reading. The toll.

Sable pressed her palm against the lectern and felt, unmistakably, the mark on her arm shift—lines crawling beneath the skin like currents under ice, rearranging themselves, completing some final stretch of coastline. The last map was nearly finished. It was being finished in her.

Somewhere below, muffled by floors of stone, she heard Vane's voice raised in argument with the ghosts—or with himself; with Vane the two had always sounded alike. And beneath that, farther off, carried on the wind through the broken windows, another sound was arriving. Hooves. Many of them, moving fast through the dead streets, and the thin metallic music of harness and chain that Sable had learned in Hollowmere to hear before she saw anything at all.

The crown had found the city. Or the crown had been finding it all along, following Vane's false reports like hounds on a trail he'd meant to lay—and now the trail had led somewhere true.

Sable looked at the treaty, at the toll written in drinking letters, at the signature on the wall-map that was her name and not her name. Then she turned toward the stairwell, toward the sound of the hunters, and began to run down through the glowing dark, the last cartographer's memories rising in her like water into a lung.

The winter, she understood now, had never been weather.

It was the country, holding its breath.
