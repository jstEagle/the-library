## 8. The Door Out of Nowhere

The sea was not water.

Sable had known it since the White Mile, since the first night her magic woke in her like a limb that had slept wrong, but knowing and understanding were different countries, and she crossed the border between them now, walking down through the frozen dark beneath Carreth. The drowned sea was memory—solidified, stratified, pressed into layers the way she pressed ink between blotters—and it remembered being water the way an old woman remembers dancing. Every step she took rang faintly against it, a sound like a spoon against the rim of a cup, like a bell rung once in an empty house.

Vane's blood was on her hands still, though she had scrubbed them at a frozen fountain until the skin cracked. She could not scrub away the way he had looked at her when he told her to go. Not grateful. Not sorry. *Certain*, the way a man is certain about a debt he has already priced.

*Hold the pass,* he had said. *I'm good at holding things I hate.*

She had not said goodbye. Goodbye was a word for people who expected to be remembered, and they both understood by then that whatever happened at the door, one of them would not be coming back to be remembered.

So she walked alone, down through streets of blue-black ice where lamplighters' ghosts had hung their lanterns for a winter fifty years deep, past shopfronts whose windows still showed the shapes of goods no one would ever sell, and the cold did not touch her because the cold here was not temperature. It was attention. The whole drowned country was watching her walk toward its heart, the way a sleeper watches the figure standing at the foot of the bed, waiting to learn whether the figure has come to wake or smother them.

The door was where her grandmother's map said it would be, which meant it was where Sable's dreams had always said it would be, which meant she had been drawing her way toward this place since she was six years old and woke weeping from a dream of a coastline she'd never seen.

It stood at the bottom of everything.

Not a door as the valley built doors—no frame, no hinges, no wood. It was a seam in the world, a vertical line of paler darkness perhaps twice the height of a man, hanging in the ice like a crack in a kiln-fired bowl. Around it the frozen sea had arranged itself in rings, strata upon strata, and Sable saw with a cartographer's eye what she was looking at: years. Rings of winter, laid down season after season, and every ring was thinner than the last, as if the country were being compressed, pressed flatter and flatter, a flower in a book that someone kept adding pages to.

She put out her hand.

The seam was warm.

That undid her more than anything else—the warmth of it, faint as breath on a wrist, after miles of that attentive cold. She pressed her palm flat against it and felt, unmistakably, a heartbeat on the other side. Slow. Enormous. The pulse of something asleep that had been asleep so long its dreams had become geology.

"Show me," she said aloud, because the dead city behind her had taught her that this country answered speech, and because her voice was the only thing she had left that was entirely hers.

* * *

The door showed her.

It did not open—not yet—but it opened *in her*, the way a book opens when you know it well enough to find the page blind. Knowledge poured up through her palm like heat through iron, and she understood the mechanism, and understanding it she made a small sound, involuntary, the sound of a woman finding the price tag on something she had already decided to buy.

The Sundered Sea had not been locked away. It had been *folded*, folded out of the world the way you fold a letter closed, and a letter stays folded only while something holds it shut. For fifty years, the thing holding it shut had been a bargain: existence traded for survival, a kingdom's continuance exchanged for its people's lives during the famine-winter, sealed by the last cartographer, who had drawn the fold herself and then walked into it.

But folds loosen. Memory leaks. Dreams of coastlines cross valleys that magic cannot enter, because nothing can be erased cleanly; erasure is just forgetting performed badly, over and over, forever.

And the door—the door opened outward only. It could be pushed open from inside the fold, but not by force, not by kings, not by armies. It required a key, and the key was not a thing. The key was a weight. Something loved enough to anchor a life had to be set against the door, and the door would swing on that hinge, and the one who paid would remain on the outside of everything they had ever loved.

Memories of love, traded for a country's return. Not all memories. Only those. The door was precise that way, precise as a contract, and Sable understood suddenly why her grandmother had chosen it, why any cartographer would choose it: because a cartographer knows better than anyone that a map is not the territory, that the name on the page is not the place, that what you draw is not what you keep. Her grandmother had traded her love and kept her craft, and the craft had leaked down the decades like light under a door, into a granddaughter's sleep.

"That's not a price," Sable whispered. "That's a theft."

The door did not disagree. The door simply waited, warm against her hand, beating its slow buried heart.

* * *

She sat down on the ice before it, because such things should not be decided standing, and she let it show her the futures, because she had learned in Carreth that this country did not lie—it only remembered, and memory is honest even when it is cruel.

First future: she turned from the seam. She climbed back up through the drowned streets, crossed the White Mile going the other way, and found Hollowmere still there—or what was left of it. Vane had held the pass; some of the hunters had died; some had lived and gone home and reported a dead witch in a dead country, and the hunts would end eventually, hunts always ended, the crown moved on to newer fears. She would go back to her little house with its south-facing window. She would draw maps of real places for real money, coastlines that existed, roads she could walk herself. She would marry, perhaps—the thought surfaced without shame; there had been a boy once, Tam, who mended her roof and laughed at her jokes and whom she had left unexamined for eleven years because examining him might have meant staying forever. She would have children. She would teach one of them to hold a pen. And on winter nights she would wake from dreams of a coastline she had seen with her own eyes and saved with her own hands, and she would remember none of it clearly, because—

Because that was the other half of the bargain, the fine print written in her grandmother's hand: the safe future was not free either. The door showed her that too. Turn away, live, and live *hollowed*—not punished, merely lighter. The memories of love she carried would thin on their own, quietly, year by year, like ink left in the sun. Her mother's voice first. Then Tam's laugh, if she went back to him. Then the shape of Vane's stubborn ruined face. She would keep facts the way you keep dates in a history book—*my mother died in autumn, I once knew a hunter*—and the love itself, the weight of it, would belong to the door.

There was no future in which she kept both. The door had been very clear. A hinge must bear weight on one side or the other. You could not stand in a doorway forever; she had tried it her whole life, and the doorway was closing, and behind her she could hear, faint and far, the sound of the crown's horns in the pass above Carreth, and the ghosts singing back at them, and under it all the enormous slow heartbeat of a sleeping country asking, in the only language it had left:

*Will you wake me, or will you let me sleep?*

* * *

She thought of her mother.

Not the fact of her—the *her* of her. Flour on her forearms. The specific off-key note she hit on the third line of the lullaby, always the third line, as if the song had been built with a flaw on purpose, the way a weaver leaves a mistake in a rug so the pattern isn't proud. The way she'd said *Sable* when Sable came in wet and freezing, half scold and half prayer. Sable sat on the ice of a murdered sea and held that voice in her mind the way you hold a bird—carefully, aware that the holding is the danger, that your own grip can crush the thing you're keeping.

If she opened the door, she would lose it. Not death—death keeps things; death is how the dead stay vivid. This was worse. This was the voice smoothing out, flattening, becoming *a woman who sang, once, presumably*. She would stand in whatever came after and feel a grief with no object, mourning a person she could not name, and everyone would tell her that was normal, that grief is like that, and no one would ever know she was right.

And if she did not—if she took the hollow road home—she would lose it anyway, slowly, politely, with interest. Fifty years of thinning. Her mother's voice becoming weather. Becoming nothing.

The choice, she saw at last, was not between keeping and losing.

It was between losing *for something*, and losing *for nothing*.

She laughed—a small, cracked sound that rang off the ice rings and came back to her from fifty years away, and she thought she heard, underneath her own laughter, another laugh answer, older, a woman's, hitting a slightly wrong note.

"Oh," Sable said to the door, to her grandmother, to the whole patient listening dark. "You clever, terrible woman. You didn't leave me a map."

"You left me a *choice*. The only kind worth drawing."

She stood. Her knees ached; she had forgotten to eat; none of it mattered; all of it mattered. Above her, miles up through the frozen streets, the horns sounded again, closer now, and somewhere up there Aldric Vane was holding a pass he hated for a woman he had betrayed, unforgiven and unforgiving, buying minutes with his guilt because minutes were all he had left to spend. He was spending them on her. She would not waste them standing in a doorway.

She pressed both palms flat against the seam, and it was warm, it was so *warm*, like a cheek, like a forehead checked for fever, like a child's hand in yours on a cold road, and beneath her hands the great slow heartbeat stuttered—woke—quickenened.

The door began, very softly, to open.

And Sable Galloway, who had spent her whole life drawing places that no longer existed, took one breath—one full breath, cold and clean, the last one that would ever wholly belong to her—and stepped through the seam of the world, reaching back for her mother's voice the way you reach for a hand in the dark.

The dark reached back.

It took the voice first.
