## 9. Sundered

The door beneath the frozen sea was older than the ice above it.

Sable had known it would be. Everything down here was old—the drowned streets furred with pale coral, the bell tower still standing though its bell had long ago been eaten to a lace of rust, the stair that descended from the palace archive into water that should have killed her and did not, because the water remembered her too. Her grandmother's memories moved through it like currents, and the sea parted for them the way it once parted for the kings.

The door stood at the bottom of everything: a slab of black stone set into the seabed itself, tall as a cathedral door, carved with a single symbol.

Her birthmark.

It had stopped shifting when she touched the first step of the stair. All her life it had crawled across her skin in her sleep, drawing coastlines she didn't understand, and now it lay still against her shoulder blade like a hand pressed flat, holding her together until the work was done.

*Open outward,* her grandmother's memory had said, in a voice that was also Sable's own voice heard from very far away. *Nothing here opens inward. The Sundered Sea did not close its doors on the world—the world closed its doors on us. So the last door opens the other way. And it will only open for someone willing to be unmade by it.*

Above her, faint as a heartbeat through miles of stone and salt, she could hear the hunters coming.

---

She had thought there would be more time.

There was never more time. That was the lesson of Hollowmere, the lesson of Vane bleeding out at the mouth of the pass with his sword arm ruined and his eyes open toward her because he had wanted the last thing he saw to be something honest. He had bought her an hour. Perhaps two. The winter wind carried the sound of their dogs even now, thin and high, threading down through the frozen sea like needles through cloth.

Sable knelt before the door and put both palms flat against the black stone.

It was warm. That undid her a little—after so much cold, after the White Mile and the dead city and the ghosts who had screamed at her in voices like tearing silk, the warmth of the stone was almost unbearable. It felt like being welcomed home by something that did not know yet what her arrival would cost.

*I'm ready,* she said, and did not say it aloud, because there was no one left to hear her, and because some part of her understood that speech was already becoming a kind of luxury.

The door answered.

Not with words. With light—a slow gold seepage along the carved mark, brightening, and then a pressure against her mind gentle as a tide coming in over sand. It asked nothing. It simply waited, the way the sea waits, and Sable understood that the choosing was entirely hers, that no power in creation could make this decision but her own small stubborn heart.

*Take it,* she thought. *All of it.*

The first memory went like a tooth pulled clean.

Her mother's voice.

She had been saving it without knowing she was saving it—the low hum of her mother singing over the wash basin, off-key, always off-key, the song about the miller's daughter and the seven silver fish. Sable clung to it for one whole second. She felt the melody fray at its edges like wet paper, felt herself reach for it the way you reach in a dream for a doorknob that keeps sliding away, and then it was gone, and worse than gone: the space where it had been was smooth and painless, and she knew only that something had been there, and that she would never ache for it again, because she would never know there was anything to ache for.

That was the cruelty of it. Not loss—loss she could have borne. It was the tidiness. Each memory lifted out left the surrounding ones polished and intact, a wall with one brick removed and mortared over so seamlessly that no one standing inside the house would ever guess a brick had ever been missing.

The Sundered Sea began to rise.

She felt it before she saw it—a shudder through the seabed, deep and vast, like a great body turning over in sleep. Above her, through a thousand feet of black water, the ice groaned. Somewhere far off, the drowned city exhaled.

And the door drank her.

---

*Her father's hands.*

They were rough, cracked at the knuckles, smelling of lamp oil and pine resin. They had taught her to hold a pen. They had—

Gone. Smoothed over. She stared at the place where they had been and found nothing, not even the shape of the absence, only the next memory waiting patiently behind it like a traveler in a queue.

*Hollowmere.*

The valley came apart in pieces, which seemed fitting. The smell of the tanneries first, sour and familiar, stripped away so cleanly she gagged at how little it mattered to her now. Then the crooked lane to Marisol's house. Then Marisol herself—taken by the witch-hunters in the chapter of Sable's life she could no longer read, her face going soft and featureless as a thumbprint in wax. Sable tried to say her name and found her mouth full of seawater and silence.

The sea was rising faster now. She could feel it in the tremble of the floor, in the groaning of the bell tower above, in the way the darkness around the door had begun to lighten—not with the door's gold light but with something else, something green-grey and alive, the color of open water under morning.

Somewhere above her, boots on stone. Many boots. Shouting, thin and distorted by all that water between.

*Let them come,* she thought, and was surprised by the fury in it, the fierceness. *Let them drown in what they burned.*

---

*Vane.*

No.

She had known this would come. She had walked down the stairs rehearsing it, telling herself she was ready, that a man who died unforgiven and unredeemed could not cost her more than his story had already cost her. But when the door's patient tide reached for Aldric Vane, Sable caught herself bracing against the stone, fingers white, and the door paused—it actually paused, a courtesy or a cruelty, giving her the length of one breath to refuse.

His face. The scar through his eyebrow that he'd never explained. His voice saying *I know that mark. I drew that mark, when I was six years old, on the doorpost of a house that doesn't exist anymore.* The look on his face at the pass, when he turned back toward the hunters with his ruined arm and said, *Go. Don't watch. I'd rather you didn't watch.*

She held him for three breaths. She counted them.

Then she let go, because the alternative was letting everyone else go instead—the whole rising country, the hundred thousand drowned souls pressing upward behind the ice, all of them waiting on the mercy of her remembering one disgraced man's crooked smile.

He went gently. That was the worst of it. His memory left her the way he himself would have wanted to leave: quietly, without ceremony, owing nothing.

The sea broke the surface far above. She heard it—a sound like the sky splitting, like every bell in Carreth ringing at once—and through the black water she saw the first true daylight in forty years filter down in long green shafts, touching the drowned rooftops, waking the coral to colors it must have forgotten it owned.

---

*Hollowmere is a valley where magic cannot enter.*

The thought arrived and she examined it like a stranger's letter. True, somehow. Important, somehow. But the name meant nothing. *Hollowmere.* A word shaped like a place, empty as a cup.

More of her was going now, and faster, and she found she did not mind. That was the strangest mercy of all—that the dissolving did not hurt, that each removal was painless, that terror required a self to terrify and hers was thinning into something that could not be frightened. She was becoming less a person and more a process. A coastline being drawn. A tide coming in.

*My grandmother was the last cartographer of the Sundered Sea.*

Gone.

*The famine-winter. The bargain. Survival was the original sin.*

Gone, and with it the whole terrible architecture of understanding she had built in the archive, the revelation that had reorganized her soul—smoothed over, mortared, seamless. She knew only that the door was opening, and that it was right, the way a river running downhill is right.

Above, the shouting had changed. No longer commands. Screaming now, and the screaming was being swallowed. She imagined the hunters on the cliffs of a country that had returned overnight, drowning in geography they had helped to erase, hunted at last by the thing they had hunted. She felt no triumph. Triumph needed a victor, and she was mostly tide now.

*My name.*

She reached for it and found it already loosening, a thread slipping from a hem. *Sable.* A dark color. A bird. A girl in a valley that forgot. The door waited, courteous to the end, and she stood inside her last moment of selfhood the way you stand in a doorway before stepping out into snow, taking one final breath of the air you've always breathed.

*Let it rise,* she thought, with what was left of her, with the fierce stubborn heart that had survived the burning of her maps and the crossing of the White Mile and the death of the only ally she'd ever trusted. *Let it rise, and let me be the ink, not the hand. Let me be the line on the map and not the name beside it.*

*Sable Galloway.*

The name went last, and it went like a wave withdrawing from sand, and where it had been there was only the sea.

---

The door opened outward.

Black stone swung wide onto morning, and the Sundered Sea poured up through it—not water now but land and water both, the whole drowned country unfolding like a chart drawn by a sure and loving hand. Cities surfaced. Forests shook off forty years of salt. Rivers remembered their courses and ran. On the cliffs above Carreth, the hunters' fires went out one by one, drowned in a history too large to burn.

And at the bottom of the new bay, in a doorway of black stone that no diver would ever find again, a woman's shape stood for a moment in the gold light—then thinned, and brightened, and was gone, leaving nothing behind but a warmth in the water, and a birthmark-shaped shadow on the seabed that faded even as the sun touched it.

The sea closed over the place where she had been, gently, the way a hand closes over a coin.

Far to the north, in a green valley ringed by mountains, a village woke to a strange morning. Something had changed. No one could say what. An old woman stood at her window a long time, looking east, weeping without knowing why, and when her granddaughter asked what was wrong she said, "I don't know. I feel as if I've forgotten someone," and then she laughed at herself, and made the bread, and lived her life.

On the desk of the palace cartographer in the risen city of Carreth, in an archive that had surfaced intact, lay a map no one had drawn—or rather, a map whose maker no record named, signed only with a small dark bird and a word worn nearly illegible:

*Sable.*

* * *

Years later, a young cartographer tracing that impossible coastline would pause at a small unlabeled cove, brush her fingers across the ink, and feel—for no reason she could ever explain—utterly, inexplicably wept-for.

And somewhere in the new country, on that same night, a woman with no past and no name fell asleep by her fire, and began, for the first time, to dream of a valley.
