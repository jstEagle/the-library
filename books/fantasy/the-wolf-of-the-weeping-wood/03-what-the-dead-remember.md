## 3. What the Dead Remember

The boy slept in the loft above the workshop, curled beneath three blankets and Hazel's oldest coat, and still his bare feet hung out into the cold as though winter were a rumor he had declined to believe. He had eaten half a loaf, drunk a cup of milk gone thin with water, and spoken not one word—only looked at Hazel with those grey eyes that seemed to hold more years than the face could carry, and then at the wolf-token carved of pale wood that he would not let out of his hand even in sleep.

Hazel sat below among the shavings and the smell of linseed, and he did not sleep at all.

He had touched the token once, and heard thousands of voices layered like strata in stone, and that was enough. Once in a lifetime was enough for a sound like that. But the question it had planted grew in him all night, rootless and hungry: *what is this child?* Not who. What. The dead spoke to Hazel through their bones—their small cold histories—but the boy's wooden wolf had sung with something that had never been alive at all, or had been alive so long ago that life was no longer the right word for it.

There was only one way to ask. And asking meant going down into the box he kept locked, the box he opened perhaps twice a year, because what waited inside it loved him, and love from the dead is a heavy thing to carry.

* * *

The box was elderwood, iron-banded, older than the house. Inside, packed in wool gone grey with decades, lay the bones of Edith Quintana—his grandmother, dead eleven years, and before her death the only person alive who had known what Hazel was and had not crossed herself or reached for a priest.

He laid them out on the workbench in their proper order, the way she had taught him to lay out a reading: skull at the north, spine curving south like a river on a map, ribs fanned, hands folded where her hands had always folded when she listened. Her finger-bones were yellowed ivory now, each joint a separate note waiting to be struck.

"You'll want payment first," he said aloud, feeling foolish, feeling the familiar ache behind his breastbone. "So here it is."

Payment was memory. That was the old rule, the rule the dead themselves had taught his grandmother and hers before her: nothing given to the dead is free, and they will take their price in the coin you least wish to spend. Hazel closed his eyes and gave her the morning of the day she died—the smell of her kitchen, barley and woodsmoke; her laugh, low and cracked as a dry reed; the weight of her hand on his hair when she'd told him, *You are not cursed, little hawk. You are only listening where others shout.* He gave it fully, holding nothing back, and felt it leave him the way warmth leaves a stone held in snow.

Then he took up her right index-finger and pressed it gently against his brow.

Her voice came the way it always came—not through his ears but through the bone itself, rising up his arm like sap, like water finding its level. Eleven years dead, and still her voice was the warmest thing in the cold room.

*Little hawk.*

"Grandmother." His throat was tight. "I need the old knowledge. The deep shelves. There's a child in my loft with a wolf-token that sings with a thousand throats, and I don't know what I've let in through my door."

A long silence. When her voice came again it had changed—cooler, careful, the voice she'd used for things said once and never again.

*I hoped you'd never ask. I hoped the world would stay asleep another hundred years, and you'd die an old man who carved pretty trinkets and never knew why your gift chose you of all the Quintanas born.*

"What do you mean, *chose me?*"

*Ask me properly. Ask what the Covenant of Thornfell was.*

"I'm asking."

*Ask what your family did.*

The fire ticked in the stove. Snow sighed against the shutters. Above him, faintly, the floorboard creaked under a sleeping child turning over.

"Grandmother," Hazel said slowly, "what did my family do?"

And she showed him.

* * *

Not words this time, or not only words. She poured the memory into him the way she had once poured tea, steady and scalding, and Hazel stood inside it whether he wished to or not.

He stood on a ridge above a forest that made the Weeping Wood of his own time look like a garden hedge—a forest vast and dark and *breathing*, every tree tall as a watchtower, and between the trees moved shapes of grey and silver, wolves the size of oxen with eyes like banked coals, moving not as beasts move but with a terrible grave purpose, like a congregation. The wolf-spirits of Thornfell, old as the mountains, bound by oath to the human valleys below: protection given freely, borders honored, the wild kept wild and the tame kept safe. A covenant sworn in blood on both sides, sealed in a standing stone at the forest's heart—a stone carved with interlocking spirals, wolf-tooth and human hand together.

He saw the humans of that far time come up the mountain in torchlight. Hundreds of them. Priests in iron-grey robes, soldiers with pikes, and men carrying a stake of black iron taller than a door, forged—he understood without being told—from melted-down treaty-gifts, from the silver and iron the wolves themselves had once given in friendship.

And at the head of them, riding a horse too fine for the mud, a man with Hazel's own jaw, Hazel's own deep-set eyes, a man whose name rang in the memory like a struck bell:

*Alder Quintana.*

*First Warden of the Vale,* the memory whispered. *Keeper of the Covenant. The one they trusted most.*

Hazel watched Alder dismount before the standing stone. Watched him kneel as if in reverence—and drive the iron stake into the heart of the stone while the wolf-mother came running through the trees too late, screaming a sound that peeled bark and stopped hearts, a sound Hazel felt in his own marrow two centuries later. Watched the stone split along the spirals. Watched the great wolves fall—one by one, like puppets with cut strings—writhing, shrinking, folding inward upon themselves as the broken covenant dragged them down into the earth, bound beneath the roots, imprisoned in the soil of the forest they had loved. Watched the forest begin, that very night, to weep: sap running down the bleeding bark like tears, and would run for two hundred years.

*Alder Quintana drove the stake,* his grandmother's voice said, quiet and level above the roaring of the vision. *For a purse of Crown gold and a title. And the Crown wrote it down as a holy victory, and our family kept the gold, and bought land, and married well, and forgot. But forgetting is not the same as being forgiven, little hawk. The debt doesn't rot. It waits. It waits for a Quintana with ears.*

The vision broke. Hazel found himself gripping the edge of the workbench hard enough to whiten his knuckles, breath coming fast, sweat cold on his back despite the stove.

"That isn't—" He stopped. Started again. "That can't be right. My family were carvers. Farmers. Bone-carvers, wood-carvers—"

*What do you think the carving was for?* Her voice was gentle, which was worse than anger. *Why do you think the gift runs in our blood, of all the blood in the vale? Alder couldn't have driven that stake alone. The stone trusted him because our line had been the covenant's keepers for generations—we were the ones who spoke with the wolves' permission, who carried messages between root and hearth. We were the ones whose word the stone believed. Our whole gift, everything you are, everything I was—it was the covenant's leash, passed hand to hand. And Alder used the leash to hang them.*

"No." He said it flatly, the way he'd once refused to believe his father dead. "No, I won't—I didn't ask to be shown fables. Grandmother, tell me about the boy instead. Tell me what the child is, since you know so much."

A pause. Then, softly, almost sadly:

*You already know. You've known since the token sang. You just haven't dared say it.*

"Say it anyway."

*When the stone broke,* she said, *the covenant didn't die. Covenants don't die; they're not alive. They wait for mending. But a binding needs an anchor, something living to hold the knot—and the wolf-mother, Ulva, in her last free hour, wove what remained into a seal of flesh. A living anchor. Passed down outside the kingdoms, outside the Crowns, hidden in whatever vessel could hold it. One at a time. Always one. Each bearer lives and dies ordinary, and passes the seal on, and never knows—or knows, and pays for knowing.*

"The boy."

*The last of them. The last anchor left in the world. Which means the binding is failing, Hazel. It means the wolves beneath the Wood are waking, and angry after two hundred years of dark, and the only thing standing between their rage and the valleys is a mute child who doesn't know what he carries.* A pause, and when her voice came again there was iron in it. *And it means the Crown will come looking. The Inquisitors have hunted the seal for three generations—they call it the Stolen Relic in their papers, as if a child were a candlestick. If Veyra's people get wind of a mute boy appearing in Thornfell in the dead of winter—*

"They already have." Hazel's mouth was dry. "She came yesterday. Before him, even. Asking after a stolen relic."

*Then you have until she finds her papers again,* his grandmother said, *which is to say: no time at all. Listen to me, little hawk. Hear the rest, because you must hear it all if you're to choose rightly.*

"There's more?"

*The covenant demands a price from whoever reforges it. That's written in the oldest bones, the ones I burned rather than leave lying about. A willing sacrifice. From the betrayer's bloodline. Give what you treasure most, freely, or the knot cannot be tied.* Another pause, longer. *I'm sorry. I should have told you at twelve, when I saw what your ears could do. I thought I was sparing you. It seems I only saved the wound for later, and larger.*

Hazel set her finger-bone down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a live coal.

"I don't accept any of this." His voice came out strange, distant, someone else's voice. "I don't accept that my ancestor was a butcher. I don't accept that some fairy-tale debt lands on me. I carve bones and sell secrets and keep my head down—that's the whole of my ambition. Whatever the Wood wants, whatever the Crown wants, they can want it from someone else."

*Little hawk—*

"There's a *child* upstairs," he said, louder now, and hated the crack in it. "Frost doesn't touch him and he looks at me like I'm the one being judged. And you're telling me he's a knot in an ancient binding and my family tied it wrong and now I owe the interest?" He laughed, and it came out ugly. "No. I refuse. Find another Quintana."

*There are no other Quintanas,* she said simply. *You are the last. That's rather the point.*

Silence filled the workshop, thick as tallow. The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls a beam settled under the snow-load with a groan like an old man in sleep.

"Grandmother," Hazel said at last, very quietly. "If the seal breaks—if the boy dies or is taken—what happens?"

He felt her hesitate, and his grandmother had never hesitated in her life or death.

*The wolves wake unbound,* she said. *All of them, all at once, after two hundred years in the dark with nothing to remember but the stake and the face of the man who drove it. And the face of the man who drove it, Hazel—this is the part you truly don't want to hear—is the face you shave every morning. They will smell Quintana on the wind and they will follow it anywhere.*

Above him, the floor creaked. Small feet, bare against cold boards, crossing the loft. Hazel looked up at the ceiling as though he could see through it, and his refusal—which had felt so solid a moment ago, so load-bearing—shifted under him like river ice.

The boy appeared at the top of the ladder, silent as ever, wrapped in Hazel's coat, the wolf-token clutched to his chest. Grey eyes found Hazel across the dim workshop. Found the bones laid out on the bench. Something moved in that too-old face—recognition, maybe, or grief, or both—and the boy came down the ladder without being asked, slow step by step, and stopped before the workbench, and looked at Edith Quintana's remains laid in their careful order.

Then he reached out one small finger and touched, with extraordinary gentleness, the curve of her temple-bone.

Hazel heard it happen. Heard the dead woman's voice rise up through the bone and fill the room—and for the first time in eleven years, Edith Quintana's voice was not speaking to Hazel at all.

*Oh,* she breathed, wonder-struck, the way she'd spoken at festivals, at births, at the first snow of every year. *Oh, hello, little keeper. We've been waiting so long for you.*

The boy smiled. It transformed his face entirely—years falling away, the ancient grey eyes suddenly just a child's eyes, bright and wet—and he looked up at Hazel and pointed at the bones, and then at Hazel's chest, and then, deliberately, at his own mouth, opening it wide as if to say the thing he had no words for:

*Tell them. Tell them for me.*

Outside, far down the valley road, a bell began to ring. Not the chapel bell—wrong rhythm, urgent, arrhythmic, the alarm pattern the town used for fire, for flood, for riders coming under official seal.

Three tolls. A pause. Three more.

Hazel's blood went cold and stayed there.

Inquisitor Veyra had found her papers.
