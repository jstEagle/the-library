## 4. The Inquisitor's Summons

The bells woke the town before the light did.

Hazel had heard the chapel bells of Thornhollow all his life—the flat morning peal that called the faithful to prayer, the brighter evening chime that marked the closing of the market gates. But this was neither. Three strokes, a silence, three strokes again, and then a fourth note struck alone, low and long, like a finger dragged across a wet glass rim. The sound crawled down through the shuttered windows of his workshop and settled in the marrow of his bones, which was an uncomfortable thing for a man whose bones were always talking.

They were quiet this morning. That was the first wrongness. On most days the dead murmured from every shelf of his workshop—a rib here with a sailor's homesickness still soaked into it, a skull there that hummed lullabies in a dialect no one living spoke. He kept them wrapped in wool and wax to muffle the chatter, the way other men stuffed their ears against tinnitus. But today the whole collection lay silent as stones, as if they too had heard the bells and drawn inward to hide.

*Even the dead know what's coming,* Hazel thought, and reached for the boy.

The child—Hazel had begun, privately and guiltily, to think of him as *mine*, though he had no right—was curled in the cot by the cold hearth where Hazel had slept himself since the night the boy arrived. He did not shiver. He never shivered. His bare feet stuck out from under the blanket, toes pink and warm as if the snow outside were a rumor told by fools. Around his neck, on its leather cord, hung the carved wooden wolf-token, and when Hazel's eyes passed over it, he felt the faintest pressure behind his ears, like standing near a door that thousands of voices pressed against from the other side.

He had not touched it again. He did not intend to. Some doors, once glimpsed through, you bar with everything you own.

"Hungry?" he asked, knowing the answer would be the same solemn nod, the same small hand pressed flat over the boy's breastbone—*yes, here*—as if hunger lived somewhere deeper than the stomach. The boy ate bread and honey and salt fish without expression, watched Hazel work with eyes the color of rainwater over slate, and said nothing, because he could say nothing. Except that once, two nights ago, half-asleep by the fire, he had made a sound—not a word, not quite. A soft rising note that had made the fire lean sideways and made Hazel's dead grandmother's jawbone, up on its high shelf, rattle against the wool wrapping like teeth chattering.

Hazel had not slept well since.

Now the bells finished their strange liturgy, and beneath them came another sound: hooves. Many hooves, riding in formation, the disciplined cadence of soldiers rather than the scatter of merchants or the ragged rhythm of smugglers coming down from the passes with contraband in their saddlebags. Hazel stood at his window, hands pressed to the freezing glass, and watched them enter the market square below.

They wore white.

That was the detail that turned his blood to snowmelt. Crown soldiers rode in the king's blue and iron grey; border levies wore whatever they'd looted last season. But riders in unblemished white cloaks, hoods back, faces pale above black gorgets—they belonged to only one office in all the Nine Reaches. The Office of Inquiry. The Inquisition. Men and women who did not collect taxes or patrol roads but collected *certainty*, and burned away everything else.

And at their head, dismounting from a grey mare the color of her own cloak, a woman in a long coat of white wool trimmed with silver thread, her dark hair bound in a severe coil at the nape, her gloved hands bare of any weapon Hazel could see.

He knew her name before anyone in the square spoke it. Everyone in the Nine Reaches knew it. Veyra. Inquisitor Veyra. The woman who had gone into the Fenmark alone, unarmed, and come out three days later with the entire marsh-cult kneeling in chains and weeping apologies to gods they'd renounced. They said she never raised her voice. They said she never needed to.

She paused in the center of the square, lifted her face as if tasting the air, and looked directly up—at the row of shuttered workshops, at the crooked sign above Hazel's own door that read QUINTANA: CARVINGS, COMMISSIONS, RESTORATIONS—and smiled.

It was a small smile. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes.

* * *

He should have stayed home. He knew it even as he pulled on his coat, even as he pressed two fingers briefly to the boy's shoulder—*stay, be still, be stone*—and slipped out the back into the frozen alley. But there are moments when fear stops being a weight and becomes a current, and you find yourself moving with it whether you will or no, and besides: he told himself he only wanted to see. To count them. To learn the shape of the thing before it learned his.

The market square was full but silent. Veyra had not brought many—eight riders, perhaps ten—but the whole town had come out to watch anyway, the way crowds gather at executions, and Hazel understood with a sick lurch that this was exactly the correct instinct. She stood on the steps of the moot-hall with a scroll in one hand, and she did not read from it. She simply held it, the way a preacher holds scripture already long memorized, and let her voice carry.

"People of Thornhollow." Her voice was low, unhurried, almost gentle. "I have not come for taxes. I have not come for tithe or conscription or quarrel." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "I have come for a relic."

The word moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. Hazel, standing at the edge among the bakers' stalls, felt it pass through him too and leave frost behind.

"Three nights ago," Veyra continued, "a thing of great antiquity went missing from the custody of the Crown. A carving of wood, small enough to hang about the neck. It is very old. Older than the Reach. Older than the kingdom. It belongs—" and here she let the pause stretch until the square itself seemed to hold its breath— "*where it belongs.* I am asking no one to confess. I am asking only that whoever has seen such a thing remember that relics do not rest quietly. They draw things after them. Cold. Silence. Wolves." Another pause, and her gaze traveled slowly across the assembled faces, unhurried, thorough, a lamp passing along a wall looking for cracks. "You have nothing to fear from me. Only from what follows it."

Then she stepped down, and her riders dispersed through the town in pairs, polite as tax assessors, and began knocking on doors.

Hazel bought a loaf of bread he did not want, because a man fleeing would not buy bread, and walked home the long way with his heart beating so hard he could feel it in his teeth.

* * *

They came to his shop at midday.

He heard them from the stairs—one knock, measured, patient—and took the time to bank the hearth, wrap the sleeping boy deeper in the blanket, and put on the face he'd spent forty years perfecting for customs officers and suspicious widows: the pleasant, slightly dull expression of a craftsman with nothing in his head but grain and glue.

There were two of them. A young rider in white, broad and nervous, and behind him, unannounced, unhurried, the Inquisitor herself. Up close she was smaller than Hazel expected and somehow larger, the way a blade is larger than its length. Her coat smelled of cold air and something underneath it, faint and clean, like snow on stone. Her eyes were grey-green and absolutely still, and they did not flick around the room taking inventory the way a soldier's would. They rested on Hazel's face and stayed there.

"Master Quintana." She pronounced it perfectly, every syllable, including the old Reach inflection his family hadn't used aloud in three generations. "Bone-carver. And guide." She let the second word sit between them a moment longer than the first. "May I come in? The cold is remarkable this year."

"It is," Hazel said, and stepped aside.

She walked his workshop the way a physician walks a body. Her gloved fingertips hovered above the shelves without touching—over the wrapped bundles of client bones awaiting commission, over the display carvings of stags and saints and mourning angels, over the little drawer where he kept offcuts of horn and antler. She stopped before the high shelf where his grandmother's remains sat in their woolen cocoon, and tilted her head, and for one vertiginous moment Hazel saw, reflected in the polished curve of a hanging mirror, that her lips were moving slightly. As if listening.

"You work in bone," she said. "Most carvers won't. They say bone remembers."

"People say a lot of things about bone-carvers, my lady."

"Mm." She turned. "We are searching for a relic. A wooden wolf, worn smooth, strung on leather. Perhaps four hundred years old. Perhaps considerably more." Her voice remained level, courteous, almost bored. "Such a piece would have considerable value to collectors. Considerable value to *scholars*. It may have been sold. Smuggled goods change hands quickly in mountain towns, and Thornhollow sits at the crossroads of every road the king does not officially acknowledge exists."

"I carve what I'm commissioned to carve," Hazel said. "Wood, antler, bone. If a wolf-token passed through my shop, I'd remember it."

"Would you?"

"My lady?"

Her head tilted, birdlike, and the stillness of her eyes became suddenly terrible, because he realized it was not calm. It was attention. Total, absolute, predatory attention, focused on him the way sunlight focuses through a lens.

"When I ask a question," Veyra said softly, "people's hearts answer before their mouths do. Did you know that? The pulse in the throat. The breath. The little flinch of the eyes toward the thing they wish to protect. Most people cannot help it. It is a language older than words." She smiled her small smile. "So. Master Quintana. Have you seen the relic I describe?"

"No," said Hazel Quintana, liar of thirty years' practice, and heard his own heartbeat stumble like a man catching his toe on a root.

Veyra held his gaze for five seconds. Six. Seven. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked—or did not; perhaps he imagined it; *please let him be asleep, please let him be stone*—and something in the Inquisitor's expression shifted by a degree so fine it might have been nothing at all. A cartographer noting a river that appeared on no map.

"No," she repeated pleasantly. "Of course not." She drew out a card of heavy cream stock and laid it on his workbench beside a half-carved femur. "If you should hear anything. See anything. Dream anything." The pause on the last word was surgical. "The Crown rewards loyalty generously. And it remembers the alternative just as well."

She left the way she'd come, unhurried, her boots silent on his floorboards, and it was only when the door closed and Hazel sagged against his bench that he noticed the half-carved femur in front of him—the one he'd been working that morning, a commission for a widow's memorial—had split cleanly down its length. Two halves, lying apart, as neat as if he'd sawn them.

He hadn't touched it since dawn.

* * *

He did not pack immediately. Packing was panic, and panic got people hanged. Instead Hazel sat in the dark with his grandmother's jawbone unwrapped in his cupped palms, feeling the familiar dry rasp of her voice rise up through the ivory, thin as smoke.

*You waited too long,* the dead woman said, in the tone she'd used on him alive whenever he'd been slow about chores or honest about feelings. *You waited too long, and now you'll run in the worst weather of the year with a child who doesn't speak and no plan worth the name.*

"I have a plan," Hazel muttered.

*You have a road,* she said. *A road is not a plan. Take the Greyfell track past the burned chapel. Don't take the low bridge over the Sorrel—it's rotted through, and if you need it to fall behind you, better it falls easy.* A pause, and then, quieter, in the voice she'd saved for the rarest tenderness: *Boy. Whatever you're carrying—whatever came to your door—you can't outrun it forever.*

"I know, Gran."

*No, you don't,* she said. *But you will. Go now. Before the moon fills.*

He wrapped her jaw in wool, kissed it—he was not ashamed; the dead deserved kisses as much as the living—and set her back on her shelf. Then he worked. He packed the way he guided smugglers: fast, silent, nothing left behind that could testify. Coin from the loose board. The good knife. Dried meat, wax candles, flint, a coil of rope, the oilskin map-roll he'd sworn never to use again. He left the shop's ledger open on the desk, deliberately boring, full of legitimate commissions, and he took nothing that named him beyond what any carver might carry.

Last, he climbed the stairs to the cold room where the boy slept.

The child was awake. Of course he was awake. He sat up in the blankets, wolf-token glinting at his throat, and watched Hazel with those rainwater eyes, and did not look afraid, which frightened Hazel more than anything else that day. A child should be afraid. A child hunted by the Inquisition should be terrified.

"They came," Hazel said stupidly, and then, because it didn't matter anymore: "A woman in white. Asking about your—" he gestured vaguely at the token— "about a wolf."

The boy looked down at the carving in his lap. Then he raised his head, and his small hand rose and pressed flat against Hazel's chest, over the hammering heart, the way he answered questions about hunger. *Yes. Here. This is about you.*

"We can't stay," Hazel whispered. "Do you understand me? She knows. Maybe not my name yet, maybe not this house, but she'll narrow it down by tomorrow, and when Veyra narrows something down, it dies." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it. "I've spent my whole life hiding what I am. Listening to dead women's secrets and selling them cheap and telling myself it was kindness, telling myself it was a livelihood, telling myself every lie a coward tells to sleep at night. And now—" He stopped. Breathed. The boy watched him without judgment, without pity, which was somehow easier to bear than either.

"Now I suppose we run," Hazel said.

He dressed the boy in layers of his own clothing, rolled and pinned, sleeves and trousers cut down with quick knife-strokes. Wool socks over the bare feet that never seemed to need them. The boy stood patiently for all of it, arms lifted, turning when turned, and only once moved on his own—to touch the wolf-token, and then touch it to Hazel's hand, lightly, questioningly.

Every dead thing in the workshop rattled at once.

Hazel snatched his hand back. "Don't," he said hoarsely. "Not yet. Not until I understand what you're asking."

The boy lowered the token. But he did not stop looking at Hazel, and in the dark, in the cold, with the bells of Thornhollow finally silent and the white riders surely asking their patient questions three streets away, the child opened his mouth and produced a sound. Not the half-word of the fireside. A word. One word, shaped carefully, clumsily, the way a traveler shapes a phrase in a foreign tongue:

"*North.*"

The syllable hung in the freezing air. On his high shelf, wrapped in wool, Hazel's grandmother's bones gave one long, unmistakable sigh—the sigh of the dead watching the living finally do the thing they'd been telling them to do all along.

"Yes," Hazel said. He shouldered the pack, took the boy's hand—small, impossibly warm—and opened the back door onto the blue-black night and the falling snow. "North. Past Greyfell. And may every god the Wood remembers forgive us both."

Behind them, far off across the rooftops, a single bell rang once—as if marking the moment. As if counting it.

As if starting a clock.
