## 6. The Hermit of Greyfell

The pass opened onto Greyfell valley at dusk, and Hazel stopped so suddenly that the boy walked into his back.

Below them, the lake lay flat as poured pewter, holding the last copper light of the sky in a skin of ice along its northern shore. Mist moved across the water in slow ropes, and where the mist thinned, Hazel could see the far shore: black pines climbing into cloud, and above them, half-swallowed by rock and lichen, the broken tower of an old shrine. He had smuggled men past this valley for eleven years. He had never once brought anyone into it.

The dead had always told him why. Even from three ridges away, he could hear them here—not voices, exactly, but something like the hum a struck bell keeps after the sound has ended. A congregation with no bodies, singing under the water.

"Greyfell," he said aloud, mostly to hear a living voice. The boy looked up at him. Snow had crusted in the child's pale hair, and still the boy did not shiver, did not stamp his bare feet against the cold. Two days on the mountain now, and the boy had eaten half of everything Hazel offered and slept without blanket in the open like a deer. "There's someone below. If she hasn't gone mad or died. She was old when I was young."

The boy's eyes went to the drowned shrine on the far shore, then to the water itself, and his small hand rose—hesitated—and pressed flat against his own chest.

Hazel's mouth went dry. *Yes,* he thought. *Something down there is calling you too.*

He shouldered the pack and started down through the scree, and the boy followed, silent as falling snow.

* * *

Maren's hut crouched at the valley's edge where the treeline gave up, built of driftwood and lake-stone and roofed in turf gone silver with frost. Smoke rose from it, thin and straight. Hazel stood before the door a long moment, listening—for the dead inside her walls, for anything that might mean she'd turned to worse things than exile in her years alone. But the hut was quiet in the way of honest places. Only the bell-hum from the lake, patient as tide.

He knocked.

"It's open," said a voice like gravel rolled in honey. "It's been open for six years. Knocking is vanity."

Inside was one room, warm from a peat fire, hung with drying herbs and strings of river-mussels and, above the hearth, a single object polished by years of handling: a small wooden bell, cracked down the middle, that could never ring again. An old woman sat by the fire mending a net, white hair braided tight to her skull, forearms brown and knotted as root-wood. She looked up. Her eyes were the pale grey of the lake in winter, and they went past Hazel entirely—to the boy in the doorway.

The net fell from her hands.

"Maren," Hazel began, "I need—"

"Hush." She rose slowly, joints cracking like the ice on the shore, and crossed the room, and knelt. The boy watched her come with that unnerving stillness he had, head tilted, and when Maren lifted both hands toward his face she stopped short of touching him, the way one stops before an altar.

"*Vael en sora,*" she whispered.

Hazel heard nothing in it. But the boy's lips parted, and though no sound came out, Hazel saw the shape the boy made with his mouth—the same two syllables, returned.

Maren began, very quietly, to weep. She did not wipe her face. "Forty years," she said. "Forty years I kept the rites of a covenant the Church burned out of the books, and I told myself it didn't matter anymore, that I was keeping ashes warm for no one. And now you walk through my door." She looked up at Hazel, and there was iron under the tears. "Where did you find him?"

"On my doorstep. Barefoot, in the coldest night of the year." Hazel set down the pack. His hands were not quite steady, and he made fists of them. "He doesn't speak. Not our tongue. But I touched his token—a carved wolf—and I heard—" He stopped. How did you say it? *I heard thousands of voices speaking a language older than kingdoms, and they knew my name.* "I heard a great deal."

"A great deal," Maren repeated softly. She got up, fetched a bowl of barley soup from the pot, and put it in the boy's hands. He ate neatly, hungrily, watching the fire. Only when he was fed did Maren turn back to Hazel, and the priestess looked out of her face again, the exiled woman receding. "Sit, bone-carver. You've brought the seal of Thornfell into the house of the last woman who remembers what it seals. We have talking to do, and not much night to do it in."

"The seal," Hazel said. His grandmother's bones had shown him a wolf pinned beneath a stone by a stake driven through its heart, and men in Quintana colors standing around the stone with their heads bowed like mourners—or executioners. "You know what he is."

"I know what he *is*. Whether I know *what he means*—" Maren lowered herself back into her chair. "Ask your questions."

And Hazel asked them, all the ones that had been gnawing at him since the bones spoke: What was the covenant? Why did the boy carry it in his body? Who had made it, and who had broken it, and what did any of it want with a smuggler who only wanted to be left alone?

Maren answered some. Others she answered with silence, staring into the fire until the lines of her face deepened into channels.

"The Covenant of Thornfell was older than the Crown, older than the Church, older than every kingdom whose coins you carry," she said at last. "When men first came into these mountains, the Wood was already here, and the Wood has a heart, and the heart has wolves—not beasts, bone-carver, *spirits*, the anger and the mercy of the forest given teeth. There was war, in the beginning. Then there was a bargain. Three bones, cut from the body of the first wolf-mother when she let herself be bound willingly, so that the binding would hold both ways. One bone for the Wood's patience. One for its hunger. One for its grief. And a seal to keep them—a living seal, flesh and blood, renewed each generation, because stone forgets but flesh remembers."

"And the boy is the last one."

"The last one alive." Maren's gaze flicked to the sleeping alcove, where the boy had curled up near the fire, the wooden wolf-token clutched to his sternum even in sleep. "Which means the binding is failing. Which means the wolves are waking angry. And which means, I am sorry to say, that whoever walks beside him has already been chosen whether he likes it or not."

"I wasn't chosen. I found him."

"You *found* him." Maren laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "Child, do you know what the old rites called a man who could hear the dead in bone? They called him a *door*. The dead walk through such men. And you think the covenant stumbled to your doorstep by accident, in all the world, on all the nights?" She shook her head. "The Wood doesn't knock. It sends."

Hazel looked at his own hands. Carver's hands, scarred and steady, that had held a hundred dead people's secrets and sold most of them. "My family name was in it," he said quietly. "In the breaking. My grandmother showed me. A Quintana drove the sealing stake when they bound the wolf-mother the second time—not the first binding, the *betrayal*, when the bargain was made into a prison. My ancestor did that."

"Yes," Maren said simply. "I know the name Quintana. Every keeper knew it, and prayed it would end." She met his eyes. "It hasn't ended. It's sitting in my chair asking me questions."

The fire popped. Outside, wind came down off the passes and dragged its fingers across the turf roof, and under it all, faint as memory, the bell-hum of the drowned dead went on.

"There are three bones," Hazel said at last. "The grandmother's bones said scattered. Do you know where?"

For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then Maren leaned forward, and the firelight moved in her grey eyes like weather over water.

"One," she said. "One lies where the Church tried to drown it and couldn't. When the Inquisition came to Greyfell, eighty years ago, the last keeper of this valley carried the bone of the Wood's grief to the chapel on the point, thinking holy ground would hide it better than any vault. The Inquisitors burned the chapel with her inside it. But the ground gave way—some say the Wood pulled it down itself—and the whole chapel slid into the lake, with the bone and the keeper and the bell." She nodded at the cracked wooden bell above her hearth. "Her sister cast that. It hasn't rung since the night the chapel went under. Some nights I swear I can almost hear it, down there, ringing anyway."

Hazel felt the cold come into him that had nothing to do with the season. He thought of the hum under the water. He had assumed it was ghosts. Perhaps it was. Perhaps it was also something else—a congregation, still singing, still keeping the bone company in the dark.

"How deep?"

"Fifty feet to the roofline. The door faces north. The keeper's bones will be just inside; she sat down in the doorway and waited for the water, and the water respected her for it." Maren's voice flattened. "Bone-carver, listen to me. That lake has killed every diver who's tried for the chapel since—treasure-seekers, priests, fools. Not because the water is cruel. Because the dead in that chapel are *loyal*, and they don't know the difference between a thief and a pilgrim anymore. You'll have to make them know."

"I make my living being understood by the dead."

"Then you may live through tomorrow." She studied him a moment longer, then glanced at the sleeping boy, and her voice gentled into something almost like pity. "But understand this before you go under. The other two bones—you'll find them. Grief calls to grief, and the Wood will lead you if you let it. And then comes the part nobody survives intact. The covenant can be reforged. The old rites say so plainly. But the covenant demands a price from whoever reforges it, and the price is never coin, and it is never blood spilled unwillingly. It asks for what you treasure most, given freely, at the heart of the Wood, under the full moon. And it does not tell you beforehand what that will be. It only tells you *after*—when your hands are empty and the bargain is sealed."

"That's not a covenant. That's a trap."

"That's a vow," Maren said. "They're shaped the same. The difference is only whether you love what you're swearing to." She reached up and took the cracked wooden bell from its hook, turned it in her brown fingers, and held it out to him. "Take this. When you're down in the dark and the dead won't hear your voice, show them the bell. Her sister's bell. Let them remember that somebody outside the water kept faith for forty years."

Hazel took it. It weighed almost nothing and more than he could carry.

Behind him, in the alcove, the boy stirred and sat up—not waking, Hazel realized, but *listening*, his head cocked toward the lake, toward the hum beneath the ice. His lips moved, shaping soundless syllables in that language older than kingdoms, and whatever he said, the hum seemed to answer, rising for a moment like a choir catching its cue before settling back into its long submerged hymn.

Maren crossed herself with an old rite, thumb to brow to breastbone, leftward, the forbidden way.

"They know he's coming," she murmured. "Rest tonight, bone-carver. Tomorrow you dive for the grief of the Weeping Wood—and pray to whatever's left to pray to that you surface knowing what it cost you to try."

Hazel sat down by the fire and turned the broken bell over in his hands. Through the little window, past the frost ferns on the glass, the lake lay black and patient, and somewhere beneath it, fifty feet down in the cold and the dark, a drowned chapel held its drowned congregation, and a bone cut from the body of a betrayed god waited to be picked up by the descendant of the man who helped put it there.

He did not sleep. But sometime in the deepest hour of the night, the boy padded over and sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and together they watched the fire burn down—and for the first time since the knocking on his door, Hazel felt something that was not fear.

It felt almost like being expected.
