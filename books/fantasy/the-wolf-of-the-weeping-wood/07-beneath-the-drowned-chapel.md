## 7. Beneath the Drowned Chapel

The lake at Greyfell's foot was the color of old pewter, and it did not freeze.

Hazel had known cold lakes all his life—Thornfell ponds that skinned over by autumn's end, thick enough by midwinter to bear a cart. This one refused. A skin of ice formed at the reedy margins each night and melted by morning, as though the water itself were warm with something, or unwilling, and the villagers of the drowned parish below would not speak of it except to cross themselves and look away toward the mountains where the chapel bell tower still broke the surface like a drowned man's last reaching finger.

"They say there's a village down there," Rowan had signed to him that morning, in the small language of hands they were building between themselves. The boy had learned perhaps thirty words of Hazel's tongue now, spoken aloud in a voice rusty and strange as a door long unopened, but his hands remained quicker, surer. "Before Greyfell. The water took it."

"The water took it," Hazel agreed, and did not say what Maren had told him by candlelight: that the water had been *sent*, in a manner of speaking, when the chapel's founders sought to hide something holy beneath a hundred feet of silence and stone. That the drowning had not been an accident of weather but an act of desperate devotion—the whole valley given up, fields and graves and christened bells, so that no profane hand could ever reach what lay beneath the altar.

He knelt at the shore now and unpacked his tools on a flat stone: cord, a whale-oil lamp in its brass cage, a knife, a lump of pine pitch, and the bones.

He had brought bones because he always brought bones. Not for luck—Hazel did not believe in luck; he believed in the dead, which was worse—but because a bone-carver who walked into danger without them was a man walking into a dark room without his hands. And Maren's warning rang in him still: *the covenant demands a price from whoever reforges it.* If he was going to pay a price, he wanted witnesses. He wanted the dead to see.

Rowan crouched beside him, barefoot on stones that should have bitten cold into the boy's flesh and never did. Hazel had stopped marveling at it. Mostly. In the grey light the boy looked carved rather than born, all clean lines and patient stillness, watching the lake the way other children watched fires.

"You don't have to come down," Hazel said.

Rowan shook his head once. Then he pointed at the broken bell tower, and made the sign they'd settled on for *waiting*: both hands open, palms up, lifted slightly, as if offering something invisible to the sky.

*They are waiting,* he'd said around the fire in the whiteout, and Hazel had not asked who *they* were, because some part of him already knew, and knowing was heavy enough without saying it aloud.

* * *

The dead of Greyfell began screaming before his head went under.

That was the thing about drowned places that no living diver ever understood: water carried voices better than air. On land, the dead came to Hazel thin and distant, whispers through wool. Underwater, they were everywhere at once, pressing against his ears like hands. He had dived rivers before—for smugglers' caches, for a drowned courier's satchel once—and every dive was a battle not to listen, because listening underwater was drowning by another name.

Greyfell was not a river. Greyfell was a graveyard with pretensions of being a lake.

He went down the old way, weighted belt and braided-hair breathing cord tied off at the bell tower's submerged arch, the lamp burning in its cage and throwing trembling gold across the water. Cold took him first, a fist closing around his chest, squeezing the breath he'd banked in his lungs down to a hard little coal. Then the voices took him second.

*Cold. So cold. Where are the bells? Why don't the bells ring?*

*My daughter. My daughter was in the mill house. Someone tell me about my daughter.*

*It rises. It rises when the moon is wrong.*

*Stranger. Stranger in the water. Stranger, go back—*

He shut his eyes against the lamp-light and the ghosts together and pulled himself hand over hand along the arch, down into the body of the drowned church. The nave opened below him like the ribcage of something enormous, roof beams furred with pale weed, a crucifix of black wood still hanging above an altar furred with more. Fish hung in the shadows, motionless, watching him with the flat indifference of fish. Silt drifted up in slow clouds wherever his boots touched, and in the drifting silt he could see them—bones. Not scattered the way riverbones scatter. Arranged. Pews still held their seated congregations, skeletons slumped in attitudes of prayer or despair, and every skull in the building was turned toward the altar.

Every single one. As if, in the final moment before the water closed over the valley, the whole parish had turned to watch something happen at the front of the church.

Hazel's lungs tightened. He made himself count, the smuggler's discipline, four counts in, eight counts held, six out through the nose—a trick he'd learned from a dead pearl-diver named Ilsa whose bones he'd carved one hungry winter, and who had taught him her trade out of gratitude or boredom. The counting steadied him. The dead murmured their grief around him, and he let it wash past, looking for the altar.

The covenant-bone, Maren had said, was set into the altar itself, mortared into the stone like a keystone, disguised among ordinary relics. *A rib,* she'd said. *A rib of the first wolf, cut away when the binding was made. The priests thought burying it under a hundred feet of consecrated water would keep it safe from the wolves, and safe from men.* Her mouth had gone bitter then. *Men are never safe from anything. We only ever make hiding places for the things we've already decided to take.*

The altar rose out of the silt haze ahead, black stone streaked white with mineral bloom. Hazel swam to it, boots finding the altar step, and put his lamp close.

And the screaming started.

Not the low grief-murmur of the congregation. This was new, sudden, a wall of sound that hit him like cold water down the collar: dozens of voices at once, the seated dead rising in him all together—

*GO BACK*

*—it knows you're here—it felt you—*

*—the wolf who weeps, the wolf who weeps, do not wake the wolf who weeps—*

*—we sealed it in, we gave our valley, we gave our bells, we gave our CHILDREN, do not undo what we gave—*

*STRANGER. STRANGER. THE STONE IS NOT EMPTY. THE STONE HAS NEVER BEEN EMPTY—*

Hazel's hands were shaking so badly the lamp light shivered wildly across the altar face. He gritted his teeth. *I know,* he wanted to tell them. *I know it isn't empty. That's why I've come.* But there was no talking underwater, not to the living and certainly not to the dead, and so he bent to the work instead, fingers finding the mortar seams around the inset reliquary panel, chisel from his sleeve, tapping, prying, the small bright agony of effort in a body already starved for air.

The panel shifted. Grated. Came free in a rush of released silt.

Behind it was a cavity lined in lead, and in the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, something long and curved and yellow-white that was not any animal Hazel knew. It was too large for a wolf's rib and too slender for a bear's, bowed like a drawn bow, and when his bare fingers closed around it through the torn cloth, the voices *stopped*.

All of them. Every ghost in Greyfell went silent at once, a silence so total it roared, and in that roaring silence Hazel heard—felt—understood—one voice only, vast and slow and deep as the lakebed itself, coming not from the dead but from the bone in his hand:

*THIEF.*

His vision sparked. His chest was iron. He kicked for the ascent with the bone thrust inside his coat, up along the bell rope's drowned length, up through the arch, the cold peeling his strength away layer by layer, and the last thing he heard below him, fading, was that same vast voice, patient as winter:

*We remember Quintana. We will remember you.*

* * *

He broke the surface gasping, and Rowan's hands were on his collar before he'd finished coughing, hauling him onto the shingle with strength no child should have, and for a moment Hazel lay on the stones and shook and laughed breathlessly at the sky because he had done it, he had *done* it—

Then his hand found the bone inside his coat, still cold as the lake's heart, and the laughter died.

Up close, in daylight, he could feel it now the way he felt all bones, except this one did not whisper. It *purred*. Low and continuous, like a great cat half-asleep, like a fire just banked, and threaded through the purring came images that were not his own: green depths and running shapes, moonlight through branches, chains of iron and words of power, a wound that had never closed in three hundred years.

And underneath the purring, faintly, unbearably, weeping. A sound like wind through a hollow reed. Like someone crying very far away who has been crying for a very long time and has forgotten anything else exists.

*The wolf who weeps,* the drowned dead had screamed. He had thought they meant a warning about some monster sleeping under the altar.

They hadn't meant a warning. They'd meant a description. The bone itself was weeping. Had been weeping since the day it was cut away, down here in the lead-lined dark, for three hundred years.

"Rowan." His voice came out wrecked, half-water. The boy crouched over him, snow-melt dripping from Hazel's hair onto the stones. "Can you hear it?"

Rowan tilted his head. Listened. Then his small hands moved, careful and grave:

*She is sad. She is glad you came. She is afraid you are him.*

"*Him?*"

Rowan pointed at Hazel's chest. At his name. At the blood under the name.

Quintana. *We remember Quintana.*

Hazel got up. His legs nearly failed him getting up. He stood on the grey shore with a god's rib aching against his ribs and looked north along the lake, toward the pass, toward the Wood he could not yet see and could feel anyway, the way a tooth feels a storm coming.

"We move," he said. "Now. Before—"

Hoofbeats.

They came around the lake's eastern shoulder at a canter, six riders in Crown grey, and even at this distance Hazel knew the livery, knew the black-and-silver badge of the Inquisitorial office, knew the lean purposeful way they spread along the shoreline without a wasted order. Veyra herself was not among them—he'd have felt her, he realized; after two weeks of flight he could feel her now like weather—but her men rode like her shadow cast forward, and the foremost rider lifted a spyglass, swept the shore, and stopped.

Stopped on the two figures standing wet and small at the water's edge, with nowhere left to run but up.

"Trees," Hazel breathed. "Rowan—trees, *now*."

They ran. Behind them a horn sounded, one long note that rolled across the water and came back off the mountains doubled, and ahead the treeline of the lower forest waited, dark and dense and—Hazel understood suddenly, with the bone pulsing cold against his heart—not empty either.

Nothing, anymore, was empty. Everything listened. Everything remembered.

And somewhere behind them, riding hard along the shore, Veyra's men were about to learn that the drowned parish of Greyfell had guarded its secret for three hundred years, and had opinions about thieves.

The lake, very quietly, began to rise.
