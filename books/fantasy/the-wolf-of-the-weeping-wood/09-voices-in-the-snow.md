## 9. Voices in the Snow

The storm came down on them like a held breath finally released.

Hazel had weathered whiteouts before—had walked through them half-blind on smuggler's roads where a single misstep meant a hundred fathoms of empty air—but he had never known one like this. The snow did not fall so much as fill the world, erasing sky and stone alike until there was nothing left but the grey-white roar and the small circle of himself and the boy. He could not see his own boots. He could not see the boy three paces ahead, only hear the faint crunch-crunch-crunch of small feet that made no prints in the drifts, because of course they didn't; he had stopped being surprised by that somewhere past Greyfell.

He could not even hear the dead.

That was the worst of it. All his life, the voices had been with him—a murmur under every silence, bone whispering to bone across any distance—and now the storm swallowed them too. The mountains here were old enough to be crowded with ghosts, shepherds and soldiers and smugglers who'd misjudged a pass, all of them usually clamoring at the edges of his mind like beggars at a kitchen door. But in this whiteness there was nothing. Only wind. Only the boy's footsteps, and his own heart hammering out a rhythm that said *lost, lost, lost*.

"Hold up!" he shouted, and heard his voice die two feet from his lips, strangled by snow.

He groped forward and caught the boy's shoulder. The child turned, unhurried, snowflakes landing in his dark hair and refusing to melt—or rather, melting and refreezing into tiny crowns of ice, so that he glittered faintly, like something carved rather than born. His eyes were calm. They were always calm. Hazel had come to think of that calm as a kind of shore he could swim toward.

"We can't go on," Hazel shouted. "The pass bends east here—I know it does, I've run this road a dozen times—but if I can't find the bend we'll walk straight off the shoulder and neither of us will feel a thing until we stop feeling everything."

The boy tilted his head. Then he raised one hand—not toward the east, where Hazel's memory insisted the trail bent, but north, into the teeth of the wind, where nothing sensible lay except a cliff called the Widow's Tooth and the frozen lake beneath it.

"No," Hazel said. "No, lad. That way's death."

The hand stayed raised. Patient. Certain.

"I've been guiding people through these mountains since before you were born," Hazel said, and immediately felt the absurdity of arguing seniority with a creature who was older than kingdoms, older than the language Hazel cursed in, older perhaps than the mountains themselves. The dead had taught him the passes—the smugglers' corpses, the shepherd women, the old mail-carriers whose finger-bones he'd carried in his coat for years. Every trick he knew had been bought from a ghost. And now the ghosts were silent, and the only living thing in all this white that seemed to know where it was going stood three feet tall in bare feet.

He thought about Maren's warning, spoken over the last embers of her fire: *the covenant demands a price from whoever reforges it.* He thought about Veyra's riders somewhere behind them, and the bridge groaning into the gorge, and how far trust could stretch before it snapped.

Then he thought about the boy's hand, still lifted, still pointing north, unwavering as a compass needle that answered to something truer than iron.

"Damn it," Hazel said softly, without heat. It had become almost a prayer between them, that phrase. He took the boy's mittened hand—it wasn't a mitten really, just a scrap of wool Hazel had wrapped around those impossible fingers—and let himself be led.

* * *

For an hour, maybe two—they might have been walking ten minutes or a full night, time went strange in the white—he followed.

It was the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than lying to Veyra's face while she smiled at him like a cat deciding whether the mouse was worth the trouble, harder than diving into the drowned chapel with the screaming dead all around him. Because every instinct he owned screamed against it. His body knew these mountains the way a tongue knows its own teeth, and his body said *left*, and the boy turned *right*. His body said *downslope, follow the grade*, and the boy climbed when climbing made no sense at all, hands pulling Hazel upward over wind-carved ice where no path had ever been cut.

And each time, when the whiteout thinned for a heartbeat—a tear in the storm, a glimpse of the world—Hazel saw what would have met them on the other choice. Once it was a cornice of snow hanging over emptiness, so perfectly blended into the slope that even his smuggler's eye would have walked onto it without a qualm. Once it was a crevasse breathing cold air upward like an open mouth. Once, most terrible, it was nothing at all—just the ordinary-looking slope he'd have taken, and then a gust peeled the surface snow away for a moment and showed him the blue-black glass of glacier ice beneath, steep as a roof, ending in a fall that would have tumbled them both a thousand feet into the gorge.

After the third such revelation, he stopped doubting.

There is a particular kind of surrender in letting a child lead you through mortal danger. It strips something away. Hazel had spent his whole adult life being the one who knew—who knew the passes, knew the dead, knew which lord paid in honest silver and which paid in knives. His gift had made him necessary, and necessity had been the wall he hid behind: *I am needed, therefore I am safe, therefore I need never be simply a man standing in the snow with no idea where he is.*

Now he was exactly that. A man in the snow, holding a child's hand, blind, guided by faith alone. And underneath the terror, quiet as a coal buried in ash, something in him was—relieved. He hated that he was relieved. He examined the feeling the way he'd examine a strange carving, turning it over, looking for the flaw in it, and found none. It was simply true. It was good, after forty years, to put the weight down, even if only for an hour, even if only because he had no choice.

The wind changed. He felt it before he understood it—at first just a softening, the roar dropping to a mutter, then the flakes coming down fat and slow instead of sideways. The boy tugged him left, then down a short steep pitch Hazel's feet remembered only after they'd descended it—the back side of the Widow's Tooth, the hollow behind it where the wind couldn't reach. And there, in the lee of the cliff, half-hidden behind a curtain of frost-feathered stone, stood a hut.

A shepherd's bothy, ancient, its turf roof furred white, its door held shut by nothing but habit. Smoke-blackened stones. A hearth. Dry wood stacked inside by some long-dead shepherd whose ghost, Hazel realized with a start, was not there to greet him. Nowhere in these mountains were the dead silent. Except near the boy.

"How did you know?" Hazel asked, kneeling to unlace his frozen boots. His fingers wouldn't work properly. "How did you know this was here? Have you walked these passes before? In another life? Another—" He gestured vaguely, helplessly. "Another age?"

The boy crouched beside him and, with great seriousness, took over the lacing. His small fingers were deft. He pulled the knots tight, tucked the ends in, patted Hazel's ankle once, decisively, as though certifying a piece of finished work.

While Hazel coaxed the fire alive—flint and steel and thirty years of practice, sparks leaping into a nest of dry grass until at last a flame stood up shivering and began, greedily, to eat—the boy fetched wood without being asked, chose each piece with odd deliberation, arranged the pile by length as if setting a table. When the fire was roaring and the hut had gone gold and warm around them, Hazel dug in his pack and found the last of Maren's black bread, hard as slate, and the heel of smoked goat cheese wrapped in waxcloth. They ate in silence, close to the flames.

The boy ate slowly, formally, holding the bread in both hands like something sacred. Watching him, Hazel felt the ache again—the one he'd been carrying since the doorstep on the coldest night of the year, since a mute child had appeared out of the dark barefoot in the snow. The boy had never spoken. Not a word, not a cry, not even a scream when Veyra's riders had chased them to the bridge. Hazel had built whole conversations out of nods and gestures and the boy's unnervingly expressive silences, and he had told himself that was enough.

But tonight, warmed through, watching firelight move on that ancient face in a child's frame, Hazel wanted—selfishly, humanly—to *hear* him. Just once.

"You know things," Hazel said quietly. "You knew the pass. You knew this hut. You knew my door, that night, out of all the doors in Thornfell." He fed a split log to the fire. Sparks swarmed upward. "Maren says you're a seal. A living seal on an old covenant. She said the Wood put you... wherever you came from... and sent you to me." He laughed, short and bitter. "Sent you to me. Of all people. To the blood of Quintana."

At the name, the boy looked up sharply. Firelight caught his eyes and for a moment they were not a child's eyes at all; they were deep and layered, like looking down a well and seeing, far below, another sky.

Hazel held that look and didn't flinch. "I'm not going to pretend anymore," he said. "Not with you. Whatever your people know about my family—about what my ancestor did to yours—I'd rather hear it from you than from the bones. The bones always tell it slanted. Dead folk hold their grudges the same as the living, only longer." He drew a breath. "So talk to me. If you can. However you can."

The boy set down his bread. He looked at the fire for a long moment, and Hazel watched him gather something the way a carver gathers himself before the first cut—shoulders settling, breath drawn, the whole body becoming intent.

When the boy spoke, his voice was rusted from disuse, thin and cracked, and each word arrived separately, carried with visible effort, like stones hauled up from a deep place:

"They—are—waiting."

Three words. Hazel's whole body went still. The fire popped. Snow ticked softly against the shuttered window, and outside, the storm moaned over the Widow's Tooth like something mourning.

"Who?" Hazel whispered. "Who's waiting?"

The boy's brow furrowed with the strain of it. He pressed his lips together, tried again, and this time the words came easier, smoother, as though the channel were clearing:

"The wolves. In the Wood." A pause. The boy touched his own chest, then pointed at Hazel, then at the ground between them. "They sent me. To find you." His voice was gaining strength with every sentence, shedding centuries of rust. "They are waiting for what your family owes."

The words landed in Hazel's chest like a dropped stone hitting the bottom of a well. *What your family owes.* He had known—had known since his grandmother's bones had whispered the truth to him in his workshop, since the name Quintana had surfaced in the broken covenant like a corpse surfacing in a lake. But knowing a thing in the safety of your own walls and hearing it spoken aloud in a child's rusted voice, in a firelit hut at the top of the world, are different countries entirely.

"They sent *you*," Hazel said. "To the man whose blood staked them into the ground." He shook his head slowly. "Why? Why me? Why not a priestess, why not—anyone better than me?"

The boy considered this with grave attention. Then he reached out and touched Hazel's hand—his right hand, the carving hand, the one that heard the dead—and held it in both of his small ones, warming it against the firelight.

"Because you listen," the boy said.

Just that. Two words. But Hazel felt them go all the way through him, past the shame and the denial, past forty years of hiding his gift like a disease, down to the place where the gift actually lived—which had never, he realized, been in his ears at all. It lived in the listening. In the choosing to hear what others refused to hear. The betrayal in his bloodline had begun, his grandmother's bones told him, with men who stopped listening to the Wood, who decided its voices were inconvenient. And whatever else the Quintanas had become—smugglers, liars, keepers of shameful secrets—every one of them down the generations had kept one strange, useless, dangerous habit: they listened.

"Ulva," Hazel said, testing the wolf-mother's name in his mouth. The boy flinched at it, a small sharp motion, and Hazel understood: the name hurt to hear. Old iron hurts even to be named. "She gave us until the full moon. Bring the bones, or you die as the seal breaks." He looked at the boy—this small, patient, ancient creature sitting cross-legged by a shepherd's fire—and something in his chest twisted and refused to sit still. "I won't let that happen. You understand me? Whatever the price is—and Maren says there's always a price—I'll pay mine before I let them break you open."

The boy studied him for a long moment. Then, very deliberately, he leaned forward and rested his head against Hazel's shoulder—the first time he had initiated touch, the first time the calm had cracked into something warmer. He weighed almost nothing. He smelled of snow and pine and something older underneath, the scent of deep forest, of sap and shadow.

They sat like that while the fire burned low and the storm spent its fury against the mountain. Hazel found himself talking, quietly, the way he talked to his carvings—about his grandmother, about the first time he'd heard a dead voice at seven years old and screamed for a day, about his father selling their name and moving them town to town whenever Hazel's talent showed. The boy listened the way Hazel listened to bones: completely, without judgment, taking everything in.

Somewhere past midnight, the boy slept—truly slept, breath slow and even, looking at last like the child his body claimed to be. Hazel sat watch, feeding the fire, and let himself plan. Two days' hard travel south to the Weeping Wood's edge. The second bone waiting in the barrow his grandmother's riddling verses had described. And beyond that, the third—

He stopped. His hand had drifted, without thinking, to the boy's wooden wolf-token, still hanging at the child's throat, the little carving Hazel had touched on that first night and heard thousands of layered voices singing through.

*The third covenant-bone,* whispered a voice that was not a voice, a thought that arrived wearing the cadence of the drowned dead of Greyfell chapel, *is not buried in any barrow. It walks, and eats, and sleeps beside you, and it is warmer than any bone in the ground.*

Hazel sat very still, staring into the fire, and did not sleep again.

* * *

Dawn came clean and merciless, the storm blown out overnight, the world remade in blinding white and blue shadow. From the mouth of the hollow, the southern horizon showed clear all the way to the distant darkness of tree line—a smudge like ink spilled on paper, miles away and waiting.

The boy stood beside him in the new snow, blinking in the light. He looked smaller by daylight, younger, and somehow more resolute.

"They are waiting," he said again. Not a warning this time. Almost a comfort. Almost a promise.

Hazel shouldered his pack, took the boy's hand, and turned south toward the Wood—toward Ulva and her scarred patience, toward the barrow and his ancestor's begging ghost, toward the price he could feel circling him already like a wolf he couldn't yet see.

Behind them, the wind rose once over the Widow's Tooth, and in its voice Hazel almost—almost—heard the dead returning, murmuring at the edge of the silence like a tide coming back in.
