## 10. The Weeping Wood's Edge

They smelled it before they saw it.

Hazel had spent three days telling himself the sweetness on the wind was rot—some elk carcass melting out from under the snow, some bog breathing its usual breath of decay. He knew every stink the Thornfells could offer. He had catalogued them over fifteen years of guiding men who paid in silver and asked no questions: the copper-and-iron reek of the old mines, the sulfurous belch of the hot springs at Vetch's Hollow, the sweetish miasma of the peat bogs where unwary sheep went down singing. This was none of them.

This was sweeter than all of them and sadder too, like honey left to weep from a broken comb, like incense burned in a chapel where everyone had died mid-prayer.

The boy smelled it too. Rowan—Hazel still thought of him as *the boy*, though the name had begun to stick somewhere behind his ribs—stopped dead on the game trail and lifted his face into the wind with his eyes closed, the way a man lifts his face to rain after a drought. His bare feet, which had never blistered, never blackened, never even reddened in three days of frozen mountain, stood planted in the snow as if the ground had grown up around them.

"What is it?" Hazel asked, though he was beginning to suspect he didn't want the answer.

The boy opened his eyes. He looked at Hazel with that expression that had stopped unnerving Hazel only because Hazel had forced himself to stop thinking about it—the expression of someone very old looking out through a face perhaps eight years old. Then he pointed.

Through the thinning pines ahead, where the slope fell away toward a valley Hazel had never once crossed in all his years of smuggling, there stood a wall of trees.

Not a treeline. A *wall*. The pines and birches of the high country simply stopped, the way a conversation stops when a stranger enters the room, and beyond them rose a forest of a different order altogether—darker, denser, older, the trunks so broad that four men linking hands could not have circled one. The canopy was black-green even at midday. Snow lay thick on the mountainside above it and thin beneath it, as though the forest refused to let winter settle on its shoulders for long.

And from the seam where the two forests met—from every tree along that ragged border, but most of all from the great dark trunks just within—ran slow lines of gold.

Sap. It bled down the bark in long unbroken threads, catching what little light filtered through the clouds, so that the whole edge of the forest gleamed like something freshly cut, like wounds dressed in amber. Here and there the threads pooled at the roots in small glassy beads, freezing half-formed, so that the ground at the forest's feet was strewn with what looked like fallen tears turned to stone.

"The Weeping Wood," Hazel said. Saying it aloud felt like stepping off a ledge.

He had heard the name his whole life, the way you hear the names of things your elders use to frighten children. *Mind the passes. Mind the weather. And whatever you do, mind the Wood.* Smugglers traded stories about it in low voices over bad wine—a forest that ate travelers, trees that walked, wolves the size of horses whose eyes burned like coals. Hazel had always filed such tales where he filed everything else: under *stories*, useful only for frightening clients into paying more.

But the bones had told him otherwise. His grandmother's finger-bones had rattled against each other in their pouch and spoken of a covenant, of a betrayal, of a seal wearing a child's skin—and now here stood the thing itself, bleeding golden tears into the snow, exactly as the dead had said it would.

*They are waiting,* the boy had said by the fire, three nights ago. First full sentence he'd ever given Hazel. Hazel hadn't slept well since.

* * *

They made camp that night a hundred yards back from the border, close enough to watch, far enough—Hazel hoped—to be nothing more than shadows among shadows. He built a small fire in a hollow of stone, using dry wood he'd carried up from below the snowline rather than risk breaking so much as a twig from the dark trees. It felt right to bring his own fuel, the way you brought your own bread into another man's house.

The silence was the first strange thing.

Hazel had lived with voices for thirty-one years. Not madness—Maren had confirmed that much, in her gentle exile's way; not the sickness his father had died raving about—but the actual dead, actual voices, caught in bone and ivory and sometimes, on bad nights, in anything that had ever been part of something living. A chair spoke to him of the tree it had been. A fur cloak murmured of the fox. He had never known true quiet in his waking life. Even now, with the boy asleep beside the fire, curled like a comma with the wooden wolf-token clutched to his chest, Hazel could hear the faint chorus of the mountain itself: the bones of a ptarmigan in an owl's pellet, the last ghost of a shepherd who'd frozen in this pass forty years before, whispering his own name over and over like a prayer that had forgotten its god.

Except—

Hazel sat up straight.

The ptarmigan was silent. The shepherd was gone. The fire's own crackle was just crackle, empty wood, wood with nothing left inside it to say. And when Hazel reached out with the sense he had spent a lifetime trying to cage, the sense he hated and depended on the way a man hates and depends on a lame leg—it met nothing. A blankness. A smooth white silence stretching away toward the dark trees like fresh snow with no footprints in it.

No. Not quite nothing. When he strained toward the Wood itself, he felt something worse than silence. He felt the silence *looking back*. As though the blankness were not absence but appetite—as though the Wood did not lack ghosts but *collected* them, drew them in the way a drain draws water, and everything that had ever died within those shadows was still there, held, swallowed, kept.

The Wood eats ghosts, his grandmother's bones had whispered, weeks ago now, in a voice like dry leaves. That is why the dead will not speak of it. That is why the living must.

Hazel looked at the sleeping boy and understood, finally, why the child had been sent out. Why a seal would be sent walking, barefoot, through the teeth of winter, out of the one place in the world where the dead were devoured. Because the covenant could not be renewed from inside the silence. Someone had to carry the memory out.

"Gods," Hazel said softly, to no one, to the fire. "What am I walking into?"

The fire answered with ordinary, blessedly empty noise.

* * *

The scout came at dawn.

Hazel woke because the cold had changed—not grown colder, but grown *attentive*, the way air feels before lightning. He came up off his bedroll with his knife already in his hand, heart hammering, and found the boy sitting upright beside the dead fire, watching the treeline with an expression of perfect calm.

It stood between two of the great dark trunks, maybe forty yards away.

His first thought, absurdly, was *wolf*. His second was that no wolf ever built had stood like that—upright, weight settled back on its haunches, forepaws loose at its sides in a posture that was unmistakably, terribly patient. It was large as a yearling horse and leaner, its coat neither grey nor black nor brown but all of them at once, shifting the way smoke shifts, so that Hazel's eyes kept losing it against the shadowed bark and finding it again. Its head was broad and heavy-jawed, its ears pricked forward. Its eyes were the color of deep water over deep stone, and they were fixed not on Hazel's knife, not on the boy, but on Hazel himself—with an attention so complete it felt like being flayed.

Hazel did not move. Every instinct he owned screamed at him to run, and underneath that instinct ran a colder, smuggler's instinct: *running marks you as prey. Stillness marks you as something worth considering.*

So he stood still, knife low, breath steaming, and let the creature look at him.

It did not growl. It did not bare its teeth. It did not flee, which somehow frightened Hazel more than either. It simply watched, and in its watching Hazel felt something he had no clean word for. Judgment was close. Weighing was close. It was the feeling he imagined a defendant might have, standing before a tribunal whose language he did not speak, awaiting a sentence pronounced in a tongue older than courts.

*I'm not the one you want,* he almost said. *I'm just the one carrying him.*

But even as he thought it, he knew it was a lie, or at least no longer wholly true. Three weeks ago it had been true—he had taken the boy in out of pity and fear and the simple smuggler's arithmetic of *if the Crown wants him, he's worth protecting or worth selling*, and pity had won only because selling felt like something his grandmother's ghost would haunt him for. But somewhere on the frozen passes, somewhere between the drowned chapel and the whiteout and the small warm weight of the boy asleep against his side, the arithmetic had changed. The boy was not cargo. The boy was not a seal, not a relic, not a task. He was a child who hummed tunelessly while Hazel cooked, who had learned the word *warm* and used it extravagantly, who had pressed the wolf-token into Hazel's hand during the worst night of the storm as if lending him courage the way another child might lend a blanket.

The scout's ears tilted. Just slightly. As though it had heard the thought.

"They're deciding," Hazel breathed, hardly moving his lips. Whether he spoke to the boy or to himself, he couldn't have said.

The boy shook his head slowly. He touched his own chest, then pointed at the wolf-spirit, then made a gesture Hazel had learned on the passes: fingers spread, drawing a circle in the air. *All of us. Everything here.* Then he pointed at the trees, at the weeping sap, at the buried dark of the Wood, and pressed both hands flat together.

*Being judged. All of us. By the whole of it.*

The scout took a single step forward, and the snow did not crunch beneath its paws. That detail slid into Hazel's mind and lodged there, cold and bright: the creature weighed nothing, or the snow loved it, or the snow feared it. Up close he could see scars now—long pale seams through the smoke-dark coat, one crossing the muzzle at an angle that spoke of iron, of blades, of a history written in wounds. Old hatreds. Older griefs.

Its gaze moved from Hazel to the boy and back again, unhurried, thorough, the way a moneylender turns a coin in his fingers before biting it.

Then it did something that undid Hazel entirely.

It lowered its head.

Not a bow—Hazel would not insult them both by calling it that. More a gesture of acknowledgment, formal and grave, the nod one traveler gives another across a dangerous ford: *I see you. I mark you. Pass on, and know that you are known.* And then the smoke-dark shape simply wasn't there—one heartbeat a huge presence filling the space between the trees, the next only shadow and the faint sweet smell of weeping sap, as though the forest had closed around its child like water closing over a stone.

Hazel let out a breath he felt he'd been holding for three days. His knees informed him, somewhat belatedly, that they would like to stop working now.

"It saw us," he said. His voice came out ragged. "It saw *me*. Not him. Me."

The boy was smiling—that rare, crooked, entirely childish smile that made him look his age for once instead of nine centuries old. He pointed at Hazel's chest, then at the treeline, then nodded once, firmly.

*Yes. You. On purpose.*

"Why?" Hazel's laugh came out cracked. "Why would they care about me? I'm nobody. I'm a carver. I carve dead women's secrets into trinkets and sell them to sad daughters. I've stolen from every lord between here and the coast. I'm—" He stopped. His grandmother's bones seemed to rattle in his memory, unasked. *Your blood drove the stake, grandson. Your blood broke the pack.* "I'm the wrong man," he finished quietly. "That's what you're trying to tell me, isn't it? They looked at me and saw *him*. Quintana. My grandfather's grandfather's grandfather, or whatever he was. The one who started all this."

The boy's smile faded into something gentler and sadder. He stepped close—bare feet soundless on the snow—and took Hazel's hand, the scarred carver's hand, the hand that had held the stake's descendant in the form of a carving knife for thirty years. He pressed it flat against his own small chest, over his heart.

Then he pointed at Hazel's chest.

Then he laced his small fingers through Hazel's larger ones and squeezed, and looked up at him with those old, old eyes, and shook his head: *no.*

*You are not only that. Whatever they see—you are not only that.*

Hazel's throat tightened. He looked past the boy's shoulder to the dark wall of the Weeping Wood, where the sap ran gold and slow down ten thousand wounded trunks, where everything dead was kept and everything living was measured, where something vast and patient had just decided—not to kill them, which would have been simple—but to *let them walk in*.

That was the part that frightened him most, he realized. Not the teeth, not the iron-scarred hunter, not the hungry silence. It was the invitation. You did not get invited into places like that unless the place wanted something from you. And things that wanted something from you always, always collected the debt.

"All right," he said aloud, because saying it aloud made it real, and making it real was the only kind of courage he had ever possessed. "All right. We go in."

He kicked snow over the ashes of his fire, shouldered his pack, checked the first covenant-bone wrapped in oilcloth against his spine—cold as a knuckle of ice even through the layers—and took the boy's hand.

Together they crossed the last hundred yards of snow, and stepped over the border where the tears of trees had frozen into amber beads, and passed into the Weeping Wood.

Behind them, faint as a held breath released, the mountain's dead began—very softly—to sing again.

Ahead, the darkness waited to learn their names.
