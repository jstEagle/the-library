## 11. The Wolf Who Weeps

They came for him in the hour before dawn, when the frost on the tent-flaps had gone grey and the boy's breathing was the only warm sound left in the world.

Hazel woke to silence—not the ordinary silence of sleeping men, but a silence with weight to it, a silence like held breath. The dead did not speak here, at the Wood's edge; he had learned that much in three days of walking beneath trees that bled pale sap down their black bark. But something had replaced the silence, and it pressed against his ears like deep water.

He crawled from the shelter of the leaning stone where they had made their camp and saw her.

She stood at the tree line where the Thornfell scree gave way to the Weeping Wood proper, and she was larger than any wolf he had ever seen—larger than any wolf that had ever lived, perhaps, because no living wolf carried that many winters in its shoulders. Her coat was the colour of ash and old snow, streaked through with silver that might have been age or might have been moonlight caught and kept. And across her body, crossing her flank, her shoulder, the long line of her throat, ran scars of a kind Hazel knew too well: straight-edged, deliberate, rust-brown at their seams even after all these centuries.

Old iron. Forged iron, drawn slow across living flesh.

The boy was already outside, standing barefoot on the frozen ground as he always did, unbothered by cold, his wooden wolf-token clutched against his chest. He was looking up at the great wolf the way a child looks at a mother coming home from a long absence—with joy and terror in equal measure, and neither one winning.

"She's here," Hazel said quietly, though the boy could not hear him. It didn't matter. The boy nodded anyway.

* * *

Ulva crossed the open ground without hurry. Each footfall made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than sound—a creature that size should have shaken the stones, should have announced herself to the whole mountain, and instead she arrived as grief arrives: suddenly, completely, and with no way to refuse her.

Up close, the scars were worse. They were not only on her hide. When Hazel met her eyes—amber, ancient, ringed in the same ash-grey as her coat—he understood that the iron had been drawn across things deeper than skin. There were wounds in her gaze that had never closed. She looked at him the way the dead looked at him when they had waited too long to say what they needed to say.

"You are Quintana's blood."

Her voice did not come through his ears. It rose out of the ground, out of the roots beneath the snow, out of the sap weeping down the trees behind her—a voice made of everything growing and everything buried. The boy flinched at it, or perhaps at the way Hazel staggered.

"My name is Hazel," he managed. "I carve bones. I guide smugglers over the passes. I'm nobody's blood but my own."

It was a lie, and they both knew it. Ulva's lips peeled back from teeth as long as his fingers—not quite a snarl. Something older and wearier than a snarl.

"Your grandmother's bones told you otherwise." The amber eyes did not waver. "You sat up three nights with them. You heard what they remembered. You burned the first one rather than hear it twice."

Hazel's stomach turned over. Three nights ago, two hundred miles south, in a room with the shutters barred. No one had seen. No one could have seen.

"The Wood hears its own," Ulva said. "And you have been walking inside my Wood since you crossed the tree line, bone-carver. Everything you carry, I can smell. Including your shame. It smells like wet ash."

The boy stepped between them then—small, barefoot, absurdly brave—and pressed his wooden token against Ulva's scarred foreleg. The great wolf lowered her head and breathed once along the boy's hair, and some of the terrible weight went out of the morning.

"He is dying," Ulva said, to Hazel now, over the boy's bowed head. "Did you know? The seal runs through him like a vein. Every day the covenant stays broken, it draws him thinner. He was a vessel once, whole and willing. Now he is a cup with a crack in it, and what leaks out of him leaks into me and mine." Her voice roughened. "Rage, little carver. It leaks rage. Do you understand what it means when a thing like me cannot control her own rage?"

Hazel thought of the scout they had seen at the forest border—the wolf-spirit that had watched them for an hour without moving, neither attacking nor fleeing, its eyes burning like coals banked under ash. He thought about how long an hour of stillness must cost a starving thing.

"I need to know," he said. "What happened here. Not what the Crown teaches. Not what my grandmother's bones hinted. All of it."

Something moved behind Ulva's eyes—surprise, perhaps, that he would ask to be shown. Or perhaps it was only hunger, the hunger of a creature who had carried a story alone for six hundred years because everyone who should have heard it was either dead or complicit.

"Then look," she said. "But know that looking has a price. You will carry what I show you until you die. That is how memory works among my kind. We do not forget. We only learn to stand it."

"I've stood worse," Hazel said, and reached out, and laid his scarred carver's hand against her scarred brow.

* * *

The world went white, then went away entirely, and Hazel fell into memory as into cold water.

He was standing in the Weeping Wood, but it was not this Weeping Wood. The trees were taller and glad, their leaves moving in wind that smelled of summer, and there was no sap bleeding down their trunks—only light, green-gold, falling through the canopy in shifting coins. Wolves walked openly between the trees, and they were vast and shining, their coats carrying the colours of weather: storm-grey, dawn-rose, the deep blue of midnight in winter. Men and women walked among them without fear. A child rode on a wolf's back, shrieking with laughter. At the centre of it all stood a stone—not broken, not yet—a tall standing stone carved over every inch with interlocking script, human hands and wolf paws wound together around its length, and it hummed with a power that Hazel felt in his teeth, in the marrow the way he felt the voices of the dead.

The Covenant of Thornfell. Alive. Whole. He was seeing it as it had been.

*Two peoples, one promise,* Ulva's voice said through the memory, everywhere and nowhere. *We kept the high passes clear of the old hungry dark. They kept our seasons with song and offering. Neither realm could be taken while the other stood beside it. Six hundred years, bone-carver. Longer than any kingdom you have ever heard named.*

The scene shifted. Seasons spun past like a wheel thrown from a cart. And then the light changed.

Hazel was in a war-camp now, and the smell hit him before anything else—pig-fat and pitch and forged iron, hundreds of pounds of it, iron being hammered into shapes that hurt to look at. Men in the livery of a crown so old its heraldry had been forgotten knelt around fires, sharpening. Priests in grey moved among them with censers, and the smoke they burned made the wolves' names impossible to speak.

And at the centre of the camp, giving orders in a voice like a blade being drawn slowly, stood a man with Hazel's own face.

Not his face exactly. But the same heavy brow, the same deep-set eyes, the same hands—broad, scarred, precise, a craftsman's hands. The man wore a carver's apron over his armour, and hanging at his belt, wrapped in oilcloth, was a stake of black iron as long as a man's forearm, worked along its length with the same interlocking script as the standing stone—but reversed. Unmade. A key filed into a knife.

"Aldric Quintana," Hazel said aloud, though there was no air in the memory to carry it.

*Your ancestor,* Ulva said. *The finest bone-carver of his generation. The wolves trusted him above all men, because he could hear them as you hear the dead—as I hear the earth. They gave him the words of the covenant to study, so that he might renew the carving each generation. He was the only living soul who knew how to unmake what he could make.*

The memory lurched forward, and Hazel wanted to close his eyes and found that he had no eyes to close. He was at the standing stone on a night of hard frost. Fires ringed the clearing. Aldric Quintana knelt at the stone's base with his chisel and his mallet, working by firelight, and the wolves came running through the trees too late—they always came too late; the priests had sung the wards that slowed them, had salted the ground so their paws blistered—and Hazel watched, forced to watch, as his own face, his own hands, drove the black stake into the heart of the covenant-stone.

The sound it made was not a sound. It was the opposite of a sound, a tearing-out, and every wolf within a hundred miles screamed at once, and the screaming did not stop for—

The memory folded. Changed. Now the wolves were bound, pressed flat into forms of flesh and fur far smaller than their spirits, chained to the borders of a shrunken Wood, and the trees wept sap that stank of grief. Aldric Quintana stood before the sealed stone, an old man now, alone, and he was weeping, and he was carving one final thing into the stone's broken base: three lines of script that Hazel recognized with a sickening jolt, because he had dreamed them since childhood without ever knowing why.

*A debt unpaid. A blood owed. A hearing given back.*

"He tried to leave a door open," Hazel whispered. "At the end. He tried to—"

*Too little,* Ulva said, and her voice shook the memory like a storm shakes a window. *Six hundred years too little. Every winter the seal drains us further. Every year our young are born smaller, duller, angrier. We were guardians of the high places, bone-carver, and now we are prisoners who guard our own cell. Your ancestor took our eternity and spent it. And now his blood walks into my Wood carrying the last seal in a cracked cup of a child, and asks me to believe the debt can be paid.*

The white light returned, then fell away, and Hazel was on his knees in the frost with blood running from his nose, and the boy's small hands were gripping his shoulders, and the sky overhead was going from grey to the colour of old pewter.

* * *

He wiped his face with his sleeve and found his voice somewhere near his boots. "The remaining bones. My grandmother showed me two more—one in a barrow, one—" He stopped. He had not yet understood where the third lay, and some instinct, old and animal, told him not to say what he half-suspected aloud.

"Two remain hidden beyond my reach," Ulva said. "Sealed by Aldric's own craft, so that only his blood might find them. That is the cruelty of it—or the mercy, depending on the hour. The betrayer's line is the only key." She circled him once, slowly, and the frost creaked beneath paws that still made no sound. "So hear the terms, Quintana's blood, and hear them once."

She stopped before him. This close, he could see that her scars moved when she breathed, and that beneath the oldest of them, faint as a watermark, the interlocking script of the covenant still showed—the iron had cut her so deeply that it had written on the bone.

"The moon waxes tonight. In nine days it stands full above the heart of this Wood, and the seal thins to nothing, as it does each month, and each month it costs us more to hold the breaking back. If the covenant-bones are not set upon the stone by moonrise on that night, the seal fails entire." Her gaze slid to the boy. "And the seal does not fail gently. It breaks the way a dam breaks. Everything stored in it—every century of bound rage—will pour out through the last vessel it holds. Through him."

The boy did not step back. He lifted his chin and looked at Ulva, and then—at Hazel, deliberately, unmistakably—and touched his own chest, and shook his head once. *Don't do it for me.* Or perhaps: *I am not afraid.*

Hazel had faced down Inquisitors' riders on a collapsing bridge. He had held a drowning woman's skull in his hands while the lake screamed through it. None of it had prepared him for a mute child telling him, wordlessly, not to be afraid on his behalf.

"And if I bring them?" Hazel asked. "If I set all three bones on the stone—what does the covenant ask then?"

Ulva was silent for a long moment. Wind moved through the weeping trees, and the sap caught the greying light and ran like tears down black bark.

"That," she said finally, "is a question you are not yet ready to hear answered. Bring the bones. Bring the boy alive. Come to the heart of the Wood by the full moon, and you will learn the price when it can no longer be avoided." She turned away, then paused, her great scarred head swinging back toward him. "One thing more, bone-carver, and mark it well. You are not the only one hunting the bones now. The woman in the red-trimmed cloak who burns what she cannot bend—she has tasted the seal's edge already, in dreams. She will be at the stone before you if you waste a single day."

"She wants to consume it. The whole covenant. I've seen what she is."

"I know what she is," Ulva said, and for the first time something other than ancient grief moved in her voice—something almost like pity. "I spared her life once, when she was small and lost and crying in my snow. Remember that, when you meet her again. Mercy has longer teeth than vengeance. It only bites later."

She walked back toward the tree line, and the shadows took her by degrees—first her flanks, then her scarred shoulders, then the amber banked-coal gleam of her eyes—until there was nothing left but the Wood itself, breathing, waiting, weeping.

Hazel stood a long time in the frost. Then he went back to the lean-to, pulled his grandmother's riddled bones from their wrapping cloth, and cast them on the flat stone beside the boy.

They fell into a pattern he had never seen before and understood instantly: two lines of script pointing into the mountains like arrows, and beneath them, the third bone of the casting—his own finger-bone, the one he always carried, the one he had carved from his father's hand—lying directly across the place where the third covenant-bone should have been marked.

The boy looked at the casting. Then at Hazel. His eyes were very old, and very sorry, and very certain.

Hazel picked up the bones with hands that did not quite shake, and began packing for the barrow country.

Nine days. Two bones. One of them, he was beginning to fear, he had been carrying all his life.
