## 12. Prisoner of Two Courts

Hazel woke to birdsong that was not birdsong.

It came through the walls of the hollow tree the wolves had given them—a chamber scooped from the living heart of a trunk older than any kingdom, its inner wood polished smooth as river stone and veined with amber sap. The sound outside was layered: true wrens and thrushes, yes, but beneath them a second chorus, voices with no throats to make them, singing in the register of memory rather than air. He had spent his whole life hearing such voices in bone and ivory and the teeth of the dead. Here, they simply hung in the branches like fruit.

He lay still for a long time, listening, and understood that he was not chained, not barred, not watched by anything so crude as a guard. He was held the way a jewel is held—carefully, completely, by something that knew exactly what it possessed.

Beside him, the boy slept on a bed of moss and shed winter fur, curled small, one hand still closed around his wooden wolf-token even in dreams. His breathing was slow and even. Whatever dreams came to the last seal of Thornfell, they did not frighten him here. That was its own kind of unsettling. Hazel had known smugglers who slept badly their entire lives after one night in the foothills of this forest, and here was a child sleeping like a cat in a sunbeam, three days' walk inside the Weeping Wood, surrounded by the creatures every fireside story called monsters.

*Monsters.* Hazel turned the word over as he rose quietly, joints stiff with cold, and looked out through the narrow seam of the doorway.

The clearing beyond was silvered with frost. Three wolf-spirits stood at its edge in the early light, and Hazel made himself look at them properly for the first time—not with the sideways glance of a man trying not to see, but fully, the way he would study a bone before carving it.

They were wrong in the way old stories were wrong, which is to say they were wrong only in the details that mattered least. They were larger than wolves, yes—the tallest stood the height of a horse at the shoulder—but they were not the slavering horrors of chapel frescoes. Their coats were grey and white and one deep russet, thick with winter, and where the morning light caught them it did not quite land; it slid off, as if their fur remembered being something else once, something brighter. Their eyes were the color of deep water under ice. And each of them wore scars. Not the honest scars of hunting and territory-fights, but long, deliberate lines, pale as candle-wax, crossing shoulders and flanks and throats in patterns too regular to be accident. Iron-marks. Old iron, the kind that never rusted clean out of a wound.

One of them—the russet one, a young male with a limp that favored his left foreleg—noticed Hazel watching. It did not growl. It did not bare teeth. It looked back at him with an expression Hazel recognized from years of reading faces across smuggling campfires: wariness, and beneath the wariness, a question. *What will you do?*

Hazel raised one hand slowly. Not a wave. Just a showing—that his hand was empty, that he was only a man standing in a doorway.

The young wolf's ear flicked. After a moment it turned away, and the three of them dissolved into the treeline without a sound, which was somehow more courteous than if they had fled.

"They let you see them when they are deciding what you are."

Ulva's voice came from behind him, low as a landslide heard through a mountain. Hazel turned. The ancient wolf-mother filled the far end of the hollow, though she had made no sound entering—and perhaps she had been there all along, the way weather is there all along. Her coat was the white of birch-bark in winter, crossed by so many iron-scars that she seemed map-marked, a country whose borders had been redrawn by violence again and again.

"Deciding," Hazel repeated. His voice was hoarse with sleep.

"What to do with you. What you are worth. What you might become." She lowered her great head through the doorway seam to regard him, her breath stirring his hair, smelling of snowmelt and crushed pine and something underneath both—something old and green and grieving. "You gave me your word you would bring the remaining bones before the moon is full. Words are thin things. So we watch."

"I'm not going anywhere." Hazel glanced at the boy, still sleeping. "I can't."

"No," Ulva agreed, and there was no triumph in it, only a tired certainty that was worse than triumph. "You cannot. You have learned that quickly. Most men take longer to learn they are prisoners of their own blood." She withdrew her head. "Eat. There is food by the east wall. Then come walk with me, bone-carver, if your legs are brave enough. I have something to show you, and I am tired of showing it to people who scream."

* * *

What she showed him first was not terrible at all, which was how she made it terrible.

She led him along paths that seemed to rearrange themselves politely around their footsteps, down through ravines where the trees wept their slow sap-tears into pools that steamed faintly with warmth, past stones carved with writing in no alphabet Hazel knew but which his fingers ached to trace. The Wood was beautiful. That was the trap of it. Every story Hazel had ever heard about the Weeping Wood spoke of horror—of bones in the brambles, of travelers driven mad—but the truth was worse, because the truth was lovely. Rivers ran clear as glass. Flowers bloomed impossibly in the frost. And everywhere, threaded through all of it, the silence where ghosts should have been pressed against him like deep water, like a weight on the chest. The Wood ate ghosts, Maren had said. Now, walking in that ghost-hungry hush, Hazel felt what that meant: this place was *hungry*, and everything in it fed the hunger or starved.

The wolves were starving.

He saw it first in the young russet male, limping ahead of them along a ridge. Up close, in full daylight, the wrongness resolved itself. The wolf's coat was thinning at the haunches, patches showing skin beneath that was grey as ash. Its ribs showed when it breathed. Its eyes, those deep-water eyes, had a tremble at the edges, like a flame in a draft.

"He is called Sorrel now, because that is what he chose to be called," Ulva said, following Hazel's gaze. "He was born inside the binding. Did you know that? He has never run free. Never hunted under open sky. Never known a single day when the stake in his chest did not pull." She said it flatly, without performance, and the flatness made it land like a hammer. "Look at him properly, Quintana. You have an eye for bodies. Tell me what you see."

Hazel swallowed. "He's... bleeding out. Slowly. Inside." The words came from somewhere deeper than craft. "Like a wound packed with moss that keeps soaking through. Something's draining him."

"The covenant does." Ulva stopped on the ridge-top, and below them the land fell away into a wide valley—and Hazel's breath went out of him.

There were dozens of them. More than dozens. Wolf-spirits scattered across the valley floor like fallen stars, some lying motionless in the frost, some pacing slow circuits they had clearly paced ten thousand times before, some simply sitting, staring at nothing, at the impossible flowers, at the sky they could see but never reach beyond. The valley was ringed by hills, and above the hills Hazel could feel it—a pressure, a ceiling, invisible but absolute. The binding. It curved over the whole Wood like the vault of a bell jar, and every creature beneath it lived in the space of a struck bell, ringing and ringing and unable to stop.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"When we were betrayed? Nine hundred and forty souls." Ulva's voice did not change, but the temperature around her seemed to drop. "Now? Fewer than two hundred. We do not age as you age. We do not die as you die. We *thin*. Each year the binding takes more of us, and what it takes does not go to any heaven. It goes into the seal. Into the boy." Her head turned toward him, one ice-water eye vast beside his face. "Your grandmothers drove the sealing stake into our hearts, Quintana, and they used us to hold the door shut between realms, and the door has been drinking us ever since. Three hundred years. Can you count three hundred winters of that?"

Hazel could not. He thought of the dead woman's finger-bone he had carved in another life, weeks ago now—her secret, her grief, sold gently to her daughter. One voice. One death. He had built his whole existence around bearing witness to the dead, and here was a mass grave that was still alive, still walking, still watching the sky.

"Why haven't you torn out of here?" he asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "You're spirits. You're strong enough to level kingdoms, from what the stories say. Why stay in a cage?"

And Ulva laughed. It was the worst sound Hazel had heard yet in the Weeping Wood—not cruel, not mad, just *tired*, a laugh with all the joy boiled out of it, like wine left open for a century.

"Because we tried, little carver. In the first years, we threw ourselves against the binding until the mountains rang. And do you know what happens when a bound spirit rages against its chains?" She stepped closer, and deliberately—she made sure he saw her do it deliberately—she turned her head so the light fell across her throat, where an iron-scar ran deepest, widest, oldest. "The rage has to go somewhere. It goes inward. It eats the spirit from the inside, and what is left afterward—" she gestured with her muzzle toward the far end of the valley, where a shape moved that Hazel's eyes kept sliding off of, huge and wrong and hunched, muttering to itself in a language of snapping branches, "—is what your stories are made of. That was my sister. She fought the longest. Now she fights nothing. She fights *weather*. She fights shadows. And every year, one or two more of us begin to fray the way she frayed, because the rage does not sleep, it only waits, and the binding gives it nowhere to go but home."

Hazel stood very still in the cold. Below, the imprisoned stars of the valley paced their circuits, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the Crown's tales had the whole thing backward. The wolves were not caged because they were monsters. They were becoming monsters because they were caged. The horror stories were not warnings about what the wolves had done. They were forecasts. Weather reports from a storm three hundred years in the gathering.

"And Veyra wants to consume what's left of you," he said. "The covenant's power. She'd drink the cup before it spills."

"She would unmake us entirely and call it mercy," Ulva said. "She is not wrong that it would end. She is wrong about what ending means." The wolf-mother began to walk again, downhill, toward the valley. "Come. If you are to choose—and you will choose, bone-carver, everyone chooses eventually—then choose knowing us. Not the stories of us. Us."

* * *

The days took on a strange rhythm, neither captivity nor freedom but some third thing Hazel had no word for.

In the mornings he sat with the boy while the child traced letters in spilled tea-leaf water on the tabletop of woven root, learning Hazel's alphabet with an appetite that bordered on alarming. The boy's vocabulary grew daily: *water*, *bread*, *cold*, *fire*, *name*, *home*. He pointed at everything and demanded its word, and when Hazel gave it, the boy would repeat it twice, once softly and once firmly, as if testing whether the word could bear his weight. He had begun, tentatively, to offer words back—to point at things and say sounds that were not Hazel's words at all, but older ones, rounder, full of vowels like wind through a canyon. Teaching became trading. Two languages passing between them like bread broken and shared.

"You need a name," Hazel told him one gray afternoon. "Not 'the boy.' Not 'the seal.' A name someone chose for you because they loved you."

The boy considered this with enormous seriousness, brow furrowed, then pointed at the fire.

"Ember?"

A shake of the head. He pointed at the window, where snow clung to the branches. Then at himself. Then he made a sound: "*Rrrr-oh.*"

"Rowan?" Hazel tried. The rowan tree was the woodcarver's friend, its wood hard and bright-grained, planted by doorways in the old country to ward off evil. Rowan, the ward-tree. Rowan, the protector.

The boy's whole face changed. It was like watching sunrise happen to a person. "*Rowan,*" he repeated, firm this time, claiming it, and then he laughed—high and startled, as if surprised to find laughter still worked—and threw himself at Hazel and hugged him with fierce, bony arms.

Hazel held him and stared over the boy's shoulder at the wall of the hollow and found that his eyes were burning, and did not examine why. Somewhere in the last week, *the seal*, *the relic*, *the burden* had become *Rowan*, and somewhere in the same week Hazel had stopped counting the days to the full moon with dread of what he must do, and started counting them with dread of how little time remained. Those were different dreads. He was not ready to look directly at the difference.

* * *

It was Sorrel who broke through last of all—the young wolf, the limping one, the one who had been born in the cage and knew nothing else.

Hazel found him on the fourth evening, or Sorrel found him; it was hard to say which. Hazel had gone to the spring at dusk to fill the water skins, and the russet wolf was simply there when he turned around, standing in the falling snow, watching him with that trembling flame-eyed wariness.

Neither of them moved for a long moment. Then Hazel did the thing he knew how to do, the thing his hands had always known how to do: he reached into his pocket and drew out a scrap of antler he had been whittling idly during the long evenings, and held it out on his open palm.

It was crude work—he had no proper tools, only his knife and patience—but it was recognizable. A wolf. Running.

Sorrel's ears went forward. He approached step by careful step, favoring the bad leg, until his breath fogged warm against Hazel's wrist, and then—so gently it barely stirred the hairs— he took the little carving from Hazel's palm in teeth that could have sheared through bone, and carried it three steps away, and set it down in the snow, and looked at it, and looked up at Hazel.

The voice that reached Hazel then did not come through his gift. His gift had been silent since he crossed the Wood's border, drowned in the ghost-eating hush. This came some other way—through the eyes, through the posture, through whatever old channel runs beneath language in all living things. It said: *No one has ever made my shape before. Everyone makes the other shape. The snarling one.*

"I know," Hazel said aloud, uselessly, and his voice cracked in the middle. "I know. I'm sorry. We made the snarling one for three hundred years, and it was easier than making this one."

Sorrel tilted his head. Snow gathered on his ears. And then he did something that undid Hazel more thoroughly than any accusation could have: he lay down, right there in the snow beside the spring, deliberately, folding his scarred body close, and rested his chin on his paws, and closed his eyes. Showing his throat to a human. Sleeping, or pretending to sleep, within lunging distance of the bloodline of the man who had staked his people to the earth.

*Trust*, offered like a wound offered for cleaning. *Do what you like with it.*

Hazel sat down in the snow next to him. He did not touch the wolf. He simply sat, and the snow fell on both of them, and somewhere in the darkening valley the frayed sister-shape muttered and snapped at the weather, and Hazel thought: *My ancestor did this. My name did this. And I have spent my life selling whispers of the dead while the dying were locked behind these hills, waiting for a Quintana to finish the job.*

Loyalty, he realized, did not break like a bone. It wore through like rope, strand by strand, unnoticed, until one day the load swung free and you discovered you had been holding on to nothing for some time. Somewhere in the past four days his loyalty to the Crown, to duty, to the tidy story of righteous men keeping monstrous wolves penned—had worn through entirely, and here he sat in the snow on the far side of the severing, feeling not lost but *arrived*.

He would bring the bones. Not to destroy the wolves. To free them.

He was still sitting there when Rowan came looking for him, a small dark figure hurrying through the blue snow-light, and when the boy saw them—man and wolf sitting together in the falling white—he stopped, and smiled, and said his third full sentence:

"*Rowan knew. Rowan told you they were kind.*"

And Hazel opened his mouth to answer—

—and froze, because the wind had shifted, and on it, faint and filthy and utterly impossible this deep in the Wood, he smelled smoke. Not hearth-smoke. Not pine-smoke.

Pitch. Torch-pitch, and burning oil, and beneath it the reek of hot iron, coming up the valley from the south.

Sorrel's eyes snapped open. Across the darkening slopes, one by one, every wolf in the Weeping Wood lifted its head and began, softly, terribly, to sing.
