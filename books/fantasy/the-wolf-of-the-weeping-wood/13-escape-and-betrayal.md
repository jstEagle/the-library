## 13. Escape and Betrayal

The first sign was the smell.

Hazel woke in the hollow beneath the wolf-mother's oak with his heart already hammering, though he could not say why—until the wind shifted and carried it to him: pitch-smoke and hot iron, a stink that did not belong among the weeping trees. He had smelled that smell twice before in his life. Once when the Crown burned a smuggler's cache in the Thornfell passes. Once at his father's funeral pyre, when he had been too young to understand that some fires are lit out of fear and some out of greed, and Veyra's would be both.

He rose without waking Rowan, or tried to. The boy's eyes were already open, dark and wide in the gloom, fixed on the eastern sky where the stars were being swallowed one by one—not by cloud but by a glow, orange as an ember breathing under ash.

*Fire,* said the voices in the bone-token at Rowan's throat, thousands of them layered into one word, and for once Hazel wished he could not hear them. The Wood's dead did not speak of fire often. When they did, they spoke of it the way sailors speak of drowning.

The wolves knew too. He heard them moving through the dark—a low tide of bodies flowing toward the forest's edge, silent despite their size, their hackles up. Ulva passed close enough that her scarred flank brushed him, old iron-wounds gleaming pale even in the starless dark, and her voice came into his mind like cold water poured over stone.

*They bring the iron carts. They bring fire-oil. They have found the paths your kind cut through my borders long ago.*

"Smugglers' roads," Hazel said. His mouth tasted of copper. "I've walked half of them myself."

*Then you know how quickly a road can become a wound.*

Above the treeline, far off, something bloomed: a second sun, small and wrong, and a moment later the sound reached them—a deep *whump*, like the world's breath knocked out of it. The eastern edge of the Weeping Wood was burning.

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles have lines and orders and the dignity of two wills meeting. This was a slaughter conducted at a distance, by men who never saw what they killed.

Veyra's soldiers did not enter the Wood. They stood at its border with torches and casks of resin-oil and long iron pikes tipped with blessed steel, and they threw fire into the trees the way children throw stones into a well—to hear how deep it went. And the Wood *did* go deep; Hazel felt it flinch around him, a vast slow consciousness contracting like a hand closing. The trees bled harder where the flames took hold, sap running down blackened bark in scalding tears that hissed and steamed, and each tree that fell screamed—not loudly, not in any voice human ears were built for, but Hazel heard it all the same, a chorus of green agony that made his teeth ache and his eyes run.

The wolves fought anyway. They harried the flanks where the line thinned, dragging soldiers screaming into the underbrush, and the soldiers died badly—but there were so many *soldiers*, rank upon rank in the firelight, and every wolf that fell stayed fallen while the men simply stepped over their own dead and kept throwing fire.

Hazel watched a young wolf—one of the yearlings, russet-flanked, all legs and bravado, the one who had spent the past three days shadowing Rowan everywhere like an anxious elder brother—take an iron pike through the shoulder rather than abandon a cornered cub. The boy who held the pike screamed when the yearling's jaws closed on his arm, and then there were four more soldiers, and the young wolf broke free limping, trailing blood that smoked where it touched the burning ground, and vanished into the smoke.

Ulva's voice filled Hazel's skull, stripped now of all its ancient calm. *The heart-stone. If the fire reaches the heart-stone while the seal is whole, everything bound to it dies at once. Take the boy and go north to the barrow-road. That is no longer a request, bone-carver.*

"You're staying?"

*I am the oldest thing here. Fire and I have an old acquaintance.* A pause, and something almost like humor, grim as a grave. *Go. You have bones to gather and little time to gather them. The moon does not wait for wars.*

Rowan was already standing, already gripping Hazel's sleeve with fingers that trembled only slightly. The boy had seen fire before. Hazel did not ask where. Some things the boy's silence kept better than words ever could.

They ran north.

* * *

The Wood around them was coming apart. Hazel had smuggled through these mountains for eleven years; he knew how to read broken country, how to find the seam of safety inside disaster. But this was different. Here the disaster was alive and grieving, and the grief made the paths lie. Twice the trail he followed dissolved into bramble that had not been bramble an hour before—the Wood itself convulsing, rearranging, unable to tell friend from flame. Once they waded a stream that had gone scalding where burning sap had drained into it, and Rowan pulled him back by the collar with a sharp hissed breath just before Hazel stepped in.

The boy saved his feet. Hazel noted it distantly, the way you note anything in a nightmare: *the child protects me, the child I am meant to protect.*

Behind them the glow spread along the southern sky like an infection. Ahead, through gaps in the canopy, the peaks of Greyfell showed white and indifferent under the stars. North, then up, then east along the ridge Maren's map had described—the barrow-road, where his ancestor's ghost waited with the second covenant-bone and, presumably, a reckoning Hazel did not want.

A shape crashed out of the fern-brake ahead of them and Hazel spun, knife half-drawn, before his eyes resolved it: the yearling. The young wolf. Blood had soaked the fur of its left shoulder to a dark crust, and it moved with its foreleg held carefully off the ground, but it had come *toward* them, toward the noise of their flight, and now it planted itself in the path and looked at Rowan and made a sound Hazel had never heard from a wolf-spirit—a high, thin whine, urgent and unmistakable.

"It can't keep up," Hazel said, understanding arriving like a stone dropped into still water. "It's telling us—it wants—"

Rowan was already kneeling. The boy put his arms around the yearling's neck and pressed his forehead to the blood-crusted fur, and the bone-token at his throat hummed, and Hazel heard the thousand-voiced language pour between them, fast and rising, argument-shaped.

And then the trees behind them began to fall.

Not burn—*fall*, pushed, axes and ropes and the groaning crash of centuries of growth brought down in rows, because Veyra had learned that fire alone would not crack the Wood's heart and had begun instead to cut a road into it, a road her soldiers could march along, a road pointed straight at the heart-stone. The sound rolled through the forest like thunder learning cruelty. Somewhere in that direction, wolves were dying.

The yearling pulled away from Rowan and staggered upright, favoring the ruined shoulder, and turned its head north—the way they needed to go—and then south, toward the crashing and the screams, and then back to Rowan, and its eyes were terrible with the arithmetic every soldier knows: *I cannot do both.*

"No," Hazel said, though no one had asked him anything yet. "No, we take it with us, it'll slow us—"

But even as he said it he was doing the other sums, the smuggler's sums, the cold ones. Three-legged pace versus two-legged flight. Soldiers cutting a road that would intersect the barrow-trail within the hour. The moon rising in five nights whether they lived or died. And the boy—the seal, the last hope of everything here—needed to reach the second bone, and then the third, faster than Veyra reached the heart-stone.

Every sum came out the same. That was the horror of it. There was no version of the night in which mercy and survival fit in the same pack.

The yearling seemed to hear the sums in him. Wolves of the Weeping Wood could smell a lie, Ulva had told him, and perhaps they could smell a calculation too. It looked at Hazel for a long moment, and whatever was in his face, the wolf read it, and understood it, and something in its posture changed—not submission, nothing so gentle. Acceptance of a debt it intended to collect.

Then it lowered its head to Rowan one last time, touched its nose to the boy's palm, and turned south, toward the axes, toward the fire, toward the soldiers, limping into the smoke with its head held high and its blood dripping a trail any hunter could follow.

Toward the fight it could not win, so that the boy would not have to watch it die slowly on the road.

"Hear me," Hazel called after it, uselessly, his voice cracking like green wood. "There's a hollow under the third falls on the Cold Larch trail—smugglers' shelter—dry, defensible—I left stores there in autumn—"

The wolf did not look back. Smoke took it the way water takes a stone, gradually and then completely.

Rowan was staring at Hazel. Not shouting—Rowan never shouted—but the boy's face held a question so naked and so damning that Hazel would have preferred a scream. His hands moved in the quick gestures he used when words failed him: *Why? Why did you let it go?*

"Because if we carry it, we die, and if we die, everything dies." The words came out flat, practical, a smuggler's ledger read aloud. "That's the truth of it. That's the whole ugly truth."

Rowan's hands answered slowly. *You didn't even try.*

"I did try—" But had he? Had he tried, or had he simply done what he always did, what his family had always done, weighed the lives on his scale and let the light ones tip off the edge into the dark? His grandfather had weighed lives that way. His ancestor Quintana had weighed a whole people that way and driven the sealing stake home and called it duty. The blood in Hazel's veins knew this arithmetic the way rivers know downhill.

He knelt and took the boy by the shoulders. "Listen to me. In three days, maybe less, I'm going to stand in front of a stone and give everything I have to fix what my family broke. Everything. Do you believe me?" Rowan's jaw set; he neither nodded nor pulled away. "Then believe this too: I am not a good man. I'm a man trying to stop being a bad one, and the difference is measured in nights like this one, and I will fail more times than I succeed. What I promise you is that I will hate myself properly for every failure. That's all I have. It isn't much. But it's honest."

For a long moment the boy searched his face with those ancient dark eyes, older than kingdoms, and Hazel endured it, because he deserved to endure it.

Then Rowan signed, briefly, coldly: *We go. Now.* And he turned north and ran, and Hazel followed his own student down the burning borders of the world, carrying nothing heavier than what he already held inside his chest.

* * *

They gained the ridge an hour before dawn, lungs raw, and looked back only once.

The Weeping Wood glowed along its whole southern length like a wound packed with embers. Cutting-roads scored the darkness in straight unnatural lines, and along them moved points of torchlight, patient as maggots. Yet the Wood did not break. He could feel it even from here—that vast contracted consciousness holding, holding, spending its oldest trees like coins to buy hours, and somewhere in its heart Ulva stood at the stone, and somewhere in its smoking edges the yearling was either fighting or dead, and Hazel could not know which, and the not-knowing sat in him like swallowed glass.

"They are waiting," Rowan had said once, around a fire, in his first full sentence. Hazel understood it differently now. Everyone was waiting. The wolves waited for justice. The dead waited for release. Veyra waited for power. And Hazel waited for the full moon, when all these hungers would meet at one stone, and someone would finally pay.

He thought about the yearling's eyes as it turned south. He thought: *when this is over, if I live, I will walk back into that wood and find out.* He thought: *and if it's dead, I don't deserve to be.*

Below them, on the switchback trail, torchlight appeared. Many torches. Moving fast, and not toward the heart-stone at all.

North. Toward the barrow-road. Toward the second bone.

Someone had read Maren's map after all—or taken it from her ashes.

"Run," Hazel breathed, and took Rowan's hand, and they fled along the spine of the mountains with the dawn breaking red behind them like something freshly opened.
