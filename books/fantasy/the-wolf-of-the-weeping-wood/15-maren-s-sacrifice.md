## 15. Maren's Sacrifice

The raven came at dawn, or what passed for dawn in the high passes—a grey seeping of light through cloud so thick it seemed the sky itself had grown old and tired. It dropped out of that murk like a stone with opinions, circled the hollow where Hazel had made camp, and landed on a frost-blackened branch not three feet from his face.

It looked at him. He looked at it.

"I know," he said quietly. "I know."

Ravens did not carry messages in the Thornfells. That was crows' work, and worse than crows: this was a bone-bird, a courier of the kind his grandmother had kept, its throat stuffed with a scroll of birchbark tied in red thread. He had seen them as a child and hated them, because they always meant someone was dead, or about to be.

He untied the bark with fingers that would not stay warm anymore. Not since Greyfell. The cold of the drowned chapel lived in him now, he thought, lodged somewhere behind his sternum like a splinter of lake-ice.

The message was written in Maren's hand—quick, slanting, pressed hard enough into the bark to tear it in places. She wrote the way she prayed: urgently, and without much hope of being answered.

*They took me at the ford. I let them. Do not come for me—I mean this, carver, do not be a fool—but if you are already coming, then listen. She has an armory wagon, iron and oil and firepowder enough to crack the mountain. It travels slow on the switchbacks below Raven's Tooth. And she wants to trade.*

Hazel read it three times while Rowan slept beneath the fir boughs, curled around the wooden wolf-token like a child around any comfort, which was all it was now, or so Hazel kept telling himself. Two covenant-bones rode against his own ribs, wrapped in wool and oiled leather, humming faintly when he breathed too near them—the first cold as lake-water, the second warm as grave-dirt, and between those two temperatures his whole body had become a disputed borderland.

Below the message, Maren had drawn something: a rough circle, crossed by a line, with three small marks inside it. Her old sign, the one she used for *sacrament*. For *that which is given freely and cannot be taken back*.

He sat very still until the raven grew bored and flapped off into the grey.

"She told me not to come," he said to the sleeping boy.

Rowan opened one eye. He did that sometimes—woke without seeming to wake, surfacing from sleep the way a fish surfaces, just far enough to watch. He looked at Hazel now with those too-old eyes, and said nothing, because he almost never spoke, and when he did it was only words that mattered.

"You'd tell me not to go," Hazel said. "Wouldn't you."

Rowan considered this with the gravity of a small judge weighing a large crime. Then he reached out, took Hazel's hand, and pressed it flat against the frozen ground. Held it there. Held it there until Hazel understood—not *stay*, not *go*, but something older. *The ground remembers where you stand.*

"Poets," Hazel muttered, "the both of you," and began to pack.

* * *

Raven's Tooth was a spur of granite that jutted over the lower switchbacks like the jaw of something long dead, and from its crown Hazel could see the Inquisitor's camp strung along the valley floor—tents white as teeth, cook-smoke rising straight in the windless cold, and beside it, exactly as Maren had promised, the armory wagon: a great black box of iron-banded oak drawn by four shaggy garrons, guarded by six men who stood too far apart from it, the way soldiers stand from things that frighten them.

And near the fire, wrapped in a blanket but sitting upright with her chin high, her silver hair loose down her back for the first time since Hazel had known her—

Maren.

She looked small at this distance. Everything looked small from up here. That was the trick of heights, Hazel thought; they lied to you about size and never about consequence. From the top of Raven's Tooth he could see the whole shape of the trap laid out below him, plain as a carving half-finished, every grain showing: the camp arranged so that anyone descending the single visible path would be funneled straight past Veyra's fire. Straight past Maren. Straight into the open palm of whatever bargain the Inquisitor had folded shut.

A rider was already climbing toward him. Not hurrying. A white banner on a lance, and behind it, riding sidesaddle with the ease of a woman who had never once been thrown, came Inquisitor Veyra herself.

Hazel considered running. He was a smuggler; running was his oldest craft. But there were archers in the rocks—he could feel them the way you feel a draft under a door, the faint wrongness of held breath—and besides, Rowan was watching him with that patient, terrible attention, and the boy had already taught him that some things you walk toward because walking away is simply a slower way of losing.

He waited.

Veyra reined in ten paces off and dismounted herself, which surprised him. Up close she was beautiful the way winter is beautiful: flawless, and indifferent to whether you survived it. Her burned arm was bound in silk today, black silk, and she carried no weapon that he could see, which frightened him more than a crossbow would have.

"Bonesinger," she said. His family's name, the old name, spoken like a key tried in a lock. "You've been busy."

"You've been thorough." He nodded toward the valley. "The whole Crown's reach, hunting one old woman and two travelers."

"One old woman," Veyra said, "who walked into my hands at the ford with her arms open and a look on her face I have seen before, on soldiers going over walls. She wanted to be caught, your priestess. It has made interrogating her remarkably tedious." Something moved behind her eyes—not quite pain, not quite envy. "She keeps saying things like *the Wood will provide* and *what is given freely cannot be taken back.* Do you know, I burned a man in Corvale for saying less, and even he wept by the end. She only smiles at me. It's very rude."

"What do you want, Veyra?"

"The obvious thing." She gestured, and an archer rose from the rocks with a crossbow trained—not on Hazel, he noticed, but on the space beside him, where a boy might step. "Two bones for one life. A fair exchange. Better than fair; the Church would call it generous. Bring them down at moonrise tonight, lay them at my feet, and the priestess walks free. Refuse—" she smiled, and it did not warm anything, "—and I will burn her tomorrow at noon, in front of my men, as a relic-thief and a witch. Slowly. I have learned, these last months, how slowly fire can be persuaded to eat."

"You'd kill her either way," Hazel said. "Once you have the bones, why leave a witness?"

Veyra tilted her head, genuinely considering the question, which somehow made it worse.

"Because I remember being spared," she said softly. "Isn't that strange? All my life, everything I've done, and underneath it there is still that village girl standing in the snow, waiting for the wolves to take her, and they looked at her and went away. Mercy is the only debt I've never been able to repay, bonesinger, and I intend to pay it by ending mercy altogether. When the covenant's power runs through my veins, no child will ever again stand in the snow waiting. No one will ever need to be spared. Fear will die, and everything fear protects will die with it." Her voice stayed gentle throughout, which was the most horrifying thing about her. "So yes. I will keep my word about the priestess. Words are the last currency worth honoring. Even mine."

She mounted, gathered the reins, and paused.

"Moonrise," she said. "Bring the boy if you like. It will save me a second journey."

Then she rode down into the valley, and the archers melted back into the rock, and Hazel stood alone on Raven's Tooth with the wind trying to pry the bones from his chest.

* * *

Rowan heard it all, of course. Rowan always seemed to hear everything, though his ears worked no better than anyone's. When Hazel returned to the hollow and told him plainly—because lying to the boy had become impossible, like lying to weather—Rowan sat turning the wooden wolf-token over and over in his hands.

"No," Hazel said, before the boy could say anything, or rather before he could say nothing, which was worse. "Whatever you're thinking. No. We are not trading you for her. Both of those things are true: we're not leaving her, and we're not giving you up. Find me another road."

Rowan looked at him for a long moment. Then he set the token down, took a stick, and drew in the ash of their dead fire: a square. A wagon. Inside the square, small marks—iron bands, and at the center, a flame.

"Yes," Hazel said grimly. "Her armory. I saw it. Six guards, a whole camp around it, and it's iron-banded—how does one burn a wagon built to hold fire?"

Rowan drew more: the wagon, then lines radiating outward from it like rays from a sun. Then he looked up, and there was such certainty in his face that Hazel felt the hair rise on his arms.

"She's not asking us to rescue her," Hazel said slowly. "Maren. In her letter. *If you are already coming, then listen.* She told me where the wagon would be. She drew the sign of sacrament. She's been planning this since the ford—since before the ford. She let herself be caught because Veyra's armory was the one thing in these mountains she couldn't reach from outside."

The boy nodded once.

"She means to burn it herself." Hazel's throat closed around the words. "With herself inside it, if it comes to that. What is given freely cannot be taken back."

Rowan picked up the stick again and added a final mark to the ash drawing: a small figure beside the burning wagon. Outside it. Walking away. Then he pointed at Hazel, and at himself, and at the path north.

"We get her out first," Hazel said. "That's the price. If she won't agree to it, we don't light so much as a candle. Swear it with me. Swear it however you swear things, boy—swear it on the ground, on the wolves, on whatever ancient thing you are. We go together or not at all."

Rowan pressed his palm flat to the frozen earth, and held Hazel's hand against it beside his own, and the ground was cold and patient and remembering, and Hazel chose to believe it was a vow.

* * *

They went down in the blue hour after sunset, when the campfires burned low and the sentries' breath made ghosts of them all. Hazel knew these slopes the way other men knew their own doorsteps; he had run brand and fugitives across them for fifteen years, guided by tricks the dead had sold him cheap. He knew which scree sang underfoot and which kept silent. He knew that dogs could not smell a man who had rubbed his boots with juniper ash and walked downwind of the latrines. He knew that the third watch after midnight was when guards stopped looking at the dark and started looking at the fire.

He got them to within thirty yards of the armory wagon without a sound.

Maren was awake. Of course she was. She was sitting against the wagon's wheel with the blanket around her shoulders, and as Hazel slid out of the shadows she turned her head and found him instantly, the way she had always found him, as though holiness were just attention perfected.

"Carver," she whispered. "You magnificent fool. I told you not to come."

"And I've never once listened to anyone sensible. Come on. We're going north."

"No." She said it gently, the way you decline an offer of tea. "No, I'm not."

"Maren—"

"Hush now, and listen, because we haven't long." Her hands found his in the dark, strong as roots. "Look at the wagon. Look at what she's carrying. Iron for binding spirits, oil for burning the Wood's edge, firepowder to bring down the heart-tree itself. That wagon is the death of everything we love, and tonight it sits in the middle of her camp like a heart waiting to be cut out. There is no version of tomorrow, Hazel, where that wagon doesn't exist—unless it burns tonight."

"I'll burn it. You walk away. That was the bargain—"

"That was your bargain. Mine was made years ago, in a different church, to a different God, and He has finally sent me work worthy of the title." She smiled, and in the firelight's last embers her face was young. "Besides, child, think. Who can move through that camp unseen? Not you—you smell of the bones, of the Wood; their hounds cough blood when you pass. But an old prisoner, wandering confused among the tents? Guards see what they expect. I've spent the day learning where the oil is stacked, where the powder is stored, which band comes loose. I've spent the whole day, Hazel, being tedious on purpose."

"You'll die."

"Eventually," she agreed. "Everyone does. The only question the Wood ever asks is what you were doing at the time." She pressed something into his hands: a roll of soft leather, tied with red thread, heavy for its size. "From the chapel archive. I copied it the night before the ford, when I understood what was coming for me. It shows the way to the third bone—or to where the third bone waits, which may not be the same thing. And Hazel—" her grip tightened, "—when you open it, don't weep over the map. Weep later. Weeping is good work, but it must be done in order."

Somewhere across the camp a dog began to bark.

"There's my cue," Maren said, and kissed his forehead, once, like a mother, like a priestess, like a woman closing a book she had finished writing. "Tell the wolf-mother the Church sends its apologies. Tell the boy he was the best parish I ever had. And carver—" she was already moving, blanket shed, moving through the dark with the sureness of someone whose whole life had been preparation for exactly one unremarkable night, "—tell them what I told you at Greyfell. The covenant demands a price. Make sure it's a price, and not a theft. Those are different griefs."

Then she was gone among the tents, and Hazel stood holding the leather roll and shaking, and Rowan's hand found his sleeve and pulled, gently, northward, and he went.

He was two hundred yards up the slope, lungs full of pine-dark air, when the world turned orange.

It began as a bloom of light between the tent-lines, then a roar, then a column of flame that stood up out of the valley like a tree growing in one terrible instant—the oil catching first, then the powder, then the wagon itself going up in a thunderclap that rolled off the mountainsides and came back doubled, trebled, a bell tolling in iron and fire. Sentries screamed. Horses screamed. The camp became a scatter of tiny burning figures, and above it all, impossibly, Hazel thought he heard singing—one thin voice lifted in some old hymn of the chapel, audible over the ruin as though the fire itself had parted to let it through.

He did not look away. She deserved witnesses.

When the column collapsed inward and the night went dark again, Rowan tugged his sleeve once more, and Hazel knelt in the snow and opened the leather roll with numb fingers.

Inside was the map, drawn in Maren's precise, urgent hand: the passes beyond Raven's Tooth, the Weeping Wood's border marked in ink that shimmered faintly, and at its center a symbol he knew from the barrow-carvings, from the covenant-stone, from the bottom of a drowned lake—a circle crossed by a line.

Beneath it she had written five words:

*The third bone is the boy.*

And below that, smaller, in letters gone wavery, as if written quickly and perhaps while crying:

*Forgive us all. Run.*

Hazel looked up. The moon was rising over the eastern peaks—three nights shy of full, and climbing.

Rowan stood beside him, watching the dying fires below with eyes that reflected them perfectly, and said, in his small clear voice, the first words Hazel had heard him speak since *they are waiting*:

"Now you know."

"Yes," Hazel said, folding the map against his heart, next to the two bones that hummed like living things. "Now I know."

He shouldered his pack, took the boy's hand, and turned north—into the passes, into the gathering dark, toward the Wood's heart and the wolf-mother's deadline and the truth he had been carrying all along without knowing its weight.

Behind them, the fires sank. Ahead, the snow began again, soft and endless, erasing their tracks one by one, as though the mountain itself had decided to keep their secret a little longer.

Three days to the full moon. Three days to learn what a life was worth, and whose.

Hazel ran.
