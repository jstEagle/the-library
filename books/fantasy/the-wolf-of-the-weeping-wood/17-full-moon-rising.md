## 17. Full Moon Rising

The moon would rise before they cleared the last pass, and Hazel knew it the way he knew the hour of his own death: not as a thought, but as a pressure behind his eyes.

He ran with Rowan strapped against his chest in a sling of oiled leather, the boy's small weight riding each stride like a second heartbeat. The two covenant-bones hung at Hazel's throat inside a pouch of wolf-hide, and even through the leather he could feel them now—warm, insistent, *awake*—pulsing against his sternum like two hearts that were not his own. Since Maren's map had shown him the truth, since he had stopped running and turned instead toward the thing he'd spent his whole life fleeing, the bones had been speaking to him. Not in words. In hunger.

They wanted to go home.

"Almost," Hazel breathed, though the word was more for himself than for the boy. The pass ahead was a black seam splitting the shoulder of Thornfell, its walls rising sheer on either side, crusted with ice that caught the last grey light of dusk and threw it back like old mirrors. His boots found the smuggler's path by memory and by the dead—the dead who no longer spoke to him here, this close to the Wood, but whose knowledge lived in him still, worn smooth into instinct. *Left at the split stone. Three steps past where the cairn leans. Trust the ice that doesn't shine.*

Behind them, far below and winding up the valley road, torchlight moved.

Hazel did not look back. Looking back cost breath, and breath cost time, and time was the one coin Veyra's hunters could not take from him because he refused to spend it. But he heard them—he had always heard too much—and what he heard made his jaw tighten. Not shouts. Veyra trained them better than that. Just the rhythmic crunch of iron-shod boots on frozen scree, thirty or forty men moving fast and silent as a landslide deciding which way to fall.

And beneath it, fainter, threaded through the mountain's own groaning: a sound he recognized from the night the armory burned. A low, sustained note, like a bow drawn across a single string and never released.

Veyra's horn. She had blown it once before, at the edge of her patience. Now she blew it without stopping, a call that said *I am coming, and I am not tired.*

Rowan stirred against his chest. Small fingers found Hazel's collar and gripped.

"I know," Hazel said. "I know, little seal. Hold on."

The boy could not answer—not yet, perhaps not ever again, if the covenant took him the way Hazel feared it must—but his grip tightened, and that was answer enough.

* * *

The seam of the pass narrowed until Hazel turned sideways, dragging breath through his teeth, the rock scraping his pack, Rowan's sling protected between his body and the stone. The air grew colder as they climbed, a clean cold that smelled of nothing—no pine, no smoke, no rot. The Weeping Wood did not breathe out here. It only waited.

His grandmother's bones had led him true. He carried them still, wrapped separately, and once, pausing at a fork where three gullies dropped away into darkness, he unwrapped them with numb fingers and held them up. They were quiet. All three knuckle-bones pointed the same way, trembling faintly toward the leftmost path, and when he lifted them to his ear—an old habit, older than sense—he heard nothing at all.

That frightened him more than the hunters. His whole life the dead had crowded him, clamored, begged, wept, lied. Silence from the bones meant they had given him everything they had. Meant they were, in some way he couldn't name, *finished* with him.

"Thank you," he whispered to them, and wrapped them away, and ran on.

The moon rose while he climbed the final switchbacks—a full moon swollen huge and copper over the eastern ridge, so low and so near it seemed he could have reached up and dragged his fingers through its light. And the moment its rim cleared the rock, everything changed.

The covenant-bones went hot against his chest. Hot enough that he gasped, fumbling the pouch open, and in the copper light he saw them glowing through their wrappings like coals breathing. And Rowan—

Rowan was *glowing*. Faintly, beneath the skin, along the lines of his veins, a pale gold tracery that pulsed in time with the moon, in time with the bones, in time with something vast and slow turning beneath the mountains like a heart beneath a breastbone. The boy looked down at his own hands and then up at Hazel, and his eyes were wide, and unafraid, and ancient.

"They're calling you home," Hazel said. "Aren't they."

Rowan nodded once. Then he lifted one small hand and pressed it flat over Hazel's heart, over the burning pouch, and held it there, and though no voice came, Hazel felt the boy's meaning settle into him like a stone dropped into deep water:

*I'm ready. Don't be afraid. You taught me how.*

Hazel laughed—a cracked, broken sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man who has just understood the shape of the price and pays it anyway. He shifted the sling higher on his chest and ran.

* * *

The last mile was all downhill and all nightmare. The path plunged toward the tree line in long falling sweeps, ice-glazed, and Hazel gave up trying to be careful and simply *fell* well—again and again, sliding on his heels, catching himself on outcrops, the boy clutched tight. Behind and above, torches spilled over the ridgeline he'd just abandoned. He counted them without meaning to. Eleven. Where were the rest?

Then the wind shifted, and he had his answer. The rest weren't on the path.

They were ahead.

Hazel froze in the lee of a boulder, lungs sawing, and listened the way only a man raised on the voices of the dead can listen. Below, maybe two hundred yards down-slope where the path bent around a granite shoulder, iron rang softly on stone. Leather creaked. Someone coughed and was silenced with a hiss. Six men, maybe eight, set in the bend of the trail like a hand closing around a wrist.

Veyra hadn't chased him. She had *anticipated* him. Of course she had. She knew these mountains better than any Crown man alive, because she had spent twenty years learning every road that led to the Wood, and there was only one road that mattered tonight.

For a long moment Hazel stood in the dark with the moon climbing behind him and considered, with strange calm, lying down. Letting the cold take him here, above the trap, with the boy warm and glowing against his chest. It would be easy. It would be almost kind.

Then Rowan tugged his collar and pointed—not down the path, not back up it, but *out*, into the blackness beyond the trail's edge, where the mountain fell away in a chaos of boulder fields and frozen waterfalls.

Hazel stared. There was no path there. There was nothing there but death at speed.

But the smuggler's first lesson—the lesson the dead had taught him in his nineteenth winter, when a customs patrol had boxed him into exactly such a bend—rose in his memory with perfect clarity: *The mountain has more roads than men can walk, boy. The dead remember all of them. Walk where the fallen walked.*

There were no dead to ask anymore. But there was a boy who was older than kingdoms, who had been a seal upon this land since before the first stone of the first keep, and if anything in the world knew where the mountain's hidden roads ran, it was him.

"All right," Hazel breathed. "Show me."

He stepped off the path into the dark.

It should have killed them six times in the first hundred yards. Instead Rowan's small hand guided him—pressing left against his collarbone, pulling right, going rigid when Hazel's boot hovered over crust that would have swallowed him into a crevasse—until Hazel understood and surrendered to it entirely, closing his eyes between steps and letting the boy steer. They descended through boulder fields by moon-shadow, crossed a frozen cascade on its blind side where the ice lay thick over living water, and twice passed within a stone's throw of hunters' torches whose bearers never turned their heads, because no sane man would come this way.

Once, halfway down a slope of shattered slate, Hazel's foot went out from under him and they slid together, twenty feet, thirty, Rowan making no sound at all until they ground to a halt against a snowdrift—and then the boy patted Hazel's cheek twice, gently, the way one pats a horse that has done its best.

Despite everything, despite the hunters and the moon and the price waiting below, Hazel laughed for real this time, breathless and helpless, and pulled himself upright.

"You," he panted, "are going to be the death of me."

Rowan's expression, in the moonlight, said: *Yes. Probably. Come on.*

* * *

They came down out of the boulders onto the old pilgrim's road—the original way to the Wood, abandoned for three centuries, swallowed nearly whole by frost-heave and root—with the moon two hours past the zenith and the copper light pouring down the valley like slow fire. The Weeping Wood rose ahead of them, black against black, and even from here Hazel could see the sap running down the trunks of the outer trees, gleaming wetly, silver-copper in the moonlight. Weeping harder tonight than he had ever seen it. Grieving, or rejoicing. With the Wood, he had learned, the two were not always different things.

No hunters on the pilgrim's road. Veyra had spent her men guarding the passes a man would use. She had forgotten—or perhaps, in some buried corner of her, had refused to remember—that the girl she once was had entered the Wood by no road at all.

The moment his boots touched soil the trees had grown from, the change came over Hazel like a tide crossing a threshold. The silence he'd endured since the Wood's edge deepened and then *broke*, not into the familiar clamor of human dead but into something vaster: a murmur without words, the sound of ten thousand roots drinking, of sap climbing, of the slow green memory of the forest turning over in its sleep. And threaded through it, unmistakable now, close now, the wolves. Not voices. Presence. Attention. The whole wood aware of him, aware of the boy, aware of the two small burning bones at his throat, the way a sleeping animal is aware of a fly.

*You are late,* the Wood seemed to say, without malice, without mercy. *And you are early. Both at once. Run anyway.*

Hazel ran.

The trees closed around them. The Weeping Wood at night under a full moon was a place Hazel's language barely had words for—every trunk bleeding light, every shadow holding the shape of something that might be wolf, the ground soft and strange beneath his boots as though the forest itself were flexing muscle it had not used in three hundred years. Rowan glowed brighter here, gold threading up his neck, his jaw, lighting the hollows of his eyes from within, and the wolves' attention gathered around them as they ran, flowing alongside through the dark just past the reach of sight, an honor guard or a herding or both.

And through it all, Hazel finally let himself understand what he had known since Maren's map, what he had been refusing to say aloud even in the privacy of his own skull:

Two bones. Three seals. The boy was the third. The boy had always been the third—the last living seal, the covenant's final anchor, the piece of the pattern that breathed. To reforge the covenant, Ulva needed all three upon the shattered stone. And what happened to a seal when the covenant reforged—

Maren had told him, in her last letter, in words she'd chosen carefully: *A seal is a thing that holds a door shut, Hazel. Ask yourself what happens to the wax.*

Hazel ran faster, as if speed could outrun arithmetic, and the Wood let him run, and the moon poured down its copper fire, and somewhere far behind, thin and relentless, the horn sounded again—closer now, so much closer, Veyra driving her surviving hunters straight through the paths the wolves would not defend because the wolves *wanted* her to come.

Of course they did. The Wood was not choosing Hazel over Veyra. The Wood was gathering everyone it needed into one place, the way a river gathers rain.

At his chest, Rowan stirred, and lifted his glowing face, and for the first time since the drowned chapel, since Greyfell, since the beginning of everything, the boy opened his mouth and spoke—not a word, but a sound: a single low note, pure and clear, that harmonized with the horn behind them and the hum of the bones and the heartbeat of the moon. A note of summons. A note of arrival.

Ahead, the trees fell away.

The heart of the Wood opened before them like a wound in the world—a clearing wider than any cathedral, ringed by the oldest trees Hazel had ever seen, their trunks scarred with old iron grown over but never healed, their crowns open to the sky. And at its center stood the covenant-stone: shattered, toppled, its broken halves furred with moss and lichen, its fractured faces carved with spirals worn nearly smooth, weeping sap in a dozen streams that pooled at its base in a mirror of liquid light.

Around it, emerging from the treeline in silence, the wolves gathered. Dozens. Scores. Great grey shapes with eyes like banked embers, and among them, larger than all the others, moving with the deliberate grace of old pain, the wolf-mother herself—Ulva, scarred by iron across muzzle and shoulder, her gaze finding Hazel across the clearing with the weight of three hundred years.

Hazel stopped at the clearing's edge, lungs burning, legs shaking, the boy radiant against his chest. Behind him, distant but no longer distant enough, the horn fell silent—which meant she had stopped blowing it, which meant she was close enough not to need it.

Ulva padded forward, alone, and stopped before him, and looked—not at him, but at Rowan. At the seal. At the last living anchor of the covenant her people had paid for in blood and betrayal.

Then her ember-eyes rose to Hazel's, and in them he read no welcome and no wrath. Only a question, old and patient and terrible:

*Do you know what you have brought me?*

Hazel's hand rose—of its own accord, it seemed—and touched the pouch at his throat. Two bones, burning. One child, glowing. One heart in his own chest, hammering out the only answer he had left.

"Yes," he said, and his voice did not shake, and he would remember that with wonder for the rest of whatever life remained to him. "All of it. Everything I owe."

The moon reached its height. The sap-mirror at the stone's base blazed like a sheet of hammered copper. And out of the dark behind him, unhurried, came the sound of iron-shod boots entering the Weeping Wood.
