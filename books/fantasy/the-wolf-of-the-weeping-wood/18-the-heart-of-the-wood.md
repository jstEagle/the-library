## 18. The Heart of the Wood

The moon came up over the Thornfell passes like a blade being drawn slowly from its sheath, and Hazel ran beneath it with the boy in his arms and two bones against his chest, and behind him—always behind him, patient as winter—the sound of hooves.

He had not stopped running since the barrow. His legs had gone past burning into a kind of numbness he did not trust, the way he did not trust ice that looked thick enough to bear a man. The boy weighed almost nothing. That frightened him most of all: that the last seal of an ancient covenant should weigh no more than a bundle of kindling, that all the weight of centuries could be carried by something so small and warm and trusting against his shoulder.

The trees closed around them as they climbed the last rise, and the Weeping Wood opened before them like a wound.

Even in deep winter, even under snow, it was wrong in a way that made his teeth ache. The trunks wept sap in long frozen ropes, amber and dark, like candles left to gutter. The air tasted of iron and old grief. And the silence—Hazel had lived his whole life inside a sea of voices, the dead murmuring in every carved thing, every bone, every grave-soil—he had crossed into the Wood's edge days ago and felt them fall away one by one like lamps going out. But here at the heart, the silence was total. Absolute. A silence with weight, pressing on his eardrums like deep water.

The dead did not come here because the dead were afraid.

Ahead, half-swallowed by root and stone, stood the covenant-stone.

It had been tall once—that much was plain even now, even shattered. It reared up out of the frozen ground like the stump of some colossal tooth, split down its face by a crack wide enough to put his fist through, its carvings worn nearly smooth by three hundred years of weeping water and wind. He could still make out fragments of the old work: interlaced wolves and human hands, a circle of figures holding one another's wrists, and at the crown, where the stone had broken away entirely, the empty socket where something had been set and sealed and betrayed.

Ulva was waiting.

She came out of the treeline without sound, which was itself impossible—a creature her size should have announced herself to the world—but then nothing about Ulva obeyed the world's rules. She was larger than any wolf that had ever drawn breath, gray as old iron, her flanks crosshatched with scars where blessed blades had bitten and burned and never quite killed what could not be killed. Her eyes caught the moonlight and held it, amber and ancient and unbearably sad.

*You came,* she said, and her voice did not travel through air but arrived directly in the marrow of him, the way the dead spoke through their bones. *Betrayer's blood. You came.*

"I came," Hazel said. His voice cracked on it. He set the boy down gently, and Rowan stood beside him, barefoot in the snow as he had been on that first night, unharmed by cold, looking up at Ulva with an expression Hazel could not read—or perhaps could read too well. Not fear. Recognition. Grief, maybe, for something lost so long ago that grief had become the shape of it.

"The bones." Hazel drew them from his shirt with shaking hands. They were small things, deceptively small—one dark and dense as river-stone, the other pale and honeycombed, humming faintly against his palm with a frequency he felt in his back teeth. "Two of three. I know the third. I've known since the map."

Rowan touched his own sternum, just below the hollow of his throat, and nodded once.

*Then do it quickly,* Ulva said. *The moon is rising. When it clears the ridge, the seal breaks, and everything your family stole from us comes undone—and not gently.* Her gaze went past him, down the slope they had climbed, toward the black thread of the pass. *And you are followed.*

"I know."

*You bring her here?*

"She'd have come anyway. Better she arrives exhausted than rested." It sounded like strategy. It was mostly desperation, and they both knew it.

Ulva's lip lifted from teeth as long as his fingers. But she said only: *Place them on the stone.*

Hazel stepped forward across ground that crunched with frozen sap-shards, and laid the two covenant-bones into the shallow basin at the stone's broken crown, where the third socket gaped empty beside them. For a moment nothing happened. Then the pale bone began to glow—not with light exactly, but with memory; he could see shapes moving inside it like figures seen through fogged glass—and the dark one answered with a low tone he heard in his skeleton rather than his ears. The crack in the stone began, very slowly, to weep. Not sap. Water, clear and cold, running down the ancient carvings, filling the worn channels of the interlaced wolves, so that for one moment the whole broken face of the stone seemed alive, the covenant remembered, the circle of joined hands shining wet under the moon.

And the Wood held its breath.

Hazel felt it—the whole forest, thousands upon thousands of acres of bound and bleeding spirits, turning its attention toward this one clearing the way a man turns toward the door when he hears, after years, a key in the lock.

Then the hoofbeats reached them.

* *

Veyra came up out of the treeline alone.

That was somehow worse than if she had brought twenty riders. Hazel watched her walk into the clearing—unhurried, immaculate despite the climb, her gray traveling cloak dusted with snow, her gloved hands folded loosely before her—and understood that the riders had been left behind not from mercy but because whatever she intended required no witnesses. Or permitted none.

Behind her, at the clearing's edge, the shadows between the trees were full of eyes. The wolves of the Weeping Wood had gathered, dozens of them, spectral and solid at once, watching their ancient enemy arrive at the seat of their imprisonment. None of them moved. None of them made a sound. The silence of them was louder than howling.

"Inquisitor Veyra," Hazel said. His voice came out steadier than he expected. He stepped sideways, putting himself between her and the stone, and felt Rowan press close against his leg. "You're a long way from your bonfires."

"Hazel Quintana." She smiled, and it was almost a real smile, which was worst of all. "Bone-carver. Smuggler. Liar. You know, I have interrogated four hundred souls in my service to the Crown, and I have never once needed to raise my voice to you. Do you know why?"

"Because you enjoy the sound of your own."

"Because you want to tell me the truth." She walked closer, unhurried, her boots printing neat holes in the snow. "Everyone does. Truth is the one hunger that never fills. I saw it in your face the day I came to your workshop, and you lied to me about a child, and your soul screamed the truth so loudly I could taste it. So—" she spread her hands, elegant, inevitable— "spare us both. Step aside."

"The bones stay where they are."

"The bones," Veyra said softly, "were never yours. Nor the Wood's. Nor the wolves'." Something moved behind her eyes then, something vast and hungry and terribly old, older than her office, older than her ambition. "Do you know what I was, before the Crown found me? A village girl. Ash-hollow. My village lay at the Wood's border, and one night the wolves came down, and I hid in a meal barrel while they took my father's herd and my neighbor's son, and I heard them breathing outside the wood slats, close enough to touch." Her voice did not waver. That was the horror of it. "They spared me. Do you understand? Out of all of them, they *spared* me, and I have spent thirty years wondering why, and hating them for the question. Fear is a leash, Quintana. I mean to break it. Not for the Crown. For everyone who has ever lain awake listening to the forest breathe."

"You'll consume the covenant," Hazel said. "Maren told me what happens to the one who tries. It doesn't end fear. It ends *her*."

"Maren," Veyra said, and for just a moment something flickered across her face—something that might have been regret, quickly filed away, "burned brightly and briefly. As we all shall. Now."

She moved.

There was no describing it honestly except to say that a woman who had walked calmly all evening simply stopped walking and became motion instead, and Hazel threw himself aside and her gloved hand closed on the rim of the stone above the glowing bones, and she *pulled*.

The sound that came out of the covenant-stone was not a sound. It was the opposite of a sound—a great inhale, the whole Wood drawing breath through clenched teeth—and the light in the two bones bent toward her fingers like iron filings toward a lodestone. Veyra's head tipped back. Her mouth opened. And power poured into her, three hundred years of it, the binding and the betrayal and the rage of every imprisoned spirit, flooding up through her arm, and her arm—

Her arm turned to shadow given flesh, veins standing black beneath the skin, and she laughed.

Around the clearing, the wolves began to scream.

Not howls. Screams—human-throated, anguished, the sound of beings whose forms were being torn loose from their moorings as the covenant's energy drained into the woman drinking at its wellspring. Ulva staggered, huge and gray and suddenly translucent at the edges, her outline flickering like a candle in wind. The trees convulsed, weeping sap in torrents, branches lashing though there was no wind. And Hazel, on his knees in the snow with Rowan clutched against him, felt the Wood's agony come through the ground itself, through his palms, through his teeth—felt, beneath it, the thin bright thread of the boy's presence fraying like rope under strain, because Rowan was the seal, and the seal was being unwound.

"*Stop*," Hazel shouted, and Veyra did not stop, could not stop, her eyes rolled white and ravenous, drinking, drinking—

And Hazel Quintana, who had spent his whole life listening, did the only thing he knew how to do.

He listened harder.

He shut his eyes and plunged into the silence of the heart of the Wood, past the screaming, past the convulsing trees, down into the place where the dead would have been if the dead dared come here—and found, buried at the bottom of everything, in the oldest layer, the sediment of memory that soaked this ground like blood-soaks cloth. Memories soaked into soil for three hundred years. The Wood remembered everything. The Wood had been *watching*.

And among all of it, one memory lay near the surface, recent, still warm: a girl in a meal barrel, thirty winters ago, hearing breath through wood slats.

Hazel opened his eyes, found Veyra's transfixed face, and gave it back to her.

"You're in the barrel," he said, and his voice came out strange, resonant, carrying the way the dead's voices carried, because for these few moments he was not speaking as a man but as the Wood's own remembering. "It is night and it smells of meal-dust and your own urine and you are eight years old. The wolves took the Hendersons' boy an hour ago. You can hear them in the yard—the click of claws on frozen mud, the wet sounds you will not let yourself name. One of them stops at the barrel. Its breath moves the slats against your cheek. You press both hands over your mouth. You are certain you will die, and part of you—part of you is already deciding that if you live, you will never be afraid again, whatever it costs, whoever pays—"

Veyra's head snapped around. Her eyes found his. The black veins in her arm pulsed.

"And then," Hazel whispered, "it speaks. Not words. *Meaning*, straight into your skull, the way the old ones speak. And the meaning is: *Little one, we are sorry. This is not our hunt. Go home and grow old and forget us if you can.* And it walks away, and you sit in the barrel until dawn, and you never tell anyone, because who would believe that the monster apologized?"

The flow of power stuttered.

Veyra stood frozen with her hand on the stone, the stolen light guttering in her veins, and tears were running down her face—she was not sobbing, she was not making any sound at all, she was simply leaking tears the way the trees leaked sap, as if her body had forgotten how else to respond to being broken open. Thirty years of a question, answered. The wolves had spared her. Not randomly. Not mercifully. *Apologetically.* Because the hunt that destroyed her village had not been theirs—or had been theirs in a way that shamed them, in the madness of their long binding, and some remnant of what they had been before the betrayal had looked at a child in a barrel and grieved.

"You *knew*," Veyra breathed. Her voice was unrecognizable, scraped raw. "You—how could you—"

"It's in the ground," Hazel said. "It's always been in the ground. The Wood remembers everything, Inquisitor. Every kindness. Every crime. It remembers what was done to *them*, too. Would you like to see? Would you like to feel what three hundred years of iron and stake and fire feels like, from the inside?" He rose slowly to his feet, the boy's hand gripping his. "Because it's in the bones you're drinking. All of it. Their rage, yes—but underneath the rage, their *grief*. You're swallowing three centuries of grief, Veyra. How does it taste?"

Veyra made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a scream.

Her arm was still sunk in shadow. The bones still glowed. But the pull had faltered, the concentration shattered, and the covenant-stone's light was bending back toward itself, seeking its socket, seeking the empty third place where the last bone—the living one, the willing one—had not yet been placed.

And the moon cleared the ridge.

Full. White. Absolute. Its light fell into the clearing like a judgment, and everywhere at once the Wood cried out—wolves and trees and stone together, one vast voice with ten thousand throats—and Hazel felt the seal begin to fail, felt the boy sway against him, felt time run out like sand through fingers.

Ulva dragged herself forward, translucent, fading, and fixed her dying amber eyes on Hazel.

*Now,* she said. *Whatever you have decided, decide it now.*

Hazel looked down at Rowan. The boy looked up at him, and did not look afraid. He squeezed Hazel's hand once, and stepped toward the stone—toward the empty socket, toward the moonlight, willing, unbearably willing—

"No," Hazel said.

And behind them, at the clearing's edge, Inquisitor Veyra sank to her knees in the snow, weeping without sound, the stolen fire of the covenant still crawling black beneath her skin—and began, very quietly, to scream.
