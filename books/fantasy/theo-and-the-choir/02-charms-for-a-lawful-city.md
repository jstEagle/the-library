## 2. Charms for a Lawful City

The sign above my door reads *Sandoval's Fortunes — Honest Luck at Fair Prices*, and I want you to appreciate what a masterpiece of dishonesty that is.

Honest luck. There's no such thing. Luck is what people call the weather when it agrees with them. What I actually sell is carved bone and river stones and knotted thread, each one blessed with a great deal of ceremony and exactly zero magic. The ceremony is the product. People don't buy luck; they buy the feeling that someone has taken their fear seriously enough to wrap it in red cord and charge them four coppers for it. I am, in a very real sense, a grief-counselor with better margins.

Twelve years ago I would have found all this humiliating. Twelve years ago I was an apprentice hedge-witch who could sour milk with a word and sweeten it again with two more, and I thought that made me important. Now I know it made me kindling. Vell is a lawful city, and law here has teeth.

* * *

You have to understand the Edict to understand me, so let me take you back — back before the rain, before the boy, back to when I was nineteen and stupid in the way only the magically educated can be stupid.

I was apprenticed then to Mistress Corvale, out past the eastern farms where the hedges grew thick enough to hide a cottage and the road signs pointed at nothing anyone official cared about. She was teaching me the First Tongue. Not fluency — nobody living had fluency — but the fragments. The words for *open* and *close*, for *mend* and *remember*. Words that didn't describe things so much as suggest things, the way a good lie suggests a truth.

"Careful," she told me, the day I first spoke one aloud and watched a wilted bean-plant straighten like a soldier hearing his name. "Words like these were how the old kingdoms fought. You're not doing a trick. You're clearing your throat in a room where kings once shouted."

"What happened to the kings?" I asked.

She looked at me for a long moment, and her face did something complicated. "Vell happened," she said.

Here is the history they teach in Vell's schoolrooms, and because Vell won, it is mostly true: there was a war. Not a war of armies, exactly — a war of *sayings*. The old kingdoms had orders of singers who could speak reality into new shapes, and every kingdom's choir sang against every other's until the world itself began to fray. Fields forgot how to grow. Rivers forgot which way was down. Children were born remembering battles they'd never seen, because the songs had soaked into everything, memory bleeding through the walls of the world.

So the Conclave was founded — scholars, priests, survivors — and they ended it. They burned the choirs. They burned the tongues. And they issued the Edict of Silence: no working of magic within Vell's walls, on pain of the Quiet Rooms, from which no one has ever described anything, because no one has ever come out to describe it.

What the schoolrooms leave out is how thorough the burning was. It wasn't just singers. It was herbalists whose poultices worked too well. Midwives who could ease a labor with a hum. Hedge-witches. Apprentices. Anyone who'd ever said a word that the world bothered to listen to.

Mistress Corvale survived it by being small and careful and far away. She taught me the same trade: be small, be careful, be far away. And when I turned twenty-one and got restless — got *confident*, gods help me — she caught me trying a cantrip over the supper pot just to show off and hit me with a spoon hard enough to raise a welt.

"You think the Edict is about punishment," she said. "It isn't. Punishment is just maintenance. The Edict is about *forgetting*. Vell doesn't hate magic, boy. Vell has been taught not to remember it. That's worse. You can hide from hatred. Where do you hide from a hole where a thing used to be?"

I didn't understand her then. Give me credit for understanding her now, twelve years later, running a shop of honest lies in a city that had taught itself to be empty.

* * *

I came to Vell at twenty-three, which any sensible fugitive will tell you is madness — you don't hide in the wolf's mouth. But Vell was the one place the Conclave never thought to search carefully, because searching requires suspicion, and suspicion requires belief that magic exists, and Vell's whole architecture was built on the polite fiction that it didn't. A witch hiding in Vell is like a fish hiding in a desert. Nobody's looking for you in the sand.

So I became Tam Sandoval, charm-seller, a man of no particular history. I rented the narrow shop on Candlemaker's Row with the crooked chimney. I learned to carve bone without ever letting the knife slip into a rhythm, because rhythm invites song and song invites attention. I learned to sell luck that was nothing at all, and I learned — this took longer — to stop flinching every time someone in the street laughed too loudly or swore at a stubborn lock. Most words are just words. I had to relearn that daily, like a man convincing himself the floorboards hold.

And it worked. For twelve years it worked. Do you know what that costs? Not danger — danger would have been almost restful, something to point at. No. What it cost was *practice*. Every single day, the practice of being less. Speaking smaller. Wanting smaller. You take the whole loud animal that is yourself and you fold it down, year after year, until you fit inside a shop the size of a coffin, and then one morning you realize the folding has become the shape, and you can't remember what you were before you creased.

That's the exhaustion nobody warns runaways about. Not the fear. The *tedium* of being a smaller person forever.

* * *

The boy was asleep in the back room when the inspector came. This matters, so hold onto it.

He'd been with me three days by then. Three days of a child who did not speak — not Vellish, not anything — who ate like a sparrow and slept like the dead and stared at my wards with an expression I refused to interpret. I'd told myself he was a stray. Strays wash up on doorsteps in the rain. Strays don't make wards flare. Whatever had happened three nights ago — the light under my door, the taste of copper in the air, the wards singing for the first time since I hung them — I had filed it under *coincidence*, and I am a professional at filing things under coincidence. Twelve years of experience.

The bell over my shop door rang at midmorning, and Inspector Doss walked in.

I want to be fair to Doss. He was not a monster. He was gray in the way furniture is gray, a middle-aged man in a Conclave inspector's coat worn soft at the elbows, and he browsed my shelves with the genuine interest of a hobbyist. He came maybe twice a year. Inspectors always do — not to catch anyone, just to be *seen* checking, which is most of what lawfulness consists of anywhere.

"Master Sandoval." He nodded. "Cold morning."

"Bitter. Can I get you tea?"

"No, thank you." He picked up a knotted luck-charm, turned it over. "For travelers, this one?"

"For travelers, couriers, anxious mothers, and one regular fellow who buys one every month and swears he'd have died twice without them."

Doss smiled politely. "And do they work?"

There it was. The question he asked every time, in the same mild tone, the question that was not really a question but a little trapdoor he liked to stand on. And every time, I gave him the same answer, the one that had kept me alive and small for twelve years:

"They work exactly as much as luck works, Inspector. Which is to say, my customers sleep better, and I eat regularly, and neither of us asks too many questions about why."

"Mm." He set the charm down and drifted along the shelf. Bone birds for safe flight — carved, not conjured. River stones painted with eyes, for seeing trouble coming — paint, just paint. Salt in blue glass, for keeping ill intent out of a house — salt, actual salt, the most honest ingredient I stocked. He examined everything with those incurious eyes, and I stood behind my counter doing the hardest work of my life, which was standing still while every folded-up part of me screamed *look normal, look bored, look like a man who has never once cleared his throat in the wrong room.*

Then he stopped at the shelf behind the counter.

Now, that shelf was supposed to be empty. I kept it bare precisely so it would never hold anything interesting. But three days of harboring a mysterious child had rearranged my housekeeping, and sitting there, plain as a confession, was the boy's cup. Chipped clay, the kind sold ten for a penny. Except the chip wasn't chipped anymore. The break had sealed — smoothly, cleanly, the clay knit like skin over an old wound — sometime in the night, on a shelf where nothing magical had happened, near a sleeping child who could not speak.

Doss picked it up.

My heart did something acrobatic and painful. I heard myself say, in a voice I barely recognized as mine, "Ah — that one's not for sale. Cracked. I keep meaning to throw it out."

"It doesn't look cracked."

"They never do, right up until you pour hot tea in them." I reached across and took it from his hands, and my fingers were steady, because twelve years of practice buys you steady fingers even when the rest of you is falling down a well. I turned it in the light. "See? Hairline. Clay remembers its breaks, Inspector. Doesn't matter how smooth it looks."

Something flickered across his face — not suspicion, exactly. Recognition? Nostalgia? Something older than either. "Clay remembers its breaks," he repeated softly, as if the phrase had been left in his mouth by someone else, long ago. Then he blinked, and the grayness closed back over him, and he bought a traveler's charm for his niece and left a tip and wished me a cold-but-honest morning, and the bell rang, and he was gone.

I stood there holding the cup.

Clay remembers its breaks. I hadn't planned to say that. It had come up out of me like water from a tapped spring — and it was true, was the thing. Clay *did* remember. Everything remembered; that was the dirty secret underneath the Edict, the truth Vell had papered over with laws and Quiet Rooms. The world was full of memory, soaked into stone and thread and clay, waiting for a voice to ask it back. The Conclave hadn't killed magic. It had only silenced everyone who knew how to call it by name.

And three nights ago, something had knocked on my door and called it anyway.

I looked toward the back room, where a mute child slept behind a curtain, and felt the first crack go through the careful masonry of my twelve years — not a collapse, nothing so dramatic. Just a hairline. Just the sound a wall makes right before it decides what it's going to do.

Because here's what I couldn't file under coincidence anymore, try as I might: the wards hadn't just flared that night. They'd flared *in answer*. Twelve years hanging silent, and then a voice outside my door speaking syllables I hadn't heard since Mistress Corvale's kitchen — syllables she'd made me swear never to use, syllables the Edict had supposedly burned out of the world —

The First Tongue. On my doorstep. In the mouth of a child.

I put the cup down very gently, the way you put down a thing that might be listening, and I went to the back room and sat beside the boy's pallet and watched him sleep and told myself, firmly, the way you tell a frightened apprentice: *he's a stray. He's a coincidence. The moon is nearly full and everything is fine.*

The moon, as it happens, was more than full. But I didn't know that yet.
