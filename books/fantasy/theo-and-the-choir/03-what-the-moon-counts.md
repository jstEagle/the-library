## 3. What the Moon Counts

The almanac had belonged to my mistress, and like most things she'd owned it was heavier than it needed to be. Brass corners, a spine stitched with wire because she'd never trusted glue, and pages that smelled of tallow and old winters. I kept it under the floorboard beneath the till, wrapped in oilcloth, next to the other objects that could hang me: a stub of chalk that drew true circles, three drops of my own blood in a vial for reasons I'd stopped explaining to myself years ago, and a letter I hadn't opened.

I told myself the almanac stayed there because selling it would be suspicious. Who keeps a thirty-year-old farmer's almanac in a charm shop in Vell? Nobody. That was the point. It stayed hidden because its existence was the anomaly, not because on certain nights—rainy ones, mostly—I took it out and read it like scripture.

That night was one of the rainy ones.

Theo slept upstairs in the room that had been storage until four nights ago, when a mute child appeared at my door speaking a language that made my wards scream. He slept the way children sleep, which is to say completely, bonelessly, with the total trust of something that has decided you are furniture with opinions. His breathing came through the floorboards in a slow rhythm, and every time it hitched I stopped reading, and every time it resumed I hated myself a little for the relief.

The moon was out. Of course it was. Clouds thin as gauze, the city silvered, and the moon sitting up there fat and patient as a creditor.

I found the page by feel. I'd found it by feel before; I just hadn't let myself finish it.

*Observations of the Thirteenth Turn,* the header read, in my mistress's cramped hand, though the body of the text was older—copied from an almanac of the old kingdoms, the kind the Conclave burned by the cartload during my grandmother's grandmother's time. The arithmetic was simple, which was the worst part. The moon does not count the way we count. Every twelfth year it slips a gear, adds a turn to its ledger, hangs in the sky one month longer than any calendar admits. Farmers knew this. Farmers called it the Long Harvest and planted accordingly and thought nothing more of it, because farmers are sensible people who understand that the sky owes them nothing.

But the old kingdoms hadn't been farmers. They'd been *singers*. And they had built their entire war around a simple observation: the thirteenth turn is a door. Things that were closed can open. Things that were killed can be rekindled—or finished properly, if you know where to stand.

Under a year. Ten months and some change. I did the arithmetic twice hoping to be wrong both times, which is not how arithmetic works but is very much how denial works.

*The Choir can be rekindled or killed.*

Killed. As if it were alive. As if it were a thing with a pulse you could find with two fingers pressed to the world's wrist.

I put the almanac down and looked at my hands. Charm-seller's hands. Ink-stained, burn-marked from a soldering iron I used for "repairing" lockets, ordinary hands that had spent twelve years learning to look ordinary. Twelve years since I'd stood in my mistress's garden and sworn—actually sworn, with chalk and blood and everything—that I was done. Done with hedge-work, done with the First Tongue, done with the whole rotten inheritance of a craft that got villages burned and apprentices scattered like seed.

Twelve years of selling luck that was really nothing at all. Copper charms stamped with a four-leaf pattern, glass beads I called "storm-warding" because storm-warding sounded lawful and useful and didn't sound like what my fingers itched to do, which was reach into the seams of things and tug.

And now there was a boy asleep over my head whose *words bent bread*.

Coincidence. I said it aloud to the empty shop, testing the weight of it. "Coincidence."

It weighed nothing. That's how I knew it was false. True words have mass; lies are buoyant.

Let me walk through the coincidence, since no one else was awake to hear me do it. A mute child appears—at my door, mine, out of every door in a city of forty thousand—in the rain, on the one night my wards flare for the first time in twelve years. He speaks the First Tongue, which maybe six living people can identify and perhaps three can speak, and one of those three is dead and buried under an apple tree two provinces east. He doesn't cry. He doesn't startle. When I burned my thumb on the kettle he looked at my hand with an expression I recognized, because I'd seen it in a mirror once, aged nine: the expression of someone watching a thing behave wrongly and knowing, in their bones, that they could make it behave rightly if only they were permitted.

And the moon, meanwhile, was beginning its thirteenth turn. The interval in which, legend says, the Choir can be rekindled or killed.

You didn't need hedge-craft to do that sum. You needed only honesty, and honesty was the one charm I'd never stocked.

* * *

I climbed the stairs quietly, which took effort, because the fourth step announces visitors like a town crier and I'd never fixed it precisely so that I'd always hear myself coming home. Theo lay curled in the truckle bed, one fist under his chin, breath fogging faintly in the cold room because I couldn't afford coal in October and heating charms were, well. Not something I sold.

He'd drawn on the wall again. In the dark I could just see it: chalk lines, careful ones, spiraling out from a point behind the headboard. Not scribbles. Spirals with structure—each ring spaced exactly as wide as the last, the way ripples space themselves, the way sound spaces itself. I'd wiped them off twice. He'd redrawn them twice, identically, to the line. You don't get identical from memory that loose. You get identical from memory that *isn't memory*, that's something older wearing memory's coat.

"Talk to me," I whispered. He didn't, of course. Mute, or near enough—except for those words at the door, those syllables in the First Tongue that had lit my wards up like festival lanterns. Except that yesterday morning, when the bread went stale, he'd held it in both hands and made a sound low in his throat, half-hum, and the bread had come back soft. I'd eaten the evidence. That was the level of my courage: I ate the miracle rather than think about it.

I sat on the floor beside the bed, back against the wall, and let myself think about it now, because the almanac had pried the lid off and there was no resealing it.

Here is what my mistress taught me about the Choir, in the single lesson she ever gave me on the subject, delivered while pruning apple trees as if the words were too dangerous to say indoors. She said: before Vell, before the Edict, the old kingdoms fought their war with singers instead of soldiers. The Choir was their weapon and their treasury and their god—a collective memory, hundreds of voices braided into one knowing, and whatever the Choir remembered, reality agreed to. That was the whole of magic at its height. Not spells. *Agreement.* And then the kingdoms saw what they'd made, or saw what it cost, and they ended it the only way they knew how: they burned the archives, cut the throats of the singing orders, and salted the language itself out of human mouths. The Edict of Silence wasn't Vell's invention. Vell just inherited it, the way you inherit a scar.

"They didn't kill it," she'd said, snapping an apple branch. "You can't kill a memory by killing rememberers. Memory goes where it goes. Into ground, into children, into—" and here she'd stopped, and pruned another branch, and never finished the sentence.

Into children.

I looked at the sleeping boy and the chalk spirals and felt the floor tilt gently, the way it does on a boat.

"No," I told him, softly, so as not to wake him, which was absurd, because I was the one who needed not to be woken. "No. There were eleven years between us and the last resonant birth on record, and the records are ash anyway, and you're just a lost boy who hums at bread. Children hum at bread all the time."

They don't. I knew they don't. I've known four children in my life as an adult, and none of them hummed at bread, and none of them drew perfect spirals, and none of them looked at locked doors the way Theo looked at them—not wanting in, but *expecting* them to realize they should open.

Denial, I'll grant myself, is a craft like any other. It takes practice. I'd had twelve years of practice at smaller denials—the charm that was almost magic, the ward that was almost maintenance, the life that was almost a life—and I brought all that skill to bear on this one and it held for nearly six hours. I sat on the floor, I catalogued innocent explanations, I even drafted, in my head, the advertisement I'd run in spring: *Ward foundling available for honest work, speaks little, eats less.*

Near dawn, the denial curdled. Curdling is the right word—it happens all at once, and afterward you can't unmix it.

What curdled it was this: I heard him turn over in his sleep and murmur. One word. Not First Tongue—something smaller, sleepier, a child-word. But the candle on my desk downstairs, three floors of timber away, guttered and relit. I heard no wind. There was no wind.

He was doing it asleep. Untrained, unwilled, leaking wonder into the world like a split sack of grain, and the thirteenth turn hadn't even properly begun, and somewhere above us that patient creditor-moon was ticking off its extra month, and somewhere across the city the Conclave's listeners—because there were always listeners; I'd been dodging them for twelve years and they were good, they were thorough, they had instruments—somewhere those listeners were going to start hearing a resonance in the low quarter that had never been there before.

If I turned him out, he'd be found within a week. Found and registered and silenced, and *silenced* in Conclave usage is not a euphemism, it's a procedure, and I have seen one silencing, from across a square, when I was nineteen, and I still can't describe the sound the crowd made afterward. Something between a sigh and a swallow.

If I kept him, I was keeping a lamp lit in a city whose entire constitution was written by people terrified of light.

Either way, the moon counted on.

I got up stiffly, knees cracking like the fourth step, and went to the wall, and this time I didn't wipe the chalk spirals away. I looked at them for a long moment. Then, below them, in the corner where the wainscoting met, I added a small mark of my own—a hedge-sign, the first I'd drawn openly in twelve years. A warding knot. Crude, small, meant to do one thing: keep his leak contained, keep the resonance muffled, buy us quiet. It wouldn't fool a real listener forever. It might buy ten months.

Ten months to decide whether I was hiding a child or raising a weapon someone else had built and abandoned on my doorstep like a parcel.

I blew out the candle—manually, with a pinch, the way a lawful man does—and stood in the dark listening to him breathe, and made myself a promise I already suspected I wouldn't keep: *just through winter. Just until the thaw. Then I'll find someone who knows more than I do, and hand it off, and go back to selling nothing at all.*

Downstairs, as the sky went grey, I unlocked the front door to sweep the step, because a lawful shopkeeper sweeps his step, and lawful men notice things like weather and deliveries.

There was a letter on the mat. No courier's stamp. No seal. Just my name—my true name, the one I hadn't used aloud in twelve years—written in ink that was still faintly warm.

The handwriting was my mistress's.
