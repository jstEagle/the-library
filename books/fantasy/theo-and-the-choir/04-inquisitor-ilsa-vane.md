## 4. Inquisitor Ilsa Vane

The resonance woke me at the hour when the candles gutter and honest people pretend to be asleep.

I want to be precise about that word—*resonance*—because precision is the only inheritance the Conclave ever gave me worth keeping. A resonance is not a sound. It is not a feeling, though it arrives dressed as one, like grief wearing your mother's shawl. It is an absence of stillness. The Edict of Silence settled over Vell two hundred years ago like snowfall over a battlefield, and everything it touched went quiet—not dead, quiet—and when something moves beneath that snow, you feel it the way you feel a floorboard shift under a stranger's weight. You do not hear it. You simply know the house is no longer empty.

So at the fourth bell of the morning, alone in my office above the Hall of Registers with three fingers of plum brandy I had promised myself I wouldn't pour, I set down the glass, and I said aloud to no one, "There you are."

Twelve years the city had been silent as a held breath. Twelve years since the last registered practitioner was burned out of the Tanner's Row cellar where she'd been curing fevers for half the neighborhood—the same neighbors, I note, who reported her, because gratitude has never once survived contact with fear. Twelve years of nothing but paperwork, which is what peace looks like when you file it.

And now this. Faint. Flickering. Somewhere south of the Weft, in the low quarter where the rain comes in off the salt flats and the buildings lean on each other like drunkards pretending they can still stand.

I finished the brandy anyway. Discipline is knowing which promises are load-bearing.

* * *

You should understand something about me before we go further, because everyone in Vell thinks they understand Inquisitor Ilsa Vane, and they are all wrong in instructive ways.

They think I hate magic. I don't. Hatred requires proximity; hatred is what you feel for the dog that bit you or the neighbor whose rooster wins. What I have is older than hate and colder. Magic killed my family, and what remains after that is not hatred but *accounting*. Someone must keep the ledger. Someone must write down every name and every date and every small bright thing that was taken, and balance it against nothing, because there is nothing to balance it against. That is the work. I am good at the work.

I was nine years old the autumn the fever came through Harrowgate. My father was a cooper; my mother kept bees; my sister Liss was four and could already recite the Conclave's Articles better than the priest, which made everyone laugh, which is the sort of memory that turns traitor on you decades later in the fourth bell of the morning. There was a woman who came through the village that year selling charms. Little knotted cords, river-stones wrapped in wire, a salve "guaranteed by the old ways" to draw fever out through the soles of the feet. My mother bought six of them with money we did not have, because love makes economists of us all, and terrible ones.

Liss wore hers until the skin under the cord went grey. My father died on a Tuesday. My mother followed him inside the week, still clutching her stone, still believing, right up until the belief stopped being a comfort and became the last lie she ever told herself.

The charm-seller was gone by then. Of course she was. They always are. And I stood in the doorway of a house that smelled of wax and sickness and bee-smoke, aged nine years old in a single season, and I understood—with the terrible clean clarity only children and fanatics are permitted—I understood that the old ways were not medicine. They were theft. They took coin from the desperate and gave back string, and when the string failed they were nowhere to be found, because the whole trade runs on the assumption that the dead cannot complain to the authorities.

People say the Conclave is cruel. People say the Edict broke the world, that we burned wonders along with dangers, that we amputated the city's soul to save its skin. I have heard every version of the argument, usually from men drinking wine their grandfathers stole. But here is what those men forget, and what I will never forget, because forgetting is not among my available luxuries:

Magic murdered my family. Not the fever. The fever would have come regardless—but the *hope* was murder. The hope was sold to my mother by someone who knew better, and hope purchased from a fraud kills more surely than any plague, because the sick stop fighting while they wait for miracles to arrive.

So when people ask me—and they do ask, usually through lawyers—why I hunt practitioners who mostly just cure warts and find lost goats, I tell them the truth: because every wonder begins as a kindness, and every kindness in that trade ends in a doorway, and a child standing in it, doing arithmetic with the dead.

I am aware this is not a comfortable philosophy. Comfort is not among my available luxuries either.

* * *

By midmorning I had called up the resonance maps.

The Conclave keeps instruments for this—brass spheres filled with ash and silence, calibrated annually, worshipped by no one but maintained obsessively by everyone, which is the true religion of bureaucracies everywhere. The senior cartographer, a nervous man named Pell who irons his robes, brought them to my office personally, which meant he had already looked at them, which meant he was already frightened.

"The Weft again," he said, unrolling the chart across my desk as though it might bite him. "South quadrant. Very faint, ma'am. Could be instrument drift. The spheres have been temperamental since the humidity—"

"Pell." I let him hang a moment. "In nineteen years, how many times has instrument drift produced a reading in the same quarter twice?"

He looked at the chart. Two marks, both in the low quarter near the salt-gate, eleven days apart. He knew the answer. Everyone who works the registers knows the answer, which is zero, which is why he'd carried the map up himself instead of sending a clerk, because some messages you deliver with your own hands so that history records you were on the right side of them.

"Zero times, ma'am," he said.

"Then it isn't drift." I traced the quarter with one finger. The Weft: tanners' yards and charm-shops-that-weren't, pawnbrokers, a fish market that smelled like a crime scene, tenements stacked so greedily against each other that fire spread between them the way gossip does. If I had to hide a working, that is exactly where I'd hide one—buried in a district where the Conclave's authority arrived secondhand and late, where every third door opened onto a room full of things we'd forbidden, sold quietly by people who called themselves lucksellers and herbalists and wise-folk, as though the vocabulary changed the arithmetic.

"Draft a sweep order," I said. "Standard pattern. Four inspectors, door to door, starting at the salt-gate and working inward. Quietly, Pell. No bells, no proclamations. If there's a practitioner down there, I want them comfortable before they know they're caught. Fear makes them stupid, and stupid practitioners burn whole streets."

"Yes, ma'am." He gathered the maps, hesitated. "And if it's nothing? If it's a false reading and we turn the Weft over and frighten a thousand honest people for nothing?"

I considered telling him what he wanted to hear. I considered it the way one considers a warm coat in cold weather: briefly, and knowing better.

"Then a thousand honest people are frightened for a night," I said, "and they'll curse my name in their cups, and life will go on. But if it is *not* nothing, and we waited, and something grew roots down there—" I tapped the map, the two small inked stars sitting in the Weft like splinters. "My family died of waiting, Pell. Everyone always waits. Waiting is the most popular form of cowardice in the world. Draft the order."

He drafted the order.

* * *

I walked the Weft myself that evening, which surprised my inspectors and would have surprised the district more if they'd known. There is a school of thought in the Hall that chief inquisitors should direct operations from offices, surrounded by maps and clerks, descending upon districts like weather. I have never subscribed to it. Maps lie. Clerks summarize, and summaries are where truth goes to be embalmed. If I am going to spend the Conclave's authority in a place, I intend to smell the place first.

The Weft smelled, as predicted, of fish and tallow and wet stone. Rain had started again—that fine salt-grit drizzle off the flats that gets into everything and forgives nothing—and the district hunched under it, lamplit windows steamed gold, gutters running brown. Ordinary misery. Honest misery, even. I have a professional tenderness for places like this, which I keep strictly to myself, because a soft inquisitor is a useless one.

We worked outward from the salt-gate. Door to door. My inspectors asked their questions—household size, trades practiced, any unusual purchases, anyone ill lately, anyone *lucky* lately—and I watched faces. That is ninety percent of the work, watching faces. Guilt has a texture. So does fear, and so does the specific strained blankness of a person deciding, in real time, whether to lie to you.

Most doors gave us nothing but suspicion and bad tea. A cooper's widow. A rope-maker with eight children and the eyes of a man doing sums forever. An old salt-boiler who told me the Conclave could boil its own head and I thanked him for his cooperation, which confused him wonderfully.

Then, on a crooked lane called Candlemakers' Row—a street named for a trade that left it generations ago—we came to a shop with green paint peeling off its lintel and a hand-lettered sign: **SANDOVAL'S — CHARMES & LUCKE, HONESTLY PRICED**.

Honestly priced. I stood in the drizzle reading that sign longer than it deserved, and something about it itched. Charm-sellers, in my long experience, price things however the market of desperation will bear. Honesty is not their native tongue. Either this Sandoval was a fool, or he was very clever indeed, and in my line of work fools are rare and cleverness is a confession.

The shop was closed, lights out, wards—ordinary ones, the legal kind, iron nails and salt lines, the spiritual equivalent of a padlock—undisturbed. But I stood there a moment in the rain with my bare hand flat against the door, the way you'd check a horse for fever, and I listened.

Nothing. Stillness. The deep, lawful quiet of a city that had learned its lesson two hundred years ago and paid for the tuition in blood.

Except.

Beneath the stillness—so faint I might have invented it, so faint I *wanted* to have invented it—there was a tremor. Like the memory of a bell after the ringing stops. Like a floorboard settling under weight in a house that should be empty. Eleven days apart, the map had said. Twice. In this quarter. Perhaps within a hundred paces of where I stood getting rained on.

"Ma'am?" said Inspector Dree behind me. "Anything?"

"No," I said. "Note the premises. Proprietor's name, trade license, tax record. Routine follow-up next week."

Dree wrote it down. We moved on, door to door, deeper into the winding dark of the Weft, and I told myself the standard things. That readings fade. That resonance without confirmation is rumor with instruments. That twelve years of silence do not break in a single season. I am very good at telling myself things; it is the closest skill I have to prayer.

But walking back toward the Hall through the rain, past the shuttered green-painted shop with its absurd honest sign, I found myself doing the other arithmetic. The one I do at fourth bell with the brandy. The one where I am nine years old and the door is open and the house smells of wax, and somewhere out in the dark a seller of small mercies is counting coins that will buy a grieving woman six knotted strings and a lie.

The ledger has been balanced for twelve years, I thought. Let it stay balanced. Let it be drift.

It was not drift. Some part of me knew it even then, knew it the way you know a storm is coming from the ache in old scars—scars, in my case, that were never mine, that belonged to a house and a family and a sister who could recite the Articles, and that ache has never once been wrong.

Two days later, the follow-up report on Sandoval's came across my desk, and I read it standing up, and I read it twice.

The proprietor had held his license for twelve years. Perfect taxes. No complaints. No recorded illness among his customers—no recorded *anything*, which was itself remarkable, a shop in the Weft with a spotless ledger was like a pond with no reflection. And appended to the routine inspection notes, in Inspector Dree's careful clerkish hand, a single observation flagged for my attention:

*Neighbor (fishmonger, adjacent premises) reports proprietor has recently taken in a child. Nephew, he says. No record of any nephew. Neighbor adds the boy does not speak. Has never spoken a word since arriving.*

A mute child, in the Weft, eleven days after the first resonance, living above a charm-shop that was too clean.

I sat down slowly at my desk, and outside the window the thirteenth moon—though I did not yet know to call it that, though no one in Vell had called it anything for two hundred years—was rising fat and pale over the salt flats, one night shy of full.

"Routine follow-up," I said to the empty office, and reached for a fresh sheet of paper, and began drafting a warrant instead.
