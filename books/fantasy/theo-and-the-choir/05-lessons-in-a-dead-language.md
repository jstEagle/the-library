## 5. Lessons in a Dead Language

I want it on the record that I did not name him Theo out of any grand sentiment. I named him after my second-best teacup.

The cup in question was a squat brown thing with a chip in the rim and a glaze like pond water, and when I asked the boy — on his third morning in my shop, over breakfast he'd somehow made taste of honey without either of us touching the honey — what I should call him, he looked at me with those grey eyes and said nothing, because saying things was rather the whole problem. So I looked around for inspiration, saw the cup, and said, "Theo," which is Vellish for *small brown vessel*, more or less, and he repeated it back to me in that voice that made the floorboards hum, and that was that.

He was named after crockery. He has since forgiven me. Mostly.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding a child who speaks the First Tongue: you cannot hide him by keeping him quiet. Silence, it turns out, is not the absence of the language. Silence is just one of its words, and he was already fluent in it.

So I made the decision that every sensible bone in my body voted against: I taught him more.

The logic went something like this — and I have had twelve years to polish this particular self-deception into something almost load-bearing — if the words were going to come out of him regardless, better they come out small. Better he learned *bread* and *door* than whatever word lived behind his eyes that made my wards scream like gutted cats. A child taught to whisper can live a whole life whispering. I knew. I'd been doing it since I was barely older than him.

Besides — and here the wiser part of me, the part I kept locked in the cellar with the good brandy, murmured its objection — besides, I wanted to know what he could do. Curiosity is the hedge-witch's original sin. My mistress beat it into me that curiosity was how practitioners died, and then she died of it herself, which I choose to find instructive rather than ironic.

We began in the back room among the drying racks and the jars of things I labeled *flour* and labeled *also flour*, because a lawful city rewards a man whose shelves are boring.

"Say *bread*," I told him.

He said *bread*.

The loaf on the table rose.

Not metaphorically. Not the gentle doming of a well-proofed dough. It *rose*, swelling against its cloth like something inhaling, cracking its own crust in long golden seams, and the smell that came off it was the smell of every bakery I had ever walked past hungry, concentrated, weaponized. My mouth watered so hard my eyes stung. The boy looked at the bread. The bread steamed. Neither of them seemed particularly surprised.

"That," I said, in a voice an octave above its usual register, "is going to be a problem."

Because here's the thing about bread in Vell: bread is counted. Bread is registered. A baker who produces twice his flour gets visited by the Guild of Weights, and the Guild of Weights sends for the Conclave, and the Conclave sends for someone like Ilsa Vane, and Ilsa Vane sends for a very deep hole. I had spent twelve years selling luck that wasn't, charm-bottles full of colored oil and confidence, and the entire architecture of my survival rested on the principle that *nothing in this shop actually works.*

And now there was a child in my back room who had improved a loaf by naming it.

I ate the evidence. All of it. Both of us sat there while I systematically destroyed a miracle one slice at a time, and Theo watched me with an expression I would learn very well over the following weeks — patient, faintly amused, the look of someone watching a grown man panic at breakfast.

* * *

By the fourth week we had rules.

Rule one: no words indoors except the ones on the list. The list hung inside the pantry door, written in my own cipher, and it contained forty-one safe nouns — bread, water, wool, stone, chair — each tested individually in the yard behind the shop at three in the morning, wrapped in three layers of silence-wards that cost me two months' profits and a favor from a smuggler I owed less than nothing to.

Rule two: no words at all on market days. On market days Theo stayed in the back room with a slate and chalk, drawing circles. He drew circles constantly. Spirals, rings, concentric whorls like ripples in standing water. I told myself it was just what children drew. I told myself a great many things in those weeks; I was very industrious about it.

Rule three, written largest: *if the wards sing, we go.* There was a bolt-hole under the floorboards, a satchel packed with coin and dried plums, and a route out of the city I had never once used in twelve years of needing it. I showed Theo where it was. He traced the map with one finger and then said, in the Tongue, a short soft phrase I didn't know, and the candle flame bent toward him like a listener.

"What does that mean?" I asked.

He considered. Then he pointed at the candle, at himself, at the map, and finally — gently, the way you touch something breakable — at me.

I decided it meant *understood*. I decided that very hard, the way you decide things at night with the lamp turned low.

The lessons themselves were the strangest work I've ever done, and I once spent a summer de-fleasing a witch's familiar that was legally a bear. Ordinary teaching goes one direction: the knower pours into the unknown. With Theo it went both directions, and the direction *out* of him had consequences.

I taught him *cup*. He said it, and the chipped brown teacup — my namesake gift to him — knit itself smooth before our eyes, the chip flowing shut like wax, the glaze going glossy and new. I nearly wept. That cup was the last thing my mistress ever gave me that wasn't a scar or a debt.

Then I taught him *broken*, because I am a thorough teacher and thought he should know both sides of a concept.

Every glass in the shop shattered. Every jar, every bottle, the window, the mirror I'd been avoiding my own reflection in for a decade anyway. Forty seconds of ringing chaos, dust and gin and pickled newt everywhere, and Theo stood in the middle of it with both hands pressed over his mouth, stricken, and I understood two things simultaneously: that the Tongue had no separate word for what he meant, and that he had understood *broken* to mean *make everything honest*. Because that's what the language did. It didn't describe reality. It *insisted*.

I swept up for two days. I sold the story to my neighbors as a cart accident outside. Old Ferren from the chandlery believed me, or pretended to, and brought over a bottle of plum wine as consolation, and I drank it alone that night sitting on the floor of the wrecked back room while Theo slept upstairs, and I let myself think the thought I'd been refusing:

*Someone built this child.*

Not born. Built. No natural speaker of the First Tongue had existed in two hundred years — the old kingdoms had burned the language out of the world root and branch, that was the founding story of Vell itself, the Edict of Silence, the great unmaking of the words that command. And yet here was a boy of perhaps seven who carried it whole in his mouth, every syllable loaded, every noun a small god. You don't *find* that kind of knowledge in an orphan. Someone had put it there, carefully, syllable by syllable, the way you'd pack a weapon for transport.

And someone else, presumably, wanted it delivered somewhere.

I sat on the floor among my own broken inventory and realized that I wasn't hiding a child.

I was raising one. And whatever hands had made him were going to want him back, and I had exactly one skill relevant to that situation, which was disappearing, and even that skill had a shelf life measured in wards and luck that wasn't.

* * *

But I'll tell you the truth, since this account is for no one: the terror was only half of it. God help me, the other half was joy.

There is a morning I keep returning to, the way the tongue keeps returning to a missing tooth. Sixth week, maybe seventh. Frost on the yard. Theo had learned the word *green* the day before, and I'd watched him spend an hour applying it to everything — the moss on the north wall, the bottle-glass, my coat, my face, laughing his silent laugh at that last one. And then in the morning I came out to the yard and the frost had *gone green*. Not melted. Turned. Every crystal across the flagstones glittered emerald, and the light coming off it made the whole little yard look like the bottom of the sea, and Theo stood in the middle of it in his too-big coat with his arms spread like he was holding the color down, and he looked at me with such naked triumph —

Well. I laughed. First time in years I laughed the way children laugh, from the floor of myself, and he laughed back, and for one whole minute neither of us was hiding anything from anybody.

Then the green drained out of the frost as the sun cleared the roofs, ordinary white again, and I remembered that the Conclave burned people like him, and that I had a shop to open, and that the smile on my face was now another item on the list of things I couldn't afford.

That's the shape of it, living with Theo. Wonder and terror sharing a page, sharing a room, sharing a bed of coals between them. You cannot have the green frost without the shattered glass. You cannot teach him *morning* without wondering what the Conclave would pay to hear him say it.

* * *

It was near the end of the second month that he found the almanac. Or rather, I found him finding it — sitting cross-legged on the shop floor with the old thing open on his knees, turning pages with a care that would have shamed a librarian, stopping at the tables of lunar reckonings in the back.

"Theo." I held out my hand for it. "That's not a toy."

He gave it up willingly enough, but he tapped the page first. The moon-tables. The long spiral of years where the reckoning runs twelve turns and begins again, twelve and twelve and twelve, and then — I felt my stomach drop before my mind caught up — the thin column of annotations where the cycle *doesn't* begin again. Where the moon runs a thirteenth turn, once in a generation, once in a lifetime if you're unlucky. The year my mistress went quiet and stopped answering letters. The year the Conclave doubled its inspections of the low quarter.

Under the thirteenth turn, in some dead compiler's hand, a single marginal note: *the count that listens.*

Theo looked up at me. He touched the note. He touched his own throat.

"No," I said, before I knew I was speaking. "No, whatever you're thinking, no."

He said something in the Tongue, softly, and though I didn't know the words, I knew their shape. I had heard that shape in the letter my mistress sent me at fifteen, the one line she ever wrote in the old script: *when the moon miscounts, wake nothing.*

Outside, the evening bell rang across Vell, all nine towers, orderly and dumb and safe. Inside my shop, a boy who could name a thing into being waited for me to explain why the sky was counting to thirteen.

I told him it was time for supper.

It was not time for supper. But I needed my hands busy and my back to him, because if he'd seen my face just then he'd have known what I already knew and hadn't said aloud to anyone, including myself:

The thirteenth turn wasn't coming someday. By my reckoning, it began in the spring. Less than a year. Whatever the Choir was — and my mistress's grave-silence, and the Conclave's fear, and the boy himself all agreed it *was* something — the interval in which it could be rekindled or killed was opening like a door, and I was standing in the doorway holding a child's hand.

That night I bolted the door twice, checked the wards, and took out the one thing I'd never shown Theo: a letter, thirty years old, in my mistress's hand, that I'd read so many times the creases had gone to fur. I read it again by candlelight while the boy slept overhead.

*If you are reading this and the moon has begun to miscount,* it ended, *then you are already too late, and I am sorry, and do not go looking for answers in the low quarter.*

Which is, of course, precisely where I went the next morning.
