## 6. The Hedge-Witch's Last Letter

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Bad news ought to arrive with some ceremony—a storm, a knocking at the door at midnight, at minimum an omen. Mine came folded inside a delivery of candle wax I hadn't ordered, wrapped in brown paper, addressed in a hand I would have known anywhere even if it had been written in mud on the underside of a boot.

*For the boy who steals my thyme.* That was all it said.

I sat down on the shop stool and did not open it for the better part of an hour. This is a thing about grief nobody warns you: it has excellent manners. It waits until you're holding something mundane—a parcel, a kettle, a spoon—and then it puts its hand on your shoulder like an old friend.

Maren had been dead eight months. I'd burned rosemary for her in a chipped dish on my windowsill, said the words wrong on purpose because she'd have hated them done properly, and gotten on with selling luck to people who didn't know they were buying nothing. That was the whole of mourning when you lived in Vell. You grieved quietly, in a city that had outlawed the louder versions of it.

Now her handwriting was in my hands, and she was going to make me read it anyway, because Maren Oduya had never once in her life let me have the last word.

I opened it.

---

*Tamsin.*

*If you are reading this, then the wax-seller kept his promise and you kept your shop, which means you are still hiding, which means I am dead and still annoyed with you. Good. Let that be the first thing between us: I am annoyed with you, from beyond, professionally.*

*You will want to know why I am writing instead of haunting you. The answer is that hauntings are illegal in Vell and I died obeying the Edict to the letter, out of spite, so that when they finally come to collect my soul they'll find nothing but paperwork.*

I laughed. It came out of me like a cough, sudden and ugly, and I had to put the letter down on the counter and press the heels of my hands into my eyes for a while. Eight months. Eight months of missing her, and she'd waited until now to make me laugh about it, because she knew—she always knew—that if she'd led with kindness I'd have stopped reading.

*I will come to the point, because you never learned to sit still and I refuse to write you a novel.*

*The child at your door. Yes, I know about the child. I have known since before he was born, and I need you to listen to me now in the way you never listened to anything except when I was angry, so imagine me angry.*

*The Choir was not destroyed.*

---

I read that sentence four times. Then I got up, walked to the window, checked the street both ways the way you do when you've spent twelve years checking streets both ways, drew the shade, lit no candles, and read it a fifth time by the grey light coming through the fabric.

*They told us we burned it out. They stood in the Ashen Square in their grey robes and they showed us the pyres and they called it peace, and I believed them, Tamsin, because believing was easier than what the alternative asked of me. I was there at the burning. I carried one of the torches. I want that written plainly, because everything else I say will sound like wisdom, and I will not have you mistake me for a wise woman. I was a coward with good posture.*

*Here is what actually happened, as near as I have been able to piece together in the years since, most of it after I swore I was finished piecing things together:*

*A choir is not a building. You cannot burn a harmony. What they killed that day were the singers—the vessels, the throats, the bodies the song wore. But a song lives wherever it can find a mouth. The old texts call it scattering. When the Conclave unmade the Choir, it didn't die. It broke apart into pieces small enough to hide, and it hid the only place a song can hide.*

*In children.*

I looked through the wall toward the back room, where Theo was asleep. He slept like the dead, that boy, curled around the rag doll I'd won off a dice game I'd cheated at, breathing in that deep, unbothered rhythm of the very young. Two weeks he'd been with me. Two weeks of bread rising unasked and cups knitting themselves shut and wards flaring like festival lanterns every time he talked in his sleep.

He talked in his sleep. I hadn't let myself think about what that meant yet. There's a skill to not-thinking, and twelve years in Vell had made me very skilled.

*Eleven of them, as far as anyone could count. Born across twenty years, scattered across the old kingdoms, each one carrying a fragment of the song the way a shattered bell carries a note. The Conclave knows. Understand me—they have always known. Why do you think the Edict hunts children's magic harder than any other kind? Why do you think the resonant ones die so conveniently? Fevers. Falls. Ordinary causes, arriving with extraordinary punctuality. They have been killing the Choir one quiet funeral at a time for two decades, and calling each one a mercy.*

*And here is the part where you will hate me, and you should, and I deserve it:*

*I helped them find three of the eleven.*

---

There was a long moment where the shop was very quiet and I was very far away from it. I remember the smell of the candle wax shipment, tallow and something chemical, cheap stuff. I remember a moth bumping against the shaded window, patient as a debt collector.

Three of the eleven. My mistress. The woman who taught me to read weather in a sheep's entrails, who fed me nettle soup and corrected my pronunciation of words that could blister paint, who taught me that magic was a responsibility before it was a gift—she had walked children to the pyre. Or whatever the Conclave used instead of pyres now. Probably paperwork. Probably a room with good lighting and a sympathetic clerk.

*Before you judge me—and again, do judge me, I insist—know what I believed. I believed the war stories. Everyone did. Magic tore the world's memory open, they said. The Choir sang cities into forgetting their own names. Children woke up not knowing their mothers. Whole bloodlines erased mid-laugh. I lost people in those years too, Tamsin, though you were too young to be told. My sister sang in the last choir. I found what was left of her mind in an asylum outside Corveth, smiling at a wall, singing to a wall, knowing nothing but the song. So when the Conclave came offering an ending—when they said, help us finish this and no more children will ever carry it—I said yes. I gave them names. Three names. I have written them here and struck them through, because I cannot bear to look at them and I cannot bear to leave them unwritten, because unwritten feels like pretending they never existed, and they existed, and they deserved better than me.*

*Then I grew older, and older people do a dangerous thing: they start reading documents instead of rumors. And what I found, in the places I was still allowed to look, did not match the story I had bought my conscience with.*

*The war did not end because the Choir was destroyed. Read that again. The war ended because the Edict itself was a working—an act of the Choir's own power, turned backward. They took the song's deepest verse, the one that unmakes remembering, and they laid it over the whole world like a shroud. No one remembers why the war started. Not the kings. Not the Conclave. Not me. We burned a generation of singers and called it peace, and we cannot even remember what we were avenging.*

*The Choir's power was never destruction, boy. It was cost. Every miracle takes a memory to pay for it. That was always the bargain, and the old choirs understood it, and they chose their payments carefully, and sometimes they chose wrongly, and that is how wars happen—not from evil, but from arithmetic done in panic. The Conclave didn't end the bargain. It just made sure no one would ever again be wise enough to strike it.*

*Which brings me to the twelfth child. Yours.*

*Let him sleep.*

---

I set the letter down. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Everything else in me was shaking, but the hands had apparently decided to handle this like professionals.

*Let him sleep,* she'd written. As if it were a choice. As if the boy had a lid I could close. As if I hadn't watched a cracked cup knit itself whole because he'd wanted tea and hadn't wanted to bother me.

*Do not teach him. Do not name him—naming anchors a vessel, and an anchored vessel begins to wake. Do not take him to any of the old market people; half of them would sell him to the Conclave by morning and the other half would try to sing him awake out of nostalgia. Above all, do not let him speak the Tongue aloud past the counting words. A fragment that learns its own melody becomes a singer. A singer becomes a choir. And a choir, woken without thirteen voices to spend its costs wisely, will pay for every miracle with whatever memory stands nearest. It will eat this city's mind to warm its hands.*

*You will be tempted. I know you, Tamsin Sandoval. You collect strays and you tell yourself it's charity, and underneath it you are starving for a reason to matter, and a child made of the oldest song in the world is the largest reason anyone ever offered you. Resist it. Be his keeper, not his teacher. Keep him small. Keep him ordinary. Give him away, if you can bear it, to someone far from Vell who will never ask him questions. Let him grow up a farmer's son who hums tunelessly and thinks bread rises because of yeast, like everyone else.*

*This is the coward's advice. I know that too. I am giving it anyway, because I have earned the right to give bad advice: I paid for mine with three children, and I would rather hand you my shame than watch you mint fresh coin of your own.*

*One last thing, and then I will stop lecturing you across the grave, which I promised myself I wouldn't do, and see how well promises hold.*

*If the moon turns its thirteenth before the boy is found and quieted—if the fragments begin to reach for one another—then none of this advice will matter, and I am sorry, and I have failed you twice, once in the past and once in the future, which is a trick I did not know a dead woman could manage.*

*Burn this letter. Do not keep it. Paper remembers, in this city especially.*

*With everything I have left, which is mostly regret and a little love, and the love is real even though it comes third in the sentence—*

*M.O.*

---

Theo woke while I was rereading it the third time. I heard him through the wall—no crying, just that soft babble, the syllables sliding past each other in patterns no nursemaid ever taught. The wards along the back door flickered once, twice, a heartbeat pattern, like a lamp being tested.

I folded the letter along its original creases. I did not burn it. I'm telling you this honestly because everything else in this account deserves honesty: I meant to burn it. I held it over the cold stove for a full minute. But paper remembers, she wrote, and so do I, and if I burned it then the only proof that any of this had happened would live inside my skull, in a head that had already spent twelve years practicing forgetfulness. Some part of me—the part that checks the street twice—did not trust that head anymore.

So I put it under a floorboard with my mother's ring and the counterfeit Edict medallion I'd never had the nerve to sell, and I went into the back room.

Theo was sitting up in the narrow bed, hair flattened on one side, watching me with those grave dark eyes that seemed to catalog everything and file it somewhere I couldn't reach. He patted the blanket beside him. An invitation. Twelve years ago I'd have needed a ward-sign and a prayer to feel safe accepting an invitation from anything magical. Now I just sat down, because he was four, and four-year-olds get the benefit of the doubt.

"Bad dreams?" I asked, uselessly, in the way adults ask questions they already know the answers to.

He shook his head. Then he pointed at the floorboard direction—at the shop, at the letter I might as well have been wearing—and made a small sound. Not the Tongue. Just a hum. Two notes, rising, questioning.

But the two notes were *right*. They were the exact interval the cup had hummed as it knitted. And as he held them, gentle as breath, the splintered leg of the stool in the corner—splintered since before I rented this shop—healed. Smoothly, silently, wood remembering a straighter version of itself, the way Maren's letter remembered her handwriting.

I sat very still.

*Naming anchors a vessel,* she'd written. Too late, old woman. *Tamsin*, she'd called him in my head since the second night, when he fell asleep against my ribs in the rocking chair and I thought: *this one is Theo, and I am keeping him.*

Anchored. Named. Waking.

And somewhere across the city, in a tower with grey banners, someone whose job was noticing exactly this kind of thing had probably already noticed.

Theo leaned against my arm and hummed the two notes again, softly, like a question he didn't know how to finish.

Outside, the rain had stopped, and the clouds were pulling back off the moon, which hung above Vell fat and white and patient—one turn short, I'd later learn, of a number that mattered—and I sat in the dark with a sleeping weapon in my lap and a dead woman's warning under the floorboards, and I did what cowards with good posture have always done in Vell.

I decided, firmly, to think about it tomorrow.

It was the worst decision of my life, and I stand by it, because the alternative was unthinkable, and I had not yet learned that the unthinkable always sends advance notice. Hers arrived the next morning, in fact, in the shape of a grey-robed inspector asking after a resonance in the low quarter, polite as a bill collector, thorough as rot.

But that was tomorrow. That night, there was only the humming, and the weight of a small boy against my ribs, and the terrible, tender knowledge—arriving all at once, the way truth does—that I had never been hiding a child at all.

I had been raising a song. And it was beginning to learn the words.
