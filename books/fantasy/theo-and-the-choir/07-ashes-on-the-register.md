## 7. Ashes on the Register

The Conclave Archive smells the way all important buildings in Vell smell: of cold stone, lamp oil, and the particular staleness of paper that no one has been permitted to read. I have worked here for eleven years, and in that time I have learned that truth in Vell is less like a well and more like a pantry—carefully stocked, inventoried, and accessible only to those with the right key.

I had the right key. I always did. That was rather the point of me.

"Vane." The night registrar, a stooped little man named Odell whose entire personality was a filing system, looked up as I came down the stairs into the records vault. "It's past midnight. The resonance sweep isn't until—"

"I know when my own sweep is." I set my lantern on his desk and laid my warrant beside it. "I need the birth registries. Low quarter, last twenty years. All of them."

He blinked at me. Odell blinks slowly, the way a ledger closes. "All of them? That's—" He actually began counting on his fingers, which told me everything about why he was suited to this work. "That's forty-one volumes. Forty-three, if you count the flood year, which we keep separately because of the damp."

"Then bring me the damp ones too."

"Inquisitor," he said carefully, "may I ask what this concerns? There's a form for requisitions after hours. Form 9-C. It requires a countersignature from—"

"Odell." I leaned on his desk, and let him see my face do the thing it does—the thing that has made three separate registrars request transfer out of my jurisdiction. "Somewhere in those forty-three volumes is a child who hums like a struck bell. Somewhere near him are other children who used to do the same thing. I want their names before dawn, and I would very much like not to write Form 9-C, because Form 9-C gets read by people above my pay grade, and if they learn what I'm looking for before I do, then whatever I find will arrive back to me already cleaned, polished, and arranged into a conclusion. Do you understand me?"

He understood me. People usually do.

* * *

Here is what the Conclave teaches, and teaches well, and teaches first: magic killed your family.

Not mine specifically—I was four when the fire took my parents' house on Candlemakers' Row, too young to remember anything except the smell of my mother's hair and, afterward, the absence of it. But the lesson doesn't require specifics. Every child in Vell learns the shape of it. The war. The sorcerer-lords burning cities over grammar disputes older than any living tongue. The Choir, singing armies into madness, rewriting men's memories until sons marched against fathers without knowing why. And then the Edict of Silence, the blessed, merciful Edict, when the wise founders of Vell said: no more, never again, and burned magic out of the world the way you burn infection out of a wound.

I believed it the way I believe in rain. Not as an opinion. As weather.

So when the resonance instruments in the low quarter had started singing back two weeks ago—a faint harmonic ghost, there and gone, like someone testing a bell behind a wall—I had gone to the archive expecting confirmation, not complication. Find the source. Silence it. Go home. The work was grim, but grim work done cleanly is its own kind of comfort. I have always been good at clean.

Forty-three volumes. I started with the oldest and made Odell bring me tea, because if I was going to be awake until dawn doing something I hadn't yet found words to justify, someone else was going to suffer alongside me.

The registry is a dull document, which is its charm. Name of child. Name of parents. Trade of father. Parish of baptism. Cause of death, where applicable, entered in a clerk's careful hand years later. Twenty years of ordinary lives, catalogued in ink the color of old bruises.

I wasn't looking for anything specific. That's what I'd tell myself later, and it would even be true. I was simply reading every name, the way you run your hand along a wall in the dark—not searching, exactly. Listening.

The third volume in, I found Wren Halloway.

Born in the low quarter, spring of the flood year. Father a net-mender. Baptised at Salt Chapel parish—which interested me mildly, since the chapel had been a ruin for thirty years; some clerks kept using the old parish names out of habit. Cause of death, entered when the boy was six: fever.

Fever kills children. Of course it does. In the low quarter, fever, bad milk, bad air, and bad luck kill children so routinely that the clerks stopped writing elegies sometime in the last century. There is nothing suspicious about a fever.

But I had spent eleven years training myself to notice the shape of entries, and this entry had one. Fever deaths get a physician's notation, or at least a parish warden's mark. This one had neither. Just the word, alone in its column, written in ink slightly darker than the rest of the page.

I wrote the name down and kept going.

By the fourth hour I had nine names. By the sixth, eleven.

Eleven children, born across nineteen years, scattered through the low quarter like seeds from a dropped sack. Different families, different trades, different parishes. Nothing connected them—except that every single cause-of-death entry was bare. No physician. No warden. No witness. Fever, chill, a fall, a drowning in a rain barrel, "declined," twice, which is not a cause of death but an administrative surrender. Eleven children, and not one of them died in front of anyone whose name the Conclave thought worth recording.

And every one of them, I realized somewhere around the tenth cup of tea, had been baptised under a parish name that no longer existed. Old parishes. Pre-Edict parishes. Either the registry clerks were sentimentally attached to dead geography—or someone had wanted these births filed where casual readers wouldn't look.

Or where a certain kind of reader would look only once.

* * *

"Odell," I said, when the grey light had begun leaking down the stairwell, "who maintains the death registers?"

"The same clerks maintain both. Why?"

"Because either your office employs eleven consecutive incompetents, or someone has been editing your books for twenty years, and I'd like to know which answer lets me sleep."

He came around the desk and looked at my list for a long moment. To his credit—he was, whatever else, a professional—he didn't ask how I'd found them. He just ran his thumb down the column of causes and said, quietly, "These aren't right."

"No."

"Fever deaths get marked. Even pauper fevers. There's a whole code." He tapped the margin, where a small symbol should have sat. "Someone struck them. Look—you can see the ghost of the stamp if you tilt the page. Someone stamped them and then lifted the stamp off again. Or..." He hesitated.

"Or what?"

"Or the stamps were never applied, and the register was written up afterward, from elsewhere, by someone who knew our format but not our habits." Odell straightened up, and for the first time in eleven years I watched the filing system look afraid. "Inquisitor, these pages weren't corrected. They were composed."

I stood there in the lamplight with my list of eleven dead children, and I felt the first crack go through—not in the register, in me. A hairline. The kind you don't hear, only feel, the way a wall feels before it shows.

Because here is the arithmetic that was assembling itself in my head, uninvited, patient as dust: eleven resonant children, born across two decades, all dead of nothing in particular, all documented by hands that were not quite the Conclave's hands. And now a twelfth signal in the low quarter, faint and alive and hiding.

Either magic had killed eleven children—

—or the Conclave had.

And I want to be honest, because honesty is the only discipline I have left after tonight: my first instinct was to burn the list. My hand actually moved toward the lantern. That is what faith does when it meets evidence. It reaches for fire. It has always reached for fire. We taught it to.

I didn't burn it. I folded it instead, into my coat, against my heart, which is where the Conclave taught me to carry warrants, and which suddenly felt like a very different place to keep things.

"One more thing, Odell."

"Yes, Inquisitor."

"The twelfth signal. The resonance sweep I'm running tomorrow—whose order is it? Truly? Trace it up for me. Not the form. The hand behind the form."

He paled in the way that paperwork men pale, all at once and completely. "You don't want to know that."

"That," I said, "is the most honest sentence anyone has said to me in eleven years, and I'm going to need a name anyway."

He gave it to me. I won't write it here. Writing it makes it real, and I have spent one night already learning how much of my life is built on things being real only as long as they stay unwritten.

The sweep that I am to lead at dawn—the sweep I designed, or thought I designed—was commissioned, countersigned, and blessed by the same office that has been composing death certificates for twenty years.

They are not sending me to find the child.

They are sending me to confirm that the twelfth grave is properly filled in.

* * *

Dawn came the way it always does in Vell: grey, reluctant, smelling of coal smoke. I stood on the steps of the archive with my instruments in their case and my list of eleven dead children against my heart, and I did what inquisitors do. I walked toward the low quarter to do my job.

But I'll tell you what I did first, in the dark, before the bells. I went to the shelf where we keep the histories of the Edict—all of them approved, all of them identical—and I pulled down the oldest one, and I held it, and I noticed for the first time in eleven years that every copy of it, in every edition, across ninety years, is exactly the same length. Word for word. Page for page.

Histories drift. They grow footnotes, quarrels, corrections. Living accounts breed. These haven't bred. They've been *kept*, the way you keep a thing that must never change.

Ninety years ago, we didn't win a war and write it down.

We forgot a war, and called the forgetting peace.

I don't know yet what happened to Wren Halloway, drowned-or-not-drowned in his rain barrel. I don't know what happened to the ten others. I know only that somewhere in the low quarter there is a twelfth child humming behind a wall, and that the people who sent me have buried eleven before him, and that today I am expected to walk into that quarter with a clean conscience and a calibrated instrument.

The instrument is calibrated.

The conscience, as of last night, is under review.

I turned toward the low quarter, and the crack in me went on spreading, quiet as damp through paper, and I let it.
