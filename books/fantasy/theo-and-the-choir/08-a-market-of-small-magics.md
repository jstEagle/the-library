## 8. A Market of Small Magics

There are exactly three rules to surviving in Vell with a pulse and a secret, and I had spent twelve years obeying all of them: don't practice, don't talk about practicing, and never, under any circumstances, go looking for other people who practice.

I broke rule three on a Thursday, because Thursdays are when the Conclave changes its patrol rotations and the low quarter smells least like tar and most like the fried onions that make a man forget he's about to do something stupid.

The boy — Theo, though he didn't know it was his name yet; names are things you grow into like coats — sat on my counter eating bread that had risen twice as high as any dough had a right to, because he'd looked at it that morning and said one soft syllable of the First Tongue and the yeast had apparently decided to overachieve. He chewed with the solemn concentration of a child who has learned that food can be taken away, and watched me lace up my boots.

"I'm going out," I told him, which was absurd, because he couldn't answer and possibly couldn't understand, except that he always *did* understand, in that unnerving way of children and cats. "You will not open the door. You will not speak. If anyone knocks, you are a deaf orphan and I am a terrible landlord."

He blinked at me, slow, and set down the bread, and said — quietly, the way you'd mention weather — "*Sela veth.*"

Stay safe. That's what I'd decided it meant. It might have meant *the moon eats the sky* for all I knew, but it sounded like stay safe, and I chose to be flattered rather than terrified, which is a discipline I've practiced daily for twelve years.

"I'm always safe," I lied, and went out into the rain to find the one kind of person in Vell more dangerous than an inquisitor: a friend.

* * *

The market doesn't have a name, because named things can be found, and found things can be burned. The practitioners of Vell call it *the Undercroft*, when they call it anything, and they call each other nothing at all. You get there by buying a candle from the chandler on Wren Street who has been dead for eleven years and whose daughter maintains the fiction because her mother's wax recipe opens a door that officially does not exist. You pay too much for the candle. The overpayment is the toll. Then you light it in the stairwell of the tannery and walk down past where the stairs should end, and the smoke goes up your nose like memory, and suddenly you're standing in a cellar the size of a cathedral that no surveyor ever drew.

It hadn't changed much. Candles everywhere, obviously — a market run by hidden witches is not subtle about its lighting preferences. Stalls of charms disguised as trinkets, potions labeled as vinegar, a fortune-teller who actually told fortunes and therefore did brisk business in being ignored. Maybe forty people, moving through the smoke with the particular hunched gait of people who have spent years making themselves small.

Twelve years since I'd stood on that floor. Twelve years, and the first face I recognized belonged to a woman named Odile Fenn who used to sell love-charms back when I sold real luck, and who took one look at me and made the sign against the evil eye with both hands.

"Tamsin Sandoval," she said, loud enough that heads turned. Wonderful. "They let *you* back in."

"They let anyone back in who pays the candle tax. That's the whole business model."

"Last time you came here, the Conclave followed you." Her voice dropped, and so did the temperature, or the room's opinion of me did, which amounts to the same thing. "Three stalls burned. Pellam hanged. A girl of nineteen taken to the White House and never seen again. Do you remember?"

"I remember." I remembered all of it. I remembered Pellam's face specifically, which is not a gift; it's a debt. "Which is why I'm here. Something's happened—"

"Something always happens. That's the anthem of our whole sad species." She crossed her arms. "Whatever it is, the answer's no."

"You haven't heard the question."

"The question is always the same. *Help me.* And helping you is how we all end up ash. Go home, hedge-man. Sell your fake horseshoes to tourists."

I want to tell you I argued brilliantly. I want to tell you I swayed the room. What actually happened is that I stood in front of forty people who had every reason in the world to fear me and made the pitch anyway, voice cracking on the second sentence, because I'd rehearsed it in the rain and the rain hadn't heckled.

"A child," I said. "A child walked up to my door speaking the First Tongue. Not words from it. *Speaking it.* Natively. Like it was milk and honey instead of a dead language that used to hold up the sky."

Silence. The bad kind — the kind with weight.

"And I need someone," I went on, "who knows more than I do. Someone old enough to remember what the Tongue was *for*. Because the thirteenth turn is coming, and if even half the almanac stories are true, then this boy isn't a curiosity. He's either the last ember of something enormous or the fuse, and I don't know which, and I can't tell the difference alone."

An old man near the far wall — I didn't know him, which meant he was new, which meant he was already scared — said, "Kill it, then. Whatever it is. Kill it and burn the body and salt the ground."

"He's six years old."

"So were we all," said the old man, "and the war still ate us."

And there it was. The whole argument in one sentence. I looked around the Undercroft at the candlelight and the hunched shoulders and I understood, finally and fully, that I hadn't come looking for allies. I'd come looking for people willing to stop being afraid, and there is no coin that buys that, because fear is the only currency everyone's rich in.

Odile's face softened by maybe a degree. "You were always the stubborn one," she said. "Even as an apprentice. Marisol's boy." She used my dead mistress's name like a knife laid flat between us, not stabbing, just showing. "If she were alive she'd box your ears for coming here."

"If she were alive she'd already know what the boy is," I said, "and she'd have told me, and I wouldn't be asking strangers in a cellar."

That landed harder than I intended. Several people looked away. Somewhere in the smoke, someone whispered — I caught only the shape of it — *scattered*, and *children*, and then hush swallowed it, the way Vell swallows everything.

Nobody helped me. Forty practitioners, four centuries of accumulated craft between them, and not one hand raised. I bought a candle I didn't need on the way out, out of some stupid politeness, and climbed the stairs into the rain, and stood under the eaves of the tannery feeling the specific loneliness of a man who has just learned that his community is a rumor.

That's when the woman in the grey coat spoke up behind me.

* * *

She'd been at the edge of the market the whole time, near the wall, where the candles didn't reach. Older than me by ten years or twenty — hard to say, because her face had that quality of having been beautiful once and then having decided against it. She wore a scarf high on her throat despite the cellar heat, and she moved like someone whose body had been trained very well and then punished for it.

"You're Marisol's apprentice," she said. Not a question.

"I was. Before the Edict."

"Mm." She studied me the way you study a lock you're deciding whether to pick. "Marisol Oyelaran taught counter-song. Did you learn any?"

Counter-song. The word hit some sealed room in me and rattled the door. My mistress had hummed, sometimes, working over her cauldrons — patterns that weren't quite melodies, that made herbs dry faster and fevers break. She'd called them *tidying*. I'd copied them without understanding, the way a parrot copies thunder.

"Some fragments," I admitted. "She never called it singing."

"No. She wouldn't have." The woman pulled back her scarf, and I saw why she kept it high: a scar across her throat, old, surgical, deliberate. Not a wound. An excision. "My name is Maren Solde. Twenty-two years ago I sang descant for the Salt Choir. Before the Edict took us apart. Before they took—" she touched the scar, lightly, like touching a grave marker, "—this."

I should have run. Every rule I owned said run. But I'd spent the evening collecting refusals, and there's a point past which a man stops calculating and starts simply falling forward.

"Why would a choir-singer be hiding in a smugglers' market?" I asked.

"Same as you. Where else does garbage go?" She smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "I heard what you asked them. A child speaking the First Tongue." Her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely — somewhere twenty years away. "Say it isn't true."

"It's true. I wish it weren't."

"What words? Give me the words."

"*Sela veth.* He says it when I leave. I think it means stay safe."

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, and she didn't wipe them, and something in her posture broke and reassembled itself into something older and more terrible than grief.

"That's a blessing," she said. "The hearth-blessing. Mothers sang it over cradles before the war. It's the first thing every Choir child ever learned." Her hand closed around my wrist, grip like iron gone soft with rust. "Take me to him. Tonight. Now."

"Why? What do you think he is?"

"I don't think anything." She was already pulling me toward the stairs. "I *know*, and knowing has cost me everything I own except this coat. So we'll go to your shop, hedge-man, and you'll put the boy in front of me, and if I'm wrong, I'll apologize and vanish forever. And if I'm right—" she stopped at the bottom step, and the candle-smoke curled around her like it recognized her, and her voice dropped to almost nothing:

"If I'm right, then the Choir isn't dead. It's sleeping in a child's throat in a charm-shop above a cobbler, in the most anti-magical city on earth, eleven months before the moon finishes its turn. And every soul in this city — starting with the two of us — is standing on a lit fuse."

We climbed into the rain together. Behind us the Undercroft's candles guttered, and I swear — though I'll deny it if you ask — I swear the whole buried market exhaled, forty hidden hearts hoping, the way cowards do, that someone else would be brave first.

Theo was asleep when we got there, curled on the cot behind my counter with the half-eaten loaf beside his head like a teddy bear. Maren stood over him a long moment. Then, softly — softer than I'd heard anyone speak in twelve years — she began to hum.

And the boy opened his eyes, and answered.
