## 9. Maren's Voice

I had not sung in eleven years, and I want to be precise about that, because people in the Salt Quarter assume exile means you've been *forbidden* to sing, as though some Conclave clerk stands outside my window with a tuning fork and a warrant. No one forbade me anything. I forbade myself. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of my life.

Forbidden singing is a wound. Chosen silence is a scar you pick at nightly until it opens again.

So when Tamsin Sandoval came down the fish-gut stairs of my tenement with a boy tucked against his side like contraband — which, I suppose, he was — and said, "Maren, I need you to listen to something," my first instinct was to throw him back out into the rain.

My second instinct was worse. It was hope.

* * *

You should know what I was before I was nothing, or none of this will land properly.

The Cantors of Vell were not a religious order, whatever the histories say now. We were archivists of a kind — the only ones who understood that the old kingdoms' war hadn't been ended by treaties or armies but by a *deletion*. The Choir was real. The Choir sang the world's memory, and when the kingdoms burned it out of itself, they burned out more than magic. They burned out the reasons for everything. My order kept fragments. Descants. Little scraps of melody written in cipher on vellum we hid inside psalters, because no one audits a psalter.

We called ourselves preservers. The Conclave called us heretics. Both words were flattery.

There were nine of us at the end, in a cellar under the Old Bridge, singing a reconstruction of the First Descant from notation none of us fully understood. I was nineteen and I had a voice like a struck bell, and I believed — this is the part that shames me still — I believed we were doing something holy.

The Edict-men came at dawn. They didn't kill all of us. Killing would have been a mercy with an ending. They took our voices instead. There is an art to it, a surgical cruelty; the Conclave's throat-singers know how to unmake a voice without touching the flesh. You can still speak. You can still scream, if someone gives you cause. But the singing instrument — the thing that made you *you*, that you'd been building since before you could walk — it goes out like a lamp. Not broken. Just... elsewhere. In a room you can never find again.

Eight of the nine went mad within the decade. I am told this is common. The body keeps reaching for the voice the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth.

I survived by becoming very, very good at not wanting things.

And then a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice stood dripping in my doorway with a child, and asked me to listen, and every one of those eleven careful years tipped sideways.

* * *

"Absolutely not," I said. "Whatever it is, no."

"You haven't heard it."

"I don't need to hear it. Whatever it is, it's either nothing, in which case why are you here, or it's something, in which case why are you here *with witnesses*." I looked past him at the boy. "Who is this?"

"Theo." Tamsin said the name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "He's mine now. Don't ask questions I can't answer in a stairwell."

"I'm asking them anyway. Inside. And if anyone sees you—"

"No one sees anyone in the Salt Quarter. That's its whole charm." He almost smiled. Eleven years since I'd seen him last, and he'd gone grey early at the temples, and his hands had the particular stillness of a man who has taught himself to move like furniture. I knew that stillness. I'd invented it.

Inside, I lit one candle, which is extravagance for me, and two lamps, which is madness, because I wanted to see his face while he lied to me about how bad this was.

"It's bad," he said, reading me exactly as well as he always had, damn him.

"How bad?"

"Moon-turn bad."

I sat down slowly. "Thirteenth turn?"

"Not yet. Within the year." He crouched so he was level with the boy, who had wedged himself into the corner between my shelf and the wall with the instinctive competence of a creature that has learned corners are safer than rooms. "Theo. You remember what I said? This lady might understand your words."

The boy looked at me. And I'll tell you what I saw, because everyone else in this story seems determined to describe him as a vessel, a weapon, a remnant, a key. I saw a child of perhaps seven with rain-damp hair and a smudge of soot on his cheekbone, watching me with the terrible patient attention children have when they've learned that adults say important things over their heads.

Then he hummed.

It was nothing. That's what I thought at first — four notes, maybe five, wavering, the tuneless wandering sound any child makes when filling silence. My heart did its eleven-years-dormant lurch anyway, the way it does at any melody, any snatch of song from a passing cart, any drunk bawling in the street. I had trained myself out of the lurch. It came back anyway.

Then he hummed it again, and it wasn't nothing.

The intervals were wrong. That's the only way I can put it. They were wrong the way a door is wrong when it opens onto air where there should be stairs — internally consistent, perfectly shaped, and built for a world with different proportions than ours. Four notes rising through a seventh that doesn't exist in any scale permitted to human throats, resolving downward through a suspension that shouldn't resolve, and underneath it, faint as breath on glass, something *else* — a second line, a descant, moving against the melody the way a shadow moves against its walker.

I knew that descant.

I had copied it out at nineteen in cipher on vellum hidden in a psalter. I had sung it once, in a cellar, at dawn, with eight other fools. I had lost everything for it: my voice, my order, my name in the registers of the living.

I did not weep gracefully. Let the record show that. I made a sound like a gull being stepped on and put my face in my hands and shook, and the candle flame bent toward the boy as though leaning in to hear better, and Tamsin said, quietly, "So you do recognize it," and I wanted to throw a shoe at him.

* * *

When I could speak again — speak, mind, not sing; let's keep our tragedies sorted — I said, "Where did he get that? Nobody has that descant. The vellums were burned. I watched them burn half of them and I burned the rest myself, after, because keeping them felt like—" I stopped. "Where did he get it?"

"He didn't get it anywhere," Tamsin said. "That's what I've come to tell you. It isn't in him, Maren. It *is* him."

And he told me. All of it, or enough of it: the wards flaring, the First Tongue, the almanac, the thirteenth turn, the letter from his dead mistress warning that the Choir had been scattered rather than slain. He told it plainly, without drama, which somehow made it worse, because Tamsin Sandoval lying sounds exactly like Tamsin Sandoval telling the truth and always has, so plainness was the only proof available.

The boy — Theo — watched us both, and hummed fragments while we talked, little unconscious snatches, and each one went through me like a hook through soft meat. A phrase from the Lamentation. A bar of the Dedication that my teacher Sorrel used to sing while kneading bread. Things that existed nowhere on earth anymore except in ash and in my ruined memory and, apparently, in the blood of a seven-year-old.

"They didn't destroy it," I said at last. "They couldn't. You can't kill a song. You can only stop it being sung, and songs are patient. Songs wait." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Eleven years I thought we failed. Eight people driven mad and a cellar full of corpses and my voice in some Conclave vault like a pulled tooth in a jar — and we didn't fail, we were just early. It came back *without us*. It came back as a *child*."

"Is that what it is?" Tamsin asked. "A child?"

I looked at Theo. He had fallen asleep sitting up, chin on chest, mid-phrase, the way exhausted children do, and the candle flame had gone still and tall as a spearpoint.

"That's the question that gets everyone killed," I said. "Yes. And no. Listen — you came to me because I know things. So let me know you one thing properly, because I think nobody's said it straight to you yet, including yourself."

He waited.

"The Choir isn't a spell. Spells spend power — yours, the moon's, a goat's, whatever you've got. The Choir spends *memory*. That's the bargain, that's the whole engine of it. To work its miracles it takes the past out of people and burns it as fuel. Every verse costs a remembering. That's why the old kingdoms feared it more than plague — not because it leveled cities. Because it leveled *reasons*. You fight a war, the Choir sings over the battlefield, and afterward nobody remembers why they hated each other. Peace! Wonderful peace! Also nobody remembers their mothers' faces, or the names of their dead, or why they ever planted wheat in that valley instead of barley. The war ends because there's no one left inside it."

Tamsin had gone very pale. Good, I thought, uncharitably. Welcome to my cellar, apprentice. Mind the bones.

"The Edict of Silence," he said slowly. "Vell's founding. 'That Vell be a city of law and not of wonder.' The Conclave teaches it was a renunciation. A choice."

"A choice made by people who couldn't remember making it," I said. "Ask yourself why a whole civilization woke up one morning agreeing magic was filth, and no record survives of the debate. Ask yourself why the Conclave's true archive is sealed even from most of the Conclave. I've thought about it for eleven years with nothing to do *but* think, Tamsin. I think the last verse of the old war was sung over this valley, and Vell is what grew in the scorched place. And I think the Conclave aren't villains. I think they're scar tissue. The city's own forgetting, grown organized, given uniforms and warrants, and told to keep the wound closed forever."

"And if the wound opens?"

"Then everything the last verse paid for comes due again." I rubbed my eyes. "Or gets paid anew. One memory per listener per verse. That's the exchange rate. It doesn't negotiate. It doesn't aim. You cannot point this miracle, Tamsin. You can only stand in front of it and choose what you're willing to lose."

The boy slept on. Outside, the rain kept up its own dull descant against the shutters, tuneless, safe, permitted.

"There were eleven others," Tamsin said. "Children like him. Born across twenty years. All dead."

"All dead," I repeated. "Of ordinary causes. How convenient. Either the Conclave found them—" I saw his face. "No. You already checked. Too convenient even for the Conclave. Then the Choir itself has been shedding vessels. Or someone else has been pruning them. Or—" I stopped, because the next thought was the one I'd been circling for an hour and refusing to land on.

"Or what?" Tamsin asked.

"Or the thirteenth turn needs a living vessel to gather around," I said, "and something has been clearing the ground. Leaving one." I looked at the sleeping boy. "One seed, one field, one harvest. That's not how nature works, Tamsin. That's how *husbandry* works. Somebody planned this."

* * *

He stayed another hour, and we argued, because that's what we'd always done — in his mistress's kitchen, years ago, when he was a sulky apprentice and I was a visiting Cantor and neither of us knew we were rehearsing our own ruin. He argued that the boy deserved to stay a boy. I agreed. He argued that meant hiding Theo until the turn passed and nothing happened. I told him nothing wouldn't happen. The humming alone was already working small miracles — he admitted the bread, the cup, the chair that remembered being a sapling — and each small miracle was a small payment being collected from someone, somewhere, whether or not anyone signed for it. The Choir doesn't wait for permission. It never has.

"So teach him," Tamsin said finally, and I heard in his voice how much it cost, how far he'd already traveled past his own caution to arrive at those two words. "Teach him properly. Before the turn. If it's going to happen regardless, better it happens sung than screamed."

"Do you understand what you're asking?" I said. "I can't sing. That's not modesty, that's anatomy. I can give him structure, notation, discipline — I can be his map. But I'm a map of a country that's been flooded. Half of what I know is underwater."

"Then we start with the dry parts."

"And when he sings his first full verse, and it works, and everyone in earshot pays for it — what will you have lost, Tamsin? Think carefully. The Choir takes what's nearest the singer. What's nearest you?"

He didn't answer. He gathered the sleeping boy onto his hip with the practiced ease of someone who'd been doing it for months, and at the door he paused.

"Why did you agree?" he asked. "Eleven years of saying no to everyone. Why yes to me?"

I considered lying. I'm good at it; it's the trade skill of the silenced.

"Because I heard four notes of a dead language come out of a living child," I said, "and for the first time since the dawn raid on our cellar, I thought: maybe it wasn't wasted. Maybe the eight of them didn't go mad for a song that died. Maybe it was only sleeping, and it slept through me, and it chose a better mouth." I turned away before he could see my face do what it was going to do. "Go home, Tamsin. Come back in three days. Bring paper. And bring that boy fed and rested, because what I'm going to start teaching him will use up everything he has, and I intend to be able to look at myself afterward and say I tried to make it cost less. Not nothing. Less. That's the best bargain anyone ever gets with the Choir, and I'm tired of people pretending otherwise."

The stairwell took his footsteps down and away. I sat alone with my guttering candle and my useless, aching, empty throat, and I listened to the rain, and I caught myself — God help me — humming along.

Not aloud. Never aloud.

But somewhere in the room where my voice had been locked for eleven years, something knocked.

Three days, I'd promised him. Three days was enough time to sharpen pencils, copy ciphers, prepare lessons.

Three days was also enough time for the Conclave to finish sweeping the low quarter, and when I opened my shutters that night to check the streets — old habit, older than my silence — I saw lanterns moving down Fishgut Lane in the orderly pattern of a search that had already found what it wanted, and was now finding everyone who'd helped.

They weren't coming for the boy anymore.

They were coming for me.
