## 10. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

I want it on the record that I checked the almanac twice.

Twice, and then a third time by candlelight with my spectacles polished and my eyes squinted like a moneylender auditing a suspicious ledger. The moon's thirteenth turn did not begin on the night I feared. It began three days later, which gave me exactly seventy-two hours of false comfort, during which Theo ate his porridge, learned to say *spoon*, and made absolutely nothing in the shop levitate.

Then the moon turned over in its sleep, and everything went to hell in the most beautiful way possible, which is the worst way possible when you're trying to stay invisible.

* * *

It started with the bread.

Not rising — that had become almost routine, a small domestic miracle I'd stopped flinching at, which should have frightened me more than it did. No. This time the bread remembered. I came downstairs to find the loaf sitting on the counter looking less like bread than like wheat — dense golden heads pressing up through the crust, stalks unfurling from the slashes I'd cut for venting steam. It smelled wonderful. It also swayed gently in a draft that wasn't there, the way grain sways in a field.

"Good morning," said Theo from the doorway, in Vellish, clear as a bell, no hesitation, no fumbling.

I dropped the knife.

He'd been with me since autumn. Months of lessons — I'd been patient, methodical, teaching him words the way you feed a stray cat, one morsel at a time, never startling it. He could manage perhaps sixty words of Vellish. *Spoon. Bread. Cold. Tamsin. No.* His First Tongue he used sparingly, deliberately, because we'd both learned early that his language was less a language than a set of instructions the world took seriously.

And now here he stood delivering a full grammatical sentence with the offhand ease of a boy asking what's for supper.

"How," I managed, "are you doing that?"

"You taught me." He tilted his head, puzzled by my alarm, which was fair; I was puzzling myself. "All winter. I just — last night it all fit together. Like water finding the low places."

Water finding the low places. Wonderful. My apprentice in the dead language of creation describing fluency as a natural process beyond anyone's control. I thought of the almanac, its careful astronomical tables, the little marginal note in some long-dead astrologer's hand: *the thirteenth turn, when what sleeps counts itself awake.*

"Theo," I said, crouching to his level, keeping my voice pleasant the way you keep your voice pleasant near a lit fuse. "What else feels different this morning?"

He considered this with the gravity he brought to all questions, chewing the inside of his cheek. "The house is humming," he said. "Quietly. Underneath. It's been humming since before I woke up but now I know the word for it."

The house was not humming. I put my palm flat against the floorboards and felt it anyway — a vibration too deep for sound, like standing on a bridge while something enormous passes beneath.

* * *

By noon, the chair was a sapling.

It was one of my good chairs, oak, bought secondhand from a widow off Chandler's Row who swore it had belonged to a bishop. Theo sat in it doing his letters — actual letters now, chalk squeaking across a slate, forming words in two alphabets — and I noticed the armrest under his hand had gone soft. Then green. Then it split along the grain and pushed out a shoot, leaves unfolding with the obscene speed of time-lapse, and by the time I crossed the room the whole armrest had become living wood, bark and bud, rooted into nothing, drinking air.

Theo looked down at his work and then up at me, and his face did something complicated: delight first, unguarded and total, and then — watching my face instead of the chair — the delight folded itself away and he said, carefully, "Is this wrong?"

And there it was. The question underneath every other question. A child asking whether his own existence required an apology.

"It's not wrong," I said. "It's just loud."

"Loud?"

"Magic isn't illegal here because it's wicked, love. It's illegal because it's noticeable. Everything you do, the whole city can feel, even if they don't know what they're feeling." I touched the new-grown wood. It was warm. Chairs aren't warm. "You've started remembering things out loud without saying anything. The bread. Now the chair. You're telling objects what they used to be."

"I'm not telling them anything," he protested, and there was real frustration in it, the frustration of a child blamed for weather. "They just *remember* when I'm close. They get excited. Like dogs."

Like dogs. I thought about that. I thought about the walls.

Because the walls were next, and I'd have preferred to be wrong about it.

* * *

That evening the plaster began to sweat mortar.

It started as damp patches, which in Vell in late winter is unremarkable; every building in the low quarter leaks, and mine leaked with a certain dignified consistency, right above the bed, as tradition demands. But these patches weren't wet. They were *gritty*. And they were moving — slowly, the way frost moves across a window — tracing lines back toward the joints between bricks, and where they pooled, the plaster softened and sloughed away to expose raw mortar, fresh-looking, grey, as though the wall had decided its hundred years of paint and paper were a costume it no longer cared for.

By midnight the shop smelled like a construction site. By the small hours, the wall behind my counter had sweated enough lime paste to leave pale streaks down the skirting board, and the floorstones had begun, very faintly, to remember being quarry rock — edges sharpening, fossils surfacing in the slate like faces coming up through pond water.

I sat up with Theo wrapped in a blanket beside me, both of us watching a chair-sapling grow in the corner, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for months.

Eleven children. Maren had said eleven, born across twenty years, each carrying a fragment of the Choir, each dead of ordinary causes before their tenth birthday. Fever. Choking. A fall from a hayloft. All ordinary. All, if you lined them up, suspiciously convenient. Someone or something had spent two decades ensuring the Choir stayed scattered — and the thirteenth turn, according to legend, was when the fragments could either find each other again or be snuffed out for good.

Which meant that until the turn ended, someone would be working very hard to make sure Theo joined his eleven siblings.

And I'd just spent a day letting him turn my shop into a beacon visible from orbit.

"We can't stay here," I told him.

He didn't argue. That was the thing about Theo that kept catching me off guard — under all the wonder, he had a fugitive's instincts already, quick and unsentimental. He simply nodded and asked, "Where do we go?" in the tone of a boy who has understood, longer than I'd admitted aloud, that home is a temporary condition.

"I don't know yet. Somewhere stone doesn't listen so hard." I rubbed my eyes. "Somewhere with fewer memories in the walls."

"Everything has memories," Theo said. "That's why it hums."

"Everything hums?"

"Everything." He said it gently, the way you break bad news to someone. "You hum too. You're just singing very quietly."

I decided not to ask what song I was singing. Some questions you save for when you've slept, and I could see already I wasn't going to be sleeping.

* * *

Dawn found us packing, and packing revealed how much of my life was camouflage.

Twelve years of careful nothing. Luck-charms that were painted wood. Love-tokens of scented candle wax. A whole inventory designed to look magical enough to sell and be inert enough to survive inspection. I filled one bag with actual supplies and left four shelves' worth of theater on their hooks, and the strangeness of that struck me as I worked: I was leaving behind the largest single object I owned, which was my own lie.

Theo moved through the shop touching things goodbye — actually touching them, palm to surface, murmuring in the First Tongue so quietly I caught only the shape of it. And the objects responded. My kettle, which I'd owned nine years, gleamed like the day it was cast. The crooked shutter straightened itself with a sigh of wood. The bread-wheat in its basket ripened, went to seed, and scattered soft grains across the counter, a whole harvest compressed into a farewell.

"Theo," I said. "Sweetheart. We need to leave the shop *forgetting* us, not blessing us. Every change you make is a signature."

He stopped. Looked at his hands. "I don't know how to be quiet anymore," he said. "Before, I didn't know any words, so I couldn't say them. Now they're all saying themselves." His chin trembled, briefly, and he mastered it with a effort that hurt to watch — twelve years old going on forty, a vessel learning it had a lip. "Tamsin. Is something hunting me? You keep not answering when I ask things."

There it was. The direct question. I had a whole repertoire of non-answers prepared — *don't worry, we'll be fine, eat your breakfast* — and I opened my mouth to deploy one, and then I looked at the sweating walls, the sapling chair, the boy who could hear the hum of every object in the city, and I thought: he will know. He always knows. He heard the house humming before I felt it. Lying to him is like lying to a mirror.

"Yes," I said. "Something may be. People who are afraid of what you are. They've been afraid for a long time, longer than me, longer than you, and fear makes people thorough."

He absorbed this the way he absorbed everything: silently, completely, the information settling into him like rain into dry ground. Then he said, "Will you be afraid of me someday?"

"No."

"That's what the eleven would have said too."

I froze. "What did you say?"

"The eleven." He frowned at my expression, suddenly uncertain. "I dream about them. I've been dreaming about them all winter but I only just got the words to say it. Eleven other mes. Scattered. Most of them quiet now." He hugged the blanket around himself. "Two of them are still dreaming the same dream I am, I think. Far away. One smells like salt. One sounds like bells."

Maren needed to hear this. Maren, who had gone silent after the market, whose lodging I only half-knew, who had listened to Theo hum one fragment of descant and wept like a woman at a funeral. If the fragments were stirring toward each other — if the Choir was reassembling itself whether anyone consented or not —

But first, out. Out of the shop, out of the quarter, before the Conclave's instruments registered whatever a boy-shaped choir sounded like when it started singing in its sleep.

* * *

We went out through the kitchen, over the wall into the tanner's yard, and north through streets still blue with dawn. I had coin, I had a plan of sorts — the Salt Chapel district, ruined, half-abandoned, stone old enough that maybe its memories had worn smooth. Behind us my shop settled into its own skin again, plaster drying, the sapling chair rustling in a draft, and I did not look back, because I knew if I looked back I'd see the wheat growing through the floorboards and I'd lose my nerve.

Halfway across Miller's Bridge, Theo stopped walking.

"Tamsin," he said. "Listen."

"I don't hear anything."

"That's what I mean. Listen to what's *missing*." He turned a slow circle, and his voice dropped to the register he used for true speech, the one that made the air go taut. "The bells. Vell rings the hour. It's past the hour."

He was right. St. Ossen's in the low quarter rang the hour, and St. Ambrose in the merchant quarter answered it, and neither had spoken. The whole city hung in a held breath, and then, distantly, from the direction of the Conclave Spire, a sound I'd never heard before and recognized instantly anyway: not bells. A resonance-tone, deep and sustained, the sound of an instrument built to detect exactly one thing, sweeping the city like a lighthouse beam made of listening.

Theo grabbed my hand. His palm was hot.

"They're counting," he said. "They've started counting us."

Far to the east, over the rooftops, the moon — fat, white, three days into its thirteenth turn — was setting into the smelter-smoke of the river districts. And somewhere beneath it, I would learn later, a woman named Ilsa Vane was reading a report that bore my street on it, and the countdown everyone feared had audibly, unmistakably, begun.

"Hold my hand tight," I told Theo. "And whatever happens — don't sing."

His fingers closed around mine. And beneath our feet, faint but undeniable, the stones of Miller's Bridge began, very softly, to hum.
