## 11. Ilsa Closes the Net

The raid went wrong in the way raids go right, which is to say nothing burned, nobody screamed, and I found exactly what I was looking for—which is the worst possible outcome for a woman whose job depends on finding nothing at all.

I had forty Conclave soldiers dressed as dockworkers, a warrant signed by three clerks who'd never seen the inside of the Saltmarket, and a headache that had lived behind my left eye since the resonance reading came off the deep-quarter instruments two weeks ago. The market sat under the old tannery, in cellars where the walls still sweated something that smelled of oak bark and old mistakes. We came down through the drainage culvert at low tide, torches hooded, boots wrapped in wool.

I want to say I expected chaos. What I got instead was a man selling luck.

He had a folding table set up in the first cellar, a candle stub, and a tray of copper coins polished bright. "Blessed against drowning," he told my disguised sergeant, holding one up to the light with the sincerity of a bishop. "One silver. Two if you're planning on being drowned *on purpose*."

"Is it blessed?" the sergeant asked, playing his part.

"It's a coin," the man said. "But it's a very confident coin."

We took him anyway. Procedure. He came along without complaint, still cradling his tray of coins like a mother with ugly children, and somewhere in the third cellar down we found what the market actually was: eleven practitioners, four of them genuine, seven of them frauds so committed they'd convinced themselves, and one woman sitting apart from all of it with her back against a wall, watching us come with an expression I can only describe as *finally*.

She was maybe fifty, gray going to white at the temples, with hands that moved constantly—fingers tapping out rhythms on her knee, on the floor, on her own wrist. A singer's hands. I knew those hands. I had known them when they were younger, when they belonged to a girl who could hold a note so long the rest of the choir school used to time her out of spite.

"Maren Ostrey," I said.

"Ilsa Vane." She didn't get up. "You've gotten taller."

"You've gotten caught."

"One of us improved." Her fingers kept their rhythm. Tap, tap-tap, tap. Some old exercise, some scale worn into muscle. "Did you bring the big ones or the polite ones? You always said you'd send the polite ones for me."

"The polite ones are busy." I signaled the squad forward. "Everyone against the wall. Names when asked. Anyone who runs gets run down—nobody dies tonight unless someone's stupid enough to arrange it."

They arranged it, of course. Someone's always stupid enough. But it was over in ninety seconds and the only casualty was a fortune-teller who broke her own wrist tripping over a cat, which even she agreed was probably accurate.

Maren let them bind her hands without a word. That should have been my first warning. People who come quietly are either innocent or patient, and I have never once met an innocent person in a smuggled-magic cellar at midnight.

* * *

The Conclave keeps interrogation rooms the way other institutions keep chapels: small, quiet, designed to make you feel observed by something larger than yourself. Mine has one table, two chairs, and a lamp positioned to fall directly into the prisoner's eyes. It is meant to be cruel through architecture rather than violence, which the Conclave considers progress.

Maren sat in the light and looked bored.

"Record," I said to the wall, and the brass plate above the door began to turn, scratching our words into its wax drum. "Interrogation of Maren Ostrey, taken by Inquisitor First Class Ilsa Vane. State your name for the record."

"You know my name. You sang it in the register when we were twelve, because Sister Anselm made the whole class do it and yours cracked on the high note."

"For the record," I repeated.

"For the record: Maren Ostrey, born in Vell, trained at the Choir School before your masters burned it, voice confiscated under the Edict of Silence, sentence carried out—" she paused, and for the first time her fingers stopped moving, "—seventeen years ago. They cut it out of me, Ilsa. Not the voice. The *singing*. There's a difference. One is meat. The other was everything."

I sat down across from her. The lamp did its work; she squinted but didn't flinch.

"You were found in a cellar beneath the Saltmarket where unlicensed practice was taking place."

"Where *fraud* was taking place. Half those people couldn't find magic with both hands and a map. The other half could light a candle with effort and a bad attitude. You arrested a man whose entire gift is making coins feel lucky."

"And you? What were you doing there?"

"Listening." She said it simply, and it landed harder than any confession would have. "There was a rumor. That's all—a rumor, the kind of thing that moves through places like that the way damp moves through plaster. A child, in the low quarter. A child who talks in a language that isn't a language. I wanted to hear it for myself."

The wax drum turned. I watched it turn and felt the room get colder, though of course it didn't; rooms don't oblige you like that outside of stories.

"What child?"

"That's not how this works," Maren said. "You ask, I refuse, we both feel noble, and then you go do whatever you were going to do anyway. Ask me something real. Ask me about the resonance readings. Ask me why your instruments have been singing for two months and your superiors keep filing the results under *equipment fault*."

"They're filed under equipment fault because the equipment is old."

"My love." She leaned forward into the lamplight, and her eyes were kind, which was unbearable. "I heard a descant last week. Through a wall. Through a *wall*, Ilsa, in a city where every throat has been closed for seventeen years, I heard four notes of the old descending line—the one from the Lamentation, the one we were taught no living voice could carry anymore—and it came out of a child who doesn't know what he's saying. Your instruments aren't faulty. Your city is remembering."

I should have ended the interview there. I want that on the record too, wherever records go when the people who wrote them stop believing them. I should have stood up, marked the file *uncooperative*, and handed her to the sentence bureau with a clear conscience and a clean report.

Instead I said, "Show me."

* * *

Here is the problem with mercy: it never announces itself. It arrives wearing the uniform of procedure, whispering *this is fine, this is standard, you're allowed five minutes unsupervised with a prisoner because the record-drum needs rethreading,* and by the time you understand what you've done, the drum is stopped and an old friend is humming a fragment of a dead song in a locked room and you are standing there with your hand on your mouth like a girl again, twelve years old, hearing the choir school sing the Lamentation on the last morning before the Edict.

Four notes. Descending. I remembered them the way you remember a stair that isn't there anymore—one step into darkness and the body insists on a landing that was torn out decades ago.

Maren finished and looked at me. "Well?"

"That proves nothing. A hummed phrase proves nothing. Children mimic. Birds mimic."

"Birds don't bend bread," she said. "Birds don't knit cups back together. The tanner who owns this building—he told the raiders, did anyone mention?—that his grandmother's chair started growing again last month. Bark, Ilsa. Sapling shoots out of an old chair, and everyone in the quarter has decided it's dry rot because the alternative is thinking." She sat back. "Something is waking up down there. Something the Conclave buried so thoroughly that even the gravediggers got new memories. And your masters will respond to it the way they responded to everything: fire first, questions never, and a nice clean register entry afterward."

"You're describing treason."

"I'm describing Tuesday. Treason would require I planned something." Her fingers found their rhythm again against the table. Tap, tap-tap. "I planned nothing. I listened. There's a difference, and you of all people should know it—you built a career on listening."

"And you built one on singing, and look where the Edict put you."

"Yes," she said quietly. "Look."

The silence after that had weight. Somewhere above us the building settled, beams ticking like cooling iron. I thought about her hands. Seventeen years since the sentence, and they still kept time. The Conclave cut out the singing and left the counting, and she'd been keeping time alone ever since, in cells and cellars, tapping scales into tabletops for no audience but herself.

"Why tell me?" I asked finally. "If you believe half of what you're saying—why say it to the Conclave's chief inquisitor? Every word goes on the drum. Every word damns you further."

"Because you filed the resonance readings under equipment fault," she said, "and I read personnel files the way other women read romances. You flagged the same anomaly twice last year and were overruled both times. You requested deeper survey of the low quarter and were refused. You, Ilsa Vane, have been hearing the same thing I've been hearing, and you've been telling yourself it's rust." She smiled, and it wasn't a cruel smile, which made it worse. "I'm not confessing to you. I'm confirming."

* * *

I worked until dawn. That's not dramatic license; I have the lamp-oil receipts.

The prisoners went into holding—eleven of them, plus the coin-seller, who spent the night blessing his guards against drowning until two of them bought coins just to make him stop. The warrants went to the clerk. The inventory went to evidence. And the name—the one name that mattered—I wrote down myself, in my own hand, because I did not trust it to anyone else's.

*Maren Ostrey confirms auditory contact with unnamed juvenile source, low quarter. Source exhibits pre-Edict vocal patterns. Recommend immediate escalation.*

Twelve words of truth, and each one a stone dropped into the machinery of my order. Escalation means the survey teams. Survey teams mean the sweepers. Sweepers mean doors kicked in at 4 a.m. across every tenement south of the Tanners' Canal, mean mothers pulled into the street in their nightclothes, mean a child dragged out of whatever hiding place some fool had made for him and brought to the vaults, where the Conclave solves problems the way it solved the war—permanently, quietly, and with excellent record-keeping.

I sat with the page in front of me for a long time.

Here is what I believe. Let it be on the record, even the parts I'll deny. I believe magic murdered my family. I believe it the way I believe in rain—not because I remember the storm, but because everything downstream of that night is wet. The fire that took the Vane house when I was nine was ruled accidental, then ruled arson, then ruled *anomalous*, and the word *anomalous* got my father's name entered into a registry that no longer exists, in a file I have never been permitted to read. Whatever happened in that house, the Conclave built my life on top of its ashes, and I have been sweeping cinders into orderly piles ever since. I am good at it. I am the best they have. That is not a boast; it is a diagnosis.

And here is the second thing I believe, which I believed for the first time at approximately the fourth bell this morning, sitting in a room that smelled of lamp oil and old wax: the ash might be lying to me.

Because I heard those four notes. And when Maren hummed them, something in me rose to meet them—not fear, not duty, something older than either, something with no paperwork attached—and I have spent seventeen years learning to trust the Conclave's account of my own heart, and in one minute of humming, a disgraced choir-singer made me doubt the whole ledger.

So I did the only thing my office allows. I filed the truth.

I wrote the recommendation for escalation. I described the source, the location estimate, the confirmed contact. I cited the resonance data and recommended the sweep begin within the fortnight, before the thirteenth turn—because yes, I've read the almanacs too, and yes, I know what the moon does next year, and so does every archivist who pretends otherwise. I wrote it all in the clean, exact prose the Conclave trains into us, prose with no adjectives and no mercy, prose that converts children into coordinates.

Then, at the bottom, in the space reserved for inquisitor's notes, I added one line:

*Source may be salvageable. Request discretion.*

Which is not mercy. Mercy would be tearing the page up. That line is a coward's compromise, a hinge left unlocked in a house I'm burning down—it changes nothing, saves no one, and exists purely so that someday, when I have to look at what I've done, I can point at six words and say *I tried.* The Conclave will ignore it. The sweepers don't take requests. I knew that when I wrote it. I wrote it anyway, because a person can survive almost anything except being perfectly consistent with herself.

I sealed the report. I addressed it to Archivist-Censor Doss himself, because if it must doom the quarter, better it move fast through honest hands than slow through ambitious ones. The drum, restarted, recorded my closing statement for the interrogation log: *"Interview concluded. Subject transferred to holding pending escalation."* My voice sounded steady. Drums are liars that way.

At the window of my office the sky was doing its gray Vell dawn, the color of unwashed linen, and below it the city lay quiet in the way cities lie quiet before they're searched. Somewhere south of the canal a boy who spoke the First Tongue was sleeping—or hiding, or running already, if he had anyone wise enough to run with. Somewhere in holding, an old friend was tapping scales against a cell wall, keeping time for an orchestra that hadn't assembled yet.

I put on my coat. I picked up my seal. And I gave the order.

"Sweep of the low quarter," I told the waiting courier. "Full instrument coverage. Start with the Saltmarket and work outward." Then, because accuracy is the only virtue I have never managed to abandon, I added the detail that will haunt me more cleanly than any crime: "Begin at the charm-shops. Whoever hid that child started somewhere small. Check everywhere a frightened man might keep a secret."

The courier saluted and ran.

I stood a long moment in my doorway, hating myself with great precision—hating the exactness of it, the thoroughness, the way every true thing I'd written pointed like a compass needle at a shop with a painted sign and a keeper I'd never met. Duty and doubt walked down the stairs beside me, matched step for step, and neither one offered to carry anything.

By nightfall the soldiers would be at his door. I didn't know his name yet.

I would.
