## 12. Flight Across the Rooftops

The first thing you learn as a fugitive is that safety has a smell. Twelve years in Vell had taught me mine: beeswax, dried lavender, the faint scorched sweetness of a charm-shop that sells nothing but luck. I woke to the absence of it.

What replaced it was smoke and iron and the particular silence of a street where every dog had been paid to be quiet.

I was out of bed before my mind finished the arithmetic. Boots. The boy. The satchel under the floorboard with everything worth stealing from my own life—Maren's letter, the almanac with its thirteenth turn inked in red, three charms of true working I'd sworn never to use. Theo slept in the alcove behind the counter, curled around a broom handle like it was a lute, and when I shook his shoulder his eyes opened all at once, no fog of sleep, the way animals wake.

"They've come," I said, which was stupid, because he couldn't answer and didn't need to. He could feel it too. The whole shop felt it—the wards I'd woven into the joists were singing a note I'd never heard them make, high and thin, the sound of a fence realizing the wolf is already inside the pasture.

Downstairs, someone tried the door. Politely first. Then less politely.

I blew out the lamp out of pure habit, which is the most useless thing a man can do while soldiers are knocking his door down, and stood in the dark doing sums. Back door into the tanner's yard—the tanner would sell me for the price of a good dinner. Cellar—I'd filled the cellar with stock, which is to say sand and colored glass, weight without escape. Roof. It was always going to be the roof; I'd just spent twelve years pretending otherwise.

"Theo." I knelt so we were eye to eye. His face in the dark was all eyes. "When I say run, you run where I point. When I say jump, you jump before I finish saying it. And if we get separated—" My voice did something humiliating. I swallowed it. "If we get separated, you go to Maren. You remember her? The woman who cries when you hum?"

He nodded. Then he reached up and pressed two fingers against my sternum, right over the heart, the way he'd seen me check a pulse on a sick bird once. In the First Tongue, soft as breath, he said a word.

I don't know what it meant. But my fear went quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone starts playing music in the next house over. Not gone. Just given somewhere else to sit.

"Right," I said, thickly. "That's cheating, that is."

The door came off its hinges in a bloom of white light and Conclave grey.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about using magic after twelve years of not using it: your hands remember being ashamed. Every gesture I made, some older, better-trained part of me flinched at. When I threw the ward-sigil at the stairwell and it bloomed into a wall of hard light—the first true working this shop had ever seen, sold luck indeed—I heard my mistress's voice in my skull, dry as paper: *Visible, Tamsin? Really? Subtlety, boy, subtlety.*

"Subtle's dead," I told her ghost, and grabbed Theo's wrist, and went up through the trapdoor into the rain.

The roof of my shop met the roof of the chandler's next door across a gap of about four feet and a drop of thirty, and I have never in my life been an athletic man. But there's a working for that—a hedge-witch's step-lightening, a child's spell, the kind of thing they taught us before the Edict alongside counting and colors—and I hadn't cast it since the night I ran from Mistress Ellery's cottage with her last letter burning a hole in my coat.

I cast it now. On both of us.

It's a strange sensation, lightened bones. Like being forgiven slightly in advance. Theo went up onto the chandler's ridge-tile ahead of me, sure-footed as a cat, and looked back with an expression I'd learn to dread over the following hour: delight. Actual delight. Of course. He'd never flown before. He didn't know what we were running from. To him the whole city was a game board and the rain was confetti.

Behind us, below us, my shop filled with lantern-light. I heard the sergeant's voice—flat, procedural, bored the way men are bored by other people's ruin: "Check the cellar. Register says this address twice-flagged." Twice-flagged. So Ilsa had found something after all. Some thread from the market raid, or Maren's name dragged out of her, or simply my own careful ledger, twelve years of harmless nothing, finally read closely enough to look like guilt.

I didn't let myself think about the stock. About the little carved doorways and bottled weather and the jar of *almost* I kept behind the counter for children who needed to believe in something. Twelve years of my life, inventoried by men with gloves.

"Tamsin," I said to myself, out loud, in the rain. "You sentimental fool. Move."

We moved.

* * *

Vell's rooftops are a liar's geography. From the street the city looks like orderly terraces climbing the hill toward the Conclave spire, but up close it's a broken jaw—gaps where fires took a house and the neighbors rebuilt smaller, laundry lines strung across alleys like tripwires, chimneys leaning at angles no architect would confess to. The low quarter's roofs were built by poor people out of spite and salvage, and they will dump you into a courtyard full of hogs without a moment's remorse.

Theo loved it.

He ran the ridgelines like he'd been born up there, and maybe in some sense he had been—born of something old enough that cities were a recent idea. Twice he stopped dead and put his palm flat against a rooftop and said a word, and both times the roof answered. The first time, a chimney stack that had been sagging since before the Edict straightened itself with a groan, remembering what plumb meant. The second time, moss bloomed under his hand in a green rush, a whole winter of growth in a heartbeat, and I had to haul him away before the glow of it painted us on the skyline for every watcher in the quarter.

"No," I panted. "No magic. Not tonight. Not until—"

He looked at me, rain streaming off his chin, and said nothing, because he never said anything in our language, and the nothing was more eloquent than any argument. *Why?* the nothing said. *It's only helping. Why is helping wrong?*

Because I grew up in a country that burned for exactly that question, I didn't say. Because the last time the world heard your voice, boy, it tore itself apart trying to decide who got to hold the microphone.

"Later," I said instead. "I'll explain later."

Which was a lie, and we both knew it, and he took my hand anyway.

* * *

The fire started in Cordwainer Street, though whether it was ours or theirs I never learned. Conclave fire-arrows, probably—they flush quarry out of hiding with flame as a matter of doctrine—but it might equally have been a knocked lamp, a panicked inspector, one of the hidden practitioners down in the tenements choosing immolation over arrest. In Vell those distinctions had stopped mattering years ago. Fire was just the city's native language, spoken fluently by everyone.

By the time we crossed above Tanner's Row, three tenements were burning, and the smoke column had become a second moon, orange and enormous, and the streets below had turned to screaming water. People pouring out with their goods bundled in sheets. Soldiers at the corners, not fighting the fire but watching it, watching the rooftops especially, because anyone clever enough to flee upward was anyone worth catching.

So we stopped being clever and started being desperate.

There's a working I'd only ever read about—Ellery described it once, in the tone she reserved for things that would get apprentices killed. You take the memory of a place and you ask it, loudly, to be elsewhere. Old roads do it naturally; that's why travelers' tales disagree about distances. A trained witch can do it to a staircase, a doorway, a street. It costs. Memory always costs—it's the whole rotten economy of magic, Maren had told me, and I'd thought she was being poetic.

I did it anyway. There was a moment at the end of Cooper's Lane where the roofs ended and the Conclave line began, six soldiers with pikes and a sergeant with a resonance-lantern sweeping the dark, and behind us the fire, and beside me a boy whose hand had gone very small and cold in mine.

I asked the alley to misremember itself.

The lane bent. Not visibly—that's the horror of it, it doesn't look like anything—but the forty feet between us and the soldiers became sixty, then a hundred, then a distance with a river in it that had never been there, a stretch of green bank and standing water reflecting a sky that wasn't tonight's sky. The soldiers stopped. Their lantern swept water that wasn't real and found nothing, because there was nothing, because the alley had agreed to forget its own length.

The cost arrived promptly, itemized. I lost the smell of Ellery's kitchen. Just—gone. Twenty years of bread and herbs and woodsmoke, deleted, and I stood on a stranger's rooftop grieving a smell I could no longer name the source of, holding a child, laughing and crying in equal wet measure.

"That," I wheezed, "is why we don't do magic, Theo."

He squeezed my hand. He was looking at the false river with wide, hungry eyes, and I understood, with a lurch, that to him the cost was invisible. He could pay in memories he had thousands of. He was a vault; I was a purse with a hole in it. The Choir's arithmetic worked differently for him, and someday—not tonight, please not tonight—he was going to figure out that he could afford everything, and what that would make him.

"Not tonight," I said again, aloud, and he frowned at me, and I pulled him on into the smoke.

* * *

We came down off the roofs eventually, because all flights come down; that's the tragedy of the form. We came down in the Salt Quarter, in the shadow of the ruined chapel I'd walked past a hundred times without seeing, its salt-eaten walls glowing faintly in the firelight like the inside of a shell. Behind us, the low quarter burned prettily. Somewhere in it was my shop, my ledger, my twelve years of small safe nothing. Somewhere in it, too, were people I knew—Ossa with his illegal mending-hands, the widow Brakke who bought luck from me every market day and needed it, actually needed it. I had bent an alley out of true and left them holding the bill.

"Keep walking," I told myself, and Theo heard, and took my hand again, and we kept walking.

At the chapel gate he stopped. Turned. Looked back at the fire with an expression I couldn't read—grief, or recognition, or the terrible calm of something that had watched cities burn before, in a tongue the world had tried to kill. Then he said a word I did know, because Maren had taught it to me herself, weeping: *sorrow*, in the First Tongue, or perhaps *mourning-song*, the two being closer in that language than in ours.

Then he looked up at me and pointed, with great seriousness, at the chapel door.

"Yes," I said. "All right. Yes."

Liberation, it turns out, smells like smoke. Loss smells like smoke too. Standing in that doorway with everything I'd built burning behind me and everything I had yet to lose waiting inside, I couldn't tell the two smells apart anymore, and I suspect I never will.

Inside the Salt Chapel, something stirred—an echo, a listening. And far off across the burning city, thin as a needle, a resonance-lantern flared white, and began to move.
