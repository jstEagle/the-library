## 13. The Conclave's True Archive

There are three rules for descending into the Conclave vaults, and I had taught all three to novices in my day: never go alone, never go curious, and never—under any circumstance—read anything that isn't on your assigned list.

I broke all three before lunch.

To be fair to myself, which is a habit I am finding increasingly necessary and increasingly difficult, the third rule was broken by accident. The second was broken by temperament. The first was broken because the archive's under-custodian, Brother Anselm, has a cough that sounds like a man being slowly filed, and I could not bear another hour of it echoing off the shelves.

"I'll manage from here," I told him at the iron door. "You're ill."

"I'm always ill," he said. "It's the dust of dead arguments. It gets in everything."

He handed me a lantern and a key the size of a sermon, and he looked at me a moment too long—the way men look when they hope you will fail, so that they can be proven right about something. Then he climbed back up toward the light, coughing his filing-cabinet cough, and I stood alone at the top of a stair that went down further than any building in Vell should have been deep.

The Conclave keeps its public records above ground: registers of births, edicts, condemnations, the tidy paper trail of a city that burned magic out of itself two generations ago and called the ash peace. What it keeps below ground is supposed to be administrative residue. Old correspondence. Redundant copies. Nothing worth a chief inquisitor's time, and certainly nothing worth breaking rule one.

But I had found eleven dead children in the registers upstairs, and every one of their deaths had the smooth, convenient polish of a stone worn by water. And Maren Oduya had looked at me through the bars of her cell with an expression I could not file anywhere—not contempt, not fear, but *pity*, which is the one thing no prisoner has ever owed me—and said, "You don't know what you're standing inside of, do you?"

So. Rule one, broken. The stair took my lantern-light and gave back very little.

* * *

The lower vaults are older than the Conclave. That was the first wrongness, though I didn't recognize it as one yet. Vell is proud of its youth; we date our city from the Edict, from the founding of the Conclave, from the morning the last hedge-fires were put out. Everything official begins with us. But the masonry around me was cut in courses no Vellish mason used, and the keystones bore sigils I had spent my career cataloguing as *heretical*. Here they weren't graffiti to be scrubbed off tenement walls. They were architecture. They were load-bearing.

I want you to understand what that did to me. Imagine discovering that the floor of your house is made of the bones of the thing you built the house to keep out. Imagine realizing the house has always known.

I walked. The shelves down there were not shelves so much as strata—documents stacked in geological layers, some of them bound in leather, some in bark, some in a grey waxy substance I chose not to identify. My lantern found dates. That was the second wrongness, and this one stopped me cold: the dates ran backward into the war. The actual war. The War of the Choirs, which every schoolchild learns ended in mutual exhaustion and the wise mediation of the first Conclave. There were records here from *during* it. Records are not supposed to exist from during it. The war's archives were burned—we burned them ourselves, ceremonially, annually, a festival with bonfires and hymns about forgetting, which even as a child I had thought was a strange thing to celebrate with such good cheer.

I pulled a folio at random. It was a supply ledger from a garrison whose name I knew only from condemned texts. Grain, lamp oil, arrowheads. Ordinary. And then, entered in the same careful hand, week after week:

*"Dispensed: forgetting, 40 measures."*

I read it four times. Forgetting, forty measures, dispensed like grain. Rationed. Inventoried. A quartermaster's line item.

You can laugh at things like that, sometimes, in the moment, because the mind refuses the alternative. I laughed. It came out sounding like Brother Anselm's cough.

* * *

Deeper still, past the ledgers, there was a chamber that had been sealed with wax and wire and a Conclave seal older than any seal I'd ever pressed into anything. The wire was soft with age; it parted when I looked at it hard, which felt less like force than permission.

Inside, on a reading table that someone had thoughtfully placed there decades ago—someone who had come down here before me, and read this, and gone back up and said nothing—lay the record they call the Verdict of Ashes.

We have a copy of the Verdict of Ashes upstairs. Every novice memorizes it. It is the Conclave's founding document: the account of how the warring choirs' magics had drowned continents in memory-storms, how whole cities had torn themselves apart fighting over remembered grievances, and how the wise founders had ended it by destroying the First Tongue and its speakers. *We did not choose peace over victory*, the famous closing line goes. *We chose peace over memory.*

I have recited that line at perhaps two hundred executions.

The document on the table was not a copy of the Verdict. It was the *draft*. And drafts are honest in ways finals never are, because they show you what the writer crossed out.

The first draft said: *The war cannot be won and cannot be survived. The choirs draw their power from the accumulated grief of their singers, and grief does not diminish with use—it compounds. We propose therefore not to destroy the Choir, which cannot be done while a single singer lives, nor to defeat it, which cannot be done at all, but to make its power unusable.*

Crossed out: three paragraphs describing exactly how.

What remained, uncrossed, was worse. It described a working—a great working, sung once, at terrible cost—using the Choir's own principle against it. A song that would take memories as payment, yes, that part we half-knew, that much the Edict's histories admit. But not just the singers' memories. Not just the combatants'. The song had been pitched wide. Deliberately, methodically, lovingly wide.

*Every soul within the reach of the working will forget the cause of the war*, the draft said. *They will forget why they fought, whom they hated, what was taken. They will forget that they ever forgot. In the morning the survivors will find themselves mourning without object, hating without reason, and they will invent causes, because people always do—but the true cause will be gone from every living mind. The war will not end. It will be unwritten.*

And beneath it, in a different hand—younger, I think, or simply steadier—one sentence added in the margin:

*And when the children born after remember nothing, they will believe the peace was theirs, and that they earned it, and they will build a church on top of the grave and call the flatness of it virtue.*

I sat down on the floor. I am not ashamed of that. There are documents you stand to read and documents you kneel to, and then there are documents that take your legs away as a courtesy, because you'd only fall otherwise.

* * *

Let me tell you what I believed yesterday, because I need to say it plainly while I still know how.

I believed magic murdered my family. I was nine. Our quarter caught fire in the winter of the resonance riots, and the fire moved against the wind, and everyone said afterward that a hedge-worker had called it, and everyone said it the way people say things they have decided in advance to believe. I watched my mother's house go up in colors fire doesn't make. I have spent thirty years turning that night into a vocation. Every child I pulled out of a resonant household, every singer I silenced, every register I audited—I did it so that no one else would stand in a street watching impossible colors eat their home.

And now I sat in the dark holding a page that suggested the fire might have been ordinary after all. That it moved against the wind because of a chimney draft and a lie that had been waiting thirty years for someone to need it. That the hatred I had organized my entire soul around was not a hatred I had chosen but a hatred that had been *installed*—inherited, generation to generation, like a title or a debt, from people who themselves could not have told you where it came from.

Because here is the mechanism, and I understood it with a clarity that felt like falling: the founders hadn't destroyed the Choir's power. They'd borrowed it. One last verse, sung by the last willing singers, spending not their own memories but everyone's. And a payment like that doesn't come due once. The draft was explicit, in the crossed-out sections Anselm's predecessors had failed to burn: the working needed *maintenance*. A small forgetting, dispensed regularly, forty measures to the garrison, to keep the peace flat. To keep anyone from digging. The Conclave hadn't ended the song. The Conclave had become its choir.

We weren't the cure. We were the prescription, refilled monthly, for a disease whose name we had also forgotten.

I thought of the eleven children in the registers. Born across two decades, each resonant, each dead of fevers and falls and quiet accidents, each death signed off by clerks whose hands I recognized—decent hands, tired hands. They hadn't murdered those children. I truly believe most of them didn't know they were doing it. They were instruments playing music written before their grandparents were born. Forty measures of forgetting, and the world stays lawful, and the lawful world includes the certainty that resonance must die young, and no one asks why, because asking why is the first thing the song takes.

Maren's pity made sense now. She had stood in her cell looking at a woman singing a part she couldn't hear.

* * *

There was more. Of course there was more; archives are like wounds that way, the deeper you probe the more they hold. I found the maintenance schedules—the actual liturgy of it, disguised as census rituals and festival rites, each one calibrated to spend a little more of the city's past. I found correspondence between early custodians arguing, in genuinely anguished terms, about whether the working could be safely unwound. One of them, a woman named Custodian Ferren, wrote: *Every year we wait, the price of waking them rises. But every year we act, we prove the crime again. I no longer know which is the greater cruelty, the sleep or the alarm.* Her letters stop mid-year. There is a maintenance record for that season with unusually high dispensation figures. They had forgotten her forgetting her, I suppose. Or she had volunteered. The archive doesn't say, and I found I couldn't make myself look for the answer.

And near the end—near *now*—I found the newest layer. Reports. Mine. Copies of my own sweep reports on the low quarter, filed here among the strata, annotated in a hand I didn't know: *resonance confirmed, subject male, child, approximately seven*. And beside it, in that same hand, a single instruction:

*Thirteenth turn approaches. Full extraction authorized. Do not inform the inquisitor.*

Not *do not trust* the inquisitor. Not *the inquisitor is compromised*. Do not *inform*. As in: she is useful, and she is not cleared, and whatever we are doing with this child, it is not what we told her we were doing.

I had assumed the order to silence Theo came from the Edict's logic. Find the vessel, end the risk, preserve the peace. I had braced myself to carry it out the way I carry everything—with precision and private grief. But this wasn't the Edict's logic. This was the maintenance schedule, worried. The thirteenth turn is when the original working frays—the draft says so, in the margins, in Custodian Ferren's hand: *the song must be renewed each cycle of thirteen, or the memory returns.* The moon is coming around to its thirteenth turn. Somewhere above me, right now, the city's imposed forgetting is beginning to wear thin at the edges, and objects are remembering their old shapes, and a mute boy is starting to speak in the language the founders tried to bury under a civilization.

And the Conclave's answer is not renewal. The annotation was specific. *Extraction.* They don't want to maintain the silence anymore. They want to strip the vessel—take whatever is left of the Choir out of the child before the thirteenth turn completes, harvest it, and hold it. Hold it the way you hold anything you've learned to ration. Forty measures to the garrison. Forgetting, dispensed.

They are going to take the last of the First Tongue out of a seven-year-old boy and put it in a vault, and I am the inquisitor they trust enough to catch him and not enough to tell me why.

* * *

I climbed the stairs slowly, because my legs had opinions and my mind had none, and because some cowardly part of me hoped the ascent would work like an argument—that by the time I reached the iron door I would have talked myself back into certainty, back into duty, back into the clean architecture of my life.

Instead, halfway up, I understood the true shape of the thing, and I had to stop and press my forehead against the cold stone until it stopped spinning.

My whole moral structure—every law I enforce, every mercy I deny myself, the entire cathedral of my purpose—was built on a foundation I had mistaken for bedrock. And the foundation was a deletion. The peace was real, yes; Vell has been peaceful. But it is the peace of a patient who cannot remember the operation. And I am not the surgeon. I have spent thirty years believing I was the surgeon. I'm the scar tissue. I'm what grew over the wound to keep anyone from looking at it.

Brother Anselm was waiting at the top with his cough and his hopeful expression, and I realized he knew. He has dusted these shelves for forty years. He gave me the key the size of a sermon and looked at me the way you look at someone you expect to come back up changed or not come back at all.

"Well?" he said.

"Forty measures," I said, "to the garrison."

Something went out of his face—not surprise. Relief, maybe, the terrible relief of a man who has carried a secret alone for longer than he can bear and finally gets to set it down, even if he sets it down on a fire.

"Then you read the draft," he said. "Ferren's copy. Most of them take the ledgers for the truth and leave. You went deeper." He looked at my empty hands, then at my face. "What will you do, Inquisitor Vane?"

The question of my life, asked by a filing cabinet of a man in a doorway between two worlds.

Because here is the arithmetic I carried up those stairs: if the thirteenth turn completes without renewal, everything comes back. Every grievance. Every reason. Two generations of buried war, returning to minds that never agreed to carry it—including, presumably, the truth of what the founders did, including the truth of what my mother's fire was or wasn't, including every debt the dead children are owed. That is not a small thing to unleash. Ilsa Vane, chief inquisitor, defender of the lawful city, could still argue for the vault, for extraction, for keeping the peace even knowing now what it was made of. There is a version of me that files the report, holds the line, chooses the flatness.

But that version of me requires believing the Conclave serves the peace. And I had read the annotation. *Do not inform the inquisitor.* They don't intend to renew the song. They intend to own it.

Anselm was still waiting for his answer.

"I'm going to need the rest of the night," I said, "and access to the extraction schedule. And Brother—" I heard myself say it, and did not stop myself— "if anyone asks, I came down, I read ledgers, and I learned nothing."

He nodded, slow, and something in his shoulders unclenched for the first time in the years I'd known him. "And what did you learn nothing about?" he asked, because even conspirators love their little liturgies.

I looked back down the stairs, at the dark where the truth of my whole life sat shelved in strata.

"How to un-sing a song," I said. "Before the moon turns."
