## 14. Refuge at the Salt Chapel

The Salt Chapel had been a chapel in the way that a shipwreck is still a boat—technically, and only if you were feeling generous. Its roof had gone to the gulls and the weather a century ago. Its altar was a slab of salt-crusted stone that wept white streaks down its face like an old man pretending he wasn't crying. The sea wind came through the empty windows and rearranged everything loose, which by now was everything.

It was, in other words, perfect. Nobody prayed here. Nobody patrolled it. The Conclave's maps marked it as *dissolved*, which was the closest thing to holy ground a fugitive could ask for.

We'd been there nine days when Theo asked me the question I'd been rehearsing lies for since chapter one of this whole miserable adventure.

But let me back up, because the days before that question were the best days I'd had in twelve years, and I want to be honest about that, even though honesty is about to become the most expensive thing I own.

* * *

Mornings at the chapel started with salt and gulls. Theo would wake first—he always woke first, sitting up in his nest of blankets with that unnerving stillness of his, listening to something I couldn't hear—and by the time I'd coaxed the little iron stove into cooperation, he'd have already swept our corner of the nave with a broom he'd found and repaired himself.

Repaired himself. That's worth dwelling on. He'd found it snapped in half, held the two pieces together, frowned at it with the concentration of a tax assessor, and said something soft and syllabic in the First Tongue, and the broom had *knit*, fibers threading back into fibers like the memory of being whole was just waiting nearby for someone to call it home.

I'd watched him do it from the doorway with my heart going like a rabbit's.

"Theo," I'd said, carefully, "we talked about this."

He'd looked at me. Twelve years of hiding magic, twelve years of selling luck-charms that were nothing but carved wood and confidence, twelve years of flinching every time a door opened in my shop—and here was a child who fixed things by looking at them, and who did not understand, not even slightly, why that should frighten anyone.

"We talked about this," I repeated, uselessly, because what was I going to say? *Don't fix brooms, someone might notice the broom?* We were in a dissolved chapel on a salt marsh with no one for company except birds who'd seen everything and judged nothing. There was no one to hide from out here. Which meant, for the first time since I'd found him shivering on my doorstep, there was no reason to stop him.

So instead I said, "Fine. But if you fix the roof, we're charging the gulls rent."

He didn't laugh. He was still learning that laughing was a thing bodies did. But his mouth did the thing it did—the small, crooked almost-shape of it—and I counted that as a victory and went back to burning the porridge.

That was the rhythm of those days. I taught him words; he taught me how badly I'd been living. He'd point at things and I'd name them—*gull, rope, tide, fire*—and he'd repeat them in that careful, deliberate voice of his, each word set down like a coin on a counter, exact weight, exact value. And then sometimes, when my back was turned, the thing I'd named would become slightly more itself. The fire burned warmer after he learned the word for it. The rope stopped fraying. Once, after I taught him *bread*, the hard heel of a loaf I'd been saving softened into something warm and fresh as if it had never gone stale at all, and I sat there holding it and thinking: *this is what language used to do. This is what they took away.*

Or what they took away from everyone else. From me they took other things—the right to speak freely, the right to exist without paperwork, the right to sleep through the night—but I've catalogued those losses so often they've worn smooth, like stones in a pocket.

Here is what I noticed instead, in those nine days: Theo hummed while he worked. Not tunes exactly. Fragments. Little arcs of sound that rose and fell in patterns no Vellish ear would call music, and that made the salt crusts on the windows glitter in ways I decided not to look at directly.

And here is what I felt, which I noticed less willingly: I was happy. Actually, disgracefully happy. Nine days of porridge and vocabulary lessons and a mute-ish child who fixed brooms, and I felt more myself than I had in over a decade. That's the trap nobody warns you about. Happiness makes you careless. It certainly made me careless, or I'd have seen the question coming.

* * *

On the fourth day, the tide left us a gift: a crate, half-rotted, wedged in the marsh grass a quarter mile south. Inside were books. Someone's drowned library—a bookbinder's stock, maybe, water-stained and swollen, most of them ruined past reading. But three survived well enough, and one of them was a primer. A children's primer, the kind used in village schools before the Conclave standardized education and standardized all the wonder out of it.

Theo held it like it was made of eggshell.

"It's yours," I told him. "Well. It's the tide's, and the tide's giving it to you. That's how ownership works out here."

He opened it to the first page. A printed apple. Below it, the word. He looked at me.

"Apple," I said.

"*Aa-pel.*"

"Close enough. Apple."

"*Apel.*" And on the windowsill behind him, a shriveled crab-apple some bird had abandoned last autumn plumped up, reddened, and gleamed.

I stared at it. He stared at it. Then, very seriously, he closed the primer and moved it three feet away from the fruit, as if distance might prevent recurrences.

"No, no—it's fine, that's—" I rubbed my face. "Theo, listen. Words are just words for me. For you they're... levers. You're going to need to learn which things to lean on."

He considered this with the gravity of a small magistrate. Then he pointed at me.

"You," he said.

"That's right. Tam-sin." I tapped my chest. "Tamsin. Say it."

"Tamsin," he said, and something went off in my chest like a struck bell, which I chose to interpret as indigestion.

From then on the lessons ran both directions. He learned my language; I got reacquainted with his—or the edges of it. His speech came slowly, in fragments, always at dusk for some reason, always facing the sea. He'd stand at the broken east wall where the chapel gave onto the flats and say single syllables to the horizon, and the horizon would listen. I don't know how else to put that. The light would change. The water would flatten. Once, distantly, a line of birds all turned at once, ten thousand wings catching the same breath.

"What are you saying to it?" I asked him once.

He thought about it. He didn't have the words yet to answer properly, so he pointed—at the water, at the light, at his own chest—and then pressed both hands flat over his sternum and made a sound low in his throat, one sustained note that made my fillings ache and my eyes sting.

*Everything,* said the gesture. *All of it. Me too.*

"Well," I said, "keep it down."

He smiled. An actual smile that time, brief and crooked and devastating. I turned away and busied myself with the stove and thought about absolutely nothing at all, because thinking was dangerous and I was getting good at not doing it.

* * *

The question came on the ninth evening, and it came the way questions from children always come—in the middle of something else entirely, when your guard is down and your hands are full.

I was mending his blanket. It had caught on a nail and torn, and I was stitching it by lamplight, tongue between my teeth, doing the clumsy workmanlike stitches my mistress would have mocked me for. *Your hands know hedge-work, boy, not needlework; keep to your talents.* Eleven years dead and she still heckled me. Theo sat across from me, cross-legged, watching the needle go in and out with fascination, and I was explaining—because explaining things to him had become my favorite vice—

"—so the thread goes over, under, pull. Over, under, pull. You're making the two sides remember they were one piece. That's all mending is. Memory with a needle attached—"

"Why," Theo said, "*fear*?"

My needle stopped.

He'd been building toward it for days—I could see that now, replaying it. The new words he'd collected and kept in a separate pile, apart from the friendly ones. *Soldier. Edict. Silence. Run. Hide.* Words he'd pulled from me in stolen conversations, from Maren's terrified face in the market, from the shape of my nights. He'd been carrying them around, turning them over, and tonight he set them on the counter between us.

"Why fear me," he said. Not quite a question. More precise than a question. He pointed at me, then at himself, then made the gesture I'd come to dread: fingers spread, pressing outward, the sign he'd invented for *the way people look when they see what I am.*

The lamp flickered. Somewhere outside, a gull complained about the state of the world.

I had prepared for this moment. Let me stress that. I had lying-down-in-a-dark-room prepared for this moment. I had a whole speech drafted, the good stuff, the stuff that wins arguments with eight-year-olds: *People fear what they don't understand, Theo. People fear what happened long ago. It isn't about you, it's about history—*

And I opened my mouth to deliver it, and I heard my own voice say, instead:

"Because of me."

His head tilted.

"Not—not because of you *you*." My hands had started moving, the way they do when the mouth has deserted its post. "Because of what you're part of. The Choir. There was a war, a long time ago, before either of us, and it was—" I laughed, one dry note. "Nobody remembers why it was fought. That's not a figure of speech. They literally removed the reasons. They burned the Choir out of the world and cut the memory of the war out of everyone's heads, and what they had left afterward was just—fear. Fear with the story missing. Fear that doesn't know what it's afraid of, so it grabs whatever's nearest."

He watched my hands. Waiting. Children are merciless that way; they actually want the answer.

"And you're nearest," I said. "You're the thing the fear would grab. Because you're proof that the old thing isn't dead. And the men who run this city built their whole house on top of the grave, and you—you keep humming, and the grave keeps humming back, and they can't abide it."

Silence. The lamp steadied. Theo looked down at his own hands, spread flat on his knees, the hands that mended brooms and ripened apples and flattened tides.

"But I fix," he said. Careful, placing each word. "I don't break."

"I know."

"People broke." He looked up. His eyes were dark and completely unblinking, the way they went when the First Tongue was close underneath him. "Long ago. Who?"

And there it was. The second question, the one under the first, the one I really couldn't answer—because the true answer was *everyone*, was *the kingdoms together*, was possibly, in a way I've never had the courage to trace all the way down, *my own trade, my own lineage, the hedge-witches and charm-sellers who helped sweep up afterward and called it peace.*

"They were afraid too," I said, which was a lie, or a quarter-lie, or the coward's portion of the truth.

He knew it was a lie. Here's the terrible thing about a child who speaks the First Tongue: he reads the truth of a sentence the way you or I read its grammar. I watched him hear the hollow place in my words, and I watched him decide—not consciously, not cruelly—to accept the lie anyway, because I was giving it to him with both hands and he loved me, God help us both, he trusted me, and a lie offered lovingly is still a kind of shelter.

"Okay," he said softly. And then, after a moment, in the tone of a boy filing something away in a drawer he intended to open later: "*Later*, Tamsin."

"Later," I agreed, and my voice cracked on it, and I bent back over the blanket and stabbed the needle through the cloth and thought: *he'll ask again when he has more words. And when he has all the words, I won't be able to lie at all.*

* * *

I finished the blanket. He fell asleep wearing it, curled near the stove with the primer open on his chest, one hand resting on the page as if even unconscious he was keeping his place in the world.

I sat up a while longer, because I couldn't sleep and because somebody had to keep watch, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do.

Thirteen turns of the moon. We were somewhere in the middle now—I'd lost exact count during the flight from the city, which was its own kind of horror. The turn would complete whether I understood it or not. Theo's speech was opening like a floodgate with the timbers creaking. Every day he had more words, and every word was a lever, and somewhere south of us a woman named Ilsa Vane was following a thread straight through the ashes of the underground market toward a disgraced singer named Maren who had heard Theo hum and wept, and who was, at this moment, in a Conclave cell or worse, because of us.

Because of me. I'd brought her his song. I'd walked into her exile and asked her for help and she'd given it, and I'd left her standing in the rain with the price of that help already falling due.

Theo stirred in his sleep and murmured something. One syllable, soft. The stove flame stood up straighter. The salt tears on the altar stone across the dark nave gleamed, briefly, like they'd been freshly wept.

I looked at that sleeping child and I understood—with the cold, total clarity that visits only when it's least welcome—that I was going to have to choose, soon, between telling him the truth and keeping him safe, and that these were about to stop being compatible options. The Choir's bargain sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: *every verse takes a memory.* I'd known it four days. I hadn't told him. I hadn't told anyone. And the list of memories I'd already begun, silently, secretly, to rank—which ones I could bear to lose—had grown appallingly long.

Outside, far off along the coast road, a light moved. Lantern-bright, steady, wrong. No fisherman walks the flats at midnight with a lantern held high like a torch of office.

No fisherman carries a Conclave seal.

The light paused at the foot of the dunes below the chapel. Held there a long moment, the way a person holds still when they're listening.

Then it began to climb.
