## 15. Maren's Bargain

I had rehearsed my death, in the way you rehearse anything when the waiting is long enough and the cell is small. I'd settled on dignity: chin up, no weeping, perhaps one line of the old descant hummed low as they walked me out—quiet enough that the guards wouldn't hear it, clear enough that whatever remained of my order on the far side of the veil would know I died in tune.

It's remarkable how little of that survives contact with actual rope.

The Conclave kept me in a cellar beneath the Hall of Registers for nine days. Nine days of bread that could have shingled a roof, water that tasted of lead pipe, and a clerk who came twice daily to ask me questions I'd already answered, in case my answers changed. They didn't change. I was a disgraced choir-singer with no voice worth silencing and a name worth erasing; there was nothing to negotiate. On the ninth day they told me the execution would be at dawn, public, in the Register Yard, because the Conclave believed in education.

I slept anyway. That surprised me most. You would think terror would keep a woman awake, but terror is exhausting work, and somewhere past midnight my body simply filed it under *tomorrow's problem* and quit.

What woke me was not the dawn bell. It was a sound I had not heard in twenty years and had never expected to hear again in Vell: a counter-melody, sung badly, by someone who clearly did not understand what he was holding.

*That,* I remember thinking, with the clarity of the half-asleep, *is going to get us all killed.*

Then the wall of my cell came apart like wet bread, dust and old mortar raining down, and a hand seized my wrist, and Tamsin Sandoval—hedge-charmer, coward by his own cheerful admission, the only man in Vell who sold luck that was genuinely nothing at all—said the single least reassuring sentence in the history of rescues:

"Don't scream. Also, don't ask questions yet. I have maybe four minutes before I stop being able to do this."

He could not do it, as it turned out, for even two. The spell that had unmade the wall guttered out halfway across the yard, and we spent the rest of our escape doing things the honest way: running, hiding in a drainage culvert while three soldiers walked past close enough that I could smell the tallow on their boots, and finally climbing through a coal chute into a cellar that smelled of salt and old incense—the ruined chapel below the Salt Quarter, where he'd stashed the boy.

Theo was sitting cross-legged on the altar step when we tumbled through, watching us with those eyes that were too calm for a child and too kind for a weapon. He looked at Tamsin first. Then at me. And then, without any warning at all, he opened his mouth and sang four notes.

Four notes. A fragment. The opening phrase of the Vesper Descant, the one my order died preserving, the one the kingdoms burned eleven thousand singers to forget—and this child tossed it off like a nursery rhyme, in a voice so clean it made my chest hurt.

Tamsin watched my face while it happened. "So," he said. "You recognize it."

"Get me some water," I said, "and then sit down, hedge-witch, because we need to talk about how much trouble you're actually in."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving your own execution: the relief curdles almost immediately into arithmetic. Every hour I lived was an hour the Conclave spent looking. Every kindness these two showed me—a blanket, a bowl of stew Theo insisted on stirring himself, badly, with a wooden spoon he'd charmed into remembering it was once a branch—was a debt accruing interest. I am not a sentimental woman. My order trained sentimentality out of us early, because a singer who loves the wrong thing will spend the song on it, and songs are not free.

But I sat in that salt-crusted chapel with a stolen child and a frightened shopkeeper, eating stew, and I let myself want something. That was my first mistake. Wanting comes before bargaining. It always does.

"So explain it to me properly," Tamsin said. "Not the version for children. Not the version the Edict left us."

"The version the Edict left *you*" — I pointed the spoon at him — "is the version my order wrote for people exactly like you. People who needed to be afraid in the right direction." I set the bowl down. Theo immediately refilled it. I let him. There are small mercies and there are strategic ones.

"The Choir was not a weapon," I said. "That's the first lie. It was a *practice*. Thirteen voices, thirteen disciplines, each voice carrying one thread of the world's memory—the way a loom carries threads, not the way a fist carries a stone. When all thirteen sang together, they didn't break reality. They *renegotiated* with it. Healing. Weather. The mending of broken things. Real work. Slow work. Work that required every singer to understand exactly what she was spending."

"And what were they spending?" Tamsin asked. He knew. I could see he knew; he'd been living next to the answer for weeks, watching cups knit themselves shut. But he wanted me to say it, because saying it made it someone else's confession.

"Memory," I said. "Every verse takes one. From every listener within earshot. One memory per verse, taken without asking, given without receipt. That was the bargain the Choir struck with the world at its making, and the world does not renegotiate. You sing, and the listeners pay. You cannot aim it. You cannot choose which memory leaves or whose. You can only decide whether the miracle is worth what it costs the room."

Theo had stopped stirring. He was looking at me with his head tilted, the way he did when a word was too big and he was trying to catch it anyway. I softened—not for Tamsin's sake. For his.

"They told you the Choir was destroyed," I said to the boy. "Burned out at the end of the war. That part is true, in the way official truths are true. Eleven thousand singers died. The great choirs were pulled down. The language itself was forbidden." I touched my own throat, briefly. Twenty years since the Edict took my voice from public use; the habit of touching it hadn't faded. "But a choir isn't a building, child. A choir is *voices*. And voices can be scattered. Hidden. Planted."

"In children," Tamsin said flatly.

"In children," I agreed. "Thirteen of them, born across twenty years, each carrying a thread. The order hoped the world would soften. That someday they could be found and gathered and taught gently, and the Choir would rise again whole, with consent, with understanding." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Instead the Conclave found them first. One by one. Fever, accident, 'ordinary causes'—eleven children buried so tidily that even the registers barely noticed. Your almanac-reading friend there can confirm the count."

"Theo makes twelve," Tamsin said quietly.

"Theo makes twelve," I said. "And here is the arithmetic that has been keeping me awake since the coal chute. A choir of twelve can hum. Can bend bread and knit cups and make walls sweat mortar—which, incidentally, is why half the low quarter is whispering about ghosts. But it cannot *sing*. Twelve threads without the thirteenth is a knot, not a weave. And a knot under that much pressure—" I looked at the boy. He was still watching me. Still patient. "A knot frays. Wildly. The turn of the moon is coming, and when it completes, whatever is in him wakes fully whether anyone consents or not. Untrained, ungathered, alone. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means Vell burns," Tamsin said.

"It means everything within a day's walk of Vell burns," I said. "Or worse than burns. Memory is the ground everything stands on. Let a raw Choir loose in a city of a hundred thousand people and you won't get corpses. You'll get something more interesting. You'll get a city that forgets itself mid-scream. Streets that forget they're streets. Ten thousand people standing in the rubble trying to remember their own names and finding nothing there."

Silence, except the drip of water somewhere in the dark behind the altar.

"That's if we do nothing," Tamsin said. "And if we run? Take him north, past the border—"

"Past the border is where the thirteenth thread was planted, or where it died—we never knew which. Running might buy years. It might buy nothing. And the Conclave's reach is longer than your legs." I leaned forward. "So. Those are the choices as they stand. Do nothing and lose everything. Run and probably lose slightly less quickly. Or—"

"No," Tamsin said immediately.

"You haven't heard the option yet."

"I've heard the shape of it in your voice. No."

"The option," I continued, because I had rehearsed this too, over nine days in a cellar, and unlike my death it was a rehearsal I intended to use, "is that we finish it ourselves. Deliberately. Thirteen singers, gathered, taught, singing in harmony instead of in accident. A controlled completion. We choose the verses. We choose the moments. We stand in front of what we sing and we watch it take what it takes, with our eyes open."

"One memory per verse," Tamsin said. "From everyone listening."

"Yes."

"Taken without asking."

"It doesn't ask. It never asks. That's not a flaw in the design, Tamsin, it's the *price*, and prices don't negotiate—"

"Whose memory?" His voice had gone very quiet, which in my limited experience of the man was worse than shouting. "If we do it your way. Whose memories go first?"

I had prepared a dozen answers to that question. Diplomatic ones. Mathematical ones. None of them survived his eyes.

"Whoever is closest," I said. "Whoever listens longest. Which is why the singers must be volunteers who understand the cost, and why the congregation must be small, and why every verse must be chosen like a surgeon chooses a cut." I spread my hands. "Yes. It's monstrous. I'm not dressing it up. The disease and the cure are the same substance, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling charms."

Something flickered across his face at that—pain, quickly folded away. Good. He needed to feel it now, in this room, rather than later, in front of a crowd.

"There's a third path," he said. "You said twelve threads. What if the twelfth simply sleeps? What if we bind Theo—"

"You'd have to find a binding strong enough to hold what he is, and the last binding that strong was the Edict itself, and look what that cost the world." I shook my head. "Besides. Ask yourself what you'd be binding. Not a power. A *child*. You'd be nailing the lid down on a person to protect a city from what that person never chose to be. The Conclave tried that. It's called murder with better paperwork."

Theo touched my arm. Small fingers, deliberate. He didn't speak—he spoke rarely, still, choosing words the way misers choose coins—but he looked at Tamsin, then at me, then back, and made a gesture I didn't understand until Tamsin translated, his voice rough: "He's asking what happens to him. In your plan. At the end."

And there it was. The question underneath every other question, the one I'd been circling for days, because the honest answer was the cruelest thing I knew.

"At the end," I said carefully, "when the thirteenth voice joins and the Choir rises whole—it spends itself. That's what a completed song does. It pays out everything it gathered. The hatred in this city, the fear, the Edict's long shadow—all of it can be unwritten, if the price is paid in full. And when the last verse ends..." I reached out and touched the boy's cheek. "There won't be a Choir in you anymore, little one. There'll just be you. An ordinary boy, with an ordinary life, and no one hunting him, and no one afraid of him. Ever again."

Hope crossed his face like sunrise. I hated myself for it, because I hadn't finished.

"But the song needs a first singer," I said, turning to Tamsin before hope could settle anywhere. "Someone outside the thirteen threads. Someone who understands both sides—who knows magic's cost and the city's fear, who has lived the lie long enough to know exactly what it weighs. Someone the others will follow." I held his gaze. "The counter-melody you sang in that prison yard. Where did you learn it?"

His face went white. "My mistress. Before she died. She taught me one melody and swore me never to use it. She said it was—" He stopped.

"She said it was the anchor," I finished. "The line that holds the Choir steady while the rest of the weave turns. Yes. I know the teaching. My order passed it down the same way. Which means, hedge-witch, that the woman who raised you didn't just teach you to hide. She taught you to *lead*. Whether she meant to or not."

"She's dead," Tamsin said. "She's been dead six years. Whatever she wanted—"

"Is written in your throat," I said, "whether you like it or not. First verse is yours. You sing it, you choose what moment it lands on, and you stand in the room and watch it take its due—from strangers, yes, but also from us. From me. From the boy. From you." I stood, because my knees had opinions about the floor. "That's the bargain. Thirteen voices. One memory per verse. Eyes open all the way down. And the first payment—"

"—comes out of me," he said.

"Comes out of you," I confirmed. "Horror dressed as hope. I warned you I wasn't dressing it up."

He looked at Theo. The boy looked back, and whatever passed between them had nothing to do with the First Tongue and everything to do with a rainy doorstep months ago, a mute child, a man who should have shut the door.

"We'll need twelve singers," Tamsin said at last, and his voice sounded like a man reading his own sentence aloud. "Where in all of Vell do you propose we find twelve people willing to have their memories carved verse by verse?"

I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like wearing someone else's shoes.

"I was executed nine days ago," I said. "Everyone the Conclave drags out of the underground market, everyone Maren's name touches—they'll come looking for the woman who died. Or the woman who didn't." I picked up the stew spoon and handed it back to Theo. "Fear gathers crowds, hedge-witch. So does hope. We're going to need both."

Outside, far off toward the Hall of Registers, a bell began to ring—not the dawn bell, not the hour bell. The alarm. Somewhere in the dark, soldiers were discovering an empty cell and a wall shaped like wet bread.

Tamsin closed his eyes. "They'll sweep the Salt Quarter by morning."

"Then we'd best start recruiting tonight," I said.
