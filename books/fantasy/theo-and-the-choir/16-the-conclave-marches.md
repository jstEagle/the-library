## 16. The Conclave Marches

The summons came at dawn, which is how the Conclave always delivers its worst news—early enough that you haven't yet eaten, so your hands shake and you can blame the shaking on hunger.

I read it standing in my quarters with the window open behind me, letting the cold sharpen whatever the words were trying to do to me. *Inquisitor Ilsa Vane is hereby appointed Marshal of the Purge of the Low Quarter.* My name in High Script, all vertical strokes and certainty, the way they write things when they want them to look like history before they've even happened.

Marshal. Not participant. Not advisor. Marshal.

They wanted my face on it. That was the whole point. Twenty years of sweeps, eleven resonant children found and closed out, one inquisitor who had never flinched—and now a boy in the salt-stained chapel district who hummed in a dead language, and who better to end him than the woman who'd ended all the others?

Except I had ended none of them. That was the thing I'd learned three nights ago in the vaults, kneeling among ledgers older than Vell itself: the children hadn't died of fevers and falls and convenient accidents because magic killed them. They'd died because someone made sure they would, tidy as bookkeeping, while an entire civilization's memory of why any of it mattered was scraped away like frost from a window.

The Edict wasn't peace. It was a lobotomy performed on a city, and I had spent my career administering the follow-up treatments.

"You're up early," said Denner from the doorway. He was my second, or had been until about four seconds ago in his own mind, and he wore the expression of a man delivering good news he didn't understand was terrible. "Congratulations, Inquisitor. Marshal Vane. There are those who've waited decades for that appointment."

"Have there." I set the summons down carefully, as if it might go off. "Denner, what do you remember about the war?"

He blinked. "Which war?"

"Exactly."

"The Founding War," he said slowly, the way you recite something learned by rote, which was the only way anyone remembered anything about the Founding War. "The mages burned the old kingdoms. The Conclave rose from the ashes. The Edict brought peace." He frowned. "Why? Is this for the address? Are we doing the address again? Because last time you told me if I used the word 'ashes' one more time—"

"It's nothing. Congratulations accepted. Tell the clerks I'll review the purge manifests by noon."

He went. I stood at the window a while longer, watching smoke rise off the low quarter's chimneys—the ordinary smoke of ordinary people making breakfast, some of whom I was going to lead soldiers against within the week.

Here is what nobody tells you about discovering your entire life has been built on a deletion: it doesn't feel like revelation. It feels like vertigo in a room you've walked through ten thousand times. The furniture is all where you left it. It's the floor that's missing.

* * *

The memory-engine core sat in Vault Nine, which required two keys, a spoken seal, and a clerk named Brother Ossian whose job description, as far as I could tell, was to be forgettable. He was very good at it. I had genuinely never been able to describe him to myself afterward, which should have struck me as suspicious years ago.

"I need the vault," I told him. "Marshal's authority. Inventory audit before the purge."

"Of course, Inquisitor." He produced the second key from somewhere inside his robes without appearing to move. "Will you be requiring assistance?"

"No. And Ossian—" I looked at him hard, trying, for once, to hold his face in place. "You'll be elsewhere for an hour."

"Yes, Inquisitor," he agreed pleasantly, and went somewhere I could not afterward have named.

Vault Nine smelled of dust and tallow and, faintly, of ozone, though I only recognized the smell in hindsight. The Conclave kept its true archive down here—not the public reliquary upstairs with its heroic murals of the Edict's signing, but the working machinery of what had actually been done.

The memory-engine was a sphere of black glass the size of a heart, caged in brass ribs, humming at a pitch below hearing. You felt it more than heard it, a pressure behind the eyes, like standing too close to a bell. Around it, racked in ordered rows, sat the cores of every engine the Conclave had ever built—the instruments of the Great Forgetting, the machines that had reached into the minds of a continent and taken the war out of it, root and branch and reason.

I had come intending, I think, merely to look at it. To confirm what the ledgers said. To give doubt one more chance to be wrong.

But the core was warm when I lifted it from its cradle, warm like a living thing, and the pressure behind my eyes resolved—for half a heartbeat—into something almost like a voice. Not words. A sense of *capacity*. Of held things. This machine had swallowed the memories of millions, and somewhere in its black glass, I understood with absolute clarity, those memories still existed. Nothing the Choir did was ever truly destroyed. It was only stored, and paid out, and spent.

The Conclave hadn't erased the war. It had *banked* it.

And a bank, unlike a grave, can be robbed.

I wrapped the core in my cloak. It weighed less than a loaf of bread. I had expected it to weigh more—sin usually does—but perhaps weight was another thing the engineers had engineered away.

The guards at the vault door saluted me. I saluted back. My hand did not shake, and I noted this with a strange professional detachment, the way you'd note a colleague's competence: *Vane, I. — performs flawlessly under conditions of total moral collapse.*

There was a version of me—I could see her clearly, standing just over my shoulder like an unquiet ghost—who reported the theft herself. Who turned around, carried the core back down, and confessed everything to the First Chair, accepting whatever purification followed, because that was the shape of a lawful life and I had been nothing if not lawful.

But the lawful life had been built on a floor that wasn't there. And somewhere across the city, in a ruined chapel smelling of salt and old incense, a hedge-witch I had sworn to destroy was sheltering a child who spoke the language reality used to listen to—and if the Conclave marched on them with fire, they would not stop at the chapel. The purge manifests listed forty-one addresses. I had reviewed them. I had *approved* them, hours ago, with a marshal's signature, in ink I had mixed myself according to tradition.

Forty-one addresses. Maren Odile's cell was the ninth.

I had put her name on that list myself, three days ago, in the flat voice of duty, and she had looked at me through the bars of her interrogation room with something worse than hatred—recognition—and said, "Ils. You used to sing descant. Don't you remember?" And I had said, *the Edict forbids singing*, and she had laughed, and the laugh had followed me home and sat on the edge of my bed all night like a cat that knew a dead mouse was under the floorboards somewhere and was waiting, patiently, for me to find it.

I found it. It was in Vault Nine, wrapped in my cloak, humming.

* * *

Stealing out of the Conclave citadel proved insultingly easy, which told me something important: no one steals from the Conclave, because no one believes the Conclave can be wrong. The gates opened for my rank. The bridges cleared for my escort pennant. I dismissed the escort at the Tanners' Stair with a lie about meeting informants alone, and then I was simply a woman in a grey cloak walking downhill through evening rain, carrying the sum of a civilization's stolen past in a satchel against her hip, and not one soul in Vell looked twice.

The low quarter received me the way it received everyone—with suspicion first, then indifference. I passed the shuttered charm-shop with its faded sign, the luck-seller's door still marked with the Conclave's seizure wax. I passed the burned tenements from the flight two nights ago, black ribs against the dusk. Children had already chalked hopscotch grids in the ash. That was the low quarter for you. Grief was a surface; life got written on top of it.

Two streets from the chapel, I stopped under an awning and took stock, because a career of inquisition teaches you at least one useful skill, and mine was knowing when I was walking into a trap—even when I was the trap.

They would know I was coming. Tamsin Sandoval had evaded me for twelve years; he would have watchers, wards, probably the boy himself, who could presumably unmake my weapons with a well-placed syllable. If I arrived armed, I confirmed their fears. If I arrived empty-handed, I confirmed their fears differently. There was no configuration of approach that did not say, in some dialect, *I am Ilsa Vane, and here is what I have done to people like you.*

So I did the only honest thing available. I laid the satchel on the wet cobbles, knelt, and unwrapped the core where any watcher could see it—black glass gleaming in the rain, brass ribs beaded with water—and then I left my sword beside it. Both of them. The short blade too, the one in my boot, which I had carried since I was nineteen and which had gotten me out of more rooms than my rank ever had.

Then I picked the satchel back up, left the steel shining on the stones like an offering or an accusation, and walked the rest of the way with my hands visible and empty.

Rain ran down the back of my collar. I found I was praying, which was absurd, because the Conclave taught that prayer was a form of unlicensed petition, and also because I no longer believed in anything the Conclave had taught me, and also because the god I was half-praying to was possibly a mute child eating bread in a ruin.

The chapel rose out of the mist at the street's end, salt-white walls furred with lichen, roof gone to sky, doorway gone to dark. A ward flared as I crossed the threshold—or rather, a ward *noticed* me, a prickling along my skin like the memory of being watched, and out of the shadows came Tamsin Sandoval with his hands raised and light gathering in them, forbidden and beautiful and terrified.

Behind him, half-hidden by a fallen pillar, the boy. Theo. Smaller than the manifests had promised. Watching me with eyes the color of deep water, utterly unafraid, which frightened me more than any weapon could have.

"Inquisitor Vane." Sandoval's voice cracked on the rank. "You shouldn't be able to stand there. The ward should have—"

"It let me through." I spread my empty hands. "Or it decided I'm not worth stopping. I honestly don't know which would insult me more."

"What do you want?"

"A fair question." I set the satchel down between us, slowly, and folded back the cloth. The core caught what light remained and threw it back warped, and the pressure of it filled the ruined nave like a tide coming in. I saw Sandoval's face change—saw him *recognize* what it was, hedge-witch's apprentice once, he'd have known the engines' work intimately, from the receiving end. "This came out of Vault Nine tonight. It's the memory-engine core. The original. The one that took the war out of everyone's heads and left us all standing in the ash asking each other what we were so angry about." I paused. "It can give it back. I believe that's what these do—they store, and they return. The ledgers call it 'restoration of baseline.' As if we were patients. As if forgetting were health."

The boy stepped forward. Sandoval moved to stop him and couldn't quite bring himself to touch the child, and the child crossed the space between us and crouched by the satchel and looked into the black glass the way you look into a well.

He said something. Two syllables, soft, in the First Tongue, and the core *answered*—a single low tone, felt in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the floor. Every pigeon in the belfry went up at once. Somewhere outside, a dog began to sing, horribly, beautifully, harmonizing with a machine.

Theo looked up at me and smiled, and it was the smile of a child showing you a beetle, delighted, guileless, and I understood suddenly and completely why the Conclave feared him, and why they were right to, and why they were damned for it anyway.

"He says it remembers him," Sandoval translated quietly. His hands had lowered. The light in them had gone out, replaced by something more dangerous, which was thought. "He says it's lonely in there. Full of everything and no one allowed to look." He studied me across the satchel, across twenty years of pursuit, across the smoking ruin of everything I had believed at dawn. "Why are you here, Ilsa? Say it plainly. I've earned plain speech from you, of all people."

Plain speech. From me. I tried to assemble it—to find the sentence that would make this conversion sound like principle rather than collapse, like strategy rather than surrender—and discovered there wasn't one, and that this was, perhaps, the first honest moment of my adult life.

"I was ordered to burn this quarter," I said. "With you in it. With him in it. Forty-one addresses, and I signed the manifest, because signing manifests is what I do, it's what I've *been*, since the night my family died and the Conclave told me who to blame." My voice did something I disliked. I let it. "Then I read the true archive. Then I stole this. And now I'm standing in your chapel with no sword and no order and no idea what I am anymore, offering you a stolen god in a bag, and I want you to understand—" I stopped. Started again, plainer than plain. "Don't trust me. Don't forgive me. I'm not asking. I've done things that don't wash out. But I'm here, and I'm not leaving, and if the Conclave comes—and they will come, they'll have noticed Vault Nine by morning—then they come through me first, and I am *very* good at what I do, and what I do, it turns out, works for whoever I aim it at."

Silence, except the rain and the fading dog.

Maren Odile emerged from the shadow of the choir stalls—she'd been there the whole time, listening, arms folded, and I hadn't sensed her, which meant her exile had taught her things my training never had. She looked at me a long moment. At the sword lying in the rain outside. Back at me.

"Well," she said. "The Conclave marches, and its marshal arrives singing alto. Thirteen voices, Ils. The moon won't wait for your conscience to finish arriving." She jerked her chin toward the nave, where candlelight was beginning to gather, and figures with it—hedge-witches and defectors and faces I knew from wanted lists, assembling in the dark like a congregation that had always been there. "Come in out of the rain. Rehearsal starts at moonrise. And Vane—"

She smiled, and there was no warmth in it, only survival.

"—you'll need to learn the descant. You used to know it. Before they took that, too."
