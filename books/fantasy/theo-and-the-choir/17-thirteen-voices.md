## 17. Thirteen Voices

I had spent twelve years learning how to be alone without noticing it, and it took one afternoon to unlearn everything.

They came to the Salt Chapel the way water comes into a cellar—quietly, through cracks you didn't know existed, until you looked down and found yourself ankle-deep in people.

The first was a hedge-healer named Brannoc who had sold poultices out of a fish cart for twenty years and whose magic was so small and stubborn it could only ever do one thing: keep wounds from going bad. He arrived with his cart, parked it outside the chapel like he'd been meaning to visit for ages, and asked me, with the flat pragmatism of a man discussing weather, whether I needed anything boiled.

Then came two sisters who could speak to moths, which sounds useless until you learn that moths see everything at night and gossip terribly. Then a former Conclave clerk named Pellam who arrived shaking so hard I thought he'd collapse on the threshold, carrying a satchel of stolen ledgers and an apology forty years overdue to no one in particular. Then a tanner's widow who could make rope remember being grass. A boy of nineteen who could find lost things but not, tragically, lost people. An old woman who grew flowers in her footprints when she walked barefoot through ash.

Twelve, counting me. Thirteen counting Theo, though Theo wasn't a singer yet. He was the song.

Maren counted us twice that first evening, standing in the ruined nave with salt crusted up the walls like tide-marks—which is where the chapel got its name, she told me, though I suspected she invented half her history—and said, "Thirteen. Of course it's thirteen." She laughed, and there was glass in the laugh. "The old orders never wrote down why thirteen. I used to think it was doctrine. Now I think it was arithmetic. You need enough voices to hold a verse and enough memory between them to pay for it."

"And if we're short?" I asked.

"Then someone pays double." She said it lightly. She was very good at saying terrible things lightly; I'd begun to understand it was the only way she survived her own knowledge.

* * *

I should describe the chapel, because by the end of that week it deserved describing.

It sat above the old salt flats east of Vell, a day's walk from the walls, roofless in places and proud of it. The Conclave had burned it during the Edict—not because anything had happened there, Maren said, but because chapels were where singing lived, and the Edict hated singing the way rot hates salt. What remained were bones: arches like ribs, a floor of pale stone gone soft-edged with rain, and one wall still bearing a faded fresco of figures with their mouths open. Nobody knew if they were singing or screaming. We chose to believe singing. That choice felt like the first honest thing any of us had done in years.

We cleaned it the way poor people clean things—badly, cheerfully, and together. Brannoc boiled water. The moth-sisters swept with brooms that the moths steered, which was faster than it should have been and deeply unsettling to watch. Pellam, the clerk, organized our supplies with a bureaucratic fervor that suggested he'd been waiting his whole life for someone to let him make a list.

And Theo ran.

I want to get this right, because it matters more than any verse we sang. Before Vell, before the shop, before all of it, Theo had been a creature of doorways—the doorstep, the cellar door, the gap under the shutters. He moved through rooms like a rumor. But in the chapel, with the wind coming through the broken roof and the sky doing its enormous indifferent business overhead, he *ran*. Flat-out, arms-wide, shrieking-with-joy running, from one arch to the next, and every time his bare feet hit the stone the salt crusts bloomed with tiny white flowers that weren't any flower that had ever grown anywhere.

He'd learned maybe sixty words by then. He deployed them recklessly, in combinations no grammar would sanction, and the world bent politely around each one. "Light," he'd say, pointing at a shadow, and the shadow would reconsider. "Green," he'd say to a dead bush, and by morning it would be having second thoughts about being dead. He was six years old and he was made of the language reality had been written in, and mostly what he wanted to do with this cosmic inheritance was catch moths.

I caught myself watching him instead of working, more than once. Maren caught me watching him.

"You look terrified," she observed.

"I'm always terrified. It's my best feature."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Terrified looks different. You look like a man memorizing something." She let that sit between us, then went back to tuning nothing, humming quarter-tones to herself the way other people crack their knuckles.

She was right, of course. I was memorizing him. Every practitioner knows the feeling—you learn it young, when your mistress teaches you your first cantrip and tells you it might be the last magic you're ever free enough to do. You hold the moment up to the light and turn it over, because moments are the only currency that doesn't devalue. I was memorizing Theo mid-run, the moth sisters laughing, the flowers blooming in his footprints. Some part of me had done the arithmetic Maren refused to say aloud, and it was hoarding.

* * *

The rehearsal began badly, which Maren insisted was traditional.

"Every choir I ever trained started as a crime against music," she announced from atop an overturned salt barrel, addressing us like a general addressing troops she expected to desert. "Your job today is not to be good. Your job is to be *together*. Goodness comes later, if it comes at all. Together comes now or never."

She'd spent three days constructing the descant from fragments—her order's surviving notation, Theo's hummed pieces, and, she admitted reluctantly, one line that came to her in dreams, which she trusted about as far as she could throw the dream. The structure was simple to explain and impossible to do: a ground-tone held by four voices, a rising figure answered by four more, and then the descant itself, which was less a melody than a shape, a thing that existed in the space between voices rather than in any voice at all.

"The Choir was never thirteen soloists," she said. "That's the mistake the old kingdoms made, thinking they could conscript it, command it, point it at cities. It's one voice distributed across thirteen throats. You don't sing *at* each other. You sing *as* each other's missing parts."

Pellam raised his hand like we were in committee. "What happens if we fail?"

"We sound terrible," Maren said, "and everyone remembers exactly why they came, which will be its own small miracle in this city."

The first attempts were, indeed, crimes. The moth-sisters harmonized instinctively with each other and nobody else. Brannoc's voice was a foghorn with opinions. Pellam could read notation perfectly and sing none of it, producing the precise pitches of a man being audited. My own contribution was worse than all of them, because twelve years of enforced silence does something to a voice—it makes it careful, and careful singing is dead singing. Maren stopped me four times in the first hour.

"You're hiding inside your own throat," she said, not unkindly. "I know the trick. I invented it. You put just enough voice out to comply and keep the rest locked away where no one can take it. But the descant won't accept a partial voice. It's not a tax collector. It wants everything you've got."

"That's what worries me," I said.

"Good," she said. "Worry means you understood me. Now again, from the ground-tone, and this time I want to hear the man who burned down his own life rather than hand over a child. Sing like *that*."

* * *

It happened on the third evening.

There was no dramatic cause. The light was going amber through the broken roof, Theo was asleep against Brannoc's cart with salt flowers in his hair, and we were tired in the specific way that makes people honest. Maren gave us the pitch. Four voices took the ground-tone—Brannoc, one moth sister, the tanner's widow, me. And because I was tired, I forgot to be careful.

The tone locked.

I felt it happen the way you feel a key turn—a click somewhere behind the sternum. Suddenly the four of us weren't four people making a sound; we were one sound that happened to have four histories. I could feel Brannoc's fish-cart calluses in the note. I could feel the widow's grief for a husband thirty years dead, folded into the harmony like salt into bread. Not their memories—I wasn't taking them, thank every merciful power—but their *weight*, offered freely, which turned out to be a completely different substance than weight taken.

The rising figure answered. Eight voices now, and the chapel changed around us. I don't mean acoustics. I mean the chapel *remembered*. The burnt arches flushed faintly warm, the way stone does after sun. The fresco figures on the far wall seemed to lean toward us—or perhaps they'd always been leaning and we'd never had ears to notice. Dust rose off the floor in slow spirals, not disturbed, *dancing*, each mote tracking a line of the harmony like it had been waiting decades for the choreography.

Then Theo woke up.

He didn't cry or startle. He stood up in the cart bed, hair full of white petals, and listened with his whole body the way only children and animals listen. And when the phrase came around again, he opened his mouth and joined.

I have heard descriptions of angels. I have written some myself, in the dishonest early novels I prefer to forget. None of them were ready. His voice wasn't loud. It was *central*, the way a hearth is central to a house—not the biggest thing in the room but the thing everything else arranged itself around. The descant found him the way iron finds lodestone, and suddenly the shape Maren had been building from fragments all week snapped into focus, and it was vast, and it was old, and it was *glad*.

The salt flowers opened across the entire floor at once. The moths came down through the broken roof in their hundreds and hung in the air above us in a slow revolving galaxy, wings catching the last amber light. Old Wrenna—the ash-flower woman—was crying and singing and growing lilies with every barefoot step she swayed. Pellam was singing, actually singing, the audit-man, tears cutting clean lines down his dusty face, and later he'd tell me he could hear his mother's kitchen in the ground-tone, though he hadn't thought of that kitchen in thirty years, and the hearing cost him nothing because he'd given it.

Nobody took anything. That was the miracle of it, the one I couldn't stop turning over afterward. Everything in the chapel that night was *given*—voices, grief, joy, attention—and the song spent only what was handed over, and gave back change besides. For eleven minutes—for exactly one verse—we were what the old kingdoms must have glimpsed before they decided such a thing was too dangerous to permit.

Because it is too dangerous to permit. Hold onto that. I'll need you to remember it in a few chapters, and so will I.

The verse ended the way tide goes out—not stopping, withdrawing. Silence rushed in after it, enormous and ringing. In the quiet, the moths settled on the arches like a congregation taking pews. Somewhere outside, real night birds had gone silent, listening.

Theo looked at me across the nave, face lit gold, and used one of his sixty words. He pointed at all of us—at the singers, the sleeping cart, the flowers, the sky through the roof—and said, with the total confidence of a child stating physics:

"*Home.*"

The word landed in the stone and the stone agreed. I swear to you the floor warmed under my feet.

Maren sat down hard on her salt barrel. She was laughing and weeping in equal measure and neither was winning. "Thirteen voices," she managed. "Do you understand what we just did? We did it *gently*. The records all talk about the Choir spending memory like fire spends forests, and we—" she gestured at the blooming floor, the sleeping child, the weeping clerk— "we paid in gifts. Freely offered. Maybe that's what the kings never learned. Maybe that's why it ate cities. They tried to command a thing that only works when it's loved."

"Then we teach it to the Conclave," said Pellam, wiping his face, "and everyone goes home."

Nobody laughed. We all wanted to. That wanting, I think, was the warmest moment of my life—thirteen people in a ruin, wanting the same impossible kindness at the same time.

* * *

We rehearsed for six more days. Each time the verse came easier, each time the chapel remembered more of itself—melted candle stubs re-forming, a shattered bell in the yard knitting its crack halfway before giving up, exhausted. We learned each other's breathing. Brannoc learned he could sing after all, if you let him sing low enough. The moth-sisters taught us all a trick of listening with closed eyes. Wrenna danced. Theo napped through rehearsals and joined for the final verse, always, as punctual as moonrise, and every time his voice came in the whole structure lit from within.

On the sixth evening, Ilsa Vane arrived.

I saw her first, standing at the edge of the salt flats in the last light, a thin dark figure with a heavy pack and no horse, walking toward us the way people walk when they've burned their ships and would simply prefer not to discuss it. She carried something in her arms wrapped in a Conclave cloak, and even at that distance I could feel it—a wrongness, a hum, like a struck bell that wouldn't finish dying.

She stopped at the chapel gate. Behind her, miles east, tiny against the dusk, lights were moving along the road from Vell. A lot of lights. Arranged in ranks.

Ilsa looked at the lights, then at us—at Theo asleep in the cart, at Maren, at the flowers still open on the stones—and said, in the flat voice of a woman who has already said goodbye to everything she was:

"They're coming tonight. And I brought the thing that remembers everything."
