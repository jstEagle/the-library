## 18. The Verse That Takes

I lost my voice to the Edict eleven years ago, and I have missed it every single day since, the way you miss a limb—phantom aches where the music used to live. So understand that when I tell you what happened in the Salt Chapel on the night we sang the first full verse, I am not speaking as a singer. I am speaking as the woman who spent eleven years learning, note by agonizing note, what it costs to want something back.

We wanted it back too much.

* * *

The chapel smelled of salt and old rain, which is fitting, because grief does too. Tamsin had done his best with the place—swept floors, mended pews with hedge-charms he no longer bothered hiding from us, hung lanterns that burned without oil and pretended they'd always been allowed to. Thirteen of us sat in a rough crescent behind him. Not thirteen trained voices; let's be honest about what we were. A disgraced choir-singer with half her range. Two defector clerks who could hold a pitch the way a drowning man holds driftwood. A hedge-witch named Odile who sang like a kettle. And at the center, Theo, whose voice was not a voice at all but a door left open in the world.

We had rehearsed for six days. Six days of fragments, of single syllables tested and withdrawn, of Maren-do-you-feel-that and stop-stop-stop whenever the candle flames leaned toward us like listeners. We thought we understood the bargain. One verse, one memory, surrendered freely by whoever stood in the circle. We had even chosen our payment in advance—a small thing, we told ourselves. The clerk Pellam offered up his memory of a mediocre holiday. I offered the name of my first singing teacher, which sounds generous until you know she died twenty years ago and I had already begun losing her face.

The Choir, it turns out, does not take orders. It takes payment. And it sets its own exchange rate.

* * *

The dying man came to us on the fifth day.

His name was Corvel, and he had been a bell-founder in Vell before the cough took root in him, and by the time they carried him into the chapel on a door torn off its hinges he was past anything Tamsin's charms could touch. Tamsin tried anyway. I watched him kneel in the straw with his hands hovering over Corvel's chest, working little warmings and easements, and I watched the magic go through the man like water through a net. Hedge-craft mends what's broken. Corvel wasn't broken. He was ending.

"He's asking for last rites," said one of the clerks, awkwardly, because none of us were that kind of church anymore.

Corvel himself said, "I heard there was singing here." His voice was a ruin. "My mother used to sing. Before the Edict. I don't remember the songs, but I remember that there were songs." He looked at me—I suppose someone had told him what I was—and asked, "Could you sing me one? Even a wrong one?"

And there it is. That is how the monstrous things get done. Not through ambition, not through the Conclave's cold arithmetic, but through a dying man asking politely for a song his mother might have known.

Tamsin looked at me across the straw and I saw him do the math he didn't want to do: one verse could reach Corvel. One verse might knit what was ending. And one verse cost one memory, freely given, and hadn't we already lined ours up? Hadn't we rehearsed?

"It's not ready," Tamsin said. "Maren, tell him it's not ready."

"We've rehearsed for six days," I said.

"Six days of *fragments.*"

"Theo has been singing whole phrases in his sleep since the thirteenth turn began. He's more ready than any of us." I crouched beside Corvel and took his hand. It weighed nothing. "You understand there's a price?"

"I'm dying," Corvel said, with the terrible good humor of the nearly dead. "Name it."

"We don't get to name it," I said. "That's rather the point."

He considered this. Then he laughed, or coughed, or both, and said, "Take whatever you like. I haven't got much worth keeping anyway."

Everyone says that. Everyone believes it, right up until the taking starts.

* * *

We arranged ourselves in the crescent. Tamsin stood at the center with one hand on Corvel's forehead, refusing—absolutely refusing—to leave the circle, though I begged him to step back. Theo knelt opposite, and when he opened his mouth for the first note I felt the whole chapel lean in, walls and rafters and the salt-crusted saints in their broken niches, all of them listening.

Here is what I can tell you about the First Tongue, having studied it my whole life and sung it once and paid for it forever: it is not a language the way Vellish is a language. Words in Vellish mean things. Words in the First Tongue *do* things—they did them once, constantly, casually, the way we breathe, before the old kingdoms burned the Choir out of the world to end their war and then, in an act of breathtaking hypocrisy, called the burning peace.

Theo gave us the ground-tone. Low, patient, older than the stones under us. My descant came in over it—the descant my order died preserving, the twelve notes I had carried in my head through eleven mute years like smuggled jewels—and I will not pretend otherwise: for eight bars, it was the most beautiful thing I have ever been part of. The lantern flames stood straight as soldiers. The dust rose off the floor in slow gold spirals. Corvel stopped coughing.

Then the verse turned.

There's a hinge in the melody—I knew there would be; my order's last testament warns of it in language so careful it's basically useless, something about *the turning of the giving toward the giving's source*. I had read that line a hundred times and thought it meant the singer pays. It doesn't mean the singer pays. It means the song reaches out and finds payment wherever payment is loosest. Wherever a heart has been holding something open. Wherever love has left a door unlatched.

The verse went through the congregation like wind through wheat.

I felt mine go. There was no pain—that's the obscene part, it doesn't hurt, it feels like setting down something heavy after a long walk, it feels like relief—and suddenly I was standing in the middle of the descant trying to remember why my eyes were wet. Something had been taken out of me and the space where it had been was warm and clean and *lighter*, and I kept singing, because the song needed me to keep singing, and some distant part of me screamed while the rest of me smiled.

Beside me, Odile the hedge-witch faltered and looked around at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. Pellam the clerk was weeping without knowing why. And down the crescent, a young couple—we'll come to them—went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with the music.

But Corvel—

Corvel breathed. I heard it happen, the rattle going out of him between one bar and the next, the way frost leaves a window in the sun. By the final cadence he was sitting up on his door-bier demanding to know what everyone was staring at, and his voice was whole, and his lungs were whole, and Tamsin had both hands pressed flat against the straw because his legs had apparently decided to stop being reliable.

The verse worked.

That is the sentence I keep trying to make peace with, so let me write it again: the verse worked. A man who was dead by morning's honest arithmetic stood up and thanked us. The Choir healed him completely, effortlessly, the way you'd smooth a wrinkle from cloth.

And half the congregation forgot their own weddings.

* * *

It was the young couple, Tam and Rill, who'd married two years past in defiance of everything, a hedge-witch and a fisherman's daughter sneaking off to a salt marsh with a stolen ring. They had told us the story a dozen times during rehearsals, mostly to make each other laugh. When the song ended, Rill looked at Tam with the polite blankness you give a stranger on a ferry, and Tam looked back at her the same way, and for a long moment neither of them could account for the rings on their fingers except as odd jewelry.

Pellam figured it out first—he was a clerk, after all; pattern-reading was his trade. He counted the congregation, counted the wet eyes, counted the ones wearing expressions of mild, unaccountable loss, and said, very quietly, "It didn't take what we offered. It took what was closest to the flame. It took what we loved most recently, most openly, most—" he swallowed, "—most available."

One memory per soul touched by the verse. Not per singer. Per *listener*. The song had reached every heart in the chapel, and from each it had taken one thing, and it had chosen those things itself, and its criterion appeared to be simply this: whatever was held most tenderly. Whatever was least guarded. Love, it turns out, is the loosest coinage in any heart. The Choir spends it first.

Tamsin got it worst of anyone, though he wouldn't say so. He'd put everything into the circle—his counter-melody, his whole self, twelve years of hoarded discipline—and so the song went through him deepest, and I watched him stand in the aftermath touching his own memories like a man patting his pockets after a cutpurse passed. Checking. Finding gaps he couldn't name because the shape of the gap goes with the memory. He still knew he had run. He still knew why, roughly. But somewhere in him a room had gone dark, and he couldn't find the door to check what used to be inside it.

"I offered a mediocre holiday," Pellam said numbly. "A *holiday.*"

"The Choir doesn't take requests," I said.

"No," said Tamsin. His voice was very steady, which is how I knew. "It takes tithes. From everyone. Whether they joined the congregation or just walked past the window."

* * *

We sat among the ruins of it for a long time. Corvel ate bread—bread!—and kept thanking us, and every thanks landed like a stone. Tam and Rill sat at opposite ends of a pew, introducing themselves to each other, and I will tell you the thing I cannot forgive about that night: they liked each other. The song had taken the wedding and left the people, and the people, meeting fresh, found each other funny and kind, and by midnight they were laughing together, and it was almost sweeter than what they'd lost, and that sweetness is the trap. That is exactly what the Choir offers. Healing that arrives pre-forgiven. Miracles with the receipt already shredded.

I found Tamsin outside in the chapel yard, feeding sticks into a small fire because his hands needed occupation.

"You knew," I said. Not cruelly. I wanted to be wrong.

"I suspected." He fed the fire another stick. "My mistress's letter said the songs rewrite memory to fuel miracles. I thought—freely offered memory. Voluntary. Aimed." He laughed, short and bitter. "You can't aim it, can you."

"No."

"It's not a weapon. It's not even a tool. It's a—" He searched for the word.

"A fire," I said. "It burns whatever's nearest. You build the hearth as carefully as you like; it still eats the house if the house is what's closest."

"And the Conclave was right about the danger," Tamsin said quietly. "That's the worst of it. They were right. They just weren't right about *anything else*—not about the children, not about the killing, not about building a whole civilization on top of an amnesia and calling it peace. But the danger was real. The war that made the Choir ended because the Choir ate the reason for the war. Whole kingdoms forgot what they were fighting about mid-battle. And everyone who remembers anything of those days remembers only that the singing must never start again."

"So we stop," I said. "Thirteen singers, one rehearsal, one verse. We've proven it works. We also proven what it costs. Walk away now, scatter, hide Theo somewhere green, and the ledger stays small."

He was silent for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere behind us, inside the chapel, Corvel was snoring with the magnificent unconcern of the recently resurrected.

"You heard them in there," Tamsin finally said. "Half of Vell is one bad winter away from a purge. Ilsa Vane is coming with an army, Maren. She's coming for Theo, and she won't stop, and the Edict will grind on for another century, and children born with doors in them will keep dying conveniently of ordinary causes. Or—" he gestured at the chapel, at the warmth in it, at Tam and Rill laughing over shared bread, "—we finish the song, and hate forgets itself. On purpose. Chosen. Paid."

"At what price?" I said. "A city's hatred is a big fire, Tamsin. What's nearest to *that* flame?"

And he looked at me, and I watched him arrive at the answer I had already arrived at, the one sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone: whatever we love most. All of us. Whatever each heart in that choir holds most tenderly, the song will spend it, because love is the only currency it recognizes, and we are about to hand it a congregation full of people who came precisely because they love something enough to fight for it.

We would be, in other words, the richest congregation the Choir had ever eaten from.

"The plan is monstrous," I said. "At any scale. One man healed, fifty weddings forgotten. A city saved, a hundred lives' worth of tenderness burned. There is no size at which this stops being monstrous. There's only the question of whether the monster gets fed on purpose or by accident."

Tamsin stared into the fire. "Then we'd better decide," he said, "before Ilsa Vane decides for us."

Which is when the sentry came running down from the ridge with a lantern swinging wild, shouting that there were fires on the far road—too many, too orderly to be accidents—and that the Conclave had found us.

* * *

I want to record, before whatever comes next takes its toll, the exact sound the chapel made that night when we understood the army was coming. It was not fear. It was thirteen people looking at each other and realizing, all at once, that the decision we'd been circling for hours had just been taken out of our hands—or rather, handed back to us with a deadline attached.

Somewhere on the dark road, torches. Somewhere in my chest, a warm clean gap where my teacher's name used to live, and below it, deeper down, the descant, waiting. Somewhere beside me, a boy who was never asked to be any of this stood in the chapel doorway with firelight on his face, and said, in the First Tongue, the words I have since learned translate roughly as:

*I can pay for all of it. I know what I am.*

God help us. He meant it as comfort.
