## 19. Siege of the Salt Chapel

I have stolen many things in my life. A sweet off a market stall when I was six. A kiss from a boy named Daven who never asked for it back. Twelve years of my sister's laughter, which I traded to the Conclave one coin at a time until I couldn't remember what it sounded like.

But I had never stolen from the Conclave itself until tonight.

The memory-engine core sat in my satchel like a sleeping animal—warm, faintly humming, heavier than brass had any right to be. Archivist Pelloran would notice its absence by dawn. By then, I intended to be either forgiven or dead, and I had already crossed forgiveness off the list of realistic outcomes.

The Salt Chapel stood on the headland above the drowned quarter, its spire snapped by some old storm and never mended, its walls furred with white mineral bloom that gave the place its name. From the ridge path I could see candlelight in the broken windows, and figures moving inside—dozens of them. Hidden practitioners, defected clerks, a handful of soldiers who had looked at their orders and decided their orders were wrong. All of them gathered around a mute boy with a voice like the end of the world.

And Tamsin Sandoval. My friend. The word still fit in my mouth, though I hadn't used it aloud in twelve years. *Friend.* I rolled it around like a stone I'd kept under my tongue so long I'd forgotten whether it was bitter or sweet.

Below me, on the coast road, the Conclave army came up out of the dark with torches banked low, four hundred strong, and at their head rode Prelate Corvane's banner: a closed eye stitched in gold thread. *We see nothing,* the eye said, in the language of institutions everywhere. *Nothing is happening. Nothing has ever happened.*

I checked the core's housing one last time. Then I walked down into the light of the chapel and knocked on a door that had no lock, because chapels are optimistic that way.

* * *

"You're joking," said Maren.

"I've been told my sense of humor is an acquired taste."

"She's *joking*," Maren announced to the room, as though invoking witnesses might make it less true. "The chief inquisitor of Vell turns up at midnight carrying stolen Conclave property, and she's making *jokes*."

Tamsin stood near the altar with Theo half-hidden behind his coat, and he was looking at me the way you look at weather you've been dreading for weeks—the thing finally arrived, and now all that remains is to stand in it. He hadn't run. I'd half expected him to run. There was time; the army wouldn't reach the ridge before matins.

"Say your piece," he said quietly.

So I said it. Four hundred soldiers. Two companies of wardens with null-halberds. Corvane himself riding at the rear because Prelates prefer to watch their miracles from a safe distance. Arrival before first light. And no negotiation, because Corvane had stopped negotiating the day the archive showed him the boy could sing, and men who build their power on silence do not tolerate choirs.

"And this," Maren said, jabbing a finger at my satchel, "is supposed to help how?"

"It's the memory-engine." I set it on the altar between a candlestick and a chipped statue of some saint whose name the salt had eaten. "The machine the Conclave used after the war. The one that took the war out of everyone's heads."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when something enormous has been said carelessly. I watched faces: the hedge-witch with her hands pressed over her mouth, the two runaway clerks exchanging a look, old Ferro the tinker going pale around his jaw. They didn't know. Most of them had never known. That was rather the point of the engine.

"The archive taught me what it does," I went on, before courage could reconsider. "It doesn't destroy memories. It *edits* them. It takes a pattern—a hatred, a grief, a whole war—and it rewrites the remembering. You can aim it. Narrowly, if you're careful. Broadly if you're willing to pay."

"Pay with what?" Tamsin asked.

"Memory," said Maren, in a voice gone flat and old. She had turned away from us both, toward the broken window and the dark beyond it. "That's always the payment. It eats the operator first. Everyone who ever worked the engines ended up—" she stopped. Started again. "There were women in my order who tended them. Sweet people. By the end they couldn't tell you their mothers' names, but they could recite the operating liturgy perfectly. It takes what you use most."

"So whoever runs it loses whatever they feed it," I said. "Yes."

"And what are you planning to feed it, Inquisitor?" Maren turned back, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Because I can see the shape of it behind your face and I want to hear you say it out loud."

I made myself say it. "Corvane's order is built on certainty. Every man down there believes three things absolutely: that magic murdered their families, that the Conclave saved them, and that I am loyal. If the core takes those certainties—" I shrugged, and hated how casual the gesture felt against what it cost. "Four hundred soldiers will wake up on that road tomorrow not knowing why they marched. Not knowing who told them to. Some of them won't know their own ranks. It buys days, maybe a week."

"And you?" Tamsin said. Just that. Just *and you*, in the voice of a man who had spent twelve years learning exactly what forgetting costs, and who could price my purchase to the copper.

"I'll be standing next to it," I said. "It takes the operator first."

* * *

They argued, of course. Good people always argue at the edge of a cliff; it's how you know they're good. Maren wanted to send the core into the sea. Tamsin wanted to negotiate, which was touching and insane in equal measure. The hedge-witch asked whether we couldn't just move the chapel, as though sanctity came with wheels.

Theo settled it.

He did it without speaking, which for Theo was still the most fluent way. While the adults arranged their objections in rows, he walked to the altar, put both hands flat on the memory-engine's warm brass shell, and looked up at me. His eyes were gray, ordinary, a child's eyes—and they held me with the terrible patience of someone who has heard every argument about himself and found them all beside the point.

"They'll kill him," I said to those eyes. "You understand that. Corvane won't risk another verse."

Theo shook his head slowly. Then he pointed—at me, at the engine, at the window where the torchlight of the army was just beginning to prickle the horizon—and made a small motion, gathering, gathering, like someone folding a sheet. *Take it all. Take everything.*

"No," I said.

He pointed at the congregation. At Maren. At Tamsin. Then back at me, and tilted his head, and the question needed no First Tongue at all: *Which of us is worth more?*

"None of you is a *price*," I snapped, and immediately heard Prelate Corvane in my own mouth, and shut my eyes. When I opened them, Theo was smiling slightly, the way children smile when an adult has accidentally proven their point.

"He gets that from somewhere," Maren murmured. "That look. I've seen it before, on singers about to take the descant. It isn't bravery. It's arithmetic."

"Well," I said, picking up the satchel, "I was always better at arithmetic than any of you. Show me where the ridge narrows. If I'm going to unmake myself, I'd like to do it with a tactical advantage."

* * *

The ridge path climbed from the chapel through gorse and old sheep-walls, and at its narrowest point two rock shelves pinched together with a drop on either side—the kind of ground where four hundred men become a column, and a column becomes a queue, and a queue becomes a gift. I set the engine on a flat stone with the sea booming below, and worked by feel and by the liturgy Maren had taught me in three frantic hours: *open the aperture, name the pattern, hold the pattern, spend.*

Naming the pattern was the part that frightened me, because the engine reads intention, and intentions are honest even when mouths aren't. I had to mean it. Not the vague heroic meaning-it of stories, but the cold, specific, line-by-line meaning-it of an inquisitor composing a charge sheet.

*I take the certainty that magic killed my family.*

My hands shook. I let them.

*I take the certainty that the Conclave saved me.*

The aperture irised open with a sound like a held breath released, and light the color of deep water pooled on the stone.

*I take the certainty that Ilsa Vane is loyal.*

And there it was, underneath, the pattern I had not planned to name and which named itself anyway, rising through the engine like a body through floodwater:

*I take the years I spent with Tamsin Sandoval. The lessons in the archive stacks. The bread we burned learning to cook. The night his mistress died and he cried into my shoulder and swore he'd never touch magic again, and I believed him, and I let him go, and I have missed him every single day since and called it duty.*

"Oh," I said aloud, to nobody, to the sea. "*Oh.* So that's what I've been carrying."

The engine waited, patient as salt.

I thought about stopping. I want that recorded somewhere, wherever the record goes when there's no one left to keep it: I thought about stopping, and I understood exactly what I was about to lose, and I chose it anyway with my eyes open, which is the only kind of choosing that counts. Somewhere below me Tamsin was teaching a child to sing. Somewhere behind me an army was coming to erase them. Between those two facts stood a woman holding a machine that ate love to fuel miracles, and the miracle she wanted was hours, just hours, enough for a verse to finish.

"Then take it all," I said, using Theo's own grammar. "Take everything."

The world went white and tasted of copper and my mother's kitchen, and then it tasted of nothing at all.

* * *

I came back to myself kneeling on wet stone with a dead machine in front of me and a satchel full of cooling brass, and I did not know my own name.

I knew things—strange, orphaned things. I knew how a halberd lock works. I knew that gorse smells of coconut when the sun hits it. I knew that somewhere below me there was a chapel full of people I was fighting for, though the knowing had no face attached, no name, no warmth—just a direction, like a compass needle pointing at a country I'd never seen. And I knew, with the last coherent thought the engine left me, that the army coming up the road would round the bend in minutes, and that I had chosen, in some former life whose details were gone, to stand here and meet them.

A woman's handwriting filled the margins of my mind in letters I could almost read. *Hold the ridge. Buy the hours. You loved them. Trust the shape of that, even without the feeling.*

So I did the only sensible thing available to a person made entirely of training and no history.

I stood up, drew my warden's blade, planted myself in the narrowest pinch of the path, and began, in a clear parade-ground voice that belonged to somebody else, to lie magnificently.

"Wardens! Halt!" Four hundred torches stuttered in the dark below. "The chapel is already taken! The resonance collapsed an hour ago—Corvane knows, he sent me ahead—the operation is concluded, go home!"

It should not have worked. Armies are not persuaded by one voice on a rock. But the engine had done its work below as well as here: the front rank had been drinking from certainty all their lives, and I had just drunk the well dry, and uncertainty is contagious in the way panic is contagious, in the way doubt spreads through a column faster than any order. I saw men looking at each other. I saw a sergeant frown at his own standard as though the closed golden eye had said something unexpected.

"Prelate's orders say—" someone started.

"The Prelate," I called down, inventing fluently, borrowing a stranger's authority, "ordered me to confirm the site is clean and release the second company. Are you the second company? Does anyone actually know?"

And that—that small, poisonous, wonderful question—did more damage than my blade ever would. Four hundred men discovering simultaneously that they could not quite remember why they were climbing a hill at midnight to burn a chapel. I heard it ripple backward, mutter by mutter, like wind through wheat.

Then Corvane's personal guard pushed through, and the muttering stopped, and I learned that prelates travel with men whose certainty is installed too deep for any engine to reach. Six of them, faceless in visored helms, and they did not halt, and they did not wonder, and they came up the path at me with the patient inevitability of tide.

I fought them on a shelf of rock above the sea, in the grey hour before dawn, and I fought better than I had any right to, because my hands remembered twelve years of drills even if nothing else of mine survived. I took two of them off the path and into the dark. A third opened my arm from elbow to wrist, and I remember being surprised at how little it hurt, and how much it bled, and thinking with odd academic detachment: *so that's what I'm made of. Red. Ordinary red.*

The fourth man's halberd caught me across the temple, and the rocks came up very fast, and the last thing I saw before the dark took me properly was the east going pale, and below it, thin and impossibly far away, the sound of singing beginning in a broken chapel.

I didn't know the song. I didn't know the singers. I didn't know why the sound made me weep, there on my back in the gorse with my blood running toward the sea.

But I knew—I knew the way you know a direction, the way you know home without an address—that whatever happened next, I had bought it. Someone had bought it for them. Hours, purchased in full, paid by a woman whose name was written nowhere now except in the margins of a book she'd never read.

Hold the ridge, the invisible hand had said. You loved them. Trust the shape of that.

I held it. I trusted it.

And down in the Salt Chapel, the thirteenth verse rose to meet the dawn.
