## 20. Theo Chooses

I have learned to count the way the Choir counts.

Not one-two-three the way Tamsin taught me over bread and honey in a chapel that smelled of salt and old grief. Not even the way Maren counts music, which is more like breathing than arithmetic. The Choir counts *cost*. Every word I speak has a price written on its underside. Every verse we rehearse spends something — a face, a wedding, a name someone's mother gave them at the edge of a birthing pool. I did not know this when I first opened my mouth on Tamsin's doorstep and made his wards flare like frightened birds. I know it now. Knowing is the thirteenth turn's cruelest gift.

I am sitting on the cold stone steps of the Salt Chapel with my hands flat against the floor, listening to the building remember being a cliff.

That is what my new language does, you understand. It does not merely name things. It *asks* them what they were, and they answer, shyly, the way old people answer questions about their youth. The floor was once part of a headland over a grey sea. The sea is gone now — or rather, the sea moved, the way seas do when no one sings to them — but the stone remembers salt. Everything remembers salt. That is why they call it the Salt Chapel, though none of the others know that. They think it is a joke about tears. It is not. It is geology.

Maren calls my fluency a miracle. Tamsin calls it an alarm bell. Both of them are right, which is the trouble with wise people: they keep agreeing from opposite ends of the same rope.

* * *

Three nights ago, I dreamed the final verse entire.

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only coin I have ever had, even back when I had no other words. I did not stumble into the knowledge. I did not overhear Maren and Tamsin arguing by candlelight, though they do argue, often, in the low urgent voices of people who think a sleeping child cannot hear. No. The song came to me the way rain comes to a roof. It found the highest point of me and ran down all sides.

Here is what the dream said, and I will render it as plainly as my tongue allows:

The Choir can be finished in two ways. Thirteen voices, singing together, each verse taking one memory from every listener — a slow bleed, a fair tax, a city waking lighter each morning until the hatred of magic has been paid off in small forgettings, the way a debt is paid in installments. That is Maren's way. That is the way we have been rehearsing, and it works; we have seen it work. Old Sef, our dying tenor, sang himself whole while half our congregation lost their weddings and woke confused about why they were holding hands with strangers. It works. It is monstrous. We do it anyway because the alternative is the Conclave's army at the door.

But there is a second way. The dream showed me that too.

One voice. Willing. Entire. A single singer who steps into the circle and offers not one memory but the *self* — every year, every name, every scar and kindness and grudge, the whole continuous thread of a person — spent all at once, so completely that the song needs nothing from anyone else. The final verse would take everything that singer is and give back everything the singer asks. And what I would ask — what the song was shaped, before I could shape anything else, to ask — is simple:

*Let the city forget its hatred. Let the boy go free. Let him be ordinary.*

The dream was very specific about that last part. Almost tender about it. As though whoever built the Choir into me, all those years ago, scattered across eleven dead children and one living one, had anticipated exactly this moment and left instructions. *When he understands, he will choose correctly.* I resent that. I want it noted somewhere, in some archive that survives what is coming, that I resent being designed for sacrifice. It is a terrible thing to be a well-made tool. It makes obedience feel like virtue, and I have only recently learned enough words to tell the difference.

So. I knew the price. I knew the payment. And I made my decision the way I make all my decisions now: quickly, quietly, and with a great deal of pretending otherwise.

* * *

The morning of the last rehearsal, I got up early.

Tamsin sleeps badly these days — he sleeps the way a man sleeps on a ship, one hand always on the rail — so I was careful. I took the chalk from his satchel and drew the circle myself on the chapel floor, thirteen points, and I stood in the center of it while the light came through the broken roof in long grey bars, and I practiced.

I practiced the opening phrase of the final verse until my throat ached. It is a strange phrase. In my new language it means roughly *I consent to be spent*, but sung properly, at the right pitch, with the overtones stacked the way Maren taught us, it does not mean — it *does*. The stones under my feet went soft as bread dough. Dust rose off the pews and hung in the air like held breath. Somewhere outside, a gull cried out and forgot, for three full seconds, how to be a gull, and sat on the chapel wall looking baffled at its own wings.

I stopped. My heart was going like a drummer who has been told the war starts today.

"You shouldn't do that alone."

Tamsin stood in the doorway with two cups of barley tea and the particular expression he wears when he is frightened and hiding it behind mildness, which is most expressions, if you watch him long enough. Twelve years of practice, Maren says. Twelve years of being a man-shaped locked box walking around a lawful city selling luck that was nothing at all.

"It's just practice," I said.

"Mm." He set the cups down on a pew and came toward the circle, and I watched him stop at its edge the way you'd stop at the edge of a frozen pond. "Theo."

"I'm fine, Tamsin."

"I didn't ask." He crouched, so his eyes were level with mine, which is a thing he does that I both love and hate, because it makes lying to him feel like lying to a mirror. "You drew the circle yourself. Thirteen points, correct orientation, the descent-marked axis. You've been studying the notation I keep locked in the chest under my bed. The one I *thought* I kept locked."

"The lock remembers me," I said. "Everything remembers me. It's very annoying for everyone."

"Yes," he said softly. "It is."

And there it was. The conversation we had been not-having for weeks, standing up between us like a third person. I could see him assembling it — the question, the fear, the argument he had clearly already lost in his head a hundred times — and I decided, with the cold clarity that my language lends me whether I want it or not, that I would not let him finish building it.

"Sing with me today?" I asked. "Just the harmonies. Maren says my descant drifts sharp when I'm tired."

It was a lie. My descant has never drifted sharp in my life. But Tamsin's whole face did the thing where relief and suspicion wrestle briefly and relief wins on points, and he said, "Of course," and picked up his tea, and I drank mine, and we were, for one more hour, just a hedge-witch and his foundling, warming up in a ruin.

I hold onto that hour. I am told the dead don't get to keep things. I intend to be the exception.

* * *

They came for us at dusk. Of course they did. Armies have no imagination; they always arrive at dusk, or dawn, or during the harvest festival. Ours arrived at dusk, with torches and memory-engine wagons and a woman named Ilsa Vane riding ahead of them on a horse she had, by all reports, stolen from her own order.

But that part belongs to other chapters, and I am telling you about the circle.

Because here is what happened. The siege began. Ilsa turned the Conclave's own engine against them and bought us hours — hours paid for, I learned later, out of her own past, which is a price I think about more than any other, except one. The congregation gathered. Thirteen voices, or nearly — we had eleven singers and Maren conducting and Tamsin holding the bass line like a man holding a door shut against a flood. And Maren raised her ruined voice — the Edict took her singing years ago, but the Choir gives back what the Edict stole, did you know that? She sounded like weather — and we began.

And I stepped into the center of the circle alone.

The song faltered. Songs do that when their meaning changes underneath them. Maren saw immediately — she has eyes like a hawk that reads sheet music — and her hand came up, and the harmony bent around my silence, and I began the opening phrase.

*I consent to be spent.*

I sang it true. I sang it the way the dream taught me, the way the builders wrote it into my bones before I had a name, and the circle lit up around me like a ring of dawn, and I felt the verse reach out for me — felt it take hold of the first year, the year of the doorstep, the rain and the wards flaring and the tall tired man kneeling in the wet saying *well then, come inside* —

— and another voice entered the song.

You have to understand what that is, in the First Tongue. Two voices entering a spending-verse is not harmony. It is *bidding*. The verse takes the greatest offer on the table, and it does not care whose offer it is. And the voice that came in beneath mine was low, and cracked, and utterly certain, and it was singing the counter-melody — the one from the letter, the one his mistress hid in the margins of his oldest spellbook, the melody she composed, I now understand, for exactly this contingency, because she helped bury the Choir and she spent her whole remaining life leaving escape hatches in the grave.

*I bid higher,* Tamsin sang. *Take mine instead. Take all of mine.*

"No!" I broke the phrase apart, which hurts, it hurts like tearing cloth along the grain of your own skin. "No — it doesn't work like that —"

"It works however the song decides," Maren called, and there were tears running down her face and her hands never stopped moving. "And the song decides by *value*, boy. It always did. That's the whole horror of it. That's why the old kingdoms burned it out. It doesn't weigh years, it weighs —"

She stopped. Because we all felt it then, the whole congregation, every one of us, even Sef with his healed lungs, even the clerks who had defected that afternoon with their ledgers still inked on their fingers.

We felt the scale tipping.

Twelve years of hiding. Twelve years of a man folding himself smaller and smaller, day after day, selling luck that was nothing, flinching from inspectors, burying his gift under a shop counter in a city that hated what he was — twelve years of *deliberate self-erasure*, performed willingly, sustained by love of a craft he couldn't practice and a god he couldn't name. Set that on one side of the scale. On the other side: one child, six years old in body and older than empires in marrow, offering himself because he was *built* to.

The scale tipped toward Tamsin. The song reached for him.

"Fight it," he told me, mid-verse, calm as Sunday, while the light took him at the edges the way fire takes paper. His voice was steady. His eyes were shining wet and absolutely unafraid. "You're fluent now, Theo. Argue with it. You're the best arguer I know."

"That's not — you can't *outbid* me, that's not how —"

"I've been outbidding people since before you were born," he said. "Ask anyone in the low quarter about my prices. Terrible business model. Never once let a child pay."

And I understood — too late, exactly late enough, the way everything in my life arrives — what he had done. He hadn't stumbled into the circle. He had been waiting at its edge since the first rehearsal, since the letter, since maybe the day I arrived on his doorstep in the rain. He had read his mistress's warning. He knew the second way existed. And he had decided, somewhere in those twelve hidden years, that if the choice ever came between his past and my future, the auction was already won.

The counter-melody rose. The circle blazed white. And the last thing I heard him say, before the song took the sound of his voice and the shape of his name and every reason he ever had for running — the last thing, I swear it on the salt in these stones — was not to me.

It was upward, or inward, or wherever such men aim such words:

*"Keep him ordinary. Keep him kind. And let him remember that he was loved — that part's not for sale."*

Then the verse closed over him like water, and the song went up over Vell like a second moon, and I stood in the center of a dying circle screaming a word I had only learned that week, a word with no magic in it at all, a word that is just a mouth making the smallest possible shape:

*Tamsin.*

The city below us sighed. Somewhere in the low quarter, a Conclave soldier lowered his torch and could not recall why he had lit it. Somewhere in the vaults, a register of crimes burned itself blank. The hatred lifted off Vell like fog off a morning field, paid for, paid in full, by a man who had hidden for twelve years and was now hidden forever.

And I stood there, holding the last fading note, feeling my own mind stay exactly, stubbornly, gloriously intact — every memory, every name, every hour of the chapel and the market and the rooftop flight — because he had paid for all of it. He had bought my remembering with his own forgetting, the miser's inverse, the one bargain no one warns you about:

Love does not cost what you expect. Love costs *everything except you*.

Maren put her hand on my shoulder. Around us the congregation was waking, weeping, laughing, whole. Far below, through the smoke, I could see the city gates standing open, and beyond them, somewhere green, a road going out into a world that no longer remembered what to hate.

"He'll walk out of here tomorrow," Maren said quietly, "and he won't know your name. He won't know mine. He won't know why his feet ache or why he owns a charm-shop or why he cries at certain chords." Her grip tightened. "But the song keeps one thing, Theo. One thing per singer, always, tucked where no ledger reaches. The builders called it the *keepsake note*. What do you suppose a man like Tamsin would keep?"

I looked down at the empty circle, at the chalk lines already fading, already becoming just chalk.

And far away, at the edge of hearing — or maybe not far away at all, maybe right behind my ribs, maybe somewhere in the stones that remember salt — I heard a single note begin. Low. Cracked. Utterly certain.

Humming the tune I used to fall asleep to, back when I had no other words.
