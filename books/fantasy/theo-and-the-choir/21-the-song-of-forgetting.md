## 21. The Song of Forgetting

There is a particular kind of silence that comes just before a choir breathes in. I had never heard it before that night, because I had spent twelve years making sure I'd never be near one. It is not the absence of sound. It is the presence of everything about to happen, held in the lungs of thirteen people who have agreed to spend themselves.

The Salt Chapel was full. That's the part I still find absurd, when I can find anything at all. A ruined chapel with a leaking roof and a floor of salt-crusted stone, packed with hedge-witches and deserters and two repentant Conclave clerks who kept flinching at their own shadows, and Maren at the center with her ruined throat and her impossible patience, and Theo beside me, holding my hand like it was the only word he knew.

"You don't have to do this," I told him. For the hundredth time. He was getting tired of it; he'd learned exasperation early, along with *bread* and *rain* and *run*.

He looked up at me and said, in the First Tongue, the word for *yes*. The true yes. The kind that rearranges things.

I want to be honest in whatever hours I have left for honesty: I almost lied to him. I almost said *I'll go first, wait here*, which was the plan, technically, except plans are just lies you tell yourself with better posture. He knew. Of course he knew. He was built out of the same language that made the world, and children can smell a bad lie the way dogs smell weather.

So instead I said, "When it starts, don't let go."

And he didn't.

* * *

Maren raised her hands, and the chapel breathed in.

I had expected—what? Thunder. Light. Some theatrical announcement that magic had returned to Vell. What I got instead was a hum, low and ordinary, like a kettle deciding whether to commit. Then the second voice joined, then the third, and by the fifth I understood why the old kingdoms had burned this thing out of existence rather than learn to live with it. It wasn't power. Power you can hoard, ration, tax, forbid. This was *gravity*. It pulled everything toward itself, including the past.

The descant opened above us—Ilsa's contribution, though she didn't sing; she stood at the broken doorway with the memory-engine core cradled against her chest like an infant she'd stolen fair and square, and her face said she already knew what it was going to cost her—and the harmonies began to braid.

Here is what nobody tells you about the Choir: it doesn't take memories like a thief takes silver. It takes them like a river takes a bridge. Slowly at first, so you can watch. Then all at once, so you can't.

The first verse went out over Vell.

* * *

I felt the payment begin in my own head, because I was first singer, because my mistress had taught me the counter-melody twenty years ago without telling me what it was for, because apparently every woman who ever taught me anything had been keeping a ledger I couldn't see.

Twelve years. That was my offering. Twelve years of hiding, of selling luck that was nothing at all, of warding my door against inspectors and flinching at moonlight, of learning to laugh at jokes I didn't find funny so no one would look too closely. Twelve years, weighed out verse by verse, and the song drank them like a thirsty man drinks rain.

I watched them go.

That's the part I need someone to know, whoever ends up reading this or remembering me or not: you can watch yourself become a stranger. You feel it happening and there is nothing—*nothing*—you can do about it, because the whole point is that you've already agreed, and the Choir does not accept refunds.

The first year went. The year I arrived in Vell with blistered feet and a forged name and a hatred of cities so pure it was practically religious. Gone, like a candle pinched between wet fingers. I could feel the shape of where it had been—a smooth hollow, warm, almost comfortable.

Then the second. The third. The shop. Gods, the shop—I felt myself forgetting how much the shop had cost me, and then forgetting that it had cost me anything, and then—for one vertiginous moment—I forgot there had ever been a shop, and looked around the Salt Chapel with a stranger's eyes and thought, *why am I here?*

Theo's hand tightened on mine.

Right. Him. I was here for him.

The years kept going. Somewhere in the middle of the seventh or eighth, I lost the face of my mistress—not dead, not yet, but dying in some room I would now never remember finding—and I grieved her twice, once with the memory and once without it, and both griefs were real, and neither one was mine anymore.

* * *

Outside, Vell was changing.

You could hear it through the broken windows. Not screaming—that's what I'd braced for, the city convulsing as its convictions were pried loose—but something worse and gentler. A great collective intake of breath. Thousands of people waking from a dream they hadn't known they were having and sitting up in bed wondering why they'd ever been afraid of the dark.

Because that's what the Edict had been. Ilsa had found it in the archive, brought it to us like a confession she could barely carry: the war hadn't ended because the kingdoms grew wise. It ended because someone had reached into every mind in the world and taken the reasons out. The hatred of magic in Vell wasn't a conviction. It was a scar, and nobody remembered the wound.

The song was unmaking the scar.

From the rooftops—I saw it later, or someone told me, I genuinely don't know which—people came out of their houses into the rainless night and stood looking at the sky over the salt quarter, and some of them wept without knowing why, and some of them laughed the way you laugh when a headache lifts, sudden and embarrassing and holy. An old woman walked to the door of a neighbor she'd reported to the Conclave eleven years ago and knocked until he opened it, and then stood there with her mouth open, unable to produce either an apology or an explanation.

The hatred dissolved. And every drop of it was paid for out of somebody's life, and most of that somebody was me.

* * *

By the ninth verse I had become an interesting philosophical problem.

I knew facts. Facts are stubborn; they go last. I knew the boy beside me was named Theo and that I loved him—but the knowing had gone strange and thin, like a coat borrowed from a larger man. I knew I was singing a counter-melody, and my mouth kept producing it, note after note, faithful as a clock, while the man who had learned that melody receded behind glass.

Do you understand? I watched him go. I watched Tamsin Sandoval—hedge-witch's apprentice, liar, coward, the man who ran for twelve years and called it living—walk backward out of my own skull, waving, becoming anecdote, becoming rumor, becoming a shuttered shop with no explanation.

And here is the wretched, transcendent joke of it: I was *relieved* for him. Whoever I was becoming, she—he—it—looked at that frightened man walking out of the world and thought, *good riddance, he must have been so tired.*

The tenth verse took my name.

Not the forged one—the forged name went early, in the second verse, a courtesy. My real name. The one my mother gave me, the one my mistress shortened when she was pleased with me. I felt it lift out like a splinter coming clean, and the song poured something else into the hole, something warm and anonymous, and I sang on without knowing whose voice it was, only that it was mine tonight and that was enough.

Theo was crying. I registered that the way you register weather. A small person nearby was sad, and his hand was in mine, and somewhere very far away a version of me would have torn the world open rather than let him cry, but that version was four verses gone, and I was what remained: a singer with a job.

Except.

Except that the counter-melody has a structure, and structure survives even when meaning doesn't, and buried in the eleventh verse my mouth did something the score didn't ask for. It bent the melody down, the way my mistress used to bend it when she was being tender, and it turned toward the small crying person, and it sang—not words, the counter-melody has no words, that was always its genius—but the *shape* of words, the shape of *don't let go*, and Theo stopped crying, and laughed through it, and held on.

Whatever I was by then, I was still, for one more bar, his.

* * *

The twelfth verse closed over the city like a hand closing over a coal.

I felt the last of the twelve years drain—not painfully; that's the lie everyone tells about sacrifice, that it hurts. It doesn't hurt. That's the horror. It feels like being set down after carrying something so long you'd forgotten your arms were heavy. The fear left. The running left. The flinch left. I stood in the Salt Chapel as the final notes rose, and I was light as paper, and empty as a bell, and I could not for the life of me remember why I had ever wanted to keep anything.

The last note went out.

Vell, below us, exhaled.

* * *

Silence again. The other kind this time—the ordinary kind, the kind that just means it's over.

Thirteen singers stood swaying in a ruined chapel, most of them missing smaller things than I'd lost—a wedding, a grudge, a childhood afternoon—but missing them, feeling around the holes the way tongues find new gaps in the gums. Old Wenna from the market looked down at her hands and said, "I had a sister," and then, after a moment, "Didn't I?" and Maren put her arm around her and didn't answer, because Maren had given her own voice's history to the song and couldn't have answered in the old tongue if she'd tried.

Theo tugged my sleeve.

I looked down at him. Small, dark-eyed, ordinary—already becoming ordinary, the Choir's last gift settling over him like snow, the First Tongue draining out of him syllable by syllable, leaving just a boy. A boy who would grow up in a city that no longer feared what he'd been, and would never know he'd been it.

"Who are you?" he asked me. In plain Vellish. His first plain words.

And I opened my mouth to answer, and found—nothing. No name rising. No story. Just a pleasant blankness where twelve years used to live, and beyond it, faintly, the sense of a road leading out of a gate toward somewhere green.

"I don't know," I said, and smiled, because it was true, and truth is restful when you've got nothing left to protect. "But I think I'm going for a walk."

He considered this with the gravity of a child and the gravity of whatever he had briefly been.

"Can I come?"

Something moved in the hollow where my heart's history used to be. Not a memory. Memories were spent. But a shape where one had been, the way a pressed flower leaves a stain in a book—no scent, no season, just the proof that something beautiful had once been flat against that page.

"I think," I heard myself say, "that you already have."

Behind us, at the chapel door, a woman with grey eyes and a stolen engine in her arms watched us go with an expression I couldn't read, though it seemed to contain, among other things, grief, relief, and the specific frustration of a person trying to mourn someone standing right in front of her.

She opened her mouth as if to call out—to say my name, maybe, or hers, or the whole story—and then closed it again.

Some payments, she seemed to decide, you don't try to refund.

The gate was open. The road was green. I took the boy's hand, because it seemed like the obvious thing to do with a hand, and we walked out of Vell into a morning that belonged to no one, least of all me—

—though at the gate I stopped, just once, and looked back at the city, and felt a stain in the book of myself press flat and bloom:

a shop sign, swinging, with no keeper's name on it at all.
