## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The trick to selling outlawed charms in Corvane was to look bored. Boredom, I'd learned, was the one disguise the witch-finders never thought to check. They expected smugglers to be nervous, priests to be weepy, heretics to burn with a certain evangelical gleam. Nobody ever arrested a man who looked like he'd rather be anywhere else and knew exactly how little his life was worth.

So I yawned at my stall in the Fishmarket while the Crown's grey cloaks walked their morning rounds, and I sold small miracles out of a crate of dried herring.

Not miracles, of course. That word got you hanged. *Charms*, I called them, or *keepsakes*, or — when a customer was particularly stupid — *souvenirs of better times*. A knotted thread that kept fever out of a child's sleep. A river-stone painted with a door that no longer existed anywhere but on the stone. A pinch of ash from my own hearth, blessed by nobody, guaranteed by me, which was worth more than any god's guarantee these days.

Because the gods were silent. Twelve years silent, all of them, every name in every pantheon from the Salt Mother down to Vael, and the Crown had built its whole occupation on that silence like a house on a grave.

"Which one keeps the nightmares off?" The voice came low, under the fishmonger's shouting. A woman in a dyer's apron, hands stained blue to the wrist, eyes doing the careful slide-around that meant she'd checked for grey cloaks twice before approaching.

"The knot," I said. "Tie it to the bedframe. If the nightmares come anyway, untie it and retie it left-handed. Works about as well as prayer used to."

She flinched at the word *prayer* the way people in Corvane flinched at loud noises. "You shouldn't say that word."

"You're right. Terrible habit." I passed her the thread and took her copper. "Say it was a souvenir."

"It's always a souvenir." She almost smiled, then didn't, and vanished into the crowd.

That was the shape of my days. Six years since I'd come to Corvane with a scarred face, a false name, and a brand on my forearm that I kept wrapped even in summer, because a faded oath-mark to Vael the Unblinking was worth more than my head to the witch-finders. Not because they feared the god. Because they feared what the brand proved: that someone had once been important enough for a god to notice.

I sold charms. I kept my head down. I told myself, most mornings, that I believed none of it anymore — not the gods, not the oaths, not the debt I'd walked away from when Vael went quiet and half his sworn swords simply sat down wherever they were standing and waited to die. I hadn't sat down. I'd stood up, wiped my mouth, and gotten on with the ugly business of living, which is the one sin the old rites never bothered to forbid.

I was very good at believing I believed nothing.

* * *

The child came at midday, when the market was thickest and the grey cloaks had retired to the tavern on Tanner's Row where Captain Hale bought his informants lunch and their silences dinner.

He couldn't have been more than seven, a fisherman's boy by the salt-cracks on his knuckles, and he stood at my stall with the terrible directness of children and drunks. "Mam says you sell things that work."

"Mam should mind her own business."

"She's dying." He said it flatly, the way he might have said *it's raining*. "Coughing blood. The physicker wants four marks and we've got six coppers."

There are rules to this trade. Rule one: never sell to anyone desperate enough to pray over it. Desperate people talk, and talk reaches grey cloaks, and grey cloaks reach for matches. Rule two: never sell to a child, because children don't understand souvenirs, they understand wishes.

I broke both rules before I'd finished looking at his face.

"Here." I pressed a river-stone into his palm — the painted door, my best piece, done in real lapis ground with oil. It wasn't meant for healing. It was meant for crossing over, for safe passage, an old soldier's charm for men who didn't expect to come home. Close enough. Everything's close enough if you squint. "Put it under her pillow. Tell her it's a souvenir of a place she's been. Don't tell her anything else. Don't tell anyone anything else."

"What does it do?"

"It does what your mam needs," I said, and immediately wanted to bite my tongue off, because that was the kind of sentence that sounded like a promise, and promises were the currency of gods, not charm-sellers.

He gave me all six coppers. I let him. There are limits to charity in a city that charges you for breathing its air, and I had rent paid to a landlord whose other tenants included two informants.

He ran off toward the harbor, clutching the stone against his chest like a second heart, and I watched him go and felt something I hadn't permitted myself in twelve years, which was the faint, foolish warmth of having maybe helped somebody.

Then the bell in the Crown garrison tower rang noon, and the warmth curdled into the usual dread, and I went back to yawning at my herring.

* * *

I heard the screaming first. Then the running. Then, threading through both, the thing that stopped my blood: a woman's voice, raw and cracked, shouting a word that hadn't worked in twelve years.

"*Vael.*"

I came around the corner of the smokehouse fast, and there in the lane above the harbor steps was the dyer's boy — my customer, my rule-breaking mistake — kneeling beside his mother, who lay propped against a wall with a shawl around her shoulders, upright, *upright*, coughing nothing, coughing air, her face still grey with the death that had just decided against her. Around them the crowd had gone strange and still, the way animals go still before weather.

And the boy was holding up the river-stone, and the painted door on it was *open*.

I don't mean the paint had smudged. I mean the little lapis door stood ajar on the face of the stone, and light came out of the gap — thin, gold, ordinary-looking light, lamplight light, except it fell across the mother's face and the greyness drained out of it like water out of a pulled plug, and everyone saw, and everyone would remember, and I was standing thirty feet away with my stall full of contraband and my sleeve covering the proof that this god knew my name.

"No," I said, quietly, conversationally, to nobody. "No, no, no."

Because here is what I knew that the crowd didn't: charms don't work. Six years of selling them, and not one knot had turned aside a fever, not one stone had opened so much as a crack. They were wood and paint and honest fraud, comfort-shaped objects, and I'd told myself that was mercy enough in a godless country. The whole rotten peace of the occupation rested on that fact. The Crown hadn't beaten the gods. It had only outlasted them, and built its laws on top of the quiet like a man building on snow.

And now a door painted by my own hand had opened, and the silence was over, and every soul in this square who'd whispered a wish into the dark in the last twelve years was suddenly — *audible*.

The mother stood up without help. The crowd made a sound I'd never heard a crowd make — not cheering, not fear, something older than either, the sound people make when the sea pulls back too far from the shore.

And down the lane, unhurried, came three grey cloaks and a tall figure in black with the Crown's sunburst stitched in silver at the collar, moving toward the miracle the way a spider moves toward a plucked web-thread.

Captain Hale. Of course it was Hale. The man had a nose for blasphemy like a shark has a nose for blood, and I'd spent six years perfecting the art of being boring specifically at him.

Time to be somewhere else. I turned, casual as Sunday, and began folding up my stall.

"Tamsin."

My stomach dropped through the cobblestones. Nobody in Corvane knew that name. Nobody alive in Corvane knew that name — I'd buried it in another province with the rest of my old life, and the voice that said it hadn't come from Hale, hadn't come from the crowd, hadn't come from any throat at all. It had arrived inside my skull with the intimacy of my own thoughts and the weight of something much, much older.

Twelve years of nothing, and then my name, spoken the way you'd speak to a dog that had wandered off and was pretending not to hear you.

I kept folding. My hands shook. A charm-seller's hands don't shake; that's rule three.

"You always were a poor liar," the voice said, warm and dry and unbearably fond, the voice of a commander who'd watched you fail drill a hundred times and loved you for exactly that. "Selling my name in scraps to fishwives. Twelve years, and this is what you make of yourself."

"I made a living," I muttered into my crate of herring, and several nearby shoppers edged away from the madman arguing with his fish.

"You made a hiding place. There's a difference, little oathman. And the difference has just come due."

Across the square, Hale had reached the family. He was looking at the stone, at the open painted door, at the mother breathing clean air — and I saw something pass over his face that I'd never seen on a witch-finder and never wanted to: doubt, arriving like winter. He raised his hand, and the grey cloaks fanned out, and one of them pointed — not at the family, not at the crowd, but at the smokehouse, at the lane, at *me*, because of course the captain had seen me too, six years of boring had finally failed, and the one witness to a true miracle in occupied Corvane was a man with a wrapped arm and a crate of fraudulent herring.

I ran.

I took the alley behind the smokehouse at a dead sprint, stall abandoned, coppers abandoned, everything abandoned except the wrapped forearm burning under its bandage like a coal that had waited twelve years for permission. Behind me the crowd was chanting the god's name, hundreds of voices, a tide coming in after a decade-long drought, and ahead of me the alley split toward the docks or the walls, and under it all, patient as tide itself, the voice again:

"Run, then. You were always good at running. But the door opens on the thirteenth turn, Tamsin Ravensworth — and you will be there when it does. You owe me an army's worth of prayers, and I have started collecting."

The moon, I remembered, had turned twelve times since midwinter.

I ran faster.
