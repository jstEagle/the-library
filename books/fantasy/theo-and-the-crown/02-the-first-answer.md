## 2. The First Answer

The trouble with answered prayers, I've since learned, is that nobody prays for anything sensible. Nobody prays for rain on their neighbor's field or patience with a difficult mother-in-law. They pray for money, for revenge, for the dead to come back — and then they're shocked, genuinely shocked, when something answers.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. That morning I was still just a charm-seller with a bad knee and a stall in Corvane's Tanner's Row, watching the witch-finders make their rounds and thinking about lunch.

The boy had come back at dawn. He'd bought the prayer-charm from me three days prior — a copper bit for a knotted thread of red wool, which is highway robbery given that it cost me nothing but string and a muttered word, but the muttering was genuine and the string was blessed by a god who used to listen, so fair's fair. He'd prayed for his mother's fever to break. Standard fare. The kind of thing I sold forty of a week and thought about never.

I knew it had worked before he opened his mouth, because of how he walked. People who've been answered walk differently. Lighter. Like the ground has agreed to hold them up.

"She woke," he said, gripping the stall's edge with both hands like the world might tip. "She woke at moonset and asked for soup. She *asked for soup*, master. Twelve days she wouldn't wake."

"That's lovely," I said, and my hands were already moving, wrapping his charm-thread back around its knot, taking it from him. "Give that here. Used charms shouldn't linger. Bad luck."

He surrendered it gladly enough, beaming, and ran off toward home, and I sat there holding three feet of red wool that was warm as a live coal and thinking very unkind thoughts about gods.

Here's what you need to understand about Vael: he went quiet twelve years ago, and everyone said the same thing. *The old god is dead. The Crown burned the last of his priests and the silence that followed is just the sound of an empty house.* I told myself that too. I told myself it so thoroughly that I built a whole life inside the telling — the stall, the charms, the careful smallness of it all. A god who's dead can't collect. A god who's dead leaves you alone with whatever you swore to Him in a moment of youthful stupidity.

And now some feverish woman in the Sump had woken up because her son paid a copper to a dead man's memory, and the red thread in my hand was warm, and underneath the warmth — faint as a pulse through a wall — something was *listening*.

"Damn it," I said quietly, to no one, or possibly to someone.

Because here was the other thing, the thing I'd spent twelve years not thinking about while thinking about almost nothing else: charms don't work. Not mine, not anyone's, not since the Silence. Oh, people believed they worked, and belief is worth a copper or two, but the actual mechanism — the listening ear, the answering hand — had gone out of the world like a draft under a door. What I sold was comfort shaped like commerce. Knots and whispers and hope at market rates.

Except this one had *worked*. Which meant either I'd gotten very good at fraud without noticing, or the empty house wasn't empty anymore, and something had come home and found my little knotted offerings waiting like dust-covered furniture, and decided to start using them.

I did what any sensible man does when a god wakes up. I started packing up my stall.

* * *

Witch-finders don't look like much, which is the point. Grey cloaks, plain boots, faces like unpaid debts. Captain Hale himself led them down Tanner's Row that morning — I recognized the limp, though he probably doesn't know I know he limps, and there's a certain intimacy in knowing a hunter's body better than he does.

They stopped at the pie cart first. Then the rag-man. Working methodically toward me, asking questions, and every answer made one of them turn and look at the crowd, reading it, the way you'd read a room for the person who'd stopped breathing normally.

I had the stall half-collapsed when a woman stepped into my shadow.

She was perhaps fifty, grey-streaked hair pinned up with what looked suspiciously like a bone stylus, wearing a fishwife's shawl over the posture of someone who had once stood in front of altars for a living. You can always tell former clergy. We stand like we're being watched, even alone. Especially alone.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. Not a question. "Ravensworth."

"I'm a knot-enthusiast with inventory problems. Who's asking?"

"Odile." She didn't offer a surname. Clergy who've been stripped of office tend to lose their surnames along with everything else; the Crown takes them as part of the confiscation. "The boy with the red thread came through the Sump this morning telling everyone who'd listen. Half the district knows by now. The other half will, by noon. And the finders already know where the charm-seller keeps his stall, Ravensworth, so unless your knots have taught themselves to run—"

"They're coming," I said. "Yes. Thank you. Lovely chat."

I tipped the stall. It's a good stall, collapsible, designed for exactly this — thirty seconds of practice and it's a hand-cart, and the hand-cart is full of harmless-looking trinkets, and the trinkets are mostly harmless, and the ones that aren't are buried under the ones that are. Odile took the handles before I could protest and hauled it into the crowd like she'd done harder labor, which, judging by her forearms, she had.

We went east through the fish-market, which stinks enough to discourage pursuit, then south through the tanneries, where the smell gets worse and the alleys get narrower and the witch-finders' grey cloaks stand out like snow on slate. She moved like someone who'd planned escapes before. I didn't ask. Asking questions of helpful strangers is how you end up owing them, and I was already carrying more debt than I could service.

At the mouth of the culvert under the old city wall — Corvane has walls inside its walls inside its walls, conquest layered like sediment — she finally stopped, set the cart down, and looked at me with an expression I recognized from funerals.

"It answered," she said. "Didn't it. Something actually answered."

"I sell souvenirs, madam."

"Don't insult both of us." She pulled the bone stylus from her hair, and I saw then that it was carved all over with script — old script, pre-Conquest, the kind the Crown pays bounties on. "Twelve years ago I served Anthea of the Deep Water. Twelve years ago She stopped speaking, mid-liturgy, mid-*word*, and every priestess in my order stood in our temple feeling like abandoned children in an empty market. The Crown said the gods were dead. The Crown was very eager that we believe it." Her eyes were pale grey and absolutely steady. "This morning, a child's charm worked. So either the world changed overnight, or it never changed at all and we were lied to for twelve years. I've come to decide which. You're going to help me."

"And if I decline?"

"Then I'll scream 'witch' right now and take my chances negotiating with the finders."

I liked her immediately. There's a kindred spirit in anyone whose faith curdles into practical menace.

* * *

We hid through the afternoon in a cellar beneath a smokehouse, surrounded by hanging hams, which is a stupid place to have a religious experience and yet.

It happened at dusk. Dusk matters — I know that now, though I didn't then. The light goes thin and gold and sideways, the hour when erased things flicker at the edges of vision, and I was sitting on a ham barrel rubbing my bad knee and Odile was re-wrapping her stylus in cloth when the warmth came back.

Not the thread. Me.

It started in my left forearm, low and spreading, the exact spot where — well. Where the brand used to be. Where I'd sworn my blade to Vael at nineteen, kneeling in a shrine that smelled of iron and juniper, saying words I only half understood: *my name, my memory, my self, against the day of payment.* The brand had faded to a pale ghost-mark years ago, smooth as old scar, and I'd told myself that meant released. Discharged. Forgiven, even.

The heat traced the old shape precisely, every line, like a finger retracing a signature. And then —

You want to know what a god's voice sounds like after twelve years? It sounds like the moment after lightning, if the thunder kept talking. It doesn't arrive in your ears. It arrives in the space behind them, in the marrow, in the place where you keep the things you never say aloud, and it is *enormous*, and it is patient, and it says:

***The door opens on the thirteenth turn.***

I was on my feet without deciding to stand. The hams swung on their hooks. Odile had her stylus out and level like a blade, eyes wide.

"What," she said. "What just happened. Your face — Tamsin, what *said* that?"

"You heard it?" My voice came out wrong, scraped. "You heard — that was—"

"*Anthea* spoke to me once, at my ordination, and it sounded like cold water in the dark." She was white around the mouth. "That wasn't Anthea. That was something older. That was—" She stopped. Looked at my arm. At the ghost-brand, which I realized with distant horror must be glowing faintly through my sleeve, warm gold, like a coal remembered. "*Vael,*" she breathed. "You're sworn to Vael. The war-brands died out decades ago. You're sworn to the god of the door."

"The god of the *what*?"

"The Door of Thirteen Turns." She said it fast, like reciting scripture, which for her it probably was. "Before the Conquest. Before the borders were burned off the maps. Every erased country needs an exit, Ravensworth, and ours had one — a door that opens thirteen times, once each turning of the moon, and on the thirteenth opening it seals forever. The priests said it was a mercy. The last way out of a country being swallowed. Nobody ever needed it, because nobody ever thought we'd lose *everything*—"

The voice again, deeper now, closer, and this time it wasn't a sentence. It was a ledger opening. It was every prayer I'd ever murmured over a knot of wool being read back to me in order, dated and itemized, and at the bottom of the ledger, in figures that made my knees go soft:

***Paid: nothing. Owed: everything.***

"Stop," I whispered. "Please. I didn't break the oath. I never broke it. I just — I survived. I stayed small. I sold trinkets and I kept my head down and I *survived*, isn't that what you wanted? Wasn't survival the point?"

The silence that followed was worse than the voice. It was the silence of a creditor reviewing an account, and finding it amusing.

Odile caught my elbow as my legs gave. She lowered me onto the ham barrel, and she crouched in front of me, and her face had gone from interrogation to something rawer.

"When did it stop speaking to *you*?" she asked quietly. "Your god. When did Vael go silent?"

"Twelve years ago. The night the border fell. Everyone says the old gods died then."

"Everyone says all the gods died then." Her grip tightened. "Tamsin. All the gods fell silent on the same night. All of them, everywhere, mid-word — I told you, I was *mid-liturgy* — and the Crown annexed us within the season. Don't you see? The silence wasn't death. Silence is what you impose on something you've *locked away*." She laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "They didn't conquer a country. They imprisoned one. Gods and all. And now something has broken the lock, and the first thing out was a debt notice addressed to you."

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a bell began to ring — the witch-finders' assembly bell, three quick notes repeated, the signal for a quarter-sweep. Looking for someone. Looking for the source of the miracle on Tanner's Row, and the source was sitting in a smokehouse cellar with a glowing arm and a god's collection agent humming behind his ribs.

"The thirteenth turn," I said numbly. "When is the thirteenth turn?"

Odile went to the shuttered vent, tilted her head at the sky, and counted under her breath — she was good at that, priestesses are, moons are their calendar and their clock and their oldest argument. When she turned back her expression was the one I imagined she wore over dying parishioners.

"One month, Tamsin. The new moon after next." She picked up the cart handles. "So the real question is whether we spend that month running from the finders — or walking toward the door, and finding out what your god thinks you owe."

Somewhere behind my ribs, patient as interest accruing, something that used to be the god I mourned said nothing at all, which was somehow louder than the ledger.

I got up. I took the other handle.

"For the record," I said, "I really do just sell knots."

"No," said Odile, pushing open the cellar door onto the darkening street. "You really don't."
