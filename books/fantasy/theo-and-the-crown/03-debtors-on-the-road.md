## 3. Debtors on the Road

I had not walked a road with company in eleven years, and I had forgotten how loud it was.

Not the talking — though Tamsin talked the way rivers run, constant and mostly over stones. It was the *listening*. When you travel alone, you learn to listen to one thing at a time: your own breath, or the road, or whatever god you used to serve and have since stopped writing letters to. With Tamsin Ravensworth beside me, I could feel him listening to everything at once — the treeline, my stride, the faded brand under his sleeve that he kept touching like a man checking whether a wound had scabbed or gone septic.

"The Door of Thirteen Turns," he said, for perhaps the fourth time that morning. "You're sure it's north."

"I'm sure it's where the drunk ferryman said it was," I said. "Which is either north or nowhere, depending on how much he'd had."

"And you trust him because—"

"Because he charged me double and still couldn't look me in the eye when he said it. Liars look you in the eye, Tamsin. Honest men and terrified men don't." I adjusted the strap of my pack, which carried everything I owned in this world: two changes of clothes, a stolen psalter, and a small tin box of relics I no longer believed in but could not bring myself to bury. "He was both."

Tamsin grunted. He did that instead of agreeing, which I was learning was his way of agreeing.

Three days out of Corvane, and we were already an oddity: two adults moving fast on foot through country where everyone else stood very still, as if motion might attract attention. The Crown's witch-finders had burned something in Corvane — I saw the smoke from the harbor district before Odile ever found me crouched behind my overturned stall — and now every village we passed had its shrines shuttered, its god-niches bricked over with fresh mortar that gleamed white as teeth. The Crown had spent twelve years teaching these people that prayer was a disease. And then, three days ago, a child's fever broke because a cheap tin charm I sold her mother actually *worked*, and the whole country caught fire with the news.

Gods answering prayers again. After twelve years of silence. You could watch it move through the countryside like weather.

"You felt him again last night," Tamsin said. Not a question. "Your god. The quiet one."

"Anseret doesn't do quiet," I said. "She does *absent*, which is different. Absent means she's somewhere else. Quiet means she's standing behind you deciding whether to speak." I looked at him. "What did Vael say?"

Tamsin's jaw worked. "*The door opens on the thirteenth turn.* That's all. Twelve years of nothing, and when he finally speaks, it's logistics."

"That's worse than silence," I said. "Silence you can grieve. Logistics mean there's paperwork coming."

He almost laughed. Almost. I was beginning to collect those almosts the way a miser collects clipped coins.

We found Brannoc at dusk, in a shrine that wasn't supposed to exist anymore.

* * *

The village had no name on any map — I checked, and I own maps the way other women own shoes — and its shrine should have been rubble, because the Crown's surveyors had recorded it demolished six years ago. But demolition is a kind of lie you tell with hammers, and the truth underneath had grown back like grass through paving stones: a low stone building with a broken bell, sitting in a fold of hills that no cartographer had ever bothered to insult.

Inside, by the light of a fire built in the old offering trench, a man sat sharpening a sword that had more notches than blade left.

He heard us at the door — of course he did; men like him hear doors the way dogs hear whistles — but he didn't stop working. "If you're witch-finders," he said, "you're late. There were four of them yesterday. If you've come to pray, the god's awake but rude. If you've come for soup, I made too much, because soup is the only thing I've ever been good at rationing."

"We're debtors," Tamsin said simply.

The sword stopped mid-stroke.

That word does something to people now. Before the silence broke, *debtor* meant tax trouble. Now it means what it always should have meant: someone who swore something to a god, and whose oath has come due like winter.

Brannoc turned. He was big in the way fortresses are big — not handsome, just permanent — with a soldier's cropped hair going grey at the temples and a brand on his throat shaped like a spear crossed with a plow. "Korragan's mark," he said, touching it. "God of the long war. The war that outlasts the reasons for it." His voice was flat as a parade ground. "I swore to him at seventeen. Fought for him for nine years. Then the annexation came, and Korragan went silent along with every other voice worth hearing, and I discovered that a sworn shield with no god to shield is just a man with expensive iron." He looked at our brands — mine hidden, Tamsin's half-hidden, but debtors recognize debtors the way wolves recognize wolves. "Two nights ago he woke up and told me the war isn't over. Told me where to stand when it resumes." A humorless smile. "So here I am, standing. Waiting for the battle. And making soup."

"Is there really soup?" I asked, because someone had to.

"There's always soup. Soup is discipline." He ladled three bowls without asking whether we wanted any, which I later learned was Brannoc's entire philosophy of hospitality compressed into a single gesture.

We ate. And it was strange, eating with strangers who weren't strangers, because the same god-shaped absence had hollowed us all into matching shapes. Tamsin asked about the witch-finders — four, he said, outriders under some Captain Hale, sweeping north ahead of the main hunt — and Brannoc answered in the terse way of soldiers, all vectors and numbers, while I watched the fire and thought about how Anseret had spoken to me the night before.

She had said only: *Gather them.* No explanation. Gods are like that. They give you orders the size of continents and details the size of nothing.

"Gather them," I murmured into my bowl.

"What?" Tamsin said.

"Nothing. Eat your soup."

* * *

We found Sefa two days later, and I would trade every relic in my tin box never to have heard that sound again.

It was morning, gray and drizzling, and we smelled the woodsmoke first — a homestead burning, slow and deliberate, the way people burn things they loved. A girl of maybe fifteen stood in the yard watching it go, and beside her stood a young man, tall, broad-shouldered, holding an axe he clearly didn't know how to hold.

The wrongness hit me before anything else did. Priests develop a sense for it, like sailors and storms. Something about the boy was *off-register*, the way a bell sounds when it's cracked. He moved a half-beat late. He blinked a half-second long. He looked at the girl — Sefa — with an expression of profound, patient confusion, like a dog waiting at a door that had been painted onto the wall.

"Don't," Sefa said, seeing us approach, seeing the brands, seeing everything. Her voice was raw from screaming, though the screaming had apparently finished days ago. "Don't tell me to burn him. Everyone tells me to burn him. The priest in the next valley told me to burn him. My aunt told me to burn him. He's my brother."

"What happened?" Tamsin asked, gentle, and I noticed he'd positioned himself slightly between her and the axe.

"He drowned." She said it the way you'd report the weather, if the weather had killed you. "Tam, my brother, drowned in the millrace in spring. Two years ago. And when the gods woke up, I prayed. I prayed like they teach you — properly, kneeling, with the right words, giving Anseret the dawn-offering first because she goes first—" Her breath hitched. "And something answered. Something answered and I said *give him back*, and it did."

The boy — Tam — smiled at us. It was a warm smile. It arrived approximately four seconds after it should have.

"It's not him," Sefa whispered. "It looks like him. It remembers being him. It knows our mother's lullabies. But it doesn't *breathe* right, and it doesn't dream — I watched him sleep, he doesn't dream, his eyes just sit there — and last week he walked into the river and stood there for an hour, underwater, looking up at me through the ice-cold water and smiling, until I begged him to come out." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, furious at the tears rather than sad about them. "I got my brother back. Just not the part of him that was alive."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The house crackled. Tam smiled his delayed smile at the fire, fondly, as if he remembered fires.

Brannoc spoke first, in his parade-ground flatness. "Can it be undone? The rite reversed?"

"They won't let me undo it." Sefa's laugh was terrible. "I've tried. I've prayed and prayed and gotten nothing back except the feeling of a door being politely held shut. Whatever took my prayer took it *whole*. You can't return a gift to the gods. You can only ask for another one." She looked at each of us in turn, and I saw her make the decision before she said it. "I'm coming with you. To the Door. I want to ask whatever's behind it to fix him. Properly fix him, or properly un-fix him. Either way. I can't keep — " she gestured at the burning house, the smiling dead boy, the whole gray catastrophe of her life — "I can't keep being the only one who knows."

Tamsin glanced at me. I shrugged, which between us meant *yes, obviously, what kind of monsters do you take us for.*

"You'll walk with us," he told her. "But the boy stays close, and if he goes near water, we tie him to something heavy."

"He'd let you," Sefa said bleakly. "That's the worst part. He'd let you do anything. He's so *agreeable*."

* * *

Five of us, then, walking north through a country that was waking up around us like a sleeper turning over.

I wish I could say we became a family quickly, warmly, the way stories tell it. We didn't. We became a family the way bones knit — slowly, painfully, and only because the alternative was staying broken. Brannoc taught Sefa to hold the axe properly, and she taught him to laugh at least once a day, usually against his will. Tamsin and I argued about roads versus ridgelines and discovered that arguing, for both of us, was a form of intimacy we'd nearly forgotten existed. And Tam the not-brother walked among us, patient and pleasant and wrong, and none of us could quite bring ourselves to say aloud what we were all thinking:

that Sefa's prayer was a preview. That this was what the gods' answers looked like when they came back after twelve years — rusty, cracked, off-register. That whatever waited for us at the Door of Thirteen Turns had been silent a long time, and silence does something to a voice, even a divine one.

On the fifth night, camped under a shattered waystone, I took the night watch and felt Anseret standing behind me at last — present, attentive, deciding whether to speak.

*The door opens on the thirteenth turn,* she said, in a voice like frost forming on glass. *And the debts are called in order. Yours first, priestess.*

I sat with that until dawn, watching the east, where the sky had begun doing something I hadn't seen in twelve years.

Behind the hills, faint as breath on a window, the horizon was glowing gold — and the light was moving *toward* us, unhurried, like something that knew exactly where we were and had all the time in the world to arrive.

Far off, down the southern road, I heard horses. Many horses. And riding at their head, I would learn soon enough, a witch-finder captain carrying orders to burn every flickering village between here and the Door — and a map he should never have been allowed to keep.

The gods were awake. So was the Crown.

And both of them, it seemed, were coming north.
