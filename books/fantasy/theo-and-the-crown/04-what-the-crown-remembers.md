## 4. What the Crown Remembers

I have interrogated two hundred and eleven heretics in my career, and I will tell you what they all had in common: they wanted to talk. Not to confess — confession is a different animal entirely, one I've met perhaps four times. But they wanted to *talk*. To explain themselves. To make me understand that their god was real and kind and listening, right up until the moment it wasn't.

The woman chained to the chair across from me was no different. Except for one thing.

"Say it again," I said.

She was a laundress from the Corvane river district. Forty-odd years old, hands like boiled rope. She'd been arrested for praying aloud in a public square — specifically, for praying and being *answered*, when a rain barrel she'd been cursing at for three weeks had promptly filled itself during a cloudless night.

"The barrel," she said. "It filled itself. Clean water. Cold as well-water."

"You said that."

"It filled *itself*," she said again, with the particular patience of someone who believes the other party is being deliberately dense. "Captain, I have prayed every day of my life since I was six. My mother taught me the words to Vessia of the Wells before I could write my own name. Twelve years of nothing. Twelve years of talking into my own cupped hands." She lifted those rope-knuckled hands as far as the chains allowed, showing them to me like evidence. "And then three nights ago, Vessia answered. Do you know what that's like? After twelve years?"

"No," I said.

"You do," she said. "You have that look. The look of a man whose prayers stopped being answered too."

I did not hit her. I want that on the record. Whatever else came after, whatever they say about me now, on that morning I did not strike a chained woman for an impertinence I'd invited myself.

"Tell me about the twelve years," I said instead.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about witch-finders: we are not priests. We are archivists with knives. The Crown's Office of Unlicensed Devotion does not concern itself with whether gods exist — a question beneath both our dignity and our budget. We concern ourselves with whether gods are *licensed*. Every faith operating within Crown territory requires a charter, a tithe schedule, and a registered liturgy. Pray to the Concord God all you like; He's on the payroll. Pray to anything else, and you're not a mystic, you're a tax evader with candles.

The old gods of the annexed territories were never licensed. That was rather the point of annexing the territories.

Twelve years ago, the Crown absorbed the country of Velthmere — or what maps called Velthmere, though I have learned since that maps are documents of opinion, not fact. There was a war of eleven days. There was a treaty signed by men who'd never seen the rivers they were dividing. And there was an edict, issued from the Office above my office, that I helped carry out without ever once asking why: the shrines were shuttered, the names struck from registers, the gods' festivals folded quietly into approved holidays. No executions. No martyrs. Just paperwork. Velthmere didn't fall; it was *amended*.

And its gods went silent. Every one of them, on the same day, in every village at once. The faithful called it abandonment. The Crown called it confirmation of doctrine: false gods, false voices, nothing behind the curtain but wind.

We built our whole profession on that silence. I built my whole career on it. Two hundred and eleven heretics, each one certain their god would return, each one wrong — and me standing over them with the ledger of their futility, thinking myself a man of science.

Three nights ago, a rain barrel filled itself under a clear sky. And this morning I had forty-one prisoners in the cells below my office, and every single one of them had a story with the same shape: *twelve years of nothing, then something.*

Not gradually. All at once. Like a door unbarred.

* * *

"Say it again," I said, in the third cell that afternoon, to a boy of maybe nineteen with ink-stained fingers and a scribe's stoop.

"'The door opens on the thirteenth turn,'" he recited, flat with repetition. "That's what the voice said. In my head. It used Vael's voice — well, I assume. I never heard Vael before. But it sounded like a voice that had been waiting a long time to be used."

"Vael," I said. "God of oaths. One of the Velthmeran pantheon. Struck from the register in the Annexation Edict, section nine."

"You know His file better than I do, Captain."

"I know everyone's file better than anyone does. That's the job." I leaned against the damp wall. "This voice. Did it say anything else?"

"That the debt comes due. That those who swore should come pay." The boy rubbed his wrists inside the manacles. "Captain, can I ask you something? You keep asking us what the voices said. You haven't asked once whether we're lying."

"I know you're not lying," I said. "Lying has a texture. This—" I gestured at the cell, the forty-one stories, the impossible weather reports. "This is true, which is considerably worse."

He almost smiled. "Then you know what I keep wondering. Why now? Why twelve years of quiet and then — this?"

"I'm working on it," I said, and left him, because the answer had begun forming in the back of my skull around midday and it was the sort of answer I wanted to arrive at slowly, with witnesses, so I couldn't pretend later that I hadn't seen it coming.

* * *

The archive room of the Office of Unlicensed Devotion is in the basement, naturally. Faiths are buried things; we keep theirs accordingly. I sent my clerk away, lit two lamps, and pulled the Annexation records — the full set, not the sanitized summaries they issue to field officers.

Section nine covered religious suppression. I'd read it a hundred times. But I'd never read section nine *alongside* section eleven, because section eleven was filed under territorial engineering, which was a surveyor's matter, not a witch-finder's, and the Crown loves its departments the way a body loves its organs: sealed off, ignorant of one another, dying together if one fails.

Section eleven described the border works. The great ritual engineering project along the old Velthmeran frontier — the standing stones, the sung wards, the "stabilization of national character through geographic definition." I had always assumed it meant roads. Fortifications. The mundane masonry of empire.

It did not mean roads.

The surveyors' notes went on for pages in the dry language of men measuring things they preferred not to name. *Boundary resonance confirmed. Containment holding. Indigenous devotional frequencies suppressed at source.* Suppressed at source. Not discouraged. Not outcompeted by the cheerful Concord God and his excellent tithe schedule. *Suppressed at source* — the way you suppress a spring, damming it where it rises, so the whole river forgets it ever ran.

The gods hadn't gone silent. They'd been silenced. Muffled under eleven miles of warded stone, their names struck not just from paper but from the land itself, their faithful kept inside a border that wasn't a border at all but a lid.

And the annexation — the war of eleven days, the treaty, the amended maps — none of it had been conquest. Conquest takes a country and makes it yours. This had taken a country and made it *nowhere*. A prison shaped like an absence. Somewhere in the Crown's deepest files there was presumably a reason — a debt the gods had refused, a bargain gone sour, some sin old enough to frighten the men who sign edicts. I didn't find it that night. What I found was worse than any reason: the plain fact that there was no reason given at all. Section eleven required no justification. Empires don't explain themselves to their own instruments.

I sat in that basement until the lamps burned low, doing arithmetic I did not want to do.

Twelve years of silence. Twelve years since the lid went on. And now, suddenly, the gods speaking again — all at once, everywhere, like pressure finding a crack. Which meant the containment was failing. Or being opened. From the inside, by someone, or from outside by whatever force keeps even the Crown's worst ideas on a leash.

And the voices were all saying the same thing. *Come pay your debts. The door opens on the thirteenth turn.*

A thirteenth turn of what, I didn't yet know. But I knew what a door in a prison wall means to the people inside it, and I knew — with the cold, total clarity that arrives only when a man finally understands his own ledger — what the Crown would order done about it.

* * *

My clerk found me there past midnight. Good lad, Ferren. Writes a fair hand, asks no questions, which is why what happened next wounded me more than it should have.

"Captain, the priority dispatch came up from the capital while you were below." He held it out. "Marked eyes-only. Also—" he hesitated, "—there's a list attached. Arrest warrants. Eleven names."

I broke the seal. The dispatch ordered the Office to prepare for "devotional containment reinforcement" and instructed me, Captain Hale of the witch-finders, veteran of two hundred and eleven interrogations, to proceed at once to the flicker-villages — the settlements along the old frontier where erased places had reportedly begun showing through at dusk like bruises through skin.

And the instruction regarding those villages, in the Chancellor's own ciphered hand, was a single word.

*Burn.*

The eleven names were the ones the voices had touched most loudly. Charmsellers, defrocked priests, a soldier, a girl. The first name on the list was Tamsin Ravensworth — I knew it, of course; Ravensworth had been slipping through my fingers since before the silence ended, a charmseller with a faded oath-brand and a talent for vanishing that I'd long suspected was more than talent. I'd chased that man across three provinces and half a filing cabinet of reports. Now the Crown wanted him, and the others, and a row of villages full of families who had done nothing worse than remember.

Ferren was still standing there, waiting for orders.

"Burn the dispatch," I said.

"Sir?"

"You heard me. And wake the garrison remounts. Quietly." I looked down at the list, at the name Tamsin Ravensworth, and understood that I was about to do the one thing a hunter never plans for: change sides of the same hunt. "There are eleven people in this city the Crown wants found before they find a door. Which means, Sergeant, that we are going to find them first."

Ferren stared at me the way men stare at officers who've begun speaking a foreign language. Then he took the dispatch, fed it to the lamp, and asked the only question that mattered:

"Find them to arrest them, sir?"

I thought of a rain barrel filling itself under a clear sky. Of forty-one voices saying the same sentence. Of a country filed under *nowhere*.

"No," I said. "To warn them. And then, if they'll have me — to follow them through."
