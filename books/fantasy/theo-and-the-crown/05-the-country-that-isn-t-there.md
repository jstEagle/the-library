## 5. The Country That Isn't There

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in maps. I'd spent twelve years selling charms in cities that had names, and every one of those names was a kind of lie the Crown had agreed to tell itself. Corvane. Vell's Crossing. The Salt Marches. Places with borders drawn by men who'd never bled on them.

The land we crossed into on the fourth day had no name at all, and it was the most honest country I have ever walked through.

It began as an absence of roads. Not ruined roads — ruined roads at least admit something happened to them — but roads that simply stopped being, mid-stride, like a thought someone interrupted. We'd been following Brannoc's instinct north, because Brannoc's god apparently still spoke to him in the language of mud and direction, and Odile had Sefa's hand in hers and a look on her face I recognized from my own mirror: the look of a person walking toward something they've prayed for and dread receiving.

"The map ends here," Hale said. He said it the way other men say *the bridge is out* or *the well is poisoned*. He'd brought the Crown's survey charts with him when he defected, rolled up like a confession, and now he unrolled one across a flat stone and stared at it as if staring hard enough might shame it into accuracy.

"Of course it does," Odile said. "You can't imprison a country and then draw its picture."

"That's not how prisons work."

"No," she agreed. "That's rather the point."

I kept my mouth shut, which for me is a discipline on par with flagellation. The truth was that I knew this land. Not its geography — its *feel*. The brand under my sleeve had started warming two days ago, a dull coin of heat against my forearm, and it warmed hotter now with every step north, until it felt less like a scar and more like a hand resting there. Patient. Expectant.

Vael was awake, and Vael was walking with me, and I had not yet decided whether that was comfort or sentence.

* * *

We saw the first village at dusk.

I say *saw*, but that flatters the experience. What happened was this: the light went long and amber across a valley that had contained nothing all afternoon — grass, stones, a stream running wrong-way in places, as if it couldn't remember which direction downhill used to be — and then the light touched a cluster of rooftops and the rooftops *agreed to exist*.

They flickered. Like a candle behind horn panes. One moment bare hillside; the next, chimneys and thatch and a well with a bucket rope gone green, all of it translucent, all of it trembling at the edges like heat-shimmer given architecture. And moving among the houses, figures. People. Faint as breath on glass.

Sefa made a sound I'll never forget — small, cracked, hopeful — and started forward before Brannoc caught her arm.

"They're not alive," he said gently. It cost him something to say it; his brother-in-arms voice, the one that delivers casualties.

"They're not dead either," she shot back. "Look at them."

She was right, which was worse. The figures moved with the unhurried logic of people doing evening things: drawing water, shutting shutters, calling children in from lanes that were sometimes there and sometimes weren't. A woman stood at her doorway looking north, toward us, though I don't think she saw us. Her mouth was moving. All their mouths were moving.

"Prayers," Odile whispered. "Twelve years of prayers, going nowhere. They're still saying them."

Then the sun dropped below the ridge and the village went out like a snuffed lamp, and we stood in an empty valley with our own breathing for company, and I understood why the Crown had needed witch-finders. Not to hunt magic. To hunt *witnesses*.

* * *

The shrine came next, and the shrine was where my composure finally packed its bags and left.

It sat in a fold of hills that no map acknowledged, and unlike the villages it didn't flicker — it was stubbornly, horribly solid. Stone walls. A slate roof. Doors shuttered with iron bands, sealed from the outside with Crown wax, the royal sigil stamped fat and purple across wood that had warped around it. A shrine closed the way you close a house where everyone died: thoroughly, and by strangers.

And queued at those shuttered doors, in the last grey light, stood the dead.

Not ghosts. Ghosts would have been simpler. These were dead the way the villagers were almost-alive — present, patient, translucent at the hem. They stood in a line that wound down the hillside and vanished into the dusk, dozens of them, hundreds, each holding whatever they'd held when they died: a loaf, a hammer, a child's shoe. Waiting. They had been waiting twelve years for doors that were never going to open, and they knew it, and they waited anyway, because what else do you do with faith except spend it?

Sefa walked toward them and none of them turned their heads, but the line shifted — rippled — like water recognizing a stone.

"Don't," Brannoc said.

"I need to see."

"You've seen. You told us. Your brother—"

"My brother is *wrong*, Brannoc." Her voice broke clean through. "He came back and he doesn't eat and he watches me sleep and I asked a silent god for a miracle and something *answered*, and I have to know if the thing that answered is standing in that line."

Nobody had an answer. So she went, and Odile went with her, and I stayed back with Hale and Brannoc among the graves nobody had dug, feeling the brand on my arm burn like a struck match.

That was when Vael spoke.

Not aloud. Vael had never once spoken aloud, even before the silence — that was the joke of him, the war-god who whispered. But this time he didn't whisper words. He gave me the vision whole, the way you give a drowning man the entire ocean.

* * *

I saw a border.

I saw it being drawn — not on parchment but in the world, a seam of light driven into the earth like a nail into a coffin lid, and I saw whose hands drove it. Crown hands. Surveyor's hands. Men with instruments and imperial arithmetic, chanting annexation in the old formal tongue, the legal liturgy of conquest. Except the words they used were not the words for *taking*. They were older, borrowed words, words for *containing*.

They hadn't conquered my homeland. They had locked it. Drawn the border not around territory but around *everything in it* — the living, the dead, the prayers, the gods themselves — and sealed the seam, and called the resulting silence *peace*, and called the resulting emptiness *annexation*, and put it all down on maps as empty land waiting to be improved.

And the gods — Vael showed me this too, showed me the moment of it — the gods hadn't abandoned us. They'd been *shut in* with us. Twelve years of hearing every prayer and having no way to answer, because answers require passage, and the passage was nailed shut.

Until it started to crack. Until prayers started leaking through. Until a charm-seller in Corvane sold a sick child's mother a worthless knot of thread, and some crack in the world's seal let one small prayer slide home, and the whole rotten beautiful machinery groaned awake.

Then the vision turned, and showed me what I had actually done, twelve years ago, kneeling in a burned chapel with Vael's sigil-brand searing my arm.

I had sworn to be Vael's blade in the defense of the border-shrines. And the border-shrines fell — all of them, in one night, in the Crown's first act of containment — and every soul sworn to them was given a choice by their gods, in that last hour before the sealing: *die with your oath, or live deferred.* Live, and carry the debt. Survival itself became the loan. Every heartbeat since was interest.

I hadn't broken my oath. My oath had been *postponed* — filed away in the dark behind the world's sealed door, accruing, waiting for the day the door opened and the ledger came due.

I came back to myself on my knees in the wet grass with Odile's hand on my shoulder and the others gathered around me, and the brand on my arm glowing faintly through my sleeve like a coal refusing to die.

"Tamsin." Odile crouched. "What did he show you?"

I laughed. It came out wrong, jagged, half grief. "That I'm not a deserter. That's the funny part. Twelve years I thought I'd run, thought I'd broken faith, sold myself cheap in market squares. Turns out I'm just—" My voice failed and restarted. "Turns out survival was the debt. Every day I stayed alive, I owed him more. And now the door's opening and the bill's come due and I don't know what's left over after I pay it."

Brannoc said, quietly, "Then we make sure it's worth what it costs."

Hale said nothing. He was looking at the queue of the dead, and then at the Crown's maps lying open on the stone, and I watched a man realize the empire he'd served had built a tomb and staffed it with the living.

Sefa came back down the hill alone. Her face was terrible and calm.

"There are more shrines ahead," she said. "More doors. And the dead are all facing the same direction we're walking." She pointed north, toward whatever the Door of Thirteen Turns was, toward the thirteenth moon already climbing in her eyes. "They're not waiting anymore. They're following."

Behind us, faint as candlelight through horn, the first shuttered shrine began to glow.
