## 6. Sefa's Bargain

I had carried my brother for six days before I told anyone he was wrong.

Not dead-wrong — that was the whole problem. Tamel walked when I wanted him to walk, stopped when the road stopped, slept like a stone dropped in a well. A good brother, an obedient one, a *finished* one. The Tamel who'd died of the marsh-fever had been eleven years old and contrary as a cat, all elbows and arguments, forever asking why not instead of yes. This Tamel said yes to everything.

"You want me to walk through the night, Sefa?" Yes. "You want me to stop talking?" He hadn't been talking anyway. "Are you cold?" No, Sefa. Are you?

That last one was the worst. Because the answer was yes, always yes, and the thing wearing my brother's shape would say it with such gentle concern that I wanted to scream at it, at him, at whatever god had taken my prayer — *bring him back* — and returned the shell without the spark.

So when we found the shrine flickering at dusk on the fourth day out of Corvane — a little roadside shrine to a harvest god whose name even Odile didn't know, its stones going transparent and then solid again like breath on glass — I decided something.

I decided I would take him back.

* * *

Odile tried to talk me out of it first, which surprised me, because she'd spent the whole journey being the reasonable one and I'd expected her to approve of reason. She crouched by the shrine with her ruined hands folded — the gods' silence had taken her vocation but not the habit of prayer, and her knuckles were worn smooth as river pebbles from twelve years of pressing them together over nothing.

"The rite for unmaking isn't taught anymore," she said. "It was barely taught before. There's a reason."

"Because it's hard?"

"Because most people, given the choice, choose to keep the ghost." She looked at Tamel, who stood patiently in the bracken with his hands at his sides, waiting to be told what he wanted. "And because the ones who don't — the ones who see clearly what came back — they usually can't do it. Love gets in the way of the knife-hand."

"I'm not going to cut anything," I said. "I'm going to bargain. That's different. Tamsin says the old contracts were bargains. Everything with the gods was a bargain. So there has to be a way back out of one."

"Tamsin also says the gods are creditors, and creditors don't forgive debts out of kindness." She sighed, and her breath fogged strangely, as though the air around the shrine couldn't decide whether to be warm or cold. "But come here, then. If we're doing this, we do it properly. Kneel where the stone goes clear. That's where the listening is thickest."

Brannoc planted himself between us and the road with his arms crossed, which was Brannoc's way of praying — standing guard against the world while other people did the dangerous holy things. He'd been quieter since his own god had started murmuring to him in his sleep, war-names and muster-rolls, a soldier's god waking up hungry for the wars it was owed. "Outriders patrol this stretch after dark," he said. "Crown livery. We've maybe an hour."

"Then let's spend it well," I said.

I knelt. The stone under my knees went transparent, and for a moment I could see straight down through the hill itself, down into a dark full of slow-moving shapes, like fish under ice. The dead of the erased villages, still queueing. Still waiting at shrines that no longer quite existed, for gods who had only just remembered they were being waited on.

It made me furious, actually. It still does. Twelve years the gods were silent, and everyone said pray anyway, keep the faith, they're only sleeping. And now they wake up and the first thing they find is a line of customers they never meant to serve.

I put my hands flat on the flickering stone and I said, out loud, in the plain speech my grandmother used for scolding goats: "You gave me back my brother wrong. I want to return him."

The dark below the shrine went very still.

Then something noticed me. I don't know how else to describe it. Not Vael — Tamsin says you know your own god the way you know your own footsteps. This was older and smaller and more patient, the kind of presence that had watched ten thousand harvests and found most of them adequate. It turned its attention on me the way frost turns attention on a window.

*THE PRAYER WAS ANSWERED,* it said, in a voice like a granary door opening. *THE DEBT IS PAID.*

"He wasn't supposed to come back like this!" My voice cracked and I hated it. "He's not — you left him empty. You took my prayer and gave me back the shape of him and kept everything that made him my brother. That's not answering. That's cheating."

Silence. Long enough that Odile's hand touched my shoulder, warning or comfort, probably both.

*A PRAYER IS A DOOR,* the god said at last. *WE CANNOT CHOOSE WHAT COMES THROUGH IT. WE ONLY OPEN.*

"Then open it again. Both ways. Let me send him back and — " and here was the part I'd been building toward, the part I'd rehearsed against the night sky for four nights running — "and I'll give you something in trade. A debt for a debt. Fair terms. Isn't that what you want? Isn't that why you woke up?"

The stillness changed quality. I couldn't tell you how. But Odile's grip tightened on my shoulder and she breathed, "*Careful,*" and Brannoc turned around from watching the road because even he felt it: the moment a negotiation stops being a negotiation and becomes a feeding.

*NAME YOUR OFFER.*

Here is what nobody tells you about bargaining with gods: they let you go first. Every time. Because the first offer tells them everything — how much you think you're worth, how desperate you are, how far you'll go. Tamsin told me later that in the old days the priests trained for years just to learn how to make the opening offer slowly.

I was fifteen and I'd learned bargaining from a market stall. I said, "My prayers. All of them, ever, for the rest of my life. You'll have every word. No one will ever pray to you more faithfully than—"

*COLD OFFER,* the god said. *WORDS ARE CHEAP. THE DEAD BELOW HAVE PRAYED FOR TWELVE YEARS INTO A CLOSED EAR. YOU THINK WE WANT MORE WORDS?*

My mouth went dry. Below me, through the clear stone, the shapes in the dark had stopped moving. They were listening too. Hundreds of them. Thousands. All the unpaid faithful of a country that officially didn't exist, hearing a living girl try to haggle with heaven.

"Then what do you want?" I whispered.

*THAT IS NOT HOW BARGAINS WORK, LITTLE ONE.* Something almost gentle entered the voice, which was somehow worse. *YOU OPENED A DOOR. DOORS STAY OPENED UNTIL THEY'RE PAID SHUT. BRING ME SOMETHING TRUE AND COSTLY AND WE WILL SPEAK OF TERMS. BRING NOTHING, AND HE WALKS BEHIND YOU FOREVER, EMPTY AND OBEDIENT, UNTIL ONE OF YOU DUSTS.*

"That's not fair."

*NO,* it agreed, and beneath us, in the dark, the queueing dead stirred like a field in wind. *IT IS NEVER FAIR. IT IS ONLY TRUE.*

* * *

I don't remember deciding to bleed. I remember Odile saying my name, sharp, twice, and Brannoc saying "riders" at the same moment, and both of those becoming one sound.

Because the Crown outriders came early.

Three of them, torches high, coming up the road at a trot, and Brannoc did what Brannoc always did, which was step into the space between danger and the rest of us and become a wall. "Keep talking to it," he said over his shoulder, drawing steel. "Whatever you offered — finish the offer. Half-finished bargains are worse than none."

"What do you mean, finish it, I haven't thought of anyth—"

The first crossbow bolt took him in the shoulder.

I saw it happen the way you see things in nightmares: clearly, slowly, with terrible detail. The way he grunted more than screamed. The way he turned the wound toward the riders rather than away from them, so they could see they'd have to do it properly. The way his god — his loud, hungry, waking god — roared up through him like fire through a chimney, and Brannoc went from a wall to an avalanche.

After that I stopped watching, mostly. There was screaming and there was torchlight moving wrong and Odile dragging me back down to the stone, her ruined hands pressing mine flat against the flickering surface, shouting, "Give it something true! Quickly! Blood counts, pain counts, give it *you*, girl, give it something you'll miss!"

Blood counts. I knew that. Everyone knows blood counts; it's the oldest currency there is. But blood is cheap too, really — everyone bleeds, bleeding ends. What does a god want that doesn't end?

I looked at Tamel. Standing in the bracken, patient, obedient, watching me get hurt and feeling nothing, because there was nothing left in him to feel it with. Eleven years old forever. Saying yes to everything.

And I understood — finally, horribly — what the god had been trying to tell me. It hadn't cheated me. It couldn't reach through the door and pick. It could only hand me what fit through the shape of my asking. *Bring him back* — and what had I actually asked for? Not my brother. My brother *safe*. My brother where I could see him. My brother who would never leave me, never argue, never grow up and walk away down some road I didn't choose. I had prayed a cage, and the god had delivered exactly that, and the emptiness was mine. My prayer had hollowed him.

So I gave it the truest thing I had.

"I'll miss him," I said, and my voice didn't crack this time, which surprised me. "That's the payment. Not words. Not blood. I'll carry missing him — real missing, the kind that aches, the kind that grows — for the rest of my life. Every time I pass a boy his age. Every winter he doesn't get older. That's costly. That doesn't end. Take it and close the door both ways."

The stillness went on long enough for me to hear Brannoc bellowing, for me to smell smoke, for Odile to whisper *yes, yes, that's it, that's true.*

*ACCEPTED,* said the granary-door voice. *PARTLY.*

"*Partly?*"

*A BARGAIN MAY BE ADJUSTED. IT MAY NEVER BE REFUSED. YOU ASKED TO RETURN HIM; HE RETURNS. BUT THE MISSING YOU OFFERED — WE TAKE HALF. THE OTHER HALF STAYS IN HIM.* A pause, and the frost-attention warmed by a degree, or seemed to. *HE WILL REMEMBER DYING, LITTLE ONE. HE WILL REMEMBER THE DARK BETWEEN. NO ELEVEN-YEAR-OLD SHOULD, AND YET. WE ARE NOT KIND. WE ARE ONLY OLD. SAY YES OR NO.*

And here is the trap they never warn you about, the one Odile meant when she said love gets in the way of the knife-hand: saying yes meant my brother would come back *real* and *wounded* — carrying his own death inside him like a swallowed stone, dreaming of the dark between for the rest of his life. Saying no meant keeping the empty one forever, safe and hollow and mine.

There was no third option. Gods don't do thirds. Thirds are for markets.

Tamel was looking at me. Actually looking — and for the first time in six days, there was something behind his eyes, some far-off light, like a man surfacing from deep water. Whether it was the half-payment stirring or just my imagination I'll never know, but his mouth moved and I swear on my grandmother's goats I heard it:

"Sefa? It's cold down there."

"Yes," I said. To the god, to the dark, to the door. "Yes. Take it. Both halves. Close it."

The stone went solid under my hands all at once, warm as bread.

What happened next was ordinary, which is the strangest thing I can report. My brother sat down heavily in the bracken and began, very quietly, to cry — the ragged, furious crying of a boy waking from a nightmare, alive and aching and *arguing with the world again*. Odile went to him. I stayed kneeling, feeling the new thing settle into my chest where the god had taken its half of my missing: a cold coin of grief, minted and permanent, that I will carry until I dust.

Loophole, I thought. We found a loophole. Half a loophole, bought with half a heart.

Then Brannoc collapsed.

* * *

We got him behind the shrine with the outriders' horses stampeding off down the road and two of the three riders not getting up again. The bolt had gone deep, and worse than deep — the wound wept light, faint gold, his god's fire leaking out of him around the iron the way marrow shows in a split bone.

"He shielded us and his god spent him doing it," Odile said, tearing bandage-lengths from her skirt. Her hands were shaking. Hers, not his. "This is what they're owed, Tamsin said. Wars. Blood. Soldiers who stand in doors." She pressed the cloth hard and Brannoc groaned. "His god means to collect."

"Then we don't let it," I said, and heard how young I sounded, and didn't care.

Brannoc caught my wrist with his good hand. His eyes were fever-bright and terribly calm. "Girl," he said, "listen. I've been hearing muster-rolls in my sleep. Names. Dates. A war my god wants fought that ended before my grandfather was born." He laughed, wetly. "I always wondered what I was saving myself for. Now fetch me Tamsin. If anyone knows how to cheat a creditor—"

Down the road, in the dark, hoofbeats. More of them this time. Many more.

And walking out ahead of the torchlight, unhurried, alone, in the grey coat of the Crown's witch-finders, came a man I'd seen only in the others' tense silences: Captain Hale, the hunter himself, stopping ten paces off with his hands empty and raised, looking at our wounded soldier, our crying brother, our flickering shrine —

—and kneeling, slowly, in the mud.
