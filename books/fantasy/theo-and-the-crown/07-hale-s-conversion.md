## 7. Hale's Conversion

I had burned eleven villages in my career as a witch-finder of the Crown, and I could tell you the number because I kept a ledger. That was the trick of it — you kept a ledger, and the ledger made each entry a line instead of a life. Eleven lines. Neat little strokes of ink. Captain Aldous Hale, faithful servant of the Throne, who did not flinch at the smell of thatch and hair burning together because he had learned, early, to breathe through his mouth.

The twelfth order came with a wax seal and no signature beyond the Chancellor's cipher: *Proceed to the flicker-settlements in the erased territories. Burn all structures. Detain or destroy any persons found within.*

*Destroy.* Not "execute." Not even "kill." The word they chose told me everything about what the Crown believed those people were. Not subjects. Not rebels. Inventory.

I read the order four times by lamplight in my tent on the edge of territory that did not exist, and on the fourth reading my hand was steady, which frightened me more than shaking would have.

Because here is what nobody tells you about certainty: it is a load-bearing wall. You build your whole house against it. And when it cracks, you don't hear the crack — you hear everything *else*, all at once, the groan of every beam you ever trusted.

The crack had come three days earlier, in an interrogation cell in Corvane, with a dyer's widow named Maren Ott who had prayed to a river-god for rain and received a flood. She sat across the table from me with her wrists bound and her eyes clear as a bell, and she said, "Captain, your maps are wrong. Not badly wrong. *Categorically* wrong. There was never an annexation. There was a sealing."

I asked her what she meant, in the flat tone I used for heretics, and she told me about the year the borders were redrawn — twelve years ago, the same year the gods went silent. She told me how the old kingdom hadn't fallen; it had been folded shut like a letter. How the Crown's cartographers didn't erase the country out of ignorance but out of policy, and how the silence of the gods wasn't abandonment.

"It was containment," she said. "You don't conquer a god's country, Captain. You lock the door and wait for the god to starve." Her smile was terrible. "Except gods don't starve. They *wait*. And yours have finished waiting."

I ordered her released the next morning. Filed it as clerical error. Then I sat in my tent with her words and the burn order side by side, and I understood that I had spent twenty years hunting debtors of gods whom my own sovereign had imprisoned, and calling it justice.

*

The flicker-village was called Hallowmere. I know this because the dead told me.

That is a sentence I never expected to write, even privately, so let me be precise about it. The village stood at dusk the way heat stands above a road — visible, wavering, wrong. Its shrine still had candles in the windows though no living hand had lit them in twelve years. And along the lane between the shuttered houses, the dead queued. Patiently. In an orderly line that wound from the shrine door down past the well and out toward a darkness at the village's edge where the land simply stopped agreeing to be land.

They weren't ghosts in the way stories mean ghosts. They didn't wail. They waited. An old man in a miller's apron. A girl holding a wooden horse. Dozens of them, standing in the last light, and when my outriders saw them, two of the six men bolted their horses and rode for the border and I let them go, because I suddenly could not remember a single reason a soldier should stay.

The remaining four looked to me. That was the cruelty of rank — it follows you into damnation.

"Captain," said Sergeant Voss, my left hand these nine years, a man whose wedding I had attended and whose daughter called me Uncle Aldous. He nodded at the torches on our saddles. "Orders?"

And here was the moment. Here was the whole of my life compressed into a sergeant's question and a bundle of pitch-soaked rag. Eleven villages in the ledger. One more line of ink.

"Voss," I said, "how many people live in the living settlements nearby? The ones that flicker only at dusk — where the survivors hide?"

He frowned. "Maybe two hundred. Scattered."

"And if we burn the houses, the Crown's accounting is satisfied. Two hundred souls added to the dead's queue." I looked down the lane at the miller, at the girl with the wooden horse, at the patient dark doorway of the shrine. "Or we open the stores, give them what provisions we can carry, point them east toward the Door, and report the villages already empty when we arrived."

Voss stared at me. Behind him, young Pellit had gone white. "Captain," Voss said carefully, "that's treason."

"Yes," I said. "I've been meaning to mention that."

What I did not say — what I could not have made him understand without sounding mad — was that kneeling felt closer than standing. That some instinct older than my commission knew the shape of what was coming for all of us, and wanted to meet it on its knees rather than its feet. I had spent my life believing kneelers were weak. It took a queue of the patient dead to teach me that kneeling is just how a man positions himself to be honest.

We opened the stores. We gave away the Crown's salt and hardtack and lamp-oil to people who emerged from flickering doorways with the wary faces of animals that have learned what fire means. An old woman pressed her forehead to my gauntlet and said, "Blessings of the Nine upon you, Captain," and the words went through me like a spear, because the Nine were silent, had been silent since before this woman lost her home, and she blessed me anyway, out of habit, out of hope, out of something sturdier than both.

"Take them east," I told Voss. "All of them. I'll follow with the map."

Because that was the other thing. The thing I had taken from the Chancellery archives before riding out, wrapped in oilcloth beneath my cloak, stolen with hands that shook for the first time since I was nineteen.

The Crown's map of the Door of Thirteen Turns.

They had always known where it was. That was the part that curdled me. Twelve years of hunting charm-sellers and prayer-mongers and desperate folk who whispered to silent skies, and the Chancellery had a map — annotated in the Chancellor's own hand — of the one exit from the country they had locked. They weren't afraid of the Door opening. They were afraid of being seen near it when it did.

I was bringing them their fear, gift-wrapped in penance.

* * *

Finding Tamsin Ravensworth was not difficult. Finding him had never been difficult; that was our private joke, his and mine, except he didn't know it was a joke. For six years I had chased him across three provinces — the charm-seller with the faded brand at his throat, the oath-breaker, the man whose file in my office had grown thicker than most heresy cases twice as old. Six years, and he had always slipped me by a day, a mile, a bribed gatekeeper. I told myself it was skill. Somewhere in the last three days I had begun to suspect the truth: that the gods had been hiding him. Keeping their debtor fresh. Saving him for the collection.

I found him at a crossroads shrine at dawn, of all places — a roofless thing with a dry fountain, where a small company had camped. I walked in alone, unarmed, my sword left with my horse, and every blade in that camp came up at once.

The priestess Odile I recognized from wanted circulars. The soldier Brannoc had the look of a man who had stopped expecting to survive the week. The girl Sefa held a knife too big for her. And there, rising slowly from beside the dead coals, wearing a peddler's coat over a branded throat, stood Tamsin Ravensworth, looking older than his file and tired past his years, and pointing a charm-charred dagger at my heart with a hand that did not shake.

"Hale," he said. "You're a long way from your jurisdiction."

"I've resigned my jurisdiction," I said. "Along with my pension, my reputation, and quite possibly my soul. Though I'm given to understand the soul is spoken for."

"Careful," Odile said quietly. "Witch-finders joke. It's in the training."

"I'm not joking, priestess. I'm surrendering." I knelt then — deliberately, in the cold mud of the shrine yard, before the man I had hunted half a decade — and I drew the oilcloth bundle from my cloak and laid it on the ground between us. I unwrapped it. The map caught the dawn: the Door drawn in the Chancellor's hand, the thirteen turns marked, the annotations in red ink detailing exactly which prayers would speed it and which seals would hold.

Tamsin looked at the map. Then at me. His face did something complicated — six years of running distilled into a single expression I couldn't read and didn't deserve to.

"You burned villages," he said. Not accusation. Arithmetic. A man counting.

"Eleven," I said. "I keep a ledger. I've kept it for twenty years, and I am done pretending ink makes it clean." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. Let them hear it. A hunter's honesty is worth nothing sealed in his chest. "I'm not asking for forgiveness. Forgiveness is for the forgiven, and I intend to stand in front of whatever wakes at that Door and take what's owed me like everyone else. I'm asking for a place in the queue. I'm asking to be a debtor among debtors."

Sefa whispered something to Brannoc. Odile hadn't moved, but her eyes had gone distant, listening — and I remembered the circulars, the rumors, that the disgraced priestess heard her god again.

It was Ravensworth who crouched, finally, and picked up the map. He studied it a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"The Chancellor's seal-hand. This is original." He laughed, short and bitter. "Six years, Hale. Six years you chased me, and now you crawl out of the mud carrying the one thing we need, and I'm supposed to believe the gods sent you?"

"I don't presume to know what the gods sent," I said. "I only know what I carried."

He stood there a long moment, wind moving the dead shrine's weeds, dawn strengthening behind him like a decision being made somewhere far above us all. Then he put out his hand — not to help me up, not yet, but to clasp my forearm, soldier-fashion, the grip of men who have decided to trust each other precisely as far as the next atrocity.

"Get up, Captain," he said. "You'll slow us down kneeling. And Vael—" he touched the faded brand at his throat, and his eyes went briefly elsewhere, listening to something I couldn't hear, "—Vael says the ledger cuts both ways. Yours counts too. Toward the Door, or against it. We find out at the thirteenth turn."

I rose. My knees ached. Everything about me ached, and yet I felt lighter than I had in twenty years, the way a man feels after setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Behind Ravensworth, Odile had gone very still, her lips parted, her gaze fixed on the eastern horizon where the sky was doing something sky ought not do — rippling, faintly, like the surface of deep water disturbed from below.

"Tamsin," she said. "The moon. Count it."

He counted. We all did, looking up at the pale ghost of it hanging in the brightening dawn.

Twelve turns behind it. One to go.
