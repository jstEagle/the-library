## 8. The Twelfth Turn

The Door of Thirteen Turns did not look like a door.

It looked like a mistake a mason had made and then, rather than admit it, had built an entire cliff face around. A seam of pale stone ran up the rock in a slow spiral — thirteen turns of it, the old stories said, though I counted twelve before my eyes watered and gave up. No handle. No hinges. No frame. Just that seam, catching the last grey light like a scar on the world's knuckle.

"Charming," Odile said beside me. "All this way for a crack."

"All this way *because of* a crack," Brannoc corrected. He was walking easier now than he had any right to; Sefa's shrine-work had closed his wounds to angry red seams, though he still favored his left side like a man nursing a grudge against his own ribs. "Doors are only cracks that decided to be useful."

Sefa said nothing. She'd said very little since the rite at the flickering shrine, since she'd half-returned her brother to whatever waited between prayers. She carried him now the way you carry a debt — close, carefully, always aware of the interest accruing. Her hand rested on the little bundle at her chest where the bone-white ember of him slept.

And behind us, breathing hard from the climb, came Captain Hale with the Crown's map folded against his heart like a confession.

We stood in a bowl of stone at the top of the erased country's spine, and below us the land did its dusk trick: villages flickering into being like lamps lit one by one by a careful hand. Smoke rose from chimneys that no surveyor had ever drawn. Somewhere down there, the dead were queueing again at their shuttered shrines, patient as they had been for twelve years. Patient as gods.

I felt Vael the way you feel weather changing. Not words yet. Pressure.

"Well," I said. "Nobody's guarding it. That's either wonderful or the worst news of the week."

The seam in the rock began to glow.

*Worst news,* said Vael, in the voice that lived behind my sternum now, that had taken up residence there like a tenant who paid rent in dread. *You're learning.*

The light spread along all thirteen turns of the spiral — yes, thirteen, I counted them this time, because counting seemed suddenly very important — and the air above the seam folded outward like cloth being turned back from a bed. What stood in the opening was not a doorway onto anywhere. It was a doorway onto *them*.

I have seen gods answer prayers. I have seen a dead brother sit up wrong. I have watched a war-god take a soldier's scream and make it into a banner. None of it prepared me for seeing them stand still and *look* at us.

There were seven. Or there was one wearing seven shapes; the light wouldn't let my eyes settle the question. One was a woman made of harvest-rust and patience. One was a tower of small bones, each one labeled. One was Brannoc's war-god, red as a wound that had learned ambition. One was Odile's — the Quiet Sister, the god of thresholds nobody crosses alone, and when she saw her, Odile made a sound like a hinge.

At the center stood something tall and burning and familiar, and it wore the shape of a forge-fire given shoulders, and it looked at me with the particular attention of a creditor finding his debtor after twelve years.

*Debtors,* the seven said together, or the one said seven times. The voice didn't come through the ears so much as through the memory of every prayer we'd ever whispered. *You have walked far. The door opens on the thirteenth turn. You stand at the twelfth. Hear the terms.*

"I already know the terms," I said, because if I didn't interrupt a god quickly I never would. "One whole self. Paid entire. The door opens and stays open, and everyone inside walks out free." I'd pieced it together from visions and Sefa's bargain and Hale's map margins, which were annotated in a witch-finder's cramped hand with things the Crown had learned and burned the learners for knowing. "Or the door seals on the thirteenth turn, and this country stays erased forever. Everyone inside it. Forever is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

*You do know the terms.* Something moved in the fire-shape that might have been approval. Might have been hunger. *One payment, whole. A self surrendered entire — name, memory, will, the shape of the soul itself — freely given. The gods' silence was bought with a country. It can be un-bought with a person. The arithmetic is old and it does not bend.*

"And partial payments?" I asked. My mouth was dry. "Sefa bargained. There are loopholes—"

*Sefa bargained,* the gods said, and the girl flinched, *and bled for it, and got half. The door is not a prayer. The door is the lock itself. Half measures leave locks half-open, and half-open doors are how crowns get in.*

Silence, except for the wind coming down off the spine of the world, carrying woodsmoke from villages that officially did not exist.

Then Odile stepped forward.

I knew what she was going to do about four seconds before she did it. Twelve years of selling charms to a godless city teaches you to read people the way witch-finders read brands — the set of the shoulders, the breath before the word. She squared those shoulders. She took that breath. And I thought: *no. No, absolutely not, I will drag you out of here by the hair, priestess, I swear it on a god I'm currently negotiating with.*

"I volunteer," Odile said clearly.

Her Quiet Sister inclined what might generously have been called a head. *Named and willing. This is acceptable—*

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Odile turned, and her face did the thing it did when she was about to be reasonable at me, which was infinitely worse than when she was unreasonable. "Tamsin. Look at them." She gestured at our ragged procession — Brannoc leaning on his sword, Sefa with her ember-brother, Hale standing apart with his map and his guilt. "Look at *all* of them. Someone has to pay. It was always going to be someone, and I've been ready for twelve years. Do you know what I did during the silence? I kept saying mass to an empty room. Every dawn. For twelve years. Because if I stopped, then it wasn't faith anymore, it was just habit, and I couldn't—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through anyway. "A whole self, Tamsin. Mine is mostly used up anyway. I'm a disgraced priestess with bad knees and a talent for forgery. What am I saving it for?"

"For yourself," I said. "That's what it's *for*. That's the entire point of having one."

"That's very mercenary of you."

"I was a mercenary. We contain multitudes."

"Tamsin." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice, and the lowering of it was the cruelest thing she could have done, because it meant the next words were just for me and not for the gods. "You've been paying in installments your whole life. Charms sold under the table. Oaths half-hidden. Survival scraped together coin by coin. Let me be the one who settles an account *whole*. Let me do one clean thing."

And there it was. Love, weaponized. Aimed at me by the one person who knew exactly where all my soft places were, because she'd been stitching them shut for months on the road. She wasn't asking permission. She was asking me to stand aside and call it mercy.

Behind her, the fire-shape of Vael burned patiently, and I understood suddenly that the gods had known this would happen. That they'd arranged the terms knowing someone would volunteer, knowing love would do their bargaining for them, because love always volunteers first and loudest and most fatally. It was the oldest trick in theology.

"You don't get to," I said.

Odile blinked. "Excuse me?"

"You heard me. You don't get to volunteer. It's not yours to give." I turned to face the seven-and-one, and my legs were shaking, and I made myself stand like a man selling charms under a witch-finder's nose anyway, because that was the only courage I owned and I was finally going to spend all of it. "Here's my counter-offer. And before anyone says the door isn't a prayer — I know. But Sefa proved the arithmetic bends. She bled for the bend. So hear me out, and if you don't like it, seal the door on my head."

*Tamsin,* Vael warned.

"You want a whole self," I said. "Fine. But ask yourselves what a self actually *is*. Is it the name? I've got three — Tamsin Ravensworth, Tam of the Stalls, the branded man the witch-finders hunt. Take whichever one you like; names are coats. Is it the memories? Half mine are nightmares with better production values. Is it the will?" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "My will has been for sale since I was sixteen years old. First it went to you, Vael. Then it went to survival. I have never once, in my entire life, owned the whole stock."

The gods were very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"So here's what I'm offering," I said. "Not a whole self, because I'll be honest with you — I don't think I have one. I have pieces, and some of them belong to other people now, and you can't collect debts on property that's been honestly transferred. Odile holds a piece. Brannoc holds a piece. That girl and her brother hold a piece. Captain Hale, gods help us all, holds a piece. Ask *them*. Go ahead. Try to collect a whole self out of a man who spent twelve years giving himself away in installments to keep other debtors breathing."

*I could take it anyway,* Vael said softly. The fire leaned closer. *Freely given means freely taken, when the giver stands inside the terms.*

"Try," I said, "and see what's actually left when you dig. And see what the others do. Because I promise you, Vael, I promise you on every charm I ever sold to every desperate soul in Corvane — if you take me whole against my will, these four are going to stand right here and refuse to walk through, and your beautiful open door seals with everyone inside it anyway. You didn't gather debtors, old friend. You gathered witnesses. And witnesses talk."

The fire-shape went still in a way fire should not be able to go still.

And then — I will swear this until the day whatever's left of me forgets it — the burning thing made a sound. Low. Rough. Twelve years of silence cracking open down the middle.

*You always were the worst bargainer I ever swore,* Vael said, *and the only honest one.*

"The thirteenth turn begins at moonrise," the seven said together, and the seam in the rock pulsed once, twice, like a heart remembering its rhythm. "Come to the door when it starts, debtor. Bring whatever you believe a self to be. We will count it."

The light folded inward. The gods were gone, or were cliffs again, or had always been both.

Odile grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise. "What did you just do?"

I looked up at the seam, twelve turns glowing faintly now, waiting for the thirteenth. Somewhere below us, a village that didn't exist lit another lamp.

"I opened negotiations," I said. "Moonrise is in an hour. Somebody hold my coat — I'm about to find out how much of me there ever was."
