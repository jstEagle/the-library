## 9. The Price of Survival

The Door of Thirteen Turns did not look like a door. It looked like an apology.

It stood in the middle of a field that no longer existed, a ring of thirteen stone arches nested inside one another like the ribs of something that had swallowed itself, and every surface of it was worn smooth — not by weather, but by hands. Thousands of years of desperate people pressing their palms against stone, asking to leave, and I understood with a sick lurch that most of them never had. The door wasn't built for escape. It was built to make the wanting of escape bearable.

The gods were there when we arrived. All of them. Not in bodies — they didn't bother with bodies anymore — but as pressure, as weight, the way a storm announces itself through the ache in old bones before the first drop falls. Vael was the heaviest. Vael was always the heaviest.

*You came,* he said, and his voice was the voice of the brand on my arm, twelve years faded and suddenly scalding again.

"You knew I would."

*I hoped. Hope is not knowledge. Even for us.* A pause that tasted like ash. *Especially for us.*

Odile stood at my left shoulder, her priestess's shawl gone gray with road dust, and Sefa at my right with her brother's cold hand clutched in hers because she refused to let go of him even now, even here. Brannoc leaned on Hale — leaned on *Hale*, which would have been funny any other week — his war-god's wound bound in a witch-finder's cloak. Behind us, the flickering villages of the erased country stretched back toward the horizon, all those shuttered shrines, all those patient dead, all those living people who had prayed into twelve years of silence and gotten nothing but the echo of their own voices.

All of them debtors now. All of them sealed in if the door closed.

*The terms are simple,* Vael said, and around me the other gods stirred their agreement like sediment rising in disturbed water. *One whole self, freely given. One soul surrendered entire — name, memory, history, self. Paid by one, and the door opens for all.*

"And if nobody pays?"

*Then the thirteenth turn completes, and the door seals, and this country remains what the Crown made it: nowhere. Forever.* He said it gently, which was somehow worse. Vael had never learned cruelty. He'd only ever learned honesty, and honesty at this volume was indistinguishable from cruelty.

Odile stepped forward. "I'll pay it."

"No," I said.

"Tamsin—"

"I said no." My voice came out flat and hard, the mercenary's voice, the one I'd spent twelve years building like armor plate over everything soft in me. "Your god took your order from you. Your vows. Your name in the rolls of your temple. You've paid enough just surviving that. I'm not letting them take the rest."

"Then who?" She turned on me, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Brannoc can barely stand. Sefa is a child. Hale—" she faltered on the name, still not used to saying it without spitting, "—Hale owes debts to a Crown that isn't here to collect them. Who else is there, Tamsin? Who else has a whole self worth a door?"

I looked down at my arm. At the faded brand of the oath I'd sworn at nineteen, kneeling in a shrine that smelled of tallow and iron, promising Vael my blade and my life and meaning it so completely that the mark had burned itself into the bone beneath the skin.

Twelve years ago the gods went silent, and everyone said the oaths were broken. Everyone said we were free. And I'd believed it, right up until I didn't, right up until the night a child's prayer got answered in a market stall in Corvane and I felt the brand wake up like a scar remembering fire.

Everyone said the oath was broken. It hadn't been. It had been deferred. Deferred interest, accruing all this time, and now the note had come due.

"Me," I said. "But not the way you're offering it."

*There is no other way,* Vael said.

"There's always another way. That's what you taught me, back when you were allowed to teach things. You're a god of contracts, Vael. Of terms. Of the letter and the spirit and the gap between them where mercy lives." I turned to face the place where his weight pressed hardest against the world, where the air itself seemed to kneel. "So let's read the contract carefully. You asked for one whole self. You didn't ask whose. And you didn't say what counts as *whole*."

The silence that followed had texture. The other gods went very still, the way creditors go still when a debtor starts reading fine print aloud.

*Speak plainly,* Vael said, and there was something new in his voice. Something almost afraid.

"I'm offering partial payment," I said. "Not the whole man. Part of him."

*Partial payment seals the door on no one.*

"Partial payment opens it for everyone or I walk away and we all find out how much a god wants his money." I held up my hand before Odile could speak, before Brannoc could groan himself upright to argue, before Sefa could do something brave and stupid and sixteen. "Listen to me. All of you. You want a whole self because a whole self is clean. No arguments, no negotiation, no risk of getting cheated. But you've been silent for twelve years, and before that you were fed on fear and flattery and burnt offerings, and I don't think — I honestly don't think — anyone has ever offered you an honest bargain. Not once. Not in all the centuries. They've groveled or they've fled or they've thrown their children into fires to buy your favor. Nobody ever stood in front of you and said: here is what I have, here is what it cost me, here is why I'm giving it, and here is what I'm keeping, and I'm keeping it because I'm human and humans keep things."

The wind moved through the thirteen arches. Somewhere behind us, in the flickering dusk, the dead at their shuttered shrines had stopped queueing and turned, all of them, toward the door.

"So here's my offer." I pulled back my sleeve. The brand caught the last light, faded silver going gold at the edges, waking. "Twelve years ago I swore you my blade. And then you went silent, and I kept swearing it anyway — every day I woke up alive when I shouldn't have been, every charm I sold to someone who needed a lie to hold onto, every night I survived in Corvane under the noses of men who burn people like me. I survived, Vael. That was the debt. Survival itself was what your oath purchased, and survival came due, and I paid it in installments for twelve years without knowing I was paying."

*Yes,* he breathed.

"The man who did that surviving — the smuggler, the liar, the mercenary who priced other people's hope by the ounce — that man is yours. Take him. Every hardened year of him. Every instinct that kept him breathing and hollowed him out doing it. Take the whole twelve years, complete, the way you wanted a whole self. Name the part of me that was Tamsin Ravensworth, soldier-for-hire, seller of outlawed charms, and I will give it up freely, gladly, as payment rendered."

"And keep?" Odile whispered beside me, though I think she already knew.

"Keep the boy who knelt in the shrine," I said. "Keep whatever's underneath the armor. I don't know what's down there anymore — maybe nothing, maybe something worse than nothing — but it's mine, and I'm keeping it, and that's the bargain. Partial payment. Honest terms. Take it or leave it, god of contracts."

For a long moment nothing happened. The thirteenth turn began — I felt it in my teeth, in the stones underfoot, in the slow grinding rotation of the innermost arch as the moon hauled itself over the horizon fat and final — and still the gods said nothing, and Odile's hand found mine and gripped it hard enough to hurt, and Sefa was crying without any sound at all.

Then Vael laughed.

It was not a sound I'd heard before. Gods don't laugh the way we do; theirs is a thing that happens to the world rather than in it. The grass shivered. The dead at their shrines bowed their heads. And the pressure of him — that storm-weight, that creditor's silence — eased, the way a fist eases, the way a debt eases.

*Twelve hundred years,* he said softly, *since anyone bargained with us instead of begging. Twelve hundred years since anyone offered payment with love in it instead of terror.* His attention settled over me like a mantle, heavy and warm and terrible. *The gods accept. Freely witnessed, freely given, freely taken. The mercenary is ours. The rest of you — walk out.*

"What happens to him?" Sefa asked. "The part you're taking?"

*He stays with us,* Vael said. *Someone should remember what it cost to survive. That is a prayer too, little sister. Perhaps the truest one we've ever been offered.*

The innermost arch completed its turn. Light came through it — not moonlight, not godlight, but plain gray honest dawn light from somewhere on the other side of everything — and the thirteen rings began to open outward, one after another, like a fist unclenching after twelve years.

I felt it start to happen then. The taking. It didn't hurt, which was the worst part; it was more like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was carrying, except the pack was made of scars and calluses and the exact weight of every hard decision, and as each year of the twelve lifted away I could feel the shape of myself changing underneath, lighter, stranger, a man with room in him for things he'd walled off so long he couldn't remember what they were.

The last thing the mercenary did — the very last, as Vael gathered him up like a debt finally honored — was look back at Odile. Not at me. At her.

And he smiled.

*Come,* Vael said, and his voice had gentled into something I had no word for yet, some tone that belonged to whoever I was becoming. *The door is open. Dawn doesn't wait.*

Odile squeezed my hand — my hand, still mine, still callused but differently callused now, calluses I couldn't account for — and together we turned toward the light.

Behind us, faint as a bell rung once in an empty house, the mercenary's voice said goodbye.

Ahead, through the first arch, the dawn was waiting to learn who I was.
