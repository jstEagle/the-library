## 10. A Godless Dawn

I have officiated at more funerals than weddings, which is the occupational hazard of priesthood, and I will tell you what I learned from all of them: the worst part is never the dying. The worst part is the silence afterward, when everyone stands around not knowing what the world is supposed to look like now.

That was what walking through the Door of Thirteen Turns felt like. A funeral for a country that had come back to life on the other side.

The last thing I saw before the threshold was Tamsin Ravensworth standing in Vael's light with his hand outstretched like a man haggling over fish, offering twelve years of himself — the mercenary, the survivor, the man who'd learned to flinch — and I was screaming at him not to, and Brannoc was holding me back because someone had to, and Sefa had her face buried in her brother's shoulder so she wouldn't have to watch. And then the god *laughed*. Not cruelly. The way an old friend laughs when you finally say something true after years of lying politely.

And then there was light, and then there was grass, and then there was morning.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the crossing, though memory is a suspect witness now, since half of mine was spent praying to a god who'd gone deaf:

One moment we were inside the erased country — the flickering villages, the dead queueing patiently at shuttered shrines, the sky the color of a bruise that never healed. The next, we were standing in a valley that smelled of woodsmoke and wet earth and thyme, the ordinary smell of home, except home had been a rumor for twelve years and here it was, solid as a stone wall.

Behind us, the Door stood open in a hillside that no map would ever acknowledge. It did not look like a door. It looked like a wound in the air shaped like a doorway, and through it we could still see Corvane's rooftops, the Crown's gray banners, the witch-finders' towers. The whole prison, visible through the keyhole.

People came through in ones and twos. Some carried children. Some carried nothing but themselves, which is heavier, if you've ever tried it. The dead did not come through — they had their own arrangements, made long ago at shrines we'd passed — but several of them stood at the threshold and bowed to us as we left, which I am choosing to interpret as forgiveness rather than irony.

Sefa came through holding her brother's hand. He was wrong still — quieter than a boy should be, his eyes the color of candle smoke — but he was *hers*, and she had bled and bargained and refused to give him back twice, and the gods had learned, apparently, that some debts you renegotiate or you don't get paid at all.

Brannoc came through leaning on a spear he'd broken over a Crown outrider's helmet, grinning through the blood on his teeth, saying something about how his war-god owed him a very good afterlife and he intended to collect it slowly.

And then, last of all, Tamsin.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, because I don't think anyone else saw it clearly and someone ought to bear witness. It's the priestess in me. Old habits.

He stepped through the Door and he was — lighter. That sounds mad. But I watched a man walk out of a doorway carrying exactly the same body, the same scarred knuckles, the same faded brand on his wrist that had burned white-hot during the negotiation and now showed only a pale seam, like a scar from a wound that had decided, finally, to heal.

But the weight was gone.

You spend enough years hearing confessions, you learn where people carry themselves. Tamsin had always carried himself like a man walking past a guard post: shoulders drawn in, eyes doing arithmetic about exits. The charm-seller's posture. The fugitive's posture. Twelve years of being hunted had taught his spine a permanent apology.

Walking out of that door, he straightened. Not all at once — vertebra by vertebra, like a rope uncoiling — and his face did something I'd never seen it do in all our weeks on the road.

It went blank.

Not empty. Blank the way a page is blank before a sentence. The mercenary had been paid out of him — every hard trick, every flinch, every year of selling outlawed charms under the noses of witch-finders while pretending he didn't believe in anything — and what remained was a man roughly my own age with a stranger's calm and no name he remembered owning.

He looked at me. He looked at the valley. He looked at his own hands, turning them over with the frank curiosity of a child examining a caught fish.

"Do you know me?" he asked.

And here is the thing I will defend until my own god collects me, wherever she's got to: I said yes.

"You're Tamsin," I said. "Tamsin Ravensworth. You sold charms in Corvane for eleven years and were terrible at haggling and worse at lying. You once gave a girl named Merrin a luck-charm for free because her father was dying, and it worked, and you never took credit for it. You complain about the cold and you give away your cloak anyway. You are afraid of deep water and you jumped into it twice to pull people out."

His face moved through several expressions, none of which had names yet. "I don't remember any of that," he said.

"No," I agreed. "But we do. Memory isn't only what you carry, love. Sometimes it's what other people carry for you." I took his arm. "Consider us your ledger."

* * *

We walked down into the valley as the sun cleared the ridge, and I will confess something now that I confessed to no god, because my god was busy listening instead of speaking:

I had expected grief. I had braced for it — the grief of a homeland returned but ruined, of twelve lost years, of a man I had — well. Of a man I cared for, returned to me subtracted. I had rehearsed my mourning on the road, in the dark, the way you rehearse a difficult sermon.

Instead what I felt was absurdly, embarrassingly close to joy.

Because the country was *there*. Not on any map — Hale had checked, bless his converted heart, spreading the Crown's master charts across the grass while refugees stepped around him, and the land where we stood appeared as blank parchment, an absence, a cartographic apology. But under our feet it was real. Thyme and woodsmoke and wet earth. A river that remembered its name even if no atlas did. Ruins that were not ruins — villages that had flickered for twelve years in the erased country now stood solid in the morning, doors hanging open, tables still set from the day the borders were swallowed. Dust everywhere, and weeds in the hearths, and not one living soul who hadn't walked out of that door behind us.

Home, rebuilt not by gods or crowns but by foot traffic. By the people who prayed alongside a stranger and then walked beside him.

Brannoc planted his broken spear in the mud of the first village square and declared it a flagpole. Sefa found her family's house — or the shape of it; her brother drifted through the rooms touching things without recognition, and she followed him, narrating, telling him what each object was, building his memory aloud the way you build a fire. Hale sat on a milestone with the Crown's maps burning in a small respectful fire and did not pray, because he didn't know how, but kept glancing at the sky like a man waiting to be struck, and when he wasn't struck, looked quietly astonished about it.

And Tamsin sat on the church steps — our church, Odile's old church, its bell still hanging, its god's niche empty as a cupped hand — and watched the survivors move through the streets below, and I sat down beside him.

"They're quiet," he said. "The gods."

"Yes."

"Is that bad? Silence was bad before."

"It's a different silence," I said. "Silence before was a held breath. This is — attention. They paid out everything they had to open that door, I think. Or they gave up demanding. Either way—" I gestured at the valley, the smoke beginning to rise from chimneys people were already unbricking, the sound of a hammer somewhere, absurd and holy. "They're listening now. First time in history a congregation has had the advantage."

He considered that. His new mind turned things over slowly and honestly, without the mercenary's instinct to find the angle, and I found I loved that about him already, this version, this beginner.

"What do I owe?" he asked. "Vael. If the bargain was only part — what's left?"

"I don't think it works like that anymore. I think you owe the living now. Which is heavier, but at least they'll haggle."

He laughed. It was an unfamiliar laugh, one he hadn't used before, and it cracked open his whole face, and I thought: *there you are.* Whoever you turn out to be. There you are.

Below us, Merrin — the girl with the luck-charm, grown now, a woman of twenty with her father's chin — was directing strangers toward the well, and she looked up at the church steps and waved at Tamsin without hesitation, without confusion, because she remembered him even if he didn't, and he raised his hand back slowly, wondering, and I saw the first new memory take root behind his eyes like a green shoot through rubble.

The bell in the tower, untouched for thirteen years, chose that moment to shift in the wind and ring. Once. Softly. No god rang it. Just wind, just metal, just morning.

Everyone in the village square stopped and looked up.

Nobody knelt. Nobody prayed. Somebody laughed, and somebody started hammering again, and Sefa's brother pointed at the bell and said his first clear word in either life: "*Again.*"

Tamsin leaned toward me, and his shoulder touched mine, unnamed and unbowed, a man with no debt and no country and no history except ours, and he said:

"So. What happens now?"

And I, priestess of a silent god, in a country that existed on no map but our memories, in the first dawn of a world where the gods listened instead of spoke — I smiled, and told him the truest thing I knew.

"Now," I said, "we begin."
