## 1. The Price of Forgetting

The night market in Vell smelled of wet wool and fried onions and fear. Felix Zhou had lived here long enough that the smell had stopped bothering her — mostly — but tonight it clung to everything like a second skin, and she knew exactly why.

The Ministry had posted new notices at every gate. She'd passed three on her way in: black ink on gray paper, the official seal pressed so hard it had torn through in places, as if whoever stamped them wanted to leave a bruise on the city itself.

MAGIC IS THEFT. THEFT IS SILENCE. REPORT ALL CHARMWORK TO YOUR DISTRICT CLERK.

Which was funny, in a way Felix did not feel like laughing about, because theft was her trade and charmwork was her inventory, and the Ministry was her best customer.

She moved through the crowd with the unhurried gait of someone who belonged nowhere in particular. A woman selling candied ginger caught her eye and looked away first. A pair of dockhands argued over dice. Somewhere a fiddle played badly, and no one minded, because bad music was one of the few things left in Vell you could have for free.

Felix wore her hair tucked under a plain wool cap and her hands bare — always bare, for this part. Gloves made the lift clumsy, and clumsy got you noticed, and getting noticed in Vell meant getting reported, and getting reported meant a visit from the Ministry of Silence, whose clerks didn't ask questions so much as they asked *everything*, in a room with no windows, until you told them things you hadn't known you knew.

She spotted the widow before she spotted the charm.

That was how it always went with Felix. The charms were small — a bead of amber glass on a cord, or a knuckle of riverstone, or in the expensive cases a sliver of bone — and they sat against the skin where they were hard to see. But grief had a shape. Grief changed how a person stood. This woman stood like a tentpole with the canvas half-collapsed, shoulders drawn in around something heavy, and she kept touching her collarbone the way people touch a wound they've forgotten isn't there anymore.

The widow was buying eels. That detail would stay with Felix later, the way useless details do. She haggled over eels with a fishmonger who was clearly letting her win, and her sleeve slipped back just enough, and there it was: an amber charm on a leather cord, worn soft at the knot, glowing faintly at its heart the way a coal glows after everyone else has gone to bed.

A memory-charm. Old work, too — pre-Ban, or close enough to it. The glass had that deep amber color you couldn't fake, the color of a memory that had been steeping in a person for years.

Felix drifted closer. Bumped the widow's elbow — gently, apologetically, *oh, forgive me* — and in the half-second of shared flustered space between two strangers in a crowd, her ring finger found the cord and her thumb found the knot and the knot, worn soft, gave without a fight.

By the time the widow turned around to glare at nothing, Felix was three steps gone and the charm was warm in her palm, humming like a trapped fly.

She should have felt worse about it than she did.

* * *

Here is what Felix told herself, every night, in the litany she performed while she cleaned her tools and counted her coin: *I only take what hurts.*

It wasn't strictly true. It was true often enough to be load-bearing, which in Vell was the same thing.

People came to memory-thieves the way they came to surgeons — reluctantly, secretly, and only when the pain got bigger than the fear. And most thieves weren't choosy. Most thieves would take your first kiss if the price was right, would hollow out your wedding day, your mother's laugh, the taste of bread from a bakery that burned down ten years ago. There were buyers for all of it. Nostalgia paid better than grief, gram for gram, because rich people wanted happy memories and poor people only ever wanted to stop hurting.

Felix took the painful ones. The ones that kept their owners awake. The widow's amber charm — she could feel it through her shirt pocket, a low dull ache, like holding someone else's bruise — was almost certainly grief. Almost certainly something the widow had tried to set down a hundred times and couldn't, because memory doesn't work that way, not honestly, not without magic, and magic in Vell was contraband.

So Felix stole it. And tomorrow she would sell it, legally, to a Ministry clerk, because the Ministry bought confiscated charmwork at fair rates and never asked where it came from, which was either hypocrisy or policy depending on how cynical you were feeling, and Felix had been feeling cynical for about five years now.

And then some clerk in a gray coat would file the widow's worst day into a vault beneath the Ministry tower, where it would sit forever, unremembered by anyone, doing harm to no one.

That was the story Felix told herself. It held up fine as long as she didn't look directly at it.

* * *

The Ministry's district office stayed open late, because fear keeps good hours. It occupied a former countinghouse near Tanner's Row, all stone and iron and windows fogged from the inside — they kept their rooms warm, the clerks, warmer than anyone in the market could afford, as if comfort were a perk of enforcement.

Felix knew the door clerk by his schedule, if not his name. He worked the evening window Tuesdays and Fridays, and he had a habit of tapping his pen twice before he wrote anything down, and he had never once looked at her face for longer than it took to confirm she wasn't someone he needed to arrest. She liked that about him. It was practically a friendship.

"Evening," she said, setting three charms on the counter's brass tray. Two were hers from earlier in the week — a riverstone, a chip of green glass. The third was the widow's amber, and she put it down last, and her hand hesitated over it for maybe a quarter of a heartbeat, which was a quarter longer than usual.

The clerk tapped his pen twice. "Provenance?"

"Finds." Felix shrugged. "Clearing out a dead aunt's house up on Kettle Street. You know how it is."

"I don't," said the clerk, who was perhaps twenty-five and owned, as far as Felix could tell, four identical coats. He picked up each charm with tongs, held it to a small brass instrument that clicked softly, and noted something in a ledger with handwriting so neat it looked printed. "Glass, minor charge, common sentiment. Glass, minor charge, common sentiment." Then he reached the amber one and stopped.

His pen hovered. Tapped once. Tapped twice.

"This one's heavier," he said.

"It's older," said Felix. "Old work holds more."

He looked at her then — actually looked, for the first time in eleven transactions — and Felix felt the particular cold that comes of being seen by someone whose job is seeing. His eyes were pale and patient and entirely without curiosity, which was somehow worse than if they'd been suspicious.

"You look tired," he said.

"I'm always tired."

"Mm." He set the amber charm in a felt-lined tray with its sisters and slid the tray into a slot in the counter. Somewhere behind the wall, something mechanical swallowed it — Felix heard the click of locks, plural. "Payment's on the slip. Same terms."

The slip printed itself out of a slot with a sound like a cough. Eleven marks. Fair rates, like she said. Enough to eat for a week and patch the roof leak and maybe, if she was reckless, buy a bottle of the plum brandy she pretended not to like.

"Thank you for your civic cooperation," the clerk said, in the tone of a man reading off the inside of his own eyelids.

"And thank *you*," Felix said, "for keeping the city safe from sad widows."

She regretted it before it finished leaving her mouth. But the clerk only blinked, slow as a lizard, and tapped his pen twice, and said, "Sentiment is how it starts," and turned to the next person in line, and Felix walked out into the cold with her coin and decided not to think about what he'd meant by that.

* * *

She didn't go straight home. That was rule two, right after *never lift from anyone who knows your name*: vary your route, double back twice, be a rumor instead of a person. So she looped through the fish stalls and cut along the canal and stood for a while watching the lamplighter do the bridge lamps, his pole rising and falling with the patience of a man rowing a very slow boat.

Vell at night, from the middle of the bridge, almost looked like a city worth saving. Lamplight on black water. The fiddle still going somewhere, better now, or maybe she'd just gotten used to it. Smoke from a hundred chimneys standing straight up in the windless air like the city was burning incense to something.

To what? Felix wondered. Nobody prayed in Vell. Not really, not out loud. The gods had gone quiet a hundred years ago — that was history, that was schoolroom stuff, the Silence, the Ban, the great shrugging-off when humanity looked at heaven and heaven didn't answer, so humanity locked heaven's leftovers in vaults and posted notices and got on with the business of being cold. Whatever the old prayers used to rise toward, they rose into nothing now. The smoke just went up and thinned out and became weather.

Still. People kept little shrines anyway. Under floorboards, in cupboards, behind loose bricks — Felix had found dozens over five years of stealing, little niches with a candle stub and a carved figure with its face worn smooth. Not worship, exactly. More like writing letters to someone you know has moved away and won't write back.

She touched her shirt pocket, out of habit, and found it empty, and remembered the amber charm sitting in a felt tray behind three locks, and felt the guilt arrive right on schedule, punctual as the clerk.

*I take what hurts,* she told herself. *I take what hurts, and I sell it to the people who hoard it, and nobody bleeds.*

Except that wasn't quite the litany. The real litany, the one under the one, was simpler and less flattering: *I'm good at this.* She was. Five years, hundreds of lifts, and she'd never been caught, never been named, never left a mark on anyone that anyone could trace back to her. In a city built on forgetting, Felix Zhou was the best at it, and being the best at something is a terrible reason to keep doing it and also, frequently, the only one available.

The lamplighter finished the bridge lamps and moved on. The water went dark again, polite about it.

That was when Felix noticed the stranger.

* * *

He was across the market, near the spice stall, and he was doing the one thing nobody in Vell ever did, which was looking directly at her.

Not glancing. Not the flick-and-away of a pickpocket sizing her up or a neighbor wondering whether they recognized her. Looking. Standing still in a moving crowd, the way a rock stands still in a river, with his whole face aimed at her like she was something written down that he was taking the trouble to read.

He was young — younger than her, maybe, though it was hard to tell; there was something about him that made age seem like a costume he'd put on for the occasion. Brown coat, unremarkable. Dark hair. Ordinary in every feature except the stillness, and the way the market light seemed to find him even though he stood in shadow, as if the lamps nearest him were burning a little harder than the others.

Felix's hand went, without asking her permission, to her empty pocket.

He smiled. Small, rueful, like an apology offered in advance. And then he raised one hand — not waving, not beckoning, just open, palm out, the way you'd show a frightened animal you were unarmed — and mouthed something across thirty feet of noisy market that Felix read clearly without hearing:

*You dropped something.*

Her pocket was empty. Her pockets were never empty. She checked them compulsively the way other people checked locks.

She looked down at her hands. Looked back up.

He was gone. Not walking away — gone, the way a candle is gone when you blow it out, leaving the shape of the light behind. The crowd flowed into the space where he'd been and closed over it, and the spice stall man kept weighing saffron, and somewhere the fiddle sawed on, and Felix Zhou stood alone on a bridge in the middle of her city with her heart going like a bird in a box.

The lamps near where he'd stood were still burning harder than the others. She was almost sure of it.

She told herself it was a trick of the eyes, a tired thief's brain stitching meaning out of nothing. She told herself she'd imagined the way the air tasted afterward — faintly warm, faintly sweet, like the air above a banked fire, like the air in the half-second after a struck match.

She walked home fast, varying her route, doubling back, and all the rules did absolutely nothing against the thing following her, which wasn't a person and therefore couldn't be lost.

In her shirt pocket, where no charm had been since the bridge, something small and warm weighed almost nothing and hummed like a trapped fly.

Felix didn't reach in to check. Some part of her understood, with the cold clarity of a woman who has spent five years handling other people's memories, that whatever waited in that pocket had been waiting a great deal longer than one night —

— and that it knew her name.
