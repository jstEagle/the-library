## 2. Charm for a Dying Man

The lamplighter found Felix at the bottom of Tanner's Stair, where the city's smell changed from salt to rot and the gaslights gave up pretending to work.

She'd been counting her take from the night market — eleven marks and a brass button someone had paid her by mistake, or as an insult, she hadn't decided — when his shadow fell across her ledger. He was leaning on the stair rail like the rail was the only thing in Vell holding him upright. His uniform coat had once been Ministry-issue gray, back before the lamplighters' guild absorbed them, before even that, maybe. The buttons were tarnished green. His hands were stained with lamp oil so old it had become part of him.

"You're the memory-thief," he said.

Felix closed her ledger. "I'm a charm-seller. Antiques, mostly."

"Everyone on the Stair says you're the thief." He coughed, and the cough went on long enough that Felix looked away out of politeness and saw, at the top of the stairs, the last working gaslight of the district guttering over its own reflection in a puddle. "They say you only take the bad ones. The ones people want gone."

*People say a lot of things,* she almost said. Instead she said, "What do you want gone?"

He came down two steps, slowly, each one a negotiation. Up close he was younger than his coat suggested — forty, maybe forty-five, with a lamplighter's windburned face and eyes gone yellow at the edges. Sick, then. Badly sick. "My name's Corvin Hale," he said. "I light the lamps along the Embankment. Have done for twenty-two years."

"Congratulations."

He smiled like she'd meant it kindly. "I've got a daughter. Mira. She's seven." He fumbled inside his coat and produced something wrapped in oilcloth: a photograph, soft-edged from handling, of a girl missing both front teeth and grinning like she'd won a war. "Her mother died two winters back. Consumption, same as what's eating me now, though mine's faster about it." He folded the cloth over the photo again, carefully, like he was tucking it into bed. "The doctor gives me till the first frost. Maybe less."

Felix waited. She'd learned to wait; grief always filled silence if you gave it room.

"I don't want to leave her carrying me," Corvin said. "That's what dying does to children. It sits on them their whole lives. I watched it sit on my mother." His yellowed eyes came up to hers. "I want you to take my memory of her. All of it. Every day of her seven years. I'll die clean, and she'll grow up without a ghost on her back."

The wind came off the harbor and smelled of tar and cold iron. Somewhere above them a door slammed, and a woman's voice swore cheerfully at somebody named Pieter.

"No," Felix said.

Corvin blinked. "You're turning down coin?"

"I'm turning down *that.*" She tapped his coat, right where the photograph lay against his heart. "There's a difference between taking a splinter out and cutting off the limb. A man who forgets his daughter isn't clean. He's just empty in a new place."

"She'd be better—"

"She'd be better off with a father who died loving her than one who died not knowing why the little girl keeps visiting his grave." Felix shouldered her bag. "Find another way to carry it. That's free advice, which makes it the most valuable thing you'll get tonight."

She made it three steps up before his hand caught her sleeve — light, apologetic, but there.

"Please," he said. "Not all of it. Take—" He stopped, swallowed, started over, and this time his voice cracked straight down the middle. "Take the worst night. Just the one. Last winter, when the fever first came, she sat by my bed for three days and wouldn't sleep, and on the third night I got angry. Scared-angry. I told her I didn't want her there. I told her to stop looking at me like I was already dead." His hand shook against her sleeve. "She ran out crying. And then I got worse instead of better, and I never fixed it. I never apologized because apologizing means saying the word *dying* out loud, and I can't say it while she still thinks I'm going to be here at Midwinter."

Felix stood very still.

This was the rule. This had always been the rule, since the widow in Chapter One of her miserable career, since the dockworker who couldn't stop seeing his crewmate drown, since every charm she'd ever lifted or bought cheap from someone drowning: *only the wounds, never the whole person. Only what they beg you to take.*

It was also, she reminded herself, a story she told herself so she could sleep. She took payment either way. Mercy with a price tag was still commerce. But the rules were the rules, and this man was begging, and the wound was real.

"How much do you have?" she asked.

"Nine marks."

"That's robbery. Of me."

"It's everything I have that isn't going to Mira."

Felix sighed and sat down on the stairs beside him, which was as close to compassion as she let her body show in public. "One night. The worst one. And you understand what you're buying? You'll know you had a daughter. You just won't remember being cruel to her. The guilt goes, but so does the reason to apologize."

"Then I'll apologize anyway," Corvin said. "For everything. Blanket apology. Fathers are allowed those."

Despite herself — despite the cold, the hour, the eleven marks in her pocket — Felix laughed. "Fine. Give me your hand."

* * *

Memory-charms weren't hard to make if you had the knack. Most people didn't, and the Ministry had spent a century executing everyone who did, which kept prices high and practitioners rare. The trick wasn't force. Anyone could tear a memory out and leave the person bleeding on the inside forever after. The trick was patience: you warmed the glass, you let the memory come to the surface on its own like a fish rising to bread, and you drew it off gently, the way you'd draw poison from a cut, letting the body keep everything it needed.

Felix set the small glass orb — a blank charm, unmarked, bought from a glassblower in the undercity who asked no questions — in Corvin's cupped palms. "Think about that night. Don't fight it. Just hold the door open."

His hands trembled around hers. They were warm, lamplighter's hands, oil-stained and steady for twenty-two years and shaking now. "Will it hurt?"

"Less than the coughing does."

He laughed, wetly, and closed his eyes, and began to remember.

Felix felt the memory rise like heat off summer stone. Three days of fever. A small figure in a too-big nightgown sitting vigil in a chair dragged to the bedside, chin propped on fists, refusing all orders to sleep. The specific misery of a sickroom at 3 a.m., candle burned to a puddle, sweat soaking the sheets. Then the anger, the scared-angry, the words coming out wrong and sharp — *stop looking at me like that, go to bed, I don't want you here* — and the small face crumpling, and small feet running, and the door banging, and Corvin alone in the dark hating himself with the pure efficient hatred only the dying manage—

Felix drew it off into the glass.

And the world *stopped.*

Not the room. The room went on: wind, distant dog, Corvin breathing in wet rattles. But the memory itself — the thing flowing through her fingers into the charm — snagged, caught, *held*, like a river hitting a dam that shouldn't exist.

Because inside the memory, behind the sickbed and the crying child and the guttering candle, something else was standing.

A figure made of fire. Not burning — *being* fire, the way a person is flesh, standing calmly in the corner of a poor lamplighter's sickroom as though it had always lived there. It had a shape like a young man, if you squinted, if you were generous, but mostly it was just light with intent, gold at the core shading to white at the edges, and it was looking directly at Felix.

Memories were not supposed to look back.

"What is it?" Corvin whispered. He could feel it too, clearly — his hands had gone rigid around hers, and the transfer had stopped dead, the memory pooled half-drawn between his mind and her glass like water finding a level. "What's wrong?"

"There's—" Felix's mouth had gone dry. "There's something in here with you."

"In the memory?"

"In *you.* Or near you. Or—" She had no vocabulary for this. Five years of trade, hundreds of charms, and she had never once seen anything inside a memory except the memory. Memories were rooms. This one had an occupant. The fire-figure tilted its head, and though it had no face, exactly, Felix got the overwhelming impression of being *examined* — patiently, thoroughly, with something horribly close to fondness.

Then it raised one burning hand and pointed at the charm in her palm.

The glass cracked.

Not shattered — cracked, a single hairline fracture running pole to pole, and through the fracture came a sound. Small. Almost nothing. A whisper like a coal settling in a grate, like the last word of someone falling asleep:

"*Theo.*"

The pressure vanished. The figure was gone. The memory finished pouring into the charm as though nothing had happened, smooth and complete, and Felix sat back on the cold stone steps with the cracked orb burning — actually warm, faintly, like a stone left near a hearth — in her palm.

Corvin sagged. "Is it done?"

"It's done." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You won't remember that night. You'll remember you have a daughter, and that you love her, and that you owe her an apology. The rest goes with me."

He exhaled, long and shuddering, and pressed nine marks into her hand. "Thank you," he said, and meant it so completely that Felix had to look away.

"Go home," she said. "Apologize to your daughter. Tell her you're sorry for the whole winter. Blanket apology."

"Fathers are allowed those." He gathered himself up the stairs, one step at a time, lighter by one night and heavier by nothing, and Felix watched him go until the dark took him.

* * *

She should have sold the charm that night. That was the trade: lift, seal, deliver, spend. A cracked charm fetched less, but a cracked charm with a full memory intact still fetched plenty from certain collectors in the undercity, men with more coin than sense who liked their relics flawed.

She sat in her rented room above the chandler's shop instead, with the charm in the center of her table under a single candle, and stared at it.

Hairline fracture. Pole to pole. Faint warmth, hours later, though glass has no business holding heat that long.

*Theo.*

She turned it over. Turned it back. Names didn't live in memories — names lived in people, or on documents, or carved on tombstones. And figures made of fire certainly didn't live in the sickroom memories of dying lamplighters. Unless. Unless the man had been delirious. Fever dreams leaked into memories all the time; she'd skimmed enough of them. But fever dreams didn't *look at you.* Fever dreams didn't crack glass on purpose, from the inside, and leave a word behind like a signature.

She thought about the Ministry. Thought about the confiscation wagons, the gray coats, the way the whole market had gone quiet last spring when they burned a charm-seller's stall with the seller still attached. If anyone learned she'd pulled a memory with *something alive in it* — 

She should destroy it. Drop it in the harbor. That was the smart move, the survival move, the move the Felix Zhou who had survived five years in Vell would make without hesitation.

She picked it up.

Warmth spread through her palm, up her wrist, into the bones of her arm — gentle, unmistakably gentle, the warmth of a hand held rather than a fire applied. Through the fracture, deep inside the glass, something glowed. Not reflected candlelight. Its own light. Gold shading to white.

And beneath her fingers, faint as breath on a winter window, she heard it again — not in her ear this time, but in the charm, in the glass itself, patient as embers under ash:

"*Theo.*"

The candle flame leaned toward the orb. All the candles in Vell, she would learn later, leaned toward her that night — every flame in every window along Tanner's Stair bending like grass in a wind that wasn't blowing, every prayer muttered over every sickbed in the district suddenly feeling, for the first time in a hundred years, like it might be *heard.*

Felix set the charm down very carefully, the way you set down something sleeping, and backed away from her own table.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the cracked glass, to whatever had signed its name inside a stolen night. "Okay. What are you?"

The charm pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat. Like a knock.

Like an answer coming.
