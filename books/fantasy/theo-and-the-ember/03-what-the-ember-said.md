## 3. What the Ember Said

The name burned.

Not like fire — Felix knew fire, had held her palm over a guttering candle a hundred winter nights, had learned the difference between warmth and damage the way street kids learn the difference between hunger and appetite. This was neither. This was the crack in a charm whispering *Theo, Theo, Theo* and her own mouth, traitorous and half-asleep with exhaustion, shaping the syllables back.

She was in her rooms above the dye-shop on Tanner's Row when it happened, three hours after the lamplighter died with his daughter's face smoothed clean of him. She'd meant to sleep. She'd meant to bury the cracked charm in lime like all the spoiled goods, because broken charms were bad luck and worse business. But she'd turned it over in her fingers instead, this little disc of pressed ash and lacquer no bigger than a coin, and the crack had caught the lamplight through her window, and inside the crack something had looked back at her.

Then the whisper. Then her mouth. Then —

Light came off her palm like heat off a stove-road in August, silent and total, throwing shadows of her own hand up onto the ceiling in ten directions at once. The charm didn't melt. It *opened*, the way a fist opens, and what was inside wasn't fire at all but something older and more patient, an ember the size of a grain of barley that sat in the cup of her palm and did not burn her, which was somehow worse than if it had.

Felix did what any sensible thief does when a job goes sideways: she froze, assessed, and reached for the window to throw the damn thing into the canal.

Her hand wouldn't close.

"Put it down," she told herself, calm, professional, the voice she used on jittery clients. "Drop it. Drop it, you idiot—"

The ember pulsed once, gently, like a sleeping thing turning over. And Vell answered.

It started small. Down on Tanner's Row, old Marta Osei, who dyed wool until her knuckles were permanently blue and who hadn't prayed since the Silence took her sons' names out of the registry, dropped to her knees in her shop and said the first word of a prayer she'd kept folded up inside her for eleven years — and the vat of ruined indigo beside her, dead stock, unsellable, turned the exact blue of the ribbon her younger son had worn to his first day of school. She screamed. Felix heard the scream through the floor, thin and human and full of something that had no name yet, and felt it in her teeth.

The ember pulsed again.

*Theo,* said the crack in the world, very softly, almost shyly. And across the district, prayers that people had stopped bothering to say began, impossibly, to be heard.

Felix leaned against the wall and watched it happen the only way she could — through sound. A woman two streets over weeping with joy because the fever broke in her daughter's lungs between one breath and the next. The bells of Saint Ferren's ringing themselves, all nine of them, a carillon nobody had been able to afford to ring for thirty years. Somewhere a drunk man laughing and laughing in the empty street because his bottle had filled back up, which was, Felix thought distantly, exactly the kind of petty miracle a god would start with if he'd been asleep a century and was still stretching.

Wonder went through her first. She'd be honest about that later, when honesty got expensive. For one whole minute Felix Zhou stood in her narrow room with light pouring out of her hand and felt what the faithful must have felt before the Ministry, before the Silence, before the long gray century — that the world was large and someone in it was paying attention, and the attention was kind.

Then the second prayer got answered wrong, and wonder curdled.

* * *

It happened fast after that. Miracles are like fires; one is a marvel, twelve is a conflagration.

By the time Felix got the window open — her other hand; the ember-hand stayed stubbornly cupped, useless for climbing — the district was awake and loud. Not riot-loud, not yet. Something stranger: the quiet, stunned noise of a city remembering. Doors opening. Voices in stairwells. Someone singing a hymn badly and getting the words right by the third verse as though the words were being handed to them.

And under it, threading through, the prayers. People prayed the way they breathed once they remembered how — small greedy desperate prayers, tossed up like coins into a fountain that had finally started paying out. *Let the rent wait. Let him come home. Let me see her face again.* And the answers came back immediate, sloppy, generous, terrifying. A fishmonger's stall overflowed with herring past all reason or season. A widow's front step bloomed with lilies in frozen mud. Two brothers who'd sworn blood feud over a debt woke their whole tenement shouting reconciliation, arms around each other's necks, both of them weeping, neither able to say why.

Felix understood, with the cold clarity that had kept her alive through nine years of thieving, that none of these people had wished carefully. Nobody in Vell had practiced wanting things. For a hundred years wanting had been pointless, so the muscle had gone soft and strange, and now every raw unexamined want in the district was being granted by something that had been asleep since before Felix was born and had woken up *hungry to help*.

A child's voice rose somewhere near the canal, high and delighted: "Mama, Mama, look, the water's standing up—" and Felix shut the window hard.

Because she could feel it. That was the part nobody would believe and she couldn't deny. Every answer ran *through* her palm. The ember sat there pulsing like a second heart, and each prayer that got answered pulled a fine hot thread from it, out through her skin, across the rooftops, down chimneys, into the dark of other people's lives. She was not watching miracles. She was leaking them.

"Stop," she whispered to it. "Hey. Stop. You're going to get everyone killed."

The ember pulsed. If it heard, it gave no sign, and its indifference was the most frightening thing in the room. Gods, Felix thought, probably never learned manners. They'd never needed them.

She wrapped her hand in her spare shirt. Light bled through the linen like sun through cheap cloth. She wrapped it again. She thought about the canal, about drowning the thing, about how far she'd have to run and how fast, and while she thought it, three streets east, a prayer she'd never hear the words of got answered in a way that made grown men come out of their houses carrying iron bars.

* * *

The Ministry moved faster than she'd ever seen them move, and she'd sold to their clerks often enough to know their usual speed, which was glacial, bureaucratic, deliberate as sediment. Tonight they came down Coppergate in force — forty silencers in gray, boots striking in unison, lanterns hooded, moving through the celebrating streets like scissors through cloth.

Felix saw them from her roof, because staying indoors suddenly seemed like dying in a trap, and she got out the way she always got out, through the dye-shop's drying loft and onto the tiles. From up there Vell spread out below, and she could see the shape of the night whole: the district glowing faintly, here and there, with little blooms of impossible light where prayers were landing; and around those blooms, the Ministry's gray lines closing like a fist.

They weren't burning anything. That was the first horror. Normally the Ministry burned — confiscated charms went into the ash-wagons, suspected practitioners went to the Quiet Rooms and came out smooth-faced and missing years. But tonight the silencers walked *past* the miracles without raising a hand, past the standing water and the self-rung bells and the fish that shouldn't exist, and Felix watched an inspector — tall, dark-haired, moving with a clerk's economy — stop in front of Marta Osei's door and simply *listen* to the old woman pray, and write something down.

They weren't destroying the miracle. They were mapping it.

"They know it's new," Felix said aloud, to nobody, to the roof tiles. "They know it started somewhere."

The ember flared against her ribs where she'd stuffed it — shirt, then scarf, then against her skin under her coat, because it refused to leave her palm entirely, kept crawling back like a cat deciding whose house it lived in — and down on Coppergate, forty gray heads turned at once toward Tanner's Row.

Toward her roof. Toward her.

Felix had been hunted before. It came with the trade; memory-thieves steal from grief, and grief is the one commodity Vell guards jealously. She knew how to become nobody, how to fold herself into drain-shadow and chimney-gloom, how to be gone before a shout finished forming. She did all of it now, flawlessly, sliding down the far slope of the roof into the alley behind the tannery, heart steady, breath measured, feet finding the old safe path along the wall-tops where the mortar was rotten enough to grip.

But this time the hunt had a shape she recognized, and it was her own. Because the silencers weren't fanning out randomly. They were following the threads — she could feel each thread tug as a hooded lantern passed near it, feel the Ministry's cold attention travel down the line of a miracle back toward its source, methodical as a fisherman hauling in line. And every prayer answered anywhere in Vell pulled tighter on the net around her body.

She had done this. Her mouth, her palm, her stolen, cracked, cursed charm. Some grieving lamplighter's last memory had been carrying a god in it like a splinter carries infection, and she had spoken him awake, and now every answered prayer in the city was a rope with one end tied to her ribs and the other end tied to the Ministry's attention, and the rope was *shortening*.

In the alley, pressed flat against the tannery wall while boots crossed the street above, Felix finally let herself think the sentence she'd been dodging:

*I'm the source.*

The ember pulsed warmly against her sternum, pleased as a fed animal, and somewhere north of the river a bell rang itself, and forty silencers turned as one, and Inspector-something's pen scratched in the dark, writing down coordinates.

Felix ran.

She ran the way she'd taught herself at thirteen, all elbows and economy, over the tannery sheds and up the aqueduct spine and along the ridgeline of Coppergate's roofs with the celebrating, terrified, praying city yawping below her. Behind her the gray line flowed through the streets, patient, unhurried, certain. They didn't chase her specifically. They didn't need to. They were closing the district — she heard the great gates boom, one after another, Coppergate, Saltgate, the Weir — sealing ten thousand souls inside with a waking god and whatever came next.

At the ridge above Saltgate she paused, one breath, straddling the cold tiles, and looked back.

Vell glowed. All across the sealed district, windows lit gold where no lamps were lit, and the prayers rose off the streets like heat-shimmer, and the threads of light ran from every one of them back to the ember hidden under her coat, back to her heart, back to the thief on the rooftop who had spent nine years making sure she owed nothing to anyone.

Somewhere in the glow, a woman was praying for her drowned son to come home. Felix could feel that prayer the way you feel a hook. It wanted answering. The ember wanted to answer it. And Felix had seen enough of the world to know exactly what came out of deep water when you asked it for your dead.

"No," she told it, through her teeth, hands shaking now for the first time all night. "Whatever you are — whoever I woke up — you don't get to grant that one."

The ember pulsed. Patient. Warm. Utterly indifferent to her rules.

And in the doorway of the shrine at the end of Saltgate bridge, where no one had stood in a hundred years, a figure straightened up out of the dark — young, barefoot, unhurried, looking up at her on the rooftop with an expression of mild, terrible interest, as though he'd been waiting five years and could easily wait five more.

He raised one hand. Not a summons. An invitation.

Felix, who had survived by never answering anyone, heard herself say: "...Who are you?"

The gates of Vell boomed shut behind him, and he smiled like spring.
