## 5. Running on Rooftops

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Felix had a rule about the wrong hours: they were when the Ministry worked, because the Ministry believed fear kept better time than clocks. She'd been asleep in her cot above the dye-shop on Tanner's Row — legally she was a lodger who didn't exist, which was the only kind of lodger worth being — and the knock was three beats, pause, three beats. Uniform rhythm. Official patience.

She was out the window before the second set of knocks finished.

Tanner's Row backed onto the drying yards, and the drying yards backed onto the low roof of the tallow works, and from there Vell opened upward like a ladder built by someone with no respect for gravity. Felix had spent five years learning that ladder rung by rung. She went up it now in the dark, barefoot, boots looped around her neck because bare soles gripped wet slate better and the night had gone damp with something worse than rain.

Below her, the dye-shop door gave way. She didn't look. Looking cost speed, and speed was the whole religion of rooftops.

They'd found her ledger's trail, then. Or they'd found the clerk she'd sold the widow's memory to, or the lamplighter's cracked charm, or any of the hundred small threads she'd left dangling because for five years nothing had ever pulled them. The whisper — *Theo*, burning in her palm like a coal that refused to cool — had changed the arithmetic of the whole city. She could feel it now, even at a rooftop's distance: bells ringing in districts where no one had paid for bells, crowds gathering at shuttered shrines, prayers going up like sparks off a bonfire and *coming back down answered*. The Ministry would have locked the district within the hour of the first miracle. And the first thing every competent inspector did after locking a district was find its lock-picker.

That was her. That had always been her. She just hadn't known, until three nights ago, what she'd picked open.

She crossed the tallow works, dropped onto the cooperage, rolled under a clothesline strung between chimneys. Behind her, lantern-light bloomed in the street below — not one lantern but six, moving in formation, which meant Silence-men, which meant they weren't chasing a thief tonight. They were chasing an ember-bearer, and ember-bearers didn't get trial cells. They got the Quiet Room, where the Ministry's confiscated magic lived in jars, and where Felix suspected people went to be emptied as thoroughly as the charms she stole.

Her palm throbbed. She uncurled her fingers just enough to check: the burn-mark was still there, faintly luminous, shaped like nothing and refusing to be anything. It had been quiet all day. Now, with the hunters close, it warmed against her will, like a dog hearing its name.

*Don't,* she told it silently. *Don't you dare.*

The mark pulsed once, gently, almost apologetic. Then — light. Not from her hand. From the shrine-roof two streets over, where some desperate soul had pried the boards off a sealed altar and knelt praying into the dark, and the dark had *answered*. A column of soft gold rose over the rooftops like dawn arriving at the wrong end of the night, and every Silence-man below stopped, and turned, and looked.

Felix didn't waste the gift. She ran.

Vell's roofs were her native country. She knew them the way sailors know weather: the slate slopes of Candlemaker's Walk that shed ice in winter, the tar-flat tops of the chandlers' quarter where footsteps made no sound, the gap over Cordwainer Lane that looked impossible and wasn't if you committed. She ran through her country with the Ministry's lanterns swinging far below and behind, and for eleven rooftops she thought she might actually lose them.

On the twelfth, the sky fell on her.

Not literally. It was a net — weighted, woven with something that hummed against her teeth the way charms hummed, which meant warded net, which meant someone up here had planned for a thief who climbed. It came down over her from the bell-tower above Cordwainer's, and she twisted, and it caught her shoulder instead of her whole body, spinning her off the ridgeline into open air over a four-story drop.

She got one hand onto the gutter. The other held her boots. The net dragged at her shoulder like a patient hand.

Below: cobblestones, waiting.

Above: boots on slate, unhurried. Two of them, maybe three. A voice said, "Ember-bearer. Hold still. This is easier if you hold still."

Felix held very still, and thought, with the strange clarity of the nearly-dead: *I have never once held still in my life.*

Then the rope holding the net went slack — cut, actually, she heard the blade work — and the net slid off the tower edge and past her into the street with a sound like a whale dying, and a hand closed around her wrist. Warm. Strong. Callused in the specific pattern of someone who hauled rigging.

"Up," said a voice, "before they figure out I'm not their backup."

She went up. There wasn't really a decision involved; her body understood rescue faster than her mind did. She came over the ridgeline of the bell-tower's lower roof and found herself face to face with a woman crouched in the dark — broad-shouldered, short-haired, a knife already back in its sheath like it had gotten bored, eyes catching the distant gold light of the answering shrine and throwing it back amused.

"Hi," the woman said. "You're heavier than you look. All that stolen memory, probably."

"Who—"

"No time." She jerked her chin toward the tower's far side. "There were three on the roof. I only cut one rope."

Felix heard the third hunter's boots change rhythm. She ran.

* *

They ran together across Vell's spine for twenty minutes, and it was the strangest twenty minutes of Felix's adult life, because she'd always run alone, and running alone meant knowing every route yourself, carrying the whole map in your head, spending half your attention on the person behind you. Running with this woman was different. The woman ran like someone who also owned the map — a different map, maybe, drawn by smugglers instead of thieves, full of routes Felix had never bothered with: the rope-bridge between the granary and the customs house that official maps pretended didn't exist, the lead-lined channel of a decommissioned aqueduct that swallowed sound, a stretch where they simply *slid*, both of them, down a slope of copper sheeting worn slick by forty years of rain, laughing — the woman laughed, actually laughed, breathless and unbothered, while Silence-men crashed about below them like beetles under a lifted stone.

"They'll ring the Quarter Bell," Felix gasped, at the top of the granary. "When they do, every gate in the district seals. We need to be outside the walls of it by—"

"It rang four minutes ago," the woman said. "I know. Come on."

She led Felix down inside the bell tower itself — down, of all directions, down through the dusty dark where the great bronze Quarter Bell hung silent in its yoke, down a maintenance ladder, out through a hatch that opened, absurdly, into a candle merchant's stockroom. The woman pulled the hatch shut behind them, stacked two crates on top of it with the ease of long habit, and lit a stub of candle with a match struck one-handed off her thumbnail.

"There," she said. "Deaf cellar. The bell can't find you in here. Neither can anything else, for about an hour." She stuck out a hand, candlelight catching grease and old rope-burns. "Juniper Vale."

Felix looked at the hand. Everything in her training said don't touch strangers who appear on rooftops and cut nets and know about deaf cellars. But the hand was warm — she could feel the warmth of it from six inches away, radiating like a stove — and Felix realized, with a small internal jolt, how cold she was. How cold she'd been since the charm whispered. Since the ember lit.

She shook the hand anyway. The warmth went up her arm like a rumor.

"Felix," she said. Then, because the silence demanded filling and honesty felt strangely cheap right now: "You want to tell me why a stranger was waiting on Cordwainer's tower with a knife?"

"I wasn't waiting. I was napping." Juniper sat down on a crate of tallow candles, entirely at home. "Best nap spot in the district. Nobody checks the towers after curfew because everyone's scared of heights except me."

"You cut a warded net in the dark."

"I've cut worse things in worse light." She shrugged. "And I saw you come over the chandlers' flats, and I thought, there's a person who runs like the roof is hers. And then I saw the Ministry put a net on her, and I thought, well. That's rude."

"That's your reason? *Rude?*"

"Best reason there is." Juniper leaned forward, elbows on knees, and studied Felix with an frankness that made her want to check whether she was still dressed. "Also — and I'm saying this as a professional observation — you've got a light in your hand that doesn't belong to you, you've been leaking prayer-magic across three districts for three days, and roughly nine different dangerous parties are currently trying to find you. So maybe I wanted to meet you before somebody less friendly did."

Felix curled her fingers closed over the burn-mark. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Sure."

"I steal memories. Small ones. Sad ones. It's a trade."

"I believe you."

"It is!"

"I said I believe you." Juniper's grin softened into something else — curiosity, maybe, or recognition. "You don't have to perform for me, Felix. I'm not an inspector and I'm not a priest. I'm a smuggler. My entire profession consists of noticing what people are carrying and not asking where it came from." She tilted her head. "But I'll say this much. Whatever's burning in you — I've seen its handwriting before."

The cellar went very quiet. Somewhere above, muffled through floors of brick and candle-wax, the Quarter Bell tolled the third hour, and the sound couldn't quite reach them, arriving instead as a vibration in the floorboards, a shiver without a voice.

"What does that mean?" Felix asked. "Its handwriting."

Juniper was quiet a moment. When she spoke again, the flippancy was gone, and what remained underneath was older, and careful, and a little bit sad.

"Six years ago my ship went down off the Teeth. Forty-one souls aboard. I prayed — me, who hadn't prayed since I was small enough to believe it mattered. I prayed to anyone listening, *save us.*" She turned the match-stub over in her fingers. "Something listened. We washed ashore, all forty-one of us, every single one, on a coast where nobody washes ashore. And I've spent six years trying to figure out what it cost, because nothing saves forty-one people for free." Her eyes came up. "Three days ago, prayers started getting answered all over Vell. And I felt it in my chest like a name I'd been trying to remember." She stood, and offered her warm hand again, to pull Felix up off the crate. "So. You're the source. I'd bet my ship — the new one — that you're the source. And I'd like to keep you alive long enough to ask you a question I've been carrying six years."

Felix took the hand. She told herself it was practical — standing, warmth, the sensible physics of it. But she noticed, and hated noticing, that she held on perhaps a half-second longer than standing required.

"And if my answer costs you?" she said. "If the answer is ugly?"

"Then it's ugly, and I'll still owe whoever saved my crew a drink." Juniper smiled, and it did something unhelpful to the space behind Felix's ribs. "You look exhausted, thief. You look like you haven't slept since the whisper started, and like you've been running alone for years, and like you've gotten so good at it you've forgotten there's another way to move through a city." She released Felix's hand, gently, and picked up the candle. "Come on. I've got a safehouse with actual beds, and tea, and a door that opens inward so you can leave whenever you want. No chains, no bargains, no destiny. Just sleep."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow we figure out why the gods woke up in your hand." She paused at the cellar stairs, candlelight gilding the side of her face, and added, lightly, as though it were nothing:

"Oh — and fair warning. The war-goddess has been asking about you too. Word is she doesn't knock."

Above them, faint through stone and wax and the deafness of the cellar, something in the city began to sing — a hymn, rising from ten thousand throats in the locked-down quarter, praying to a name that had slept a century and was now, thanks to Felix Zhou, wide awake.

*Theo*, the crowd sang.

In Felix's palm, the mark burned like a held breath.
