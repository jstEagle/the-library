## 8. Prayers in the Dark

### I.

Marta Osei had prayed the way her grandmother taught her, palms cupped like a bowl of water, words spoken soft enough that no one living could hear them and mock her for it. She hadn't prayed in eleven years — not since the gods went quiet, not since prayer became something you did with your mouth shut and your heart locked, the way you breathed in a house where a jealous man slept.

But three nights ago, the well in her courtyard had whispered back.

*Rain,* she had asked. *Please. The cisterns are low, my son's beans are dying in the rows, and water costs more than bread.*

The well had said nothing. But the sky had heard. The sky had heard everything.

Now Marta stood at her window on the fourth morning of the rain and watched the Saltrow district drown.

It had come gently at first, a fine grey drizzle, almost apologetic, as if the weather were embarrassed by its own enthusiasm. Then the clouds had thickened into something muscular and green-black, and the rain had come down in ropes, in sheets, in a sound like applause that would not stop. The gutters of Saltrow were old, built for a city that rationed its weather. They filled in an hour. By dawn the lower streets ran knee-deep, brown and fast, carrying market awnings, a door torn off its hinges, a dog paddling with grim determination toward higher ground.

Marta's beans had drowned on the first day.

Her son, Tobin, nine years old and made entirely of elbows and opinions, thought the flood was wonderful until the water reached the second step of their staircase and kept going. Now he pressed his face to the window beside hers, watching their neighbor Old Sef wade through what had been his carpentry shop, hauling out chairs that floated past him like patient ghosts.

"Mama," Tobin said. "Did you do this?"

The question landed in Marta's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She listened for the splash.

"No," she said. "I asked for rain."

"This is rain."

"This is an *answer.*"

She had learned the difference between those two things over eleven years of silence, and she wished — with a desperation that surprised her — that she could unlearn it again. Because if the gods were listening now, then they had heard her small, reasonable, desperate request, and they had answered it the way a giant answers a child asking for a sweet: by handing over the whole confectioner's shop, sugar and glass and all, and letting it collapse on top of everyone.

Down in the flooded street, a man stood waist-deep in front of the shrine of Saint Vessal — a saint, not a god; saints were safe, saints didn't listen — and he was shouting at the sky. His voice carried over the water, ragged with fury.

"You wanted worship? *This* is worship? My mother's in that house! My mother can't swim!"

The sky did not apologize. The sky had never once apologized. It simply rained harder, as though the man had made a request too, and it was being generous.

Marta closed her eyes. And because terror makes fools of us all, because hope is a habit deeper than sense, she cupped her hands one more time.

*Stop,* she prayed. *Please stop. That's all. Just stop.*

And somewhere far above the drowned roofs of Saltrow, something enormous turned its attention toward her small voice, considering, weighing, deciding how best to oblige —

and Marta felt, for the first time in her life, what a mouse feels when the owl looks *interested.*

### II.

In the Lantern Quarter, where the lamps burned even at noon out of habit rather than need, Corvin Ashe sat beside his daughter's bed and told himself the thing in the bed was his daughter.

She looked like Liss. She had Liss's freckles, the constellation of them across her nose that he'd mapped like a sailor maps stars during the two years she'd been sick. She had Liss's crooked left incisor. Her hair was the same unmanageable copper, and when she'd opened her eyes an hour ago, they'd been the same grey-green as a winter sea, and she'd said, "Papa?" in Liss's exact voice, cracked with sleep, and Corvin had wept so hard he'd frightened them both.

Because Liss had died in the spring. He'd buried her himself, in the paupers' row behind the tanner's fence, because the Ministry charged for consecrated ground and grief was already expensive enough.

He had prayed. Of course he had prayed. Everyone in Vell had prayed at gravesides for a century knowing it was like shouting down a well, and then, four nights ago, kneeling in the mud with his hands still dirty from filling it in, he had prayed anyway — the way you keep dialing a dead man's number just to hear it ring.

*Liss. Bring her back. Take anything else. Take me. Bring her back.*

And now there was a girl in his daughter's bed who was warm, and breathing, and *almost right*, and Corvin Ashe was learning, hour by terrible hour, what the word *almost* cost.

"Liss," he said carefully. "What did we plant last spring?"

She smiled at him, and the smile was Liss's smile, and that was the worst part, that whoever — whatever — wore his daughter had gone digging through her and found the good pieces first. "Beans," she said. "You said they'd be up by festival and I said beans are boring and you said boring things keep people alive." She laughed. It was Liss's laugh.

"That's right," Corvin said. His hands wouldn't stay still; he kept rubbing them on his trousers. "That's right, sweetheart. And what — " his voice snagged — "what do you remember about the river? Last summer?"

Liss's smile stayed exactly where it was. Nothing behind it moved.

"The river," she repeated, in the tone of someone reading words off a card. "We went to the river. It was fun."

"We didn't go to the river, Liss. You were sick all summer. You couldn't leave the bed after Midsummer."

"I remember the river," she said pleasantly. "It was fun. We went to the river."

"Who took you?"

Silence. The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft. Outside, faintly, through the walls, came the sound of bells — not temple bells, not any pattern Corvin knew, but a slow irregular clanging from every quarter of the city at once, as if all of Vell's steeples had begun talking to each other in a language made only of alarm.

"Someone took me," Liss said, and her voice was still pleasant, still seven years old, but underneath it, very briefly, Corvin heard something else — something vast and puzzled, like a whale trying to whistle a lullaby it had heard once, through a wall, a hundred years ago. "Someone carried me home. Through the dark place. It was long. It was cold. Papa, why am I hungry? Why am I *so* hungry?"

Corvin looked at his daughter's face and saw, for half a heartbeat, looking back at him through the grey-green eyes, something that loved him — genuinely, enormously, the way a fire loves the house it consumes — and did not understand him at all.

"Sweetheart," he said, standing, backing toward the door, "why don't you rest a moment—"

"I'm hungry, Papa." She sat up. Her movements were perfect. That was the horror of it; they were perfectly, precisely a child's movements, as if assembled from memory by something that had never had a body and had studied very hard. "You said bring me back. You didn't say what to feed me."

Corvin got the door open. In the street below, the bells clanged on, and a woman was running past his building shouting about the flood in Saltrow, and somewhere across the rooftops a lamplighter's ladder lay fallen against a wall, abandoned.

Behind him, in the room, Liss began, softly and sweetly, to sing a song Corvin had never taught her, in a language that had died before his grandmother's grandmother was born.

He ran. He was not proud of it. He ran, and as he fled down the stairwell he understood, with a clarity that would follow him for the rest of his life, exactly which part of his prayer the thing upstairs had used as instructions.

*Bring her back,* he had said.

He should have said *her*. All of her. Only her.

Half-remembered gods grant half-remembered wishes, and the missing halves go somewhere.

### III.

Inspector Ada Reyes stood in the archive beneath the Ministry of Silence and read reports by lamplight while above her the city rang like a dropped bell.

They had come in all night, dictated by breathless clerks, transcribed in the Ministry's flat bureaucratic hand that could make even apocalypse look like paperwork:

*Saltrow district inundated. Cause unknown. Water table behavior inconsistent with meteorological record. Casualties: 14 confirmed. Request authorization for evacuation of lower wards.*

*Lantern Quarter, residence on Cordwainer Street: resident reported deceased minor present and ambulant. Officer dispatched. Officer has not returned.*

*A woman on Tallow Row prayed for her husband's cough to end. Husband's lungs filled with fluid within the hour. Cough ended. Widow now praying for it to return. We have no protocol for this.*

Reyes set down the last sheet and pressed her fingers against her eyes until she saw constellations.

Eleven years ago, she had walked into a room much like this one and paid the Ministry's fee — the fee nobody talked about, the service listed nowhere but available to anyone whose grief was large enough and whose coin or compliance was sufficient. She'd had a memory taken out of her the way you'd have a rotten tooth drawn. Her sister Nell, drowning. The specific weight of a wet dress. The specific silence of a lake going still afterward.

She remembered that Nell had existed. She remembered the shape of the absence where the memory had been, the way your tongue finds the gap of a pulled tooth. What she did not remember was whether she had been holding Nell's hand when the ice broke, and some nights that not-knowing was worse than the memory would have been, because at least a memory ends. A gap goes on forever.

She served these people. She had served them for eleven years, competently, dutifully, telling herself the work was mercy — that a city without magic was a city without floods summoned by accident, without dead children walking, without the particular cruelty of power answering pain.

And here it all was anyway. Not because magic had returned. Because *prayer* had returned, and prayer, it turned out, had never needed permission.

"Inspector." Deputy Quarrel stood in the doorway of the archive aisle, rain-wet despite having descended six floors underground, which meant he'd run from the entrance. "The First Speaker wants the ember-bearer found tonight. Tonight, Reyes. He's authorized full protocols."

"What protocols?" Reyes asked, because she genuinely didn't know. In eleven years she had never once been inside the vault levels below this archive. Nobody below the rank of Speaker had.

Quarrel hesitated. Rain dripped off his coat onto the stone floor, each drop loud as a footstep.

"There's a store," he said finally, quietly. "Below. Confiscated material. charms, vessels, relics — things we've been cataloguing for a century instead of destroying. Official record says destroyed, obviously. But when the ember ignited four nights ago—" He swallowed. "Everything downstairs woke up. Everything. The catalogues are rewriting themselves. The locks are singing. And the First Speaker says whatever's happening in the city, it started with one person carrying a piece of it, and if we find her, we can contain this before the whole hoard comes awake."

Reyes looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere overhead, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the bells of Vell went on ringing their languageless alarm.

"A century of 'destroyed' magic," she said, "in our basement."

"Yes, Inspector."

"And we're surprised it has opinions."

Quarrel had the grace not to answer.

Reyes picked up the report stack and squared its edges against the desk, a small violence, a tidy one. On the top sheet, the clerk's hand recorded a widow on Tallow Row praying for her husband's death to be undone, and beneath it, in different ink, someone had appended: *Prayer received. No response. Reason unknown.*

No response. Reyes stared at that line. Four days of the sky answering everyone — flooding the deserving and undeserving alike, raising the dead wrong, granting wishes with a giant's generosity and a child's understanding — and one widow's prayer had come back empty, marked like a letter returned undelivered.

As if something out there had read it and chosen, specifically, not to hear.

Or as if the listening thing had limits. As if somewhere in Vell there was a person, a point, a *source*, and the miracle had a radius, and the radius had edges, and edges could be mapped.

"Quarrel," Reyes said. "Get me every unanswered prayer from the last four nights. Every one. Time, location, wording."

"You think the gaps will show us something?"

"I think," Reyes said, pulling her coat off its hook, "that a miracle is just a crime scene where the weapon is still warm. And I've never once failed to work a crime scene."

She climbed the stairs toward the ringing city, and behind her, six floors down, in a vault no one spoke of, a hundred confiscated charms sang softly in the dark, keeping time with the bells, waiting for their owner to come home.

### IV.

On a rooftop in the Weaver's Terraces, Juniper Vale stood in the rain with her arms out and let it hit her like a blessing, which was funny, because it probably was one, and blessings had recently stopped being safe.

"Felix," she called over her shoulder, without turning around. "You need to see this."

Felix Zhou came up through the skylight behind her, hood up, moving like someone who'd learned that open sky was just a ceiling with better sightlines for snipers. Five days since the charm had cracked in her palm and whispered *Theo*, and she looked like it: hollow-eyed, jumpy, alive in a way that scared Juniper a little because it was the aliveness of a fuse.

"See what? The end of the world? Already seen it. It's wet."

"Look *down.*"

Juniper pointed. Below them, in the flooded lane, the people of the Terraces had done something Felix clearly hadn't expected: they had stopped panicking. They stood in the shallows in clusters, soaked to the skin, and they were praying — openly, loudly, arms raised, faces tipped to the boiling sky. An old man was thanking the rain for ending his thirty-year drought of faith. A young woman was begging it to spare the orphanage on the corner. Two men were arguing scripture at full volume, except neither of them had owned scripture five days ago.

"They're not afraid," Felix said.

"Some of them are terrified. Look at the corners. People packing carts. But most of them—" Juniper shook her head. Rain ran off her grin. "Most of them are happy, Felix. First time in a century somebody upstairs picked up. They don't care that the call got put through wrong. They're just glad someone answered."

Felix's jaw tightened. "A girl in Lantern Quarter got her dead daughter back wrong. Half of Saltrow is underwater because one woman wanted to save her bean rows. Whatever's answering—"

"Is you."

"*Is not me.*" The words cracked out of her sharper than she meant, and Felix flinched at her own voice, hugged her arms, made herself smaller against the parapet. "I didn't ask for any of this. I stole a dying man's last memory and something in it bit me. That's all I did. That has to be all I did."

Juniper watched her, and the rain came down, and somewhere below the happy praying crowd a bell tolled wrong, and Juniper thought about a ship going down in black water eleven years ago and a voice in the waves saying *one favor, someday, asked of me by name* — and said nothing about any of it, because some debts keep better in the dark.

"It doesn't matter what you meant to do," she said instead, gently. "You're the loudest thing in the city. Everything quiet listens to the loud thing."

"Then someone needs to turn me *down.*"

"Or," Juniper said, hopping up onto the parapet with the easy balance of a woman who had never once respected a height, "you learn what you're plugged into before the Ministry finds you. Because they're coming, love. Bells like that aren't alarm bells. They're searchlights."

Felix looked at her — really looked, the way she'd started doing lately, like Juniper was a lock worth picking — and something in her expression shifted from fear toward decision.

"How do I learn?"

"There's a man," Juniper said slowly, "who owes me a conversation. Older than the Ministry. Sleeps in the space between prayers." She held out her hand. "Come meet my god."

Felix stared at the offered hand. Rain fell between them and did not touch it, curving away, as if even the weather had decided to give them privacy.

"If I take your hand," Felix said quietly, "I'm not coming back from this, am I. Whatever I was before the charm — that's over."

Juniper smiled, and it wasn't a careless smile anymore. It was the other kind, the rare kind, the kind she spent like gold.

"Nobody comes back from being known," she said. "That's the whole trick of it."

Far below, the bells changed their rhythm — from searching to something faster, hungrier, converging — and in the high tower of the Ministry of Silence, Inspector Ada Reyes spread a map of Vell across her desk and began, with a red pencil, to draw a circle around the places where prayers went unanswered.

The circle was small. The circle was closing.

At its center, one rooftop, two women, and an ember beginning, very softly, to glow through Felix Zhou's clenched fist like a heartbeat that had finally decided to count itself.
