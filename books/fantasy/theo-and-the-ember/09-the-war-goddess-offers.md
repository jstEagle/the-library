## 9. The War-Goddess Offers

Felix had not slept properly in six days. Sleep was a country she'd been exiled from — every time she closed her eyes, she felt the ember turning in her chest like a coal someone was breathing on, and the city outside Juniper's safehouse hummed with answered prayers gone wrong, and somewhere in that noise, she was certain, Inspector Ada Reyes was reading her ledger.

So when sleep finally took her, it did not take her gently.

She dreamed of a battlefield.

Not the muddy chaos of Vell's riots — something older, vaster, wrong in its scale. A plain that stretched past the curve of the world, under a sky the color of an old bruise. Two armies faced each other across it, and they were not made of soldiers. They were made of *names*. She could see them: towering figures of light and smoke, each one a word too large for a human mouth, ranked in their thousands, waiting.

Between the armies, the snow was already falling.

"You're dreaming," said a voice behind her, "but not only."

Felix turned.

The woman standing there wore armor the way other people wore grief — close-fitted, long-worn, shaped to her over years until you couldn't tell where one ended and the other began. It was black iron chased with gold so fine it looked like writing. Her hair was white as the falling snow, cropped short for a helmet, and her eyes were the exact orange of banked coals. She was beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful, which is to say: your body understood the danger before your mind finished admiring it.

"You're Sera," Felix said. Her voice came out steady. She was almost proud of that.

"And you're the little thief who's been carrying my brother around in her pockets." Sera smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Five years of stolen charms. He told me he was being subtle. He was never subtle. When Theo hides, he hides *loudly*."

"He didn't hide," Felix said. "He slept. There's a difference, apparently. I've had the lecture."

"Oh, I'm sure you have." Sera began to walk, unhurried, along the ridge overlooking the plain. Snow gathered on her pauldrons and did not melt. After a moment, hating herself for it, Felix followed. In dreams, she'd learned, you follow. "Do you know what you're looking at?"

"The first war."

"The first *war*." Sera said the word with something almost like fondness, the way a veteran says the name of a dead friend. "Before your Ministry, before your silence, before some clever frightened mortals decided gods were a disease to be starved out. We stood here, all of us, every name that ever was, and we fought over a question so stupid I still can't say it without laughing." She stopped. Turned. "Who gets to answer the prayers."

"That's it? That's what killed the world's sky?"

"It's never 'it,' little thief. That's the question, isn't it — who answers. Every prayer is a door, and every god wants to be the one holding the key. And then—" Sera spread her hands, and the plain below seemed to lean toward her gesture, armies of light rippling like wheat— "then there was a war, and the war ate everything, because wars do. And at the end of it, one of us decided enough."

"Vessel," Felix said quietly.

Something moved through Sera's expression — fast, quickly mastered, but Felix had spent five years reading faces in a night market, faces of people deciding whether to sell their grief. She knew what she'd seen. Grief.

"Vessel," Sera agreed. "Our peacemaker. Our ender-of-things. The only one of us strong enough to hold every name at once and put them down like sleeping dogs." Her coal-orange eyes fixed on Felix, and the weight of them was physical, a hand pressing on her sternum. "You know what you are. Theo told you. I can see it sitting in you like a stone."

"I know what I *was*," Felix corrected. "I'm Felix Zhou. I steal memories. I live above a dye-shop in Copperlane. I like pickled radishes and I hate the Ministry and I have exactly three people in this world who'd notice if I died, and one of them is a cat."

For the first time, Sera laughed — a short, sharp, genuinely surprised sound, like a blade coming free of a scabbard. "Oh, he wasn't lying about you. He said you were stubborn. He said it with such *affection*."

"Don't talk about him like you know him."

"I've known him since before your city had a name." The humor drained out of her voice as quickly as it had come, leaving iron underneath. "Which brings me to why you're here, Felix Zhou of Copperlane, lover of pickled radishes. Look down."

Felix looked. The plain had changed while they talked — or she had finally seen it truly. The two armies were closer now, close enough that she could see the names flickering at their edges, hungry, half-remembered. Half-remembered. She thought of the prayer that had flooded Saltrow. The prayer that had called something *wrong* up out of a grave in Ashgrave cemetery. Gods remembered imperfectly by a century of frightened whispers, waking up crooked.

"They're coming," Sera said. "My kin. What's left of them, and what's left is not... whole. A hundred years of starvation does things to a name. They are waking up ravenous, and every single one of them has felt what you carry, the way a shark feels blood through dark water. Theo. Me. The drowned thing under the harbor. The pale one in the mountains who eats prayers instead of answering them. All of us, converging on you."

"Then let them starve," Felix snapped. "That's the Ministry's whole plan, isn't it? Silence works. A century of no gods and the worst thing in Vell was the rent."

"A century of no gods," Sera said softly, "and the winter is coming anyway."

The words dropped into the dream like stones into deep water. Felix felt them sink through her, cold spreading outward.

"What?"

"You think the snow in high summer started last week?" Sera gestured, and the sky tore open — not violently, just peeled back like skin, showing what was behind it. Not stars. Frost. A darkness so complete and so cold that Felix's dream-body ached looking at it, a hunger older than the war, older than the names. "The old divinity was the hearth-fire of the world, little thief. Not a metaphor. The actual heat. When your Ministry walled us off, the fire started going out. Slowly. So slowly no one noticed for eighty years. But the cold is patient, and it is *coming*, and when it arrives, it will not want your prayers or your cities or your souls. It will simply want everything to stop."

"So the gods come back to warm the world. Fine. Except your kin are waking up broken and cruel, the Ministry is going to burn half of Vell to stop them, and everyone I care about is standing in the middle." Felix wrapped her arms around herself. The frost-sky stared down at her like an eye. "And you're here because you want the ember."

"I'm here because I'm offering you a deal," Sera said. "And unlike my brother, I'm going to be honest about what it costs."

* * *

They sat, absurdly, on a shattered piece of wall that might once have marked the edge of the battlefield, and Sera laid it out the way a merchant lays out goods — which Felix recognized, professionally, and distrusted accordingly.

"Give me the ember. Not your life — I'm not asking you to die, and I'm not asking you to become anything. I take the fire, I take the attention, and you walk away *free*. Do you understand what I'm offering? Not power. Not glory. *Freedom*." Sera leaned forward, elbows on knees, snow sliding off her shoulders. "No god hunting you, because they'll be hunting me, and I have been hunted before and won. No Ministry, because Reyes follows the ember's residue like a hound, and once I have it, the trail leads to me, and I promise you the Inspector of Silence is not equipped for me. You keep your name. Your shop, if you ever get one. Your pickled radishes. Your smuggler with the warm hands — yes, I know about her, Theo tells me everything, it's insufferable. You get the ordinary mortal life you have spent five years clawing together out of nothing."

"And you get an army's worth of divine fire to fight a war with."

"I get the fire to fight the *cold* with," Sera said. "Yes, and the means to make my kin kneel while I do it. Both things are true. I have never pretended to be gentle, Felix. Gentle gods are the ones who flood districts because someone wished for rain. I am not gentle. I am *effective*, and right now effectiveness looks like this: one name, holding all the fire, ending the problem the way I ended the last one."

"With a war."

"With a *short* war." Sera's smile showed teeth. "There's a difference, and the difference is measured in how many of you are left breathing after."

Felix looked out at the plain. At the armies of names. At the frost beyond the torn sky, patient as arithmetic.

"And if I say no?"

Sera was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, the saleswoman was gone, and what remained was older and tireder and, somehow, more frightening.

"Then you carry it, and they come for you, one by one, and each of them will offer you something worse than freedom. Theo offers love. I offer safety. The pale one in the mountains will offer you your missing years — every memory they cut out of you, every month between the woman you were and the woman you are, laid out like a feast. And eventually, little thief, one of them will offer something you can't refuse, and the ember goes to a god anyway, except by then you'll have been carved hollow getting there, and you'll have lost the smuggler and the inspector and maybe yourself, and you'll have gotten nothing you couldn't have had today for free."

"That's not an offer. That's a threat with better posture."

"All my offers look like that from certain angles." Sera rose, and rose, and kept rising — the armor drinking the bruise-colored light, until she stood taller than the horizon had any right to allow. "I'll tell you a secret, Felix Zhou, because honesty disarms better than any blade I own. Five years ago, when they remade you, they chose the shape of your life carefully. A thief. An orphan of the system, alone, unloved, unattached. Someone with nothing to lose and no one to lose it *for*. Do you know why?"

Felix's mouth had gone dry inside the dream. "Why?"

"Because a vessel with nothing is easy to spend. But you went and ruined it, didn't you. You built rules. You stole only pain. You learned people's faces. And now there's a woman with warm hands who looks at you like you're a person, and an inspector reading your ledger and finding mercy in it, and suddenly the vessel has *things*, and vessels with things hesitate, and hesitation is the most dangerous material in any war." Sera's voice gentled into something terrible. "I don't need your answer tonight. But I need it soon. The snow doesn't wait, and neither do my kin, and neither — I'm sorry to say — does Ada Reyes. She found your ledger, Felix. By tomorrow night she'll have found you."

"She already—"

"Dreams travel faster than inspectors. Goodnight, little thief." The goddess began to fade, armor dissolving into the snowfall, and her voice came last, from everywhere, like the cold itself speaking: "When you decide — and you will decide, everyone decides — ask yourself the only question that matters. Not *what do I owe the world.* Not *what am I owed.* Ask: **who am I, when I'm safe?** Because that's the person you'll actually be. Everything else is just armor."

* * *

Felix woke gasping in the dark of the safehouse, hand pressed flat against her sternum like she could hold the door of herself shut.

The room was cold. Colder than it should have been, even with summer dying outside the windows. Frost feathered the inside of the glass in patterns that looked, if she squinted, like ranks of standing figures.

Juniper was awake. Of course she was — Juniper slept like a cat in a strange house, one ear always open. She sat up in her cot, hair wild, watching Felix with those eyes that saw too much.

"Bad dream?"

"Worse," Felix said. Her voice came out rough. "A sales pitch."

She swung her feet to the floor and froze. On the table by the window, where she'd left it, lay her lockpick kit — the small worn leather roll that had been hers since she was nine years old, the one possession she'd kept through everything, through the gaps in her memory, through death and remaking, the one constant thread of *her*.

Someone had arranged the picks in a neat row.

At the end of the row, gleaming where no pick had been before, lay a sliver of black iron chased with gold so fine it looked like writing.

A gift. Or a promise. Or a leash.

From the corner of the room, where the shadows were deepest, Theo's voice — quiet, tired, and afraid for the first time since Felix had known him:

"She gave you a token. Felix — whatever you do, don't hold it while you're deciding. Sera's gifts aren't gifts. They're *questions*, and they only have one answer."

Outside, the snow was falling harder.
