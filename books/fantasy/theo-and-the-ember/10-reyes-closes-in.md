## 10. Reyes Closes In

The flood left a signature.

Ada Reyes stood in the ruined lane in Saltrow, boots sinking into mud that had been cobblestone two days ago, and watched her team work. Water still wept from the upper windows of the tenements. A boy's shoe floated past her feet, small and laced, and she made herself not look at it.

"Residue's strongest here," said Okonkwo, crouching over a brass instrument shaped like a tuning fork crossed with a compass. The Ministry issued one to every field inspector; most of them used theirs to open sealed doors. Reyes had spent eleven years learning to read hers the way other people read weather.

"How strong?"

"Strong enough." He glanced up. "You've seen this pattern before."

She had. Every confiscated charm the Ministry ever processed left a residue — faint, orderly, catalogued. What soaked Saltrow was nothing like catalogue. It moved like handwriting. It looped and doubled back and hesitated at certain doorways as if checking whether anyone inside was home, and then it pooled, thick and bright, around the drainage grate at the corner of Weaver's Lane, where someone had knelt in the water long enough for the magic to soak through their knees.

Someone had tried to hold the rain back with their hands.

"Whoever carried this didn't cause the flood," Reyes said. "They got here after it started. They tried to stop it."

Okonkwo turned the instrument slowly. "That's not how the briefing reads."

"The briefing was written by people who weren't standing in this mud." She crouched beside him and held out her hand for the fork. When its twin prongs hovered over the grate, they shivered — not toward the Ministry vaults downtown, not toward any registered source, but east, up the hill, toward the terraced streets where smugglers kept their lofts and the rooftops connected like teeth.

East, along a trail of residue that was already fading, already drying, already running out.

"We have until sundown," she said. "After that it goes cold and we're reading tea leaves again."

* * *

The Ministry's files on the ember-bearer were thin because the Ministry's files on everything were thin; that was the point of them. Silence did not keep records. But Reyes had spent three days doing what the Ministry pretended it never did: reading.

She read the incident reports from the night market — the clerk who'd bought a stolen grief-charm and then, forty minutes later, wept openly on the tram while strangers patted his shoulder and told him his prayers were heard. She read the report from Saint Verity's hospice, where a dying lamplighter had sat upright in his bed at midnight and spoken clearly to his daughter, who had arrived too late, and then died smiling, and where the attending nurse had written in the margin of her log, in a hand that shook: *the room smelled like woodsmoke and there was no fire.*

And she read the ledger of magical incidents going back a hundred years, which was the shortest book in the building. One page. The gods went quiet. The end.

Now the gods were loud, and every answered prayer traced back along a fading thread of residue to a woman nobody could find, and Deputy Director Corvane wanted a name by Friday.

Reyes wanted something else. She hadn't named it yet.

She found it, or a piece of it, at half past four in the afternoon, in the residue itself. She'd taken samples from six sites — Saltrow's grate, the flooded stairwell, the doorway of a chandler's shop where a prayer for safe passage had been answered so thoroughly that the shopkeeper now refused to leave his own front room. She laid the readings side by side on her desk under lamplight, the brass forks humming faintly against the wood, and she saw what she should have seen in Saltrow.

The handwriting wasn't just looping. It was *choosing*. At the flooded stairwell, the residue bent away from a child's bedroom window and gathered instead beneath the floorboards of an empty flat. At the chandler's, it skirted the family quarters entirely. Whatever carried the ember had moved through the worst night Vell had seen in a decade, and the trail of it avoided every occupied room it passed.

Magic that flinched away from sleeping children. That was not the behavior of a weapon. That was the behavior of a person trying very hard not to hurt anyone while everyone hunted them.

Reyes sat back. Her own reflection looked out of the dark office window, thin and gray, and behind it the city lights came on district by district, and somewhere up in those lights was the woman whose trail tasted like mercy.

"Find the bearer," Corvane had said. "Recover the ember. Terminate resistance if required."

He hadn't specified what counted as resistance. He never did. That was how the Ministry stayed clean: it let words like *required* do all the bleeding.

She filed her interim report with the residue analysis attached and left the interpretation column blank. Then she put on her coat and went hunting anyway, alone, because whatever she was about to find, she did not want witnesses.

* * *

The trail ended above a ropewalk in Tannery Row, at a loft with a green door and no nameplate, in a building owned — according to three layers of falsified harbor records — by a shipping concern that had never shipped anything.

Reyes climbed the outside stairs quietly. She had brought no team. She had brought her sidearm, her tuning fork, and eleven years of knowing how raids actually went versus how the manuals described them, and the difference was always the same: the manuals assumed the people inside would be monsters.

She tested the door. Locked, but the lock was old, and old locks in Tannery Row were bought secondhand from the same three fences, and Reyes had arrested two of the fences. The pick took ninety seconds.

Inside: warmth. Lamplight. The smell of coffee and tar and, underneath both, woodsmoke — the same impossible woodsmoke from the hospice report. Two voices low in the next room, one sharp and quick, one rougher, amused, mid-laughter. And threaded through all of it, so faint she almost missed it, the hum.

Every hair on her arms rose. The tuning fork in her pocket had begun to sing without being touched.

*Here*, said the part of her trained for exactly this moment. *Call it in. Seal the block. Do your job.*

*She knelt in floodwater for strangers*, said the other part, the one she'd been starving for years.

She stepped into the doorway of the back room with her weapon drawn and her badge already up, because the badge mattered more — badges stopped fights, guns started them.

"Don't," she said. "Either of you."

Two women. The tall one by the window had a smuggler's build and a smuggler's hands — scarred, steady, already drifting toward the knife on the table until Reyes' badge caught the light and stopped her. The smaller one stood frozen by the cold hearth, dark-haired, ordinary-looking in the way of people who had practiced being invisible, and around her the air shimmered like heat off summer stone except it was winter-cold and it *hummed*.

The ember-bearer looked terrified. That was the first thing. Not dangerous. Terrified.

"Inspector Ada Reyes, Ministry of Silence," Reyes said. "Felix Zhou. I know that's you. Don't run — the residue is all over this loft, if you run I follow it, and I'm faster than I look."

"You look fast," the tall one said. Juniper Vale; Reyes knew the file, knew the shipwreck, knew the debt of silence the harbor authorities had been bribed into forgetting. "Look like a greyhound somebody taught to read."

"Juniper." The smaller one — Felix — said her name like a warning, and something passed between them that Reyes recognized despite herself, because she had once had someone who said her name like that, before she'd paid to have the memory of why removed.

"I'm not here to hurt you," Reyes said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it. "I'm here before the people who will."

"And we should believe that why?" Felix asked.

"Because I could have called in a full raid team an hour ago. Because I read your residue for three days and it runs away from children. Because—" She stopped. Steadied. "Because in Saltrow, somebody knelt in the floodwater and tried to hold it back with their bare hands, and the Ministry briefing says that was you causing it. I don't think the briefing is true. Prove me right or prove me wrong, but either way you need to be gone from here in the next five minutes, because when I don't check in, they send Okonkwo, and Okonkwo brings thirty people and no questions."

The loft went quiet. Down in the ropewalk, a bell rang the quarter hour.

It was Juniper who moved first — of course it was, smugglers survived on exits — sliding a loose board aside and revealing a rope-ladder down into the tannery's guts. But Felix didn't move toward it. She was looking at Reyes with an expression Reyes had seen once before, years ago, in a mirror: someone weighing how much of themselves it was safe to show.

"You erased something," Felix said quietly. "Your grief. I can smell it. It's like a room with the furniture missing."

Reyes' arm dipped an inch before she caught it. "That's not possible."

"No," Felix agreed softly. "It isn't. And yet."

Then the hearth *ignited* — no flame, no fuel, just light pouring up out of the cold ashes like dawn happening indoors — and the whole loft filled with the sound of the tuning fork singing, and Felix said, "We really do have to go now," and they went, down the ladder, into the dark, gone between one heartbeat and the next.

Reyes stood alone in the warm empty room with her heart slamming and her certainty leaking out through the floorboards.

She should have called it in immediately. Instead she stood there breathing woodsmoke, and noticed, on the table by the window, where the tall one's knife-hand had been — a book. Small, leather-bound, corners worn soft. Left behind in the scramble, or abandoned deliberately, she couldn't tell which.

She picked it up.

A ledger. Five years of entries in a small precise hand. She told herself she was doing her job. Evidence recovery. Standard procedure.

She opened it, and read, and did not stop for a long time.

*Widow, Saltrow market, third stall. Sold her husband's last words back to her twice — she keeps selling them when the money runs out. Stopped taking payment after the second time. Told her it was defective.*

*Lamplighter, dying, Saint Verity's. Took the memory he asked for. Charged him one copper, which is what it costs to pretend you're a professional. Sat with him afterward until the end. Do not write down why.*

*Clerk, Ministry of Records. Bought a grief-charm he couldn't afford. Followed him home. Left it on his step with the price scratched out.*

Page after page. Names, faces, dates. Five years of theft, yes — but theft like triage. She stole pain. Only pain, or nearly only; here and there an entry wobbled, an honest note of doubt: *took a happy one today by mistake. gave it back. felt sick for a week.* And in the margins, in the same small hand, things that were not business at all:

*Nobody should have to carry some things alone.*

*The city makes people forget so the city can keep going. Someone has to remember for them.*

Reyes sat down slowly in the smuggler's chair, in the warm room that smelled of woodsmoke and coffee, holding five years of another woman's secret mercy, and thought about the word *terminate*, and about the furniture-missing room inside her own head, and about what she had given up — what she had *paid* to give up — and whether anyone, ever, had offered to carry it for her instead.

Somewhere below, a whistle sounded. Short, short, long. The signal that meant a sweep team was moving into Tannery Row, which meant Okonkwo, which meant thirty people and no questions, closing in on an empty loft.

Except the loft wasn't empty. It had her in it, and the ledger, and the ember-bearer's whole hidden history in her hands.

The whistle came again. Closer.

Ada Reyes looked at the ledger, and at the door, and understood that whatever she decided in the next thirty seconds would decide everything after it.

She put the ledger inside her coat, over her heart, and turned to face the stairs.
