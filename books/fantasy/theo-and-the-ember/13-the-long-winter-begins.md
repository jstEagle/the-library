## 13. The Long Winter Begins

The first snow fell on Midsummer's Eve, and the whole city of Vell stopped to watch it die.

It didn't die, though. That was the thing nobody said out loud, standing in doorways with their hands full of warm bread and their mouths full of nothing. The snow landed on the cobbles of Tanner's Row and it *stayed*. Landed on the awnings, the laundry lines, the shoulders of a fishwife who had come to market in her summer shawl — and stayed.

By dawn there were four inches of it, and by noon, when the sun should have been beating down like a brass hammer, the snow was still there, still white, still refusing every law the world had ever agreed upon.

In the Lamplighters' Ward, an old woman knelt in the drifts with her bare hands pressed together and wept, because she had prayed for rain three days ago, back when prayer seemed like a harmless kind of madness, and she understood now that someone had heard her.

* * *

**Reyes**

Inspector Ada Reyes stood at her office window in the Ministry of Silence and watched the snow bury the city she'd spent eleven years keeping quiet.

Behind her, her desk had become a battlefield. Reports from six districts, each one more absurd than the last. A well in Copperkettle Lane that ran with light instead of water. A man in the Shambles whose shadow got up and walked away from him every night at dusk, and came home before morning, smelling of smoke. Children born last week with faint gold flecks in their irises, like embers caught behind glass.

"Read me the tally again," Reyes said.

Her aide, Pellam, cleared his throat. "Confirmed manifestations since the flood event: two hundred forty. Unconfirmed but credible: nine hundred or better. Prayer-cult membership is doubling every—" he checked his slate, "—every thirty-six hours. The Archivist wants to know if we're initiating the Containment Protocol."

Reyes didn't answer immediately. She was looking at the ledger lying open on the corner of her desk — Felix Zhou's ledger, five years of thefts recorded in a small, disciplined hand. She had read it four times now, and each reading made her feel less certain and more awake, which was not a combination the Ministry rewarded.

Every entry had the same structure. What was taken. From whom. And then, in a smaller script, almost apologetic:

*Marta Ellery, widow. Took memory of husband's final year. Payment: none. Left her savings untouched. She asked me to take it. She thanked me after.*

*Ivo Crane, lamplighter, dying. Took last memory per request. Held his hand while it finished.*

Two hundred entries. Two hundred people who had *asked*, and one thief who had listened. It wasn't theft so much as triage. Reyes had erased her own grief once — her mother's face, gone in a white flash at a Ministry clinic, paid for with three years' wages — and she knew exactly what mercy looked like when it was sold over a counter. It looked like this ledger.

"The Inspector?" Pellam prompted.

"No," Reyes said. "Not yet."

"Not yet on containment, or not yet on telling the Archivist?"

"Not yet on either." She turned from the window. Snowflakes drifted past the glass, fat and slow, each one catching the light like a tiny lantern. "Pellam, what do you know about why the Ministry starves the gods?"

He blinked. "That's — ma'am, that's not a question anyone asks."

"I'm asking it."

He lowered his voice as if the walls might be listening, which, given this building, they probably were. "I know there was a war. Before the Ministry. Gods fighting gods, using cities for dice. I know the founders decided no power that big should ever answer prayers again. That's all anyone knows. It's doctrine, not history."

"Doctrine," Reyes repeated. She picked up the ledger and closed it gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "And now the doctrine is falling out of the sky in July."

Outside, across the plaza below the Ministry steps, a crowd had begun to gather. They weren't shouting. That was the strange part. Three hundred people, standing in the snow, holding candles that wouldn't stay lit in the wind — and lighting anyway, every single one, the moment they were struck. They stood there in silence, faces turned up toward the gray sky, waiting.

"They're not even chanting," Pellam said uneasily.

"No," Reyes said. "They're praying." She pulled her coat off its hook. "Get my hat. And Pellam — find out everything you can about the Vault of Names. Quietly. Don't use the Archive."

"Why not?"

"Because whatever the Ministry has been doing for a hundred years, I don't think the Archive is allowed to remember it either."

**Juniper**

Juniper Vale stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Regret* — which wasn't currently a ship, being parked on a rooftop in the undercity where ships had no business being — and watched the snow come down over Vell like the sky was trying to apologize for something.

"You're brooding," said Wren, her deckhand, who was mending a sail that hadn't touched water in two months.

"I'm strategizing."

"You're brooding with your arms crossed. Strategizing looks different. You pace."

Juniper uncrossed her arms. Then, out of spite, paced twice.

Below them, the undercity seethed. Not rioting yet — the undercity was too old and too tired for honest riots — but shifting, the way a sea shifts before it decides to be a storm. The cults had found them down here. Of course they had; the undercity was where Vell kept everyone it had failed, and the failed were always the first to pray.

She could hear them through the grating: a congregation of dockworkers and gutter-runners singing to the ember-god in voices that cracked on the high notes. *Theo who waits. Theo who warms. Theo, look down.* They'd gotten the name right within a day of the first answered prayer, which meant someone was talking, which meant Felix's secret was leaking out of her like heat out of a broken window.

And somewhere down in that maze of tunnels and old bones, Felix was hiding with her guilt and her impossible name, and Juniper was up here, and between them lay the conversation they'd had three nights ago, still bleeding.

*You sought me out. Five years ago. Was any of it real, or were you just following orders from a god I've never met?*

*I don't know,* Juniper had said, because it was true, and truth was the only currency she'd never learned to counterfeit. *I honestly don't know.*

Felix had looked at her the way you look at a coin you've just discovered is shaved. Then she'd walked into the dark, and Juniper had let her go, because chasing her felt like proving the accusation.

"She'll come around," Wren said.

"She shouldn't have to come around. I should have told her on the first night."

"Would you have? If the god hadn't shown up and made it obvious?"

Juniper thought about it honestly, because Felix deserved honesty even in absentia. "No. I'd have kept finding reasons."

"There it is." Wren bit her thread. "You know what my gran used to say about debts to gods?"

"Gran was a smuggler too. Gran would have said run."

"Gran said run," Wren conceded. "But she also said a debt you can't see the shape of is the only kind that collects interest. You saved by Theo. Someone paid for you. Now winter's starting and every god in the dark is looking for what's inside that girl." He nodded at the snow. "Interest is coming due, Captain. On everybody."

Juniper looked south, past the rooftops, past the walls, to where the mountains used to be visible on clear days. There were no clear days anymore. The peaks were gone — swallowed by cloud, and the cloud was going white at the edges, and the white was creeping downhill.

Snow in the passes in midsummer. Snow that would reach the lowlands by autumn, and the far countries by spring, and keep coming, year over year, because two immortal siblings were circling each other above one stolen girl and neither of them cared what froze underneath.

"Weigh anchor," Juniper said suddenly.

Wren stared at her. "We're on a roof."

"Then unweigh whatever a ship does on a roof. Get the runners greased. We move tonight."

"Move where?"

"Down. All the way down." She was already pulling on her gloves, her heart doing something complicated that was equal parts dread and relief. "There's a girl in the deep tunnels who owes me nothing, and I owe her an apology, and I'm done letting gods decide the schedule."

**Sera**

High above the clouds — above weather itself, in the thin cold country where only gods and dying stars lived — Sera watched the world whiten and felt nothing resembling regret.

Her brother had always loved mortals too much. That was his flaw, the crack in him. He'd slept five years in a thief's pocket, content to be small, content to be *carried*, and now he woke and called it love.

"He's hiding her," she said to her generals, though generals was a generous word for what they were — half-remembered things stitched together from prayers, wearing armor made of old fear. "Underground. In the dark, among the root-people and the forgotten. He thinks I won't dig."

"And will you, War-Mother?"

"I'll bring the mountain down gently," Sera said, "and then less gently."

She extended one hand. Far below, in a fishing village on the northern coast, a woman who had prayed for her drowned sons came out of her cottage and found both of them standing in the surf, alive, unchanged, dripping — except that they did not blink, and did not speak, and smiled at their mother with borrowed mouths until the neighbors came running with fire.

Sera withdrew her attention. One miracle at a time. Fear was a lever, and she had a great many levers, and the world below had forgotten how levers worked.

But there was a splinter in her certainty, and its name was Felix.

The vessel should have come to her by now. Should have crawled, begged, bargained — they always did, in the end; mortality broke the same way every time. But this one had taken the ember and *hidden*, and worse, she'd hidden it not in a vault but in herself, in that stubborn scrap of self she refused to surrender even when Sera had offered her safety, protection, *peace* —

Refused me, Sera reminded herself. Offered her everything and she chose the cold and the dark and the company of smugglers.

It was, Sera admitted privately, almost impressive. Mortals were usually so eager to be saved.

"Prepare the descent," she said. "When the snow reaches the city gates, so do I. My brother wants a herald?" Her smile was the smile that had ended the first divine war, and started it. "I'll give Vell a revelation it can't survive."

**Marta Ellery**

In a narrow room above a chandlery in Tanner's Row, Marta Ellery lit her seventh candle and tried to remember her husband's face.

She couldn't. That was the bargain — she'd known it was the bargain, she'd *begged* for the bargain, and for three years the peace had been worth the price. But now the gods were awake, and the gods, it turned out, could see into the holes people carried, and something had noticed hers.

It had come to her last night as a warmth in the empty space where Gideon's last year used to be. A voice like banked coals.

*I cannot give him back. But I can show you what he became. Would you like to see?*

She'd said yes. Of course she'd said yes. And the warmth had poured into the hollow, and now she remembered — not Gideon as he was, but Gideon as he'd been *kept*: folded up like a letter in some vast patient pocket, alive in the way a candle flame is alive between one room and the next. Waiting for her. Still waiting.

All she had to do was go down to the plaza and stand with the others and pray loudly enough, and the ember-god would finish the work. All she had to do was help find the woman who carried him. The thief. Everyone was looking for the thief now; there were broadsheets, crude woodcut portraits of a sharp-faced young woman with ink-stained fingers.

Marta lit the eighth candle. She felt no conflict, and that frightened her more than anything else. Somewhere in the hollowed place where her grief used to live, someone else's certainty had moved in, furnished and warm, and it fit so perfectly she couldn't tell anymore which wishes were hers.

*Come out, little thief,* said the warmth, kindly, in a voice that was almost Gideon's. *You've kept him long enough.*

**Felix**

Deep in the undercity, in a dead priest's cellar that smelled of cold stone and older incense, Felix Zhou sat cross-legged on the floor with her palms open in her lap, watching the ember glow there like a captive sunrise, and counted what she had left.

One name, burning. One god attached to it, mercifully silent for the past two days — sulking, she suspected, or grieving, or simply giving her the space she'd demanded. One smuggler she couldn't stop thinking about and couldn't forgive yet. One ledger in enemy hands, full of her own handwriting, her own mercy, exposed like skin.

And outside, above her, through fifty feet of rock and the whole weight of the city: snow. Falling in July. Falling because of her.

She could hear the cultists singing through the grating overhead. *Theo who warms.* She could hear, farther off, the counter-sound: Ministry drums, the Silence patrols breaking up gatherings with their white hoods and their erasure-clamps, and the crowds fighting back now, throwing stones, throwing *prayers*, both equally useless and equally sincere. Vell was tearing along a seam nobody had known was there, and she was the needle that had made the first stitch.

"Five years," she said aloud to the empty cellar. "Five years I stole other people's pain so mine would have competition. And it turns out I was carrying the end of the world the whole time, and I didn't even have the decency to be innocent about it."

The ember pulsed, once, gently. Like a heartbeat agreeing with her.

Above, muffled by stone, a bell began to ring — not the Ministry's flat iron tone but something older, bronze and wild, ringing from a tower that had been sealed for a century. Then a second bell. Then a third, answering across the frozen city, and beneath the bells, rising up through the grates and the drains and the cracks in the ancient stone, a sound Felix had never heard in her life and recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own name:

Vell, praying. All of it at once.

She closed her fists around the light and stood, and that was when she saw, in the doorway at the top of the cellar stairs, silhouetted against torchlight from the tunnel beyond — a tall figure in a long coat, snow melting off the shoulders, and behind that figure, half a dozen more, moving with the particular discipline of people who had done terrible things efficiently for a very long time.

White hoods pushed back. Ministry issue. And at their head, holding a lantern in one hand and a familiar ledger in the other, Inspector Ada Reyes.

"Felix Zhou," Reyes said, and her breath smoked in the freezing air. "We need to talk before the sky finishes falling. And you're going to want to hear what I found in your book."
