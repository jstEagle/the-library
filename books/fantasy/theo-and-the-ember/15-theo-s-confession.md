## 15. Theo's Confession

The undercity smelled of wet stone and old iron, and for three days it had smelled of nothing else, because Felix hadn't left.

She'd found a cistern chamber off one of Juniper's smuggler routes — a vaulted room where the old waterworks had collapsed into something like a cave, stalactites dripping slow clocks onto black pools. Juniper had strung lanterns and laid out bedrolls with the brisk competence of someone who had slept in worse places, and then she had done the hardest thing anyone had ever done for Felix: she'd left her alone.

Not far. Just up the passage, where the sound of her breathing wouldn't carry. Close enough to come running if Felix called.

Felix didn't call.

She sat on the edge of the dry basin with her knees pulled up and the ember burning in her chest — not literally, or not only; there was no light leaking through her skin that she could see, no heat anyone else could feel. But she could feel it. Ever since the Vault of Names, ever since she'd stood in the frost of her own death and watched the god Vessel come apart so that a woman named Felix Zhou could walk away wearing her face, she could feel it like a second heartbeat, slightly out of rhythm with the first.

Reyes was somewhere above her with an offer of immunity and a truth that had turned Felix's whole understanding of the world sideways: *the Ministry has been starving the gods on purpose.* Not superstition. Not cruelty. Strategy. A hundred years of quiet, purchased at the price of a hundred years of unanswered prayers, and now the bill was coming due in snow, in high summer, in riots and floods and things summoned wrong from half-remembered names.

And Theo was —

Theo was sitting across the basin from her, on a broken section of aqueduct, doing what he had been doing for five years, apparently: waiting.

He looked young. That was still the thing that unsettled her most. Gods in the old stories were vast, crowned, terrible, and here he was in a body that might have been nineteen, barefoot on the stone, his coat too big at the shoulders, watching her with the patience of someone who had once waited inside a dead woman's memories for longer than civilizations lasted. His presence filled the cistern the way water fills a glass — completely, quietly, without asking.

"Say it," Felix said. Her voice came out rough. She hadn't spoken aloud in hours except to argue with herself, which she'd been losing.

"You already know."

"I want to hear you say it." She unfolded, set her feet on the floor, faced him fully. "You knew what I was. Before the lamplighter. Before the charm cracked open. You've known since — " She stopped. Made herself finish. "Since five years ago. Since the day someone made me human and hid you inside me like a coin sewn into a hem."

"Yes," Theo said.

"And you stayed."

"Yes."

"Inside my head. Inside my work. Inside every charm I stole and every memory I sold, you were *there*, and you let me think I was alone. You let me build a whole life on top of a grave and never told me whose grave it was." Her hands had become fists without her permission. The ember pulsed against them, warm as a held bird. "Why?"

It was the question underneath all the others. Why not take her over. Why not speak sooner. Why not reach up through her dreams the way Sera did, why not offer bargains and armies and safety, why not simply *claim* what he claimed she carried.

Theo looked down at his own hands. Young hands. When he spoke, his voice had none of Sera's thunder in it. It was just a voice, a little tired, a little afraid — and that fear, from a god, undid something in her before he'd even answered.

"Because everything else tried taking," he said. "And look how that's going."

* * *

He told it slowly, and he did not dress it up.

"The first divine war ended because Vessel chose it," he said. "Not because any side won. Sera will tell you otherwise, when she tells you anything — she'll say the war was ended *to* her, that the ending was taken out of her hands. It wasn't. Your predecessor walked into the Vault of Names carrying every name worth killing for, and she broke them. All of them. Herself included. There was no strategy. There was no plan for what came next. There was just a god who looked at what worship had made of the world and decided she would rather stop existing than keep being the reason for it."

Felix remembered the frost. Remembered standing inside the memory of her own unmaking, feeling the moment split — the god falling, and something small and stubborn and human-shaped being built from the pieces, quick and dirty and desperate, by hands she still couldn't see.

"Who remade me?" she asked. It wasn't the first time she'd asked. It was the first time he'd been able to answer.

"A mortal," Theo said. "That's the part nobody remembers, because mortals don't get remembered for this kind of thing. A keeper of the vault. One of the archivists who tended the names after the war, back when tending them still mattered. They saw Vessel fall and they couldn't — " He paused, and Felix realized with a strange lurch that he was choosing his words carefully. Not hiding. Weighing. Like a man walking on ice he respected. "They couldn't bear for it to be the end. So they gathered what was left. The ember. The part of her that was still *choosing*, even at the very last — and they hid it. Not in a relic. Relics get found. Not in another god. Gods get used. They put it in the only vessel in creation that the gods would never think to look inside."

"A person," Felix said.

"A person who wanted nothing to do with any of them." Something moved across his face — not quite a smile. "They gave you a life, Felix. A real one, not a shell. A childhood. Fears. That scar on your knee from the stairwell in the Lamplighters' Row tenement — that's yours. Nobody wrote that. And then they died, like mortals do, and took the secret with them, and for a long time the ember slept in you the way embers sleep: quietly, needing nothing, waiting for air."

"The charms," Felix said. "My thefts."

"That was the air." He spread his hands. "Every memory you took, every stolen moment you carried — grief, mostly, you always took the grief — each one was a door. Small ones. Cracked windows. I'd been drifting for a century, Felix, scattered thin across every prayer nobody answered, and your charms were the first solid ground I'd touched since the war. I fell into them the way cold falls into a house. I didn't mean to stay. Then I understood what I was staying *inside*, and I — "

He stopped.

Felix felt the ember turn over, slow, like something waking in warm water.

"You knew," she said again. Quieter now. "Five years ago. The first charm I ever lifted. You were in it, and you knew what I was."

"I knew there was something in you older than you," Theo said. "I didn't know what. Knowing took time. Knowing took — " he laughed, short and helpless, "— it took watching you fence a widow's worst day to a Ministry clerk for coin and feel bad about it afterward. It took watching you steal only pain. It took watching you give half your take to the beggars on Candle Street while telling yourself it was tax evasion against your own conscience. Do you know how long it takes to learn a person from the inside of their pockets? Years. It took years. And the whole time, the other gods were waking too, and Sera was casting about for her ember, and every year I waited was a year she got closer, and the Ministry got closer, and the winter got — "

"Closer," Felix finished.

"Closer." He met her eyes. "So ask me the real question, Felix. Ask me why I didn't just tell you on the first night."

* * *

She should have. She should have demanded it right then, and some hard, survival-flattened part of her had been demanding it for days — through the rooftop chase, through the safehouse, through Sera's dream-offer and Reyes's ledger and the shattering discovery of her own missing months. *Why hide? Why let me stumble blind into all of it? Why let me be hunted without knowing what I was hunted for?*

But she had spent three days alone in a cistern turning the question over, and she had noticed something about it. Every time she asked it, she was really asking a different question. A smaller, older one. The question a nine-year-old orphan had asked every foster family and every street priest and every closed door in Vell:

*Why didn't anybody want me enough to tell me the truth?*

She wasn't going to ask him that. She was not going to hand a god that question.

"Why," she said instead, flat. "Tell me straight. No parable. No patience. Straight."

"Straight," Theo agreed. He stood. He didn't approach — she noticed that, noted it the way she'd learned to note exits — but he stood, and the lantern light caught him, and for a moment she saw through the boy-shape to something enormous and gentle underneath, something that had once been prayed to by entire generations and had answered almost none of those prayers, and she thought: *because answering is a kind of taking.*

"Because if I'd told you," he said, "you'd have chosen with me in the room. And a choice made under the eyes of the thing you're choosing isn't free. It's performance. It's duty wearing freedom's clothes. Sera offered you protection and you refused her — do you know why that mattered? Not because refusing her was wise. Because you refused her *knowing what she was*. You weighed it. You chose. That's the only currency I have ever cared about, Felix, and I have watched a hundred centuries of worshippers spend the counterfeit version — love under pressure, faith under threat, devotion because the alternative is fire. I did not want that from you. I would rather have nothing from you than that."

"So you let me be hunted instead."

"So I let you be *you*," he said, and his voice cracked on it, actually cracked, a god's voice fracturing like a boy's, "for as long as being you was possible. And I stayed close enough to catch you if you fell, and far enough that you couldn't feel my hands, and I told myself that was love and not cowardice, and some nights I still don't know which it was." He exhaled. "There. Straight. I hid inside the only home I had, knowing what it was, because I wanted you to choose me with your eyes open, and I was terrified that the choosing itself would close them."

The cistern dripped. Somewhere far up the passage, Juniper shifted, pretending to sleep, failing.

Felix sat with it.

It would have been easier if he'd lied. Easier if he'd had a clean reason, a noble reason, a reason she could hate. But what he'd given her was messier than that — a confession with cowardice in it, and stubbornness, and a refusal to take what wasn't offered that ran so deep it had cost him five years of silence and nearly cost the world its spring. He hadn't wanted her worship. He'd wanted her *yes*. And he'd been willing to lose everything, including possibly her, to keep the difference between those two things intact.

Nobody had ever wanted her yes before. People wanted her skills, her silence, her ledger, her ember. Sera wanted her surrender. The Ministry wanted her extinction. Even Juniper —

No. Juniper was a wound for later. Or for never. Felix didn't have the blood to spare tonight.

"You could still take it," she said. She stood too now, across the basin from him, the black water between them like a border neither would cross first. "The ember. If you wanted. Sera thinks you're weak for not trying. Reyes assumes you're playing a longer game. Everyone assumes a god takes what a god wants."

"I'm not everyone," Theo said.

"No," Felix said. "You're the god who slept in a thief's pockets for five years and paid rent in patience." She walked around the edge of the basin. Slowly. Giving herself every exit, honoring every exit anyway. She stopped an arm's length from him, close enough to see that his eyes weren't any color she could name — ember-colored, she supposed, now, everything was ember-colored — and she said the thing she had spent three days not saying.

"I'm angry at you."

"I know."

"I'm going to be angry at you for a long time. Maybe forever. You let me grieve a life I didn't know was a mask. You let me find out what I am from a frost-memory in a hole in the ground instead of from someone who loved me."

"Yes."

"And I believe you anyway." She heard herself say it and marveled at it, the way you marvel at a bridge holding. "That's the worst part. I believe every word. Because you had five years to lie to me and what you did instead was wait, and wait, and wait, and then confess like a man with something to lose." She reached out — her choice, her hand, her motion, and he went absolutely still as her fingers found the front of his oversized coat, holding the fabric, not him. Holding the fabric was something she could take back. "So here are the new terms. Listen carefully, because I'm only saying this once and I'm only meaning it once.

"You don't get the ember. Not given, not taken, not borrowed, not in a crisis, not for the war, not to save the world. If there's a way to save the world that doesn't require me to stop being me, we find it together or we don't find it. You advise. You don't decide. You want my company, you get all of me — the anger, the thefts, the guilt I pretend is a business expense. And if you ever, *ever* let me walk into something blind again because your principles needed protecting more than I did, I will spend whatever divinity I carry learning how to hurt you, and I will enjoy it. Are those terms acceptable?"

For a long moment Theo didn't answer, and Felix braced for patience, for that terrible gentleness, for some godly deferral that would leave her holding all the weight again.

Instead he smiled. It transformed him — the boy-shape suddenly inadequate around something that bright — and he said, hoarsely, "Accepted. Witnessed. Bound." And then, softer, in the voice of someone who had waited a hundred centuries for exactly this and was determined not to waste it on grandeur: "Felix. Thank you. For the anger especially."

"Don't make it weird."

"It's already weird. I'm a god being threatened by a pickpocket in a drainage cave. This is the best day I've had in a thousand years."

Against her will — genuinely against her will, she'd note it later as a personal failure — Felix laughed. It came out cracked and rusty from three days of disuse, and it echoed around the cistern, and the ember in her chest flared warm and easy for the first time since the Vault, like a fire finally fed.

From up the passage, deliberately loud: "If you two are done negotiating, we have a problem."

Juniper came around the corner with a lantern in one hand and her face doing something Felix had never seen it do — pale under the tan, jaw tight. Behind her, faint but unmistakable, rising through the stone and the water and the bones of the city, came a sound Felix had learned to dread in dreams.

Drums.

Sera's drums — but not in a dream this time. Above. In Vell. Thousands of feet marching in step, and beneath them, thinner, colder, the sound Felix somehow knew belonged to the Ministry: the great brass horns of Silence, the ones that ate prayer out of the air.

"They're not coming for the city," Juniper said. "They're coming for the undercity. Both of them. At once." She looked at Felix, then at Theo, then back. "Whatever terms you two worked out — I hope they include running."
