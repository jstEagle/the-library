## 20. War of Small Gods

The first thing Corporal Dain Mercer noticed was the warmth in his chest.

He had been marching — had been marching for what felt like years, though the war-goddess's army did not measure time the way soldiers did — when the ember struck him. It came down through the snow like a falling star reversed, upward, rising out of the cracked flagstones of Vell's Merchant Quarter and into him before he could flinch. No pain. Just heat, spreading outward from his sternum like a swallow of strong liquor on a cold night.

He stopped marching.

That alone should have killed him. In Sera's host, you did not stop marching. You marched until the goddess unmade your doubt, until her will wore grooves in yours like water wearing stone, and then you marched some more. Dain had been one of hers for a long time — long enough that he'd forgotten there had ever been anything else inside him to march *with*. He remembered a farm, vaguely. A sister. The smell of bread. Then Sera had found him at a recruiting fire, twenty years old and grieving, and had said *give me your anger and I'll give it teeth*, and he had.

Now the anger was still there. But it had edges now. It was *his* again, the way a knife is yours when you hold the handle instead of the blade.

"Dain." Beside him, Sergeant Okonkwo had stopped too. Her spear hung loose in her grip. She was staring at her own hands as if she'd never seen them before. "Dain, I can hear—"

"Hear what?"

"Me." She laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through into something else. "I can hear myself. Inside. There's — there's someone in here who isn't her."

Around them, the column was dissolving. Not fleeing — dissolving, the way fog dissolves when the sun finds it. Soldiers stood in the snow with their weapons forgotten, weeping, laughing, shouting names of people dead fifty years as if they were calling them home for supper. A woman dropped her shield and knelt in the slush, pressing both palms flat against the ground, and where she touched, the snow melted in a perfect handprint-shaped bloom of green.

Dain looked up. Above the rooftops, vast and furious, the war-goddess burned.

Sera had worn many shapes across the centuries — a wolf, a banner, a woman in bronze with eyes like siege-fires. Tonight she wore all of them at once, and none of them fit right, because ten thousand sparks of the god-ember had scattered through her army and every single one of them was a door she could no longer close.

*"MINE,"* she said, and the word flattened windows for six blocks.

"No," Dain heard himself say.

It was a small word. It was the smallest word in any language. And it came out of ten thousand throats at once, from ten thousand small warm fires that no army had ever held, no ministry had ever catalogued, no god had ever owned — and Sera's voice broke against it like a wave against a seawall made of ordinary people saying no.

Dain turned around. So did Okonkwo. So did the column, rank by rank, an army pivoting on nothing but its own returned selves.

They walked away from the war.

* * *

Inspector Ada Reyes stood in the Ministry of Silence's deepest archive, holding a lantern, hating herself for how easy this was going to be.

The archive sat four levels beneath the Ministry's headquarters, behind three doors that required her clearance and one that required a key she'd lifted off Director Hale's corpse an hour ago. She tried not to think about Hale's face when the ember-spark reached him. Tried not to think about how the director — rigid, gray, incorruptible Hale, who had signed off on forty years of burn orders — had gone to his knees in the middle of a briefing and whispered, *"Oh. Oh, I remember my son's name,"* before anyone could stop him.

Reyes had erased her grief eleven years ago. She knew exactly what Hale had felt in that moment, because she'd spent eleven years feeling the absence of it and calling that absence peace.

The archive doors opened onto a room the size of a cathedral, and Reyes's lantern found what she'd known it would find, because she'd read the requisition ledgers, because she'd done the arithmetic years ago and buried the answer under duty:

Confiscated magic. Mountains of it.

Charms stacked in iron cages floor to ceiling, thousands deep. Memory-vials racked like wine. Bound spirits sulking in lead-lined boxes. Names written on paper and folded seven times so they couldn't be spoken. A century of the Ministry's work — everything seized in the name of public safety, everything that was supposed to have been destroyed, everything instead hoarded down here in the dark where no prayer could reach it and no god could use it.

*Starving them,* Felix had said in the vault, and Reyes had wanted to argue, had wanted very badly to argue. *That's what the Silence is. Not protection. Starvation.*

She walked the aisles. Her boots echoed. Somewhere above, the city was rioting, or celebrating, or both — hard to tell the difference anymore, in Vell, on a night when snow fell in high summer and gods walked abroad and every beggar and baker in the city had a spark of divinity sitting in their chest like a swallowed coal.

Her orders, if any authority still existed to give them, would be clear: secure the archive. Contain the assets. Preserve the Silence.

Reyes set her lantern down on the flagstones, in the middle of the central aisle, equidistant from everything.

She took out her flask — real brandy, smuggled, a habit she'd told herself was harmless — poured it out over the stones, and then thought better of wasting good brandy, and drank it instead.

"Sorry," she said, to no one. To Hale. To the eleven-years-ago version of herself who had signed the consent form for her own erasure because the grief of losing her daughter had been a weight she genuinely believed would kill her, and the Ministry had offered her a knife to cut it out, and she had taken it, and they had kept the knife.

Then she went to the first cage and broke the lock.

The charms woke like birds. That was the only word for it — a rustling, a stirring, a rising chorus of small stored prayers suddenly remembering they were meant for open air. Reyes worked methodically, cage by cage, rack by rack, breaking vials against the stone, unfolding the folded names and speaking them aloud just to hear them exist, opening the lead boxes and letting the bound spirits stream out past her like cold air finding a window.

It took hours. She didn't hurry. This was, she reflected somewhere around the third hour, the first honest work she'd done in eleven years.

When the last cage stood empty, she gathered the lantern, doused it, and climbed the four flights to the surface, where dawn was coming up gray over a city that smelled like snowmelt and woodsmoke and ten thousand small fires deciding for themselves what to warm.

Junior Inspector Chen met her at the top of the stairs, hand on his baton, face pale. "Inspector. The archive alarm—"

"Burned out," Reyes said. "Everything down there is gone. Destroyed."

Chen stared at her. Behind him, half the night shift stood in the operations room, and every one of them had heard the alarms, and every one of them had chosen, in the last hour, not to come down the stairs. She could see it in their faces. They all had sparks now too. They all remembered things.

"You'll want to report me," Reyes said. "Write it up properly. Reyes, A., insubordination, destruction of Ministry property, treason, whatever Hale's successor wants to call it."

"Inspector—" Chen hesitated. His jaw worked. When he spoke again, his voice was strange and young. "My mother died when I was nine. I don't remember her voice. I paid a memory-thief last winter to try to get it back and she wouldn't sell to me, said stolen memories go bad, and I hated her for it." He swallowed. "I dreamed about her voice tonight. First time in twenty years."

Reyes waited.

"So no," Chen said. "I don't think I'll be reporting you, Inspector."

Behind him, the whole night shift was looking at her, and not one of them moved to arrest her, and Reyes — who had served the Silence faithfully for fifteen years, who had given it her grief and her daughter's name and her certainty — felt the last of it finally let go of her, the way a fist lets go only when it remembers it was holding nothing.

"Right," she said gruffly. "Someone make tea. We've got a city to not control."

* * *

Juniper Vale found the war ending on a rooftop, because that was where she always found wars — above them, watching, counting exits.

Below her, Sera's host was walking out of Vell in ragged, weeping columns, and the goddess herself was shrinking. That was the thing nobody had words for yet, the thing that would take the ballad-makers months to get wrong: divinity needed believers the way fire needed air, and Sera's believers had all just woken up with fires of their own. With every soldier who turned away, the figure above the rooftops burned a little smaller. By midnight she was the size of a church. By dawn she was the size of a woman, standing alone in the frozen fountain in Triumph Square, bronze armor dulled to rust, screaming curses at a city that had stopped listening.

Juniper watched, arms crossed against the cold, and felt the spark in her own chest answer the sight of her — not with fear. With something almost like pity.

*You saved me,* she thought at the distant, diminished shape. *No. He saved me. And you'd have spent his saving like coin.* She touched her sternum, where her own little warmth lived. *Not today.*

"Falling off roofs is my job."

Felix dropped onto the parapet beside her, light-footed even exhausted, soot-streaked, singed at one sleeve, alive. Alive, alive, alive — Juniper had watched her tear the ember out of her own chest twelve hours ago and had aged a decade in the seconds before the light settled and Felix sat up coughing in the frost of the Vault of Names, mortal and furious and *hers*, and Juniper did not think she would ever fully recover from either part of that sentence.

"Your job," Juniper said, "is theft."

"I'm broadening the portfolio." Felix leaned into her shoulder. For a while neither of them said anything. Below, Sera had stopped screaming and started simply standing, which was somehow worse, and the crowd that had gathered in Triumph Square — armed with nothing but their own small fires — was doing the most terrifying thing any crowd had ever done to a war-god.

They were leaving. Politely. One family at a time, turning their backs and walking home.

"She'll rebuild," Juniper said quietly. "Somewhere far from here. Find some other grieving army."

"Yeah." Felix didn't sound afraid of it. That was new. "But she'll have to build it out of people who *choose* her. Takes longer." A pause. "Everything worth anything takes longer."

"Theo thinks so too?"

Felix snorted. "Theo thinks so *loudly*. He's down in the shop arguing with Mrs. Ferro about whether answered prayers need paperwork." She tipped her head back and looked at the sky, where the long winter was finally, grudgingly, cracking — a seam of blue opening in the clouds like a door left ajar. "He says the sparks aren't gods, exactly. More like... kindling. Every person in Vell is carrying a piece of the fire now, and what they do with it is theirs. He says he's never seen anything so beautiful in four thousand years, and he includes himself."

"Do you believe him?"

Felix was quiet a moment. "I believe he asks permission now. Before anything. Even small things." She glanced sideways at Juniper, and there was something careful in it, something mended-but-tender, a fracture line they were both learning to live inside. "Turns out that's the whole religion, apparently. Asking."

Juniper laced their fingers together on the parapet between them. "And you? What are you going to do with your spark?"

"Same thing I always did." Felix smiled, tired and sharp and entirely her own. "Steal things. Give them back."

Below them, in the square, the last of Sera's army walked out through the north gate, and the war-goddess watched them go, and did not follow, because there was nothing left in her to follow with — and in ten thousand chests across the city of Vell, ten thousand small fires burned on into the morning, ungoverned, ungovernable, each one asking its keeper the same simple question:

*What will you warm?*

And far to the north, where the snow lay thickest and the year had not yet learned to turn, something old stirred beneath the ice — patient, patient, patient — and began, very quietly, to count the sparks.
