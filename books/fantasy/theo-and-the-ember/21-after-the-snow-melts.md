## 21. After the Snow Melts

The first thaw came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Vell had always done its miracles on workdays.

Felix was in the shop when it happened — her shop now, though the sign still said *Vale & Co., Curiosities* because Juniper refused to change it and Felix had learned which battles were worth winning. She was sorting a crate of returned memories, each charm labeled in her careful hand: *a wedding, 40 years of it, gently worn; a mother's voice singing; the smell of a kitchen before everything went wrong.* Things people had sold away in the hungry years and come back for when they could afford to want again.

Outside the window, the snowpack on the rooftops groaned, shifted, and let go all at once. Water roared down the gutters like applause. Someone in the street laughed — actually laughed, out loud, at nothing — and Felix set down the charm she was holding and pressed her palm flat against the cold glass and watched the ice on the canal crack into a thousand white plates that slid apart to show black water underneath.

Black water. Not frozen. Moving.

"Juniper," she called toward the back room. "It's melting."

She heard Juniper's boots on the stairs, two at a time, and then Juniper's shoulder against hers as she crowded the window too, breath fogging the glass, hair still damp from washing. They stood like that for a while without saying anything, watching Vell drip.

"Well," Juniper said finally. "We didn't die."

"We didn't die."

"I'd been meaning to mention it, but it seemed rude to celebrate while there was still an active apocalypse."

Felix laughed — a real one, surprising her on its way out. It had been months since laughing came easily. Since the Vault. Since ten thousand sparks went out of her chest and into the chests of everyone in a city that had spent a century learning not to hope.

She touched her own sternum absently. The hollow where the ember had lived didn't hurt anymore. It was just quiet, the way an empty room is quiet — not absence exactly, but space. Room to put things in.

"Come on," Juniper said. "If the world's ending its winter, we're going outside to watch. I didn't survive a war-goddess and a Ministry raid and my own catastrophic honesty to spend the thaw doing inventory."

* * *

They walked the whole city that afternoon, or enough of it to feel like the whole.

Vell was ugly in the way of things being born rather than dying, which was new. Meltwater flooded the lower streets and children were sailing paper boats down the Boulevart des Ombres, standing hip-deep and shrieking. On the corner where the prayer-cults used to clash with Ministry loyalists, someone had strung lanterns between the lampposts — plain ones, no charms inside, just fire — and an old man played a fiddle badly and nobody minded.

Everywhere, small strangenesses. A baker whose loaves rose impossibly high and who kept crossing himself and grinning. A woman on the canal steps with her hands in the water, whispering, and the water going briefly, unmistakably gold around her fingers before settling back to ordinary brown. A boy who could make his shadow sit up and wave.

Not gods. Felix checked, the way you check a healed wound. Nothing answering prayers from above, nothing demanding. Just sparks. Ten thousand little fires banked in mortal chests, burning low and steady, ungoverned and ungovernable, belonging to no one but their owners.

Sera had raged, in the end. Sera had screamed across the sky until the sky cracked like river ice, and then — this was the part Felix thought about most — Sera had simply gone out. Not defeated. Outspent. A war needs fuel, and Felix had taken the fuel and given it to everyone else, and there is no army so loyal it keeps marching once every soldier realizes she is her own general now.

Theo had laughed. That sound she kept closer than any memory in her ledger.

"He'll be at the bridge," Juniper said, when they reached the Pont Neuf and the sunset was doing something obscene to the water. She said it lightly, but her eyes did the thing they did — the quick sideways flicker, measuring Felix, offering her an exit before she needed one.

"You don't have to keep doing that," Felix said. "Translating me to myself."

"Don't I?"

"Juniper." Felix turned to face her fully. The bridge was crowded with people watching the ice go out to sea, and she didn't care. "I'm working on saying things directly. You get to hear them whether they're graceful or not. That was the deal we made in the undercity, remember? No more debts. No more commands. Nobody sent anybody."

Juniper's jaw worked. "I remember."

"So: he'll be at the bridge. Good. I'm glad. And you're coming with me, because I'm not choosing between you two. I did enough choosing for one lifetime, and half of it wasn't even mine." She took Juniper's hand. Warm hands, always warm — the shipwreck girl, the saved one, the spark in her chest burning a little brighter than most since that night on the ice. "I love you. I love him. In different directions, and both of them are real, and if that's strange then the world can be strange. It owes us that much."

For a moment Juniper looked like she might cry, and then she looked like herself instead, which was better. "Different directions," she repeated. "That's the worst romantic speech I've ever heard, and I once got proposed to by a man mid-arrest."

"And yet here you are."

"Here I am." She squeezed Felix's hand hard enough to mean it. "Come on, then. He hates waiting, except he doesn't, that's the whole problem with him, he'd wait until the sun burned out and call it a pleasant afternoon."

* * *

He was at the bridge, at the far rail, and Felix stopped dead twenty paces off because Theo looked —

Small.

Not diminished in the way of a king in exile, nothing proud about it. He was leaning on the stone like any tired man at the end of a long day, and he was wearing a fisherman's coat two sizes too big, and his hair was coming loose from its knot the way hair does when no divine wind is holding it up. When he turned and saw her, his whole face did something unguarded, and Felix understood that this — this exact expression, unarmored, hopeful, a little afraid — was worth more than every answered prayer she'd ever accidentally caused.

"You're shorter," she said.

"You're exactly the same height, which feels unfair, considering what I've been through." His voice was just a voice now. Rougher. Human-rough, sea-and-smoke rough. "Hello, Felix."

"Hello, Theo."

They looked at each other. Around them the crowd flowed past, and nobody gave the tall thin man in the bad coat a second glance, and Felix thought: five years asleep in stolen charms, a century sleeping before that, an eternity of being prayed at, and here he is, invisible, free, and asking for absolutely nothing. That was the thing. That was the thing she kept turning over like a coin she suspected was counterfeit. Everyone wanted something from her. The Ministry wanted the ember. Sera wanted the vessel. Even Juniper, sweet doomed Juniper, had arrived carrying a command wrapped in a rescue. But Theo stood at the rail of a bridge in a coat that smelled of fish, having poured nearly all of himself into ten thousand strangers' chests, and he wanted —

"What do you want?" she asked, because she had learned that the only way through was straight.

"Nothing," he said. And then, because honesty had become their language, their private dialect forged in confessions: "No. That's a lie, and you'd catch it. I want things. I want breakfast tomorrow. I want to see the spring properly, the real one, not the one in dreams. I want—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I want to know you when neither of us is carrying anything. If you ever want that too. No hurry. Truly none. I've gotten very good at waiting, and I intend to get worse at it, but slowly."

Felix looked at him for a long time. The crowd moved around them. Somewhere downstream a ship's horn sounded, deep and glad.

"You know the worst part?" she said. "Five years ago I stole a charm off a grieving widow and told myself I only ever took pain. And maybe that was true, mostly. But what I never took — what I didn't know how to take — was anything offered freely. Kindness. Company. Love. Those I couldn't lift, so I went without." She stepped closer. "So this is me practicing. Freely offered. Take it or leave it, god."

"That's not my name anymore," he said, smiling.

"No," Felix agreed. "It isn't."

She kissed him on a bridge in the melting city, and it was not fate, and it was not destiny, and it was not the will of any power above or below. It was a decision, made on a Tuesday by a woman with a shop and a ledger full of returned memories, and it was entirely her own.

* * *

Spring, when it committed, committed completely.

Within three weeks the hills above Vell were green to their shoulders, and the market stalls overflowed, and Felix's little shop had a line out the door most mornings. Word had spread, the way word does — not the Ministry's kind of word, stamped and filed, but the human kind, passed over fences and across counters. *There's a woman on Rue Candelaire who gives memories back. Fair prices. Sometimes free, if she likes your story.*

Reyes came on a Thursday in April.

Felix saw her through the window and her hand went instinctively to the counter drawer — old habit, though the drawer held nothing more dangerous than receipt paper these days — and then she made herself stop, and breathe, and watch the Inspector of Silence stand awkwardly in a doorway full of morning light, hat in her hands like a man at a funeral.

"Inspector," Felix said.

"Retired," Reyes said. "As of last month. There wasn't much left to inspect. We burned the vaults, after. All of it. Every confiscated charm, every hoarded name." Her mouth twisted. "Took me eleven years to understand what the job actually was, and then it took one week to be finished with it."

"Why come here?"

Reyes looked around the shop — at the shelves of labeled charms, at the child in the corner solemnly listening to a memory of her grandmother's garden, at Juniper in the back room pretending very badly not to eavesdrop.

"To buy something back," Reyes said simply. "I erased my grief. Eleven years ago. My brother's death — I filed it away like evidence, and then I filed it away like contraband, and then it was just gone, and I have been walking around the hole it left like a man pacing a room he can't remember the shape of." She set a modest purse on the counter. "I don't know what I'll find in there. I'm told it hurts."

"It does," Felix said. "That's how you know it's real."

Reyes nodded once, the nod of a woman accepting terms, and Felix found the charm — she knew right where it was; she kept the hard ones within reach — and pressed it into the Inspector's scarred hands.

"Theo sends his regards, by the way," Felix added, as Reyes turned to go. "He says you'd have made a good priest, in another life. Terrible inspector."

A ghost of a smile crossed Ada Reyes' face, there and gone like sun through cloud. "Don't tell anyone," she said, and walked out into the light.

* * *

Summer found the three of them on Juniper's boat, which was less a boat than a rumor of a boat held together with optimism and tar, drifting down the coast with nowhere to be.

Felix sat on the deck with her bare feet in the shallows of a cove, watching Juniper argue with Theo about rigging — Juniper arguing from expertise, Theo arguing from five thousand years of watching sailors and retaining nothing useful — and felt the particular peace of a life with no prophecy in it.

The sparks were everywhere now. In Juniper, gold-warm. In Theo, faint as banked coals, nearly drowned in the human dark of him, and he treasured that dimness the way misers treasure their last coin. Even in Felix, a flicker — hers by right of making, the original flame's daughter, or its mother, depending on which version of the war you believed. Small things. A flower that opened early when she sang to it. A kettle that boiled faster when she was impatient. Nothing that commanded. Nothing that crowned.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked Theo that night, the stars thick overhead, Juniper asleep against her shoulder with her mouth open and her dignity abandoned. "Being vast."

Theo considered it seriously, the way he considered everything.

"When I was vast," he said, "I was loved by millions and known by none. Now I am known by two, thoroughly, including all my worst qualities, several of which apparently include hogging blankets." He pulled the blanket higher over Juniper. "I miss nothing. Except, occasionally, being able to make it rain. The crops this year—"

"Juniper says you're banned from weather."

"Juniper says many things."

Felix laughed and leaned her head against his shoulder and watched the water, and thought about the widow from five years ago, the first theft, the beginning of everything. She had taken that woman's grief because the woman had begged her to, because pain is heavy and forgetting is cheap, and Felix had built a whole self on that trade: take the weight, sell it on, stay light, stay moving, belong to no one.

She belonged to no one still. That hadn't changed. What had changed was the direction of it — that she was no one's, and therefore free to give herself, daily and deliberately, to whoever earned it. To a smuggler with warm hands. To a god learning to be a man. To a city full of small fires.

The tide came in slow and silver. Somewhere north, past the horizon, the world went on healing from its long winter, and would go on healing for years, imperfectly, the way all living things heal.

"Ask me something," Felix murmured, half-asleep. "Anything. I'll answer true."

Theo was quiet a moment. Then, softly, in the voice of a man and not a god:

"Will you still be here tomorrow?"

"Yes," said Felix Zhou — thief, herald, vessel, woman, free. "That one's easy."

And far out on the dark water, the first lights of the fishing fleet came up one by one, like prayers being answered, like embers refusing to die, like a city full of small gods rowing home.
