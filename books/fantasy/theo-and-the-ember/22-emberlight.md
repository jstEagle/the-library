## 22. Emberlight

The shop smelled of cedar oil and old paper, and Felix had learned to love it the way other people loved the smell of rain.

She opened at dawn, because that was when Vell remembered things. The city woke in pieces — shutters banging open, kettles singing, a lamplighter dousing his last flame of night with a yawn — and along with the waking came the forgetting. A woman would stand in her doorway with bread in her hands and no idea why she'd baked two loaves instead of one. A man would find a child's drawing tucked behind a loose brick and not know whose hand had made it. A year ago, Felix would have found those loose memories the way she'd once found charms: lifted them, bottled them, sold them to whoever paid.

Now she sold them back.

The sign above the door read EMBERLIGHT, in letters Juniper had painted freehand, slightly crooked, refusing every offer to fix them. *Memories returned. Names restored. Fair prices or none.* Below it, smaller, in Felix's own careful hand: *No thefts performed on premises.*

Juniper said the last line made the shop sound like a tavern with rules. Felix kept it anyway.

The first customer of the morning was the widow Marren, back again for the fourth time this season. She came in wrapped in grey wool despite the spring warmth, and she set three coppers on the counter the way she always did, gently, as though they might break.

"You're sure," she said. It wasn't a question anymore. It was liturgy.

"I'm sure." Felix drew the small glass vial from the shelf behind her — shelf number three, the ones labeled in Juniper's looping script: *RETURNED ON REQUEST ONLY.* Inside it swam a memory like a minnow of light: a man laughing in a kitchen, flour on his knuckles, saying something Felix couldn't hear but Marren could, had heard four times now, would probably hear four more before winter came again. Her husband. The day before he died.

"I keep thinking I'll have enough of him," Marren said. "And then it runs out, and I come back."

"That's not how it works," Felix said softly. "You don't run out. You just get to choose which morning you live inside."

Marren smiled the way she did at the end of every visit — wet-eyed, steadier than when she'd arrived — and pressed the vial to her temple, and for a moment she was somewhere else entirely, somewhere warm, and Felix watched her face do what faces did: soften, grieve, and hold both at once without breaking.

That was the whole trade now. That was everything she'd built from the wreckage of what she'd been.

When the bell over the door chimed out Marren's departure, Theo looked up from the workbench in the back room, where he was — of all the tasks a former god could be given — sanding down a shelving unit.

"She comes every week," he said.

"Every week."

"And you never charge her full price."

"The ledger says fair prices or none."

"The ledger," Theo said gravely, "was written by a smuggler who once traded an entire cargo of Ministry silks for a barrel of pickled eels because, quote, 'the eels spoke to me.'"

"The eels were right," came Juniper's voice from the stairs. She came down into the shop with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back and ink on her cheek from the morning's accounts, and Felix felt the familiar turn in her chest, the one that hadn't dulled in a year, the one she'd stopped fighting around midwinter. "Also we needed the barrel. Don't ask. Felix, the Ketterdam woman's here — the one who lost her brother's name? And there's a kid outside who's been staring at our door since before dawn like it owes him money."

"A kid?"

"Maybe eight. Thin coat. Been standing in the street looking at the sign like it's written in a language he almost knows."

Felix went to the door.

The boy stood at the edge of the lamplight — the new lamps, ember-fed, burning gold instead of gas-white — and he had the look Felix knew better than any look in the world: hungry, wary, calculating whether the shop held something worth taking. He had sawdust on his sleeves and a bruise going yellow on his jaw, and when he saw her watching, he didn't run. That was the part that got her. A year ago, a boy like this would have bolted. Now they all stood their ground, because everyone in Vell carried a spark now, and sparks made people strange and stubborn and brave.

"You're the thief," he said.

"I was."

"Mama says you stole half the city's sad."

Felix leaned against the doorframe. Behind her, she heard Juniper pause in the accounts, listening, and say nothing. "Your mama's mostly wrong. I only took the heavy ones. Or I told myself so."

"Why?"

Why. Eight years old and already asking the question Felix had spent five years running from, then another year learning to answer. She crouched down to his height, which put her face in the warm gold light, and she thought about lying, and decided against it, because children could smell lies the way sharks smelled blood.

"Because carrying grief alone is heavier than carrying it shared," she said. "I used to think taking it away was kindness. It isn't. It's just theft with good manners."

The boy considered this with the terrible seriousness of the young. Then: "Are the gods gone?"

It was the question of the year. It was asked in taverns and temples and Ministry hearing rooms — the few hearing rooms the Ministry still kept, now that Inspector Reyes had burned most of its archives in a fire officially ruled accidental and unofficially celebrated. It was asked by priests with empty churches and by mothers with full graves. Are the gods gone?

Felix looked past the boy, down the street, where the morning was coming up gold. Somewhere a baker was pulling loaves. Somewhere a fishwife was arguing about prices. Somewhere a woman was pressing a glass vial to her temple and living inside a kitchen full of flour-dusted laughter.

"No," she said. "They're closer than ever. One spark in every chest. You've got one. So does your mama. So does the meanest man in Vell and the kindest. Nobody gets to own them anymore. Nobody gets to hoard them or pray them into floods or build armies out of them." She tapped his sternum lightly, once. "You're carrying your own god, little one. Try not to let anyone talk you out of it."

The boy's eyes went wide, and then — this was the part Felix would remember later, the part she'd carry the way Marren carried her kitchen morning — he grinned, gap-toothed and sudden, and ran off down the street shouting to some other kid that *he had a god in his chest, he had a GOD*, and the shout echoed off the cobblestones and came back sounding like bells.

* * *

Midmorning brought the Ketterdam woman, whose brother had died in the flood quarter two winters back, before the snows, before everything. The flood had taken more than lives; it had taken names off stones, records into rot, whole family histories into the water. She'd spent two years unable to say her brother's name because the grief had eaten it.

Felix worked slowly. This was the craft now, finer than theft had ever been: she held the woman's hands across the counter and listened — really listened, the way Theo had taught her listening worked, which was to say the way gods listened, minus the divinity — until she found the snag in the woman's memory where the name should be, and she coaxed it loose the way you'd coax a splinter from skin. Gently. Without taking anything else with it.

"Ondrey," the woman whispered, when it surfaced, and then said it again, louder, testing it, and then she was crying in the good way, the way that washed things clean instead of drowning them.

Theo appeared in the doorway with tea, unasked, three cups, because he'd somehow learned exactly when people needed a third cup they hadn't asked for. He set them down without a word. The woman took hers with shaking hands and drank, and looked at him, and frowned slightly, the way people sometimes did around Theo — a faint sense of recognition, a name on the tip of the tongue that never quite arrived.

"You seem familiar," she said.

"I have one of those faces," Theo said pleasantly.

He did not. He had a face people forgot immediately, which had been the point, once. But diminished as he was — mortal-adjacent, emberless, content — he'd kept one small divine trick, and it was this: he could give away recognition without receiving credit for it. Every person who met him walked away feeling they'd known someone kind once, somewhere, and couldn't place him. Felix thought it was the most honest thing a god could do with himself.

After the woman left, wiping her eyes, promising to send her sister next week ("she lost a daughter, do you—"), Felix stood in the middle of her shop and let herself feel it, fully, the thing she still didn't quite trust even after a year:

This was hers. Not assigned, not prophesied, not fated. Chosen. Built plank by plank, customer by customer, crooked painted sign by crooked painted sign. She had spent so long being a vessel — first for an ember, then for a destiny, then for other people's definitions of duty — that owning a small shop with bad shelving felt like the most radical act of her life.

Juniper came up beside her and bumped her shoulder. "You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The one where you're bracing for someone to tell you it's temporary."

Felix huffed. "Am I that readable?"

"To me? You always were. Even that first night on the rooftops, when you were all knives and suspicion and 'who sent you,' I could read you like a broadsheet." Juniper's grin softened into something else. She turned Felix gently by the shoulder, so they faced each other in the ember-gold light of the shop. "You know what I figured out? About the debt. About why I came looking for you."

"Juniper—"

"Hear me out. I used to wonder if I loved you or loved the god in you. Spent months chewing on it. And then the ember shattered and you were just — you. Stubborn, thieving, terrible-at-apologies you. And I stayed anyway." She shrugged, like it was simple. Like it hadn't taken a year of slow repairs, of hard conversations in undercity safehouses and long walks along the winter canals, to get from fracture to here. "Turns out it was never the god. Turns out it was the girl who stole only painful memories and lied to herself about why."

"They weren't all painful."

"Some of them were beautiful. You stole those too — the ones people were too scared to keep." Juniper kissed her, brief and certain, and pulled back with the smirk that had started everything. "Fair prices or none, Zhou."

* * *

In the afternoon, an unexpected visitor.

Inspector Ada Reyes came through the door in plain clothes — truly plain, these days, no insignia, because the insignia had been retired along with most of the Ministry of Silence. She still walked like an inspector, though. Some things a person couldn't erase, even if they tried, even if they erased worse.

"Inspector," Felix said.

"Not anymore. Just Reyes." She surveyed the shop with the same methodical eye she'd once swept crime scenes with, cataloguing shelves, customers, exits. Old habits. Then her gaze landed on the ledger behind the counter — the new one, the return-ledger — and something in her face eased. "I read your old ledger again. Last month. Found it in the recovered-evidence archive before I burned the archive."

"You read my thefts."

"I read your mercies." Reyes picked up a stray copper from the counter and turned it over. "Five years of names. Five years of taking only what people begged you to take. I built a career on the theory that people like you were parasites." She set the coin down. "I was wrong about a great many things. I came to say so. And to buy something."

"Recovering something, more likely."

"Both." For the first time, the ex-inspector's composure flickered. "There's a memory I erased myself. Twenty-two years ago. My mother's funeral. I signed the requisition myself — I was so proud, filling out the form correctly, doing it *by the book*. I told myself grief was inefficiency." She met Felix's eyes. "I'd like it back. Whatever it costs."

Felix reached under the counter and brought up a box she'd kept for a year without knowing why — confiscated charms from the Ministry raid, the ones nobody had claimed, waiting. Somewhere in it, she hoped, was a grieving woman's last morning with her mother, filed under a case number by a clerk who believed silence was mercy.

It took an hour to find it. When Felix finally held the charm up to the light — a small, ordinary thing, dull with twenty years of dark storage — Reyes took it in both hands like something holy.

"Will it hurt?" she asked.

"Yes," Felix said. "That's rather the point."

Reyes nodded once, pressed it to her temple, and stood very still while twenty-two years of absence crashed home. Felix watched her grieve in real time — watched the woman who had once hunted her across rooftops shake apart quietly in the middle of a memory shop — and when it passed, Reyes straightened, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and looked ten pounds lighter and ten years older, and grateful for both.

"The city's quieter," Reyes said, recovering her voice. "You notice? Since the shattering. Less praying up, more—" she gestured vaguely at everything— "*outward*. People helping each other. It turns out when everyone has a little god in them, nobody waits around for miracles."

"That was the idea."

"Sera hates it, I hear."

"Sera can get in line behind the shelving." Theo called from the back room, and Reyes laughed — actually laughed, short and rusty, like a door opening after years — and shook Theo's hand, and left a fair price on the counter, and paused at the door.

"Galloway," she said — using Felix's chosen surname, the one she'd given herself, the one no god or Ministry had ever issued. "For what it's worth. Whoever remade you — whoever hid the ember in a human girl — they gave you everything you needed to end up exactly here. Maybe that was the plan. Maybe it was luck. Either way—" she tapped the doorframe, twice, inspector's habit— "you kept yourself. Through all of it. Do you know how rare that is?"

The bell chimed her out.

* * *

Evening came soft over Vell.

They closed the shop together, the three of them, in the easy choreography of a year's practice: Felix at the till, Juniper on the accounts, Theo sanding the last stubborn corner of the new shelving. Outside, the ember-lamps bloomed gold down the length of the street, and the first stars came out over a city that had buried its dead, melted its impossible snow, and gone on stubbornly living.

Later — much later, after dinner, after Juniper fell asleep mid-sentence over a shipping manifest, after Felix carried a blanket out to the roof where Theo sat looking at the sky — she settled beside him on the warm tiles and let the quiet sit between them.

"Do you miss it?" she asked. "Being big."

Theo considered the question honestly, which was the only way he ever considered anything. "Sometimes. The way you miss being asleep. It was restful, being enormous. Nothing mattered." He turned his head. His eyes caught the lamp-glow and held it. "Now everything matters, all the time, and it's exhausting, and it's the best thing that ever happened to me." A pause. "You gave me that. You gave everyone that. Ten thousand sparks, and every one of them free."

"Ten thousand and one," Felix said. "Don't forget yours."

"I could never." He reached over and took her hand — no pull, no gravity, nothing taken, everything offered — and laced his fingers through hers. Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and beautiful, and below them Vell glowed, ungovernable, alive.

A year ago tonight — she realized it suddenly, the date arriving like a letter from an old self — a stranger had watched her from across a night market while she lifted a grieving widow's sorrow out of her palm and pocketed it for sale. A year ago, she'd been a thief with rules and a hole where her history should have been, and the whole weight of heaven had been balanced on her unbeknownst shoulders.

Tonight, a widow had paid three coppers to hold her husband's laughter one more time. Tonight, a boy had run down the street announcing the god in his chest. Tonight, Felix Zhou — self-named, self-made, vessel of nothing but her own choices — sat on a rooftop with a fallen god's hand in hers and a smuggler sleeping downstairs who loved her for exactly what she was.

Tomorrow, the Ketterdam woman's sister would come, and lose a daughter's name and find it again. Tomorrow, the shelves would need fixing, properly, because Theo's sanding was enthusiastic but structurally unsound. Tomorrow, spring would keep on keeping on.

But tonight—

Across the street, in the doorway of the shuttered bakery, an old man stood holding a photograph of a wife the flood had taken, staring at the golden light of the shop, too shy to cross, too hopeful to leave.

Felix rose, brushed off her knees, and crossed the street to meet him. She held out her open palm — empty, offered, warm.

"Come inside," she said. "Tell me her name. We'll see if we can't give it back."
