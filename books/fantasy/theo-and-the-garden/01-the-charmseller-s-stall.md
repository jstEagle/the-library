## 1. The Charmseller's Stall

The luck-charm was a lie, and Freya Sandoval sold it with a smile.

"River stone," she said, turning it in her fingers so the morning light caught the pale spiral veined through its heart. "Pulled from the shallows below the Weir Gate, on a night the moon stood full over still water. Carry it in your left pocket, and no ledger will ever catch you short."

The clerk — a narrow man named Odell Vetch, who came to her stall every market-day and every market-day pretended he hadn't decided to come until he arrived — clutched his satchel to his chest as though she might snatch it from him.

"You're certain it's *quiet*?" he whispered.

Freya held the stone up between two fingers and let her eyes go half-lidded, the way a woman looks into a well she already knows the depth of. "As quiet as your aunt's knitting, Master Vetch. No spark in it at all. The censors could hold it to their glass for an hour and see nothing but pretty geology."

This was true, in its way. There was nothing in the stone. There had been nothing in any of her charms for fifteen years, because there had been nothing in *her*, and the day the god went silent was the last day Freya Sandoval had made anything that worked.

She dropped the stone into a twist of blue cloth, knotted it three times — an old habit from when knots had meant something — and slid it across the scarred plank of her stall.

"That'll be six coppers."

Vetch paid without haggling, which was itself a kind of prayer. He took the bundle like a communicant taking bread, tucked it deep into his left pocket, patted it twice, and only then remembered to breathe.

"If the auditors come through the counting-house again—" he began.

"They won't find you short," Freya said. "And if they do, you'll have the wit to blame the ink."

He laughed — a small, startled sound, like a bird startled off a wire — and thanked her twice more than necessary, and moved off into the churn of Market Row with his shoulders a little straighter than they'd been when he arrived.

Freya watched him go and felt the familiar hollow open behind her ribs, the one shaped exactly like a voice that used to answer her.

*You're welcome,* she said to the silence where her god had been. *Six coppers for a rock. You'd have hated this.*

The silence, as always, said nothing back.

* * *

Market Row ran the length of Vharen's old trade quarter like a bright ribbon stitched across a gray coat. Even now, even under the Covenant of Silence and the seventeen years of gray sky since, people needed rope and bread and secondhand boots and someone to tell them tomorrow would be survivable. Freya supplied the last of those, in cloth-wrapped bundles, at prices calibrated precisely to what hope cost a clerk or a laundress or a widow with one son on the fishing boats.

Her stall sat at the crook where Copper Lane bent toward the fountain square: a folding table, a striped awning gone pink with sun-bleach, and a display arranged with the care of an altar. River stones for luck. Knots of braided thread for safe travel. Dried sprigs of rosemary tied with red wool — *for remembering*, she told customers, *so grief doesn't take what love left behind.* Little tin medallions stamped with a sunburst that had once been the sigil of something far older and larger than any sun, though nobody living knew that, and Freya wasn't fool enough to say so.

Everything harmless. Everything legal. Everything dead.

That was the arrangement, and Freya had kept it scrupulously for fifteen years: sell nothing that worked, swear nothing that bound, pray nothing that listened. A mercenary sworn to a god was a weapon; a mercenary whose god had gone quiet was just a woman with a stall and a past she kept folded up small, like the awning at nightfall.

She was rewrapping the rosemary when the bells changed.

Not the market bells — those rang the quarter-hours, cheerful and dumb. This was the other bell, the one mounted above the Hall of Vigilance, and it didn't ring so much as *state*: three low strokes, a pause, then three more. Every head on Market Row turned the way wheat turns before wind.

"The patrol's early," muttered Tamsin from the fruit cart beside her, not looking up from her apples. She was arranging them with sudden, furious diligence. "They weren't due till noon."

"They're never early," Freya said quietly. "They come when they want to be seen coming. That's the point of them."

The witch-hunters came down Copper Lane four abreast, and Market Row parted around them the way water parts around a cold stone. They wore ash-gray coats cut long and severe, high collars, and on each breast a badge of white enamel: a closed eye. Not blind — *closed*. Watching inward, watching everyone, seeing nothing and everything by turns. Their leader walked with a censer swinging at his hip, except it burned no incense. It burned something else — something that smoked thin and colorless and made the air taste faintly of struck flint. Censor-smoke, the city called it. Hold anything touched by the old craft near it, and the smoke would curl green.

Freya had watched that smoke test a hundred objects in her time, back when she'd worn a different life. These days she felt about censor-smoke the way a eunuch feels about talk of heirs: distant, wry, and underneath, a grief too old to spend another hour on.

The hunters moved down the row without breaking stride. One paused at a peddler's cart of carved figures — saints of the old calendar, fat and smiling, sold nowadays strictly as folk art — and passed the censer over them. The smoke stayed white. The hunter moved on. At the far end of the lane, near the fountain, a woman in a shawl was pulled aside for the crime of muttering to herself, and there was the brief theater of inspection — hands turned over, tongue checked for prayer-knots, shawl shaken out — before they found nothing and let her go, and the whole row exhaled and resumed pretending to trade.

"They enjoy it," Tamsin said. "That's the thing nobody says. They *enjoy* it."

"Don't say that where the smoke can hear you." Freya stacked her last sprigs. "Enjoyment is a symptom. That's practically doctrine."

Tamsin snorted, but her hands were still tight on her apples, and Freya understood why. Seventeen years ago — no, fifteen, though some mornings it still felt like yesterday and others like a previous incarnation — the sky over Vharen had gone gray. Not cloudy. Gray. A flat, sourceless dimness, like ash held against the eye, and it had never fully lifted. The old light still came through; crops still grew; children were still born squalling and golden. But color had drained out of the high air, and along with it something else, something the priests had tried and failed to name and the Council of Vigilance had named quickly enough: *residue*. The afterglow of a power that had been present in the world and then, in a single night, withdrawn.

The covenant had collapsed in that same night. Every oath-sworn worker of the craft in Vharen — healers, weather-readers, wardsmiths, mercenaries like Freya — had felt their patron go silent mid-word, like a door closing during a conversation. And the city, terrified, had done what frightened cities do. It had outlawed the craft entire, founded the Hall of Vigilance, hung eleven people in the first year, and taught its children that the gray sky was a wound and magic was the knife.

Freya had been twenty-six. She had knelt on the floor of a rented room with her oath-token burning cold in her fist and prayed until her throat tore, and gotten back exactly what she got now: nothing. A silence with weight. A door with no handle on this side.

She had sold her sword within the month. She had kept the token, which was either sentiment or treason depending on who found it, and buried it at the bottom of a trunk beneath three winters' worth of clothing she never wore.

"Stone-face again," Tamsin observed.

"I'm pricing rosemary."

"You're pricing rosemary somewhere else entirely. Here—" Tamsin pressed a bruised apple into her hand, the way she did every market-day, an act of charity disguised as inventory management. "Eat something. You've got the look of a woman arguing with somebody who isn't there."

Freya looked at the apple. "And what would you know about who I argue with?"

"Nothing." Tamsin grinned, gap-toothed and unbothered. "But I know the argument's losing."

* * *

Midmorning brought the usual procession of the hopeful and the broke.

A fisherman's wife bought a travel-knot for her boy's duffel and asked, in the tone of a woman asking about weather, whether it would keep him from the deep water. Freya said the knot was for roads, not waves, and watched the woman's face do the arithmetic of disappointment, and added, gently, "But he can carry both if he likes. Wool doesn't weigh much." The woman paid double and left lighter.

Two apprentices bought matching stones so they'd match each other, giggling, and Freya let herself laugh with them, briefly, like stepping into warm water.

An old man with a soldier's carriage — wrong build for it now, spine curved, hands knuckled — stood at the edge of her stall a long moment before approaching, and when he spoke it was in the clipped cadence of a man reading from a list he'd memorized. "Charms. Quiet ones."

"All my charms are quiet, grandfather. That's rather the law."

"Mm." His eyes went over her display, and stopped, and stayed, on the tin medallions with their sunburst stamp. Something moved through his face like a current under ice. "Where'd you get these stamps?"

"A die I bought at estate auction. Some dead silversmith's stock."

"Dead," he repeated. He picked up a medallion, turned it, and his thumb — deliberately, Freya thought, or perhaps out of habit older than thought — traced the sunburst's inner spiral. "My mother wore one like it. Before the Silence. Said it was a door-knock. You knocked, and something answered."

Freya kept her face pleasant and her hands busy and her heartbeat very carefully level. "Folk used to say a lot of things."

"They did." He set the medallion down with strange gentleness, almost a laying-down of arms. "Did yours ever answer?"

The question landed in the hollow place behind her ribs, dead center, and for one unguarded instant Freya Sandoval — competent, careful, fifteen years unsprung — felt her face begin to crack along a seam she'd plastered so long ago she'd forgotten it was there.

"No," she said.

The old man nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd carried a long way. He bought nothing. He touched the brim of his hat to her, an oddly formal gesture, and said, "Sorry for the question, charmwoman," and walked away into the crowd, and Freya stood a moment longer than she meant to with her hand resting flat on the medallions, feeling the tin go warm under her palm and knowing — *knowing*, the way you know a dream isn't real while it's happening — that it was only the sun.

Only the gray, thin, grudging sun.

* * *

The trouble came at noon, as troubles in Vharen preferred to, when the market was fullest and flight hardest.

It began with a shout from the fountain square — not fear, at first, just volume, the particular carrying bellow of a man who believes himself right. Then the crowd's texture changed. Freya had spent fifteen years reading crowds the way sailors read water, and she felt this one shift the way she'd feel a floor tilt: a pressure moving through it, heads turning, a ripple of backs pressing subtly away from something.

She climbed onto her stool.

Near the fountain, a young man was standing on the rim of the basin, arms spread, coatless in the chill, shouting at the top of a voice cracked raw from shouting already. He was maybe twenty. He had the feverish brightness she remembered from converts and fanatics and men three days without sleep.

"He's *there*!" the young man cried. "Under the ground, under the garden, He's *alive* — the Silence isn't death, it's a prison, and the Covenant of Silence is the lock, and every one of you who buys a dead stone and calls it luck is polishing the bars—"

"Censor! Censor here!"

The crowd around him had formed its instinctive ring — close enough to watch, far enough to deny involvement. Two hunters pushed through it, ash-gray and unhurried, and Freya saw the third, the one with the censer, hanging back with the deliberate patience of a man letting the scene ripen.

The young man saw them coming and did not run. That was the frightening part. He opened his arms wider, as if to embrace them.

"Arrest me, then! Arrest the truth! But ask why the Vigilance fears a *garden* — ask why the oldest garden in Vharen stands locked and rotting while the city lets it rot — ask what they buried under the roses—"

The censer-man stepped forward at last, unhurried as tide, and swung the censor-smoke in a slow arc around the young man's head. The smoke stayed white — of course it stayed white; the boy was empty, just a mouth full of borrowed fire — but the gesture itself was the sentence. Two hunters took his arms. The crowd murmured its approval and its relief in the same breath, because the arrest of a madman is the cheapest reassurance a city sells.

"—under the *roses*—" the young man shouted, twisting, his eyes raking across the crowd as they dragged him backward, hunting faces, finding none, finding—

Finding hers.

For less than a heartbeat their eyes met across forty feet of Vharen's finest citizens, and Freya saw the look in his face change — the frenzy banking, focusing, sharpening into something terribly like recognition — and his mouth shaped words she couldn't hear over the crowd, but read plainly enough anyway:

*You know. You know what they took.*

Then the fountain corner swallowed him, and the crowd closed like water over a stone, and Market Row resumed its noise with the practiced speed of people eager to have seen nothing.

Freya sat down on her stool. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were steady. Fifteen years of practice will do that.

*Prison,* the boy had said. *Not death. A lock.*

Every charlatan and prophet in fifteen years had said something of the kind — the god is dead, the god fled, the god was never there, the god is angry and demands this sacrifice or that tithe. She'd heard them all in the first gray years, when she'd still gone looking, still knelt in strange chapels and drank with strangers who claimed visions. Hope had sent her to worse places than fountains.

But none of them had ever said *the garden*. None of them had known about the municipal garden — the old one, walled, locked, officially condemned these fifteen years, officially *nothing*, which in Vharen was the word the city used for things it wanted forgotten. Freya had walked past its rusted gate ten thousand times. She had never once, in fifteen years, allowed herself to wonder why the grass inside grew greener than anywhere else in the city, or why the ivy on its wall climbed toward the gate instead of away from it, or why — on the worst nights, the nights when the silence behind her ribs weighed most — she had sometimes dreamed of roses, and woken weeping without knowing for whom.

"Oi." Tamsin appeared at her elbow, low-voiced, eyes forward, arranging apples that didn't need arranging. "You went the same color as the sky. What did he say to you?"

"Nothing," Freya said. "He said nothing to me."

"Liar," Tamsin said fondly, and moved off to serve a customer, and Freya sat among her dead charms in the gray light and admitted, in the privacy of her own skull, that the boy had said one thing, and that it had gone into her like a splinter working toward the heart.

*Ask what they buried under the roses.*

Down the row, at the mouth of Copper Lane, a new figure had stopped — not a hunter, not a guard, just a man in a work coat with soil on his sleeves and a bundle of cut flowers cradled in one arm like a sleeping child. He was looking at her stall. Or she thought he was. He stood there a moment, unreadable in the flat light, and then turned and went on toward the fountain square, and Freya told herself she'd imagined the pause.

She was good at telling herself things. She'd had fifteen years of practice at that, too.
