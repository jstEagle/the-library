## 2. The Man Who Grew Things

The flowers were Freya Sandoval's excuse, and she knew it.

She told herself she was scouting the municipal garden because it bordered the tanner's row where her best charm-buyer lived, and because the old iron gates stood open these days — open gates meant foot traffic, and foot traffic meant customers who might want a luck-charm along with their cut stems. All of that was true. None of it explained why she had walked past the tanner's row entirely, or why she was standing in a garden that had been dead for fifteen years, holding three coppers and looking at the only living thing in it besides herself.

A man was on his knees in the ruined rose bed, up to his elbows in black soil, with dirt to his wrists and a smear of it across one cheekbone like a war-paint gone wrong. He was not a young man — thirty, perhaps, with the kind of face you forgot twice before you'd finished looking at it — but there was something about the way he knelt. Most people knelt to beg or to pray. He knelt the way other men leaned into a fight.

He was talking to the roses.

Not loudly. Not the muttering of a madman, which Vharen had plenty of and Freya knew how to ignore. It was more that his mouth moved as he worked, low and rhythmic, and when he lifted a cane of thorned wood as thick as her wrist — wood that should have been dead twenty years, gray and hollow — he held it against the light like an apothecary judging a root, and said something to it she couldn't hear. Then he set it into the earth he'd turned, packed the soil down around it with both palms, and patted it twice, gently, the way you'd settle a child under a blanket.

Freya's whole body went still.

She knew what dead rosewood looked like. She'd spent fifteen years learning what everything in Vharen looked like when the life went out of it — the sky first, then the fountains, then half the trees on the processional way, then the charms in her stall going dull as river stones one by one until all she could sell were lies with pretty knots tied in them. Dead was a language she spoke fluently.

That cane was not dead. That cane was *waking*, and the man kneeling over it had no right to be able to do that, no tools for it that she could see, no sigils, no tokens, nothing. Just hands and dirt and whatever was moving through him quiet as groundwater.

*Don't,* she told herself. *You don't know what it is. You don't know whose it is. You have survived fifteen years by not asking questions like this.*

She walked over anyway.

* * *

"You're killing that," she said.

The man looked up. His eyes were brown — ordinary brown, the brown of cheap tea, of doorframes, of ten thousand unremarkable things in Vharen — and they blinked at her with genuine, unhurried surprise, as if she'd interrupted him mid-sentence in a conversation he fully intended to resume.

"Killing it?" He considered the cane. "I'm planting it."

"It's been dead since before I came to this city." Freya crossed her arms. "I've walked past this bed every market-day for six years. There's nothing alive in that hedge but beetles and one very committed spider."

"There's roots." He said it simply, the way another man would say *it's Tuesday*. "Everything that looks dead has roots. You just have to get below the gray part." He wiped his hands on his thighs, leaving the dirt worse instead of better, and offered one of those filthy hands up toward her. "Theo. I'm the new gardener."

"There hasn't been a gardener here in fifteen years."

"I know." Something flickered behind the tea-brown eyes — not guilt exactly, but a cousin of it. "The city finally found money for one. Or found me, rather. I applied four years running and they kept saying the garden was slated for demolition, and then last month a clerk made an error and here I am." He smiled, and the smile was crooked and unpracticed, like a tool used so rarely it had stiffened in its drawer. "I intend to be very hard to fire."

"Freya." She took the hand despite the dirt. His grip was warm. That was such a small thing to notice — warm, like anyone's — but it lodged in her chest and refused to leave, the way a splinter does. When did she last shake a hand that felt like anything at all? Fifteen years of gloves and nods and coin passed palm to palm without contact. "I sell charms. Market square, third row, the stall with the blue awning."

"A charmer." His face did something complicated. "Is that — allowed?"

"It's allowed the way selling dried flowers is allowed. Mine are decorative." She heard the lie come out smooth and hated how smooth it was. "Knots and beads and blessed thread. Luck for clerks. Courage for grooms. Nothing that would interest the witch-hunters, if that's the look on your face."

"That's not—" He laughed, short and startled, and rubbed the back of his neck, which only spread the dirt further up into his hair. "It's not suspicion. I just never met one. A charmer. My grandmother used to talk about them like weather. 'Mind your temper, Theo, or the charmer'll hear it.'" He shook his head. "I thought that was a story."

"Most people think we're a story." Freya found herself smiling and didn't remember deciding to. "Your grandmother sounds like she was from the old country."

"The valleys, north. She grew things nobody could grow." He said it lightly, but his hand drifted — unconsciously, Freya thought — to touch his own collarbone, just below the throat. "She died believing the gray skies were temporary."

"They're not," Freya said, more sharply than she meant to. "Fifteen years. The covenant broke, the god went silent, magic got outlawed, and the sky went the color of dishwater. That's not weather. That's permanent."

He looked at her for a long moment. Not offended by the sharpness — interested in it, the way he'd probably been interested in the dead cane, getting below the gray part.

"You say that like you lost something personal."

"I say that like I can count, gardener."

But he was still looking at her, patient as topsoil, and something in Freya — something she had buried under oath and habit and fifteen years of careful neutrality — squirmed toward the surface like a worm after rain. *He asked. Nobody asks.* In Vharen everyone knew the rules: the gray was permanent, the gods were done, the past was a locked room, and polite people kept their voices down around all three. This man knelt in the wreckage of the oldest garden in the city and talked to dead roses like they were sleeping, and he asked her questions like answers wouldn't cost him anything.

"Everyone lost something," she said finally. Quieter. "That was the point of the gray. It fell on everyone equally. Very democratic, the end of the world."

"The end of the world usually is." He gestured at the bed beside him, at the row of pale woody stubs he'd already set into the earth. "Sit, if you want. The bench is rotten but it holds. I've got water, if you don't mind it tasting of the cistern."

She should go. She had charms to knot, lies to sell, a patrol schedule memorized down to the quarter-bell, and absolutely no business sitting on a rotten bench in a haunted garden talking to a man who woke dead wood with his bare hands.

She sat.

* * *

They talked for two hours.

Or rather — he talked, and she answered, and somewhere in between the answering became its own kind of talking, and neither of them noticed the sun crossing the yard. He told her about the garden's history the way some men tell war stories: the fountain that used to run wine on festival days; the rose tree in the far corner older than the city walls, "they say the first mayor swore his oath under it"; the year everything grayed and the gardeners' guild dissolved overnight because, as Theo put it, "there's no guild for grief." He talked with his hands. He got dirt on everything, including, somehow, her sleeve, though neither of them ever established how.

And Freya — who had not laughed in so long that the sound surprised her own ears — laughed three times. Once at his impression of the city clerk who'd hired him ("he signed the papers with his eyes closed, I swear, like he couldn't bear to watch himself do it"), once at the story of the goat that had broken into the garden during the abandonment years and eaten the entire herb border ("the neighbors still call it the Goat War"), and once at nothing at all, at the mere fact of him turning a clod of earth over and finding a worm and addressing the worm by name.

"Did you name that worm?"

"He's called Aldous. He's earned it. Look at him — he's carrying half his weight in soil. That's character." He held the worm out toward her with the solemnity of a man presenting a medal, and Freya laughed her third laugh, and felt something crack in her chest that had been calcifying for fifteen years, and felt immediately, hotly guilty about it.

Because that was the arithmetic she couldn't escape, wasn't it? Every minute of warmth here was a minute subtracted from the vigil. Fifteen years ago the sky went gray and her god — her loud, vain, stubborn, *generous* god, who had answered her prayers with signs and jokes and once, memorably, with a hailstorm timed precisely to ruin a rival mercenary's wedding — went silent between one heartbeat and the next. And Freya had kept the vigil anyway. Had kept the oath-token under her shirt and the faith in her spine and had treated every small pleasure since as a kind of theft. Warmth was desertion. Laughter was looting the ruins. You didn't get to be happy inside a wound.

Except this man's hands smelled like rain, and he'd named a worm, and when he looked at her it wasn't the way men looked at a woman alone at a stall — appraising, calculating, the arithmetic of purchase. He looked at her like she was a plant he hadn't identified yet. Curious. Careful. Willing to wait for the blooming.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm wondering why you bought flowers you didn't need." He nodded at her basket, where — she realized with a lurch — sat three cut stems she had absolutely no use for, bought from a flower-seller at the gate on pure impulse, the first impulsive thing she'd done in a decade. "Unless you're courting someone. Are you courting someone?"

"No."

"Pity. They'd have to compete with Aldous."

"*Theo.*"

"What?" He was grinning now, the crooked smile loosening into something real, dirt and all. "I'm a serious competitor. I have a garden. Well. I have a *projected* garden. Give me a season."

Freya looked at him — really looked, past the dirt and the ordinary face and the tea-brown eyes — and felt the vigil tremble. Not break. Tremble, like ice over a well that still, somewhere far below, held water.

"Give me one of those flowers," she heard herself say.

"You bought them."

"And you're going to tell me they suit me, and I'm going to pretend that line works, and in exchange you'll tell me when the roses bloom." She stood, brushing off her skirt, reassembling the armor of banter piece by piece. "Market-days. I'm at the stall."

"The first buds will be weeks yet," he said. "Roses are slow. They make you wait."

"I'm good at waiting."

Something crossed his face then — quick, unreadable, there and gone like a fish under ice — and for half a heartbeat Freya could have sworn the air in the garden changed temperature. Warmed. Like a door opening somewhere onto a lit room. Then it was gone, and Theo was just a gardener again, offering her a white flower with soil-dark fingers.

"For the stall," he said. "So the luck-charms have competition."

* * *

She was halfway across the yard when she stopped.

The garden behind her had gone quiet in the wrong way — not empty-quiet but *attentive*-quiet, the hush of a room when someone important enters. And every plant along the path, every weed and shoot and gray half-dead shrub, had turned. Not metaphorically. Not the slow heliotrope of leaves following a sun that hadn't shone in fifteen years. Turned. Stems bent. Heads inclined, all of them, every growing thing in the abandoned municipal garden of Vharen, oriented toward the man kneeling in the rose bed with his back to them, humming to himself, unaware.

Freya stood very still with the white flower in her hand and her oath-token cold against her sternum, and thought: *fifteen years I've sold dead charms and told myself the silence was permanent.*

The flower in her hand was warm.

It had been cut that morning. Cut flowers die in hours. But it lay against her palm like a small animal, alive and faintly pulsing, and when she raised it to her face she caught a scent no cut flower had any business having — green sap and summer rain and something older underneath, something like the air of a temple before the doors opened.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, no.*

She turned back. Theo looked up from his bed of planted canes, saw her expression, and his own changed — the grin fading into that careful, patient watchfulness, the gardener reading weather.

"Freya?"

"The roses," she said, and her voice came out strange to her own ears. "How soon can you make them bloom?"

His head tilted. "Why?"

"Because," Freya said, closing her fist around the impossible flower, feeling its warmth beat against her skin like a second heart, "I think I need to show you a book."
