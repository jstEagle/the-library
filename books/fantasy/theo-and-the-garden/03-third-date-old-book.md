## 3. Third Date, Old Book

Theo had never been inside a woman's apartment that smelled like iron and lavender at the same time.

That was Freya's place in a sentence, he thought as she shut the door behind them and lit two candles with a taper from the hearth. Lavender because of the little bundles hanging from the ceiling beams — she'd mentioned once, offhandedly, that she dried her own. Iron because of everything else: the poker by the fire, the short blade on a hook near the door that she'd pretended not to notice him noticing, the window latch that looked like it had been replaced three times with progressively heavier hardware. Even her teacups were thick-walled things that could double as weapons if you were desperate enough.

"Sit anywhere that isn't covered in paperwork," she said. "Or do sit on the paperwork. It's mostly invoices for charms that don't work."

"They work," Theo said, settling onto her couch, which was old and deep and swallowed him to the shoulders. "The one you sold me. The anti-frost charm on my seedlings. Nothing's died since."

"That's called winter ending."

"It's called your charm," he said, "and you know it."

She looked at him across the candlelight for a moment too long — that look she did, like she was checking a door was locked — and then laughed, quick and low, and went to put the kettle on.

Third date. He'd counted. He suspected she counted too and would die before admitting it.

Their first date had been an accident, technically. She'd come back to his garden stall at closing time to argue about whether marigolds actually deterred aphids or just made people *feel* like they did, and the argument had lasted until the market lamps were lit and neither of them wanted to go home. The second date had been deliberate: bread from the baker on Coriander Street eaten walking the old aqueduct wall while she pointed out which rooftops the witch-hunters used for their evening patrol routes, casually, the way other people pointed out interesting birds. Tonight she'd invited him here, which felt like a step up some staircase whose height he couldn't quite judge.

He liked her. That was the simple version. The complicated version was that being around her felt like standing next to a well — pleasant on top, unfathomably deep underneath, with a sound coming up out of it sometimes that he didn't have a name for.

"Theo." Her voice came from the kitchen alcove. "Do you take honey?"

"Is that a real question or a character test?"

"Both."

"Lots of honey."

"Correct answer." A pause. "There's a bookshelf behind you. If you're going to snoop, at least do it where I can see you."

"I wasn't going to snoop."

"You've been looking at it for ten seconds. Your ears went red."

His ears, traitorously, went redder. He got up and crossed to the shelf because it was easier than arguing with her about it.

It was a good shelf, the kind that told you more than its owner probably intended. Two shelves of cheap novels, spines cracked white. One shelf of gardening manuals — actual ones, dense-looking, which surprised him until he remembered she'd won the marigold argument. And on the bottom shelf, lying flat because it was too heavy and too old to stand upright, a handful of books that clearly belonged to a different life than the rest: leather-bound, corner-worn, their titles stamped in faded gilt.

*VHAREN CODEX*, said the fattest one. *Being a Collection of Oracles, Prophecies, and Civic Testimonies Concerning the Covenant Years.*

He knew the name the way everyone in Vharen knew the name — the way you know the shape of a country on a map without ever having gone there. The Codex was history-school furniture. Old prophecies from before the Ban, from the century when the city still had its god and its priests and all the machinery of wonder that had been dismantled, outlawed, and swept into memory fifteen years before Theo was old enough to remember anything else. Nobody read it anymore except scholars and cranks. There was a copy in the public reading room behind glass, and a man named Ostrel who hung around the fountain steps reciting bits of it and was generally understood to be mad.

Freya owned a copy. Freya, who sold luck-charms with a wink and called them nonsense even as she sold them. Freya, who flinched — he'd seen it, twice now, small as a blink — whenever a hunter's boots rang past.

"Honey or not?" she called again.

"In a minute." He worked the book free. It was heavier than it looked, its pages soft as cloth at the edges, smelling of dust and something faintly sweet, like old smoke. Someone had loved this book once; someone had read it so many times the spine had gone loose and the ribbon marker had frayed to a thread. "Why do you have this?"

The kettle stopped singing. In the quiet after, he heard her cross the room, and then she was beside him, drying her hands on her apron, looking at the Codex with an expression he couldn't read at all.

"My mother's," she said. "Family thing. Everyone kept one, back in the day. Like keeping a bible nobody believes in."

"Do you believe in it?"

"I believe in selling charms to clerks who need to believe in something." She took the book from him gently, weighed it in both hands like a stone she'd carried a long way, and set it back on the shelf face-out, deliberately, the way you'd turn a photograph toward the light. Then, softer, almost to herself: "It's just words. Dead men's guesses. Half the prophecies in there contradicted each other, and the other half came true only because people made them come true, which is worse."

"But you keep it."

"I keep a lot of things I don't use."

He should have let it lie. He knew that later, with the terrible clarity hindsight gives you, the way you can see exactly where on the ice it cracked. But she'd turned back toward the kitchen, and the candles were doing something kind to the line of her jaw, and the evening was warm and close around them like a held breath, and he wanted — stupidly, greedily — one more piece of her. Any piece. She gave him herself in fragments, always fragments, and he'd started collecting them without realizing that's what he was doing.

"Read me something," he said.

She stopped with her hand on the kettle handle.

"What?"

"One prophecy. A short one. You pick." He smiled, to make it easy. "Consider it a party trick. If it's gloomy, we'll drink tea and mock it. If it's romantic, I get to be smug all night."

"Theo—"

"One passage. Come on. What's the worst dead man's guess ever cost anyone?"

She stood very still. Outside, far off, a hunter's whistle sounded — two notes, the changing-of-the-watch — and he watched her whole body listen to it and then deliberately unlisten. When she turned around her face was arranged into something wry.

"Fine. One passage. And then we eat, and the book goes back on the shelf, and you stop collecting me like a magpie."

"I don't—"

"You absolutely do." She lifted the Codex down again, but she didn't hand it over. Instead she sat on the couch with it in her lap and patted the cushion beside her, and he went, and she opened the book to somewhere in the middle — no searching, no browsing, straight to a page as if her thumb knew the way without her permission — and the frayed ribbon fell out into the gutter and lay there between them like a shed skin.

"This is the part everyone quotes at festivals, a hundred years ago," she said. "Before the Ban. It's called the Verse of the Bridge. Scholars think it was written by three different hands, which is why it doesn't scan properly." Her voice had gone dry and lecture-flat, armor of a kind he was starting to recognize. "Ready? Prepare to be underwhelmed."

She read:

"*When the last garden lies fallow and the city forgets the green tongue,*"

Her voice was nice to listen to even flattened like that. Low, unhurried, a little rough at the edges. He closed his eyes, partly to enjoy it, partly because he'd learned that she spoke more freely when she wasn't watched.

"*there shall come a keeper of growing things, unlettered in the arts, unblooded by the covenant, who tends what others abandoned.*"

Theo's eyes opened.

Not all at once. First just his attention sharpening, the way it did when a customer asked a question that was slightly wrong. Then something colder, moving up through him slowly, like frost climbing a pane.

"*He shall be marked at the wrist with the sign of the root-stock,*" Freya read on, "*born under a gray sky, third-born of a third-born, and he shall not know what he carries until it is shown to him.*"

"Theo?" she said. "You've gone strange."

"Keep reading," he said. His voice came out level, which surprised him.

"*On the eve of his knowing, he shall sup by candlelight in a high room, in the house of one sworn and silent.*"

The candles were right there. On either side of them, on the little table where she'd set out bread and cheese and two cups, burning steadily, throwing their light up the wall to the beams where the lavender hung. High room. Third floor. He could see the rooftops below the window, black against the gray sky — the sky was always gray now, everyone said so, the whole city said so, the sky had gone gray the year the magic died and had never quite cleared—

"*And she shall set before him bread and the flower called heartsease,*"

His gaze went to the table.

To the small clay pot sitting beside the bread board. The one he'd brought her tonight, cut that morning from his own beds because he hadn't been able to stop thinking about her and hadn't known how to say it and flowers were the only language he'd ever been fluent in. Little violet-and-yellow faces nodding in the candle-heat.

Heartsease.

He hadn't told her what they were. He was almost sure he hadn't told her what they were. He'd said *I brought you these* and nothing else, and she'd found a jar for them and put water in it and hadn't commented, and now the word *heartsease* was sitting in the air between them like a coin someone had dropped and neither of them would reach for.

Freya had stopped reading.

The book lay open in her lap. She was looking at him — not at his face, he realized, but lower, at his left wrist, where the birthmark sat in the crook of the joint, the dark branching mark his mother had called a strawberry stain and his grandmother had called nothing at all, refusing ever to speak of it, turning away whenever he'd scratched at it as a boy. It branched, that mark. Forked and forked again, fine dark lines running up under his sleeve.

Like roots.

"Theo," she said again, and now her voice had none of the flatness in it. It had something underneath instead, something he'd heard only once before, the night a hunter had followed them for three blocks and she'd steered them down an alley without breaking stride, chatting about weather, her hand finding his in the dark and gripping it hard enough to hurt. "What does the next line say. Read it yourself."

"No," he said. "You read it."

"Theo—"

"*Please.*"

She looked down. Her finger found the line. He watched her lips move ahead of her voice, one line, two, and then he saw the color leave her face the way water leaves a sink — fast, total, all at once — and every comfortable explanation he'd been building collapsed at its foundation.

"*On that night,*" she read, and her voice had gone thin, "*he shall read the verse aloud in the house of the silent-sworn, not knowing why, and the knowing shall begin.*"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past on cobbles. The kettle ticked as it cooled. The candles burned.

"That's not possible," Theo said. He heard himself say it and heard how young he sounded. "That's a trick. That's — you planted the flowers, or you knew what they were, or—" He stopped, because he was listing reasons and every reason was smaller than the fact in front of it. "How long have you had that book open to that page?"

"A hundred years," Freya said. "The ribbon hasn't moved since my mother died. I haven't read this passage in six years. I don't—" She closed the book, gently, the way you'd close a door on a sickroom. "Theo, I swear to you on whatever you want me to swear on, I did not plan this. I don't plan anything anymore. Planning is—"

She stopped. He watched her decide not to finish the sentence, and the shape of the unsaid thing was enormous.

"*Unblooded by the covenant,*" he said slowly. "What covenant? What does that mean? Freya. What is the covenant?"

"It's nothing. It's old history. It's—"

"You knew what the flowers were." It came out of him quietly, and it was worse for being quiet. "You knew what they were called and you didn't say anything, because you recognized the line. You recognized it before you finished reading it. Which means you've read it recently, or you never forgot it, or—" He stood up. His legs felt borrowed. "Which means you looked at me sometime in the last month and thought about this page."

Freya rose too, the Codex still in her hands, holding it against her chest like a shield or a wound. Up close her eyes were doing the thing he'd noticed on their first date and filed away without understanding: that look of someone standing at the edge of a well, listening to something come up from far below.

"I looked at you," she said, "and I hoped I was wrong."

"What does that mean?"

"It means sit down, Theo."

"It means *tell me*."

For a moment neither of them moved, and the candles threw their shadows huge up the wall, two figures leaning toward each other over a small clay pot of heartsease, and outside the gray sky pressed at the window like a hand laid flat against the glass.

Then someone knocked at the door.

Three blows, evenly spaced, heavy enough to make the hook by the frame jump. Not a neighbor's knock. Neighbors knocked lightly, apologetically, twice. This was the knock of someone who expected the door to matter less than the door itself.

Freya went white to the lips. The Codex slipped from her arms onto the couch, falling open to the frayed ribbon, to the verse, to the line about the knowing that begins — and she moved, fast and silent, putting herself between Theo and the door as though her body were something that could stand against whatever was on the other side of it.

"Theo," she breathed, without turning her head. "Listen to me very carefully. However this goes in the next minute — whatever I say, whoever I tell you to be — don't argue with me. Don't be brave. Be ordinary. Can you do that? Can you be completely, boringly, forgettably ordinary?"

The knock came again. Harder.

"Freya," Theo whispered, "who is it?"

From the hallway, muffled through the door, a voice — official, bored, terribly patient:

"Freya Sandoval. Open in the name of the Hall. We know he's there. We've memorized the page."

* * *

Later — much later, in a cold room with wet stone walls — Theo would try to remember exactly what happened in the next thirty seconds, and find it arrived in pieces, like a plate broken and gathered up by feel.

He remembered Freya's hand shoving the Codex under the couch cushions in one motion, smooth as sleight of hand. He remembered her snuffing one candle with her fingers without a hiss, halving the light. He remembered her mouth next to his ear, faster now, urgent: *"Back door's through the bedroom, bolt's rusted, lift then push —"* and then the door opening before he'd taken a single step, because of course it was already unlocked, because of course she'd never really believed locks were the point.

He remembered three hunters filling the doorway, gray cloaks, gray badges, faces like men arriving to collect a debt rather than arrest a criminal. He remembered the leader, a woman with a scar through one eyebrow, holding up not a warrant but a piece of paper covered in handwriting — handwriting Theo somehow knew, with a sick lurch, was a transcription. Of a verse.

He remembered Freya saying, in a voice he had never heard her use, bright and bored and market-day light: *"Officers. At this hour? It's only the gardener. He's fixing my window box."*

And he remembered — this most of all, this above everything, because it was the first crack in the world he'd lived in for twenty-six years — the scarred woman looking past Freya directly at him. Looking at his wrist, visible below his pushed-up sleeve, at the dark branching mark. And smiling, without any warmth whatsoever, the way a person smiles at a seed they have been waiting all season to sprout.

"There you are," she said softly. "*Root-stock.*"

And the candle flames — both of them, the one living and the one dead, the dead one impossibly — leaned toward Theo like flowers toward the sun.
