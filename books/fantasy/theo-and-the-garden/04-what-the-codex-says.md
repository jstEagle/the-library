## 4. What the Codex Says

*Freya*

The tea went cold between them, and neither of them moved to warm it.

Theo sat on the far end of her sofa with the Vharen Codex open on his knees, and he had not turned a page in ten minutes. The fire ticked. Rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere below, a neighbor's dog complained about the weather and gave up.

"Read it again," Freya said.

"You've heard it twice."

"I want to hear how *you* say it."

He looked at her then — that slow, level look of his, the one that made her feel like soil being tested for something worth growing — and she saw that behind the mildness he was frightened. He was better at hiding it than she'd expected a gardener to be. Or worse at hiding it than she'd hoped. She hadn't decided which.

"*When the Bridge comes,*" he read, "*he will come in the gray months, with rain on his hands and loam under his nails. He will not know what he is. He will walk past his own reflection in shop glass and greet it like a stranger.*"

His voice had gone quiet in the way voices go quiet when they're trying not to shake.

"*He will buy flowers he does not need from a woman who sells what she should not.*"

The words hung there. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the roof, as if it had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

"That's us," Theo said. "That is — word for word. That's the flower stall. That's me buying cut flowers I didn't need because you were—" He stopped. Started over. "Freya. This book is a hundred years old."

"A hundred and six," she said. "The binding's been re-sewn twice."

"How would you know that?"

Because she'd handled it often enough. Because in fifteen years of a silent god and a banned craft, she had read every page of every surviving text about the covenant, hunting for one line that explained the silence. Because she knew this book the way an exile knows a map of a country that no longer exists.

"I've read it before," she said.

"When?"

"Years ago." She wrapped both hands around her cold cup. "It didn't mean anything to me then. It meant nothing to anybody. Half the passages are gibberish, the other half are vague enough to fit anyone — the classic fortune-teller's trick. *A tall man will come. A woman will weep.* It's cheap prophecy. The witch-hunters keep it on their list of permitted texts precisely because it's harmless." She paused. "This passage isn't."

"No," Theo said. "This passage isn't."

He got up — restless, suddenly, too big for her small room — and carried the book to the window, where the lamplight fell better across the page. She watched him stand there with the rain silvering the glass behind him, and something in her chest did a slow, painful turn, because he fit the window. He fit the light. He fit the passage. And she had known it since last night, since the moment he'd laughed at his own reading and said *what a coincidence*, and she had kissed him to stop herself from saying what she thought, which was: *it isn't a coincidence, it never was, and I think I have been walking toward you for fifteen years without knowing the road.*

"The next verse," she said.

"There isn't one. The page ends here. Look — someone's cut the rest out. There's a stub where the leaf was trimmed away, see?" He tilted the book so she could see the ragged edge near the spine. "Someone didn't want this part read."

"Hunters do that. They trim the dangerous verses out of permitted copies." She set down the cup. "The untrimmed ones exist. A few of them."

"Where?"

"In places people like me aren't welcome anymore."

He turned around fully. "Freya. Tell me what you know. Not the market version. Not the charming version. What you actually know."

And there it was — the question she had spent three dates building walls against. He deserved better than walls. He also deserved a life in which the answer to that question was *nothing, darling, it's just an old book*. She could give him one or the other, but not both.

* * *

*Theo*

She told him in pieces, the way people tell things they've rehearsed never having to say.

She told him that magic used to be legal. Not common — legal. That there had been a covenant, an old agreement between the city and something beneath it, and the something had been a god, and the god's power had run through sworn servants called oath-bearers, and those oath-bearers had healed and warded and grown things that had no business growing in Vharen's thin stony soil.

"And then?" Theo asked.

"Then, fifteen years ago, the sky went gray."

"What does that mean, exactly?"

"It means exactly that." Her hands moved as she talked — he was learning her language, the way her fingers spoke when her mouth held back. "One morning. No clouds, no storm. Just — gray. Like the color drained out of the light and nobody could say where it went. Every oath-bearer in the city felt it happen. Our gods went quiet. Not dead — quiet. The way a house goes quiet when the person in it stops breathing."

She said it flatly, but her knuckles had gone white around nothing at all.

"The council declared the covenant broken," she went on. "Declared the gods gone or hostile or fraudulent — they tried all three versions on different audiences. And magic, which only works through the covenant, became contraband overnight. Sworn servants became smugglers. Some of us got out of the trade." A short breath that wasn't quite a laugh. "Some of us sell luck-charms that are mostly string and good intentions."

"And you?"

"I sold what I shouldn't, for a while. Then I stopped. Mostly." She met his eyes. "You're looking at me like—"

"I'm not looking at you any way."

"You're looking at me like a man deciding whether to be afraid."

He considered that honestly, because she had earned honesty and he suspected she could taste anything less. "I'm deciding whether to believe you. Fear can wait its turn."

That surprised a real laugh out of her — brief, cracked in the middle, quickly mended. "Fair. Believe this, then: the Codex says a Bridge is coming. Someone who stands in both realms at once. Someone who can reforge what the city broke." She nodded at the book in his hands. "The Codex says the god beneath the garden will wake when the Bridge stands. And the passage we just read—" Her voice did something careful. "It describes you, Theo. The gray months. The rain. The flowers. Me."

"Or it describes some other gardener, sixty years ago, and the verse was trimmed because it came true already."

"Do you believe that?"

"I'm a gardener, Freya. I believe in weather patterns and root rot and the fact that most miracles are compost with good publicity." But even as he said it, his thumb was moving over the birthmark on his wrist — the dark, branching thing he'd had since birth, the one his mother had called a *stain* and covered with his sleeve whenever company came. The mark looked, he had always privately thought, like a root system. He had never told anyone that.

"Theo." Freya crossed the room and took the book gently from him, and her hand stayed on the cover after it closed, pressing it flat, as if it might open on its own. "I need to tell you why I can't laugh at this."

"So tell me."

"I swore an oath once. To one of the quiet gods. Fifteen years ago I prayed to her and she answered me every day of my life until one morning she didn't, and the silence has been — it's been the whole shape of my life since. Everything I am is built around that silence." Her eyes were bright and steady and terrible. "And tonight you read me a hundred-year-old verse about a Bridge who wakes the god beneath the garden, and every scar in me stood up and paid attention."

* * *

*Freya*

He didn't touch her. That was the thing she remembered afterward — that in the whole long conversation he never once reached for her, though they had spent two evenings learning each other's reach. He understood, somehow, that touching her now would be taking a side, and he hadn't decided yet.

"So your theory," he said slowly, "is that I'm this Bridge. That I've been — what? Grown? Planted? Sent?"

"I don't have a theory. I have a dread."

"That's not much of an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

He sat down again, heavily, and rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he looked older and younger at once — older in the eyes, younger everywhere else, like a man watching his own life get re-potted.

"Say I believed you. Say I believed all of it. What happens to the Bridge, in the book? After he reforges the covenant. Is there a happy ending?"

"I don't know. That's the part that's cut out."

"Wonderful." He laughed — genuinely laughed, a short helpless sound — and she realized with a lurch that he was going to laugh his way through this, that humor was his shelter the way neutrality was hers, and that she was watching him take refuge in it right in front of her.

"You think this is funny."

"I think it's *hilarious*. I think a hundred years ago somebody wrote my Tuesday into a forbidden book, and I think the woman I've been falling for turns out to be a smuggler-priestess waiting for a messiah, and I think the messiah is supposed to be the guy who can't keep basil alive indoors." He spread his hands. "What else am I supposed to do? Cry?"

"Yes," Freya said quietly. "Crying would be appropriate."

The laughter died in him. Not gradually — all at once, like a candle pinched out.

"Why?" he said. "Why would it be appropriate? What do you know about endings, Freya? You said yourself the last page is gone."

"Because I've read the untrimmed versions." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "I told you they exist. I've seen fragments. And the fragments don't end well for the Bridge. They end with him *used*. Standing in the door between realms while everything pours through him, and what's left afterward isn't a man. The old texts call him a bridge because bridges don't get to be anything else. They get walked on."

The fire popped. Neither of them flinched, which somehow made it louder.

"That's what you believe," Theo said. "You believe I'm a door, and doors get opened by other people."

"I believe the prophecy needs you alive and willing and *empty*," Freya said. "And I have watched this city empty people my entire adult life, and I cannot—"

She stopped. He waited. She found she couldn't finish the sentence, because finishing it meant admitting she already loved him, and admitting that in the same hour she told him he might be consumed by a god seemed like cruelty dressed as honesty.

But he heard it anyway. She watched him hear it — watched something move behind his face like water finding a new channel.

"You can't lose another one," he said softly. "Your god. Now me."

"Don't."

"It's alright. I'm not mocking it." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time all night he reached for her — took her hand, turned it over, studied the faint old scarring across her palm where an oath-token had once burned its mark. His thumb traced it like a man reading braille. "Fifteen years of silence. And along comes a verse that says the silence ends. Of course you can't laugh. You're not afraid for me, Freya. You're afraid to hope."

She wanted to deny it. She opened her mouth to deny it and found nothing there but truth, and hated him very slightly for making her sit in it.

"The garden," Theo said. "The municipal garden. The one I've been restoring — the abandoned one, off Wren Street."

"What about it?"

"It's the oldest ground in the city. Everyone says so. The records office wouldn't even give me the full survey — parts of it are sealed, old foundation layers they won't let anyone dig." His grip on her hand had tightened without either of them noticing. "And the roses there — the wild ones, the century-old stock nobody planted that keeps coming back no matter how many times the city cuts them down — the first week I worked there, they turned toward me."

"Toward the sun," Freya said automatically. Weakly. She knew better. Plants did not track a man across a fenced lot.

"It was overcast," Theo said.

The rain filled the pause for them, patient and enormous.

"I'm not saying I believe it," he said at last. "I'm saying I believe *you*, and you believe it, and the plants — Freya, the plants have been doing it for months, and I told myself it was drainage and light and every boring explanation I could find, because the true explanation was insane." He looked down at his own wrist, at the branching mark, and slowly pushed back his sleeve, showing it to her as if presenting evidence in a trial he was losing. "Does this mean anything to you?"

She stared at it. Something ancient stirred in her memory — a diagram, a marginal sketch in one of Maren's heretical pamphlets, a root-stock drawn with a human wrist—

The knock, when it came, was polite. Three measured raps. The kind of knock that had all night and the full authority of the city behind it.

They both went still. Through the window, down in the wet street, lanterns moved — more than one. More than two.

"Freya," Theo whispered, "who visits anyone at this hour?"

She was already moving, snuffing the lamp with practiced fingers, and the last thing the darkness let her say was:

"Nobody good."
