## 5. The Knock at the Door

The knock came at the hour when the lamplighters were still working their way up Copperlane, three hard raps with the flat of a gauntlet, the sound of a fist that had never learned to ask politely.

Freya knew before she opened her eyes. She had spent fifteen years learning to sleep like a cat in a strange house — one ear always open, one hand always near the knife taped beneath the windowsill — and some part of her, the old part, the part sworn to a god who no longer answered, had been listening for this knock since the night Theo read the Codex aloud and the room went cold around them.

Three days. They'd had three days of pretending it was nothing, of laughing too quickly, of Theo saying *coincidence* in a voice that didn't believe itself, of Freya kissing him and meaning it and hating herself a little every time, because she could feel the truth gathering somewhere below the floorboards like water rising in a cellar.

She lay still in the dark of her bedroom and counted. Three raps, then silence, then — yes — three more. Not a neighbor. Neighbors knocked twice and called your name. This was protocol. This was the Hall's cadence.

Beside her, Theo slept on, his breathing slow and even, one arm flung across the space where she'd been. He slept like a man with nothing under his bed but dust. She envied him that with an ache that felt almost physical.

*The back stair,* she thought. It ran down through the old tannery wall behind her building, narrow as a coffin, and it let out into the alley by Madame Serra's washing-lines. If they moved now, quietly, if he didn't stop to argue —

But even as the thought formed, she knew its shape. She had run from things before. She had run from debts, from contracts gone sour, from a duel in Saltmarket that she still dreamed about. Running was a craft, and she was good at it.

Running with Theo meant running forever. The Hall did not lose interest in prophesied subversives. They would hunt him the way they hunted everything the covenant's ghost had touched — patiently, across borders, through years — until there was nowhere left in Vharen or beyond it where a gardener could be a gardener.

And besides. Even then. Even if they made the back stair.

She would have to go with him.

The thought arrived without mercy, and she let it sit there, because she had promised herself, three days ago, that she would not lie to herself about what was coming. That was the mercenary's discipline, the last discipline she had kept when all the others rotted away: *know the ground you're standing on.*

If she ran with him, she broke her oath. Not bent it — broke it. Oath of neutrality, oath of the gray years, the vow she'd spoken at nineteen with her whole heart burning: *I will take no side, I will make no war, I will carry my god's quiet like a lantern and light no fires.* Fifteen years of small compromises built on that foundation — the harmless charms, the luck-knots, the love-sigils that worked exactly as well as any honest placebo. All of it legal because it was nothing. All of it survivable because she had promised never again to be anything.

If she stayed, she lost him. And the Hall would take him up those stairs, and she would hear the door close, and that would be the last ordinary moment of Theo's life.

The knock came a third time. Harder.

"Freya," said a voice through the door, and her blood went to ice-water, because she knew it. Sergeant Brannoc of the Third Vigil, who bought headache charms off her twice a season and had once, drunk on festival wine, told her about his daughter in Corrow. A human voice. A voice that had laughed with her over a market stall. "We know you're home, mistress. Your shutters were open at dusk."

Beneath her, in the space between sleeping and waking, Theo stirred.

"Don't," she breathed, though he hadn't moved yet, though there was still time. "Theo. Don't answer it."

He blinked up at her in the dark. Even now — she would remember this, she would remember it for the rest of her life — even now, half-asleep, his first instinct was to reach for her hand. His fingers found hers and closed around them, warm and callused from the spade.

"Is it the collectors?" he murmured. "You said rent was—"

"It's not the collectors."

She watched understanding move through him the way frost moves through a windowpane, slow at first and then all at once, creeping inward from the edges until the whole glass is white and you can see nothing through it. His hand tightened on hers.

"They can't prove anything," he whispered. "It's a book, Freya. An old book. Half the passages are gibberish. Anyone could match themselves to it if they tried hard enough. It's — it's horoscopes. It's fortune-telling."

"Yes."

"Tell me it's horoscopes."

She couldn't. That was the whole ruinous truth of the last three days; she couldn't tell him it was horoscopes, and worse, she couldn't tell him why she believed otherwise, because the reasons lived in the sealed rooms of her own history — the gray sky, the silent god, the day the covenant died and took the world's warmth with it. She had kept those rooms locked even from him. Especially from him.

"I'll answer it," she said instead, and heard how steady her voice came out, how mercenary-calm, and hated that steadiness more than she had ever hated anything. "Stay here. Don't make a sound. If it goes badly — the back stair, down past the tannery, Madame Serra will hide you if I've paid her, and I paid her last week."

"Freya—"

"*Stay here.*"

She dressed in the dark: shirt, coat, the plain brown cloak of a woman with nothing to hide. She checked the oath-token at her throat out of fifteen years' habit — lead-gray metal, stamped with a sigil no one living could read — and felt it hang there like an anchor, like a chain, like the only thing left of the person she'd meant to be.

Then she went out into the main room and opened the door.

* * *

Four of them on the landing. Sergeant Brannoc in front, helmet under his arm, looking everywhere except at her face. Behind him, two vigil-men she didn't know, spears grounded, faces blank as coins.

And behind them, filling the doorway's edge like a shadow that had learned to wear a coat, a witch-hunter.

Not the rank-and-file vigil. Not patrol-spears and tax-collector manners. An actual hunter of the Inner Vigil: long charcoal coat, silver chain of office, gloves. Freya had seen maybe five of them in fifteen years, always at a distance, always at executions. They were the ones who read the banned books so no one else had to. They were the ones who carried the memory of the covenant like a blade, honed on a century of ash.

This one was young. Younger than her. That was somehow the worst part — the smooth, unlined face, the eyes that held nothing but professional attention, the way he looked past her into the room as though she were furniture.

"Mistress Sandoval." Brannoc's voice had a forced brightness to it, the tone of a man apologizing with his whole body while his orders spoke through his mouth. "Apologies for the hour. Routine inquiry."

"At this door," Freya said, "nothing is routine. Who's asking?"

"The Inner Vigil," said the young hunter. His voice was soft and precise, each word set down like a coin on a counter. "May we come in?"

"You may not. State your business or state it to my closed door, sergeant's courtesy aside."

Something flickered in the hunter's eyes. Not anger. Interest. He produced, from inside his coat, a folded paper — cheap paper, printer's stock, the kind sold by the ream to offices — and unfolded it with two gloved fingers.

"We're seeking a man," he said. "A laborer. Possibly a gardener. We have reason to believe he has been staying in this quarter." He looked down at the paper. He didn't need to look. Freya understood, with a lurching, sickening certainty, that he wasn't reading at all. He was reciting. He had the page by heart. "Tall. Dark-haired. Hands marked with soil-stain that does not wash clean. Born under the waning star, in the month of roots. Carries a birthmark—" and here the young man's gaze lifted, and found hers, and held it with terrible gentleness, "—shaped like a seed."

Behind her, in the bedroom, in the dark, Freya heard Theo's breathing stop.

"He sleeps," she said. Her voice came out flat and perfect and she marveled at it from a great distance, the way you marvel at a rope bridge holding when it should have snapped. "Alone. My lodger's gone a fortnight. If your page says *gardener*, master hunter, then half of Copperlane matches it — the municipal garden's got six men on its rolls and every one of them born under something."

"The page," the hunter said softly, "says more than gardener." He refolded the paper with the same two-fingered care. "It says the Bridge will come *unheralded, walking among the small folk, healing what he touches without knowing it*. It says the flowers of winter will bloom at his passing. Mistress Sandoval — you sell charms."

"I sell knots and knickknacks. Superstition for clerks."

"You sold a fever-charm to the wife of the underwarden of the Rose Quarter. Nine days ago. Healed in a day, the physicians said. Miraculous, they said." He tilted his head, birdlike, curious. "Did you heal her?"

"I sell string and blessed nonsense. If it worked, the credit belongs to whatever gods aren't listening these days." The words tasted like ash in her mouth — her own old joke, her armor, turned traitor now, because every word of it was a lie she'd been telling so long she'd forgotten it was one.

The hunter studied her for a long moment. Then, without raising his voice, without gesturing at all:

"Sergeant. The bedroom."

Brannoc's jaw worked. "Sir, with respect, the warrant names—"

"The warrant," the hunter said, still gently, "names a description. Descriptions are found in bedrooms. Go."

And Freya stood in the middle of her own front room, and the moment split open around her like a dropped mirror.

Here is what happened in the next four seconds. Here is what she would take out and examine, night after night afterward, turning it over, finding new facets, cutting herself anew on each one:

She could have moved. She was fast — fifteen years of selling charms was fifteen years of staying sharp underneath the dullness, the knife-hand kept true the way a widow keeps a wedding ring polished. Two steps put her between the hunters and the bedroom door. A drawn steel changes every conversation. Brannoc would hesitate — she'd known him ten years, he'd hesitate — and in the hesitation there was a door, and through the door a stair, and through the stair a city full of shadows she knew better than the vigil ever would.

She could have saved him.

Instead she stood still. Instead she folded her hands in front of her like a market-woman waiting out a customs inspection, and said, in the flat voice of neutrality, the voice of her oath, the voice of fifteen gray years: "Search, then. You'll find dust and dried flowers."

She heard herself say it. She watched herself choose it. And some part of her — the part that had knelt at nineteen before a god whose name she still couldn't say in public, the part that had sworn *I will take no side* and had believed, truly believed, that taking no side was a kind of holiness — that part stood apart from her body and screamed.

Because this was what the oath actually meant. This was what it had always meant, under the fine words. *Take no side* meant stand in the doorway and fold your hands. Meant watch. Meant survive. Meant let them take him, because intervening was a side, and sides were forbidden, and the forbidden was the only law left to her when her god went quiet and the world outlawed everything else she loved.

Neutrality, she understood in that moment with total and merciless clarity, was not a virtue. It was a costume cowardice wore to church.

The bedroom door opened. Torchlight spilled out into the hall, yellow and wrong, and two vigil-men came out holding Theo between them, one on each arm, though he wasn't resisting — wasn't even fully awake, blinking, barefoot, his shirt untucked, looking around the room the way a man looks when the dream has ended and the world refuses to explain itself.

His eyes found hers.

That was the worst of it. Not the gauntlets, not the spears, not the young hunter's soft recitation of his birthmark. His eyes found hers and there was no fear in them yet — there was confusion, and trust, absolute and unarmored, the trust of a man who had spent three days being told it was only a book, and who looked at her now expecting her to fix it. Expecting her to say the thing, make the gesture, produce the charm, do whatever it was she did — because she was Freya, who handled things, who always handled things, whose competence he had once called *the most beautiful thing about you*.

"Freya?" he said. Just that. Just her name, with the question hanging off it like a loose thread.

"Answer their questions honestly," she said. "Don't sign anything. Ask for a receipt of custody — they have to give you one, it's regulation. Sergeant Brannoc—" and her voice did not break, she made certain of that with the last of her strength, "—will see you get water. Won't you, sergeant."

Brannoc looked at her. In his eyes, brief and unbearable, was pity — and beneath the pity, disappointment, the specific disappointment of a good man watching another good man do the arithmetic of survival. He'd bought her charms. He'd told her about his daughter.

"Ma'am," he said quietly, and looked away.

They took him down the stairs. Barefoot — someone threw him his boots at the landing, tossed, not handed, and he had to hop and stumble into them while they held his arms, and no one laughed, which was somehow worse than laughter. At the bottom step he stopped, just for a second, just long enough, and looked back up through the doorway at her.

He didn't call out. He didn't plead. He was Theo, after all — the man who grew things, who waited out winters, who trusted seeds and seasons and her. He looked up at her standing in her own lit doorway with her hands folded, and he nodded, small and terrible, as if to say: *I understand. Whatever this is. I understand.*

And then the door of the wagon closed, and the wheels went away down Copperlane toward the Hall of Vigilance, and the street was empty except for the lamplighter, working his way along with his pole, flame by flame, lighting nothing.

* * *

Freya stood in the apartment for a long time.

The bedroom still held the shape of him — the pressed hollow in the pillow, the blanket thrown back, his jacket on the chair where he'd hung it with a gardener's care, smoothing the shoulders flat. On the table, the teacups from the evening just gone. Two of them. His still had a ring of leaf-dust at the bottom.

She picked it up. She held it. She did not wash it, and she knew, even as she stood there cradling a dirty cup like a relic, that she was already building the altar, already marking the spot where grief would live.

*You could follow the wagon.*

No. You couldn't follow the wagon. Following the wagon was a side.

*You could write to Maren — no. You don't know Maren anymore. You could go to the Hall and claim kinship, demand visitation rights — civilians can petition, there's a form, there's a queue —*

A queue. A form. Petitioning the machine that ate prophets, asking politely for scraps of a man, while upstairs in some stone room they compared his hands against a century-old page and wrote *match confirmed* in a clerk's beautiful hand.

She set the cup down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

Then she went to the window and looked out at Vharen, at the gray sprawl of it under the gray sky, the city that had outlawed magic and called the silence peace. Somewhere out there, in the municipal garden, the roses he'd been coaxing back were blooming out of season. Out of season, at his passing. The page had said that too. She'd read it three days ago and told herself it was horoscopes.

Her fingers rose to the oath-token at her throat. Lead-gray. Cold. Fifteen years of *take no side*, and here was the side, arriving late, wearing a gardener's boots, taken from her doorway because she'd stood in it and done nothing.

*I will take no side,* she had sworn. *I will carry my god's quiet like a lantern.*

Her god's quiet. Her god, who had gone silent the same year the sky went gray. Her god, whose name was on none of the Hall's lists because the Hall had struck every name but its own from the record. She had spent fifteen years assuming the silence was abandonment — that she'd failed some final test, prayed some final prayer wrongly, and the divine door had closed.

But the Codex spoke of a god beneath a garden. Of a covenant broken not from above but from below. Of a prison.

She had never let herself finish that thought before. Tonight, alone, with two teacups on the table and the wheels of the wagon still ringing faintly in her ears, she finished it.

And the finishing of it changed the taste of everything — the oath, the gray years, the charms that worked better than they should have, the knock at the door she'd heard coming for three days. Because if the god had not abandoned the world — if the god had been *taken* — then the silence she'd been carrying like a lantern was not faith. It was a hostage's hush. It was the quiet of a thing made to be quiet.

Freya Sandoval stood at her window until the lamps burned low, doing the only kind of arithmetic she had never allowed herself: what an oath was worth when the god who received it had been stolen; what neutrality weighed when neutrality was the prison's favorite law; and what, precisely, she would trade to walk into the Hall of Vigilance and walk out again with a barefoot gardener beside her.

There was a name she hadn't spoken in fifteen years. There was a corrupt sergeant on the Third Vigil's night roster who took bribes and hated himself for it. There was a lockbox under her floorboards holding the last true charms in Vharen — real ones, working ones, the ones she'd sworn never to use because using them was a side.

She unlocked the floorboard.

Down in the dark, wrapped in oilcloth, the old magic waited for her — patient as seed, patient as grief — and it felt, when her fingers finally touched it, less like a weapon than like an answer.

Somewhere across the city, in the cells beneath the Hall of Vigilance, Theo was waking up for the second time that night, in a different kind of dark. He would meet people there. One of them, a dying old woman, would look at his hands and know his name before he said it.

But that was hours yet. For now there was only Freya, kneeling on her cold floor with the lockbox open and her oath lying broken beside her like a shed skin, whispering into the gray silence the first prayer she had dared in fifteen years:

*Hold on. I'm coming. Whoever's listening — hold on.*
