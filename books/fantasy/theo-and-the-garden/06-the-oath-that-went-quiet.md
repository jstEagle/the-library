## 6. The Oath That Went Quiet

The apartment was too quiet.

Freya noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue — constantly, uselessly. Fifteen years of living alone had made silence a country she knew every road in, and tonight it was unbearable anyway, because for three weeks she had known its opposite: Theo humming over her kettle, Theo asking where she kept the salt as if he'd always had a right to know, Theo asleep on her sofa with his boots off and his hands folded like a man who trusted the world to hold still while he rested.

And now the witch-hunters had walked him out of that same doorway with his wrists bound in gray silk — gray silk, they'd actually chosen silk, some clerk's idea of mercy — and the quiet had come back in behind them like floodwater.

She should be moving. She knew that. She was a mercenary; movement under threat was the first law of her body. But instead she sat at her own table with her palms flat against the wood, doing the thing she had sworn never to do again.

She prayed.

*Answer me,* she said to the dark above the lamp. *You never answered when I begged you to stay. Answer me now. Tell me what they've taken. Tell me if he's the one.*

Nothing. Of course nothing. The silence of Vharen, the silence that had been the city's second sky for fifteen years, received her words and gave back nothing at all.

Freya put her head down on her arms and let herself remember, because there was nothing else left to do, and because she had learned long ago that grief is a door you have to walk through in the right order or it walks through you.

* * *

The day the sky went gray had begun beautifully.

That was the cruelty of it, the part that still caught in her throat like a fishbone: it had been *beautiful*. She was twenty-two years old and newly oath-sworn, one season into her service to Vael, the Quiet God, the Gardener Beneath, and she remembered standing in the temple courtyard in the last hour of sunlight thinking that she had finally, finally arrived somewhere in her life. The light came down gold through the smoke of incense braziers. The high priestess Maren — young then, iron-haired even then, with a voice like a bell dropped in snow — was leading the evening rite, and Freya stood in the second row of the sworn, hand on the token at her throat: a small disc of living wood, warm as skin, that had been pressed into her palm the day she knelt.

*By root and rain,* the oath went, *by seed and stone. I keep the ways between. I take no side but the covenant's side. I hold my hand from harm while the covenant stands.* Neutrality, sanctified. The mercenary's discipline turned holy. She had loved that about Vael — that the god did not want her sword pointed anywhere, only held ready, the way a gardener holds a spade between tasks. Other gods wanted blood and banners. The Gardener wanted *tending.* Wanted the small work done faithfully, year upon year, until the small work became the world.

She could hear the garden that evening. That was the thing no one believed afterward, when she told it — that before the Silence, you could *hear* the god. Not words. A hum, deep as roots, felt in the teeth more than the ears, rising out of the ground beneath the old rose tree in the municipal garden two streets from the temple. Every sworn soul in Vharen heard it. It meant: *I am here. Grow well.*

Maren raised her hands to close the rite, and the whole courtyard full of priests and mercenaries and candle-sellers and children leaned toward the benediction like wheat leaning toward sun—

—and the hum stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. Cut clean, the way a lute string cuts a note when the blade comes down mid-song.

Freya would spend the rest of her life trying to describe the next four seconds to people who hadn't felt them. It was not like losing a sound. It was like losing *downward* — like the floor forgetting it was a floor. Every charm in the courtyard guttered at once. The incense smoke fell instead of rising. And the sky — she looked up because everyone looked up — the sky over Vharen went gray. Not clouded. Grayed, evenly, all at once, from horizon to horizon, as though someone had drawn a sheet of dirty glass over the world. The gold light died in the braziers' smoke and did not come back, though the sun was still half an hour from setting.

Then the screaming started, and the falling, and the long gray years.

* * *

Here is what the histories say happened, in the dry language the council allowed to be printed: the covenant failed. The god withdrew. All workings of magic, being sustained by covenant, ceased to be lawful, stable, or safe, and were therefore banned by emergency decree, and the Office of Hunters was chartered to enforce the ban, and the city endured.

Here is what the sworn knew, and what the sworn who survived were never permitted to say:

It wasn't failure. Failure has a shape. What happened in the courtyard that evening was an amputation. One moment the covenant ran through all of them like water through soil — and the next moment it was gone, torn out, and every one of them felt the tearing. Freya had fallen to her knees with both hands pressed flat to the flagstones, reaching for the hum, reaching for her god the way you reach for a limb that isn't there anymore. Around her, others were worse. Old Brother Callen's luck-charms detonated in his pouch, scalding him. The temple's wards collapsed inward and took three acolytes' voices with them — permanently; Freya saw those acolytes decades later, mouthing hymns they could no longer sing.

And the token at her throat — the little disc of living wood, warm as skin — went cold.

Not gradually. Cold as riverbed stone, cold as a coin left out overnight, in a single breath. She had gripped it and thought, absurdly, *he's dead*, because warmth had been the one constant of her service. But gods don't die, Maren said later, gray-faced, gripping the podium to stay upright. Gods can only be *kept.*

Kept. Freya had been too young, too shattered, too busy surviving to ask the obvious question that night: kept by whom?

The council answered it for everyone, in their way, within the month. They announced the Silence was a mercy — that the god had withdrawn to spare the city instability. They announced the ban. They announced the hunters. And they burned the temple records in the plaza, publicly, ceremonially, so that no one could ever check what the covenant had actually contained, what rights it had granted, whose signatures were at its root.

Freya had watched the records burn and felt the oath-token knock once against her sternum — one single tap, like a fingertip through a wall — and had told herself it was her heartbeat.

She was still telling herself that.

* * *

The ban changed everything and pretended it had changed nothing. That was the trick of it, the genius of the gray years. Magic didn't vanish; it just stopped being *legal*, which in a city means it stopped being visible. Freya's training didn't evaporate — you cannot unlearn how to hold luck in your palm any more than you can unlearn swimming — so she did what the scattered survivors of the sworn did. She went underground, and then, more cleverly, she went *small.*

A luck-charm that does nothing but make a nervous clerk feel steadier is not illegal. A knot tied a certain way in a courier's bootlace is not illegal. Freya built a stall in the market selling things that worked by confidence and craft and only faintly, faintly, at the very bottom of their workings, by the dead ember of a covenant that no longer fed them. She charged honest prices. She cheated no one. And every night she checked the token at her throat — cold, cold, always cold — and every morning she swore the oath's remnant to herself in the mirror without sound, because saying it aloud was now technically sedition: *I hold my hand from harm. I take no side.*

No side. For fifteen years, no side. While the hunters dragged hedge-wives and herb-women and one poor madman who claimed to dream in prophecy out of their homes in front of their neighbors. Freya had watched, and kept her hands at her sides, and told herself: *neutrality is the oath. Neutrality is faithfulness. If I break it, I break the last thing left of him.*

It was a good argument. It was airtight. It was also, she was beginning to understand tonight, sitting alone at her table with Theo's teacup still unwashed beside her, a coffin.

Because here was the thought that had been circling her for three weeks like a bird looking for a window, and that tonight, with Theo in a cell somewhere beneath the Hall of Hunters, finally found the glass:

Her god went silent the same season the city began building downward.

She'd never let herself line the dates up properly. Grief keeps its own disorderly calendar. But now, alone, she made herself do it — made herself count backward from the gray sky. The Silence had come in the autumn. And in the spring before that autumn, the council had commissioned the great municipal works: the new cisterns, the deep foundations for the Hall of Hunters, the drainage galleries beneath the oldest quarter. She had been newly sworn, newly proud, and she remembered the surveyors coming to the garden — the municipal garden, the one with the ancient rose tree, the one that hummed — and arguing over the ground, and being refused access by the temple, and leaving with faces like closed doors.

Refused once. And then, apparently, not needing permission.

The god lived beneath the garden. Everyone sworn knew that much; the Gardener Beneath was not a metaphor. The hum rose out of that specific earth. And within eighteen months of the surveyors' visit, the sky went gray and the council burned the records and outlawed the very magic that might have been used to *look* beneath the city and see what the cistern-builders had actually found down there in the dark.

Freya sat up slowly. Her hands had gone cold around the teacup.

They didn't fail the covenant. They *sealed* it. They buried him. The Silence wasn't abandonment — it was a mouthful of dirt, a hand clamped over a mouth. Fifteen years of her mourning a god who had left her, and he had never left. He'd been *taken*, and she had spent those fifteen years keeping the peace of his prison, selling harmless charms, holding her hands at her sides while the jailers paraded through the streets wearing silver badges, calling themselves hunters of witches, hunting — what? Not witches. Witches were incidental. They hunted anyone the old covenant touched. They hunted *memory.*

And now they had taken Theo. Theo, whose birthmark curled up his forearm like a graft-line. Theo, whom the plants in that same garden turned toward as he passed, though neither of them had understood it yet. Theo, whose name matched a page in a book the council had tried to burn along with everything else and somehow failed to reach.

The prophecy. The Codex. The Bridge.

If the god was imprisoned rather than departed, then the prophecy wasn't a doomsayer's fantasy — it was an escape plan. A hundred years old, maybe older, written by someone who had seen this coming and hidden the instructions inside a book the council couldn't quite bring itself to destroy, because destroying it would mean admitting it was real.

And the Bridge was the man she loved, sitting in a hunter's cell, marked for questioning by an office that existed for one purpose: to make sure nothing under the city ever woke up.

Freya set the cup down. It rang against the wood, a small clear note in the silent room, and the silence swallowed it, and she realized she was no longer willing to live inside that silence.

* * *

She moved at last — the old training waking in her limbs like a dog hearing its name. From the false bottom of her chest she took the things she had promised herself she'd never need: her last true charms, the ones that actually drew on the dead ember, the ones that could hang a woman if a hunter searched her right. Three of them. Maybe four, if you counted the oath-token itself, which she was suddenly, terribly willing to count.

She wrapped them in oilcloth. She pulled her coat on. In the mirror, for half a second, she saw the twenty-two-year-old kneeling on the courtyard stones, reaching for a god who hadn't left her, and she spoke to her aloud, across the fifteen gray years, without moving her lips:

*I'm sorry I believed the sky. I'm going down to find out what they built over you.*

Somewhere beneath the Hall of Hunters, a man with a gardener's hands was sitting in the dark, and the dark was deeper than the hunters knew — it went all the way down, past the cisterns, past the foundations, to a door that no one in Vharen had opened in a hundred years.

Freya blew out the lamp, and the oath-token at her throat tapped once against her sternum — one tap, unmistakable, like a knuckle on wood, like something knocking from the other side.

She went out into the gray evening to buy a corrupt man's silence with the last miracles she owned.
