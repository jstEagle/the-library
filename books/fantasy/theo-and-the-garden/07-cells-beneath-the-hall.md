## 7. Cells Beneath the Hall

They took his boots at the door.

Theo hadn't understood why, at first — he'd stood in the antechamber of the Hall of Judgments with his stockinged feet on cold slate while a hunter with a ledger face catalogued him like a shipment of turnips: height, hair, the mole below his left ear, and then a long pause over the birthmark on his forearm, the one shaped like nothing much, a brown smear he'd had all his life. The hunter wrote something beside it. Wrote it twice.

"Boots," the hunter said, holding out a canvas sack. "Laces too. Belts. Anything you could tie or hang yourself with."

"I'm not going to—"

"Regulations."

So Theo walked down into the Hall's undercells barefoot, laces trailing from the sack of a man who never looked at his face, past torch-brackets that held no torches because the witch-hunters preferred cold light, pale glass globes that made everyone look drowned. The stairs went further down than any building in Vharen had a right to go. He counted forty-one steps and then stopped counting because it felt like the kind of thing that would matter later.

The cell they gave him was stone and straw, six paces by four, with a slot-door and a bench bolted to the wall. No window. The light-globe in the corridor hummed like a trapped fly.

He sat on the bench. He put his head in his hands. He thought about Freya — her face when the knocking came, the way she'd gone still in a way he'd never seen her go still, like an animal deciding which direction not to run — and he thought, *she hid*. He wasn't angry about it. That surprised him most. She'd pressed herself into the bedroom dark and he'd understood, even as the hunters' hands closed on his arms, that hiding was the bravest thing she knew how to do badly. Her whole life was a held breath. Fifteen years of it.

*The Bridge shall come walking out of the water-meadow,* the Codex said, *with green stains on his hands and no sword.*

He looked at his hands. There were green stains on them. There had been green stains on them every evening for two months, since he'd started restoring the municipal garden, and before that there had been green stains on them for other reasons, other gardens, his mother's allotment, his grandfather's orchard rows. His whole life was green stains. He'd never once thought of it as a qualification for anything except dirt under other people's fingernails when he shook their hands.

"No sword," he said aloud, and his voice sounded wrong in the cell, small and swallowed. "No sword. Great."

He didn't cry. He wanted to, distantly, the way you want sleep or bread — as an idea rather than a possibility. What he did instead was listen, because the cells talked. He discovered this within the hour. The walls carried sound the way river gravel carries footprints; everything said anywhere down here arrived eventually everywhere else, worn smooth but legible.

* * *

The first voice belonged to a man three cells down who introduced himself, without being asked, to the entire corridor.

"They'll see," the man said. "That's all I want on record. When the Bridge comes, they'll see I was true and they were blind, and I will stand at his right hand." A pause. A wet, satisfied breath. "I've been marked since I was nine years old. My mother knew. She wrote it in my baby book."

"You're in prison, Dossel," said another voice, farther off, dry as old paper. "Your mother's baby book is not a legal defense."

"The prophets were all imprisoned!"

"The prophets were all *invented*, Dossel. Every last one of us. You know how many of us they've had down here? I do. I've been down here eleven months. I keep count. Forty-three prophesied vessels, bridges, roots, keys, and heralds, and every single one of them a fraud, a fanatic, or a fool. Usually all three."

Theo lay back on the bench and stared at the ceiling, where moisture traced slow maps.

"What are you?" he asked the ceiling, and then louder, toward the wall. "What are you in for?"

Silence. Then the dry voice again, closer now, amused. "The new one speaks. Let me guess. Green stains?"

"How did you—"

"It's always something. Hair the color of wheat. Born during a storm. A scar shaped like a leaf. The Codex is vague on purpose, boy, that's what makes it useful. Forty-three matches in eleven months. The hunters got promoted for finding the first three. Now it's a quota." A cough, long and grinding. "I claimed I was born holding a sprig of rowan. Which the Codex mentions, if you squint. Which I squinted. I'm a forger by trade. Documents. It seemed like a natural extension of the business."

"And the others?"

"Dossel you've heard. Fanatic. Genuinely believes it, which is worse than fraud — frauds can be reasoned with. There's a woman down the end who claims the prophecy refers to her cat. There was a man who ate only things he grew himself and recited the passage backward until they moved him somewhere with padding. And there's—" the voice paused, and its dryness changed texture, softened into something careful. "There's Sefa. Cell nine, if you can hear me, girl. New man's asking."

From far down the corridor, faint as smoke, came a voice so thin Theo had to hold his breath to catch it. "Is he the real one?"

"No, Sefa."

"He might be."

"He's not."

"But he *might* be," she said, and there was such gentleness in it, such terrible hope, that Theo found himself sitting up straight on the bench with his heart going hard against his ribs. "Then tell him I said hello. Tell him the Bridge shouldn't forget the ones who waited."

"She thinks her dead son was the Bridge," the forger said quietly, when the silence had settled back. "Died in the purges, fifteen years ago. First wave. They burned the oath-tokens in Vharen Square and anyone who argued. Her boy argued." A pause. "She's dying, by the way. If that matters to your sense of drama. The cells are damp and she's old and the hunters don't call physickers for prophetic frauds."

Theo sat with that. Outside these walls it was evening, probably. Freya would be pacing her apartment, doing whatever a woman did instead of screaming. Somewhere above, the city was eating dinner.

"My name's Theo," he said.

"We know," said forty-three cells, more or less, in overlapping voices, and someone laughed, and someone sobbed, and Dossel began reciting the Codex passage from memory, getting it wrong in ways that made the forger correct him wearily, line by line, like a schoolmaster with the world's worst classroom.

* * *

They fed him barley soup at some point that might have been midnight. The slot scraped open and a tin bowl slid through and Theo said thank you to the boots of the person outside, and the boots paused — just a fraction — before moving on.

Later — hours, maybe — footsteps came again, slower ones, accompanied by a third sound: the soft drag of cloth, the shuffle of someone being helped along. Keys turned at cell nine, three doors down, and through the wall Theo heard the hunters' voices, bored and low.

"One visit. Make it quick, grandmother."

And then a voice like dry leaves settling, close to the wall, close to him though she couldn't have known which cell he was behind.

"Which one are you?" Sefa called. Not to the corridor. To him. "The new one. The gardener. I heard them bring you in. They always say the trade out loud, did you know that? Like it proves something. 'Gardener, male, twenty-six.' As if the Codex cares about paperwork."

"I'm the fraud," Theo said. "Apparently."

"Mm." He heard her lower herself, slowly, the many small negotiations of an old body finding a floor. "Come to the front of your cell, gardener. Where the light is."

"There isn't any light."

"There's always some light. Come where I'd see you if the slot opened."

He did. He knelt on cold stone with his face near the door-slot, feeling foolish, feeling watched.

"Hands," she said. "Put your hand in the corridor. They won't stop you. They think we're all mad, and mad people hold hands through door-slots all the time."

His hand hovered there. The corridor globe hummed. Somewhere Dossel had fallen silent mid-recitation, listening.

He put his hand through.

Her fingers found his almost at once — papery, cool, astonishingly strong for their size. She turned his palm up. Ran one fingertip along the lines of it like a reader following text. Then she turned his wrist, gently, and her thumb stopped.

Stopped, and pressed.

The birthmark. The brown smear on his forearm, the nothing-shaped thing he'd stopped seeing decades ago.

She went very quiet. The whole corridor went quiet. Even the globe's hum seemed to lean in.

"Root-stock," Sefa said.

The word went through him like cold water through warm. "What?"

"Root-stock." Her thumb stayed on the mark, tracing it now, and her touch changed — no longer searching, but *reading*, the way Freya read the Codex, line by line, sure of every word. "My grandmother worked the god-garden. Before the gray sky. Before the covenant broke. She used to say the god kept gardeners the way orchards keep bees — not servants, root-stock. Cuttings from the oldest tree, planted out in human soil, generation after generation, so that something of the garden would survive even if the garden itself was lost." Her breath rattled, caught, steadied. "She showed me the mark once. On her own arm. Said if I ever met a person wearing it, I should kneel, and I should ask them to remember us."

"Sefa," the forger said softly, warningly, from his cell. "Girl. Don't do this to yourself."

"Hush, Tam." Her fingers tightened on Theo's wrist. Not painful. Anchoring. "Boy. Gardener. Fraud, if you need to be that tonight. Look at the mark properly. Have you ever actually looked?"

He had. Of course he had — as a child, in baths, in summer, idly. It was a smear. A stain. Nothing.

But kneeling there in the cold light, he looked the way she meant, and his memory unspooled it larger than life: the whorled edge of it, the branching darker lines running through the brown like veins through a leaf, like roots through soil, like — 

Like the rose tree in the middle of the municipal garden. The old one, the enormous one, half-dead and half-unkillable, the one the city's records said predated the park itself. He'd pruned it his second week. Its bark had exactly those branching lines.

Theo's mouth went dry. "It looks like a tree."

"It looks like *the* tree," Sefa whispered. "Root-stock. Cutting of the first garden. Planted out in human soil." Her hand trembled against his wrist, and he realized the trembling wasn't fear. It was joy — fierce, spent, terminal joy, a woman emptying her last purse. "Fifteen years I've sat in rooms like this and told them my son was the Bridge because I couldn't bear a world where he died for nothing. And now the gods — the god — whoever's left to listen — sends me *you*. In my last month. In a cell. With your hand in mine." She laughed, a cracked bell of a sound. "They think we're the mad ones."

"I don't understand what it means," Theo said, and his voice came out young and frightened and honest. "Sefa, I don't understand any of it. I grow things. I fixed a garden because it was empty and nobody stopped me. I fell in love with a woman who sells charms, and now I'm going to die for a book I never read."

"You're not going to die for the book." Her grip shifted; her other hand came through the slot and covered his, both her hands around his one, a blessing given bodily. "You're going to live for what the book was pointing at. That's different. That's everything. Listen to me, root-stock. My grandmother told me three things about the god-gardeners, and I've kept them fifteen years like coals in ash, waiting for a reason to give them away. One: they dream in growing things. Do you?"

He blinked. "I — yes. Since I was small. Rows. Roots. Things coming up. I thought everyone did."

"Two: plants turn toward them." She paused. "Even cut flowers in a bowl. Especially cut flowers in a bowl. Have you noticed?"

He thought of Freya's apartment. The market bunch he'd bought her — he'd brought her flowers from his own beds, absurdly, a gardener buying flowers — and how each morning they leaned, all of them, every stem, toward wherever he sat. He'd thought it was the window. He'd rearranged the room in his head to account for the window. He had *built explanations* rather than see it.

"Oh," he said.

"Three," Sefa said, and now her voice dropped to nearly nothing, and the corridor bent all its attention down to hear. "Three: when the time comes, the root-stock doesn't get chosen. The root-stock *chooses*. That was the whole covenant, boy. That was the entire point of it. The god could have grown anything out of anyone. It waited for yes." Her thumbs pressed his pulse, once, twice, like a knock on a door. "Whatever they do to you up there. Whatever the Codex says, whatever the hunters say, whatever the woman you love does or doesn't do. Nobody gets to make you a bridge. Remember that. It's the only thing they can't take from you, and it's the only thing that matters."

Footsteps at the end of the corridor. The hunters returning. Sefa drew her hands back through the slot, and even that withdrawal was deliberate, unhurried, a priestess ending a rite.

"Wait," Theo said. "Sefa. Your son's name. Tell me his name. You said the Bridge shouldn't forget the ones who waited. So tell me, and I won't."

A pause. When she answered, the dry leaves in her voice had gone soft and green somehow, springtime at the bottom of a well.

"Aldric," she said. "His name was Aldric. He argued in the square."

Keys turned. Cloth dragged. The footsteps receded, taking her back to cell nine, and Theo knelt alone on the cold stone with his hand still stretched out into the empty corridor, holding the shape of her grip, and found that his terror had not gone away — it had simply been joined by something else, something that grew in the space terror left unclaimed.

Belonging. Strange, unwanted, undeniable. Forty-three frauds and fanatics down here in the drowned light, and one dying woman three doors away who had touched his arm and known him better than he'd known himself for twenty-six years. Root-stock. A cutting of the first garden. Planted out in human soil.

*Plants turn toward them.*

He thought of Freya's flowers leaning. He thought of the rose tree's bark. He thought, with sudden vertigo, of the garden at midnight, every stem he'd ever passed, every seedling lifting its face.

Above him — muffled by forty-one steps and more, by stone and law and the whole gray weight of the city — a bell began to ring. Not the alarm bells. The proclamation bell. Three slow strokes, a pause, three more. The sound Vharen made when it had decided something.

The boots came back down the corridor. Stopped at his door. A folded paper slid through the slot and lay on the stone between his knees.

Theo picked it up. Unfolded it. Read it by the thin drowned light.

It was a schedule. Public, printed, official — tomorrow's executions listed in neat columns, names and crimes and times. His name was not on it. Instead, across the top, in letters large enough to read upside down:

**BY ORDER OF THE HIGH HUNTER: THE GARDEN IS CONDEMNED. BURNING AT DAWN. THE BRIDGE WILL BE GIVEN TO THE FLAMES WITH IT.**

Below, in smaller type, the final line:

*Witnesses required. Attendance mandatory.*

Theo sat on the bench with the paper in his hands and listened, very carefully, to the sound of the city above deciding to burn the only place he had ever felt like himself.

Three doors down, faint through the stones, Sefa had begun to sing. An old song. A growing-song. He didn't know it — and then, horribly, wonderfully, he did.
