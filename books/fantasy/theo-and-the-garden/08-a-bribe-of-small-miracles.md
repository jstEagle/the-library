## 8. A Bribe of Small Miracles

The hunter's name was Doss, and he drank his beer the way he did everything else: slowly, and with one hand free.

Freya had watched him for three nights before she approached him. She'd watched him take a coin from a fishmonger to lose a citation, watched him look the other way while a smuggler's cart rattled past the customs house, watched him palm an unlicensed candle-stub from a shrine alcove because it smelled like the ones his mother used to burn. Corruption, in Freya's long experience, was rarely grand. It was small. It was hungry. It was a man who wanted to feel that the world owed him something and was willing to settle for beer money.

He sat in the back corner of the Gilded Ewe every evening after shift change, alone, nursing the same tankard for two hours because refills cost money. That was the detail that decided her. Not the bribes — those were common as pigeons. The loneliness. A man who was lonely would talk to anyone who sat down.

She bought the tankard before she sat down.

"You're the charmseller," he said, when she slid it across the table. His eyes were pale and watery, the color of dishwater left too long. "Market row. Little stall by the fountain."

"Freya."

"I know what you sell." He turned the tankard a quarter-turn on the scarred wood, aligning its handle with some private north. "Luck-charms. Knots and beads. Harmless."

"That's the law," Freya said. "Harmless is the law."

"Hm." He drank. He looked at her over the rim, and there was nothing stupid in that look at all, which meant the whole shuffling-drunk-routine was exactly what she'd suspected: camouflage, worn so long it had grown into the skin. She felt a flicker of professional respect, and beneath it, colder, the knowledge that respect would make this harder.

"I'm looking for someone," she said.

"Aren't we all."

"The gardener they took from the market three days ago. Theo. He sells cut flowers by the south gate." She kept her voice level, bored even, the voice of a woman asking about weather. "They say he matched a page in the old Codex. They say he's under the Hall."

Doss set the tankard down. For a long moment the only sound was the taproom's ordinary noise — dice, argument, a fiddler tuning badly — and then he laughed, short and dry as a snapped twig.

"They say a lot of things about the cells under the Hall." He leaned back. "Most of them are true, which is the sad part."

"I want to see him."

"Why?"

Because I love him, she thought, and did not say it, because the word *love* spoken aloud to a witch-hunter would be a knife she handed him hilt-first. Because I hid behind a flour barrel while they put irons on him. Because I have been neutral for fifteen years and neutrality is just cowardice with better posture.

"Curiosity," she said instead. "I sold him charms once. He was kind to me. Kindness is rare enough that I'd like to know how the story ends."

Doss studied her. Somewhere across the room the fiddler found his key and ruined it anyway.

"You know what a visit costs," he said.

"I imagine it's more than beer."

"It is." He turned the tankard again, quarter-turn, handle aligned. "It's the thing you don't sell. Everyone in your trade has one. The real charm. The one you keep wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of your kit because if you ever use it, it stops being yours." He smiled without warmth. "I want it."

Freya's hands went still in her lap.

There were four charms left in the false bottom of her satchel. She had counted them every night for fifteen years the way other people counted savings, and they were not savings, they were relics — the last working works of a covenant that had died with her god's voice. A knot of red thread that would hold a door shut against anything without a name. A riverstone that warmed when someone lied to you. A bird's skull, hollowed and sealed with wax, that carried a whisper up to a mile. And a sprig of rowan, iron-bitten, that would show a person the true shape of whatever stood in front of them.

Her god had made none of them. Her god had only taught her how, back when teaching was allowed, back when the sky over Vharen was blue and prayers came back answered like letters from a friend. Every charm she'd sold since the silence had been a fake — knots and beads, exactly as Doss said, harmless because harmless was all that was left. She'd told herself it was honest work. She'd told herself it was penance. She had never quite believed either.

And now a corrupt man in a hunter's gray coat wanted the last of them, and she was going to give them to him, and the worst part — the part that made her want to put her fist through the table — was that it wasn't even a hard choice. It should have been. Fifteen years ago it would have been unthinkable; ten years ago, merely shameful; five years ago, impossible. Now it was arithmetic. Four charms against one man's life. The god was silent. The god could not object.

*That*, she thought, staring into her untouched drink, *is what surrender actually sounds like. Not thunder. Arithmetic.*

"When?" she said.

"Tomorrow. Shift change, second bell. There's a stair door on the river side of the Hall, the one the coal deliveries use. Ten minutes is what I can give you. Ten minutes and no more." He finished the tankard at last. "Bring the charms tonight. All of them. I'll know if you're holding back."

"How?"

"Because I've seen a hundred people hand over their last miracle," Doss said, standing, dropping a coin beside the empty tankard though it had been a gift. "They always try to keep something. You can smell it on them. Like smoke."

---

She didn't sleep. She sat on the floor of her apartment with the satchel open between her knees and the oilcloth unwrapped, and the four charms lay in the lamplight looking smaller than they had any right to look. Relics. Savings. The last four words of a dead language.

She picked up the red thread first and held it to the light, and memory came the way it always did, uninvited: fifteen years and one season ago, her knees in the mud of a hillside shrine, her hands tying this same knot while the god — *her* god, the quiet green one, the one whose name was less a word than a season — poured through her fingers like warm water. *Tie it thus,* the god had said, or had seemed to say, in the way that gods speak, which is not sound but certainty. *A door is only a promise. Make the promise strong.*

Then the sky over Vharen went gray. Then the prayers stopped coming back. Then the city, frightened and leaderless, wrote its law: *no magic, no mercy, no exceptions.* Then the purges. Then the years of selling lies to people desperate enough to buy them, and calling it kindness.

Freya set the thread down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Here was the truth she'd been circling for three days, since the witch-hunters' boots receded down her stairs with Theo between them, since she'd come out from behind the flour barrel smelling of dust and her own disgrace: she hadn't hidden because hiding was wise. She'd hidden because hiding was easy, and because fifteen years of practicing helplessness had made helplessness feel like virtue. *I swore neutrality,* she'd told the flour barrel, the dark, the ceiling. But oaths are living things, and hers had died quietly years ago, and she had been keeping its corpse embalmed on the shelf of her conscience and calling it faithfulness.

Theo had looked at her over the hunters' shoulders. Just once. Just a flicker, brown eyes finding hers through the doorway crowd, and there had been no accusation in them, which was infinitely worse than accusation. He had looked at her the way he looked at frost-bitten seedlings — with a kind of patient, hopeless tenderness, as if to say, *well, this happens; grow where you can.*

She had not slept. She had counted charms instead.

Now, by lamplight, she laid them out in a row: thread, stone, skull, rowan. And because there was nothing left in her that respected her own rules anymore, she did the thing she had sworn off doing since the sky went gray.

She worked.

Not a fake-work, not the market pantomime of muttering and knotting that she performed for clerks and grandmothers. Real work. She took the red thread between her fingers the way the god had shown her on the hillside, and she spoke the old binding under her breath — not a prayer, because prayers needed someone listening, but a working, which only needed hands — and she waited for the deadness to rise up and swallow it, the way it had swallowed everything for fifteen years. The flat gray nothing where power used to live. The silence answering silence.

The thread warmed.

Freya's breath stopped. The thread *warmed*, a faint pulse of heat like a sleeping bird's heartbeat against her fingertips, and the knot drew itself tight with a soft creak of fiber, and the little charm lay on the floorboards glowing the way embers glow — not alight, but remembering fire.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room. "No, that's —"

She tried the riverstone. Held it and spoke the truth-taste into it, the old words, half-forgotten, stumbled over twice. The stone flushed warm as a held hand.

The skull. The rowan. One after another, clumsy with fifteen years of rust, she woke them, and one after another they answered, faintly, imperfectly, *but they answered*, and Freya Sandoval sat on the floor of her apartment with four small miracles breathing around her and wept like a girl, ugly and open-mouthed, because she understood immediately what it meant and what it couldn't mean.

It didn't mean her god was awake. If her god were awake, everything would answer — the air, the ground, the rain, the whole drowned language of the world. This was narrower. This was local. This was a radius, a reach, a *proximity* —

This was Theo.

Under the Hall, three streets away, behind iron and stone, the man the Codex called the Bridge, and around him — leaking out of him like light under a door — the covenant itself, waking up, waking *early*, waking because he dreamed, maybe, or because his blood remembered, or because divinity imprisoned for a century had finally found a crack in the wall and the crack was shaped like a gardener.

Freya laughed, wet and disbelieving, wiping her face with the heel of her hand. Of course. Of course her charms had been dead for fifteen years and alive again for three days and she hadn't known, because she hadn't dared to try. Hope, it turned out, wasn't a feeling. It was a test you failed by refusing to sit it.

And here was the bitter joke, the one she told herself while she rewrapped each charm in its oilcloth with hands that wouldn't quite steady: the magic was coming back *because of him*, and to reach him she had to give away the proof.

---

Second bell. Coal-smoke and river fog. The stair door on the Hall's river side opened at her knock exactly wide enough for Doss's face, and his watery eyes went straight past her to the satchel.

"All four?"

"You can count."

"I will." He let her in, checked the alley both ways with the ease of long habit, and closed the door. Inside, by the light of a shuttered lantern, he unwrapped each charm and examined it with the same quarter-turn deliberation he gave his tankards, and Freya watched him and hated him and was grateful to him in equal, sickening measure. When he reached the red thread he held it a moment too long.

"This one's warm," he said.

"Body heat."

"It's been in your satchel." He looked at her. "You worked these recently. Within a day. Maybe within hours." Something moved behind the dull water of his eyes — not suspicion exactly. Hunger of a different flavor. "Magic's supposed to be dead, charmseller. Dead for fifteen years. Everyone knows it."

"Then what you're holding is a knot of thread that got warm by accident," Freya said. "A coincidence. Sell it to a priest as a relic; they'll pay more than I did."

For a moment she thought he would push. She saw the thought move through him — the ambition of it, a living charm in the hand of a Hall hunter, promotion, notice, *leverage* — and she saw him weigh it against the simpler appetite, the certain coin, and she watched simplicity win, because corruption is always the victory of the near over the far. He wrapped the thread away.

"Ten minutes," he said. "Third landing, last cell. Don't speak loud. Don't touch the bars — they're warded with cold-iron filings and it burns. And Freya." He paused at the stair door, lantern low. "Whatever he is, whatever they say the book says — I've walked men down to the pyre yard. It doesn't get quieter down there. It gets louder. Remember that on your way out, and decide what kind of fool you're going to be."

"Every kind," Freya said.

Something that was almost a smile crossed his face and died there. He opened the door onto the dark of the stairwell, and the dark breathed out at her: stone-cold, coal-dust, and underneath it, faint as a memory of summer —

green. Growing things. Warm rain.

Her heart slammed once, hard, like a fist on a door.

She went down.

The cells beneath the Hall of Judgment were older than the Hall above them; everyone said so. The stair spiraled through masonry that predated the city's current name, blocks fitted without mortar, and the walls sweated in the river damp, and the lamps burned low and blue-ish in the bad air. Freya passed landings she didn't look at. Sounds came up the shaft — a man reciting numbers endlessly, somewhere below; a woman singing something that might have been a lullaby or a lament; and once, horribly, a voice that called out *I'm the Bridge, I'm the Bridge, I'm the Bridge* in a cracked sing-song until a guard's boot struck a door and it stopped.

Frauds and fanatics, Doss had said, or would have said. The Codex had been public property for a century; half of Vharen had read the prophecy page; and now every madman who'd ever memorized it was in a cell proving himself. Freya climbed down past their voices with her jaw clenched so hard her teeth ached, thinking: *they locked up the whole city's grief and called it security.*

Third landing. Last cell.

The ward-line glittered faintly across the bars — cold-iron filings suspended in wax, a cheap cruel trick, the kind of anti-magic the city had perfected during the purges. Beyond it, the cell was dim, lit by one slit window high in the wall, and in the dimness a figure sat cross-legged on the stone floor with his hands resting palms-up on his knees, in the exact posture of a man watching something grow.

Theo lifted his head. Even across the cell, even in that light, Freya saw the bruise along his jaw and the way he'd lost weight in three days, and she saw something else too: he was calm. Not broken-calm. *Rooted*-calm. And the air in the corridor — the dead, lamp-flattened, century-silent air — was moving. Faintly. Inward. Toward the bars. Toward him.

Like a garden drawing breath.

"Freya," he said. No surprise in it. As if she'd come home late from market.

"Ten minutes," she whispered, kneeling, keeping her hands well back from the ward-line. "Maybe less. Listen fast."

"Are you all right? They said —" He stopped. Started again. "I saw you. That day. Behind the barrels. I want you to know I'm not — I never expected you to do anything. Your oath —"

"My oath is dead," Freya said, and heard her own voice come out strange and steady, like a blade being drawn slowly. "It died years ago and I've been carrying the corpse around telling myself it was piety. Three days ago I hid behind a flour sack while they took you, and I have spent every hour since understanding exactly what I am." She pulled the satchel around. "So I bribed a corrupt man with my last miracles, and I'm going to get you out of here, and then I'm going to break the other half of my oath, and probably a few laws besides. I'm done being neutral. Neutral is just grief wearing armor."

Theo looked at her for a long moment. Through the bars, through the ward-glitter, his brown eyes were wet and shining and utterly without pity, which was the only way she could have borne it.

"Freya Sandoval," he said softly, "you're the bravest person I've ever met, and I grow things for a living, and I know how long courage takes."

Above them, boots on the stair. Two sets. Descending fast, unhurried the way official feet are unhurried, and Freya was on her feet with her hand on her knife before her mind caught up, and in the cell Theo rose too, smooth and silent, and the air in the corridor bent toward him like wheat toward sun, and somewhere below the woman stopped singing and began, very quietly, to laugh.

"Freya," Theo whispered. "Whatever's coming down those stairs — I think the garden knows we're here."

The boots reached the second landing. And stopped.
