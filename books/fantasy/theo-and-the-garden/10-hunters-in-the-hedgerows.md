## 10. Hunters in the Hedgerows

I had never run from anything in my life before tonight. I hadn't known that running was something you could learn — that it had a grammar, a rhythm, a way of rewriting your body into pure animal arithmetic. *Gap. Hedge. Gap. Turn.* My lungs burned like wet paper. Freya's hand closed around mine and hauled me left where I would have gone right, and the decision she made in half a heartbeat saved us both.

"Down," she hissed. "Low branches."

We folded ourselves beneath an ancient hornbeam whose roots had heaved the flagstones into broken teeth. Behind us, lantern-light swung through the dark of the garden in long, greedy arcs. Boots on gravel. The wet jingle of chain and steel. Six hunters, maybe seven, fanning out from the gate we'd burst through less than a minute ago.

"They saw which way?" I whispered.

"Doesn't matter. They'll split and sweep." Freya's voice was flat as slate. She'd gone somewhere else behind her eyes — some cold country I hadn't visited before — and I understood with a small, sinking clarity that this was the mercenary I'd heard about in fragments: the woman before the charm-stall, the woman before me. "The maze will slow them. It slowed everyone who built it too. Come on."

The hedge-maze occupied the garden's western quarter, planted a century ago by some city council that wanted a place for respectable citizens to lose themselves decorously on summer afternoons. Now it was our only ally: eight-foot walls of yew gone wild and leggy, lanes choked with bramble, moonlight falling in silver coins through gaps the pruning shears had never reached since magic died. Since *she* died — or slept, or was taken; the words still rearranged themselves every time I thought them.

Freya moved through the dark like water finding its level. I followed, and the garden followed me.

I felt it again — the thing I'd first noticed at midnight, fleeing between the beds: plants turning. Not visibly, not dramatically, nothing a lantern-lit hunter would catch. But I felt it the way you feel someone watching across a room. The ivy leaned its blind face toward my passing. The brambles drew their thorns aside from my sleeves and did not draw them aside from hers. At the mouth of each lane we took, the hedges stood a half-inch taller than they should have been, dense as walls, as if the whole labyrinth were quietly closing doors behind us.

"You're doing that," Freya breathed. We crouched at a junction, listening to boots crunch gravel two lanes over.

"I'm not doing anything."

"Keep not doing anything."

* * *

They found us anyway. Of course they did. Hunters are paid to be thorough, and Vharen's witch-hunters were paid very well indeed.

It happened at the maze's heart, a little circular clearing with a dry fountain at its center, a stone basin crusted with lichen and one stubborn water-plant that should have died years ago and hadn't — because I'd been here three weeks ago cutting back the roses nearby, and I remembered how it had turned its leaves toward me then, too, hungry as a cat.

We came into the clearing and there was already a man standing in it.

He was young — younger than me, maybe twenty-three — with a hunter's gray coat buttoned wrong, as though he'd dressed in a hurry, and a short sword drawn but held low, the way you hold something you've never used. His lantern sat on the fountain's rim, throwing his shadow huge against the hedge. He saw us and his breath went out of him in a rush.

"Bridge," he said. Just the word. Like naming a storm.

"Walk around him," Freya murmured. "Left. Don't look at him. He's green, he won't—"

"Stop!" The boy's voice cracked straight up an octave. "In the name of the Covenant of Ash, I — you're under arrest, both of you, I — *stop.*"

His sword came up. It wobbled.

And then the second man came out of the hedge behind us.

I never saw where he'd been hiding — pressed into a gap in the yew, maybe, patient as a spider. He was older, broad, gray-bearded, with the unhurried competence of a man who had done this many times, and he moved directly at Freya with a weighted net in one hand and a cudgel in the other, and everything got very fast and very simple.

Later, I tried to reconstruct those ten seconds and couldn't. Memory broke apart the way a dropped cup breaks — all the pieces real, none of them fitting back together.

I remember the old woman's word — *root-stock* — rising in me like sap. I remember the hedges shuddering without wind. I remember Freya saying, in a voice I'd never heard from her, low and almost gentle: *"Don't. Walk away. Last chance."*

I remember the net coming down anyway, and her catching it — bare-handed, letting the weights wrap her forearm — and hauling the old hunter off-balance into her knee, and the cudgel swinging, and her ducking inside it the way you'd step through a doorway you'd walked through a thousand times.

And I remember the young one. The green one. Coming at me.

That's the part I keep returning to, the piece that doesn't fit: he came at *me*, not at her. Maybe he thought the Bridge was the prize, that taking me would make him a hero, that his wrong-buttoned coat would be replaced with a commendation. Maybe he panicked. His sword was up and his eyes were wide and terrified and certain, all at once, and I did the only thing I knew how to do in a garden.

I asked the ground for help.

I didn't know I was asking. I didn't know asking was possible. But the moss on the fountain's stones — the lichen, the creeping Jenny spilling over the rim, the roots of the hornbeams at the clearing's edge — answered. The flagstones under his boots went slick and alive, moss surging green-black across the stone like a tide, and he slipped, and his sword-arm windmilled, and he fell —

— onto his own blade.

Not onto hers. Onto his own. That's what I tell myself at three in the morning. *His own blade, his own fall, the moss only made him slip.*

But I asked the ground. I know I asked the ground. And the ground answered *me*.

* * *

The sound he made was small. That's the thing nobody tells you. All the stories make dying loud — screams, roars, thunder. But he made a small sound, a punctured sort of gasp, and then the clearing was so quiet I could hear the lantern's flame licking at its glass.

Freya killed the other one.

Not — no. I need to be exact, even now. Especially now. She *dropped* him. A strike to the temple with the weighted edge of the tangled net that put him down hard and still. Whether he lived I don't know. She checked his pulse with two fingers, businesslike, and whatever she found, she said nothing, and we left him breathing or not breathing in the nettles while the world tilted sideways around me.

Because the boy was looking at me.

He was on his side in the wet moss with his own sword in him under the ribs, and he was looking at me with an expression I will carry until I die: not hatred. *Confusion.* The confusion of a man who cannot reconcile what is happening with what was supposed to happen. He'd come to arrest a prophecy and instead he was dying in a garden at the hands of a gardener, and his face said plainly that no one had explained this part to him.

"Freya." My voice came out sanded flat. "Freya, he's—"

"I see him."

"Can you—"

"No." She was beside me suddenly, gripping my shoulder, forcing me to look at her and away from him. Her hands were steady. Her eyes were not; there was something being held down behind them with great effort, like a lid on a boiling pot. "Theo. Listen. There's nothing to be done. Nothing. You hear me? There is *nothing—*"

"He's looking at me."

"I know." Barely a whisper. "I know, love. Look at me."

But I looked at the boy. I think I owed him that. I think that's the least of what anyone owes the people who die near them.

He said something. Blood in his throat made it a wet rattle, and I knelt — Freya's grip slid off, she let me, God help her she let me — and I put my ear close, and what he said was:

"It's real, then."

That was all. *It's real, then.* As if the whole night, the hunt, the arrest, the prophecy, the gray sky under which he'd grown up believing in nothing — as if all of it had been a question he'd carried, and my face above his was the answer arriving too late to matter.

Then his lantern-glass eyes lost their light, and the garden was quiet, and I stayed kneeling in the moss with my hands full of nothing.

* * *

Freya gave us until the count of sixty. Then she took the boy's lantern, and his cloak — practical, unsentimental, and somehow the most merciful thing anyone did that night — and she pulled me up and said, "Mourn him walking. Grieve him later. Right now grief gets us killed."

So I mourned him walking.

Through the maze, lane after lane, with the dead boy's cloak on my shoulders smelling of woodsmoke and cheap tallow, I mourned him in the only language I had: I named the plants we passed. Yew. Holly, gone feral. Elder — bad luck to cut elder, my grandmother said, the elder mother lives in it. Boxwood. Bramble. I named them like beads on a string because naming things was how I kept myself together, because I was a man who grew things and tonight I had asked the ground to hurt someone and the ground had obeyed, and I did not know yet what that made me.

The hedges opened ahead of us. Every junction we approached, the growth had loosened, parted, offered us the way — and behind us I could hear the remaining hunters blundering, cursing, their lanterns snagging on branches that seemed suddenly, inexplicably thick.

"They can't find the lanes," Freya muttered, glancing back. "The maze has changed shape. Theo, the maze is *changing shape.*"

"It knows I'm scared," I said. It came out before I could stop it, and it sounded insane, and it was also simply true, the way weather is true.

She stopped walking. In the blue-dark of the hedge tunnel, she turned and really looked at me — at the cloak, at the blood on my knees where I'd knelt, at whatever my face was doing — and something in her expression broke open.

"Theo—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't say it wasn't my fault. Don't say it would've been worse. I know all of it. I know every version of it. None of them help."

"No," she agreed quietly. "None of them do." She touched my cheek with the back of her hand, careful, as if I were a seedling newly set out and the frost hadn't quite passed. "Fifteen years ago I swore I'd never take a side again. Neutrality. It was the only prayer my god ever left me that still worked. Tonight I broke it." Her jaw tightened. "And the worst part isn't that I fought. The worst part is that when the net came down, I wasn't afraid, and I wasn't sorry. I was *glad* to finally be fighting for something instead of nothing. That's what choosing costs. Not the killing. The relief."

I looked at her — this woman who had spent fifteen years sanding herself down into harmlessness, selling luck-charms that worked by coincidence and courage, and who had tonight wrapped a net around her arm and stepped into a doorway she'd sworn shut forever — and I understood that we had crossed some line together, and that there was no path back across it, only paths forward, all of them narrower than this one.

"What happens now?" I asked.

"Now? Now we get off the streets before the gates seal at dawn. Now we find the one person in Vharen who might know why a hundred-year-old book has your Tuesday evenings memorized." She was already moving, pulling the cloak tighter around my shoulders. "There's a woman. Maren. They defrocked her twenty years ago — called her a heretic, stripped her oath-token, threw her out of the seminary. She keeps a shop of ashes and answers down by the tanneries. If anyone knows what the Codex doesn't say out loud, it's her."

"And if she turns us in?"

"She won't." A pause. "She owes me. And more to the point—" Freya glanced up, and I followed her eyes, and my breath caught.

Above the hedge-line, past the maze, past the wall, the municipal rose garden spread out under the moon — and every rose in it was open. Every single one. Out of season, out of reason, hundreds of blooms unfurled wide in the midnight cold, pale as paper lanterns, all of them facing the same direction.

Facing me.

"They won't turn us in," Freya said softly. "Nothing in this city will, anymore. Come on. We have until dawn, and dawn brings fire."
