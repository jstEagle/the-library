## 12. Root and Stock

Maren's house smelled of ash and old paper.

It sat at the end of a lane that the city had forgotten to pave, behind a wall of blackthorn gone wild, and it leaned like a drunk against its own chimney. Theo stood in the doorway while Freya knocked — three sharp raps, then two soft ones, then one more that seemed less like a signal than an apology. He was still shaking from the garden, from the chase, from the memory of every plant in that overgrown wilderness turning toward him as he passed like faces toward a candle.

He had not told her yet how much it frightened him.

The door opened on a chain, and one eye looked out — pale, watery, unimpressed.

"Freya Sandoval," said the eye.

"Maren."

"You brought a hunter's problem to my door."

"I brought *him*," Freya said. "Open up."

The chain rattled, the door swung wide, and Maren stood there: a small woman wrapped in a shawl the color of dried blood, her white hair cropped close as a monk's, her hands stained to the wrist with something dark. Ink, Theo thought at first. Then he saw the soil under her nails and revised his guess to both.

She looked him over the way a horse-trader looks over a lame colt. "So," she said. "The root-stock comes home."

"My name is Theo."

"That remains to be seen." She stepped aside. "In. Quickly. The walls of Vharen have more ears than a field of corn."

* * *

Inside, the room was a single chamber crowded with books — not shelves of them but *drifts*, towers and landslides of them, some stacked spine-up, some open and face-down as if their owners had simply died mid-sentence a hundred times over. A cold hearth. Two chairs. A table bearing a single candle burned down to a stub, and beside it a clay pot in which a rose cutting struggled to live in a fistful of poor grey dirt.

Theo stared at the cutting before he could stop himself.

"It won't take," Maren said, watching him watch it. "Nothing grows for me anymore. Hasn't since the silence." She lowered herself into one chair with the care of someone whose bones had opinions. "Sit down, boy. You look like a man about to be told which disease he has."

"Maybe because I am."

"Good. You're quicker than most of the frauds they've pulled off the streets." She turned to Freya. "And you. You know what I'm going to show him?"

"I know what you showed me last night. I still don't understand all of it."

"No one understands all of it. That's what understanding is." Maren reached into the drift of books beside her chair and drew out a volume with no title on its spine — just a symbol pressed into the leather, worn nearly flat: two hands cupping a single seed.

Freya went very still. Theo saw it — saw her hand drift toward the token at her throat, an old coin or medallion she wore on a cord, and stop halfway, as if the air near it had thickened.

"You recognize that," Theo said to her.

"I recognize it," she said quietly, "from my oath. From the day I swore it, fifteen years ago, when my god still—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "Later. Let him hear it first. It should come from the book."

Maren opened the volume across her knees. The pages were ragged-edged, hand-copied, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood. "This is the final chapter of the Vharen Codex," she said. "Not the one they read aloud at executions. The one the Council suppressed a century ago and hunted every copy of but mine." She looked up at Theo, and her pale eyes were suddenly not watery at all. "You want to know who you are? Then listen to who your family has been."

She began to read.

"'When the god of growing things walked openly among the cities of men, It chose for Its hands a people. Not priests — the god had no use for prayers. Gardeners. Those who could hold living green in one realm and dead stone in the other without either dying. Through them ran the covenant, as water runs through roots: unseen, unbroken, feeding everything.'"

Her voice dropped into a rhythm, half recitation, half lullaby, and Theo felt the words land somewhere below his ribs.

"'But the city grew proud and hungry, and it bound the god beneath the earth and built its law upon the binding. And the god's gardeners did not flee. They stayed. They married. They buried their dead in the god's soil and raised their children on it, generation upon generation, so that the covenant would never fully die — so there would always be a stock from which the Bridge might grow.'"

Maren turned the page. The next passage was written larger, the letters pressed deep into the paper by a hand that had been shaking or weeping or both.

"'Mark the stock by these signs: born in the shadow of the garden; a birthmark like a leaf-vein upon the left forearm; a way with living things that is no craft but kinship; grief that greens what it touches. When such a one stands upon the god's ground at the turning of the age, the covenant shall find its Bridge, and the god shall rise through him, and the walls between the realms shall stand open or be broken forever.'"

The room was silent. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past, and the blackthorn scraped the window.

"Theo," Freya said softly. "Your arm."

He already knew. He had known since the cells, since the dying old woman with her cracked voice and her word — *root-stock* — since the garden, since every houseplant he'd ever coaxed back from the edge of death with nothing but attention and something he'd always assumed was patience. But knowing it in the marrow was different from hearing it read aloud by an archivist of ash in a leaning house full of dead men's books.

He pushed up his sleeve. There it was: the birthmark on his left forearm, the branching lines like the veins of a leaf, the mark his mother used to kiss and call his *lucky tree*, the mark he'd stopped mentioning to anyone by the time he was ten because strangers asked too many questions.

"Born in the shadow of the garden," Maren said, ticking items off on ink-stained fingers. "Cobbler's Row, yes? Three streets east of the municipal garden's wall. A way with living things — I'm told your allotment roses were famous before you ever touched the abandoned garden proper. Grief that greens—" she paused, and her voice gentled by a degree, "—I'm told about your father, boy. I'm told the year he died, everything you planted came up twice as tall."

"How do you know any of that?" His voice came out wrong. Thin.

"Because my order kept records. Because the gardeners' families were watched for a hundred years, and when the covenant broke, we went underground and kept watching anyway. Because someone had to remember what the Council made illegal to remember." She closed the book. "You weren't chosen last month, Theo. You were chosen a hundred years ago. Your great-great-grandparents were chosen. Every marriage in your line was steered — gently, invisibly, by grandmothers who knew and fathers who didn't — toward this. Toward *you*."

* * *

The rage arrived slowly, the way frost arrives — imperceptible, total.

At first Theo just sat there, looking at his own forearm, at the leaf-veined mark he'd carried his whole life like a freckle, and thought: *my mother kissed this.* And beneath that thought, quieter, colder: *did she know? Did she sit me in her lap in the shade of the garden wall because the book said to, or because she loved me?*

Both, probably. That was the trap of it. Both things could be true at once, and neither canceled the other, and there was no version of his childhood left intact where someone hadn't been following instructions written before his grandparents were born.

"Say it again," he said. His voice was steady, which surprised him. "The part about being steered."

"Theo—" Freya started.

"No. Say it again. I want to make sure I heard it."

Maren met his eyes without flinching. He gave her that much; she didn't soften it. "Every marriage in your line was steered toward producing a viable Bridge. Your line was tended like a crop, boy. Pruned. Grafted. Kept pure enough to root and strong enough to bear. You are not the first fruit of that tending, but you are the ripest."

"A crop." He laughed, and the laugh came out ugly. "That's what I am. Not a man. A *harvest*."

"You're angry at the wrong century," Maren said. "The people who steered your line are dust. They did it to save the covenant, and whether they were right—"

"They didn't ask!" He was on his feet now. He didn't remember standing. The candle flame bent away from him, which was impossible, which he would think about later, which right now meant nothing at all. "Nobody asked! Not my grandmother, not my mother, not your book, not your *god*! A hundred years of grandmothers making matches and keeping secrets and pointing the bloodline like a tool, and nobody once said to anybody, *perhaps the child might want a say in what he's for!*"

His voice cracked on the last word. He hated that. He hated all of it — the book, the mark, the garden that had turned to face him, the witch-hunters who'd dragged him through the streets because of words written before he existed, the prophecy that had picked him up like weather picks up a leaf.

"Do you know what I wanted?" he said, and now the steadiness was gone entirely, and he didn't care. "I wanted to grow things. That's all. I wanted a bed of roses and a woman who laughs at the right moments and a life small enough to fit inside itself. I applied for the municipal garden grant because the paperwork took four months and during those four months I got to imagine being happy. That was the whole ambition. And it turns out even *that* wasn't mine — even the garden chose me before I chose it. Even my love of growing things is a *symptom*."

"Theo." Freya was beside him now. She didn't touch him, not yet. "Your love of growing things is yours. I've seen a hundred gardeners. None of them made cut flowers lean."

"Then it's worse," he said. "If even the parts of me I love best are designed—"

"*No.*"

The word came from Maren, and it cracked across the room like a whip, and the candle guttered. The old woman had risen from her chair — slowly, wincing, but risen — and she stood there gripping the armrest, her pale eyes blazing with something that had clearly waited a long time for permission to burn.

"Listen to me, boy, because I have buried everyone who ever knew this, and I will say it once. Yes. You were bred. Yes. You were aimed at this like an arrow before you drew breath. And here is what your rage is too young to see:" she jabbed a dark-stained finger at the book. "Read the prophecy again. Read the *end* of it. The covenant does not find its Bridge by force. It cannot. The binding works only through consent — that is the oldest law of the growing arts, older than the god, older than the realms: *nothing rooted against its will takes hold.* Your grandmothers could aim the arrow, but they could not loose it. The god can offer, but It cannot take. The Council fears you because they believe you'll be compelled. They are fools. If you stand on that ground tomorrow and refuse — if you turn your back and walk away and grow roses until you die an ordinary old man — the prophecy dies with you, and no power in either realm can make you the Bridge. *None.*"

The room rang with it. Theo stood there breathing hard, the fury draining out of him not because it was answered but because it had nowhere left to stand.

"So it's all waiting on me," he said finally. "A hundred years of breeding, a century of hiding, a god starving under the ground, the whole rotten machine — and it stops or starts depending on whether I say yes."

"Now you understand what the Council actually fears," Maren said. "Not a demon. A choice."

* * *

Afterward — he didn't know how long after; the candle had lost another finger's width — Theo found himself standing at the window, looking out at the blackthorn, holding his marked forearm with his other hand like a wound.

Freya came to stand beside him. In the smudged glass their reflections overlapped.

"I keep thinking about my mother," he said. "Whether she knew. Whether the kisses were—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't know how to be angry at her and miss her at the same time. Nobody taught me that."

"You learn it," Freya said. "It's called being from Vharen."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then: "Freya. If I say no. If I walk away. What happens to your god? The one under the garden — the one your oath belonged to. It stays down there. Starving. Forever. Because of me."

"Yes."

"And if I say yes, whatever I am now — the cobbler's-row gardener, the man who buys cut flowers for no reason — how much of him survives it?"

She was quiet a moment. "I don't know," she said. "I'd be lying if I promised you I did. But I know this much, and I'd stake my oath on it, what's left of it: the thing in the cells called you *root-stock*. Root-stock isn't the fruit, Theo. Ask any gardener. Root-stock is the *living part* — the part that decides what the grafted branch becomes. Everything else gets grafted onto you. Not the other way around."

He turned that over. Root-stock. The old woman's word. The gardener's word. The word that meant the part that endured.

Outside, far off toward the heart of the city, a bell began to ring — not the hour, but the alarm pattern, fast and repeating, the sound Vharen made when the gates were closing. Freya stiffened. Down the lane, lamplighters were running instead of walking, and somewhere a crier's voice rose, thin and urgent:

*"By decree of the High Hunter — the municipal garden is condemned — all persons sheltering the fugitives will answer to the law—"*

Maren rose from her chair, already pulling the Codex from its place in the drifts, already wrapping it in oilcloth with the speed of a woman who had packed to flee a hundred times before.

"They're going to burn the garden," Freya said quietly. "They're going to burn it at dawn."

Theo looked down at his forearm, at the leaf-veined mark, and felt — beneath the fear, beneath the rage, beneath everything — the faintest pull, like a root feeling for water.

Dawn was coming either way.
