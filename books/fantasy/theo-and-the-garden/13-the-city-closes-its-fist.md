## 13. The City Closes Its Fist

By the time we reached the tannery district, the bells had started.

Not the market bells, not the watch-bells that rang for fires or floods. This was the *Doomsman's Peal*, rung from the Hall of Judgment only twice in my lifetime — once for the plague year, once when the old Margrave died — and it had a sound like a fist closing. Each stroke landed in the chest before it reached the ear. Theo stumbled on a loose cobble and I caught his arm, and we stood in the mouth of an alley between a chandler's shop and a shuttered dye-house, listening to the city name us both.

"They know," he said. His voice was very quiet. "They know where we went."

"They knew where we'd go before we did." I pressed my back to the damp brick and watched the street. "Maren's cellar was the first place any hunter with a memory would look. I told you that."

"You told me," Theo agreed. He had ash from Maren's burned archive still ground into the creases of his knuckles, and I could not stop looking at it. "I just didn't believe it would matter this fast."

Below the alley's mouth, the street was emptying the way streets do when they are afraid — not all at once but in a slow tide, stall-covers coming down, shutters folding, mothers calling children by names pitched to carry over the bells. And through the middle of it walked the hunters. Not patrols. A procession. Twenty of them in their gray-and-iron, moving with the unhurried certainty of men who no longer need to hurry because the whole city has been made guilty in advance, and at their head —

At their head rode the High Hunter.

I had seen Corvus Ashe maybe four times in fifteen years, always at distance, always at ceremonies where oath-sworn mercenaries like me stood in ranks along the walls while he took some new pledge of vigilance. Up close, even at forty feet across an emptied street, he was worse than his reputation. He rode a gray mare the color of dead sky, and he sat her the way a man sits who has never once doubted that everything beneath him belongs there. He was not old. That was the thing I'd forgotten. He was barely fifty, broad through the shoulders, with a face like something carved by a sculptor who had been asked for authority and given nothing else to work with. No cruelty in it. Cruelty would have been easier to hate.

He stopped his horse at the well-head in the square below us and let the last bell-stroke die, and then he spoke without raising his voice, and every soul in the district heard him anyway. I have heard hedge-witches do that trick. I have done it myself, once, with a charm bought off a river-priestess. But this was not magic. This was what happens when a city has spent one hundred years learning to listen for condemnation: it hears it even in a whisper.

"Citizens of Vharen." The word *citizens* did work in his mouth; it made each listener individually addressed. "Three nights ago, a man named Theo Alderbrand entered this city's garden under cover of dark, in company with a sworn mercenary of this city, and committed acts of sorcery against the peace of Vharen."

Beside me, Theo breathed out slowly through his nose. "*Alderbrand,*" he murmured. "That's not even — my mother's surname was Hale."

"They don't care what your name is," I said. "They care that it sounds like something they can print."

"The Codex recovered from the premises of the archivist Maren Ostrand," Ashe continued, and my stomach dropped, because so much of Maren's library had gone into the fire with her cellar that I had hoped — foolishly, hopelessly — that the hunters had left with nothing but ash, "identifies this man as the figure prophesied under the title of the Bridge: the vessel through which an imprisoned power intends to return itself to the world. You have heard the old stories. You have sung the songs your grandmothers should not have taught you. I am here to tell you what those songs omit."

He paused. He was good at pausing. Somewhere in the crowd — thinning now, but still thirty strong, because fear is also a kind of theater and people cannot look away — someone coughed.

"There is no god beneath the garden," Corvus Ashe said. "There is a demon. There is a hunger older than the covenant, older than the city, which our founders bound at terrible cost so that their children might live unafraid. For a century it has starved behind the seal, and for a century Vharen has prospered in that starvation's shadow. Now it has found a door. It has found a face. It wears the face of a gardener because a gardener's face is one you will trust." His eyes lifted, unhurried, and swept the upper windows of the street as though reading them. As though they might read mine. "It wears the face of love, because love is how hunger gets invited inside."

Theo's hand found mine in the dark of the alley. His palm was cold.

"At dawn tomorrow," said the High Hunter, "the municipal garden will be burned to its roots, and the ground beneath it salted, and the door it conceals destroyed forever. Any citizen found sheltering the fugitives — the demon's vessel, or the mercenary Freya Sandoval, oath-breaker —" and here his voice changed, just slightly, just enough, and I understood that the whole speech had been built toward this single sentence like a wall built toward a gate, "— will share their fate. There is no neutrality anymore. Neutrality is the door they walk through."

The procession moved on. The bell began again, slower now, tolling rather than pealing, counting down hours none of us could see.

* * *

We did not go back for the boat. We did not go anywhere near the river gates, because within an hour of the Doomsman's Peal the river gates would be chained, and I knew it the way I knew which cobblestones were loose on Wren Street after fourteen years of walking them. What we needed was a hole. A small one. A hole with a person in it who owed me something.

The problem was that everyone in Vharn who owed me something owed me for charms. Luck-charms. Headache-charms. Charms against nightmares, against colic, against the landlord's inspection. Small miracles, sold small, and I had spent fifteen years telling myself that this was nothing — that a dried rosemary bundle tied with a knot I'd whispered over was commerce, not covenant, that I had kept my oath's letter if not its ghost. I had believed, most days, that nobody in this city remembered I had ever been anything more than the woman at the third stall on Tallow Row who could make your bread rise and your bad knee behave.

I was about to learn what fifteen years of small mercies actually weighed.

"The cooper's on Copper Lane," I said, as the bells tolled on. "Dagny Holt. Her son had night-terrors for two years and I gave her a sleep-knot that worked when the physicians didn't. She owes—"

"She doesn't owe you anything," Theo said. Not unkindly. "You sold her a charm. Fair price, fair trade."

"I charged her half price and told her it was full."

Theo looked at me for a long moment. In the alley-dark, with the bells falling on us like slow rain, he smiled — a small, tired, entirely real smile, the same one he'd worn the day I bought flowers I didn't need from a garden everyone said was dead. "Then she owes you," he said. "Lead on."

* * *

Dagny Holt answered her door on the second knock with a carving knife held low and competent, saw my face, and went the color of tallow.

"Saints' bones," she whispered. "Get *inside*."

Inside was warm and smelled of oak shavings and onions. Her husband was not home — at the guildhall, she said, being counted, because the Peal meant a census, and the census meant lists, and lists meant the hunters would know by morning exactly who lived where and who was missing from where. She bolted the door behind us, put the knife down, picked it up again out of habit, set it down for good, and then turned around and looked at Theo the way people look at a storm front.

"That's him," she said. "That's the gardener. It's all over the square criers already — the demon with the gardener's face." Her eyes flicked to me. "You're the mercenary. They said your name too. Freya Sandoval, oath-breaker." And then Dagny Holt, a barrel-chested woman of sixty with flour in the creases of her apron, did the thing that undid me more thoroughly than any hunter's speech that day: she snorted. "Oath-breaker. Fifteen years that woman sold me charms for half price and called it full. If that's breaking an oath, I'd like to see her keep one."

"Dagny—" I started.

"Hush. You'll want the back room and you'll want it tonight and you'll take my sons' beds because they're sleeping at their aunt's since the Peal started and I don't trust the roof-beams anyway." She was already moving, banking the fire, pulling bread from the cold-box, laying out cheese with the brisk economy of a woman feeding soldiers rather than criminals. "And you—" she pointed the bread knife at Theo, who straightened as though at attention, "—will eat. Demons eat souls, do they? Well, mine's been wanting feeding for years. Sit."

We sat. And it was there, over barley bread and hard cheese in the kitchen of a cooper's wife on Copper Lane, that I understood the shape of what was happening to my city.

Because it did not stop with Dagny.

Before the last bell had finished tolling, there came a scratching at the kitchen's back window — three taps, pause, two taps — a pattern so old I had to dig fifteen years down to recognize it, and when I did my throat closed. It was the signal the old hedge-practitioners used, the ones who'd kept tiny illegal devotions alive in the covenant's shadow: *friend at the door, friend in trouble.* Dagny opened the shutter on the chain, and outside in the yard stood a boy of perhaps twelve, breathless, sent — he reported, gulping — by his grandmother, Widow Ferrow, who had this message for the charm-woman of Tallow Row: *the tanner's loft on Bellows Street is open, and the loft ladder is down, and there is straw enough, and tell her the widow has not forgotten the winter her granddaughter coughed.*

And an hour after that, a knock at the front door, proper and polite, and Dagny answered it with the carving knife behind her back, and it was the chandler's apprentice from two streets over, a girl with ink-stained fingers, who had been sent by the chandler himself, Master Ilbin, who wished it known that his cart would be making a delivery of tallow to Bellows Street at dawn, and if certain persons needed to be *inside* certain barrels during that delivery, well, the chandler was a man of precise measurements and generous capacity.

I stood in that warm kitchen holding a cup of nettle tea I could not drink and understood, finally and completely, what I had been doing for fifteen years.

I had thought I was surviving. Selling trinkets, keeping my head down, honoring a dead oath by living small. I had thought the charms were nothing — knots and herbs and whispers, placebos for the anxious, pocket-money for a fallen priestess. But every half-priced luck-knot had been a thread, and every thread had been tied to a person, and tonight, when the city closed its fist, those threads did not snap. They *held*. All of them, at once, quietly, without drama, without any of the oaths or rituals or grand covenants that were supposed to be the architecture of faith — just a cooper's wife with a knife, a widow's grandson in a cold yard, a chandler measuring barrels for fugitives.

The covenant had not died when my god went silent, I realized. It had gone underground. It had been hiding in kitchens all along, in half prices and remembered winters, waiting for someone to need it.

Theo was watching me from across the table with that expression he had — the one that saw too much for a man who claimed to be ordinary. "You're crying," he said, surprised, gentle.

"I'm not." I was. "It's the onions."

"There are no onions cut yet."

"Then it's preemptive." I laughed, wet and hopeless, and Dagny, passing behind me with more bread, put one broad hand briefly on my shoulder and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of sacrament.

* * *

We did not stay at Dagny's past midnight. She had sons; she had risked enough; and the census-lists would reach the hunters by morning. So at the hour when the bells went quiet — the dead hour, the hour I had once kept vigils in, the hour my god had last spoken and then never spoken again — we crossed six dark streets by routes only I remembered, Theo carrying our whole future in a satchel containing Maren's map, my oath-token, and half a loaf of Dagny's bread, and we climbed the ladder to the tanner's loft on Bellows Street where the straw was deep and the widow Ferrow's promise smelled of hides and lye and safety.

Through the loft's one small window, facing south, we could see the garden.

Even now. Even marked for death. It rose above the rooftops like a green thought the city was trying to suppress, and the moon lay on it, and I watched Theo look at it the way other men look at a burning house their family is trapped inside.

"They're really going to burn it," he said. "All of it. Every tree Maren mapped, every bed, the maze, the roses. A hundred years of growth. Because of me."

"Because of what they're afraid of," I said. "You're just the shape the fear came in."

"Freya." He turned from the window. In the moonlight his birthmark — the root-shaped mark below his collarbone that the dying woman in the cells had kissed and called *root-stock* — was just visible above his shirt's edge, pale as a scar. "When they burn it. When they burn the garden and salt the ground. Will that hurt him? The god? Whatever's down there behind the door?"

I wanted to lie. I opened my mouth to lie. Fifteen years of practice at small comforts, at the merciful half-truth, and I found — looking at him — that I couldn't. That was what he'd done to me. That was the first miracle he'd ever worked, and he'd worked it without a single charm: he had made lying feel impossible.

"Yes," I said. "I think it would hurt him. I think it might kill what's left."

Theo nodded slowly. Then he looked back at the garden, and his jaw set, and I saw — clearly, unmistakably, the way you see weather change — the moment when a frightened man becomes a decided one.

"Then dawn is too late," he said, "to start figuring out how to get down there."

* * *

Sleep would not come, so I kept the watch, sitting in the straw with my back against the slanted roof-beams while Theo finally drifted off beside me, his breathing evening out, his hand curled loosely around mine as though even asleep he refused to be separated from the only solid thing left in his world. Below us, Bellows Street slept badly. Above us, the moon moved. Somewhere across the city, in the Hall of Judgment, Corvus Ashe was writing orders, and carts were being loaded with pitch and oil, and the garden that had fed half the poor districts cuttings and greens for a hundred years was being measured for its own pyre.

And somewhere — closer than the Hall, closer than the pyre-carts, down through clay and root and a century of silence — something small and starving felt the hunters' preparations like hands pressing on a bruise, and could not cry out, and waited.

I got out my oath-token.

It lay in my palm, dull silver, cool as a coin from a drowned country: the seven-branched sprig, the circle, the word worn nearly smooth. *Keeper of the growing dark, tender of the threshold.* I had carried it through fifteen years of gray skies and illegal knots and careful, cowardly, half-price kindnesses, and I had never once known why I couldn't throw it in the river.

Now I knew. It wasn't a relic. It was a key, and keys do not grieve; they wait.

The loft floor creaked. I looked up, heart hammering — and it was only the chandler's apprentice, the ink-fingered girl, climbing the ladder on some errand of the widow's network I hadn't been told about. She stopped at the top, saw me awake with the token in my hand, and her eyes went wide.

"You're her," she whispered. "You're the priestess."

"I'm not," I said automatically. "I'm a charm-seller. I'm nobody."

The girl looked at me the way Dagny had, the way the widow's grandson had, the way the whole hidden city had looked at me since the bells began — and she shook her head, slowly, with the absolute confidence of the young.

"That's not what everybody downstairs says," she said.

She came the rest of the way up into the moonlight, and behind her, up the ladder, hand over hand, silent as gardeners, came three more of them.
