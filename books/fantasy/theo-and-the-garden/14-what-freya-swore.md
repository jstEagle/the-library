## 14. What Freya Swore

Maren's house smelled of dust and lamp oil and something older underneath it all — a smell Theo couldn't name until he realized it was the smell of the garden at night, of turned earth and green things breathing in the dark. The old priestess had gone out at dusk to buy bread and gossip, leaving instructions that amounted to *don't burn anything down*, and for the first hour Theo had obeyed them faithfully.

The second hour, boredom won.

He hadn't meant to snoop. That was what he told himself, standing in the narrow room off Maren's kitchen where she kept her papers — the defrocked archives, shelves of ledgers with spines cracked like old knuckles, bundles of letters tied with string gone soft as cobweb. He'd only come in to look for the map. Maren had promised to draw them a way beneath the garden, a way past the hunters' cordon, and he'd thought perhaps she'd left an earlier draft somewhere among the clutter, something he could study while she was out.

Instead, on the corner of her desk, half-buried under a ledger of confiscated goods from forty years ago, he found a wooden box no bigger than his two hands cupped together.

It wasn't locked. It wasn't even closed properly; the lid sat crooked on its pegs, as if someone opened it often and never quite bothered to shut it right. Theo knew better than to touch other people's things — his mother had raised him on that rule alongside the rules about thinning carrots and not naming the hens you intended to eat — but his fingers were already lifting the lid before the better part of him could object.

Inside, on a bed of faded blue cloth, lay a coin.

Not a coin. He saw that at once. It was cast in the shape of one, round and palm-worn smooth, but it bore no currency mark, no face of any magistrate or king. Instead there was a tree — a single tree, rendered in shallow relief so worn by thumbs and years that its branches had nearly melted into the sky around it. Its roots spread below in the same shallow carving, and the roots and branches met at the edges of the token, closing into a circle. A tree that held up its own sky. Underneath, in lettering so small he had to tilt it toward the window light to read it: *I KEEP.*

Theo turned it over. The back said nothing at all — just a blank field, polished bright in one small spot, as if it had been pressed against something for years. A heartbeat, maybe. Or a scar.

He knew whose it was.

There was no reason to know that. No reason except that he had watched Freya's hands for weeks now — the way a gardener watches hands, reading habit the way farmers read weather — and he knew every object those hands touched. Her knife. Her dice. The chipped blue mug she refused to replace. And once, only once, late at night when she thought he slept, he had heard a small hard sound against her sternum, a tap of metal or bone, when she pressed her fist to her own chest the way people press a wound.

*Freya Sandoval keeps something under her shirt,* he had thought, and being a decent man, he had not asked.

Now it lay in his palm, warm from the box, and the warmth was wrong. Things in dusty rooms should be cool. This felt like it had been carried against skin very recently, very often, for fifteen years.

"Put it back."

He spun. Maren stood in the doorway, bread under her arm, her white brows drawn down — but not in anger, he realized. In something more tired than anger.

"I'm sorry," Theo said. "I was looking for the map, and then—"

"I know what you found." She set the bread down and came toward him, and she did not snatch the token away. She looked at it the way one looks at a photograph of the dead. "That's hers. She doesn't know I have it. She thinks she lost it the night the sky went gray." Maren held out her hand, palm up, and after a moment Theo laid the token in it. "She came to me that night, soaked through, half-mad, asking if I'd seen it. Fifteen years ago. She'd torn her whole room apart looking."

"Then how—"

"She didn't lose it." Maren closed her fingers over the token gently, the way one closes a dead bird's wings. "She tried to throw it in the river. Into the Weep, from the Tanners' Bridge. And the river gave it back."

* * *

They sat in Maren's kitchen while she told it, and Theo did not touch the tea she poured him because his hands had begun, faintly, to shake.

"You understand what an oath-token is," Maren said. "Or you don't, being a gardener and a law-abiding one, praise be to nobody. So listen. Before the ban, before the gray sky, there were orders. Sworn companies, each bound to a patron power — gods, if you like that word, and most of us did. A mercenary took an oath, and the oath-token was the seal of it. Not jewelry. Not a badge. A *living link*. You kept it against your heart and your god could reach you through it — strength when you needed it, warnings, sometimes words. And you reached back. Prayer, we called it, though it was less like church and more like holding someone's hand."

"And Freya's order—" Theo began.

"The Gardeners' Vigil," Maren said, and smiled without any humor in it at all. "Least glamorous company that ever swore. While other orders sold steel and storm, the Vigil kept growing-arts. Healing herbs that actually healed. Orchards that fruited in drought. They were sworn to the quietest god in Vharen — no thunder, no glory. Just root and stock, seed and season. The god under the hill. The god in the green."

Theo's mouth had gone dry. "The god in the garden."

"The municipal garden," Maren said, "was not always municipal. It was the god's body, boy. The oldest ground in this city, older than the walls, older than the Hall. Every rose in it grew from stock the first gardeners planted with their own bleeding hands. And Freya Sandoval was sworn to it — the last of the Vigil still keeping faith after the others drifted off or died or took the city's pardon. Fifteen years ago she was the last living oath-sworn servant of the god beneath that ground."

"What happened?" he asked, though some part of him already knew, had known since the cells, since the dying woman's cracked whisper — *root-stock* — had lodged under his skin like a splinter. "What happened fifteen years ago?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then she got up, went to the shelf of confiscated-goods ledgers, and drew out the one from the year the sky went gray. She opened it to a page near the end and turned it toward him.

The entry was brief, written in a clerk's cramped hand:

*Item: one (1) covenant-seal, recovered from the person of the prisoner, designation GARDENER'S KING / THE QUIET ONE. Interred with prisoner per Council decree 44-C. Site sealed. Access restricted to High Office.*

"They didn't just outlaw magic," Maren said quietly. "Anyone can pass a law. Laws die when the magistrates who wrote them die. No — they did something worse. The city had been leasing the god's power for decades by then, did you know that? The Hall's wards, the harbor lights, the plagues they turned aside. All of it drawn from the ground under the garden, siphoned through the old Vigil rites, paid for with nothing. The Council decided leasing wasn't enough. They wanted ownership. So they took the god's seal — the thing that let it speak to its servants and its servants speak to it — and they buried it with the god itself, walled up behind a door under the oldest rose tree, and they starved the link shut. Every oath in Vharen went silent on the same night. Do you remember it? Of course you don't. You'd have been a child."

"I remember the sky," Theo said. His voice came out strange, distant, as if someone else were using it. "My mother said the light changed overnight. Everything gray ever since. Everyone blamed the ban. They said the gods abandoned us because we were wicked, or because magic rots the world—"

"The sky went gray because the god went dark," Maren said. "Its light is in everything that grows, boy. When they walled it up, the color drained out of the world like blood from a cut throat. And every sworn servant in Vharen woke that night to a token gone cold and a prayer falling into silence. Most of them raged. Some killed themselves. Some took the city's gold and became witch-hunters, hunting the very arts they'd served." She set the ledger down. "And one of them tore her room apart looking for a coin the river wouldn't let her throw away, and then spent fifteen years selling harmless charms and pretending she'd never believed in anything."

* * *

He found her on the roof.

Maren's house was low, and its flat roof overlooked a slice of the gray city and, beyond the chimneys, the dark mass of the garden — the garden where hunters walked the rows now with lanterns and lists, cataloguing what would burn at dawn. Freya sat against the parapet with her knees drawn up, watching it, and she didn't startle when he climbed up beside her. She must have heard him on the stairs. She heard everything. That was the mercenary in her, and he understood now that the mercenary was only the shape her grief had learned to wear.

"Maren showed me the ledger," he said.

Freya didn't move. "Of course she did."

"The token. The Gardeners' Vigil. The god under the garden." He paused. "Your god, Freya. The one you pray to every night even though it's illegal, even though it's dead — it isn't dead. It's *imprisoned*. It's been imprisoned the whole time, and you've been—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and quick, a blade drawn partway. Then, quieter: "Please don't."

But he was angry, suddenly, and the anger surprised him with its heat. "Fifteen years," he said. "You let me think you were neutral. You let the *whole city* think you were neutral — a charm-seller, a coward, a woman who kept her head down and her hands clean. You stood in that cell block hallway and hid while they dragged me out, and you told me later you hated yourself for it, and I forgave you, because I thought you were protecting yourself the only way you knew how. But you weren't protecting yourself. You were sitting vigil. You've been sitting vigil for fifteen years at the bedside of something everyone else pronounced dead, because you're the only one in this entire cursed city stubborn enough to keep faith with a prisoner!"

"Stop it." Her voice cracked on the second word.

"*You never broke your oath.*"

"I DID!" 

She was on her feet. In the gray light from the city she looked younger and older at once, stripped of the market-day competence, the easy banter, all the armor she wore so well he'd mistaken it for skin.

"You think I don't know what I am?" she said. "I'm the servant who stopped listening. I'm the one who survived. Do you know what happened that night, Theo? The night the sky went gray? I was on contract in the eastern orchards. My token went cold between one heartbeat and the next — cold as river stone — and I screamed my god's name until my voice broke, and nothing answered. Nothing. And every other sworn soldier in Vharen did exactly what I did: assumed the worst, grieved, and moved on. Except they *moved on*, and I—" Her breath hitched. "I couldn't. I told myself it was loyalty. I told myself the Vigil doesn't abandon post. But I know what it really was. I was afraid to find out the truth. Afraid it had abandoned *me*. So I kept the token against my heart and I never once went looking for answers, because as long as I didn't look, my god could still be alive somewhere, silent but alive, and I could go on being faithful instead of being *fooled*." She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "Fifteen years of neutrality, Theo. You want to call it vigil. I call it cowardice wearing mourning clothes. Both things are true. Maybe all of them are true."

"It wasn't abandonment," Theo said softly. "Maren showed me the record. They buried the seal with it. Your god didn't stop answering, Freya. It couldn't. They locked its voice behind a door and starved it for a century, and you — you were the only one who never stopped knocking."

That undid her.

He had seen her fight — brief, brutal, efficient, in the hedgerows, and he carried the memory of the hunter going down with a weight he'd never fully put down. He had seen her afraid, in the apartment, the night the hunters knocked. But he had never seen her break, and she broke now, quietly, terribly, folding down onto the parapet with both fists pressed to her sternum — pressing the place where the token used to hang — and the sound she made was not crying. It was smaller than crying. It was the sound of someone setting down a weight they'd carried so long their spine had grown around it.

He knelt in front of her and took her hands. They were cold. Her knuckles were scarred, her palms callused in the patterns of sword and coin and, he noticed now for the first time, of soil — old calluses, deep ones, from years she never talked about, years she'd knelt in the Vigil's gardens before the world went gray.

"Fifteen years," she whispered, "I thought it left me."

"It never left you. You're the last thread it has to the world. Don't you see what that means?" His own voice shook now. "The prophecy needs a Bridge. I'm the stock, fine — whatever Maren says I am, whatever my blood is. But a bridge needs two ends. It needs someone on *this* side who knows the way, who knows the rites, who kept the faith when faith was illegal. Freya — it's you. It was always going to be you. You're not the coward of this story. You're the door."

She looked at him then, eyes wet and fierce, and for a moment he thought she'd argue. He almost wanted her to. Arguing was a kind of armor too, and he'd have let her wear it.

Instead she said, "If we do this — if we go under that garden tomorrow and open that door — and my god looks at me after fifteen years of silence, what do I even say to it?"

Theo smiled, and it hurt, and it was the truest smile he owned. "You say *I kept.*"

Her breath caught. "How did you—"

"The back of the token," he said. "It's worn smooth in one spot. From being held. Someone held that oath against their heart every day for fifteen years, Freya. Whatever words are carved on the front — *I keep* — you already answered them. You answered them every single day. You just never knew anyone was counting."

For a long time neither of them spoke. Below them the city murmured its gray evening murmur, and beyond the chimneys the garden stood black and waiting, full of hunters and lanterns and one very old rose tree.

Then Freya pulled the collar of her shirt aside, and he saw it — the pale groove worn into her skin by a cord long since hidden, the spot where a coin-shaped piece of wood had rested against her heartbeat for half her life. She touched it once, lightly, like touching a scar.

"When this is over," she said, "if we live — I want it back. Not to wear. To bury. Properly. In the ground it belongs to."

"We'll plant something over it," Theo said. "Something that blooms."

Something moved in the street below — a flicker of lantern-light at Maren's gate, wrong somehow, too deliberate for a passerby. Freya's head snapped up, mercenary-fast, and her hand found his wrist.

"That's the third time that light's circled the block," she breathed. "Someone's watching the house. Theo — Maren's map. Wherever that door is under the garden, we need to find it tonight. Because I don't think we're the only ones who figured out what's buried there."
