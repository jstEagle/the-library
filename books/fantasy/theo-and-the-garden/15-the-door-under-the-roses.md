## 15. The Door Under the Roses

*Freya*

Maren's map was not drawn on paper. It was drawn on skin — the inside of the old priestess's forearm, inked in a brown that had gone silver with age, and she had pressed Freya's hand against it and said, *Memorize it. I'm not giving you anything they can cut off me.*

So Freya memorized it. She had been trained for this, once: the way a mercenary memorizes a client's face, an escape route, the weight of a coin purse. The garden rendered as a body. The hedge-maze as ribs. The rose terrace as a spine, curving down toward the oldest planting, marked with a symbol she hadn't seen in fifteen years and had never stopped seeing when she closed her eyes.

Her god's mark. The seed-and-circle. The sign of the covenant.

She stood now at the spine's end, in the blue hour before dawn, and looked at the oldest rose tree in Vharen.

It did not look like much. That was the first thing. After everything — Maren's revelations, the cells, the chase through the hedgerows, the hunter's blood under her fingernails that still hadn't washed out entirely — after all of it, the door to a god's prison was a gnarled, half-dead rose bush leaning against a crumbling wall, its canes twisted into knots like arthritic fingers. The blooms had gone leggy and pale. Weeds crowded its base. It looked like something the city had meant to tear out decades ago and simply forgotten.

"They let it live," Theo said quietly beside her. "Out of laziness."

"Or fear," Freya said. "Cities forget things they love. They never forget things they're afraid of."

The garden around them breathed. Even now, even hunted, there was a strange peace to it at this hour — the hedges black against the graying sky, dew smoking off the grass, somewhere a nightbird working through its last few phrases before sleep. Above the wall, Vharen was waking. She could hear the market bells, the early carts. In a few hours, by the High Hunter's decree, fire crews would assemble at the gates. By noon, if they failed tonight, all of this would be ash and the god beneath it would be cinders.

Theo crouched at the rose tree's base. He didn't touch it, not yet. He just looked, the way he looked at everything he grew — with a patience that seemed to Freya almost devotional, though he'd have laughed at the word. His sleeves were pushed up. The birthmark on his inner wrist — the one the dying woman in the cells had kissed, calling him root-stock, root-stock — caught the light like a stain of dark wine.

"It's dying," he said. "Slowly. Look at the graft line." He pointed to a swollen ridge near the soil where two woods met, one old and failing, one newer, stubborn, alive. "Someone grafted new stock onto it. A hundred years ago, maybe. Keeping it going without anyone knowing why."

"Like your family," Freya said.

He glanced up at her. There it was again — that flicker in his face, the grief and the anger and the terrible acceptance all fighting for the same ground. *Descendants of the god's first gardeners.* Maren had said it gently, the way you tell someone their house has burned down. Bred, tended, kept unknowing, generation after generation, so that when the city finally needed a Bridge, the wood would be ready.

"Like my family," he agreed. He exhaled. "Okay. Show me where."

Freya knelt beside him and traced the map in the air from memory. "The roots run east, under the wall. There should be a hollow — Maren said a stone, set flat, with the mark on it. But the map is a hundred years old. The garden has shifted."

"Then ask it," Theo said.

She looked at him.

"The plants," he said, and his voice went careful, the way it always did when he approached the thing he was becoming. "They've been turning toward me since the maze. Maren says it's because of what I am. So — maybe they know where the door is. Maybe they've been keeping it all along."

Freya hesitated. Then she leaned close to the rose tree's pale blooms and did something she had not done in fifteen years: she spoke aloud to a living thing as if it could hear her. Not a charm. Not a transaction. Just words.

"We've come to open it," she whispered. "We've come to bring them home."

For a moment, nothing. The garden held its breath. And then every bloom on the ancient tree turned — not toward Theo, but toward her — a slow, creaking, impossible rotation, like a hundred small faces waking, and the nearest cane lifted from the wall and swept aside the weeds at its base with the deliberate motion of a hand clearing a table.

Beneath the weeds: a flat stone. And carved into it, worn nearly smooth, the seed-and-circle.

Freya's throat closed. Fifteen years of silence, and her god had just moved a rose cane for her.

"Well," Theo managed, "that's either very welcoming or a trap."

"With gods," Freya said, standing, drawing her knife, "it's usually both."

* * *

*Theo*

The stone came up easier than it should have. Roots had grown around it, through it, gripping it like knuckles — and then released, all at once, the moment Freya's blade touched the edge, as if the whole root system had decided to cooperate. Beneath it was a stair. Narrow, cut from dark stone, descending into a cold that came up out of the earth smelling of wet clay and, impossibly, of roses.

They had one torch between them. Freya lit it with flint and steel, refusing — Theo noticed — to try anything else. Her magic, when she'd used it in the cells to bribe the hunter, had worked for the first time in fifteen years, and he understood now why she treated it like something fragile and frightening. It wasn't hers anymore. Or rather, it had never only been hers. It belonged to the silent thing below, and using it was a kind of prayer.

"I'll go first," she said.

"You always go first."

"That's because I'm the one with the knife and the death wish."

"That's because," Theo said, "you still think you have to protect me from what I'm walking into." He kept his voice level. Below them, the stair went down farther than the torchlight reached, and the cold breathed upward like a sleeping animal. "You can't. You know that, right? Whatever's down there — Maren thinks it's been waiting for me for a hundred years. Maybe longer. You can walk beside me, Freya. You can't walk in front."

She looked at him for a long moment. Torchlight moved across her face — the hard mercenary lines of it, and underneath them the tired, grieving woman he'd fallen for over bad coffee and unnecessary flowers. He loved both women. He had never once thought of them as separate.

"Beside," she said finally. "Fine. But if anything lunges, I'm stepping in front anyway and you can be angry about it later."

"That's fair," he said, and took her hand.

They descended together.

The stairs were older than the garden above them; Theo could feel it in the walls, the way he'd learned to feel things lately — some new sense opening in him like an eye, reading stone and soil and root the way he'd once read weather. The masonry down here predated Vharen. It might have predated the idea of Vharen. Grooves ran down the walls on either side, deep channels worn smooth, and when he brushed one with his fingertips it hummed faintly under his touch, like a plucked string still ringing from a song played centuries ago.

"What are these?" he whispered.

"Maren called them veins." Freya's voice was tight. "Channels for the covenant. Before the imprisonment, power flowed between the realms through channels like these — through gardens, rivers, graveyards, anywhere the wall between worlds was thin. The garden above was built on top of this because this was the thinnest place of all." Her hand tightened around his. "When they imprisoned the god, they sealed the veins. Cut the flow. Everything went gray."

Theo remembered the stories. Everyone remembered the stories — the day the sky over Vharen went gray, fifteen years ago. Colors dimming. Charms failing. The long silence that followed, and the witch-hunters rising in the silence like mushrooms after rain. He had been nine years old. He had thought the world was simply getting worse, the way children think weather is simply getting worse. He had not known it was a wound.

The stair ended at a landing, and the landing opened into a chamber, and the chamber took the breath out of him.

It was round, vast, vaulted — a cathedral turned upside down and buried. And it was grown, not built. Every surface was wood: pillars that had once been trees, walls that were woven root, a ceiling of interlaced branches so old they had fused into solid timber. The vein-channels converged here from every direction, running along the floor like rays of a sun, all of them ending at the center of the chamber.

At the center stood a door.

Not a grand one. That was the second thing Theo would remember afterward — how ordinary it looked. A simple door of grey wood, human-sized, iron-banded, standing free in the middle of the chamber with nothing behind it. No wall. Just the door, upright in the converging veins, and beyond its edges only more stone floor, as if it had been placed there as a suggestion, or a question.

Carved into its grain was the seed-and-circle, large enough to cover the whole face of it.

And the door was starving. Theo knew it the way he knew blight on a leaf — instantly, bodily. The wood was desiccated, cracked, drinking at nothing. The iron bands were rusted to lace. Whatever flowed through those veins a century ago no longer flowed, and the door had been holding itself shut — or being held shut — through all that famine, all alone in the dark under the roses.

"Oh," Theo said softly. "Oh, you poor thing."

"Careful—" Freya started.

But he was already moving forward, kneeling at the door's threshold the way he knelt at a struggling plant, and he laid his palm flat against the grey wood.

The chamber rang.

Every vein-channel lit at once — a faint gold thread kindling in each groove, racing inward toward the door, toward his hand — and warmth poured up through his palm into his arm, into his chest, and something behind the door stirred. Not opened. Stirred. Like a sleeper turning toward a voice. Like roots feeling water at the edge of their reach.

*There,* something said, without sound, without language. *There. There. There.*

Theo pulled back, gasping. Freya had her knife up, whirling toward the empty room, finding nothing to fight. "What? What happened?"

"It knows me." His heart was slamming. "Freya — it knows me. It's been down here a hundred years and it felt my hand and it — it sounded *glad*." His eyes stung. He wiped them angrily. "It sounded glad, and it's locked in a box, and I nearly cried touching a *door*—"

"No." Freya's voice had changed. He looked up.

She was staring past him at the door — at the carved circle, which was no longer inert. The seed at its center had begun, very slowly, to glow. And around its rim, where the carving showed a ring broken in two places, the breaks were *moving*. Closing. Not fully — a hairline of darkness remained, a gap the width of a knife-blade — but closing, the way a mouth closes around a word it has waited a century to say.

"It's answering," she whispered. "The covenant isn't dead. It's been waiting for — oh, gods. Oh, gods, it needs *two*."

* * *

*Freya*

Maren had told them, of course. Freya had heard the words and filed them away the way soldiers file away intelligence — accurately, and without believing they applied to her. *The covenant was sworn by two hands,* the old woman had said, tapping the Codex's suppressed final page. *One from each side of the wall. One who holds the oath, one who holds the root. Never one alone. The city forgot that. The city thought it could keep a god the way you keep money in a vault, and that's why the vault became a prison and the prison became a tomb.*

Now, watching the broken ring struggle to close, Freya understood with her whole body what Maren had meant.

She drew the oath-token from beneath her shirt.

Fifteen years she had worn it against her skin, and never shown it to a living soul. A small disc of greenish metal on a cord — bronze that had been dipped in something living, long ago; it had never tarnished, never warmed, never cooled. On its face, the seed-and-circle, fine as engraving. Her god had pressed it into her palm herself, back when her god had palms to press with, back when the covenant lived and a mercenary's oath was a real rope tied between a real person and a real divine thing. *Hold this end,* her god had said, *and I hold mine, and neither of us falls.*

Then the sky went gray. Then the silence. Then fifteen years of selling luck-charms to clerks and telling herself neutrality was wisdom when she knew — she had always known — it was mourning wearing wisdom's coat.

She stepped forward and held the token up to the door.

The glow flared. The gap in the ring narrowed further — and stopped. Trembling. Holding. The whole chamber hummed like a struck bell that would not finish ringing, and Freya felt the token grow warm against her fingers for the first time in fifteen years, warming, *answering*, straining toward the door like a dog at the end of a rope —

— and the last gap would not close.

Because the door needed what the token could not give. The oath was one half. The blood was the other.

"Freya," Theo said quietly. "Look at the lock."

Below the carved ring, where a lock-plate would sit on any mortal door, there was a shallow basin of dark stone, dry as dust. And as she watched, the basin filled — not with water but with a slow seep of golden light, pooling, waiting. Waiting for something to be added. Something given freely, from a body, from a life.

Blood. Of course it was blood. It was always blood; the old rites were never squeamish. But not her blood. She saw that in the same instant she understood everything else, because the light in the basin bent subtly toward Theo, the way all growing things in this garden bent toward him, and the token at her throat sang against her sternum while the door drank his presence like rain.

"One who holds the oath," she said aloud. Her voice shook. "One who holds the root. Freya Sandoval swears, and the Bridge bleeds, and the door opens." She laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it at all. "A hundred years, and the prophecy was never about finding him. It was about finding *us*. It needed someone who'd already lost everything to a false oath, and someone bred to open the way, and it needed us to find each other in a flower stall on a market day like two idiots buying too many flowers."

Theo was smiling. That undid her a little — that he could smile here, in a buried cathedral, facing the dissolution of everything he'd believed himself to be. "I did buy too many flowers," he said. "Best mistake I ever made."

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of earth and root, the morning bells began. The city was waking. In three hours, maybe four, the fire crews would come over the wall with pitch and oil, and the High Hunter would stand on the terrace and preach his sermon to the crowd while the garden that had sheltered them burned.

Freya looked at the trembling gap in the ring. At the basin of waiting light. At the man she loved, rolling up his sleeve, calm as a man preparing to prune a hedge.

"Not yet," she said.

His eyebrows rose. "Freya—"

"Not yet. Listen to me." She grabbed his wrist — the birthmark side — and made him look at her. "Once we do this, there's no version where you stay only you. Maren said it. The Codex says it. You become the Bridge, and the god roots in you, and I don't — I don't know what comes out the other side. I need you to know that I would burn this whole city down myself before I let them take you, and I am *asking* you — not as the Bridge, not as the root-stock, as *Theo*, the man who grows things — are you sure?"

The chamber hummed around them. The door glowed. Somewhere far overhead, the roses leaned on their ancient canes, listening.

Theo covered her hand with his own.

"When I was in the cells," he said, "there was a woman who recognized my birthmark and called me root-stock, and I hated it. I spent weeks hating it — being a thing grown on purpose, a vessel, an instrument. And then I touched this door, and it was *glad* to see me, Freya. Glad. After a hundred years alone in the dark, the first thing it did was say hello." His thumb moved over her knuckles. "Whatever I become — I get to choose it. That's the one thing nobody bred into me and nobody prophesied. So yes. Ask me tomorrow, and I'll say yes. Ask me in ten years, and I'll say yes."

He turned his wrist over, baring the birthmark, and held it over the basin of light.

"But we do it together," he said. "Your token in the ring. My blood in the stone. Both hands. That's how the covenant worked, right?"

Freya's eyes blurred. She lifted the oath-token on its cord, and stepped to the door, and fitted the small green disc into the trembling gap in the carved ring — and it fit, perfectly, the way a word fits at the end of a sentence a century in the saying.

The ring closed. Light flooded the veins. The whole buried cathedral blazed like dawn under the earth, and the door shuddered, and the basin flared bright as it waited for the last gift, and far above them — through earth and root and wall and rose — the sky over Vharen began, very faintly, to change color.

"Now," said the door — or the god behind it, or the covenant itself, in a voice that was not a voice. "*Now, priestess. Now, gardener. Open.*"
