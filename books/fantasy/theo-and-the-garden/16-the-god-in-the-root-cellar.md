## 16. The God in the Root Cellar

The door did not open so much as *consent*.

That was the only word Theo had for it. Maren's map — a scrap of oilcloth covered in a priestess's cramped hand and one inked rose — had led them to the oldest tree in the garden, a rose grown wild and enormous, its trunk gnarled into something like a kneeling figure. Freya had pressed her oath-token to the bark, then cut his palm and let his blood fall among the roots, and the ground had simply... agreed. A seam of darkness opened in the earth like an eye deciding, after a hundred years, to blink.

Now they stood at the top of a stair that had not existed a moment before, and the air rising out of it smelled of rain on stone and something sweeter underneath — old honey, maybe, or the memory of incense.

"You first," Freya said quietly. "It's your blood it knows."

"It knows yours too." He could see it in the way she was holding herself, one hand flat against the rose trunk as if steadying herself against a wind he couldn't feel. Her oath-token hung outside her shirt now; she hadn't put it away since the door opened. The little iron disc caught what little light remained aboveground and threw it back dull and red. "Freya. It's going to know yours."

"I know," she said.

She went down first anyway. He should have guessed she would.

* * *

The stairs were older than the garden above them. Older than the city, if Theo had any eye for stone at all, and after the last three days he supposed he was learning to have one. They were not cut so much as *grown* — each step a swell of pale root-wood, banded like muscle, worn smooth in the center by feet that had climbed them long before Vharen built its walls. Roots lined the walls in braided columns, thick as his waist in places, and all of them leaned inward, downward, converging on the dark ahead the way streams converge on a lake.

His lantern made a small yellow world around them and nothing else. But the roots didn't need the light. Wherever it passed, they brightened — faintly, faintly, a green shimmer just under the bark, like veins carrying something slower than blood.

"They're alive," Theo said. "Down here. A hundred years underground."

"Everything down here is alive," Freya said. "That's rather the point."

Her voice was strange. He'd heard her afraid — in the cell corridor, in the hedgerows with hunters' crossbows snapping through the leaves around them. This wasn't fear. This was closer to what he'd heard in her voice the night he'd read the Codex aloud and her whole body had gone still: a person standing in front of something they'd spent fifteen years refusing to look at.

"How far?" he asked, because asking was kinder than silence.

"Maren's map says forty steps."

He counted. At thirty-one, the stairs ended.

* * *

The chamber below was small.

That was the thing Theo could never explain afterward, when people asked — and everyone asked, once the world changed, everyone wanted to know what the god's prison had looked like, expecting a cathedral of darkness, a chained titan, something to match the size of the miracle. It was a cellar. That was all. A low round room no bigger than the kitchen in the house where he'd grown up, ceiling close enough that Freya could have touched it with an outstretched arm. The floor was packed earth. The root-columns from the stairwell came down through the walls here and spread across the ceiling in a canopy, and every single one of them had grown *toward the center of the room* and stopped, straining, a hand's breadth short of touching what lay there.

A seed of light sat in the middle of the dirt floor.

Not a metaphor. Not poetry. A seed — the size of a walnut, the shape of a teardrop, resting in a shallow bowl of stone that might have been carved for it or might have been its own first attempt at making something. It glowed the way an ember glows: unevenly, wearily, pulsing slow as a sleeping heartbeat, dimming between pulses until Theo genuinely believed, twice, that it had gone out. Around it the earth was cracked and gray. Nothing grew within arm's reach of it. A hundred years of roots had come down those walls reaching and reaching, and none of them had ever crossed that last span of bare ground.

"Oh," Theo said. The sound came out of him without permission. "Oh, it's *small*."

"Yes," Freya said. Her voice had gone very flat, which he had learned meant the opposite of calm.

"It's supposed to be — I don't know. Big. The Codex talks about —"

"I know what the Codex talks about." She took one step forward and stopped at the edge of the dead circle, at the exact line where the living earth became starved earth, and Theo watched her hand come up and then press itself flat against her chest instead, over the oath-token, as if holding something in. "I know what it's *supposed* to be. I prayed to it for eleven years. I imagined it large enough to hold the sky, because that's what you're taught, that's what makes the prayers feel worth saying." A breath. "It's the size of a walnut, Theo. My god is the size of a walnut."

And then, because there was nothing else to do with a sentence like that, they stood there and let it be true.

* * *

Theo moved closer before he decided to move. His feet made the choice; his mind was still catching up. He crouched at the edge of the barren ring, lantern set carefully behind him so its shadow wouldn't fall across the seed, and looked at the god of Vharen the way he would look at any growing thing that had been left too long without water.

Up close he could see it was worse than starving. The light inside it moved wrong — not flowing but *flickering*, catching and guttering, the way a flame does in a room where the air has mostly been used up. Its surface, if a seed could be said to have a surface, was wrinkled and dulled, a skin gone papery with drought. And beneath the glow, so faint he might have invented it, there were lines. Fine cracks. A hundred years of shrinking inside its own shell.

"Can you hear me?" he asked. His voice sounded absurdly loud in the little room. "I'm — my name is Theo. Maren sent us. Freya Sandoval is with me, she —"

The seed pulsed.

It wasn't much. A brightening, stronger than any before it, the ember flaring — and the light didn't stay contained. It spilled outward, thin as water, and pooled against the invisible barrier at the edge of the dead ground, and then it *shaped itself*, briefly, the way smoke shapes itself into things your eyes insist on naming. A hand. An open hand, palm up, fingers slightly curled, held toward —

— toward Freya.

Toward the place where Freya had been standing before she'd stepped back against the wall.

"Theo," Freya said, and her voice broke clean in half on his name.

"She sees it," Theo said stupidly, looking between them. "It's — Freya, it's reaching for *you*."

"I can see that."

She pushed off the wall. He watched her do it — watched her make herself do it — crossing the room in four strides that cost her visibly more than the chase through the hedgerows had, more than killing the hunter had, more than anything he'd seen her survive. She dropped to her knees at the edge of the dead circle. The open hand of light trembled toward her, straining, and could come no further than the barrier, and Freya raised her own hand and matched it, palm to palm across a foot of poisoned ground that their fingers could not cross.

"It remembers me," she whispered. "After everything. After I — I sold charms out of a market stall while it *starved*, I thought it had gone silent because it *abandoned* —"

The seed flared again, hard, and this time the light came with something else. Not words. Theo felt that immediately and absolutely: whatever this was, it had no language in it at all. It was older than language and simpler than language and it hit him in the sternum like a fist and it was *grief*. Raw, undiluted, bottomless grief, pouring off the seed in pulses, in waves, in the rhythm of something sobbing without lungs or tears or any way at all to stop.

It couldn't speak. That was the horror of it, the detail Theo knew he would carry for the rest of his life however long the Bridge made that life. It couldn't speak. Whatever the city had done when they sealed it down here — whatever covenant they'd broken to build this little room and lock a god inside it — they had taken its voice first. Or its voice was bound up in the covenant, and the covenant was broken, and so it sat in the dark with a century of things to say and no way to say any of them except this: light, and shape, and grief.

"It's sorry," Freya said. Tears were running down her face freely now and she wasn't wiping them. "It thinks — Theo, it's apologizing. To *me*. Fifteen years I thought it abandoned me. It's down here apologizing because it thinks I suffered."

"Don't apologize," she said to the seed, and her voice cracked apart entirely, and she pressed both hands flat to the dead earth as though she could push through it by force of wanting. "Don't you dare apologize. You have nothing to — we did this. The city did this. I just — I didn't know. I spent fifteen years being angry at you and you were *down here* —"

The light-hand curled, gently, the way a hand curls when it wants to comfort someone and cannot reach.

* * *

Theo stayed back. Some instinct told him this was hers — this moment, this grief, this reunion fifteen years late and a foot too short — and that his part was not to intrude on it. So he knelt at the edge of the ring and watched the two of them, the mercenary and her god, palms almost touching across the barrier, and he let himself understand what he was looking at.

This was the thing the prophecy called the prisoner. This was the power the city had locked away to keep for itself, and gotten so greedy and so frightened that they'd kept it even from themselves — kept it from everyone, kept it from *use*, until it shrank to this: a walnut-sized ember in a bowl of stone, sobbing light at the one person who had ever sworn itself faithfully.

Human-scaled. That was the word that kept arriving in his head and refusing to leave. All the theology he'd half-heard his whole life, all the Codex's thunder about realms and bridges and covenants, and the truth under all of it was a small light in a small room, lonely, hurt, remembering one face out of thousands.

Divinity, it turned out, could be starved. Divinity could grieve. Divinity could spend a hundred years reaching for a hand and never touch it.

"We're getting you out," Freya said. She'd found her voice again, or a version of it, roughened and low and absolutely certain. "Tonight. Do you hear me? There's a covenant — Maren has the rite, the Codex names what has to happen, and it's going to *hurt*, and I'm sorry for that too, but tonight the door opens and you get out of this hole. I swear it. I'm breaking one oath tonight and keeping another."

The seed pulsed — and this time the pulse turned, deliberately, unmistakably, toward Theo.

The light gathered itself and shaped a second figure beside the first: smaller, standing, both hands loose at its sides. A crude outline. A suggestion. But the posture was his posture — the slight lean forward, the gardener's habit of weight on the balls of the feet — and when the light-figure tilted its head, Theo felt the tilt happen somewhere behind his own ribs.

"It knows what you are," Freya said softly.

"I know what I am." He swallowed. "Root-stock. The Bridge. The vessel." He'd been chewing those words for two days and they still tasted like someone else's. "Maren says the rite needs me to open the way. That the god has to — root in me. Grow through me." He looked at the seed, at its papery, cracking skin, at the fine hairline fractures where a century of hunger had nearly split it open. "You've been starving for a hundred years," he said to it. "When the door opens, you're going to be hungry. Really hungry. And I'm what's on the menu."

The light-figure flickered — and folded inward, arms crossing over where a heart would be, the universal gesture of *no*.

Theo laughed, and it came out wet. "No? You get one meal in a century and you're turning it down?"

The seed pulsed hard, twice, and the grief came again but changed now, braided through with something else — urgency, refusal, a fierce protective shove that Theo felt physically, pressing him backward an inch though he hadn't moved. It was saying, in the only grammar it had left: *not him. not him. not at this cost.*

"It's refusing," Freya breathed. "The prophecy needs a sacrifice and it's — it's *refusing* —"

Above them, muffled by forty steps of grown stone and a hundred years of packed earth, a bell began to ring.

Not the market bell. Not the watch bell. This was deeper, slower, tolling from the Hall of Hunters on the hill — the bell Theo had only ever heard described, the bell that rang when the city declared a state of condemnation. Between its strokes he could hear, faintly, a second sound gathering: voices. Hundreds of them. The whole city waking and pouring downhill toward the gardens, toward the bonfires already being stacked in the avenues.

Dawn was coming. The burning was coming. And the door under the roses had only consented once, and might never consent again.

Freya rose to her feet, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and looked down at the seed of light with an expression Theo had last seen on the night she'd decided to break into the cells: grief and fury welded together into something that could carry a person through a wall.

"Hold on," she told her god. "We're coming back for you. Both of us."

Behind them, far above, the first bonfire caught, and the smell of smoke began to seep down through the roots.
