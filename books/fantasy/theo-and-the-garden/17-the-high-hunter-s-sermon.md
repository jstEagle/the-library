## 17. The High Hunter's Sermon

They heard the city wake before they saw it.

From beneath the roses — from that long dark descent into the root cellar where a starving god had wept without words — they had climbed back into a world remade overnight. The garden that had sheltered them now stood ringed with iron. Witch-hunters in their gray-and-crimson livery moved along the perimeter like ants along a wound, driving stakes, unspooling rope, hanging lanterns of clear glass that held no flame but burned anyway: coldfire, the kind that ate green things and left stone untouched.

Freya crouched in the shadow of the old rose tree, its roots still trembling faintly from what they'd done below, and watched Vharen prepare to burn its own heart.

"They're making an occasion of it," Theo whispered beside her. His hand was pressed flat against the soil, and even now — even after everything — the ground seemed to lean toward his palm, grass blades tilting like faces at a window. "Banners. They've put up *banners*."

He was right. Along the garden's southern wall, where the municipal groundskeeper's shed had rotted for thirty years, workers were rigging bunting in the city's colors — gray and white, the colors of the Silence. A platform was going up at the edge of the lawn, wide enough for a dozen dignitaries, and already carpenters were hauling benches into rows facing it. Facing the garden. Facing the fire.

"It's not an execution," Freya said quietly. "It's a sermon."

She knew the shape of it. Fifteen years ago she had stood in crowds like this one — smaller, angrier, drunk on fear — and watched the last oath-sworn mercenary in Vharen publicly break her token on the Hall steps while the Council read out the new law. She remembered how the crowd had needed it. Not justice; need. They had needed to see magic die somewhere visible so they could sleep believing it stayed dead everywhere else.

Tomorrow at dawn, Vharen would burn a garden, call it a demon's nest, and go home feeling safe. And somewhere in the crowd would be clerks who'd bought her luck-charms, widows she'd warded against fever, children she'd given little knotted threads against nightmares — all of them pretending they'd never known her name.

"Freya." Theo's voice pulled her back. He was looking at her with that steady gardener's attention, the one that saw blight before it showed. "You've gone somewhere far away."

"I've gone fifteen years away," she said. "Give me a moment. I'm deciding how much of it I'm angry about."

His mouth twitched — he almost smiled, that quiet crooked smile that had undone her on a market morning over cut flowers she hadn't needed — but he didn't laugh, because neither of them could afford laughter yet. Below them, under their feet, a god the size of a seed slept in the dark, gathering itself through Theo the way spring gathers in a bulb. Above them, the city built its pyre.

"We can't stay here past nightfall," Freya said. "The hunters will sweep the garden at dusk to make sure their spectacle doesn't escape early."

"Then where?"

"Maren's. Or the tannery district — Doss said he'd keep a door open." She named the names of the small people, and saying them steadied her. The cooper's widow who'd hidden them two nights ago under a floor of pickling brine. The clerk from chapter one of her ruin, the nervous man with the ink-stained cuffs who'd bought a charm against bad news and then, when the lockdown came, had slipped a note under Maren's door with the patrol schedule copied out in a fair round hand. *The small people*, Maren called them. The ones who had never been given a covenant and had kept faith anyway, in pennies and favors and doors left unlatched.

Theo squeezed her shoulder once and went back to watching the wall, and Freya let herself watch him instead for a moment — this man who was becoming something the word *man* might soon be too small to hold, and who still flinched when the coldfire lanterns hissed, and who still, absurdly, had mud under his fingernails from a world above the god-prison that shouldn't have had any mud in it at all.

Then the bells began.

Not the alarm bells. The other kind — the great cracked bell in the Hall tower that Vharen rang only for proclamations and funerals of state. Its voice rolled across the rooftops, flat and bronze, and every hammer in the garden stopped, and every gray-and-crimson head turned toward the sound as if pulled by strings.

A herald's voice followed, magically amplified — which was its own obscenity, Freya thought; the Council used confiscated enchantments the way a butcher wore his mother's pearls — carrying across the district:

*"By order of the High Council of Vharen: at dawn tomorrow, the corrupted ground known as the Municipal Garden shall be cleansed by fire, that the demon calling itself THE BRIDGE may be driven out or destroyed. Let all citizens attend. Let all citizens witness. There is no safety in half-measures. There is no mercy in rot."*

The herald paused, and when his voice came again it had changed — deeper, more deliberate. Someone else was speaking now.

*"I am Ser Aldric Vane, High Hunter of Vharen. For fifteen years this city has known peace. Tomorrow you will learn why."*

Theo looked at her. "Do you know him?"

"I know *of* him," Freya said, and found her mouth had gone dry. "Everyone in my trade knows of him. He's the reason there is no longer a trade."

* * *

She told Theo the story as they moved, low and fast, through the drainage culvert Maren's map had marked — the same route they'd used to slip in, though it felt different going toward danger than away from it.

"Aldric Vane was oath-sworn once," she said. "Before your time in Vharen. Before mine, barely. He served a river-god upcountry — the Weeping Mother, out past Corren's Ford. Real service, real devotion. They say he carried her tokens into three battles and came out of each one untouched. They say the river rose for him when he asked."

"And then?"

"And then the Silence came." She ducked under a low arch of brick furred with moss. "Fifteen years ago. Whatever happened — whatever *they* did, here, under this garden — it didn't just take our god. It took all of them, everywhere, or close enough. Every sworn mercenary in the region woke up holding a dead ember where their god had been. Most of us broke our oaths within the year. What was the point? You can't serve silence." She hesitated. "Most of us broke our oaths and grieved. Vane did something else. He decided that if the gods wouldn't answer, then the problem was the gods — and if magic was going to exist in the world regardless, then it should belong to whoever held the leash."

"The witch-hunters."

"The witch-hunters. He didn't join them, Freya-style, selling charms in alleys and keeping his head down. He *founded* half their doctrine. The sermons about contamination. The doctrine of total cleansing. He took the grief of every abandoned servant of every silent god and forged it into a weapon, and the Council handed him the leash and called it peace." She climbed a rung of iron ladder, pushed open the grate at the top a finger's width, and looked out across a lane full of citizens walking toward the garden district to claim bench seats for the burning. "He's not a hypocrite, Theo. That's the worst of it. He believes every word. He thinks he's saving the world from the thing that broke his heart."

"That sounds familiar," Theo said softly.

She looked back at him. In the dimness of the culvert, with the light of the coldfire lanterns leaking through the grate and striping his face, he didn't look afraid. He looked sad — sad in a way that had grown roots in him these last weeks, deep enough that terror couldn't pull it loose anymore.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't look at me like I'm him."

"I wasn't looking at you like you're him." He came up the ladder behind her, close enough that she felt his breath stir her hair. "I was thinking that he must have loved something once, the way you love it. And lost it. And that the difference between you isn't strength or weakness. It's just that when your god went silent, you waited. You waited fifteen years in a market stall, keeping faith with a voice that never answered, and he — couldn't. That's all. That's the whole difference. Patience."

"Patience," Freya repeated. It tasted like ash and like honey at once. "Maren would say stubbornness."

"Maren says lots of things." His hand found hers in the dark. "Come on. If we're going to be caught, let's at least be caught doing something."

* * *

They were nearly clear of the garden district when the hunters found them — or rather, when the hunter found her.

Not a squad. One man, alone, stepping out of an alley mouth with the unhurried confidence of someone who has never in his life needed backup. Gray-and-crimson, yes, but cut finer than any patrol uniform, the crimson worked into a sash rather than a tabard, the gray polished to near-silver. Tall, broad through the shoulders, silver at the temples in a face otherwise too unlined for his years — and Freya understood that immediately, the way she understood weather. Oath-sworn servants aged slowly until their god fell silent. Then the years arrived all at once, like a debt called in.

Vane had been paying his debt for fifteen years and wearing it well.

"Lady Sandoval." His voice was mild. Courteous, even. That was the most frightening thing about it. "Or should I say *Freya of the Ember Order*? Your sigil work is distinctive. I catalogued three of your charms during the first year of the ban. Beautiful knots. Wasted craft."

Behind her, she felt Theo tense, felt the air change the way it changed when plants turned toward him — a faint leaning pressure, as if the alley's weeds and the ivy on the walls were holding their breath. She put a hand back, flat, the way you calm a horse. *Not yet.*

"High Hunter." She kept her voice level, market-stall level, the voice that had talked nervous clerks into luck-charms for fifteen years. "You honor me. I'd have worn my good cloak."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite amusement. Recognition, maybe. The recognition of one professional meeting another.

"You were good," he said. "In the old days. I watched you work, once, at the Corren's Ford muster. You were twenty-two, maybe. You laid a ward-line around a fever camp in four hours flat, singing the whole time, and the Mother's blessing came down on it like rain." He shook his head slowly. "I've often wondered what became of you. Now I know. Charms. Luck-knots and nightmare-threads." His lip curled, very slightly. "You diminished yourself, Lady Sandoval. All of us who went quiet — we diminished ourselves. I refused."

"You founded an inquisition," Freya said.

"I *answered*." For the first time his voice sharpened, and underneath it she heard it — the crack in the glaze, the grief fifteen years old and never once set down. "The gods abandoned us. Do you understand that? I gave that river everything. My youth. My hands. Three times I should have died and three times she caught me, and then one morning the water was just *water*. No voice. No warmth. Nothing. And the world kept humming with power anyway — wild magic, hedge-magic, the filth that leaks through the cracks — and no one was minding the store." He stepped closer. His boots made no sound on the cobblestones; even his footsteps had been trained into weapons. "Someone had to mind the store, Sandoval. The Council lacked the spine. So I grew one."

"And the price of the spine was the garden," Freya said. "And the seed of a god starved in a cellar for a hundred years. Was that minding the store too? Because that's what you're protecting, High Hunter. That's what your clean, safe, silent city is built on top of. A prisoner. A god in a root cellar, kept starving so the Council could hold the leash you're so proud of holding for them."

For a heartbeat — just one — something moved behind Vane's eyes. Something that was not doctrine. Something that had perhaps spent fifteen years in a locked room of its own, scratching at the door.

Then it closed, and the High Hunter was back, smooth as river stone.

"You've been below," he said quietly. "To the prison. I wondered." He sighed, and the sigh was almost genuine. "That changes the offer, actually. Improves it."

"What offer?" Theo said from behind her. His voice cracked on the second word, and Freya's whole body turned protective without her permission.

Vane looked past her at Theo for the first time, studying him the way a man studies a horse he intends to buy — or a tumor he intends to cut. "The offer I came to make before I learned who you were traveling with. It stands. It improves." He reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper, sealed with gray wax, and held it where they could both see it without coming closer. "The Council wants the Bridge destroyed at dawn. Publicly. Spectacularly. I am empowered to negotiate alternatives, provided the outcome is the same in substance."

"In substance being—" Freya started.

"He dies," Vane said simply, and Theo flinched. "Forgive the bluntness. I find euphemism insulting in matters of state. The Bridge cannot be allowed to live. Whatever he is — boy, vessel, demon, dupe — the covenant he carries ends the ban, and the ban is the only wall between this city and the chaos that swallowed the old kingdoms. That is non-negotiable. But *how* he dies, and *when*, and what happens in the hours between — those, Lady Sandoval, are negotiable. Within limits."

"And why," Freya said, very carefully, "would you negotiate anything with me?"

"Because of who you are." Vane folded his hands, and for a moment he looked less like a hunter than like a priest — which, she realized, was exactly what he had become, the high priest of the church of the Silence. "You are the last oath-sworn servant in Vharen who never formally broke her vow. Did you know that? I keep records. When the Covenant collapsed, four hundred mercenaries held living oaths in this region. Three hundred and ninety-nine broke theirs inside eighteen months. Tokens shattered, names renounced, prayers struck from the rolls." He tilted his head. "One did not. One kept her token. Kept it hidden, kept it whole, kept praying into the silence for fifteen years like a fool writing letters to a dead lover. Do you know what I thought when I finally confirmed it was you? I thought — *there. There is the faithful one. There is the one who understands.*"

"I don't understand anything you understand."

"No," Vane agreed. "But you *could*. That's the offer." He unfolded the paper a few inches, enough to show the weight of the script, the Council seal, the blank line at the bottom awaiting a signature. "Renew your oath of neutrality. Formally. Before witnesses, before dawn. Swear that the events below the garden were coercion, that you acted under duress, that you accept the Council's judgment in the matter of the Bridge. In exchange: the Bridge goes to the fire tomorrow as scheduled — but painlessly. Coldfire first. He will feel nothing. No mob, no cage, no interrogation. And you walk free, your record expunged, your charms pardoned retroactively. Every small person who helped you — the widow, the clerk, the old priestess — walks free. Their names never enter the rolls."

The alley was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the rooftops, the great cracked bell tolled the third hour, and the sound seemed to hang between them like a blade.

"You'd pardon them," Freya said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, distant, underwater. "The people who sheltered us."

"Every one. Provided their cooperation continues, which it will, once you're seen standing with us at the burning. A public renewal of your oath, Lady Sandoval. The last loyal servant of the old covenant kneeling before the new order. Do you understand what that image would do? Half the hedge-witches in this city still carry your knots under their shirts. If *you* kneel, the resistance dies in a single morning. No purge necessary. No house-to-house searches. Just you, and the platform, and the fire, and your knee on the stone." His eyes were steady, almost gentle. "One ceremony. One signature. And instead of a week of blood, we have a day of ashes and then peace. You'd be saving lives. Possibly dozens. Possibly more, if the fanatics Maren has been collecting decide to martyr themselves at the wall."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow proceeds as planned. The Bridge burns awake. The mob gets its spectacle. The Council, feeling threatened, expands the purges — and I will not be able to stop them, because I will have lost my argument, which was always *Sandoval proves the faithful can be reconciled*. And every name on my list of your helpers enters the rolls by nightfall." He paused. "Including a cooper's widow with two daughters. Including a clerk with ink-stained fingers who forged patrol schedules. Including an eighty-year-old defrocked priestess whose execution will require, by statute, a *demonstration*."

Freya's hands had gone cold. She was aware of Theo behind her, utterly silent, and she could not turn around, could not look at him, because if she looked at him she would see whatever was in his face and she would be lost, or saved, and she didn't know which.

This was the mirror. This was the thing she had spent fifteen years refusing to look at directly: not the question of whether she was brave, but the question of what she was *for*. She had told herself her neutrality was mourning. Theo had believed her. Maren had believed her. Maybe it had even been true. But Vane stood in front of her now wearing her own grief like a uniform, and he was showing her the road her grief could have taken — the road where waiting curdles into control, where faith in a silent god becomes faith in silence itself — and he was offering her a bargain that her past self, the young woman singing ward-lines at Corren's Ford, would have recognized instantly as *reasonable*. Pragmatic. Merciful, even, in arithmetic. Dozens of lives for one man's painless death.

One man.

*The one thing the prophecy never required,* she thought suddenly, remembering Maren's voice, remembering the pages of suppressed text: the Bridge's sacrifice had to be chosen, freely, or it was nothing. And Vane was offering to choose it *for* him. To spend Theo like coin. To make his death a transaction between professionals over the body of a bound god.

"You're asking me," Freya said slowly, "to do to him exactly what the Council did to my god."

Vane blinked. "I don't follow."

"Fifteen years I prayed into the silence and hated myself for my doubt. I thought my god had abandoned *me*. And all that time he was in a cellar under a rose tree, chained and starving, and the silence was a lie the city told so it could wear his power like a stolen coat." She took a step forward, and to her own surprise she wasn't shaking anymore. "You think you're minding the store, Aldric. You're the lock on the storeroom door. You built your whole church on top of a theft, and now you're offering me a share of the loot if I'll help you bury the thief's victim alive." She shook her head. "I buried my faith for fifteen years. I'm done digging graves."

Something hardened in Vane's face — not anger, worse: disappointment. The look of a teacher whose best student has failed the final lesson.

"I offered you the merciful version," he said. "Remember that, when the mob comes. Remember that you chose the spectacle." He refolded the paper with crisp, unhurried movements, and as he tucked it away he raised two fingers to his lips and blew a single note — a hunter's whistle, thin and carrying, the signal Freya had heard maybe five times in fifteen years and had hoped never to hear again at this range.

*Found.*

"I'm sorry, Lady Sandoval," the High Hunter said, and meant it, and that was the most terrible thing in the whole gray morning. "Truly. The faithful deserve better than what the world has become. I only ever tried to build them something solid." He stepped back into the shadowed mouth of the alley, and his parting words drifted back like smoke:

"Burn well, both of you. Dawn comes early."

Boots thundered at both ends of the lane. Torches bloomed. And Freya spun at last to face Theo — to face the man she had just, in front of the most dangerous soul in Vharen, refused to save — and found him already reaching for her hand, already pulling her toward the drainage grate, already smiling that impossible crooked smile.

"Well," Theo said, as the first crossbow bolt buried itself in the brick where his head had been, "I suppose we'd better go tell a god the timetable's moved up."
