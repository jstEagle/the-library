## 18. Reforging

**Freya**

The smoke came first.

It rolled over the garden walls in a gray tide, tasting of pitch and cut hedge, and beneath it Freya could hear the crowd — hundreds of them, packed into the terraces above the old municipal garden to watch the city burn its heart. The High Hunter had made a festival of it. Banners hung from the Hall of Judgment. Children were lifted onto shoulders for a better view. Somewhere a vendor was selling roasted chestnuts, because Vharen had never met an atrocity it couldn't snack through.

Below, in the root cellar beneath the oldest rose tree, the god of the garden lay dying, and Theo knelt beside it with his sleeves pushed up as though this were any other day's work.

"It's starving," he said. His voice was steady, but his hands weren't. "A century underground with no one tending it. It's like — like finding a tree that's been pot-bound its whole life. Roots circling themselves. It doesn't know how to grow anymore."

"Then we teach it," Freya said.

She knelt across from him, the withered seed of light between them — no bigger than a lantern, guttering, its glow pulsing in rhythms too slow to be called breathing. Fifteen years she had prayed into silence. Sixteen. And here was the silence itself, small enough to hold, so starved it couldn't even speak her name.

The oath-token sat heavy against her sternum. She had worn it under her shirt every day since the sky went gray, a coin of hammered tin bearing her god's mark: a seed split by a growing shoot. Every mercenary who'd sworn the old covenant wore one. Most had thrown theirs away when the magic died, when the ban came down, when selling charms became a hanging offense and neutrality became the only law left worth keeping.

*Sworn to neither crown nor creed,* the oath ran. *Sworn to the balance. My blade is no one's. My word is my own. I keep the line between.*

She'd kept it through fifteen years of watching witch-hunters drag people into wagons. She'd kept it while she sold luck-charms drained of everything but suggestion. She'd kept it three nights ago when hunters knocked on her door and she stood silent in her own bedroom while they took Theo away.

No more.

"Theo." He looked up. Above them the ceiling beams shuddered — the fire crew was already at the garden's edge, laying oil along the hedgerows, and the heat would reach the roses within the hour, and beneath the roses, this room. "The Codex says the covenant needs two hands. One that holds the oath, and one that holds the root. I can give mine. But I need you to hear what it costs before I do, because once I break it, there's no taking it back, and I won't have you learn the price afterward."

"Tell me."

"I swore my oath to *him*." She touched the token through the cloth. "The god. Not to the city, not to the guild — to the god, through the covenant. As long as I hold it, my power is his power, filtered through my keeping. But the covenant is broken, so all I've ever really held since the silence is the husk of it. To reforge the bridge, I don't just bend the oath. I surrender it. Entirely. Everything I am that was made by that promise — gone, unmade, offered up as kindling for the new thing." Her throat tightened. "And what I get back — if I get anything back — will be shaped by *you*. By whatever you become down here. That's the bargain. I hand myself to a man I've known a season, betting he'll be worthy of holding me."

Theo's eyes were wet. He didn't wipe them.

"You've known me longer than a season," he said quietly. "You've known me since the day you bought flowers you didn't need."

"Don't flatter yourself, gardener."

"You came back every week for a month."

"They were very good dahlias."

He laughed — a cracked, smoke-torn laugh — and then the ceiling groaned again, louder, and the laughter died, and there was only the fire coming and the dying light between them and the last minutes of the world as it had been.

"Freya," Theo said. "I'm ready."

Above them, the first hedge caught.

* * *

**Theo**

The fire arrived as sound before it arrived as heat — a rushing, tearing roar, like the sea standing up and walking. Dust sifted from the beams. The little god's light flickered wildly, and Theo put his hands around it the way he'd cup a seedling against wind, and felt it lean into him like something that had forgotten leaning was allowed.

*Maren said the rite is simple,* he reminded himself. *That's what frightens everyone about it. Simple isn't easy.*

*One hand surrenders. One hand opens. The covenant does the rest.*

Freya drew the oath-token from her neck. The chain snapped in her fingers — she didn't fumble for the clasp; she broke it, deliberately, the way you break a seal — and held the coin flat on her palm between them. It was duller than he remembered, the seed-and-shoot mark worn nearly smooth.

"Fifteen years ago I swore this," she said, and her voice changed — dropped into a cadence older than her, formal, liturgical, the voice of someone reciting words she'd promised never to say again except at the end. "I swore by root and rain and the quiet places. I swore my strength to the balance, my blade to no banner, my heart to no master. I have kept it. I have kept it past all sense, past all mercy, past the point where keeping it became its own cruelty." Her hand shook. "I surrender it now. Not because I failed it. Because it failed *us* — or was made to fail us — and the only way left to honor it is to let it end and build what comes after. Root and rain witness. Quiet places, witness. Take back your soldier."

The token flared white.

Theo felt it happen even though it wasn't happening to him — felt the oath leave her the way you feel a lamp go out in another room, a change in the quality of the dark. Freya gasped, swayed, caught herself on his shoulder. Where the coin had rested on her palm there was now only a print of pale skin and, faintly, a smell like green things crushed.

Her eyes when they opened were different. Not emptied — *opened*, the way soil is opened by a spade.

"It's done," she whispered. "I'm nobody's. I'm just—" She laughed, shaky. "I haven't been just Freya since I was nineteen. It's enormous in here. It's terrifying. It's—"

"Mine to guard now," Theo said. "That part I can do."

He turned to the god.

Up close, in the last of its light, he could see what it truly was — not a shriveled old man, not the grandeur the Codex promised, but something more like the heartwood of a tree, if a heartwood could grieve. Its hunger filled the cellar. A century of starvation radiated off it like cold off stone, and Theo understood, with a gardener's certainty, exactly how this went wrong. Starved things don't eat politely. Starved things take. He had seen blight move through a healthy bed in a single night.

If he opened himself to this — if the rooting took — the god would pour into him, and a hundred years of famine would come pouring with it.

"Hey," Freya said softly. "Whatever you're deciding — decide fast."

"I know." He swallowed. "I'm opening. Both hands. Here goes nothing ordinary."

He spread his arms over the seed of light and did the hardest thing a gardener ever does: he stopped protecting the plant and trusted the process. He let his own boundaries go thin — imagined himself as soil, turned, broken open, ready — and said the words Maren had taught him, the ones from the suppressed chapter, the ones written for exactly this:

*"Root in me. Stock to my stock. What was sundered, let it graft. I offer the ground. Grow."*

The god rose.

Not physically — there wasn't much of it left to rise — but its light stood up out of the ruin of it like a flame remembering its wick, and it came toward him, and Theo felt the first touch of it at the edge of his soul, and it was —

It was stopping.

The light hovered. Trembled. Drew back an inch, the way a starving dog draws back from a hand that's raised too fast, and then it did something Theo had not prepared for, something no chapter of the Codex had described.

It refused.

It pressed against him — gently, terribly gently — testing, and then it withdrew entirely, curling back down into its withered shape, and its grief poured off it in waves so strong that Theo staggered.

"What's wrong?" Freya was at his side instantly. "Why did it stop? Is he failing? Is it the rite?"

"No." Theo stared at the small, dimming light, and his chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with fear. "It's not failing. It knows. Don't you see — it can feel what I'm offering. All of it. It can feel that if it roots in me now, the man I am gets dissolved — the ordinary self, the gardener who likes bad poetry and burns toast, all the nothing-special parts. It's been starved for a hundred years and it's *refusing to eat me whole.*"

"Oh," Freya breathed.

"It would rather die than consume me." His voice cracked. "Fifteen years we thought the gods abandoned us. It's been down here starving, and its first act when salvation finally arrives is to make sure the salvation doesn't cost too much."

The light pulsed — slow, sorrowful, unmistakable. *No.*

"Well," Theo said, wiping his face with the back of his wrist, laughing at the absurdity of it, tears cutting lines through the soot. "I suppose that settles which of us is the better person."

"Theo—"

"It asked permission," he said. "All this time, everyone assumed the Bridge gets taken. Prophecy, destiny, bred for it, all of Maren's terrible truths about root-stock and vessels. But it's asking. It's been asking this whole time and none of us thought to answer." He knelt before the guttering seed of light, and this time he did not spread his arms like an offering. He reached out one hand, slowly, palm up, the way you offer anything to a creature that has been hurt.

"Yes," he said to it. "Out loud, so there's no mistaking it, in front of Freya and the fire and whatever's left of the old covenant to witness it: *yes*. Root in me. Take what you need and no more. I choose this. Nobody made me. Nobody bred it into me without my say — well, they tried, and I'm taking that back right now, because consent is mine to give, and I'm giving it. Freely. As Theo. Whatever I am afterward, I'll still be the one who said yes."

The god's light steadied.

And then, with the care of something setting down a weight it had carried for a century, it began to grow.

* * *

**Freya**

She had witnessed miracles before — real ones, back when the covenant lived, back when the world still had seams and the seams could be opened. She had seen rain called down over harvested fields and wounds closed with a spoken word. None of it had prepared her for watching a god be gentle.

It rooted in Theo the way ivy takes a wall — patiently, spiral by spiral, testing each hold before committing its weight. Light climbed him like water climbing a stem. It went into his hands first, the gardener's hands, the calloused and capable ones, and Freya saw the scars of fifteen years of ordinary work fill with soft gold. Then his arms, his shoulders, the long line of his back, and everywhere it passed it *kept* him — she could see it, could feel it through the raw openness where her oath used to be — it was rebuilding him even as it rooted, honoring its own refusal, taking only what was freely given and leaving every freely-given piece intact.

His hair stirred though there was no wind. His eyes, when they opened, had a second depth to them, like looking into a well and seeing stars at the bottom.

"Theo?"

"I'm here." His voice was his voice, and also something vaster speaking through it, the way a pipe is still a pipe even when the organ plays. He flexed his fingers, watched the light run under his skin. "It's — Freya, it's showing me. The roots of everything. The garden goes down forever. The other realm is right *there*, separated by paper, by a wall the city built a hundred years ago, and I'm standing in the doorway, and—"

The ceiling screamed.

A beam gave way at the far end of the cellar, trailing fire, and the heat hit them like a wave — the burning garden above had finally found the door beneath the roses. Through the collapsing stairwell Freya could see orange chaos, the rose tree itself going up in a crown of flame, and above that, faintly, the roar of the crowd, cheering.

The god surged in Theo like a tide against a dam.

"No," Theo gritted. He had gone rigid, fighting for control of himself, of it, of the fury pouring through the fresh-rooted bond — a century of imprisonment, of stolen worship, of children burned for carrying sparks of what this god had given freely. "Not vengeance. Not first. Freya — the charms. Every charm in the city. Can you feel them?"

She could. With her oath surrendered and the new covenant crackling into being around them, she could feel *everything* — every drained trinket she'd ever sold, every hollow luck-piece gathering dust in drawers and pockets across Vharen, thousands of them, empty vessels all waiting for exactly this.

"The covenant needs a first act," Theo said. The light in him was rising now, filling the burning cellar, pushing back the heat. "Not fire against fire. Something they can't call a demon's work. Give them back what the city took. All of it. At once."

Freya laughed — wild, sobbing, alive — and raised both hands, palms open, priestess to the marrow of her for the first time in sixteen years.

"Together, then," she said. "Bridge."

"Together, priestess."

They spoke as one voice — his rooted deep, hers rising free — and the new covenant roared through the earth like spring through frozen ground.

In ten thousand pockets and drawers and keepsake boxes across the city of Vharen, every outlawed charm lit at once — a sudden constellation of small gold lights in the dark of the burning night, impossible, undeniable, witnessed.

And the fire above the garden stopped mid-roar, like a shout cut short, like a held breath — and began, petal by petal, beam by beam, to bloom backward into roses.
