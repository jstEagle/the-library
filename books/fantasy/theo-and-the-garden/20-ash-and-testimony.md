## 20. Ash and Testimony

The fire died mid-roar.

I will carry the sound of that dying for the rest of my life — the great bonfire they'd stacked around the garden's heart, fed with lamp-oil and pitch and the broken trellises of a hundred summers, roaring up into the gray morning like a thing with lungs. And then, in one breath, not roaring. Not burning. The flames simply stopped, every one of them, as if the whole city had blown out a candle at once, and what was left was smoke hanging motionless in the air like a held breath, and light coming up out of the ground instead of down from the sky.

Light came up through Theo.

I stood at the edge of the crowd, my hands still black with Maren's ink and someone else's blood, and watched him stand in the ruined rose garden with roots of radiance running out of him into the earth, and I understood that I was watching two things happen at once: my lover becoming something vast, and remaining, impossibly, himself. He turned his head. He found me in the crowd the way he always did — not searching, just knowing where I'd be — and his eyes were full of green growing things and also, underneath all that, still the tired brown eyes of a man who liked his tea too sweet.

Every outlawed charm in Vharen lit at once.

I felt it before I saw it: a shiver through the city like frost reversing itself, like winter deciding to run its film backward. In ten thousand windows, in ten thousand pockets and lockets and hidden drawers, the little dead charms I had spent fifteen years selling — luck-braids and hearth-knots and fever-silk, all of them harmless, all of them hollow since the day my god went silent — woke up. I knew because the old oath-token against my sternum went hot for the first time in a decade and a half, warm as a hand pressed flat over a heartbeat, and then it *sang*, one clear note like a struck bell, and so did every other token in the city, all together, one chord struck from ten thousand small pieces of faith.

The crowd made a sound I had never heard a crowd make. Not cheering. Something older than cheering.

And the High Hunter drew his sword.

* * *

His name was Corvess. I had known him by reputation for fifteen years without ever standing this close to him — the man who built the witch-hunters out of nothing after the Silence, who turned a scattered guild of bounty-men into the fist of Vharen, who had once, the stories said, worn an oath-token of his own. He stood now on the reviewing platform the city had raised for the burning, in white and ash-gray, sword bare, and his face was the face of a man watching his entire life's architecture come down in a single morning.

"The demon wears the gardener!" he shouted, and his voice carried on hunter-training alone, beautiful and terrible and utterly empty. "This is sorcery! This is the plague the covenant ban was built to keep from your doors! Kill it while it stands—"

"Stand there," said a voice, "and be quiet."

It was the baker's wife. Marta Ollen, three stalls down from mine at market, who bought a fertility charm from me four years ago when she'd given up hoping, and whose daughter was standing beside her now, seven years old, holding a fever-silk I'd sold during the coughing winter. Marta wasn't shouting. That was what undid him, I think — that nobody was shouting. The whole square had gone quiet in the way of people who have just seen something true and are unwilling to let noise dilute it.

"I bought that," Marta said, lifting her daughter's wrist, the faded silk catching the new light. "From Freya Sandoval, at her stall, for two coppers, because my girl was dying and the law said magic was a crime and mercy wasn't. It never worked. Not once." Her voice didn't break. "Until today."

"It worked," said someone else — the clerk from chapter one of my own weary history, the nervous man with the ledger-hands, pushing forward with his luck-charm held up like a lantern. "Mine worked. It's working now."

"Mine worked."

"Mine worked."

It moved through the square like wind through wheat, that litany, hundreds of voices, thousands, each one holding up some small piece of hidden hope — a braid, a knot, a scrap of silk — and I realized, standing among them with my heart going like a hammer, that I had never been selling nothing. I had been selling *seeds*. Fifteen years of seeds sown into dry ground, waiting for rain, and here was the rain, and here was the harvest, rising up around Corvess on every side.

He looked at the crowd and I watched him do the arithmetic. Two hundred hunters at his back, maybe, scattered along the barricades. Ten thousand citizens. And above it all, the light still pouring up out of Theo like spring given a body.

"Kill the demon," Corvess said again, but quieter, and to the hunters rather than the crowd, and that was his mistake, if a dying man can be said to make mistakes. Because the nearest hunters were looking at their own hands. At the oath-tokens some of them had never surrendered — confiscated, archived, forbidden — which were, I learned later, singing in the Hall's strongboxes at that very moment, loud enough to be heard through stone.

One hunter lowered his sword. Then another. Then a sergeant I knew by sight, a gray woman named Ilsabet who had searched my stall twice over the years and always, somehow, missed the real charms under the floorboards, stepped off the barricade line entirely, walked to the platform stairs, and stood at their foot with her blade sheathed and her arms folded, facing upward.

Not toward Corvess. Toward the sky, and the smoke, and the light.

Corvess looked down at her, and at the crowd, and at the gardener blazing gently in the ruins of the fire they had built to destroy him, and whatever lived behind his face finally finished its arithmetic. He raised his sword anyway. Of course he did. Men like Corvess don't have anything left when the believing stops; the sword is all that's left to raise.

What happened next happened outside the law entirely, and I have thought about it many times since, because it would have been so easy for it to go the other way. Ten thousand people, cheated and frightened and governed by fear for fifteen years, and one unarmed man on a platform offering them a target.

They took the sword away from him. That's all. A dozen hands — the baker's husband among them, and the clerk, and old men and young women and a fish-porter with forearms like cable — they simply walked up the steps and took it, gently, the way you take a knife from a child, and Corvess stood there disarmed in front of his own barricades and his own soldiers and none of them moved to help him. Marta's daughter picked up the sword where it had been set down on the platform boards and couldn't lift it, and laughed, and the sound of that laugh did more damage to the witch-hunters' authority than any riot could have done.

They didn't kill him. They might have. Instead they bound his wrists with the silk cords from the execution scaffolding — the ones meant for Theo — and sat him down on the edge of the platform to wait for the council, and he sat there very straight, very pale, staring at the light coming out of the ground, and I saw his lips moving and caught the shape of the words and understood, with a strange lurch of pity, that he was praying. For the first time in fifteen years, the High Hunter was praying, to whatever was left of the thing he'd helped bury.

Theo's voice came then, from everywhere and nowhere, from the roses and the soil and the smoke-still air, gentle as watering-can rain:

"He is not damned. He is only early. Bring him inside."

* * *

The Council of Vharen convened within the hour, which for a body that once took eleven months to approve a drainage permit tells you everything about how thoroughly the morning had rearranged the world. They met in the Hall of Records — the same hall where the Codex had been catalogued and suppressed and forgotten — and they asked me to attend, and I went, and I stood in front of nine gray-robed councilors with blood on my sleeve and ash in my hair and an oath-token singing softly against my chest, and I told them the truth.

All of it.

I told them about the charms and the law and the fifteen years of small illegal mercies. I told them about the night the knock came at my door and I hid in the wardrobe while they took Theo, and I did not soften it, because softening it would have been a lie, and I had promised myself on the walk over that I was done lying to institutions even if they weren't done lying to me. I told them what Maren showed us in the ash-archive: the suppressed final chapter of the Codex, the ledgers of the Founders' Compact, the signatures of Vharen's first council on a covenant that did not break — that was *broken*, deliberately, by men who wanted the god's power flowing through city channels instead of through anyone's faith. I gave them names. Dead names, mostly, but names, and the founding charter with its seals, which Maren had kept wrapped in oilcloth for forty years waiting for someone with the standing to carry it into daylight.

"And your part in it," said the eldest councilor, a dried-apple woman named Halvene who had voted, I knew, for the burning. "You are a mercenary. You broke your oath of neutrality. You killed a hunter in the hedgerows — we have the report. Why should this council treat your testimony as anything but the self-serving account of a criminal?"

It was a fair question. I had asked myself a version of it every hour since dawn.

"Because I'm not speaking as a mercenary," I said. "A mercenary swears neutrality between powers and sells skill to whichever side pays. That's what I was. That oath is dissolved — not broken by me, but ended, because one of the powers it balanced between has changed the terms of the world." I lifted the oath-token off my neck and held it up, and it rang, one clear note, and every councilor flinched except Halvene, who only went very still. "This was silent for fifteen years. This morning it sang. I didn't make it sing. I don't get to decide anymore what I am — the god does, or rather, the god and I decide together, which is what a covenant *is*. So no. Not a mercenary."

"Then what," Halvene said, "are you?"

And I heard it before I said it — heard it in the roses still blooming out of season in the ruined garden two streets away, in the bell-note of ten thousand tokens, in the word that had been waiting in my mouth since I knelt beside a starving seed of light in a root cellar and swore the only oath that had ever felt true:

"A priestess," I said. "The first of the restored covenant. And my testimony comes with a condition, councilors, which you are free to refuse, though I'd think carefully. Refuse it, and you'll be governing a city where magic works and the law says it doesn't, and we both know how long laws like that last. Accept it, and Vharen becomes the first city in a century where the covenant between realms is reforged *by consent of both sides* — the god's, and yours."

Silence. Long enough that I could hear the tokens humming in the strongboxes below, a chord under the floor like bees in a wall.

"What condition?" Halvene asked.

"That you repeal the ban. All of it. That you release every soul in the cells beneath this hall arrested for prophetic correspondence — yes, including the frauds; they lied, they didn't blaspheme, there's a difference, look it up in your own archives. That you try Corvess openly, by law, for what the law says he did — and the law, I remind you, says he imprisoned a god, falsified state records, and ordered the execution of an innocent man. Not that he lost a sword fight. Justice was done to him this morning already, out there, by the crowd, and they were more merciful than he ever taught his hunters to be. Your job is only to write down what actually happened and stop calling it treason."

Halvene studied me for a long moment. Behind her, one of the younger councilors — the clerk's cousin, as it turned out later, though I didn't know it then — was already pulling the Founders' Charter toward herself and reading it with the expression of a woman watching her whole education reorder itself.

"You understand," Halvene said at last, "that if we accept this, the church you're founding will hold more moral authority in this city than this council does within a year."

"Yes," I said. "That's why the covenant requires *both* of us to consent. You'll have veto power over nothing and partnership over everything. It's called trust. My god invented it. You lot buried Him for a century, and He's willing to try again anyway, which should tell you something about the standard you're being measured against."

Someone laughed — the young councilor, quickly smothered. Even Halvene's mouth twitched, a movement like a fault-line shifting.

"The vote," she said, "will take until evening."

"Then I'll be in the garden," I said. "Where else would I be."

* * *

I found Theo sitting on the low wall by the oldest rose tree, in the black ring where the fire had burned and been unburned, and he was — gardening. That was the miracle within the miracle, the one I don't think anyone but me noticed. The city's soldiers had pulled back past the gates, the crowd had thinned to pilgrims and gawkers, the world had remade itself, and my love sat in the ash with a trowel someone had handed him, turning scorched earth over, planting something. Bulbs, I saw when I got close. Little paper-skinned things, labeled in Maren's handwriting: *crocus, autumn-flowering.*

"They'll bloom in six weeks," he said, without looking up. "Right when everyone's decided whether to be afraid of me again. Good timing. Crocus are good at coming up through ash."

"How are your feet?" I asked, because his boots were gone and his bare feet in the burned soil looked wrong, and right, and I needed to say something ordinary before the extraordinary crushed me.

"Rooted," he said, and smiled, and it was his smile, entirely his, the awkward crooked one from the flower stall, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the knock at my door finally let go. "It doesn't hurt. That's the strangest part. It feels like — standing in warm water. Freya." He set down the trowel. "The vote. How bad is it?"

"Halvene hates it. She'll pass it anyway. Politicians can do arithmetic too."

"And Corvess?"

"In a cell. His own cell, as it happens. Ilsabet wouldn't put him anywhere else — said it was fitting, and she was right. He asked for a priest." I sat down next to him, hip to hip, ash and warmth. "He asked for you, actually, at first. They explained the theological complications. Then he asked for me."

Theo went quiet. Below us, in the root cellar under the roses, I could feel it faintly through the stone — the presence that was no longer a prisoner down there but a tide everywhere, up through the roots, through him, through the token at my throat. It had no words yet. Words would come. But it had attention, and its attention right now was on the man in the cell, and it was grieving for him, and I understood that grief for your jailer was going to be the hardest lesson this new covenant ever taught anyone.

"He chose power over faith," Theo said. "Fifteen years ago, same as — no. No, not the same. He chose power *instead* of faith. I almost chose fear instead of love, back in the cells. Everyone gets the choice. He got it and chose wrong, and now he has to live in the country he made." He picked up a bulb, turned it in his fingers. "What will you tell him?"

"The truth. That his god forgives him and his city won't, and both of those are real, and he'll have to carry them together, and that carrying things together is the entire secret of the covenant, and I'm very sorry, because it's heavy."

Theo laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and leaned into me, and we sat like that while the afternoon came gold through the smoke-still air, two criminals on a wall in the garden at the center of a changed world, planting flowers in ashes.

Below the floor of the world, something vast and patient and newly awake turned its attention toward the Hall of Records, where nine gray robes were arguing over a charter, and waited, with the confidence of a thing that had already outlasted a century of darkness, for evening, and the vote, and the dawn after it.

And in my pocket, all unnoticed, a crocus bulb I had stolen from Theo's pile began — very quietly, ahead of schedule, the way all miracles start — to grow.
