## 21. The New Gardener

The morning after the law changed, Theo woke before dawn out of habit, and found that he could feel the sunrise coming through the soles of his feet.

That was the first thing. Not the fire's aftermath, not the crowds, not the council's proclamation being nailed to the market posts — though all of that came, in its time. The first thing was the light moving under the earth like a tide, rolling west to east beneath the garden's roots, and the way the roses turned toward it before the sky itself had decided anything. He lay in Freya's narrow bed — their bed now, though neither had said so aloud yet — and felt a hundred thousand roots drink the dawn at once, and thought: *oh*, and then, because he was still Theo, still a man who got up early and put the kettle on: *the lettuces will need thinning today.*

He said as much over breakfast, and Freya laughed until she had to set down her cup.

"You're half a god," she managed, wiping her eyes. "You can probably will the lettuces thinned."

"I could," Theo agreed. "But that's not how growing works." He tapped the table with one finger, and where it touched, a small green shoot unfurled from the wood grain, blinked its first two leaves, and settled. He looked at it with mild satisfaction, like a man checking that a fence post was level. "Besides. If I do everything by will, what's left for the doing?"

Freya looked at the shoot for a long moment. Then she reached across and touched its leaf with her fingertip, very gently, the way she touched things she didn't quite believe in yet.

"It stayed," she said.

"Of course it stayed. It lives here."

* * *

The garden was unrecognizable, or rather, it was finally recognizable — it had become what it had always been trying to be, in all those years of municipal neglect, when it grew wild and wrong and no one knew why. Now the paths were clean but soft-edged. The hedge maze stood open at every dead end, its walls heavy with fruit that hadn't grown this far north in living memory. And everywhere there were people.

They came at first out of curiosity, then out of grief, then out of need. An old man whose wife had died in the plague winter sat by the pond for three days before he spoke to anyone; on the fourth day he asked Theo if there was anything that grew for sorrow, and Theo showed him the willow cutting he'd been rooting in a jam jar, and the old man wept, and took it home. A girl who'd been born the year the sky went gray — fifteen, sullen, furious at everything — came back week after week without saying a word, until the day she put her hands flat on the soil of bed nine and the soil answered, warm, like a hand pressed against hers, and she cried harder than the old man ever had.

Theo taught them. That was the word people used — *taught* — though what he mostly did was stand beside them while they remembered something their bodies had always known. The old growing-arts weren't spells, exactly. They were conversations. You didn't command a seed; you introduced yourself to it. You didn't force a wound in a tree to close; you apologized to it, sincerely, and meant it, and trees could tell the difference better than people could.

"The trick," he told the Tuesday morning class — eight people kneeling in the dirt of the herb beds, most of them older than him, one of them the former city clerk who'd once bought a luck-charm from Freya and still blushed when he saw either of them — "is that you're not asking the plant to do something for you. You're offering to help it become what it already is."

"That sounds like religion," said the clerk.

"It is, a bit," Theo admitted. "It's also gardening. I've never found the seam between them."

* * *

He was changed. He knew that. Some mornings he caught himself standing too still, too long, listening to conversations happening a mile underground, in languages made of water and mineral and slow patience. His reflection sometimes lagged — a half-second behind, as if the glass were checking which version of him to show. When he slept, he dreamed in root-time, hours passing like seconds, seasons like minutes, and he woke sometimes grieving for winters he hadn't lived and wouldn't.

And there were parts of himself he could feel going quiet. Not dying — settling. The part of him that had been afraid of small spaces. The part that kept score of old insults. The part that had wanted, once, badly, to be someone important. Those rooms in him were filling up with green, gently, like an abandoned house being slowly reclaimed by a garden, and he had decided — deliberately, on a gray afternoon in the second week — to let them.

Because the alternative, Maren had explained, tapping the Codex's final chapter with a dry finger, was what had happened to the last Bridge-candidates the old covenant had tried to make. Vessels. Hollow men shaped by other people's purposes, filled with other people's divinity. The god had refused to be that again. That refusal — that hesitation at the threshold, that mercy — was why Theo still existed as a self at all.

"You chose it freely," Maren had said. "That's the whole of the new covenant, boy. Everything else is commentary."

So he let the unnecessary rooms fill with green. But he kept the ones that mattered. His stubbornness. His fondness for bad jokes. The specific way he liked his tea — strong enough to trot a horse across. Freya said she'd have noticed if he'd lost those; she said she'd been watching for it, quietly, every day since the burning, ready to grieve him even while he stood in front of her alive. He loved her for the watching almost more than for anything else.

* * *

On a bright midmorning in early autumn, the High Council came to the garden.

Not the whole council — four of them, in their gray robes, looking deeply uncomfortable among the bees, led by a young woman named Councillor Ashe who had voted for legalization and clearly expected to be thanked for it. She stood in the herb beds with her boots ruining the soil structure and announced that the Council wished to establish a formal academy of the restored arts, with a charter, and examinations, and a title for Theo — *Warden of the Garden* — and a stipend, and would he care to review the draft document?

Theo listened politely. Then he crouched, pulled a weed, and handed it to her.

"Do you know what this is?"

"It's... a weed, Warden—"

"It's a gallant soldier. It grows where nothing else will grow, breaks up compacted ground with its taproot, and feeds the bees when nothing's blooming. If you'd let me see your charter draft, I'd tell you it calls people like me 'practitioners' and people like my students 'apprentices,' and it puts walls around knowledge that used to grow free as this." He stood, brushing off his knees. "I'll teach anyone who comes. No fees, no exams, no title. The garden doesn't check credentials. Ask the nettles."

Councillor Ashe looked at the weed in her hand. Something moved across her face — not offense; recognition, maybe, of how long it had been since anyone handed her something living instead of a document.

"And if the Council insists?" she asked.

"Then the Council is welcome to come learn with everyone else," Theo said. "Tuesdays and Saturdays. Bring gloves. The roses have opinions about strangers."

She came back the following Saturday. She wore gloves. By winter she could keep a basil plant alive, which everyone agreed was a miracle of a modest but genuine kind.

* * *

Evenings, when the students had gone home and the light went long and gold through the hedge rows, Theo did the rounds.

This was his favorite hour, though he'd never have ranked it out loud, because ranking implied some parts of the garden mattered less, and the garden always knew when you thought that. He walked the beds and checked on things. The willow by the pond, thriving on borrowed grief. Bed nine, where the angry girl — Sefa, her name was Sefa — had planted a rowan tree over the summer, and which had shot up six feet and flowered twice, showing off, the way young trees do when they're loved. The rose tree at the garden's heart, oldest of all, beneath which a door still stood in the dark earth, closed now, at peace, its lock long dissolved into ordinary wood.

At the rose tree he stopped, as he always did, and rested his palm against the bark.

Under his hand, the tree's attention gathered — patient, vast, fond. It was not the god exactly, and it was not not-the-god. Since the Reforging, divinity had distributed itself the way a healthy root system distributes water: everywhere, equally, according to need. The god had not come back from imprisonment so much as come apart into everything, and Theo was the place where the streams rejoined. When he touched the tree he felt the whole garden breathing, and beyond it the whole city, and beyond the city the slow green pulse of the world itself, turning toward its next spring.

"How are we doing?" he asked, which was how he prayed now.

The answer wasn't words. It never was. It was a feeling like sun through leaves, like the first cool hour after a hot day, like being told *well done* by someone who never needed you to do anything at all.

He stayed there until the light failed. Then he went home to Freya.

* * *

Some nights she woke to find him gone from the bed, and would find him in the kitchen, sitting in the dark with his bare feet on the floorboards, just listening. Those nights the underground conversations ran loud — something deep and old moving in the world's sleep, turning over. He never spoke much about those nights. But Freya always came and sat with him, her shoulder against his arm, and once she said:

"When it's too loud — the listening. Does it ever feel like losing yourself?"

Theo considered honestly. That was another thing she'd given him: permission to consider honestly, without managing her worry.

"Sometimes it feels like dissolving," he said. "But here's what I learned from the god, from that seed under the garden. It spent a hundred years starving and it never once stopped being itself. Not smaller. Not less. Just — held. Waiting. So when I feel myself getting... wide..." He flexed his toes against the boards, feeling the roots below, the foundations, the bedrock, the deep aquifer singing its one long note. "...I come back to the edges. My feet. My hands. Your heartbeat through the wall. The kettle. The lettuces." He smiled. "A self isn't a wall, Frey. It's a taproot. It goes down further than you think, and it holds on."

Freya had put her arms around him then, and held on herself, and neither of them had needed to say anything else.

* * *

Winter came, and the garden taught its hardest lesson, which was rest. Theo taught the students to stop. To leave the beds asleep, to prune without greed, to trust the seeds in the cold ground the way you trust a sleeping friend. The angry girl, Sefa, struggled with this more than any of them — she wanted growth, proof, results, something to shove at the world — and in January Theo found her at the gate at dawn, having dug up her rowan's bed to check whether the roots had taken.

"They were taking," he said gently. "Past tense interrupted."

"I needed to know."

"I know. But knowing isn't love, Sefa. Sometimes love is closing the door and letting the dark do its work." He helped her pat the soil back down, and together they apologized to the rowan, sincerely, and meant it, and the tree forgave them — he could feel it, a small green shrug — and in spring it grew three feet and flowered red as an ember, and Sefa laughed for the first time in the year since her brother died, and Theo counted that among the year's true miracles, alongside fire dying mid-roar and charms lighting all at once. Miracles, he was learning, came mostly in sizes small enough to carry.

* * *

It was nearly a full year after the burning — a soft evening at the end of summer, the garden gold and humming, the students gone home, the light thick as honey on the hedge rows — that Theo felt a new arrival at the garden's edge and turned to find a stranger standing at the gate.

Not a stranger to the garden, though. That was the strange thing. The roses along the fence had turned toward the gate as one, the way they turned toward dawn, toward Freya, toward rain after drought — and the bees had gone quiet in a listening sort of way, and the ground under Theo's feet carried a note he had never felt before and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize a voice heard only in dreams.

The man at the gate was old, travel-worn, gray-bearded, with a staff of living wood — actually living, leaves budding along it even now, out of season, out of season entirely — and eyes the color of deep water over stone. He looked at the garden the way a man looks at a house he left fifty years ago. He looked at Theo the way a man looks at a son.

Theo felt the whole rooted world lean in around him, waiting, and heard himself say:

"We're closed for the evening. But for you—" His mouth was dry. "For you, I think the gate's been open a long time."

The old man smiled, and it was a smile Theo knew from mirrors, from dreams, from the first green hour of every morning under the earth.

"I know," he said. "I planted it."
