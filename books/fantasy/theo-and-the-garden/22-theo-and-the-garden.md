## 22. Theo and the Garden

The garden had never looked like this, not in all the years Freya had walked past its rusted gates and pretended not to see what was dying inside them. A year of Theo's hands had done what a century of neglect could not undo. The roses climbed the trellises in ropes of white and deep red. The herb beds ran in silver-green rivers. Even the old municipal fence — once a cage, then an embarrassment — had been woven through with jasmine until it seemed less a barrier than a garland.

And at dusk, on the longest evening of the year, it was full of people.

Not the grandees of Vharen's new council, though a few of those had come, sitting self-consciously on borrowed benches with their ceremonial sashes loosened. Not only the hunters who had laid down their warrants, though some stood at the edges with their caps in their hands, unsure whether they were guests or penitents. Mostly it was everyone else. The clerk she'd sold her first luck-charm to, gray now at the temples, holding his wife's arm. The baker from Copperlane whose bread had risen wrong for fifteen years until last spring. The old woman from the cells — Maren's sister, as it turned out, though that story took a whole winter to untangle — wrapped in shawls despite the warmth. Children everywhere, running down the rows between the beds, and no one telling them to stop.

Freya stood at the garden's heart, beneath the oldest rose tree, and tried to breathe.

She wore blue. She had argued about this for three days — with herself, mostly, since Theo refused to have opinions about anything except whether the hem would catch on the root-door's threshold if she wanted to visit the god before the ceremony. Blue, Maren had insisted. *The color the sky went when the covenant broke. Wear it back.* So: blue, with silver at the collar, and her oath-token hanging openly at her throat where for fifteen years she had hidden it beneath layers of neutral cloth.

The token no longer hung dead and cold. It moved. All day it had been doing a slow thing against her skin, a pulse like a moth turning over in sleep, and every time she felt it her chest tightened with a joy so large it frightened her.

*He's coming,* said the roses.

She heard it the way she heard everything now — not in words exactly, but in pressure, in scent, in the faint green hum that lived under the garden's sounds the way a heartbeat lives under a wrist. The god spoke through growing things, and after sixteen years of silence, Freya had learned the language again the way you relearn a childhood tongue: slowly, clumsily, weeping at odd moments over simple sentences.

*I know,* she answered, with her hand flat against the rose tree's trunk. Its bark was warm. Everything here was warm now, even in the shade, even at dusk. *I know he's coming.*

Music started somewhere near the gates — fiddles, because Vharen's council had offered trumpets and Theo had politely declined trumpets — and the crowd turned, and Freya turned with them, and there he was.

Theo came down the central path between the bean rows wearing his good coat, the one with the mended sleeve because he refused to replace a coat with one more year in it, and he was laughing at something one of the children had shouted at him, and the plants turned to watch him pass.

They always did. It still made her breath catch. Sunflowers rotated their heavy heads. The peas lifted their tendrils off their strings toward him like hands. He had stopped noticing, mostly — or he had noticed so completely that it had become as ordinary to him as walking, which Maren said was the true sign of the Bridge: not spectacle, but integration, two worlds sharing one body without complaint.

He reached her beneath the rose tree and took both her hands, and the crowd's murmur settled into a hush, and the fiddles softened to almost nothing.

"You're crying," Theo said quietly. "Already."

"I'm not."

"Your eyes are doing the thing."

"My eyes are not doing the— " She laughed, wet and helpless. "Fine. They're doing the thing."

"Good," he said. "I was worried I'd have to do it alone."

Maren officiated. She had been defrocked twice — once by the old covenant's collapse, once by the city's edict — and had declared, upon being asked to lead the rite, that she intended to be defrocked by no one ever again, and that anyone who tried would answer to her rolling pin. She stood before them now in vestments she had sewn herself from three different traditions, none of them matching, all of them correct.

"There are old words," Maren said, her voice carrying easily across the beds. "Words from before the ban, before the prison, before the gray sky. Words for joining two lives. But the covenant we keep now is not the old covenant." She looked at Freya, and something passed between them that had taken a year of arguments and reconciliations and one spectacular fistfight over theology to build. "It is not a chain sworn in fear. It is not an oath held over someone. It is a bridge. And bridges are built freely, or they are not built at all."

She nodded to Freya. *Your vow.*

Freya had written hers out seven times and burned six versions. What remained was short.

"I swore an oath once," she said. Her voice shook; she let it. "To a god who could not answer me. I kept it for fifteen years in the dark, and I thought keeping it was killing me, and I thought breaking it was killing me, and I was wrong about both." She squeezed Theo's hands. "What I learned — what *he* taught me, stubborn gardener that he is — is that duty isn't a wall you stand behind. It's a gate you open. So this is my vow. Not obedience. Not sacrifice. I vow to tend what we grow together. To open the gate every morning. To love him—" her voice cracked and she pushed through it, "—to love him not instead of my faith, but as the shape my faith finally takes. That's the whole covenant. That's all of it."

The token at her throat pulsed hard, once, like a struck bell.

Maren turned to Theo.

Theo was quiet for a long moment. The crowd waited. Somewhere in the rows, a child whispered and was hushed. Freya watched him gather himself the way she'd watched him do it a hundred times — before testimony before the council, before the descent beneath the roses, before the fire and the choosing — and she knew what was happening inside him because it happened inside her too: he was translating between realms, finding words that could stand in both.

"I spent my whole life thinking I was ordinary," Theo said at last. "That was the best thing about me. Anyone can grow things. Anyone can be kind on purpose. I thought that was mine — small, and mine." He smiled at her, and the light through the rose canopy caught the change in his eyes, the depth that hadn't been there before, the roots going down through him into ground that wasn't entirely of this world. "Then I found out I was planted. Made. Meant. And it took me a long time to understand that meant nothing unless I agreed to it." His thumb traced her knuckles. "So here's my vow. I choose this. Every day, out loud, I choose it — the garden, the god, the work, and you. Freya Sandoval, I choose you freely, which is the only way anything real has ever grown."

The fiddles came back up, soft. Maren joined their hands and bound them with a cord of braided things — rose stem and wheat stalk and a thread of silver that Freya suspected was spun from actual moonlight, because Maren did not do things halfway.

"By the old words and the new," Maren said, "by root and stock, by earth and light — you are bound. Not chained. *Bridges.* Go on, then."

The crowd came up like weather. There were embraces and shouting and someone — the Copperlane baker, she was nearly certain — began weeping into a tray of honeycakes. The children mobbed Theo immediately, demanding to know if the flowers would sing at the dancing later, and he crouched down and told them, gravely, that the flowers would absolutely not sing, because singing was for crickets, and the flowers would be dancing.

Freya stood in the middle of it with her hand at her throat, on the token, and let the noise wash over her, and thought: *sixteen years ago I prayed into silence, and tonight the whole world is loud.*

* * *

Dusk deepened. Lanterns bloomed along the paths — real ones and also, here and there, small lights that were not lanterns at all but the garden itself glowing faintly at the veins, the way it sometimes did when the god was particularly pleased, which tonight was often. Dancing started on the swept stone by the fountain. The fiddlers played old tunes nobody had been allowed to remember until recently, and the crowd sang verses half-remembered and half-invented, and it didn't matter which half was which.

Freya found Theo eventually at the edge of the celebration, standing where the paths met, watching the people with that expression she'd learned to read in the first weeks: wonder worn like a coat that might not fit.

"You're doing the thing now," she said.

"Hm?"

"The face. The 'is any of this actually happening' face."

"It's a very reasonable face." He pulled her in against his side. Around them, almost absently, a late-blooming clematis opened along the fence post, petal by petal, keeping time with the fiddles. "A year ago I was arrested in your apartment. Two years ago I didn't know you existed. Tonight the entire city is dancing in my workplace."

"Our workplace. You signed the co-tending charter."

"I signed it under duress."

"You signed it while eating the pie I bribed you with."

"That's what makes it duress." He pressed his lips to her hair. When he spoke again, quieter: "Do you ever miss it? The stall. The little charms. Being nobody."

She considered it honestly, because he deserved honesty and always had. "I miss the clerk," she said. "I don't miss hiding. I hid for fifteen years, Theo. In plain sight, in broad daylight, with a smile on — but hidden. I don't miss it." She tilted her head back to look at him. "Do you? Miss being nobody?"

"No." No hesitation at all. "Because I wasn't nobody. Nobody was just the pot. You don't grieve the pot when the plant comes up." He paused. "Although the pot was a very nice pot. Good coat."

"Terrible coat. It has a mended sleeve."

"The mended sleeve is the best part of the coat."

They were still arguing happily about the coat when the garden changed.

It was subtle — most people wouldn't have caught it. The music seemed to soften without stopping. The lantern-light warmed. The air took on the smell of rain that wasn't falling, that deep mineral sweetness of soil after storm, and the hum beneath everything rose a register, the way a string tightens before it speaks. Along the great rose tree, every bloom turned — not toward Theo, not toward the moon, but toward Freya.

Toward the token at her throat.

She went still. "Theo."

"I feel it," he said. "Come on."

They walked away from the dancing, down the path between the beds, past the fountain, to the oldest rose tree at the garden's heart — above the door, above the root cellar, above the small starving seed of light that was not starving anymore. The tree had grown a full ring wider in a single year; Maren said the wood showed it plainly, like a scar healing. Its blooms hung heavy and luminous in the dusk, white shading into a color that had no name before this year and now did, though only gardeners used it.

Freya put her hand on the trunk. The warmth of it came up through her palm, through her wrist, into the place behind her sternum where prayers used to go and die.

"Hello," she said. Her voice came out small. Sixteen years of smallness, all of it rising at once. "I'm here."

The tree breathed.

And then — through the roses, through the sap and the bark and the slow green patience of the thing, through the man standing beside her with his hand covering hers on the trunk, through all of it at once, layered like harmony —

*Freya.*

Her name.

Not pressure. Not scent. Not the language she'd had to relearn syllable by weeping syllable.

Her name, spoken. Spoken in a voice like water finding its old channel, like a door opening after a century, like someone saying a word they had been holding in their mouth for sixteen years because they were afraid that if they let it out too soon they would break it.

*Freya. Little flame. My oathkeeper. My first priestess.*

She did not hear it so much as come apart inside it. Her knees went; Theo caught her, arms around her, and she understood distantly that the voice was coming through him too, that the Bridge carried it, that this was why the god had waited — not because it couldn't speak, but because it had needed a mouth wide enough, a throat of root and man, to say her name the way it deserved to be said: *whole.*

"I'm here," she said again, into Theo's shoulder, into the bark, into the warm breathing dark between them. "I kept it. I kept the oath the whole time. Even when I thought you were gone. Even when I hated you for it. I kept — "

*We know,* said the god, gentle as rain. *We watched. Silence was our sentence, not our choice. You were never unanswered, child. You were unheard. There is a difference, and it is the whole difference, and we are sorry for every year of it.*

"I forgave you," she managed. "Months ago. I just — I didn't know if you knew."

*Now we know that you know that we know,* said the god, and there was something in it that was unmistakably humor, dry and old and fond, and Freya laughed through her tears because of course the god was like this, of course it had always been like this, and grief had simply buried the sound.

Theo's hand moved on her back, slow circles. Through him the presence steadied, localized, became almost a shape beside them — not appearing, exactly, but *attending*, the way a gardener attends a plant coming up.

*You bound yourselves today,* the god said. *By your own words, freely given. Do you understand what you have made?*

"A marriage," Theo said.

*A covenant,* said the god. *The same thing, said twice. One in the tongue of men, one in the tongue of light. This is how it was always meant to work — not oaths wrung from fear, not power hoarded in prisons, but two voices agreeing to carry each other. You have rebuilt in one evening what the city spent a century tearing down. Keep it. Tend it. It will need watering.*

"Everything does," Theo said.

*Yes,* said the god, pleased. *You may keep him, priestess. He understands composting on a spiritual level.*

"He does," Freya said, wrecked and laughing. "He really does."

The presence lingered a while longer — long enough that the dancers far behind them fell quiet one by one and turned toward the tree, sensing something, seeing only two figures beneath the roses and a glow like dawn arriving early. Then it withdrew, unhurried, the way tide withdraws: not gone, just returning to the vastness of itself, leaving the shore changed.

The garden exhaled. The music, after a moment, hesitantly resumed.

* * *

Later — much later, when the lanterns had burned low and the last fiddle had been packed away and the children had been carried home asleep over shoulders — Freya and Theo sat together on the bench beneath the rose tree, her head on his shoulder, his coat around them both. Mended sleeve and all. The moon was up. The garden glowed faintly along its veins, drowsing.

"One year," Theo said.

"Mm."

"Sixteen years of silence, and it ends on our wedding day with a joke about composting."

"It's a good god. It has priorities."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Tomorrow there's the council session. The school charters. Maren wants to argue about the seminary curriculum again, and the northern parishes still won't license hedge-witches, and the High Hunter's old officers are asking questions about reparations." He listed it off gently, wearily, the way you recite a beloved chore. "There's so much work, Freya."

"I know."

"And we'll do it?"

"We'll do it." She turned her face up to him. Above them, a single white rose, out of season, out of reason, opened petal by petal in the moonlight, opening onto the wedding night like a slow green blessing, and neither of them saw it, and it opened anyway. "Together. Every morning. Gate open."

He kissed her forehead. "Gate open."

Far below them, beneath the roots, in the warm dark of the cellar that was no longer a prison, a small seed of light turned over in its sleep — contented, rooted, home — and dreamed, for the first time in a hundred years, of morning.
