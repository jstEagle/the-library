## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The king's supper arrived at the tasting room at half past six, borne on silver by four servants who never looked at one another and never spoke above a whisper, as though the food itself might be listening. Yara Coldharbour sat alone at the long table with a clean spoon, a clean knife, and a glass of watered wine she would not drink until everything else had passed through her mouth first.

She had been doing this work for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned that poison rarely announced itself. It did not come wearing its name. It came disguised as a fondness — an extra pinch of nutmeg because the cook remembered the king liked nutmeg; a sweeter wine because the vintage was praised; honey in the glaze because it was midwinter and midwinter called for comfort. Poison came dressed as kindness. That was the whole trick of it, and that was why the crown paid her more than it paid two of its generals.

Yara bent over the first plate — quail in a dark sauce, roasted chestnuts, bread still warm enough to steam — and began.

* * *

Her tongue had been trained the way other people trained hounds: patiently, relentlessly, and with a great deal of spilled blood. Not hers, mostly. The Guild of Impossible Things kept a cabinet of vials that would have made any assassin weep with envy, and for eleven years Yara had swallowed small measured deaths so that her body would learn their signatures. Arsenic tasted of almonds gone wrong, of a sweetness that soured halfway across the tongue like a friend turning away. Hemlock left a cold numbness at the back of the throat, as though winter had gotten there first. Foxglove was subtler — a faint metallic hum beneath the flavor, easy to miss if you were eating quickly or thinking of something else.

Yara never ate quickly. She never thought of something else.

She took a sliver of the quail breast, let it rest on her tongue, closed her eyes, and listened with the only part of herself that had ever been entirely reliable.

There. Beneath the richness of the sauce, under the browned fat and the thyme, a thread of bitterness that did not belong — green and sharp, like the memory of a plant she'd once chewed on a dare. She spat into the basin, rinsed, and reached for her ledger.

*Monkshood,* she wrote, in the small precise hand that had won her a place at court before anyone knew what else she could do. *Aconite. Second pressing — the bitter note is muted, which suggests dilution in the sauce rather than direct application. Whoever prepared this dish did not add it. Whoever delivered it did.*

She worked methodically down the table. The soup was clean. The bread was clean. The chestnuts carried a dusting of powdered white willow bark — harmless in itself, but a marker; someone had handled them who also handled the willow stores, and willow stores were kept in the same pantry as the dried foxglove. She noted it without alarm. Not every coincidence was a conspiracy. Most of them were just kitchens being kitchens.

But then there was the wine.

She poured a measure, held it to the lamp, swirled it, and stopped. The color was right. The legs on the glass were right. The smell was right — black cherry, old oak, a ghost of violet. It was only when she let it sit against her tongue that she felt it: a texture where no texture should be, a faint silkiness, like water that had been boiled too long or touched with oil.

She held the sip in her mouth and counted. One. Two. Three.

On five, the sweetness turned. Not sour — *wrong*. A sweetness with edges. A sweetness that was trying very hard to be sugar and was not.

Yara spat, rinsed twice, and wrote for a long time before she understood what she was writing:

*Sugar of lead. Old recipe. Slow. This is not tonight's attempt. This is the third month of someone's patience.*

Three poisons in one supper. Three separate hands, most likely, or three separate motives, none of which needed to know about the others. The palace was a garden where everyone grew something in the dark. Her job was not to catch the gardeners. Her job was to stand between the harvest and the king's throat, meal after meal, year after year, and to be so quietly, exhaustively competent that nobody ever thought to wonder what else she might be.

She sent the quail back with a note: *Sauce discarded. Bird served dry.* She sent the wine back with a harsher one. She ate the bread herself, all of it, standing up, because hunger was the one appetite her work permitted and because warm bread was the closest thing to tenderness the tasting room ever offered her.

Then she washed her face, pinned her hair back into its plain court braid, and went home.

* * *

Home was eleven streets and one impossible doorway away.

Anyone following Yara Coldharbour from the palace would have watched her turn down Candlemaker's Row, past the shuttered chandler's, past the wall where ivy had eaten half a painted sign, and stop — apparently — at nothing at all. There was no door there. There was a stretch of brick that everyone in the city walked past every day of their lives without seeing, because the Guild of Impossible Things did not survive on secrecy alone. It survived on being unremarkable. A door that hid itself was worth ten guards.

You had to knock anyway. That was the rule, old as Odile Vane's founding: *the door is always there, but it must be asked.*

Yara knocked three times, paused, twice more. The bricks sighed apart like a book opening, and warmth rolled out to meet her — woodsmoke, onions, tallow, ink. The smell of the guildhall, which was the smell of every home she had never quite had.

"Late," said Bramble from the kitchen doorway. The hedge-witch was stirring something enormous in a pot that had no business fitting in that hearth, and she looked at Yara the way she always did — as though checking her for damage and finding, as usual, nothing she could put a poultice on.

"Three poisons," Yara said, hanging up her cloak. "The king eats dry quail tonight and drinks water."

"Three." Bramble whistled low. "Popular man."

"The opposite. Nobody wants him dead for his own sake. They want him dead for what comes after." Yara crossed the hall toward the back room, where the ledgers lived. "I'll be balancing accounts."

"You always are," Bramble called after her, not unkindly. "Supper's when supper's. Don't make me keep it warm."

Yara did not answer. She never answered that particular invitation directly, and Bramble had long since stopped expecting her to. But she noted, in the private ledger she kept behind her ribs, that supper would be kept warm, and that this was the closest thing to love the guild expressed out loud before nine o'clock.

* * *

The ledger room was small and windowless and perfect. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, holding volumes bound in leather, canvas, sailcloth, one in dragonhide that Odile Vane had acquired under circumstances the guild preferred not to document. Each volume recorded trades. Not goods. *Impossibilities.*

A widow's wish to hear her drowned son laugh again, traded against seven years of the guild's silence regarding a smuggling route. A locked door in a nobleman's house that opened onto a beach, traded against the nobleman's promise to forget he'd ever seen the sea. Debts of gratitude, debts of memory, debts of names. Every line balanced, because that was the law Odile had written into the guild's bones: *nothing given for nothing, nothing taken without payment, and every account, however strange, must close.*

Tonight's work was reconciliation — matching the wish-book against the debt-book, finding the entries where payments had come due. It should have been soothing. Arithmetic for the soul. Yara lit three lamps, opened both books, dipped her pen, and lost herself the way she always did, in columns of other people's miracles.

It was nearly midnight when she noticed it. Not an error. Errors were common and fixable. It was an absence — a page in the oldest volume, near the front, where the ink had faded not with age but with *deliberation*, scraped and rewritten so many times the paper had gone thin as breath. An entry from two hundred years ago that the guild had tried, more than once, to read.

Yara had never been able to make out more than fragments: *...sold... the Court beneath... payment rendered at midwinter...*

She was leaning close to it now, lamplight trembling on the damaged page, when she heard the knock.

Not the guild-door knock — the coded triple-and-double that members used. This was a single knock, small and high, struck from the outside by something with very little strength and a great deal of desperation. Then another. Then a scratching, like fingernails.

Bramble's footsteps thundered down the hall. Yara heard the bricks sigh open, heard the rain that had begun sometime in the evening rush in with the cold, and heard Bramble say, in a voice stripped of all its usual gruffness:

"Well. Come in, little one. Whatever you are."

Yara stood in the doorway of the ledger room with a pen still wet in her hand, and through the gap she saw what the storm had brought them: a child, soaked to the skin, barefoot on the threshold stones, looking up at Bramble with eyes that reflected lamplight the way an animal's do. And when the child opened its mouth, what came out was not any language Yara had ever heard — not the trade-tongue, not the high speech of court, not the dockside cant, not anything with roots she recognized.

It sounded, impossibly, older than all of them.

And in the ledger room behind her, unopened, untouched, the oldest volume fell open on its own to that scraped-thin page — and began, softly, letter by letter, to translate.
