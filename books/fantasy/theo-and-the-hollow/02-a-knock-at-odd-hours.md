## 2. A Knock at Odd Hours

Rain had a way of finding the Guild of Impossible Things before it found anything else in the city. Yara had never decided whether this was coincidence or some property of the building itself — whether the guildhall sat in a pocket where weather pooled like water in a cupped hand — but on nights when the gutters ran silver and the lamplight bent sideways in the wind, she felt certain the house *listened* for storms the way an old dog listens for its master's boots.

The knock came at the hour when even the drunkards had gone home.

Yara was in the counting room with her sleeves pushed to the elbow and the winter ledger open across her knees, working by the light of a single candle that smelled faintly of tallow and rosemary. She heard the knock through three walls and two floors: not loud, not desperate, just *there* — three small raps spaced evenly apart, like someone who had practiced knocking politely before ever having a door to knock upon.

She did not move at once. That was the training. The palace had taught her that first sounds lie; the second sound tells you what the poison is. So she set down her pen, capped the ink, and counted.

The second knock came slower. Weaker. The kind of knocking made by knuckles too small to make a proper fist.

"Someone's at the door," she said to the empty room, because saying it aloud made it ordinary, and ordinary things could be handled.

She took the candle and went down.

* * *

The guildhall was quiet in the way Yara loved best, which was to say it was quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Bramble's hedge-medicines hung from the rafters in bundles of dried nettle and feverfew, ticking softly against one another in the draft. Odile's old chair — empty now these twenty years, kept anyway, because the guild kept everything — sat by the cold hearth like a monument nobody prayed to. The others were scattered through their rooms above: Sef asleep after his shift hauling crates at the river docks, his snoring audible through the floorboards in slow tidal rhythms; Bramble awake, probably, because Bramble slept the way cats did, in installments, and would be up in her workroom steeping something that smelled like lightning about to happen.

Yara unbolted the door herself.

The child standing on the step was small enough that the rain seemed to have chosen it personally. Water sheeted off a hood that wasn't a hood — just a blanket, pinned with a wooden brooch shaped like a fish — and beneath it was a face so thin and still that for one terrible moment Yara thought the knock had been made by something else entirely, some trick of the storm, and this was only a bundle of wet wool left on her doorstep.

Then the child blinked, and the eyes under all that dripping hair were awake and ancient and afraid in equal measure.

"You're alone," Yara said, which was not the question she meant to ask.

The child opened its mouth and answered in a language Yara had no name for.

It was not the gutter-speech of the docks, nor the high court tongue she swallowed poison in every evening, nor any of the merchant dialects Sef swore by, nor the mountain cant Bramble had picked up from smugglers. It came out of the child in a low, unhurried stream, syllables falling like stones into deep water, each one landing somewhere below language and ringing there. And here was the strangest part, the part Yara would turn over in her mind for weeks afterward: the child spoke *quietly*. As if the words were precious. As if they'd been told, once, long ago, that speaking cost something.

"I don't understand you," Yara said gently, crouching to bring her face level. Rain crept under her collar. "But you're not standing out here another minute. Come in."

The child looked past her shoulder, into the hall, at the hanging herbs and the dead hearth and the chair that waited for a woman twenty years gone, and something in that small face *weighed* the room — Yara felt it happen, felt herself and her whole crooked household placed on scales — and then the child nodded once, gravely, and stepped over the threshold as if stepping over centuries.

* * *

Bramble arrived before Yara had finished wringing out the blanket, summoned by nothing Yara could name — though Yara suspected the hedge-witch kept some charm on the door that told her whenever it opened after midnight. She came down the stairs wrapped in a shawl the color of pond scum, gray-haired and sharp-eyed, holding a poker she clearly intended to use as a wand if wand-work became necessary.

"Whose is it?" Bramble asked, by way of greeting.

"It's a child," Yara said, "not a goat."

"Children have owners. That's rather the trouble with children." But Bramble was already kneeling, already pulling off the soaked blanket, already pressing her palm flat against the child's chest the way she did to read a heartbeat like handwriting. "Cold. Not dying-cold. Hungry-cold." She glanced up, and Yara saw the exact moment the wariness drained out of her face, replaced by something older and softer and far more dangerous. "Get the kettle."

"There's soup," Yara said. "From last night."

"There's always soup from last night. That's the miracle of soup."

They fed the child by the hearth once Yara coaxed a fire from the banked coals, and the child ate the way people eat when they have been hungry long enough to be polite about it — slowly, gratefully, watching between bites to see whether the food might be taken back. Sef came down in his nightshirt with his dark curls flattened on one side and stood in the doorway blinking until Bramble threw a spoon at him, and then he sat cross-legged on the floor and began, hopelessly, cheerfully, to try every greeting he knew. Dock-talk. Trader-sign. Two words of the southern ports. A phrase in old church-tongue he claimed meant *peace be on your house* but which Bramble insisted meant *your donkey is beautiful.*

The child watched all of this with grave attention and answered none of it.

"It's not shy," Yara murmured, watching closely. She knew watchers. She was one. This was not the silence of fear or sulkiness; it was the silence of someone conserving something. "Listen — it answers. Every time Sef speaks, there's a pause first, like it's checking whether it wants to spend the breath."

"You're doing the taster thing," Sef said.

"What taster thing?"

"The thing where you stare at something until it confesses."

"I do not—"

"You absolutely do. You stared at the king's wine steward for a full minute last month and he confessed to watering the vintage and possibly to a second marriage."

Despite everything, despite the rain still running off the child's blanket into the rushes, Yara felt her mouth threaten a smile. She killed it professionally. "Its speech. Did either of you recognize it? Truly?"

Sef shook his head. Bramble didn't answer right away. She had gone very still, the way she went still around certain plants — the ones that were beautiful and wrong. "I know what it *isn't*," she said finally. "It isn't any living language I've tasted. And I've tasted most of them; languages sit on the tongue like everything else, if you pay attention. This one's..." She frowned, hunting the word. "*Old.* Not old like grandmothers. Old like bedrock."

"That's not a thing," Sef said.

"Neither are you, most days."

The child finished the soup, set the bowl down with both hands and exquisite care, and then did something that stopped the room. It looked directly at Yara, held up one small finger — *wait* — and pointed at the hallway behind them. At the counting room. At the ledgers.

"No," Yara said.

The child pointed again, insistent, and spoke — that deep-water language, those stones dropping into silence — and this time, as the sound left the child's mouth, something happened to the air in the guildhall.

Later, Yara could never describe it properly. The nearest she ever managed was this: it was like hearing your own name called in a voice you hadn't used since childhood. The sound of the language passed through her ears and kept going, down, down, into the floors of the old house, into the bones of the place — and something answered.

Upstairs, faintly, a book fell open.

* * *

They found the ledger lying on the counting-room table, open to a page Yara had not written.

That was the first impossibility, and she catalogued it with the same cold clarity she brought to a suspicious sauce: she had locked this room. She had the only key. The ledger lay open nonetheless, and the page facing her was blank — except it wasn't, not anymore. Ink was arriving on it. There was no pen near it. The letters surfaced the way frost surfaces on glass: slowly, silently, in a spidery hand that belonged to no member of the guild, alive or otherwise, and Yara recognized the style of it instantly because she had spent six years transcribing the entries of whoever had come before her, and the hand was *hers* — the founder's. Odile Vane's hand, two hundred years dead or fled or worse.

The words, when they finished arriving, were not in any alphabet Yara knew.

"Bramble," she called, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

They crowded around the table — Sef dripping kettle-steam, Bramble clutching her shawl, the child standing on a stool to see, because the child had walked straight to this room without being shown, down halls it had never entered, as if the house had offered it an arm.

And as they watched, the strange letters *changed.* They softened, shifted, re-formed — the way a face re-forms when you stop squinting — and became words in the common trade-tongue. Words in Odile's precise, economical phrasing:

**THE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS REGRETS TO INFORM ITS READERS THAT IT HAS BEEN ASLEEP.**

"Sef," Bramble whispered, "is your kettle boiling over?"

"It does that."

Below the announcement, more lines surfaced, faster now, urgent as a rising tide:

**QUERY ONE: WHAT SPEAKS?**

The child answered aloud. One sentence, low and ringing, stones into water.

The letters on the page trembled — actually trembled, ink shivering like a horse shaking off flies — and then translated:

**IT SAYS: THE DOOR WAS OPEN. I KNOCKED. NO ONE ANSWERED FOR A LONG TIME.**

Silence in the counting room. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs the rain found a loose shingle and worried at it.

"Query two," Yara said, hardly knowing she'd spoken, her taster's instinct taking over — *when faced with an unknown substance, introduce a known one and observe the reaction* — "ask it its name."

Bramble repeated it aloud, awkwardly, pointing at the page, at the child, making a circle of the question with her finger.

The child considered. Yara watched the weighing again, that terrible careful arithmetic behind the eyes — *can I afford this? what will it cost?* — and then the child spoke four syllables, soft as an apology.

The ledger wrote:

**IT HAS NO NAME TO GIVE. IT HAD ONE. IT WAS SOLD BEFORE IT COULD BE TAUGHT TO SAY IT.**

Bramble sat down without meaning to, missing the stool's edge by an inch and catching herself on the table. Sef said a word in dock-talk that would have gotten him thrown out of church twice. And Yara stood very still with her hands flat on the table's edge, reading the line again and again, because her whole life had trained her to detect poison, and this — this sentence, sitting innocently on a page in a dead woman's handwriting — was the most poisonous thing she had ever tasted. It got under the tongue and burned going down. *Sold.* Sold like a jar of preserves. Like a debt recorded in a ledger, which was exactly what it was, which was exactly what this room was *for.*

"Who sold you?" she asked quietly.

The child looked at her. Looked at the page. And the ledger, unbidden, unprompted, wrote a single word in letters so large they pressed hard enough to dent the paper:

**KING.**

* * *

They did not sleep much after that, and none of them pretended to.

Sef built the fire up and dragged blankets down and made the third miracle of soup. Bramble examined the child head to toe by candlelight, muttering her inventory — *no brands, no scars, no collar-mark, no guild tattoo, no temple sigil, teeth good, bones good, heart* — and here she paused, palm flat on the small chest, and her face did something complicated. "Heart sounds like two hearts," she said at last. "Or one heart and an echo waiting to catch up." She looked at Yara over the child's head. "Like a room where furniture's been carried out."

The child fell asleep eventually in Odile's old chair, of all places, curled under dry blankets with its wooden fish-brooch clutched in one fist, and none of them had the heart — or perhaps all of them had exactly the heart — to move it.

Yara sat across from the sleeping child and did not pretend to work.

This was the trouble with competence, she thought. It was excellent armor and it fit perfectly and it weighed nothing at all until the moment you needed to take it off. She had spent eleven years in the palace tasting death in small doses, learning to swallow ruin with a straight face, and it had made her safe and useful and utterly alone, and she had told herself that was the price and she had paid it gladly, and now here was a child asleep in a dead woman's chair wearing a blanket that smelled of rain and feverfew, and the armor developed, without warning, its first crack.

Because she recognized the shape of it. That was the unbearable part. A child sold before it could be taught its own name — carried through the world as payment, as instrument, as *thing* — and arriving, finally, soaked and starving at a door in the worst weather of the year, because it had nowhere else in any kingdom to knock. Yara knew what it was to belong nowhere. She had merely done it with better clothes and a salary.

"We're keeping it," Bramble said. It was not a suggestion. She said it from the doorway, arms crossed, daring the universe.

"We're not *keeping*—" Sef began.

"We are absolutely keeping it. Look at it. It's asleep in Odile's chair. You think that's an accident? The house chose. The house *never* chooses."

"The ledger wrote KING, Bramble. Kings send soldiers. Kings send heads on pikes."

"Kings," Bramble said, "have sent worse to this door, and we're still here."

Yara let them argue. She had turned back to the ledger, to the page where the strange writing had stilled, and she found that one final line had surfaced while they talked, small and patient, in Odile's hand, waiting for her specifically — she was sure of it, sure the way she was sure about arsenic in almond cream:

**IF YOU ARE READING THIS, TASTER, GO TO THE OLD ENTRIES. THE YEAR OF ALDOUS. THE LEDGER REMEMBERS WHAT THE KINGDOM BURIED. START THERE.**

Outside, the rain eased toward dawn, and the mountains east of the city stood black against the graying sky — and for the first time in her life, Yara looked at them and thought she saw, or imagined she saw, or *felt*, a lightlessness beneath them, patient as a debt, waiting for midwinter.

She picked up her pen. She opened the ledgers backward, toward the beginning, two hundred years deep.

The first crack in her armor widened, quietly, all night long.
