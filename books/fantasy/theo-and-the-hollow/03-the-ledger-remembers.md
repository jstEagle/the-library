## 3. The Ledger Remembers

The guildhall kept its own weather.

While the rain outside came down in ordinary sheets, drumming the slate roof and running silver down the leaded windows, inside the hall it fell slower — a fine, patient drizzle of dust motes caught in lamplight, drifting through air that smelled of candlewax, old vellum, and Bramble's nettle tea. Yara had long ago stopped noticing how the building breathed around them, how the rafters settled like a sleeping animal, how the shadows pooled in corners with a kind of intention. She noticed everything else. That was her trade. Noticing was the one thing she had never been able to put down.

She sat at the long ledger table with the child's words written out before her on three sheets of scrap paper, and she did what she always did when something would not make sense: she made a list.

*Sounds like water going backward.*

*No word for "please" that I can find. Many words for "owe."*

*When she says the word I have written as "hollow," my teeth ache.*

That last line had taken her an hour to commit to paper, because writing it made it real, and because Bramble had read over her shoulder and gone quiet in a way that Bramble never went quiet, and quiet from Bramble was worse than any argument. The hedge-witch had simply touched the child's damp hair where she slept — curled now on the window bench under Odile's old traveling cloak, which had appeared from a trunk no one remembered opening, smelling of salt and some year long before any of them — and had said, softly, "She's not speaking nonsense, Yara. She's speaking *first*. Everything else we know is a translation of this."

Yara did not believe in much. Belief was a luxury, like sugar, and she had trained herself out of luxuries early. But she believed in ledgers. Ledgers did not lie; they only waited. And so at midnight, while the others slept — Fenwick snoring in his chair by the hearth with his boots still on, Quill folded up small in the alcove he insisted was not a nest, Bramble wrapped in shawls on the settle — Yara lit a third lamp, unhooked the chain that held the archive drawer shut, and began to look for the child's language in the only place she trusted: two hundred years of impossible bookkeeping.

The Guild of Impossible Things had existed, in one form or another, since before the current dynasty. It had been a smugglers' ring, then a debt-brokers' circle, then briefly (a period the ledgers referred to only as *the Regrettable Fortnight*) an actual licensed business. Through all of it, the rule had held: *everything traded is written down.* Wishes. Debts. Doorways. Names. Years. Once, appallingly, a Tuesday. Yara had inherited the keeping of the books the way she seemed to inherit everything — sideways, unexpectedly, without anyone asking if she wanted it — and in eleven years she had learned their grammar the way other scholars learned dead tongues. She knew which clerk's hand meant famine years. She knew the smell of the ink used during the Interregnum. She knew that whenever an entry began with the phrase *sold in good faith*, someone, somewhere, had almost certainly been robbed.

She worked backward through the centuries, lamp by lamp, hour by hour. The child's speech — those syllables that moved like water going backward, that made teeth ache — appeared nowhere in the modern volumes. Of course it didn't. Languages died upward, toward the courts and the churches first, then trickled down into market-cant and lullabies. To find the source you had to dig below the foundations. So she dug.

Past the reign of the old Queen Maribel, whose entries were written in purple ink and terrible judgment (*traded: one husband's wandering eye; received: peace, temporary*). Past the plague years, when the ledger's margins filled with lists of names and no column for what was received. Past the Long Frost. Her back ached. The lamps burned low and she fed them more oil without looking, the way she'd once fed antidotes to the king without flinching, and the night wore itself thin against the windows.

It was nearly dawn when she found the volume bound in grey calfskin, its spine cracked like river ice, and the entry that changed everything.

It was dated midwinter, two hundred years past. The hand was unfamiliar — not a guild clerk's at all but a courtier's flourish, ornate and insecure, each letter performing for an audience. The ink was brown-black and there was a stain across the lower corner that might have been wine or might have been wax or might have been neither. Yara read it twice. Then a third time, aloud, though the room was empty and the child asleep, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

"*Received in trust, one soul entire, from His Majesty King Aldous I, First of That Name, sold in good faith against debts owed beneath the mountain. Payment deferred to the blood. To be collected at midwinter when the vessel comes of age. Witnessed and sealed. Let none contest what kings have signed.*"

Beneath it, in the older, plainer hand of the guild's registrar of that era — a woman named Sable Marsh, whose entries Yara had always liked for their bluntness — a note had been added:

"*Contested. Refused witness. The king did not sell what was his. He sold what was borrowed. Registrar declines the seal. Filed under grievance, unresolved.*"

Yara sat very still.

The fire had burned down to embers. Somewhere above, a beam ticked as the cold took hold of it. On the window bench, the child stirred and murmured in that backward-water language, and the sound went through Yara like a needle finding the gap between armor plates. Because now she could hear it differently. Now that she knew what to listen for, the syllables resolved — not entirely, not cleanly, but in flashes, the way faces resolve in bad light. Words for *below*. Words for *winter*. Words for *come home*.

Not nonsense. Not a strange tongue from some far province. A summons. Or a countdown.

"No," she said quietly, to the ledger, to the room, to the sensible universe she had always relied upon to stay sensible. "No, that's — that's not how souls work. Souls don't get *sold*. That's not a thing. That's a story grandmothers tell—"

But even as she said it, her memory turned traitor and produced the evidence against her: the way the ledger's pages had begun translating the child's speech on that first night, ink crawling into new words like frost spreading across glass. The way Bramble's charms went silent around the girl instead of merely failing. The way the palace's oldest servants crossed themselves when they passed the sealed door beneath the east wing, and called the space behind it, in whispers, *the hollow under the hill*.

Kings signed things. That was what kings were *for*, in a sense — the guild's ledgers were full of royal signatures, royal debts, royal appetites. Aldous I had wanted something beneath the mountain. Power, longevity, victory in a war whose name Yara couldn't recall — the entry didn't say, because buyers rarely wrote down what they bought. And when the price came due, Aldous had done what kings do. He had reached for the nearest unclaimed thing and called it his.

A soul. Borrowed, Sable Marsh had written. Stolen, she should have written, and perhaps in her heart she had; *grievance, unresolved* was registrar's language for *this is wrong and I cannot fix it*.

Two hundred years. The debt had waited two hundred years, gathering interest the way debts beneath mountains must, until it found its shape: a child, walking out of the rain to the one door in the city that kept accounts of such things. Come to collect itself.

Yara pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes and saw, in the dark behind them, the king's supper from three nights ago. The venison she'd tasted. The wine. The little sugared plum she'd been given afterward as a kindness, sweet as forgiveness on her tongue. She was the crown's taster. She had sworn her body to intercepting harm aimed at the throne, and every immunity she owned had been built for that purpose, and here sat a ledger telling her that the crown itself had committed the oldest crime in her books: taking what was never yours and selling it anyway.

Her whole life, she had served two masters without knowing they were enemies.

"Yara?"

Bramble stood in the doorway to the sleeping rooms, gray-robed, gray-haired, her eyes sharp despite the hour. She looked at the open ledger, at the three scraps of paper covered in Yara's handwriting, at Yara's face. Hedge-witches learned to read faces the way tasters read flavors; it kept them alive.

"You found it," Bramble said. It wasn't a question.

"Aldous the First sold a soul to whatever lives under the mountain. Two hundred years ago. Midwinter collection." Yara's voice stayed level, professional, the voice she used reporting poisons. "The child is the payment. She's come to collect herself."

Bramble came forward slowly and laid her palm flat on the grey calfskin page, the way you'd check a horse for fever. "And the grievance? Sable Marsh contested it."

"She refused the seal. Filed it unresolved. Which means—" Yara stopped. Started again. "Which means the guild has *never accepted* this trade. Legally. By our own law, two hundred years standing. The sale was witnessed as theft and never ratified."

"Then the debt isn't clean," Bramble said. "A dirty debt." Something kindled in her face, fierce and old. "Dirty debts can be *contested*, Yara. They can be broken. If we stand on Sable Marsh's refusal—"

"If we stand anywhere near this, we're standing against a king's signature. Against the crown. Against whatever holds the mountain up." Yara heard her own voice fraying and hated it. "Do you understand what you're proposing? We're a smuggling ring with good handwriting. They're the Hollow Court."

On the window bench, the child woke.

She didn't cry. She sat up in the too-big cloak, hair sleep-tangled, and looked at the two women bent over the ancient book, and then at the ledger itself, and something in her face — that grave, waiting patience Yara had been trying not to think of as *knowing* — shifted. She slid down, padded across cold floorboards on bare feet, and climbed onto the bench beside Yara with the unselfconscious certainty of someone who has already decided where she belongs.

Then she reached out one small finger and touched the entry, on the exact line where *sold in good faith* was written, and spoke.

The syllables moved like water going backward. Yara's teeth ached. But the ledger — the grey calfskin ledger, two hundred years closed — answered. Ink stirred beneath the vellum like fish under ice, and new words surfaced in the margin in Sable Marsh's blunt, honest hand:

*"She asks: is it true?"*

"Yes," Yara said, and her voice broke on it, finally, professionally, irrecoverably. She took the child's hand — small, cold, rain-washed even now — and held it. "Yes, it's true. And no, it isn't right. And I keep the books, little one, and I am telling you: this account does not close at midwinter. I don't care what king signed it."

The child studied her with eyes the color of the deep places of things. Then she leaned her head against Yara's shoulder, trusting, heavy, warm, and Yara sat rigid for three full heartbeats before her arm came up around the girl entirely of its own accord, betraying eleven years of careful armor, holding on.

Above them, dawn came gray against the windows. Below them, somewhere far beneath the city, beneath the mountain, something old felt the ledger move and began, patiently, to count the days.

Bramble watched them both, and her voice, when it came, was quiet as banked coals.

"There's someone we need to talk about," she said. "Someone who knew Aldous. Someone who might know why Sable Marsh filed that grievance and never came back to close it."

Yara looked up. "Who?"

"The founder," Bramble said. "Odile Vane."

* * *

They told the others over breakfast, which was to say over Bramble's nettle tea and the heel of yesterday's loaf, and Yara laid it out the only way she knew how: precisely, in order, with sources cited. Fenwick drained his cup and said nothing for a long moment, turning his spoon over and over, a man measuring a distance he didn't want to walk. Quill unfolded from the alcove, listened, and then wrote on his slate: *what happens to her if we do nothing?*

Yara looked at the child — asleep again now, impossibly, curled against the ledger itself as if the book were a warm animal — and answered honestly.

"The entry says collected at midwinter. Nine weeks. Whatever comes for her comes for her whole. Soul, self, name. There won't be a girl left to save."

Quill wiped the slate and wrote: *then we don't do nothing.*

Fenwick set down his spoon at last. "You're talking about contesting a royal contract against the Hollow Court with a household of five and a hedge-witch's charms." He rubbed his jaw. "We'll need help we don't have. Knowledge. Someone who knows how these contracts are built. How they break."

"That," said Bramble, "is rather the point of Odile Vane."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Yara heard it fall a long way. She had seen the signature in the oldest volumes — *O.V., founder & keeper* — and she had asked about it exactly once, years ago, and Bramble had looked at her with an expression Yara had since learned to translate as *not yet, and don't ask twice*. Now Bramble rose, went to the great chest beneath the stairs, and drew out a map so old its borders described kingdoms that no longer existed. Her finger found the coast. A promontory. A mark like a small star.

"The Lighthouse at Greywater Point," Bramble said. "Frozen time, frozen light, one occupant, exiled by her own request and the guild's greatest shame. She drafted half the contract-law the Court uses. If anyone alive knows how a sale like this can be undone — it's her."

"She'll want a price," Fenwick said grimly. "Founders always do."

"Everything costs," Yara said quietly. She looked down at the child, at the small hand curled against the grey calfskin, at the margin where Sable Marsh's ghost-ink had written *is it true* — and felt the dread in her chest turn over, slowly, like a key in a lock, and become something harder and more useful. "I've spent my whole life paying for other people's safety with my own body. One more price won't kill me."

She closed the ledger gently, so as not to wake the girl.

"We leave tomorrow," Yara said. "For Greywater Point."

Outside, the ordinary rain went on falling. Inside, in the guildhall that kept its own weather, the first lamp of the new day was lit — and far beneath the mountain, something counted one day fewer.
