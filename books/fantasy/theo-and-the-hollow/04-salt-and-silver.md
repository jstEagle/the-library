## 4. Salt and Silver

The guildhall smelled of rain and burnt honey, and Bramble hated both.

The rain came in through the roof she'd been meaning to fix since the thaw, dripping into the copper pot she'd set beneath the worst of it — *plink, plink, plink* — a rhythm that had kept her awake half the night and put her in a temper with the world entire. The burnt honey was Yara's fault. The taster had tried to make a soothing tea for the child and had scorched it twice, which for a woman who could taste a grain of arsenic in a barrel of wine was either exhaustion or distraction, and Bramble suspected it was both.

She stood at her workbench crushing dried nettle with the flat of a knife, not because the child needed nettle but because crushing things helped her think, and she needed very much to think, because the guild was about to do something stupid, and she intended to stop them, or to make certain they did it properly. With Bramble, those were often the same argument.

Around the long table sat what passed for the Guild of Impossible Things: Yara Coldharbour, still in her palace grays, hands wrapped around a cup of tea she hadn't touched; Fenwick, the locksmith, turning a brass gear over and over in his fingers the way he did when arithmetic was happening behind his eyes; Marisol, who read fortunes badly but lied beautifully, braiding and unbraiding the same strand of her hair; old Tobin, who had once been a hangman and never spoke of it, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening; and Odile's empty chair at the head of the table, which no one ever moved and everyone always saw.

And on a pallet by the fire, wrapped in three blankets that Bramble herself had fetched down from the cedar chest, lay the child.

It hadn't spoken since it arrived. It didn't weep, either, which was worse. It simply watched the fire with eyes the color of deep water under ice, and breathed, and occasionally — when it thought no one was looking — pressed one small hand flat against its own chest, as if checking whether something were still there.

Bramble knew that gesture. She'd seen it on soldiers coming back from the wars, on widows in the first weeks, on a hedge-witch's own face in a cracked mirror forty years ago, the night her mother sold her winter coat and her birthright both and called it provision.

"It's simple," Fenwick said, laying the brass gear down with a decisive click. "The child is royal property, or near enough. Some ancestor of the current king signed away whatever-it-is that the child carries. There will be a reward for its return. A substantial one. We are owed eleven months' rent on this building, the roof leaks, and my spectacles are held together with wire and prayer."

"We are not selling a child," Marisol said flatly.

"I said *returning*," Fenwick protested. "There's a distinction. Returning implies the thing belongs somewhere. Selling implies—"

"You'd be delivering it to the Hollow Court with a bow tied round it," Tobin said quietly, without looking up from his knife. "That's all returning means, in this case."

Silence, except for the drip of rain in the copper pot and the wet hiss of the fire.

Yara spoke at last, and her voice was careful the way a taster's voice is careful, each word tested before swallowing. "The ledger says the debt comes due at midwinter. That's nine days away. If we return the child to the palace, the crown takes it into custody, priests get involved, someone writes a proclamation, and at midwinter the Court collects anyway — except now there'll be guards around the child and paperwork, and we'll have spent our one advantage." She turned the untouched cup a quarter-turn. "If we keep the child, we're harboring what the mountain considers its property. And the mountain has a very long memory and no need of doors."

"So we're choosing," Marisol said, "between being poor and being dead."

"We're always choosing between being poor and being dead," Tobin said. "That's what a guild *is*. Tonight we're only choosing how fast."

All eyes went, eventually, the way eyes always did, to the head of the table. To the chair where Odile Vane should have been sitting, exiled these twenty years to a lighthouse full of frozen time, reachable only by letters that took a season and answers that took a riddle. The chair stayed empty. The question stayed unanswered: *what would the founder do?*

Bramble set down her knife.

"Let me tell you about trade laws," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she meant, gravel and woodsmoke. "Since everyone at this table keeps saying *laws* like they're weather. Like they fall out of the sky and you just dress for them."

Fenwick sighed. "Bramble—"

"The old laws." She held up one knotted finger. "Law the first: nothing freely given may be taken back. Law the second: nothing taken may be kept without consent. Law the third—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she pushed through it, "—law the third: a sale made in bad faith is no sale at all. It's theft wearing a receipt."

"And you know these laws are true?" Fenwick asked, gentle now, because beneath his miserliness he was the kindest arithmetician alive, "or do you know them because your grandmother sang them while she brewed?"

"I know them because they're *true*," Bramble snapped, "the way gravity's true. You don't have to believe in the second law for it to apply. Ask any ghost. Ask any changeling. Ask—" and here she stopped, because she had been about to say *ask me*, and she had never told any of them, not in fifteen years of sharing this leaking roof, and she found she couldn't start now, not with the child watching her with those drowned-deep eyes.

So instead she crossed the room and crouched by the pallet, joints cracking like green kindling, and looked at the child at eye level, hedge-witch to small stranger.

"What's your name?" she asked, though she knew the answer would be silence, or worse than silence.

The child's mouth opened. Nothing came out — Bramble felt it in her teeth, a pressure like a word swallowed whole. But the child raised one hand and touched two fingers to its chest again. That same gesture. *Is it still there?*

"No," Bramble said softly. "I don't suppose it is."

She turned back to the table, and something in her had finished deciding, the way water finishes deciding when the dam cracks.

"Here's what I know," she said. "Two hundred years ago, some king in this palace got hungry or frightened or greedy, and he sold something that wasn't his to sell. Not his soul — kings never sell their own, that's the whole art of kingship. He sold a child's. Some other mother's child, some other family's winter. And the mountain has been holding that soul in a jar ever since, waiting for it to grow enough to walk itself down and be collected." She jabbed a finger at the pallet. "That's what's lying there. Not a guest. Not a reward. A *payment*, come to collect itself. And every one of you knows — *knows*, down where you keep your trade-law bones — that the sale was rotten. Because no mother sells her child's soul willingly. Whatever Aldous offered or threatened, it was bad faith, and bad faith voids the third law, and if the third law falls then the first and second go with it, and then there's nothing left of the world but kings and ledgers."

"That's poetry, not proof," Fenwick said, but weakly, and he picked the gear back up, which meant his arithmetic had changed.

"It's law," Bramble said. "Which is better than proof. Proof you can argue with."

Yara set down her cup. "Say we contest the sale. Say we march to a court beneath a mountain with a two-hundred-year-old contract and demand an audit. What collateral? The Court doesn't deal in apologies. It deals in equivalent value. What do we hold that equals a soul?"

And there it was. The real question, dressed up in practicality. Bramble looked around the table: a locksmith whose hands shook; a fortune-teller whose gift was mostly nerve; a hangman trying to forget his; a poison-taster who tasted everything and belonged nowhere; a hedge-witch with more scars than remedies.

Nothing. They held nothing. That was the whole truth of the Guild of Impossible Things — they traded in what nobody wanted, wishes and debts and doorways, because it was the only currency they could afford.

But the child had chosen their door. Out of every doorway in a city of ten thousand doorways, in the rain, at odd hours, mute and hollowed, it had knocked on theirs. And Bramble had spent fifteen years telling herself the guild was a business, a convenience, a waystation — anything but what it actually was.

"Collateral," she repeated, and her voice steadied, and to her own surprise she heard in it not the hedge-witch but her grandmother, singing over the brew-pot. "You want to know what we hold that equals a soul? Look at the pallet. Look at the fire. Look at the leaky roof I've been promising to fix. We hold a place where somebody knocked and somebody answered. That's not nothing. That's the rarest thing minted anywhere, above ground or below. And if the Court wants equivalent value—" she lifted her chin, daring them, daring the mountain, daring the dead king in his sealed tomb, "—then it can learn what a family costs. Because we're not paying in coin. We're paying in the only thing we've got."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. The rain dripped its patient count into the copper pot.

Then Tobin laid his knife down, flat on the table, hilt toward the child. An old hangman's gesture, Bramble realized, or perhaps its opposite — a surrender of the blade.

"I'm in," he said.

Marisol let her braid fall undone and stood. "Obviously."

Fenwick looked at his brass gear for a long moment, then at the child, then at his wire-and-prayer spectacles, and groaned from the soles of his boots. "We'll need money regardless," he muttered. "Contesting a sale requires travel, supplies, and bribes. Someone must write to Odile. Someone must research the original contract. And someone—" he pointed at Yara, betrayed, "—must keep serving the king's supper and smiling about it."

"I can do that," Yara said. Something flickered across her face — dread, resolve, Bramble couldn't separate them and suspected Yara couldn't either. "I've been tasting other people's intentions for years. One more course."

The child, wrapped in its three blankets, had gone very still. Then, slowly, it raised its hand — not to its chest this time, but outward, toward the table, toward all of them, palm open. An offering. Or a question. Or, Bramble thought with a tightening in her throat, the oldest wordless word there was: *mine too?*

"Yes," Bramble said aloud, before anyone could vote on it. "Yes, little one. Yours too."

Later, much later, when the others had gone to their beds and the fire had burned down to a red contemplation of itself, Bramble sat alone with the ledger open before her. The book that recorded wishes granted and debts incurred and doors opened. She dipped her pen, hesitated, and wrote in her crooked grandmother's hand:

*Item: one child's soul, stolen by a king, contested by a guild. Payment pending. Terms: everything.*

She blotted it, closed the book, and pressed her palm flat against the cover — that same gesture, she realized, that the child kept making. Checking whether something was still there.

Something was. For the first time in years, Bramble fell asleep without counting what she might lose.

She woke at dawn to knocking — not at the guild door, but from inside the ledger itself, faint and rhythmic, like a fist against the far side of a wall. And when she opened the book, the ink of last night's entry was moving, rearranging itself into words she hadn't written:

*Received: one inquiry. Sender: the Hollow Regent. Message follows.*

Bramble's blood went cold as well-water. The mountain, it seemed, had heard them too.

And it had written back.
