## 5. What the Court Owes

The lighthouse stood at the end of the world, which was to say: at the end of a road that only existed on Thursdays.

Yara had learned this from the ledger itself. Page nine hundred and forty-one, in Odile Vane's own cramped hand, ink gone the brown of old blood: *To find the Lantern of Vesper's Reach, walk north from the drowned mill until the road forgets your name. It will remember again when you need it to.* Below that, smaller, almost an afterthought: *Bring bread. She will be hungry. Time does not feed you.*

So Yara walked north from the drowned mill on a Thursday, with a loaf of dark rye wrapped in her shawl and a copy of the child's page folded against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

The road did exactly as promised. For the first hour it was a proper road — cart-ruts, hedgerows, a shrine to some minor saint of travelers whose face had been weathered into anonymity. Then, somewhere past the third milestone, Yara realized she could no longer recall her mother's voice. Not the words her mother had said, not even the sound of them — just the fact of having had a mother at all, a fact growing fainter with each step, like a lamp carried away down a long corridor. Her own name went next. She knew she was walking, knew the rye was warm through the cloth, knew she was *Yara* the way one knows a thing read in a book rather than lived. The hedges grayed. The milestones lost their numbers. And still the road went on, patient as a debt.

It was, she reflected, not so different from the palace. There too she was known by function rather than name — *the taster, send for the taster, has the taster eaten yet?* — and there too she had made a kind of peace with being unremembered as long as she was useful. But the guildhall had begun teaching her something else these past two weeks. Bramble called her by name. Socks-the-cat slept on her ledgers. The child — the mute, strange, silver-eyed child who watched everything and weighed it against some private scale — had taken, without ceremony, to sitting beside her at supper, shoulder just touching hers, the way one sits near a fire.

Yara found she did not want to be forgotten by the road. That was new. That was dangerous.

She held the thought like a coal in both hands and kept walking.

* * *

The lighthouse appeared all at once, the way things appear when they have been waiting for permission.

It rose from a spit of black rock surrounded not by sea but by grass — grass gone silver with frost in every direction, frost that did not melt though the sun stood high and fat above the horizon. The tower itself was white stone streaked with salt that could not have come from any nearby water, and its lantern room at the top glowed with a light that was somehow the wrong color: not gold, not white, but the pale shade of a memory you can't quite place. Around its base, frozen mid-fall like flies in amber, hung a hundred snowflakes. They had been falling, Yara understood, for two hundred years, and would fall for two hundred more, and would never once land.

At the foot of the door sat a woman made of clockwork and patience.

No — not a woman. A figure the size of a woman, assembled from brass and bone and what looked suspiciously like the inner workings of eleven different clocks, seated on a stool, holding a fishing rod over dry grass. Its head turned toward her with a sound like a house settling.

"Visitors," it said, in a voice of wind through wires. "We don't get visitors. We get debts."

"I've come about a debt," Yara said. "Two hundred years old. I'm told the person who wrote it lives here."

The figure considered this. Somewhere inside its chest, a gear turned over with the deliberate slowness of a very old decision. "Bread?" it asked.

She unwrapped the rye. The smell of it — yeast and caraway and the particular warmth of a thing baked that morning by Bramble's impatient hands — cut through the frost-silence like a knife through paper. The clockwork figure took the loaf in fingers of jointed brass, held it to whatever served it for a nose, and sighed, if gears could be said to sigh.

"She'll see you," it said. "She always sees people who bring bread. It's the only currency time respects." It opened the door. From somewhere far up inside the tower came a light, and a laugh, and a voice like a bell rung underwater:

"Is it rye? If it's rye, I'll allow the interruption. If it's that sawdust wheat nonsense, tell them to come back in a century."

"It's rye," Yara called up.

"Then climb, girl. Two hundred steps. Count them. It passes the time, and I haven't had any time to pass in ages."

* * *

Odile Vane was nothing like Yara had imagined, which was remarkable, because Yara had imagined her constantly since reading the founder's entries in the ledger — imagining being most of what the guild did with its history. In the margins of the oldest pages, Odile was a storm: a woman who had traded in impossibilities before the guild had walls or rules, who had written contracts with the precision of scripture and the ruthlessness of usury, who had been exiled by her own guild for a crime the ledgers recorded only as *the Vane Error*, three words in red ink, followed by a blank space where the explanation should be.

The woman in the lantern room was small, silver-haired, sharp-faced, and currently lying on her back on the floor with her boots against the wall, reading a book upside down. The frozen light of the lantern pooled around her like spilled honey. Books were stacked everywhere — on shelves, on sills, in towers of their own engineering — and among them, incongruously, grew flowers. Real ones, blooming, though nothing here had weather enough to grow anything. Yara recognized the species with a start: they were the same impossible frost-flowers that bloomed outside the guildhall door in midwinter, the ones Bramble swore couldn't exist.

"You're counting wrong," Odile said, without looking up. "You skipped sixty-three. Everyone skips sixty-three. Sixty-three is a step with opinions." She rolled onto her elbows and looked at Yara properly, and her eyes were the exact gray of the first entry in the guild ledger, the ink faded but unmistakable. "Well," she said. "A Coldharbour. Or near enough — I know the jaw. Your grandmother cheated me at cards in '41. Sit down before my hospitality remembers how to leave."

"You've been expecting someone?"

"I've been expecting *you* for two hundred years, give or take the years I've stopped bothering to count. That's the trouble with frozen time. Everything arrives eventually, and it all arrives at once." She sat up, took the bread when Yara offered it, and broke it open. Steam curled out — real steam, in a place where snowflakes never landed — and Odile breathed it in with her eyes closed like a woman remembering rain. "Tell me about the child."

Yara hadn't said a word about any child.

"You flinched," Odile said, mouth full. "Two hundred years alone teaches you to read flinches. Also, the door told me. Also also, the frost-flowers have been blooming black for a fortnight, and they only do that when a Hollow debt comes due. So. The child."

And because there was nothing else to do — because the road had already taken her name once today and given it back grudgingly — Yara sat down on a stack of books titled in languages she didn't know and unfolded the page she'd copied from the ledger, and told Odile Vane everything.

The child at the door in the rain. The language older than kingdoms. The ledger translating it, letter by letter, until the truth lay bare on the page: *sold, sold, sold to the Court below, payment for a king's wish, collection at midwinter.* King Aldous the First. Two hundred years ago. A soul signed away in a contract written — she had checked the hand four times, five times, until her eyes ached — in a script Yara now recognized from the spines of every book in this room.

"The Vane Error," Yara said quietly, when she finished. "The blank space in the ledger. Is this it? Is this what got you exiled?"

Odile had gone very still. Stillness, in a room where nothing moved for two centuries, was its own kind of violence.

"No," she said at last. "But it's adjacent. Hand me that book. The green one. Mind the flower."

The green book was heavy as sin. Odile opened it across her knees to a page dense with contract-script, and there it was — the same flowing hand, the same flourished capitals — a fair copy of the sale Yara had found in the ledger, every clause intact, including the ones the guild's copy had somehow blurred or smudged or simply failed to hold.

"Aldous came to me," Odile said, tracing the lines without touching them. "Young king, new crown, a war he was losing, a prophecy he'd been fed by men who wanted his gold. He wished to win. Wishes are cheap; the paying isn't. I brokered him a bargain with the Hollow Court — their terms, not mine. I wrote the contract because writing contracts is what I do, and because I was younger then and believed the Court would keep to its side of the paper." Her finger stopped on a line near the bottom. "They didn't. Neither, in the end, did he. And when the guild found out what I'd set in motion, they put me on a boat and told me to keep sailing until the world ran out. This lighthouse caught me. Or I caught it. The paperwork was never entirely clear."

"What did Aldous do?"

"He paid," Odile said simply. "That's the horror of it. Kings always pay. It's everyone else who gets collected." She looked up, and for a moment the flippancy fell away from her face like a mask slipping its string, and underneath was two hundred years of watching snow that never landed. "The child's soul wasn't sold by its parents, if that's what the ledger says. Read the fourth clause again. *Payment tendered in the form most convenient to the Crown.* Convenient. Meaning: whoever was cheapest to take. Meaning some family in the shadow of the palace, two hundred years ago, handed over a baby's soul because a king's men told them it was taxes. Meaning the child walking around your guildhall right now has been hollow for longer than either of us has been alive, and the Court has come to collect what was always theirs by theft, dressed up as trade."

The lantern light seemed colder. Outside, Yara noticed, one of the frozen snowflakes had trembled — a single shudder, like a sleeper turning over.

"So you'll help us," Yara said. "Contest the sale. Find a flaw."

"Oh, I'll help." Odile closed the green book with a soft, terrible finality. "I've had two hundred years to study that contract, girl. I know it better than the Court does. I know where it bends. I might even know where it breaks. But I'm exiled, which means the guild's laws forbid me to lift a finger for you unless the price is agreed *before* aid is rendered. Founder's rules. I wrote them myself, back when I trusted myself more than I do now." She smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile, but it was honest down to the roots. "So. Name a price, and I'll serve it, whatever it turns out to be. Or refuse, and climb back down your two hundred steps, and explain to a hollow-eyed child why the one person alive who could save her chose her ledgers over her life."

"That's not a fair choice."

"Nothing worth choosing ever is." Odile spread her hands, brass-bound, ink-stained, empty. "Price unnamed. Take it or leave it."

Yara thought of the guildhall. Of Bramble's argument still ringing off the rafters — *we don't sell children, we don't sell anyone, that's the whole entire point of us* — and of Socks asleep on the warm ledger, and of the child's shoulder pressed to hers at supper, trusting without words because words had been taken. She thought of the palace too, because she could not help it; she was Yara Coldharbour, and the palace had her tongue and her table and her oath. She thought of the king's supper tonight, three poisons catalogued and swallowed, her body a fortress built one betrayal at a time.

Her duty said go home. Say nothing. Let the ledger balance itself.

The ledger, she suspected, had other plans.

"Unnamed price," Yara said. "Agreed. On one condition of my own: when you name it, you name it to my face, and you let me decide what it costs. No surprises paid out of my pocket while I sleep."

"Spoken like a merchant," Odile said, delighted. "Or a poisoner. Same skillset." She spat in her palm — a gesture two hundred years out of practice, performed with visible relish — and held it out. "Deal, Yara Coldharbour. Now sit back down, because we have perhaps six weeks until midwinter, and I intend to spend every one of them teaching you how a contract dies."

Yara shook the offered hand. It was warm. That surprised her more than anything else in the frozen tower — that after two centuries, Odile Vane's hand was still warm as bread.

* * *

They worked until the lantern light shifted — though it did not truly shift, only seemed to, the way a held note seems to change if you listen long enough. Odile talked and Yara wrote, filling page after page of her notebook with clauses and counter-clauses, with the anatomy of the Hollow Court's law: *all sales final; all payments equivalent; all debts collectible at term.*

"There," Odile said, tapping one line. "*Equivalent payment.* The Court cannot simply take the soul back and call it square. If the original sale is voided — if we prove Aldous stole what he sold — the contract unravels. But unraveling wants a seal. Contracts don't die clean, girl. They want a last payment to close the wound, something of true weight, freely given." She glanced up, gray eyes catching the pale light. "When the time comes, you may need to give something you cannot buy back. Think about what you can afford to lose. Truly afford. Most people never ask themselves that question honestly, and it shows terribly at the bargaining table."

Yara walked down the two hundred steps in the false dusk, counting carefully, not skipping sixty-three. At the bottom, the clockwork fisherman had finished the rye and was winding itself, gear by gear, back into stillness.

"Tell the flowers to write," Odile called down from above, her voice thin with distance and thick with something else. "And girl — when you get home, check your immunities. All of them. Write down everything you've ever swallowed and what it should have done to you. You'll want the list complete."

"Why?"

The pause stretched, and somewhere in the frozen air a snowflake fell — actually fell, one inch, the first motion in two hundred years — before catching itself and hanging again.

"Because the Hollow Court collects debts," Odile said, "and you, Yara Coldharbour, have spent twenty years swallowing things that should have killed you. Do you really think nobody's been keeping count?"

The door closed behind her. The road remembered her name on the walk back, giving it back piece by piece — *Yara, Coldharbour, taster* — and she carried each word home like coins recovered from a well.

But it was the last thing Odile had said that she turned over all night, sitting awake in the guildhall while the child slept and the frost-flowers at the door bloomed slowly black: *check your immunities. Write it all down.*

Because Yara had tasted the king's supper that morning. Three poisons, catalogued, swallowed, survived.

And for the first time in her careful, armored, competent life, she wondered who else might be counting.
