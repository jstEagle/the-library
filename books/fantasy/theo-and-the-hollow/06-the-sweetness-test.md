## 6. The Sweetness Test

The palace kitchens smelled of ambition and burnt sugar, and Yara Coldharbour walked through them the way a fisherman walks a dock — familiar, unbothered, always half-listening to the water beneath.

She had been back at her post for four days. Four days of tasting the king's supper course by course, of standing in the tasting room with its copper spoons and its little glass bells, of performing the small theater of her office: the pause, the swallow, the counted breath, the nod that sent a dish up the serpentine stair to the royal table. Four days of pretending her mind was not two miles away and one story down, in a guildhall that smelled of ink and hedge-magic, where a mute child slept in a nest of quilts and a ledger wrote its own translations in the margins.

She was good at pretending. She had been practicing it for nineteen years.

"Master Coldharbour." The under-cook, Pellam, hovered at her elbow with a silver salver. "The marchpane course. His Majesty requested it himself. Almond paste, rosewater, candied violets."

Yara took the proffered sliver on her tongue and did the work her tongue had been trained to do since she was nine years old and an orphan with an unusual palate and no family to object to the crown's use of her. Almond, bitter at the edges — correctly bitter, the mark of true kernels, not the safe sweetness of imitation. Rosewater, distilled twice. Violet, crystallized in sugar so fine it crackled. And beneath it all, threaded through it all, the faint metallic whisper of — she held still, eyes closed, breath shallow — nothing. A ghost of nothing. A nervous cook's overuse of bitter-almond essence, perhaps, or simply her own imagination, stretched thin by a week of impossible things.

She swallowed. She counted to sixty. She nodded.

Pellam exhaled and bore the marchpane away like a man carrying a sleeping infant.

Yara remained at the tasting table a moment longer, her hands flat on the cool wood. That was the trouble, she thought. That was the whole trouble. For nineteen years the poisons had been the easy part. Arsenic sang to her like a struck glass. Belladonna sat on the tongue with the weight of a coin. Wolfsbane, hemlock, the seven quiet cousins of nightshade — she knew them all the way a mother knows footsteps on a stair. Her body had learned to refuse them, one small swallowed death at a time, until she carried in her blood a library of every poison the kingdom's enemies had ever thought to brew.

But sweetness — sweetness had never been a danger. Sweetness had never required her armor at all.

She had tasted the marchpane, and it had been *lovely*, and for four days that had been the only thing in her life that worked the way it was supposed to.

She was wrong about that, though she did not know it yet. She would know it by nightfall.

* * *

The courtier's name was Ser Lysander Quill, and he had the particular gift of men who had risen high without ever seeming to climb: he was simply always *there*, at shoulder height, holding a cup of wine no one had seen him take.

Yara noticed him properly on the fourth day, in the gallery outside the tasting room, where she had stopped to speak with the keeper of the king's cellars. Ser Lysander stood by the tall window with the light behind him, so that his face was a courteous shadow.

"Master Coldharbour." A bow of exactly the correct depth. "They say you can taste a lie in a broth."

"They say a great many things about the tasting room. Most of them invented by people who want the job."

"And do you enjoy it? The work?"

"I enjoy being alive. The work is how that's arranged."

He smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "You were away, recently. A week, was it? Family illness?"

"I serve at the king's pleasure. When the king's pleasure includes leave, I take it."

"Of course." He turned his cup slowly in his fingers. "It's only that Master Ferrow in the chancery swears he saw you on the Saltmarket road, walking away from the city, at dusk. Alone. In the rain." The smile stayed exactly where it was. "A strange road for a leave-taking. Most people leave by the gates."

Yara felt the old cold move through her, the cold that had nothing to do with poison and everything to do with being watched. The Saltmarket road. She had walked it at dusk, in the rain, the night the child came — had walked it and then stepped *sideways*, through the unremarkable door that was not on any map, into the guildhall where the ledger had begun to translate a language older than kingdoms. She had been careful. She had thought she had been careful.

"Master Ferrow," she said, "drinks. Kindly, but thoroughly. The Saltmarket road out of the city is the road to the salt-works, Ser Lysander, and the salt-works are where one goes to inspect the king's salt, which is, I believe, my department, since poison and salt have a long and unhappy history together."

"Of course they do." He raised his cup to her, an inch. "I only mention it because I've taken an interest in you, Master Coldharbour. In your *health*. A taster's body is a remarkable thing, they say. Nineteen years of swallowed poison. There are those who say there's nothing like it left in the world."

"There are those who say a great many things."

"Mm." He drank, unhurried. "Well. Long may you keep it all down."

He bowed again and drifted off down the gallery, and Yara stood in the light from the tall window with her heart beating a slow, deliberate, trained rhythm — the rhythm she used when she had swallowed something and was waiting to learn whether she would live.

* * *

That evening she did not go straight to the guildhall. She went instead to her own rooms — three chambers above the tasting room, sparse as a monk's cell, kept sparse on purpose, because a taster with possessions was a taster who could be bought — and she sat at her little table with a single candle and did what she always did when the world grew teeth: she made a list.

*What Ser Lysander Quill knows,* she wrote.

*1. That I was absent a week.*
*2. That I was seen on the Saltmarket road.*
*3. That a taster's body is worth something to someone.*

She stared at the third line for a long time. It had written itself, almost. Her pen had moved and she had let it.

*Worth something to someone.*

She thought about the Hollow Court. She thought about the market of borrowed things that Odile had described — Odile, in her lighthouse of frozen time, speaking in the flat voice of a woman who had spent two centuries with nothing to spend her words on: *They trade in what cannot be minted. Years. Names. The last time someone said your name with love. The taste of a thing you loved.* She thought about the child, whose soul sat in a jar on a shelf beneath the mountain, and whose debt came due at midwinter, and whose midwinter was now fewer than forty days away.

And she thought — with the particular, sickening clarity that comes when a truth you have been carrying turns over in the dark and shows you its other face — about what *she* carried.

Nineteen years of poison. Not survived — *refused*. Arsenic and belladonna and wolfsbane and the seven quiet cousins of nightshade, each one swallowed in a measured dose, each one turned away by a body that had learned the trick of refusal so thoroughly that the learning itself had become a substance, a thing with weight and worth, as real as any metal in the royal treasury. She had always thought of her immunities as armor. Armor was for wearing. Armor kept you alive and asked nothing in return.

But the Hollow Court did not deal in armor. It dealt in essence. In the distilled thing beneath the thing. And what was her immunity, if you boiled her down, if you shelved her like a preserve, if you priced her the way the Court priced everything — but a rare and concentrated vintage of *refusal*? Nineteen years of small deaths, swallowed and survived, steeped in her blood like tea. There was nothing like it left in the world. Ser Lysander had said so. Ser Lysander, who took an interest in her health. Ser Lysander, who asked about her body the way men asked about ore.

Her pen had stopped. The candle had guttered and steadied. She wrote one more line, slowly, the letters very small:

*4. Find out who told him to ask.*

* * *

The sweetness test came to her the next morning, and it came, as the worst truths did, dressed as an ordinary errand.

The king's breakfast included honey-cakes. Yara tasted them in the tasting room with the copper spoon and the little glass bell, and they were perfect — wildflower honey, dark and resinous, the cakes still warm — and she stood there afterward with the taste in her mouth and realized, all at once, standing perfectly still among the copper spoons, that she had been performing a test on herself for four days without knowing it.

Every day since the guildhall. Every day since the ledger had begun translating, since the child had taken her hand in the rain with fingers like ice and a silence like a held breath. Every day she had tasted the palace's sweetnesses — the marchpane, the honey, the candied peel, the sugared plums in silver dishes — and every day she had checked, without admitting she was checking, that the sweetness was still *there*. Still arriving. Still hers.

Because Odile had told her what the Court took. *The taste of a thing you loved.* And because some part of Yara, some cold competent part that had kept her alive through nineteen years of swallowed poison, had understood immediately that the Court might begin collecting on her early — that a creditor did not always wait for the due date to inspect the collateral.

She took a spoonful of honey from the tasting jar and held it on her tongue and did not move for a full minute.

Wildflower. Resinous. A little of the smoke of the hive-fire, a little of the green of the meadow. Sweet all the way down, sweet as it had been when she was nine years old and an orphan tasting her first stolen fig in the palace yard, sweet as it had been every day of a life in which sweetness had been the one pleasure she had never rationed, because it had never been dangerous, because nothing that harmless could ever be taken from her.

It was still there.

She let the honey go down her throat and stood with her hand pressed flat against the tasting table, and she was ashamed of the relief that flooded her — ashamed, because relief meant she had been afraid, and fear meant she had something to lose, and she had spent nineteen years arranging her life so that she had nothing to lose. That had been the whole design. Armor, not a life. A body full of refusals and a heart full of ledgers and a door she walked through sideways, at dusk, alone.

Except the design had failed. Somewhere between the rain and the child's cold hand and the smell of bread in the guildhall, she had acquired a thing to lose. Several things. A whole ragged, impossible, unaccounted-for family of them.

And now the Court knew it too. Or would. Or already did — because the Court kept ledgers of its own, and Yara Coldharbour had spent a week reading the Court's contract, and asking its questions, and standing in its antechamber of unlit candles, and a debtor who walked into the creditor's house to study the terms was a debtor who had told the creditor everything worth knowing without saying a word.

*They will not take the child's soul first,* she thought, with the cold clarity of the tasting room. *They will take mine. Not my soul. My sweetness. A down payment. A taste of what's owed. They will reach up through the mountain and the old contract and the blood of kings, and they will put their fingers into my mouth, and when I next taste honey there will be nothing there at all.*

She rinsed the spoon. She dried it. She set it in its place among the other spoons, all in a row, bright as teeth.

* * *

Ser Lysander Quill was waiting for her in the gallery that afternoon, and this time he did not pretend to be looking out the window.

"Master Coldharbour. A moment." He fell into step beside her, unhurried, his boots making no sound on the stone. "I've been reading the old household rolls. Fascinating documents. Did you know that tasters were once permitted a deputy? In King Aldous's day, especially. A taster with a deputy could be spared for other duties. Errands. Journeys." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "The rolls mention one journey in particular. Aldous sent his taster into the mountain country, two hundred years ago, just before midwinter. The rolls don't say why."

Yara kept walking. "The rolls are not my department."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But *mountains* seem to be becoming your department. Or so Master Ferrow's cousin says, and he keeps the toll-book at the Saltmarket gate, and he says a woman answering your description passed the gate two nights ago, going toward the mountain road, at an hour when honest people are asleep." Ser Lysander stopped walking, and so, because stopping was the only way to make him stop, did she. "I am not your enemy, Master Coldharbour. I want that understood. I am a man who has noticed that things are moving beneath this kingdom — old things, ledger-things, debt-things — and I have noticed that you move when they move. So I will ask you only once, and I will ask it kindly, the way one asks a person standing at the edge of something."

He leaned in. Up close, his eyes were not cold at all. They were *hungry*, and the hunger had a frightened look to it, like a man who has been hungry so long he has forgotten what the food was.

"What did you find under the mountain?"

And Yara — poison-proofed, ledger-keeping, armor-wearing Yara Coldharbour, who had spent nineteen years learning to swallow dangerous things and keep her face smooth while they went down — looked at him and made the only honest mistake available to her.

She hesitated.

It was half a breath. It was nothing. It was the longest confession of her life, and she saw him take it in, saw it land, saw the hunger in his eyes sharpen into certainty.

"There it is," Ser Lysander said softly. "There it is. Well." He straightened, and his courtesy came back over him like a coat. "You needn't answer. I'll find out. I'm patient, Master Coldharbour. I've been patient for a very long time." He began to walk away, then paused, and without turning said, almost gently:

"Midwinter is coming. You should taste everything twice."

He went, and the gallery was empty, and Yara stood alone in the long light with her heart going like a hammer in a bell, and she understood three things with perfect clarity.

That Ser Lysander Quill had lost someone to the Hollow Court, though she did not yet know how she knew it.

That she had perhaps a week — less — before he went to someone above him, and the palace's slow machinery began to turn toward her.

And that the sweetness was still on her tongue, the honey still wildflower and resin and smoke, but that somewhere beneath the mountain, in a market of shelved and jarred and priced things, something had begun — very quietly, very patiently — to count what she was worth.

She turned and walked, not toward the guildhall, not yet — first toward the cellars, first toward the salt-works, first toward every errand that would make her visible and ordinary and accounted for — and she began, in the privacy of her own head, where no ledger could read it, to make a list of everything she was not yet willing to lose.

The list was short.

That was the trouble. That was the whole trouble. It had been short for exactly one week, and already she could not imagine being the woman who had written nothing on it at all.
