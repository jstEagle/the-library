## 7. Down the Stair That Isn't There

Two hundred years in a lighthouse of frozen time teaches a person certain things. It teaches patience, though that is the least of it — patience is merely the shape boredom takes when it has nowhere left to go. What it really teaches, what Odile Vane had learned down to the marrow and the marrow's marrow, was how to read a doorway.

Every door has a grammar. A palace door says *you are permitted*. A prison door says *someone else decided*. A guild door — a good one, one built by hands that understood the trade — says *come in, but come in changed*.

The door at the back of the Hollowfell quarry said none of these things.

The door at the back of the Hollowfell quarry said nothing at all, because it was not there.

Odile stood before it in the grey pre-dawn, her breath steaming, her old bones aching in ways that two centuries of stillness had not managed to cure, and she felt the absence of the door the way a blind woman feels an open window: as a change in the pressure of the world. The rock face rose sheer above them, streaked with quartz and old rain, and somewhere in its indifferent stone was a seam that only opened for one person alive.

The child stood with one small palm flat against the cliff, head tilted, listening.

"Is it working?" Bramble whispered. She had insisted on whispering, though they were half a mile from the nearest living soul, because mountains, she maintained, were notorious gossips.

"It is deciding," Odile said.

"Deciding? Doors don't decide."

"All doors decide. Most are simply too polite to make you wait."

Yara Coldharbour said nothing. She had said very little since the salt-works, since the list she had made in her own head — Odile had watched her make it, had seen the particular stillness of a woman inventorying her own heart — and Odile found she did not mind the silence. Silence, in her long experience, was where the true negotiations happened. You could learn more from what a person declined to say than from everything they offered up like coins.

She watched Yara now, this taster who kept ledgers of impossible things, standing in the cold with her cloak pulled close and her eyes fixed on the child with an expression she probably believed was neutral. It was not neutral. Odile had spent two hundred years studying the faces of people who wanted something desperately and would not say so. Yara wore want the way other women wore mourning: carefully, in good black cloth, buttoned to the throat.

The child made a sound.

It was not a word — or rather, it was a word from before words had learned to be small, a sound that seemed to arrive already old, like light from a dead star. The quartz seams in the cliff face caught it and carried it downward, deep into the stone, and for a moment the whole mountain rang like a struck bell, so low and vast that Odile felt it in her teeth, in the fillings she'd lost forty years ago, in the ghost-teeth beyond those.

And then the rock *remembered* itself differently.

There was no grinding, no groan of stone on stone. That was the horror of it, and the beauty, and Odile had known enough of both to tell them apart. The cliff face simply agreed, all at once, that it had always contained a stairway — and so it had. Steps descended into darkness in a smooth spiral, worn at their centers by feet that had never walked there, lit by nothing, leading down into a dark that was not the mere absence of light but something with texture to it, something that breathed.

"Well," said Bramble. "I hate it."

"You're welcome to stay," Odile said.

"I didn't say I wasn't coming." Bramble hitched her satchel higher on her shoulder. It clinked. Odile had asked her twice what was in it; Bramble had answered twice with a look that said *a witch tells no one her business*, which meant, in Odile's experience, jars of pickled things and at least one item of genuine power hidden among them like a needle in a pincushion.

The child went first. Of course the child went first. It was the child's debt, the child's soul shelved somewhere beneath them in the dark like a jar of winter preserves, and children — Odile remembered this about them, remembered it with an ache that had outlasted everything else she'd been — children walk toward the thing they fear because walking away is worse. The little one put one foot on the first step, then looked back over one shoulder, and the look was not fear. It was *counting*. Making sure everyone was coming. Making sure the family, such as it had assembled itself in three short weeks, was complete.

Odile's chest did something complicated. Two hundred years of stillness had rusted her capacity for surprise, but not, apparently, her capacity to be wounded by a child trusting her.

"I'm here," she said, and heard how strange her voice sounded saying it.

They descended.

* * *

The first hundred steps were ordinary, if a staircase carved through living mountain by nothing at all can be called ordinary. The air grew warmer, which Odile noted and disliked; caves should grow colder as you descend, and warmth underground meant one thing only — something down here had a metabolism.

By the second hundred steps, the candles began.

They lined the walls in iron sconces gone green-black with age, hundreds of them, thousands, wicks trimmed and waiting, wax pooled and hardened in the shapes of previous meltings. Not one was lit. The dark between them was total, and yet Odile could see the candles perfectly, could see every drip and seam, and it took her a full minute to understand why, and another full minute to wish she hadn't.

"They're not unlit," she said quietly. "They're *unremembered*."

Bramble squinted at her. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning someone remembers them being lit. Someone remembers the light of them. And the memory is doing the work." Odile touched the nearest sconce and felt, faint as a heartbeat under snow, the warmth of a fire that had never burned here. "These aren't the Court's candles. They're borrowed. Everything down here is borrowed."

"From whom?"

"From whoever owed them something." She withdrew her hand. Her fingertips smelled, absurdly, of beeswax and a summer afternoon two centuries gone, and she had to stand very still for a moment until the smell released her.

Yara had stopped several steps below, and when Odile caught up she saw why. The taster was staring at a patch of wall where the candlelight-that-wasn't fell strongest, and her face had gone pale in the particular way of a professional confronting work far above her pay grade.

"There are people in the walls," Yara said.

"Not people." Odile followed her gaze. In the stone — no, not in the stone; in the memory of the stone — figures moved. Faint as breath on glass. A woman carrying a basket. A man laughing at something just outside the frame. Children running, their feet making no sound because sound had never been part of what was taken. "Memories. Sold, traded, pawned. The Court doesn't keep souls in the walls, Yara. It keeps the *leavings*. When a soul is taken, the life around it has to go somewhere. Grief won't fit in a jar."

"How do you know all this?" Bramble asked, and there was no accusation in it, only the flat practical curiosity of a hedge-witch who needed to know whether the person beside her was an expert or a liability.

"Because I drafted most of the contracts," Odile said, "and you cannot draft a contract honestly without understanding what it destroys." She let that sit. She did not add the rest — *and because I have spent two hundred years wondering which of my clients' memories these are, and whether any of them are mine.* Some truths you carry up the stair with you, to spend later.

They walked on. The spiral tightened. The candles thickened until the walls were forests of them, and Odile noticed — and knew the others noticed, because Yara's breathing changed and Bramble's hand crept toward her satchel — that the borrowed memories were getting closer to the path. Figures now stood *on* the steps, translucent and going about their eternal afternoons, and once, unbearably, a dog trotted past Odile's knee, solid enough that she felt the push of displaced air, warm and alive and utterly absent from the world.

The claustrophobia came on gradually, the way cold does, the way drowning does. It was not the mountain pressing in — the mountain was vast, the spiral generous — it was the sense of walking through someone else's life that had been peeled away and pasted here, layer upon layer, until the passage was less a corridor than an archive, and archives, Odile thought, are the most claustrophobic places in existence, because they are full of things that cannot leave.

"Keep talking," she said aloud.

"Why?" Yara asked.

"Because we can hear ourselves. While we can hear ourselves, we're still real to each other. The moment this place starts feeling normal—" she gestured at the silent woman wringing laundry two steps below them, forever, "—is the moment it has us."

So they talked. Bramble described her garden in exhaustive detail, the slug wars of last spring, the rival witch three lanes over whose roses were frankly fraudulent. Yara spoke of the kitchens, the king's table, the precise bitterness of almonds versus apricot kernels, and Odile listened to her catalogue poisons the way other people recite prayers and understood, finally, exactly what kind of armor competence is, and exactly how heavy it gets.

And the child — the child listened too, walking ahead with its hand trailing along the candle-sconces, and where it touched them, Odile noticed, the wicks *leaned*. Just slightly. Just barely. Like flowers turning toward a sun that hadn't risen yet.

Like things remembering what they were for.

* * *

The stair ended at a landing, and the landing ended at an antechamber, and the antechamber took Odile's breath and held it hostage.

It was a hall — no, halls have proportions, and this had none; it was large the way the sea is large, its ceiling lost in a dark that the thousand thousand unlit candles somehow failed to reach. And the floor —

The floor was doors.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Set flush into the stone like paving stones, each one different — oak, iron, birch, bone, one that appeared to be woven entirely of ribbon, one of river-water held obediently in the shape of a rectangle — and each one closed, and each one humming, very faintly, with the particular hum of a way kept open by force.

"Doorways," Odile whispered. Her own trade stared back up at her and she felt, for the first time in two centuries, like a apprentice caught thieving from her master's bench. "Every contract the Guild ever honored. Every doorway we ever opened for someone who paid us truly. They all end *here*. All roads of impossible things lead down, and this is down."

"So the Court has been collecting our work," Yara said slowly, "the whole time."

"The Court has been collecting *everything*. Look at the doors. Those aren't ours alone." She knelt beside the nearest — birch wood, silver hinges, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world, because she knew that door, had hung it herself in a village that no longer existed, for a girl who wished to visit her mother across a war. "This is a market floor. These are the stalls. Midwinter is market day."

"And tonight?" Bramble asked.

Odile looked up at the dark where a ceiling should be, and felt the mountain listening, and felt the candles leaning, all of them now, every borrowed flame-memory in the endless hall tilted subtly toward the small figure of a child who had come to collect itself.

"Tonight," she said, "the shop is open."

Somewhere far above them — or perhaps far below; distance had begun lying some time during the second hour — a bell rang once. It was not a large bell. It was the bell over a shop door, small and cheerful and utterly wrong, and it rang the way bells ring when a customer enters.

All the doors on the floor shivered in their frames.

And the nearest one — the birch door, the door Odile herself had hung for a grieving girl across a dead war — swung open one inch, silently, onto a darkness that breathed out the smell of cold wax and older winters.

Something on the other side was waiting to be paid.

Odile rose, and took the child's hand without asking, and felt the small fingers close around hers with a trust that weighed more than the mountain, and stepped forward off the edge of the world and onto the market floor of the Hollow Court.
