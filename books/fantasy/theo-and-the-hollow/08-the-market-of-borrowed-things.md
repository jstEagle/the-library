## 8. The Market of Borrowed Things

The market did not announce itself. One moment they were walking a corridor of unlit candles, and the next the corridor simply opened its mouth and swallowed them into noise — or what passed for noise down here, a hush so dense it pressed against the ears like water. Yara stopped on the threshold and let her taster's habits do what they always did: catalog, assess, survive.

She tasted the air first. That was reflex, older than thought. The air of the Hollow Court's market tasted of cold wax and iron filings and something sweeter underneath, something that made her tongue ache with recognition before her mind caught up — it tasted of sugar left too long in a damp pantry, sweetness gone slightly wrong, the flavor of things kept past their season.

"Souls," Odile Vane said beside her, in the tone of a woman identifying mold in a wine cellar. "That's what you're smelling. Kept souls. Two hundred years of them, and not one turned properly."

Yara had not yet made peace with the fact that Odile Vane walked beside her at all. Two days ago the founder of the Guild of Impossible Things had been a story, a cautionary tale, a name in the front matter of ledgers older than the kingdom's current dynasty. Now she was a sharp-elbowed woman in a coat the color of dried blood, her hair bound in silver thread, her eyes the flat grey of a winter sea, and she moved through the Hollow Court's antechambers as though she owned the deed. Which, Yara was beginning to suspect, in some terrible technical sense, she did.

The child — Wren, they had named her, for the bird that sings in winter when everything else has given up — walked between Bramble and the exiled founder with one small hand in each of theirs. She had not spoken since the stair. She did not need to. Her silence had a shape down here, a gravity; the market bent around her the way a river bends around a stone.

"Stay close," Odile murmured, not looking at any of them. "And touch nothing. In this place, looking is a kind of taking, and taking is a kind of buying. The Court keeps excellent books. It learned them from me."

* * *

The Market of Borrowed Things was built inside what Yara could only describe as a throat — a vast descending hollow of smooth grey stone, ribbed with arches, warm in the wrong way, the way a mouth is warm. Stalls lined the walls in spiraling tiers, hundreds of them, thousands, lit by candles that burned without flame: wicks of pale light, cold to any heat they should have thrown. And on the stalls, in rows, in careful ranks, in the exact arrangement a good housekeeper gives her winter preserves —

Jars.

Yara had spent her life among jars. The palace stillroom, the taster's cabinet, the little locked case where she kept her antidotes: she knew jars the way a shepherd knows sheep. So it was not the jars themselves that turned her stomach. It was the contents. Each one held a soft, slow light the color of a memory you can't quite reach — gold, or rose, or the blue of dusk on snow — and the lights *moved*. They pressed against the glass. They drifted, dreamlike, toward the hands of the browsing customers and away again. Some of them, when Yara passed close, turned. She could feel it, the way one feels eyes on the back of the neck. They were watching her. They were watching everyone.

They were *hoping*.

"Preserves," Bramble said under her breath, and her hedge-witch's voice, usually so warm and rough as wool, had gone thin. "That's what they've done. They've shelved them like damson jam."

"Better than the alternative," said a voice from the nearest stall.

The vendor was — Yara took a moment, the way she took a moment with any new poison, to establish its nature — the vendor was difficult to look at directly. She had the outline of a woman and the fill of a shadow, and where her face should have been there was a smoothness like the inside of a seashell. Her hands, though, were precise and elegant, and they were polishing a jar with a cloth, the way a jeweler polishes a stone.

"Better than being eaten," the vendor elaborated, tilting the jar toward the light. The light inside pressed toward her fingers. "The Court was hungry once. It learned restraint. Now it *keeps*. Preservation is a mercy. You may quote me."

"We didn't come to shop," Yara said.

"Everyone who comes to the market comes to shop." The vendor set the jar down and arranged it among its neighbors with a small, tender click. "The only question is what you think you're buying, and what you're actually paying. May I interest you in anything? A first kiss, lightly used. The smell of your grandmother's kitchen. The memory of a summer that never happened — those are very popular. Guaranteed to have occurred."

Yara's stomach turned, but her taster's discipline held. Catalog. Assess. "We're looking for a specific item. A recent acquisition. A soul belonging to a child."

The smoothness where the vendor's face should have been inclined, very slightly. Something in the quality of the silence around them changed — the hush tightening, the way a room tightens when a dangerous subject is broached.

"A child's soul," the vendor repeated. "Come to collect itself, has it? We heard it was walking. The jars have been whispering about it for a week." She sighed, a sound like wind through a keyhole. "This way, then. But I warn you, taster-of-kings — and yes, we know what you are, we can taste the poison in your blood from here, it's a lovely vintage — the Court honors its contracts. It has no other food. You may find that less comforting than you hope."

* * *

They went deeper. The tiers of stalls descended in a slow spiral, and Yara noticed, as they walked, that the jars changed. Near the top, the lights were bright and restless, recent acquisitions, still fighting the glass. Lower down, the light dimmed, softened, settled. By the time they had descended three full turns of the spiral, the jars held only a faint glow, patient as banked coals, and the labels — small tags of grey vellum, tied with thread — had grown older, the script on them shifting through fashions of handwriting, decades and centuries of penmanship marching backward as they descended.

Yara read them as she passed. She couldn't help it. It was the ledger-keeper in her; every entry was a debt, and debts were her native language.

*A baker's Tuesday, 1811.*
*The courage to jump, unsold, 1740.*
*A name, surrendered willingly.*
*A name, not.*

That last one stopped her for half a step. The tag was very old, the ink browned to rust. *A name, not surrendered willingly.* Someone, sometime, had amended the label — scratched a line through *willingly* and written *not* beneath it in a different hand, a hand Yara recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water.

Odile Vane's hand.

"You've been here before," Yara said quietly.

Odile didn't break stride. "I told you. The Court learned its bookkeeping from me. I learned my bookkeeping from the Court. We were young together, it and I." Her voice was level, but her knuckles, wrapped around the child's small hand, had gone white. "Keep walking."

* * *

The child's jar was on a shelf of its own.

Not among the tiers — apart from them, in a small alcove at the spiral's bottom, set off by a railing of black iron and a placard of grey vellum larger than any of the others. The alcove was colder than the rest of the market, and the candle that lit it — flameless, like all the rest — burned a color Yara had never seen from a candle: the pale, living green of new leaf.

The jar was small. That was the first thing. Smaller than a preserve jar, smaller than an inkwell — it might have held a fistful of snow, and what it held was not much bigger. A light the size of a plum, white-gold, turning slowly on itself like something asleep and dreaming of motion. It was, Yara thought, the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and the most wrong, because beauty that size should have been *laughing*, should have been running through a guildhall with jam on its face, should have had a name and a mother and a winter — and instead it was here, in glass, under a label.

The label read: *PAYMENT. One soul, entire, per contract of King Aldous, first of his name. Due at midwinter. DO NOT OPEN. DO NOT SHAKE. DO NOT SPEAK TO IT.*

Bramble made a sound low in her throat, the sound she made when a patient was dying and she was deciding whether to lie about it. The child — Wren — stepped forward to the railing and put both hands on the black iron, and the light in the jar *woke*.

Yara felt it before she saw it: a pressure behind her sternum, a warmth, a terrible familiar pull, like hearing your own name called from a house you'd forgotten you'd lived in. The little light pressed itself against the glass, toward the child. It flared. It dimmed. It flared again — in rhythm. Yara realized, with a sensation like the floor tilting, that it was pulsing the way a heart pulses. That it was trying, in the only language it had left, to say *here, here, I'm here, I'm here.*

The child pressed her forehead against the railing. Her mouth moved. No sound came out. It didn't need to.

"Right," Yara said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "We're taking it."

"No," said the vendor, who had followed them down, or had always been there, or was simply the sort of presence that arrived when it was needed. "You're really, really not. But do go on. The Court so enjoys the next part."

* * *

The next part happened in a room Yara hadn't noticed until they were inside it, which was, she was learning, how everything in the Hollow Court worked. It was a small chamber off the alcove, lined floor to ceiling with grey vellum — the same vellum as the labels, thousands of sheets of it, and Yara understood with a ledger-keeper's shiver that these were contracts. All of them. Every sale the Court had ever made, filed like a physician's notes, like a poisoner's recipes.

In the center of the room stood a lectern, and on the lectern lay a single document, and the document was glowing faintly, the way the jars glowed, and Yara knew before anyone touched it what it was.

The contract of King Aldous. Two hundred years old and still awake. Still hungry.

Odile Vane walked to it the way a woman walks to a mirror she hasn't faced in decades. She read it in silence. Yara watched her eyes move, and watched them stop, and watched something complicated and terrible happen in the flat grey of them — something that looked, absurdly, like regret.

"Here," Bramble said. "The payment clause." She was pointing, her broad finger stabbing at a paragraph near the bottom. "Read it out. All of it. Fine print and all. I've been swindled by enough hedge-lawyers to know the fine print is where the teeth live."

Yara read it. She read it aloud, because her voice was the steadiest, because someone had to, because reading contracts aloud was half her life in the guildhall and this, at least, was ground she knew.

"*Payment for the above-named soul shall be rendered in full at midwinter, when the debt matures,*" she read. "*The soul shall be delivered by its own motion or collected by the Court's. No substitute shall be accepted. No partial payment shall be accepted. No payment shall be accepted save that which is equivalent: a soul of equal age, equal wholeness, and equal — *"

She stopped.

"*Equal what?*" Bramble said.

Yara read the word. She read it twice, because her eyes, for the first time in her professional life, had tried to skip it. "*Equal willingness.*" She looked up. "The payment must be a soul given as freely as the original was — " and here her voice did something she hadn't authorized, a small crack, a hairline fracture — "as freely as the original was *sold*."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. The flameless candle in the alcove beyond flickered — or did whatever flameless candles do instead.

"Equivalent payment," Odile said at last. Her voice was very quiet and very old. "I wondered, all the way down the stair, whether I'd remembered it correctly. I hoped I'd misremembered." She laughed, once, without any humor in it at all. "I never misremember contracts. I *write* them."

"You wrote this one?" Yara said. "You wrote — two hundred years ago — you wrote the contract that put that child in a jar?"

"I wrote the contract that let a king sell a soul and call it lawful," Odile said. "Yes. And I have had two hundred years in a lighthouse of frozen time to consider my feelings about it, and I will tell you my conclusions later, when we are not standing in the pantry of a starving god." She turned from the lectern, and her grey eyes went around the room — Bramble, wide and fierce; the child, hands still on the railing, forehead still pressed to iron; Yara herself, ledger-keeper, poison-eater, keeper of other people's debts.

"Here is what the fine print means," Odile said. "We cannot buy the child's soul back. We cannot steal it — the jar is warded against theft, and even if it weren't, an unpaid contract doesn't dissolve, it *matures*. We cannot substitute a random soul; the Court would know within a heartbeat, and the penalty for false payment is written on the wall behind you, and I'd rather not read it aloud in front of the child. The payment must be equivalent. Equal age, equal wholeness, equal willingness." Her jaw tightened. "Equal *willingness*. Someone must choose this. Freely. Knowingly. With a whole soul of their own, of an age to understand what they are choosing."

"Then we find someone," Bramble said. "There are seven billion people up there. Someone somewhere —"

"Would have to *want* to be shelved in a jar in the dark forever," Yara said, "to save a child they've never met. And it would have to be true willingness, not heroics. The Court can taste the difference." She looked at her own hands. Poison-proofed hands. Hands that had swallowed twenty years of the kingdom's worst intentions so that a king could eat in peace. "The Court can taste the difference," she said again, more slowly, "because the Court tastes everything. Like a taster."

No one spoke. The candle did its lightless flickering. Somewhere far above them, through a mile of mountain, midwinter was coming at its own unhurried pace, and the debt was coming with it.

"There's a clause," Odile said suddenly.

Yara looked up. The founder of the Guild of Impossible Things was staring at the contract with an expression Yara had never seen on a face before — the expression of a woman reading her own handwriting and finding, hidden in it, a message from a stranger. From a younger stranger. From herself.

"There's a clause," Odile said again, and her voice had changed, had gone quick and sharp as a blade coming out of wool. "I remember it now. I put it there. I was young, and I was already — " she stopped, swallowed whatever the rest of that sentence was, and started again. "I hid a flaw in it. A door in the wall. I've been waiting two hundred years for someone desperate enough to need it."

She reached for the contract.

And the contract, Yara would swear for the rest of her life, reached back.
