## 10. Midnight in the Tasting Room

The tasting room smelled of beeswax and old fear.

Yara had always liked that about it—the way the two scents braided together in the dark hours after supper, when the trestles were cleared and the silver locked away and only she remained, walking the long table's length with her lamp held low. Beeswax for the candles that had burned down to stubs around the king's empty chair. Fear for everything else. Two hundred years of tasters had steeped their caution into these walls until the stones themselves seemed to hold their breath.

She set down her lamp beside the ewer of water that had not been poured—water was never poured until she drank first—and began her nightly inventory, which was really a kind of prayer.

Three poisons at the king's supper tonight, catalogued by tongue before the tongue could betray her. The first had been nothing: a bittering agent in the venison glaze, some cook's shortcut, harmless as an insult. The second had made her mouth go numb from the inside out, a creeping frost along the gum line that she had named years ago and named again now, out of habit, like greeting an old acquaintance—*foxbell, half-measured, someone wants His Majesty ill but not dead*. And the third—

The third had frightened her.

It had come in the wine, faint as a rumor, and it had not numbed her or burned her or turned her vision to smoke. It had done something worse. It had *tasted of the mountain*—of cold stone and candle-smoke and the particular sweetness-under-rot that she had breathed for one long hour beneath the Hollow Court's antechambers, when the stair that wasn't there had carried her down among unlit candles and borrowed memories. Someone had put a piece of the mountain in the king's cup. Or something had climbed up out of it and learned to pour.

Yara stood very still in the empty room and let the memory of that taste move through her the way she let all tastes move through her: acknowledged, assessed, filed. Then she took the ledger-thought she kept behind her teeth—*they know we came; they are reaching upward*—and closed over it like a door.

"You work late, Mistress Coldharbour."

Her hand did not startle. She had trained her hands the way other people trained hounds, and they would die before they shamed her. But her pulse kicked once against her throat, hard, like a fist on a locked door.

Master Ellery Voss stood in the tasting room doorway with a candle of his own, though the corridor beyond him was bright enough with sconces. He carried light into lit places; it was a habit of men who wanted you to look at what they were holding instead of what they were asking. He was Underkeeper of the Royal Cellars, which meant he controlled every drop that passed the king's lips, which meant—and this was the thing Yara had been refusing to think for eleven days—that *he* controlled the wine.

He was also the man who had begun asking questions.

"Master Voss." She inclined her head exactly as far as courtesy required and no further. "The table doesn't clear itself."

"No. It clears itself remarkably well, actually." He drifted along the trestles, trailing one gloved finger above the scrubbed wood without touching it. "That's rather what I wanted to speak to you about. The clearing. The *absences*."

"There were no absences at table tonight. I tasted every course."

"Every course, yes." He smiled. He had a courtier's smile, built to be worn in public, all polish and no hinges. "You're very thorough at table, Mistress Coldharbour. It's your hours *away* from it that interest me."

Here it was, then. Eleven days of small accumulations—his shadow at the end of the guild quarter street where no underkeeper had business, his name appearing twice on the rota so that he might stand near her at the evening tasting, his polite little notes about irregularities in the cellar tallies—and here it was, laid out between them on the scrubbed oak like a dish she hadn't ordered.

"I keep odd hours," Yara said. "Tasters do. Poison keeps worse ones."

"Poison does," he agreed pleasantly. "Though I wonder—" He lifted his candle higher, and its light slid across the wall, across the old hooks where previous tasters had hung their keys, across the faded ring-stains of a hundred thousand tested cups. "I wonder whether you know how many nights you've spent outside the palace this season. I count, you see. Counting is most of my work. Wine in, wine out, and every cask accounted for." He turned. "You have left the palace forty-one times since the equinox. Forty-one. And returned each time smelling of—"

He paused. Deliberately. Making her fill the silence, because filling silences was how people confessed.

Yara did not fill it. She let it sit there, patient as dough rising, while she thought about the smell of the guildhall—woodsmoke and Bramble's hedge-salves and the child's hair, rain and river-stone and the strange older-than-kingdom sweetness of the language the ledgers had translated for them. If Voss knew that smell by name, he would already have said it. He didn't know it. He only knew it wasn't palace.

"—of bread," she said, when the silence had gone on long enough to become hers. "There's a baker on Cordwainer Lane who works the night ovens. I can't sleep after a heavy tasting. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone does know that," Voss said softly. "It's in your file."

Something went cold in her, colder than foxbell ever managed. *Your file.* Of course there was a file. There were files on everyone in this palace; she had assumed hers said *competent, humorless, incorruptible*, and she had worn that assumption like armor for nine years. But files could be amended. Files could grow new pages overnight.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," Voss said, and the words dropped into the tasting room like a stone into a well.

Yara watched the ripples spread and gave them her face—the taster's face, the one she had practiced against mirrors until it could hold back anything, boredom and terror and grief all pressed flat into mild professional attention. Inside, behind the door behind the door, something howled.

Because he'd said the name. Not *a* guild. Not rumors of smugglers or hedge-witches. The name, whole, with its ridiculous impossible heart of it, and there were perhaps six living souls who knew that name, and four of them slept in a hall on Cordwainer Lane, and one of them was a child who spoke in a language older than any kingdom, and none of them—not one—would have sold it to a cellar-keeper with a courtier's smile.

Which meant he had gotten close enough to hear it. Which meant he had been nearer than she'd known. Nearer than she'd feared.

"I've heard stories," she said. "Children's tales. A guild that sells wishes. What of it?"

"Stories." He set his candle down on the long table with a small precise click, and looked at her across it the way a man looks across a chessboard at the move he's been waiting all game to make. "Stories that say the guild trades in debts. Doorways. Things bought and sold that shouldn't have prices." He leaned forward slightly. "Stories that say, three weeks ago, a child walked out of a storm into their keeping—a child the palace has been looking for since the harvest fair. A child with no name in any registry. A child who vanished from under the nose of the King's own Taster on the very street where she claims to buy her bread."

The lamp between them guttered. Neither of them moved to shield it.

Yara measured her breath the way she measured doses: small, even, sufficient. *He doesn't know,* she told herself, and made herself believe it, because believing otherwise was a door she could not afford to open in front of him. He knew the shape of the hole. He did not yet know what filled it. A child missing from a registry could be a runaway, a kidnapping, a corpse in the river. He was fishing. He had cast forty-one absences and a borrowed name into dark water and was waiting to see what rose.

"The harvest fair," Yara said. "Crowds of ten thousand. Children go missing at fairs the way coins go missing at fairs. I remember the day. I remember being sorry." She let a beat pass. "Is that the irregularity in the cellars, Master Voss? A lost child at a fair?"

"It's an irregularity in *you*," Voss said, and for just a moment the smile fell away entirely, and what lived underneath it was not cruelty but something wearier and more dangerous: a man doing arithmetic he did not enjoy. "Forty-one departures. A name whispered in low taverns. A taster whose tongue has never once failed the crown, standing in my cellar counts like a thumbprint on a clean glass." He straightened, retrieved his candle, and his smile reassembled itself piece by piece, hinge by hinge. "I'm not accusing you, Mistress Coldharbour. Accusation requires proof, and proof requires patience, and I am a patient man. I only wished to tell you—I wished you to hear it from me, in this room, as one servant of the crown to another—that I have petitioned the Lord Chamberlain for authority to audit the palace's *people* this winter. All of us. Our hours, our associations, our comings and our goings." He bowed, shallow and correct. "Sleep well. You'll want your strength. Midwinter is coming, and the king's table will be very full."

Then he was gone, and his candlelight with him, and Yara stood alone in the tasting room with her own small lamp and the taste of the mountain still ghosting the back of her throat, and listened to the silence rearrange itself around what had just happened.

*Audit the palace's people.*

Not suspicion anymore. Machinery. Petitions granted became schedules, schedules became lists, lists became names read aloud in rooms she would not be permitted to enter. Even if Voss found nothing—even if she scrubbed every hour, every association, every crumb of Cordwainer Lane from her days—the machinery would grind on through midwinter, and midwinter was when the debt came due, and midwinter was when she would have to walk down a stair that wasn't there and leave the king's supper untasted, and an audit would find an empty chair and an untested cup and a file with forty-two departures in it, and then—

And then they would mark her. Not traitor yet; traitor required proof. But *suspect*, which in a palace was the same animal wearing a different collar. Suspects got followed. Suspects got questioned. Suspects' friends got visited by underkeepers with candles, and Bramble would not survive a visit, Bramble argued with tax collectors for sport, and Odile was a lighthouse away and the child—

The child.

Yara thought of the child sitting cross-legged before the great ledger in the guildhall, small fingers hovering over the vellum, watching the old ink crawl and reshape itself into words the child could almost understand. Thought of the way the child had begun, in the last week, to laugh—one soft exhale, quickly hidden, whenever Bramble did something absurd, which was often. Thought of the entry she had found with her own eyes, two centuries old, in a hand like cut glass: *One soul, sold whole, payment deferred to midwinter. Signed, Aldous Rex.* Thought of the soul-jar waiting on its shelf in the market of borrowed things, glowing faintly, patient as preserves.

They would take the child back to the palace if they caught her. They would deliver the child to midwinter like a parcel. Because the crown had sold the child's soul before the child was born, and crowns do not apologize, and underkeepers with petitions do not ask what the debt was *for*.

Yara looked down at her hands.

They were steady. They were always steady. They had held cups of certain death without a tremor for nine years, and she had told herself that steadiness was her gift, her craft, her armor—and she had believed, somewhere deep in the place where she kept the things she refused to examine, that competence was a substitute for belonging. That if she was useful enough, exact enough, immune enough, she would earn her place at a table where no seat had ever actually been saved for her. Nine years of swallowing poison so the king could eat safely, and the king did not know her name. Nine years, and her file said *humorless* where it should have said *alone*.

But the guildhall knew her name. The guildhall had written it into a ledger, in her own hand, in the column marked *household*: **Coldharbour, Yara—keeps accounts; keeps watch; keeps us.** Bramble had added the last part when Yara wasn't looking and then pretended for a week that the ink had always been there. The child had drawn her, badly, in charcoal on the pantry door: a stick figure with a lamp, standing in a doorway, holding it open.

*Keeps us.*

Voss thought he had cornered her tonight. He thought he had laid out his little chessboard of evidence and watched her flinch. He didn't understand what he had actually done. He had taken the choice she had been carrying secretly for weeks—*the palace or the child, the crown or the guild, duty or family*—and shown her that it was not a choice at all, because she could not serve both, and soon she would not even be allowed to pretend. He had made the pretending expensive. He had priced her double life out of reach.

So be it.

Yara lifted her lamp and walked the length of the tasting table one final time, and as she walked she said goodbye to it—not aloud, never aloud, but in the ledger-language of her heart, entering the loss in the neat columned hand she used for everything: *one career, surrendered; one crown, betrayed; one armor, set down.* The room did not answer. Rooms never had. That was rather the point of rooms, and the trouble with them.

At the door she paused, because the last thing was hardest, and she had always faced the hardest taste first—it was how you kept the easy ones honest.

She loved the palace. She had to say it to herself plainly, at least once, or the leaving would curdle into something cheap. She loved its absurd weight and its candle-glutted corridors and the small good people mending its linens and hauling its coal. She had given it her tongue, her nerve, her nine best years. She had swallowed foxbell for it. She had swallowed things with no names for it. And the palace, in return, had kept a file on her and called her humorless and would feed a child to a debt without reading the contract twice.

Loved, then. And leaving. Both true. Ledgers could hold two truths in balance; that was what ledgers were *for*.

Yara Coldharbour snuffed her lamp, and in the dark of the tasting room she made her vow—not to the crown, not to the guild, but to herself, quietly, the way she chose everything: *When they ask me whose side I am on, I will not lie. When the audit comes, I will not run ahead of it. When midwinter comes, I will walk down the stair that isn't there with my head up, and if the palace marks me traitor, let it spell my name correctly when it does.*

Then she went out through the servants' corridors, past the sleeping kitchen, past the guard who owed her for curing his daughter's fever and asked no questions, out into the cold blue hour before dawn, where the snow had started falling—early, too early, weeks early, fat unhurried flakes like ash from a distant fire.

Midwinter was coming early. She felt it in her teeth, in the place where the mountain's taste still lived.

Behind her, in the dark of the palace, a bell began to ring the hour—and stopped halfway, strangled, as if a cold hand had closed around it.

* * *

Cordwainer Lane was white and silent when she reached it, snow filling the gutters, muffling the city, erasing the world down to essentials: one narrow hall with lamplight in its windows, and a small figure standing in the doorway.

The child had been waiting for her. Yara knew it before she saw the face—knew it from the posture, arms crossed against the cold, chin up, planted in the threshold like a sentinel who had decided that this door was worth guarding. The child looked up at her, and in the strange old language that the ledgers alone could translate, said something short.

Yara didn't need the ledger for this one. Some meanings arrive ahead of their words.

"*You're home late,*" the child had said. *Home.*

"Yes," Yara answered, in plain trade-tongue, kneeling in the snow to be eye-level, letting the cold press its cold hands against her shoulders while the warmth of the hall reached past her like an open palm. "I'm home. And we have less time than I thought. Wake the others. Midwinter isn't waiting for midwinter."

Above them, far off across the sleeping city, the mountain that was not supposed to make noise made a sound—deep, patient, and unmistakably awake.
