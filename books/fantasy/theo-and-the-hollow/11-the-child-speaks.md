## 11. The Child Speaks

*Cold,* thinks the child, and then — because the word has never had anywhere to go before, because the word is a door opening in a wall that was always wall — *cold, cold, cold*, and the thinking of it three times makes it a kind of song.

The mountain does not like songs. The mountain swallows them the way the dark swallows candleflames: politely, completely, without malice and without mercy. But this one slips through anyway, small as a coin dropped down a well, and somewhere far above — or far below; directions have stopped meaning anything since the stair that isn't there — someone laughs. A bright, cracked-bell laugh. Bramble's laugh. The hedge-witch who smells of thorns and honey and the inside of pockets where sweets are hidden from oneself.

The child knows her by smell before knowing anything else. Smell survived. Smell was too small a thing to steal.

---

Here is what the child remembers, which is to say here is what the child is:

A name. It comes back the way feeling returns to a hand held too long in snow — painfully, in patches, with an itching that is almost worse than numbness. *Miren.* The syllables arrive one at a time, each one a small act of resurrection. *Mi-* like the middle of a song hummed into hair. *-ren* like rain on a roof over people you love. Miren. A name given by a mother whose face is a warm blur above a cookfire, whose hands were flour-white at the knuckles, whose voice said the name every morning like setting a place at a table. *Miren, breakfast. Miren, come in from the cold. Miren, Miren, my little winter-born.*

The winter part is true. The child — Miren, *Miren*, say it again, hold it up to the dark like a lantern — was born in the deep snows, in the week when even the wolves stayed in their dens, and the midwife said such children belong half to the frost already, and the mother laughed and said no, this one belongs entirely to me.

That was the first lie grief ever told anyone in the child's hearing. Or maybe not a lie. Maybe a promise made by someone who did not know what kings do with promises they don't make themselves.

* * *

There is a woman walking beside the child now, down the antechamber where the candles are all unlit but somehow remembered — wicks standing straight as gravestones in their hundreds, thousands, waiting for fires that were borrowed long ago and never returned. Yara, the guild calls her. The taster. She moves like someone counting exits, but her hand finds the child's shoulder at intervals without looking, the way you'd check that your own heart was still beating.

The child wants to tell her things. This is new. For two years — or two hundred; time in a jar is thick as honey and just as blind — there was nowhere for telling to go. Words went into the walls of the jar and came back as silence. But now, walking between Bramble's cracked bell and Odile's footsteps that sound like paper unfolding, the words are rising. They taste of copper and snowmelt. They come out wrong, sideways, in the old language that makes ledgers shiver and Bramble cross herself with salt.

And yet — and this is the miracle the child will spend a whole life not believing — they listen. The tall one with the ledger listens so hard her pen hovers, writing nothing, wanting everything. The exiled founder with eyes like frozen harbors listens the way stone listens: absolutely, and forever.

They do not understand. That doesn't matter. Understanding is a door; listening is the house around it.

*Miren,* says the voice that is coming back, to no one, to everyone. *My name was Miren.*

* * *

What was taken. Let the child count, because counting is a kind of holding, and holding is a kind of having, and the Court took *having* away first of all:

A soul — yes, everyone speaks of the soul, the great round word, the jewel-word. The soul is real; the child remembers its weight the way you remember the weight of a sleeping cat on your chest, warm and breathing and yours. Down here it hangs in a jar on a shelf in a market of borrowed things, glowing faintly the color of dawn on snow. The child saw it. The child looked at its own self floating in glass and felt less horror than recognition — *there I am, there I've been, hello, I'm sorry, I'm coming.*

But a soul is only the largest of the thefts. There were smaller ones, and the smaller ones hurt more.

A name. Kings can sell names. Nobody tells you that. A name is a deed of ownership drawn up in the oldest language, and King Aldous — the child learned his name in the ledger's translating whisper, learned it the way you learn the name of a disease — Aldous needed something to sell, and he could not sell the land (already sold) or the harvest (already promised) or his own soul (already mortgaged to something worse than this), so he went looking in the villages for a winter-born child and found a mother with flour on her hands and a name she loved more than bread.

He took the name first. That was the trick of it. Once the name was his, the rest followed like water finding a crack. An unnamed child is a doorway. An unnamed child can be *entered* — written into contracts, folded into debts, sealed into jars. The mother screamed. The child remembers the screaming the way you remember weather. And then remembers nothing for a very long time, which is what jars are for.

A mother. Not the memory of her — memories are cheap down here, the Court shelves them by the dozen, whole antechambers of borrowed mothers — but the *mother*. Her actual continuing. Somewhere above, if she lives, there is a woman setting a place at a table for a child who cannot come, calling a name that no longer answers because it no longer exists. The child carries this thought carefully, the way you carry a bowl of water across a room, both hands, no sudden movements, because if it spills the child will drown in it.

*A winter.* The strangest theft, the one even Odile's paper-unfolding eyes widened at, though the child could not then explain. Winters can be owned. The Court owns this one — the child's own first winter, the deep-snow week of the birth, kept in a jar somewhere between the soul-jar and the memory-shelves. Without it, the child is unmoored from the beginning of everything. Born, yes. But born into *what?* Every story needs its weather. Take the weather and the story floats loose, untellable, a name without a season, a person without a first cold breath. The Court keeps winters the way misers keep coins, and it spent the child's first one on something — the child does not know what, some candle relit for a moment, some hunger briefly dulled — and the loss of it is why the child sometimes feels like a sentence missing its first word.

*Mi-*

*-ren.*

There. Held again.

* * *

The voice comes back in fragments because that is how it was taken: not whole, but in pieces, sold off like the good silver of a declining house. Some of the voice went into the contract itself — that is why the ledger translates, why the child's speech shakes ink out of books. The ledger is partly *made* of the child. Some of the voice went into the mountain, which is why the mountain dislikes songs: they remind it of what it swallowed. And a little of the voice — the smallest piece, the piece that matters most — stayed behind in the body, curled up under the tongue like a coal banked for morning.

It is waking now. Deep in the mountain, past the market with its shelves of souls glowing like preserves, past the antechambers where the Regent's hunger hums in the stone like a hive, the coal is catching.

The child stops walking. The guild stops with the child — four shapes, five counting the guard-courtier who smells of fear and decency, six counting the ghost of a horse nobody brought — and the stopping becomes a circle, and the circle becomes, without anyone deciding it, a family-shaped thing.

Bramble crouches first. She always crouches first. "Go on, love," she says, in the tone grown-ups use for birds with broken wings and children with broken silences. "Whatever it is. Say it here. We'll catch it."

Catch it. As if a word could fall. As if falling were the danger, and not the endless weightless not-falling the child has known for two hundred years inside the glass.

So the child opens the coal under the tongue and lets the fragments out, and the old language comes pouring — not the ledger-translating whisper this time but raw, ragged, the sound of a voice remembering itself mid-scream and choosing, mid-scream, to become a song instead.

And the guild catches it. Each fragment, caught.

Yara catches the name — *Miren* — and writes it in the ledger with her own hand, in the space reserved for debts owed *to* the guild rather than by it, and the ink goes down like a key turning.

Odile catches the mother — holds the whole screaming grief of her in those frozen-harbor eyes and says, in the old tongue, badly, beautifully, a single sentence: *"She waited."* Two words. The child falls apart and is caught.

Bramble catches the winter — wraps her hedge-witch arms around the shaking shape of it and mutters into the child's hair that they'll steal it back, they'll steal back the whole blasted season, snow and wolves' dens and all, because what's a mountain to people who trade in impossible things?

The courtier catches what's left — simply stands there and weeps, openly, uselessly, wonderfully, because someone has to weep when grief finally gets its language, and he has a sister's empty shelf somewhere in these halls and has only just admitted it aloud.

* * *

Later — later being a direction the child is learning, along with *up* and *soon* and *with* — they sit in the lee of a fallen pillar and eat bread Bramble conjured from somewhere inside her coat, and the child speaks. Small sentences. Real ones. Half in the old tongue, half in the guild's language, stitching the two together with the thread of the returning voice.

"My name," the child says, and the words come out whole, unjared, astonishing. "Is Miren."

Silence. The good kind. The kind that is a family holding its breath so as not to disturb a miracle.

"Miren," Yara repeats, tasting it — and oh, the child sees it happen, sees the taster's trained tongue take the name in like a spoonful of something rare, sees her cataloguing reflexes reach for poisons and find none, only sweetness, only the plain sweet fact of a name returned to its owner. "Miren. That's a good name for the ledger."

"And for everything else," says Bramble.

"Welcome," says Odile, who does not waste words, which makes hers weigh more, "back."

And Miren — named, voiced, surrounded — feels the last fragment of the stolen voice settle home under the tongue, warm as a banked coal, ready for whatever morning the mountain has left to give them. Because there will be a morning. The child knows this the way the newly named know things: absolutely, without evidence, against all sense.

Then the mountain flinches.

Every unlit candle in the antechamber leans at once, westward, toward the deep places, like grass before a storm going the wrong way. The hum of the Regent's hunger changes pitch — rises, quickens, takes on something horribly like *eagerness*. Far off, in the market of borrowed things, glass begins to ring: soft at first, then all together, a thousand soul-jars chiming in alarm or greeting or appetite.

Odile is on her feet. "The debt," she says, and her paper voice is tearing. "It's accelerating. Midwinter isn't waiting for midwinter anymore."

Above them — impossibly, terribly, through a mile of stone — snow begins to fall upward.
