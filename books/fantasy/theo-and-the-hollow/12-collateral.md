## 12. Collateral

The Regent's audience chamber was a throat.

That was Yara's first thought, standing on the threshold of it with the child's small hand cold in hers: that the whole room had been carved to swallow. The walls curved inward where walls should have stood straight, the ceiling arched like a palate overhead, and the floor beneath her boots was smooth and slightly wet, as though the mountain itself were salivating in its sleep. Candles burned here — real candles, the first true flame she'd seen since descending the stair that wasn't there — but their light did not so much illuminate the space as hesitate within it, pooling in amber circles that never quite reached the corners.

Bramble walked at her left shoulder, hedge-witch's staff clicking against the wet stone. Odile Vane came behind them all, two hundred years old and moving like a woman who had made a long and patient peace with every step she'd ever taken. And the child — Wren, they'd begun calling her, though the name was still soft around the edges, still more hope than fact — gripped Yara's fingers with a strength that seemed too great for such a slight body.

*Don't let go,* the ledger had translated, when Wren pressed close before the doors opened. *They know my shape. If you let go, they'll remember it without me.*

Yara had not let go. She would not.

At the far end of the swallowing chamber, upon a dais of black glass that reflected nothing — not the candles, not the figures approaching, not even itself — sat the Hollow Regent.

Yara had expected a monster. She had spent three days in the antechambers of borrowed memories preparing herself for a monster: something many-limbed and lantern-eyed, something out of the cautionary tales guild members told over supper when the bread was stale and the wine was honest. What she found instead was worse, or perhaps better, or perhaps simply *stranger*: a figure of indeterminate age seated with the terrible composure of someone who has been waiting so long that waiting has become a kind of flesh. Its face was beautiful the way an empty house is beautiful — all proportion and no life, all symmetry and no one home. It wore robes the color of candle smoke, and where its hands rested on the arms of its throne, the black glass had gone cloudy, worn smooth by centuries of the same grip.

It was starving. Yara could see it now that she was close enough to look properly — could see the hunger the way she could taste arsenic in honey, subtle and total, informing everything. The Regent's hunger did not make it monstrous. It made it *patient*, which was far more frightening.

"You bring the debt into my hall," the Regent said. Its voice was dry paper, was wind through a keyhole, was the sound a wish makes when it dies ungranted. "Two hundred years I have kept this account. Two hundred years, and now you come asking me to forget it."

"We come asking you to read it again," Odile said, stepping forward.

The Regent's empty eyes moved to her, and something flickered across that vacant beauty — recognition, maybe, or the ghost of it. "The drafter returns. You have aged badly, Vane."

"I've aged honestly," Odile said. "There's a difference."

Yara felt Bramble shift beside her, felt the hedge-witch gathering whatever hedge-magic she carried like a woman hitching up her skirts before crossing a flooded street. But it was Yara who stepped forward then, because this was the part she had rehearsed in the dark of their camp, and because she had learned long ago that the only armor she truly owned was competence, and competence meant knowing exactly what you had to offer before anyone asked.

"Hollow Regent," she said, and her voice did not shake, though she felt the shaking in her teeth, held there like a coin between them. "I am Yara Coldharbour, taster to the crown of [the realm], keeper of the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things. I have swallowed thirty-one years of poison."

The Regent went very still. Even the candles seemed to lean in.

"Every day of those years," Yara continued, "I have taken into my body what was meant to kill another. Arsenic in the morning porridge. Belladonna in the wedding wine. Cantharides, aconite, mercury ground fine as forgiveness. My blood is a museum of everything the court has ever tried to slip past my tongue. I am immune to forty-seven poisons. I can taste a lie in a broth and a grudge in a glaze." She lifted her chin. "And I am offering all of it to you. As collateral."

"Yara—" Bramble started, low and urgent.

"Collateral," the Regent repeated, and the word moved through the chamber like a stone dropped down a well. They heard it land somewhere far below them, in rooms they hadn't known existed. "You offer me your immunities. Your careful hoard. Do you understand what you are saying, little taster?"

"No," Yara said honestly. "Not entirely. That's why it's collateral and not payment. Explain it to me."

Something happened to the Regent's face then that might, on a living creature, have been surprise. Or delight. Both were equally unsettling.

Odile made a sound that might have been a laugh bitten in half. "She negotiates," she said, to no one in particular. "Two centuries I waited for someone desperate enough to use my clause, and the guild sends me a food-taster who haggles with the Hollow Court like a fishwife."

"Someone has to," Yara said, not turning around. "You're all too busy being dramatic."

* * *

They negotiated for what felt like a season and might have been an hour; time in the Hollow Court behaved like water finding its level, running toward whatever low place attention dug for it.

The terms, as they finally took shape, were these:

The Regent would not consume Wren's soul at midwinter — not yet. In exchange, Yara Coldharbour would surrender her immunities into the Court's keeping, sealed in a vessel of the Regent's choosing, held as surety while the guild sought legal grounds to void the original sale. If they succeeded, the vessel would be returned unopened, the debt dissolved, the child freed. If they failed —

"If we fail," Yara said, "you drink."

"I do not drink," the Regent said, with something horribly like delicacy. "I *decant*. Thirty-one years of swallowed death, little taster, poured back through the body that hoarded it. Every poison returning to its home all at once. You will die of everything you ever survived, in the order you survived it, and it will take—" the Regent paused, savoring, the way Yara herself savored a complex broth— "a long time."

"That's the idea of collateral," Yara said. "It's supposed to be frightening. Otherwise it's just a gift."

"And how do you propose to transfer what lives in your blood?"

"I don't propose," said Yara. "I taste. Give me the vessel."

The vessel, when a servitor of unlit candles brought it forth, was a jar — of course it was a jar; the Court shelved souls like preserves, and apparently immunities kept just as well — blown from grey glass, stoppered with wax the color of old teeth. It was small enough to hold in one hand. It looked, Yara thought, like something from a palace pantry. Something she might be asked to test.

Bramble caught her arm before she could reach for it. The hedge-witch's fingers were rough as bark and trembling. "Girl," she whispered, "thirty-one years. You built that wall brick by brick, drop by drop, and you're going to hand it over to a starving thing under a mountain because a child you've known a fortnight—"

"Because a family I've known a fortnight," Yara corrected quietly. "Wren's just the one who knocked."

Bramble stared at her. Then, muttering something about stubborn fools and hedge-curses and the general ingratitude of the young, she released her arm — and pressed something into Yara's palm instead: a sprig of dried something-or-other, tied with red thread. "For the sweetness," she said gruffly. "Whatever's left of it after. Hold onto it."

Yara closed her hand around the sprig and turned to the jar.

Here is what no one tells you about immunity: it is not absence. It is not the clean, untouched life of someone poison has never met. Immunity is a *relationship*. Thirty-one years of small deaths, survived. Every dose she had ever swallowed lived in her still, transformed but not erased, the way grief transforms but does not erase. When she uncorked the jar and set her lips to the grey glass, she was not giving away an absence. She was giving away thirty-one years of surviving.

She drank.

It tasted of nothing, which frightened her more than any flavor could have. It tasted of the space where flavors live. She felt it leave her the way you feel a tooth leave your mouth — not pain, exactly, but a sudden wrongness, a hole where weight used to be. Her tongue went strange in her own head. The world's smells — candle smoke, wet stone, Bramble's dried herbs, the milk-and-rain smell of Wren's hair — all of it dimmed, as though someone had drawn a curtain between her and the sensory world.

When it was done, the jar in the servitor's hands glowed faintly, the way a jar of honey glows when held before a window. Forty-seven poisons, thirty-one years, sealed in grey glass.

"Well?" Odile asked softly.

Yara flexed her hands. Rolled her tongue against her teeth. Everything worked. Nothing worked. She was, she realized, exactly as vulnerable as the day she'd first been dragged before the king's table as a girl of nine, terrified, tasting her first poisoned plum.

"I'm alive," she said. "Ask me again tomorrow."

* * *

The Regent watched her with its empty eyes, and Yara watched it back, and between them the jar sat on the black glass dais like a hostage, like a promise, like a coin flipped and still spinning.

"You should know," the Regent said, as they gathered themselves to go, "why I accept this. It is not mercy."

"I didn't think it was."

"It is hunger arithmetic. Your immunities are worth more to me than one small soul — *if* the sale stands. If the sale falls, if Vane's buried clause surfaces, if you prove the child was never Aldous's to sell—" the Regent's grip tightened on the throne's arm, and the black glass creaked like ice on a pond— "then I will have taken your collateral under false pretenses, and the Court's own laws will turn on me. So understand, taster: I am not hoping you fail. I am hoping you succeed *slowly*. Past midwinter. Past the debt's due date. Past the point where the contract consumes what it is owed regardless of our arrangements."

The candles guttered, all together, as though the room had inhaled.

"You have until midwinter," the Regent said. "Six days. Find your evidence. Void your contract. Or come back down the stair that isn't there, and I will decant thirty-one years of survival back into the body that hoarded it — and then I will take the child anyway, because a starving thing cannot afford scruples."

"Six days," Yara said.

"Five and a half," said the Regent. "I rounded up. Sentiment."

They walked out of the swallowing chamber in silence, past the antechambers of unlit candles, past the shelves of borrowed memories, up the stair that wasn't there and into the thin grey daylight of the mountain's shoulder. Wren's hand found Yara's again without either of them looking, the way a compass finds north.

*Why did you give it your strong thing?* the ledger translated, from the pressure of small fingers against her palm. *Your strong thing was yours.*

Yara looked down at the child — at the soul-shaped absence walking beside her, at the name still soft around its edges — and thought about the guildhall, and the bread, and the knock at odd hours that had cracked her armor straight down the middle.

"Because it was heavy," she said, "and I was tired of carrying it alone. Now it's heavy for all of us. That's what family is."

Ahead of them, Odile Vane had stopped walking. She stood at the edge of the treeline with the wind pulling at her two-hundred-year-old coat, and when she turned, her face wore the expression of a woman about to confess to a crime she'd been proud of for two centuries.

"There's something you need to know," Odile said. "About King Aldous. About what he actually stole." She looked at Wren, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "He didn't buy that child's soul. He *took* it — from someone the crown owed far more than it could pay. And I know where he wrote it down."

The mountain wind went quiet, listening.

"The royal archive," Odile said. "Sealed since his death. And Yara — you're the only one of us who can walk through a palace door without the palace noticing."

Yara felt the hole in her tongue where her immunities used to live, and understood, with terrible clarity, that she had just paid for the privilege of committing treason.

"When do we start?" she said.
