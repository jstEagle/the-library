## 13. The Regent's Hunger

The Regent had not eaten in four hundred years.

This was the first thing it wanted them to understand, though it took an age to find the words — for language, too, was a thing that starved. It sat upon its throne of unlit candles and regarded the intruders with a face it had borrowed long ago from a dying shepherd, because a face made negotiation easier, and negotiation was all the Regent had left.

*They have come down my stair,* it thought, and the thought moved through the antechambers like cold water through cracks, touching every shelf, every jar, every sleeping soul in its keeping. *They have walked past my preserves and called them horrors.*

It did not correct them. Correction required breath, and breath was expensive.

The small one stood at the front — the child whose soul had been sold twice over, once by a king's greed and once by a taster's love. The Regent could feel the child's namelessness like an open window in winter. It was the emptiest thing in the room, and the Regent had spent centuries learning to read emptiness the way mortal men read weather.

Behind the child: the hedge-witch, humming a warding tune with her whole body; the founder, Odile Vane, two hundred years older than her crimes and wearing them like jewelry; the taster, Yara Coldharbour, whose blood the Regent could smell from here — forty years of swallowed poison layered in her veins like rings in a tree, each one a small death she had refused.

And the courtier, Lord Aldery, whose sister's jar sat on the ninth shelf of the third gallery, dusted weekly by no one.

*They think I am a monster,* the Regent thought, and something that had once been grief turned over in the place where its stomach used to be. *Let me show them what a monster would have done.*

---

It remembered being fed.

That was the cruelty of memory — it kept the sweetest things sharpest. The Regent had been old when the first mountain kings built their halls above it, old when "old" still meant something down here. In those days the Court had been a court indeed: candles lit by their own devotion, halls full of petitioners, offerings left at the threshold every solstice. Bread. Salt. First fruits. Names spoken aloud so they would not be forgotten. Small things. Enough things.

A court does not need much to live. It needs only to be *remembered*.

Then Aldous the First had come down the stair with a contract in his fist and a soul in a jar, and he had said, in the language of lawyers, which is the language of hunger wearing a clean shirt: *"I cannot pay you in gold, for gold is nothing to you. But I can pay you in the one coin that never spoils."*

And the Regent, foolish with famine, had accepted.

One soul. It should have been enough. A single soul, properly kept, burns slowly — a candle in a sealed room, giving light and warmth for generations. The Court had rationed it, hoarded it, dreamed over it the way a starving man dreams over a crust he cannot bring himself to eat. For fifty years that soul had been the difference between flickering and going out.

But Aldous told stories. That was the trouble with kings; they could not keep a good trick to themselves. And so there had been another sale, and another — a baron who wanted a son, a queen who wanted rain, a general who wanted to outlive his enemies — until the Court's shelves filled with jars and the Court itself grew fat and strange on the only food it had ever truly been offered.

*We never asked for souls,* the Regent thought, watching the taster's careful eyes. *We asked to be remembered. Souls were simply what they brought instead, because souls are easier than remembering.*

And then, inevitably, the kingdom forgot.

Not all at once. Forgetting is a tide, not a wave. First the solstice offerings thinned, then stopped. Then the stair's upper door was walled over and called a cellar, then a rumor, then nothing at all. The priests wrote the Court out of their calendars. Mothers stopped telling children its name — and a thing without a name in mortal mouths begins, very slowly, to go out.

The Court had survived on its shelves ever since. On debts. On the fine print. On the slow burning of other people's continuities, because it had none of its own left.

*You call us thieves,* the Regent thought. *But we are the pantry the thieves built.*

---

It rose from the throne of unlit candles, and the motion cost it more than they would ever know. Every gesture down here was paid for. It had learned to move as little as possible, the way a poor household learns to burn one candle in three rooms rather than three candles in one.

The child flinched. Bramble stepped half in front of her, arms wide, muttering something about iron and rowan that would not have blistered a moth.

"I am not going to eat her," the Regent said.

Its voice came out wrong — rusted, many-voiced, the sound of a choir that had been locked in a cellar. It heard the mortals' spines stiffen and felt the old shame, the shame of a beggar whose rags frighten the very people he begs from.

"That is the first thing you must understand, if you are to bargain honestly," it said. "I am not going to eat her. I am going to *keep* her. There is a difference, and the difference is everything."

Yara Coldharbour's chin lifted. Brave, the Regent noted. Poison-proofed, yes — it could taste her immunities from across the hall, forty years of small suicides folded into her blood — but bravery was not something you could swallow your way toward. She had chosen this. He filed that away. Choice was rare currency down here.

"Keep her how?" the taster asked. "She's a child."

"She is a *hearth*," the Regent said. "Or she will be, once she is shelved properly. Do you know what a soul does in a jar? No. Of course you don't. Your guild trades them like coins and never once asks where the coin goes after the transaction." It gestured, wearily, at the galleries rising into the dark — thousands of shelves, thousands of jars, each one faintly luminous, each one warm. "Look at them. Really look."

They looked. Even Odile Vane, who had drafted more contracts down here than any mortal alive, looked.

"Warmth," the Regent said. "Light. Continuity. A soul in keeping gives off exactly what a forgotten court needs to survive — memory, in its rawest form. Each jar is a life being lived somewhere else, reflected here. When your farmers dream of their grandmothers' kitchens, some of that dream leaks down the stair. When your lovers swear forever, forever echoes here and keeps the walls standing. I do not devour anything, taster. I *harbor*. I am a lighthouse for the light other people make and do not miss."

"And the child?" Yara's voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman tasting something bitter and refusing to spit. "What does she give off?"

The Regent paused.

Honesty, it had learned over four centuries, was the most disarming weapon in any negotiation, precisely because no one expected it from a monster. And besides — what did it have left to protect? Its secrets had all been spent. Only its reasons remained, and no one had ever asked for those.

"The child is different," it said. "Her soul was sold before it was finished. Do you understand what that means? Most souls come to me whole — a life already lived, already flavored. Hers was taken green. It has been on my shelves for six years, ripening in the dark, and it has become..." The borrowed shepherd's face struggled with the word. "...loud. A young soul burns brighter than an old one. It remembers harder. It *wants* harder. If I shelve her, she will light these halls for five hundred years. Perhaps a thousand."

"So you admit it," Bramble snarled. "You'd burn her."

"Burn?" The Regent felt something ancient and tired curdle in its chest. "Little witch, I have watched every fire in this mountain go out one by one. I have sat alone in the dark so long that I borrowed a dead man's face just to remember what faces were for. You call it burning. I call it the last warmth in the world, and I am the only creature left willing to sit beside it."

The silence that followed was long, and the Regent let it be long. Silence was free.

---

It turned, because turning away was also a kind of honesty, and led them — or let them follow, which amounted to the same thing — down the central gallery, past shelf after shelf of softly glowing jars.

"This one," it said, stopping. "Do you see?"

In the jar swirled something like golden light and something like a summer afternoon. The Regent did not touch the glass. It never touched the glass.

"A miller's daughter. Sold by her father in 1213 to pay a gambling debt. She lived to be eighty-four, married, had seven children, died in her bed surrounded by people who loved her." It looked at Yara. "You thought I took her life. I took nothing. She lived every day of it. I merely keep the *echo*, and the echo feeds me. Is that theft? Perhaps. Is it murder? Show me the body. There is no body. There never is."

Lord Aldery had gone very pale. The Regent knew why without looking — somewhere behind them, up nine shelves and three galleries, a jar with a girl's laughter inside it had just grown a little warmer, the way a sleeping dog stirs when its name is spoken. It said nothing. Some recognitions must be allowed their privacy.

"And yet," Odile Vane said, and her voice was silk over a blade, "the contracts require *equivalent payment*. Which means you set a price on continuation. Which means you have decided whose continuing matters more — theirs, or yours."

"Yes," the Regent said simply.

Odile blinked. The Regent had surprised her. Two hundred years of scheming, and it had surprised her with a one-syllable answer.

"You want me to pretend otherwise," the Regent went on. "You want me to dress it up — the old laws, the sacred debts, the balance of things. I wrote none of those lies myself; mortals brought them down the stair and I signed them because signing was cheaper than starving. But I will not insult you with pretense, Vane. You above all people know what it is to be exiled from the world you made. So hear it plainly:

"I am starving. My court is forgotten. Every year the walls thin and the dark comes closer to the throne, and the only thing between me and the quiet is whatever mortals are careless enough to sell me. I do not want cruelty. Cruelty is expensive and I cannot afford it. What I want—" and here the borrowed face did something terrible and small, something almost like pleading— "*what I want is to continue.*"

The candles around the throne stayed unlit. They always stayed unlit. The Regent had not had the strength to light them in ninety years, and it kept them anyway, arranged in their hundreds, because a throne of unlit candles is at least a *plan*.

---

"So void the debt," Yara said suddenly.

The Regent turned. The taster stood with her arms crossed, poison-ringed blood singing quietly under her skin, looking at it the way no one had looked at it in four hundred years — not with fear, not with pity, but with the appraising eye of someone balancing a ledger.

"You're starving," she said. "Fine. I believe you. So stop eating crumbs and tell me why you can't have bread. The original sale — Aldous and the child. Void it. Prove it was never valid. Then take payment that doesn't come off anyone's shelf."

"There is no bread," the Regent said. "There has been no bread since your kingdom put my name out of its mouth. A voided contract returns the soul to the child, and I grow one winter colder. That is arithmetic, taster, not bargaining."

"Unless," Odile said softly, "the debt was never honest to begin with."

The Regent went very still. Down here, stillness was survival, and it had perfected stillness — but this stillness was different. This was the stillness of a card player watching an opponent turn over a card that should not exist.

"What do you mean," it said carefully, "never honest?"

"Come to the trial and find out," Odile said, smiling the smile of a woman who had hidden a flaw in a king's contract two centuries ago and had waited, patient as rot, for the wood to show. "Or refuse us. Keep your shelves. Keep your loud, green, unfinished little hearth. And spend eternity wondering whether the foundation stone of your whole larder was stolen goods."

The Regent looked at her for a long time. Around them the jars glowed, patient and warm, ten thousand borrowed lives keeping the dark at bay. And beneath the glow, beneath the hunger, beneath even the terrible arithmetic of survival, something in the Regent stirred that it had not felt since the last offering rotted on the threshold above —

Hope. Ugly, dangerous, expensive hope.

"If you are lying to me," it said, and its many voices dropped to a single whisper, "I will shelve every one of you, and I will keep you warm for a very long time."

"Noted," said Odile Vane. "Shall we say midwinter?"

But the Regent was no longer listening. Because far above them — up the stair, through the walled-over door, through the cellar and the rumor and the forgetting — it had felt something it had not felt in ninety years.

Footsteps. Deliberate ones. Descending.

And they were carrying a document stamped with the seal of King Aldous the First.
