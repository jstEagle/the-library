## 15. Breaking the Archive

The palace slept the way a snake sleeps: coiled, watchful, and warm in all the wrong places.

Yara knew its sleep better than anyone. She had walked its corridors at every hour for eleven years, first as a nervous girl with a tongue too honest for her own good, then as the Taster, the woman whose mouth was worth more than the crown jewels because it had learned, drop by swallowed drop, to refuse to die. She knew which floorboards sang underfoot and which held their breath. She knew that the guards on the west stair changed at the half-hour past midnight and that Sergeant Halloway always arrived four minutes late, chewing something he wasn't supposed to have. She knew that the lamps in the long gallery were trimmed but never extinguished, so that the portraits of dead kings could go on watching nothing.

She had never once used any of this knowledge against the palace.

Tonight she dressed in the dark of her own narrow room above the tasting chambers, and her hands shook so badly she had to start the lacing twice.

*This is treason,* said the voice in her head that sounded like her mother. *This is the gallows wearing slippers.*

*This is a child,* said another voice, newer, rougher, smelling of woodsmoke and Bramble's feverfew tea. *A child with no name and no soul, sleeping in a guildhall bed with three people taking turns sitting up with her because none of them can bear to leave her alone.*

Yara finished the lacing. She pulled on the plain grey servant's coat that opened every door below the third floor, and she did not look at herself in the mirror, because she was afraid of what she'd see — and more afraid that it would look like someone finally becoming herself.

* * *

The archive lived beneath the east wing, behind a door that most courtiers believed was a wine cellar and most servants believed was haunted. Both were wrong. It was worse than either: it was where the kingdom kept its memory, and kingdoms are never careful with what they remember only grudgingly.

Yara's access came from her tongue, as everything did. The Royal Taster was required to certify, quarterly, that the archive's preservation wines — used, absurdly, to treat the vellum against rot — had not been tampered with. Some clerk two hundred years ago had decided that a man who could taste nightshade in milk could taste arsenic in wine, and no one had ever thought to revoke the privilege. Her seal opened the outer door. Her presence explained itself. A taster in the archive was an oddity, but oddities attached to her office were forgiven; the whole point of Yara Coldharbour was that she was permitted to be strange.

The inner door was harder. It answered to a key kept on the Lord Archivist's chain, and the Lord Archivist slept like the dead in his rooms above — or so she'd been told by a kitchen boy she'd bribed with candied ginger, back when sweetness still meant something to her.

But Odile Vane had spent two centuries thinking about doors, and before Yara left the lighthouse, the exiled founder had pressed a thin strip of grey metal into her palm and said only: "Old locks dream of being young again. This reminds them."

It worked. Of course it worked. The lock sighed open like an old man remembering his first love, and Yara slipped into the dark, pulling the door shut until only a finger's width of gap remained, and stood there letting her eyes adjust while her heart attempted to break several records.

Lamplight from the gallery above leaked down through a grate, thin and grey as winter milk. By it she saw shelves — ranks of them, going back further than light reached, loaded with boxes, scrolls, ledgers, the sediment of two hundred years of royal paperwork. Somewhere in this sediment was a confession. Odile had been certain of it. *Kings who steal write letters,* she'd said. *Kings who steal always write letters. Guilt is the one thing they cannot delegate.*

*King Aldous I. The year of the Hollow Winter. Anything bearing his private seal.*

Yara began at the beginning, which was also, helpfully, the oldest end.

* * *

An hour in, her fingers were grey with dust and her mind had begun cataloguing poisons it wasn't finding. That was how she thought — she couldn't help it. Every box was a dish; every document, an ingredient. She read for traces of the man the way she read a suspect meal: *bitterness here, in the marginalia — fear here, in the handwriting's tremor — something sweet buried underneath, and sweetness in a king's papers is always the most dangerous flavor of all.*

She found tax rolls. She found a treaty with a nation that no longer existed. She found a recipe for quince preserve in Aldous's own hand, annotated crossly (*too much clove*), and she stopped breathing for a moment, because there it was — the sweetness, the human sweetness, of a man who had sold a child's soul beneath the mountain and still fussed about cloves.

Then she found the box marked only with a black ribbon, and her skin went cold in the way it did when her tongue found something wrong before her mind knew why.

Inside: correspondence. Aldous's hand, younger than the recipe, shakier. Letters to someone called only *the Understeward*, and the Understeward's replies, written in ink that even now, two centuries later, seemed to drink the lamplight rather than reflect it.

*The Court has agreed to defer collection one generation,* read one line. *It asks only that the payment be delivered willing, or made willing.*

And beneath it, in Aldous's hand, cramped and hurried as though the pen were fleeing:

*I did not sell what was mine to sell. I sold what I stole. Let this letter stand witness against me, whoever finds it, whenever they find it. I took the child from the woman at the ford. I took the name out of her mouth myself. God forgive me, the Court asked for so little and I gave it so much less than it deserved, and now it comes due, and it is not mine to pay, and I am glad, I am glad it is not mine to pay—*

Yara sat down on the cold stone floor with the letter in both hands and shook.

Not guilt alone. Proof. The exact proof Odile needed: the child — the child asleep upstairs in the guildhall, the child who had spoken a language older than kingdoms through a mouth that shouldn't work — had never belonged to Aldous at all. He had stolen her. Stolen her name, her soul, her mother, her winter. And a thief cannot sell what is not his. The contract was rotten at the root.

They could void it. They could save her.

Yara laughed — one small, cracked sound in the vast dark — and clapped both hands over her own mouth too late.

* * *

The lamplight through the grate changed.

She hadn't heard footsteps. No one had passed on the gallery above. But the quality of the grey light had shifted, the way water shifts when something moves beneath it, and Yara Coldharbour had survived eleven years in a nest of vipers by trusting exactly that kind of instinct.

She killed the letter into her coat, closed the box, and was three shelves into shadow when the inner door opened.

Lord-Commander Aurel Vessant stood in the doorway holding a lamp, and the light found her before she could decide whether to run.

Of course it was him. Courtier, confidant, the man who had spent three weeks asking gentle questions about her absences with the patient smile of a fisherman watching a line. He wore a dressing gown over his uniform, which somehow made it worse — this was not duty, this was personal.

"Taster," he said pleasantly. "Odd hour for certifying wine."

"The quarterly—" she began.

"It was done in autumn." He stepped inside and let the door swing shut behind him. "I checked."

The silence stretched. Yara measured it the way she measured everything: *how much time do I have, what can I swallow, what will kill me.* There was no poison here she could take. There was only the truth in her coat, burning against her ribs like a coal.

Vessant drifted along the shelves, trailing one finger over the boxes. "Do you know what I do with my evenings, Coldharbour? I read old files. It's a dull habit. And three weeks ago I read yours." He turned. "Did you know you were recommended for your post by a reference that doesn't exist? A housekeeper in Coldharbour village who died the year before you were born?"

"I was an orphan," Yara said. "Records are imperfect."

"Records are perfect," Vessant said softly. "That's what makes them useful. Yours were *made*. Someone built you a history, fitted you into this palace like a key into a lock, and I have spent three weeks wondering who turns you." His lamp rose. "So. Who turns you, Taster? Is it money? Lovers? Or is it whatever's in your coat that you're holding so very still?"

Yara's heart was a drum, and the drum was saying *run*, and her feet were saying *nowhere to run*, and she thought, with strange clarity: *so this is how it ends. Not the gallows. A quiet room, a quiet man, a lamp.*

"Give me the letter," Vessant said, "and we'll discuss what mercy costs these days."

She didn't move. She was trying to calculate whether she could reach the door, whether the strip of grey metal worked on locks from the inside, whether—

"Commander." A new voice, from the outer doorway. Flat, bored, official. "You're wanted. Gate alarm. Third time tonight — drunk merchants, probably, but protocol says rank attends."

A guard stood silhouetted in the wine-cellar door, halberd grounded, face half-lit. Yara knew him distantly: Tam Wren, young, unremarkable, the sort of man palaces grew like moss and never noticed. He had once stood post outside the tasting room for six hours without shifting his weight, and she remembered thinking: *there's a man who understands waiting.*

Vessant's jaw tightened. "I'm occupied."

"Protocol says," Tam repeated, with the magnificent stubbornness of the very ordinary.

For a moment — one long, ringing moment — Yara was certain it would come to violence, or worse, to questions. Then Vessant straightened his dressing gown, smiled at her with terrible patience, and said: "We'll finish this conversation, Taster. Soon. Locks keep, and so do I."

He swept out. Tam Wren held the door, met Yara's eyes across the dark — and winked.

* * *

She followed him out. She couldn't think of anything else to do. He walked her through the service corridors at a pace that was neither flight nor stroll, and only when they reached the junction by the buttery did he stop and say, quietly:

"Gate alarm was real. Drunk merchants. I just mentioned it loudly enough to be overheard." He looked at her boots, then up. "You're the one who leaves bread on the scullery windowsill in winter. For the yard cats. Everyone thinks it's the cook."

"So?"

"So nobody who feeds cats in secret is selling secrets." He shifted his weight, that same endless-patient stance. "Also, my sister went missing twenty years ago. Winter child. Nobody would even say her name after." His voice didn't change at all, which was how Yara knew it cost him everything. "If whatever you're doing has anything to do with the winter children — don't tell me. Just don't get caught. And if you need a gate alarm again—" he tapped the wall twice, "—knock there."

He walked away before she could thank him, because thanking him would have made it real, and real things could be reported.

Yara stood in the dark corridor with a king's confession against her heart and a stranger's loyalty sitting warm and improbable in her chest, and she understood suddenly what Bramble had been trying to teach her for months: family is not the thing you're born into. It's the thing that keeps accumulating, in unlikely places, if you let it.

* * *

She was three streets from the palace when she allowed herself to open the coat and check the letter, as if the dark might have stolen it.

Still there. Aldous's cramped, fleeing hand. *I took the child from the woman at the ford.*

Proof. Root-rot for the contract. Everything Odile needed to tear the sale apart before midwinter.

Yara started to run.

She ran through the sleeping city with snow beginning to fall, past shuttered stalls and guttering lamps, and the terror of the archive transmuted as she ran, alchemy she hadn't known she could perform, terror becoming speed becoming joy — wild, disbelieving triumph, the taste of it sharp and clean on a tongue that had tasted everything and belonged nowhere.

The guildhall door was lit. Inside, voices. The child, awake, laughing at something — actually laughing, a sound like a bell underwater.

Yara hammered the knock-pattern and burst in on a scene she would remember for the rest of her life: Bramble mid-story with a wooden spoon for a wand, Odile by the fire looking two hundred years young, the child wrapped in three blankets and grinning with a mouth full of honey-bread she couldn't taste but clearly loved anyway.

"I have it," Yara gasped, holding up the letter like a torch. "I have his confession. We can prove it. We can *void it*—"

Odile crossed the room faster than a woman her age should move and took the letter, and read, and read again, and then looked up at Yara with an expression Yara had never seen on that ancient, guarded face.

It looked almost like hope.

"You beautiful, reckless fool," Odile said. "Do you know what you've done?"

"No," Yara admitted, breathless, laughing, snow melting in her hair. "Tell me at breakfast. Tell me all of it. But Odile—" and here her voice caught, because the whole night was catching up with her at once, the lamp and the locked room and the wink of an ordinary man, "—we have to be fast. Vessant knows. He doesn't know *what*, but he knows something, and he'll come back to that archive, and when he finds the box empty—"

Odile folded the letter with great care, as though it were a living thing.

"Then we go down the mountain," she said, "before he climbs out of it."

Outside, far beneath the city, in the deep places where old debts are kept, something stirred — and began, gently, hungrily, to count the days remaining.
