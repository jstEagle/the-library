## 16. The Courtier's Choice

Lord Cassian Vey had spent thirty years learning to notice what other people missed.

It was not a gift so much as a discipline, hammered into him by his father, who had been under-secretary to two kings and had survived both by the simple expedient of seeing everything and saying almost nothing of it. *The court is a table set with knives,* his father used to say, sharpening one such knife on the wet stone of his own voice. *You do not need to be clever, Cassian. You need only be the last one at the table who remembers where everything is placed.*

Cassian had remembered where everything was placed for three decades now, and it had made him, if not loved, then indispensable — which in the palace of Vell was worth more than love ever was.

Which was why the matter of Yara Coldharbour had begun to itch at him like a splinter worked beneath the skin.

* * *

He had first noticed it in autumn, in the small way these things always begin: a discrepancy in the rhythm of a person's days.

Yara Coldharbour kept perfect hours. That was the first thing anyone learned about her. She arrived at the kitchens before the fires were fully lit; she tasted the king's breakfast while the yolk of his eggs still trembled; she stood behind the royal chairs at supper like a gray shadow given a fork. Her tongue was worth more than the crown jewels and everyone knew it, and everyone also knew that Yara treated her own value with the indifference of a woman cataloguing someone else's silverware. She was precise. She was punctual. She was, as far as Cassian could determine over years of watching, entirely without vices — which was itself suspicious, because he had never yet met a person without vices, only people who hid them well enough to survive.

And then, in the last weeks before midwinter, she began to vanish.

Not dramatically. Nothing so vulgar as absence from her post. But there were gaps — an hour here, an evening there — that had not existed before. The night the king's cousin hosted his infamous masked dinner, Yara left the tasting room early, pleading a headache, and Cassian, lingering in the corridor with a cup of watered wine he did not want, watched her cross the outer courtyard through the rain without once looking back at the palace that owned her life.

She moved like a woman going somewhere she was expected.

That was the detail that caught him. Courtiers leaving the palace at odd hours moved like fugitives — hunched, hurried, glancing over their shoulders at doors that never opened. Yara Coldharbour walked out into the storm with her chin up and her stride even, as though the rain were merely weather and the city merely a place she belonged.

People who belonged nowhere in particular did not walk that way.

Cassian began, quietly, professionally, to follow her.

* * *

He was careful about it. Thirty years at a court full of poisoners had taught him patience the way a riverbed is taught smoothness — slowly, and against his grain, and permanently.

He learned that she visited a chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane that appeared to sell no candles; that she entered through a door painted the exact gray of the surrounding wall; that when she emerged, hours later, there was often candlelight in windows above it, and laughter — actual laughter — spilling out with the warmth when someone opened the door to shake a rug.

He learned that a hedge-witch named Bramble bought arsenic from the same apothecary twice in one month and paid in buttons.

He learned that a woman answering to the name Odile Vane — a name that should have been impossible, a name attached to a traitor dead two hundred years or imprisoned forever, depending which version of history you subscribed to — had been seen on the docks asking questions about the old stair into the mountain.

And on the night of the third week of winter, he learned that Yara Coldharbour went into the sealed royal archive.

* * *

The archive sat beneath the east wing like a stone heart, and its locks were older than the dynasty's current spelling. Cassian held one of only six keys, by right of his office as Keeper of the King's Records of Household Debt — a title that sounded tedious and was, in fact, exactly as tedious as it sounded, which was precisely why no one had ever thought to take it from him. Kings gave away crowns more readily than they gave away ledgers of what they owed.

He had noticed the key missing from his own ring two nights prior. Noticed it returned, too, smelling faintly of tallow smoke, and said nothing — because saying nothing was the discipline, and discipline was all he had.

But tonight he followed.

The archive stairs were cold enough to ache. Cassian descended them slowly, one hand on the rail worn hollow by generations of clerks, and paused at the bottom, in the dark that smelled of dust and vellum and the particular sweetness of paper slowly dying, and listened.

There. A sound like a page turning. Then another.

He came around the shelves of the Aldous collection — the sealed section, the forbidden section, the section every Keeper was instructed to inventory annually and read never — and stopped.

Yara Coldharbour stood at a reading desk with a single candle, and she was holding a letter so old its seal had crumbled, and she was crying.

She did not sob. She was not a woman built for sobbing, Cassian suspected; she had been built, like himself, out of discipline and silence. But tears ran down her face in two steady lines, and she read on, and turned another page with fingers that shook only slightly, and did not hear him until the floorboard betrayed him with a soft groan of old wood.

She looked up.

Cassian had imagined this moment perhaps a dozen times since autumn — rehearsed it, the way one rehearses any confrontation, arming himself with the correct words, the correct tone, the correct amount of menace. In his imaginings, Yara Coldharbour froze, or fled, or reached for whatever weapon a food-taster carried.

In reality, she closed the letter carefully, folded her hands atop it, and said, "How much did you see?"

"Enough," Cassian said. "The confession of King Aldous the First, in his own hand. The account of a soul purchased from—" he paused, savoring the absurdity even as he said it, "—from a child's mother, for the price of a winter that would not end."

"Then you know," Yara said.

"I know that you have committed treason of a magnitude this palace has not seen in two centuries," Cassian said, "and that you are standing in the most guarded room in Vell, alone, past midnight, weeping over a dead king's handwriting." He stepped closer. "I know that you could not possibly expect to leave here unseen, which means either that you are very foolish — and I have watched you for thirty years, Mistress Coldharbour, and foolish is the one thing you have never been — or that you no longer care what happens to you."

Something flickered across her face then. Not fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman meeting, unexpectedly, another creature of her own species.

"And what will you do?" she asked. "Call the guard?"

"I am the guard," Cassian said dryly. "Or near enough. Six keys, remember. No one enters or leaves this room without my signature."

"Then I'm already caught."

"Yes," he agreed. "Entirely. Utterly. And yet—" He found, to his own surprise, that his hands were not reaching for the bell-cord, and had not been for some time. "And yet here I stand, listening to myself talk, instead of pulling it."

"Why?" Yara asked. And then, with the directness that had apparently survived thirty years of court life intact inside her: "What do you want, Lord Vey?"

Cassian opened his mouth. Closed it.

Because the truth was that he did not know what he wanted, and had not known since the moment, three weeks ago, when he had followed her through the rain to Candlewick Lane and seen the light in the windows above the chandler's shop, and heard the laughter, and felt something in his chest turn over with the slow, grinding reluctance of a door long rusted shut.

"I had a sister," he said.

Yara blinked.

"Her name was Liss," Cassian continued, and was astonished to find his voice holding, mostly. "I was nine when she was taken. They called it fever, in the household records. A winter fever. My mother grieved; my father wrote the year off in the ledger as a bad harvest and moved on. But I remember—" His jaw tightened. "I remember the man who came. Not a physician. He wore gray, and he did not knock, and when he took her hand she stopped crying. She'd cried for three days. She stopped, and smiled at me, and went with him, and I have spent forty years telling myself I dreamed it."

The candle flame leaned. Neither of them moved to straighten it.

"Two weeks ago," Cassian said, "I finally opened the ledger of my father's debts. The ones he kept apart from the crown's. There was an entry, in his hand, dated the year Liss died. *Paid in full: one winter's mercy, one daughter's portion.*" He looked at Yara, and let her see, for the first time in thirty years, what lived underneath the discipline. "I have spent every night since reading everything this archive holds about the Hollow Court. Every contract. Every sale. Every sealed confession. Do you know how many entries there are?"

"No," Yara said softly.

"One hundred and eleven," Cassian said. "One hundred and eleven souls sold out of this city by kings and lords and merchants who wanted their winters short and their fortunes long. One hundred and eleven names in the margins, most of them children, most of them recorded as fevers." He drew a breath. "Your mute child is the hundred and twelfth. And I have spent thirty years being the last man at the table who remembers where everything is placed, and I am tired, Mistress Coldharbour. I am so profoundly tired of remembering things for people who use them to hurt each other."

The silence that followed was long, and warm somehow, despite the cold of the archive — the specific warmth of a wall coming down.

"So," Yara said at last. "You're not going to call the guard."

"I'm going to tell the guard," Cassian said, "that I found evidence of damp in the Aldous collection and ordered the section resealed for preservation. It's a plausible lie; I've told better ones for worse reasons." He extended his hand, palm open, toward the letter she guarded. "And then I am going to ask you what you need from this room, and how quickly you need it, and whether your guild has room at its table for a boring man with six keys and forty years of grudges."

Yara stared at him. Then, slowly, she laughed — a small, cracked, startled sound, as if laughter were a language she was relearning.

"The guildhall smells like bread," she said, "and Bramble will try to feed you until you can't move, and a child with no voice will study you for an hour and then decide, silently, that you're acceptable. Odile will terrify you. You'll get used to it."

"Odile Vane," Cassian said. "Who has been dead for two hundred years."

"She'll enjoy hearing that," Yara said. "She collects eulogies."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night.

By candlelight, in the sealed heart of the kingdom, Yara showed Cassian what she had found — Aldous's confession, the theft dressed up as a purchase, the clause Odile claimed to have planted in the contract two centuries ago. Cassian read it twice, and then fetched from his own private shelf the one document he had never shown another living soul: his father's ledger, with its terrible neat entry, and beside it — because his father had been thorough even in his sins — a copy of the original bill of sale.

"Liss's soul was sold," he said, laying it flat between them, "by a man acting under Aldous's seal, using the same contract language. If your Odile's flaw holds for one sale, it holds for all of them. Every one of those hundred and eleven entries is voidable — if we can prove the sellers never rightfully owned what they sold."

"You realize what you're proposing," Yara said quietly. "Not just freeing one child. Unraveling two hundred years of royal debt. Every family in this city that profited—"

"I've spent thirty years watching those families eat," Cassian said. "I find I've lost my appetite."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something no courtier had done to him in decades: she took his hand — the hand that held the keys, the hand that signed the seals — and pressed it briefly, firmly, like a pledge.

"Welcome to the Guild of Impossible Things, Lord Vey," she said. "First lesson: we don't say impossible anymore. We say *pending*."

* * *

Dawn was still an hour off when Cassian climbed the archive stairs with the letters hidden against his chest and the damp-and-preservation lie already composed for the morning clerk. At the top of the stair, in the gray sliver of window that faced the mountain, he paused.

The peak stood black against the paling sky, as it had every day of his life, patient and permanent.

Except that this morning, as he watched, the snow along its ridgeline began to fall upward — a fine bright mist of it lifting off the stone and streaming into the darkening clouds above, as though the mountain were inhaling after a very long sleep.

Beneath his feet, faint but unmistakable, the archive floor hummed.

Somewhere below the city, something had finished counting the days remaining — and had decided, with the gentle hunger of a thing that had waited two hundred years, that it would not wait for midwinter after all.

Cassian gripped his keys, and ran.
