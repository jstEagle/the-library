## 17. Midwinter Comes Early

The summons came as a smell.

Yara was in the guildhall kitchen, elbows deep in flour, when the air above the hearth turned the color of old pennies. Not a smell, exactly — the *absence* of one. Every scent in the room drained away at once: the bread, the woodsmoke, Bramble's rosemary hanging in bundles from the rafters, gone, as if the whole hall had been plunged underwater. Then came the cold, rising out of the floorboards like water filling a boot, climbing her ankles, her knees, and with it a taste she knew too well — she had swallowed it once on the Regent's tongue, a courtesy draught offered with a smile full of teeth — the flavor of a candle burning down to nothing.

*Hurry,* said the absence. *The debt is hungry. The debt is due.*

She dropped the spoon. Flour ghosted up around her wrists. Across the hall, Wren looked up from mending a net, and the child — Pip, though they had not yet agreed on the name, though the child had only begun to own it — went rigid by the window, small hands pressed flat against the glass as if holding back something on the other side of the world.

"It's early," Yara said. Her voice came out flat and professional, the taster's voice, the voice that had catalogued three poisons in a king's supper without so much as a blink. "Midwinter is eleven days off. It isn't supposed to—"

"The mountain doesn't keep our calendar." Odile Vane stood in the doorway of the ledger room, and even now, even freed from two hundred years in her lighthouse of stopped time, she moved like someone perpetually surprised that rooms still contained her. She carried the contract in both hands — the original, the one with the flaw sewn into its fine print — and the parchment was *bleeding*. Ink welled up through the fibers in beads that ran like sweat down a fevered brow, pooling at the bottom edge, dripping onto the floorboards where each drop hissed and vanished.

Yara crossed the hall in six strides and took the contract from her. The paper was warm. Worse than warm — it had a pulse, faint and arrhythmic, like a heart remembering how to fail.

"The collection clause," Odile said quietly. "I told you the Regent would find a way around me. I just thought we'd have until midwinter to be smarter than it."

* * *

They gathered around the long table while the light outside went strange — a bruised yellow, the yellow of the hour before a storm, though the sky held no clouds, only a pressure, as if the whole city were being pressed gently between two enormous palms.

Bramble laid out what they knew, because Bramble always laid out what they knew; it steadied her, the hedge-witch, to make lists. "The Regent has invoked acceleration. Old law, older than Aldous. When a debtor flees the debt—" she glanced at the child, who sat very straight in their chair, hands folded, watching the adults with those ancient, patient eyes that made Yara's chest ache every single time, "—the Court may collect at its own pace rather than wait for the appointed hour."

"We didn't flee," Wren said. "We walked out the front door. There's a difference."

"There's a difference in poetry," Odile said. "There is no difference in contracts."

Yara read the bleeding clause twice, three times, her taster's discipline holding her steady the way it always had — cataloguing, measuring, refusing panic the way she refused poison. *Should the debtor seek evasion, the Court reserves the right of immediate consumption, the soul to be taken whole and unbroken, that nothing be wasted.* Whole and unbroken. She thought of the market beneath the mountain, the shelves of jars glowing like preserves in a larder, each one labeled in a script that hurt to look at. She thought of the child's jar, third shelf, second row, humming faintly with everything that had been stolen — a name, a mother, a winter.

"How fast?" she asked.

Odile's jaw tightened. "By my reading — and I wrote this damnable thing, so my reading should be authoritative — consumption begins at moonrise tonight."

"And finishes?"

"Before dawn. After that there is no jar. There is no soul to return. There is only the Regent, fed, and the mountain, sleeping, and a child walking around hollow for the rest of a long, hollow life."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere upstairs, the ledger began translating on its own again, pages turning themselves, the old language of the child's people whispering through the guildhall like wind through a keyhole.

Then Pip stood up.

The child crossed to Yara and took her hand — took it deliberately, the way an adult does, fingers curling with intention — and pressed something into her palm. A button. A plain bone button, worn smooth, the kind that comes off a coat that has been loved past all sense and kept anyway. The child closed Yara's fingers over it and looked up at her, and though the child could not yet speak more than fragments — the voice was coming back in pieces, a word here, half a sentence there, each recovered syllable costing them sleep — their eyes said it plainly enough in any language:

*I know what it costs to go back down. Go anyway. Please.*

Yara knelt. This close, she could see the hollowness in the child properly, the way you can see a well's depth only when you lean over it: a faint translucence behind the ribs, a flicker where a reflection ought to sit in the eye. Two hundred years ago a king had sold this. Had weighed a soul against a crown's convenience and signed his name beneath the scales.

"Nobody," Yara said, "is feeding you to anything. Not tonight, not ever. Hold that thought like a stone. Do you hear me? Like a stone in your pocket."

Pip nodded once, gravely, and patted Yara's pocket where the button now sat, as if confirming the arrangement.

* * *

They went down through the doorway that wasn't there, the one only the child could open, and Yara felt the mountain notice them before they were three steps in.

On the first descent, weeks ago, the stair had been merely wrong — a door in a cellar wall that opened onto cold air and descending dark, steps that smelled of snow though no snow fell underground. Now the mountain was *closing*. She heard it the way she heard poisons: not loudly, but unmistakably. Stone grinding somewhere far below, patient and vast, a sound like a giant turning over in bed. The walls of the passage had begun to lean inward, imperceptibly, the way a hand closes finger by finger.

"Move," Odile said, and for once there was no theater in her voice, no flourish, only the flat urgency of a woman who had drafted contracts for kings and knew exactly what the ink did when it dried. "Don't run — running spends breath you'll want later — but move like your supper depends on it."

They descended. Yara counted steps because counting steadied her, the same trick she used at the king's table: one for arsenic, two for henbane, three for the bitter almond under the plum sauce. Four hundred steps on the first descent. At three hundred and ten the stair simply ended — not blocked, but *unwritten*, the steps dissolving into raw rock ahead of them as smoothly as if they had never existed, and beyond it, the antechamber of unlit candles stretching into darkness where darkness had no business being.

Wren swore softly. Bramble lit a candle from her sleeve — hedge-witches carry fire the way other women carry pins — and the flame bent sideways, toward the depths, as if the mountain were inhaling.

"It's eating the road behind us and the road ahead," Odile murmured. She pressed her palm flat to the blank wall where the stairs had been. "It wants us late. If we arrive after consumption begins, the contract executes itself and no argument, no confession, no flaw in the fine print matters. The Court honors punctuality above all things. I built it that way." Her voice caught, just slightly, on the last word. "Gods, I built it that way."

"Then we aren't late," Yara said. "Because I refuse."

Bramble snorted. "Refusal's a lovely spell but I've never gotten it to light a candle."

"Then we do it the hard way." Yara turned to the child, kneeling again, ignoring the cold seeping up through the stone into her knees. "Pip. You opened this door once. The mountain is trying to shut it, but doors remember being opened. Can you ask it — not command it, *ask* it — to remember?"

The child studied her for a long moment. Then they stepped forward, laid one small hand against the blank wall, and spoke.

What came out of them was not sound, exactly. It was older than sound. The syllables moved through the stone like frost moving through water, and Yara felt them in her fillings, in the roots of her teeth, in the marrow the Guild of Impossible Things had no ledger column for. The child asked the mountain to remember being a door. And the mountain — slowly, grudgingly, with the groan of something waking from a long and resentful nap — remembered.

Steps bloomed out of the rock like flowers out of cracks. Ahead, not behind. Downward.

"Ask it for more," Odile breathed. "Child, ask it for the whole road."

* * *

The antechambers of borrowed memories were worse on the second visit. Yara had prepared herself for the candles — thousands of them, unlit, each one holding somebody's recollection like wax holds scent — but she had not prepared for how fast they were going *out*. As they hurried through, candles guttered to nothing in waves around them, and each extinguishing released its memory into the air for a heartbeat before it dissolved: a woman laughing in a kitchen, a boy flying a kite shaped like a fish, an old man's hands teaching smaller hands to whittle. Grief everywhere, evaporating. The Regent was burning through its hoard to fuel the collection.

"Don't breathe them in," Bramble warned, wadding rosemary against her nose. "Memories that aren't yours will stick to your lungs."

Yara breathed through her mouth and tasted the mountain instead: iron, ice, and underneath it all that candle-burning-down-to-nothing flavor, stronger now, closer, the Regent's hunger thickening the air like smoke before a house fire. Her taster's tongue, cursed gift that it was, read the depth of the danger the way a sailor reads weather. They had hours. Maybe fewer. The mountain's pulse was quickening; she could feel it through the soles of her boots, a rhythm like a feast being set.

In the market of borrowed things, the shelves were half-empty.

Yara stopped dead. On their first descent, the larder of souls had stretched away into the dark, row upon row, jars glowing with soft, patient light. Now whole sections stood bare, the brackets empty, dust settling where dust had never been allowed to settle. And third shelf, second row — she found it by memory, by the ache in her chest that had learned the location the way you learn the location of your own heart —

The jar was still there. But it was *lowering*. Suspended on a thread of pale light, sinking inch by inch toward a brazier-shaped basin of black glass at the aisle's end, where something waited with the patience of arithmetic.

"No," Yara said. "No, no, no—"

"Time bleed," Odile said grimly, already calculating, already drafting arguments in the air with her fingers as though the contract hung visible before her. "Acceleration clauses compound. We have until that jar touches the glass, and then—"

A sound rolled through the market, vast and weary and terribly sad, and every remaining jar flickered in answer. The Regent, speaking from everywhere and nowhere, in the voice of a starving thing that had forgotten cruelty and kindness were different words:

*You return. Good. The debt grows impatient, and I have grown so tired of waiting. Come witness. Come sign. Or come be signed.*

Yara looked at the jar — at the small, stubborn glow inside it, the name and the mother and the winter, everything a child was before a king spent it — and then at her family scattered down the aisle: Wren with his net-mender's hands flexing, Bramble with her candle guttering, Odile standing straighter than Yara had ever seen her, two hundred years of guilt finally arriving at the address it had been sent to.

And she reached into her pocket and closed her hand around the bone button, smooth and warm, and made herself a promise with a taster's precision, measured to the grain:

Whatever the fine print demanded, whatever price the unraveling named — she had swallowed worse than fear her whole life. She had trained for exactly this, decade upon decade, poison after poison, building immunities she'd never understood the purpose of until now.

"Odile," she said. "How fast can you get us to the trial chamber?"

Odile smiled, and it was the smile of a woman about to commit magnificent fraud. "Faster than the mountain can close. Run."
