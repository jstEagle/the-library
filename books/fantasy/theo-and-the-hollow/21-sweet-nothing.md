## 21. Sweet Nothing

The morning of the king's winter feast, Yara Coldharbour stood in the tasting room with a silver spoon balanced on her tongue and discovered that sugar had died for her quietly, sometime between the mountain and the dawn.

She had known it would happen. She had felt the draught go down two nights ago — cold and clean and utterly without flavor, like swallowing a mouthful of December — and she had known what it would take. The Hollow Court's final draught did not burn going down. It simply *took*, the way a tide takes a footprint, and she had stood in the unraveling dark of that antechamber with unlit candles crowding her on every side and thought: *well. Of everything I own, this is the cheapest thing to lose.*

She had not expected to grieve it anyway.

The spoon held honeyed custard, the kind the palace kitchens made only for midwinter, scented with orange peel and nutmeg. Yara let it sit on her tongue. It was warm. It was smooth. It was, as far as her mouth could tell her, nothing at all — texture without meaning, sweetness without its soul. She might have been tasting sweetened fog.

"Hm," said Master Pellinore, the under-steward who supervised the royal tastings, watching her face from across the table with the anxious diligence of a man whose entire career rested on other people's deaths not occurring. "Is it — is there something wrong with it?"

"No," Yara said. Her voice came out steady. She had learned long ago how to make her voice come out steady; it was one of the many armors she wore, along with the immunities and the ledger-keeping and the careful, practiced solitude. "It's perfect."

And here was the strange thing, the thing she turned over in her mind all through that morning while she catalogued the feast courses one by one — the candied fruits that tasted like wax, the spiced wine that was only heat, the marzipan boar that might have been carved from soap for all she could tell — here was the strange thing: *she didn't miss it.*

She kept waiting for the grief to arrive properly. She had paid, after all. She had stood before the Hollow Regent in its hall of borrowed memories and drunk the last sweetness out of her own life so that a contract could die and a child could live whole. Surely such a price should ache. Surely somewhere in her there should be a hollow place shaped exactly like honey.

But when she searched for it — standing at the king's left hand as he laughed too loudly at his fool, watching the courtiers glitter and preen beneath banners she had served since she was fourteen years old — she found no hollow at all. She found only a strange, clean lightness, like a room after old furniture has finally been carried out.

*It was never my favorite thing,* she realized, watching the marzipan boar be carried past on its silver litter. *Sweetness. I hoarded it the way I hoarded everything else — carefully, secretly, against a day when I'd need proof that life had been worth the trouble.* She had kept her taste for sugar the way she kept the guild ledgers: hidden, private, hers alone. A small treasury of pleasure that belonged to no one because she had belonged to no one.

That was gone now. Both treasuries, in a sense — though the ledgers had come home with her, wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, smelling faintly of candle smoke and Bramble's nettle tea.

The Guild of Impossible Things had a new page waiting in them. Several new pages. But that was later. That was tonight.

* * *

The courtier Lord Ambrose Vey found her at the wine tasting, which was either coincidence or the least subtle surveillance in the history of the palace, and Yara had stopped believing in coincidences around the same time she'd started keeping accounts of traded souls.

"You look well," Vey said, in the tone men used when they meant *you look different, and different frightens me.*

"I slept," Yara said. This was even true. She had slept nine hours in her narrow bed above the guildhall, wedged between the wall and a stack of Bramble's drying herbs, listening to Pip the locksmith snore through the floorboards and Fen the forger argue in his sleep with someone named Marguerite, and she had slept better than she had slept in twenty years of palace apartments where nobody snored and nobody argued and nobody knew her name beyond *the taster.*

"The feast went smoothly," Vey pressed. "No incidents."

"None." She swirled the wine, tasted it — bitter, tannic, alive; her tongue still worked fine for anything that wasn't sugar — and set the cup down. "Though I'll tell you a secret, my lord, if you promise not to spend it."

He leaned in. He had been leaning in toward her for weeks now, ever since her absences began, ever since the night she broke into the sealed archive and he caught her coming out with Aldous's confession tucked against her ribs and then — miracle of miracles, mystery of mysteries — looked away and walked the other direction. His sister's soul had been sold to the Hollow Court sixty years ago by a merchant grandfather chasing a fortune. Yara had helped him find where it was shelved. Last night, when the mountain opened and the Regent let the hoarded souls fly free like starlings released from a dark room, one of them had come home to a townhouse in Copperlane where an old woman sat up suddenly in her chair and wept without knowing why.

"Secrets are currency," Vey said. "I'm told you know more about currency than anyone in this palace."

Yara almost smiled. "What I know about currency would fill a library your grandfather couldn't have dreamed of. Here's the secret: sweetness is overrated."

"That's not a secret. That's heresy. The confectioners' guild will excommunicate you."

"Let them try. I'm immune to most poisons and apparently all persuasion." She handed him back the tasting spoon. "The wine's clean, my lord. Go enjoy your evening."

He hesitated at the door — she saw him hesitate, saw the question gather itself behind his teeth like a man working up courage to swallow something — and then he didn't ask it. Instead he said, quietly: "My sister wrote to me yesterday. She doesn't remember being gone. She remembers a dream of shelves, and a door opening, and someone holding the lantern."

Yara's chest did something complicated. "Someone had to hold the lantern."

"Yes," Vey said. "I'm glad it was you." And he bowed — actually bowed, lower than protocol required, lower than any courtier had ever bowed to a food-taster — and left her alone with the wine and the afternoon light and the extraordinary, disorienting feeling of having done something right.

* * *

She walked home through the snow as dusk came down blue over Copperlane, and the city smelled like midwinter — woodsmoke and roasting chestnuts and the iron breath of the frozen river — and none of it was sweet and all of it was enough.

The guildhall stood at the end of Tallow Lane where it had always stood: a crooked building that was bigger inside than out, because Odile Vane had built it two hundred years ago out of stolen architecture and stubborn geometry, and it had never quite forgiven physics for noticing. Its windows glowed amber. Its chimney breathed. From half a street away, Yara could smell bread.

Not sweet bread. That was the point, or part of the point, or maybe just the world being kind in the way it occasionally managed when nobody was looking. Bramble had baked the dark rye with caraway seeds, the kind Yara loved best, the kind that tasted of earth and salt and patience — and her ruined tongue rose to meet the smell through the cold air like a flower turning toward a lamp, and it *worked,* it worked perfectly, and Yara stood in the snow in front of her own front door and laughed out loud at nothing, at everything, at the sheer ridiculous mercy of a universe that had taken her sugar and left her salt.

Inside, the hall was chaos in the specific key of family.

Fen was at the long table arguing with Odile about whether the founding contract clause constituted fraud or foresight ("*Foresight,*" Odile said, in the voice of a woman who had waited two centuries to be right and intended to enjoy every syllable) while Odile herself — exiled founder, architect of doors, keeper of the flaw in kings' fine print — sat with her boots on a chair like she'd lived there all her life instead of four days, because apparently frozen time does not diminish one's talent for making oneself comfortable. Pip was teaching the child to pick locks using a practice padlock and a great deal of theatrical despair. Bramble moved through it all with a tray of bread and a expression of profound satisfaction, because hedge-witches consider a full table to be the highest form of spellcraft.

And the child —

The child looked up when Yara came in, and smiled, and said, "You're back," in the common tongue, clear as a bell, and Yara's armor cracked clean down the middle and stayed cracked, permanently, by design.

They had named her three days ago. Naming, Bramble explained, was a hedge-craft of some seriousness: you didn't just pick sounds, you picked *doors,* and the name had to be one the child could walk through into the rest of her life. They had tried several. Wren, for the bird that sings all winter. Ember, for what survives the fire. The child had considered each gravely, testing them in her returned voice like a jeweler testing stones.

In the end she had chosen for herself, which surprised no one who had watched her open a doorway that didn't exist and lead four adults down a stair into the dark of the world.

"Wren Ember Coldharbour," she said now, when Yara asked — because the child had decided that Coldharbour was hers to share, and Yara had not argued, because there are arguments you lose gladly. "That's me. You're late. Bread's nearly gone."

"There had better be a heel saved," Yara said, shedding her coat, shaking off snow, hanging the coat on a hook that had appeared on the wall sometime that week because the house itself had apparently decided she lived here now and was done pretending otherwise.

"Bramble hid one for you under a napkin. She thinks I don't know. I know everything." Wren Ember Coldharbour, eight years old, formerly mute, formerly nameless, formerly the property of a dead king's bargain, delivered this intelligence with the serene authority of a general reviewing troops, and Yara thought: *two hundred years ago a monster sold this child's soul for a crown's favor, and now she knows everything and hides nothing, and the ledger balances after all.*

The bread, when she got it, was perfect. Salt and caraway and crust. Her tongue sang.

She sat down in the middle of the noise — Fen shouting, Odile winning, Pip's lock clicking open at last to Wren's triumphant *I TOLD YOU* — and ate her heel of bread, and understood, with the quiet finality of a debt discharged, that she had made a good trade.

Everyone said that, afterward. The few who knew. *What a price, Yara. What a terrible price.* And she supposed from the outside it looked like one: a lifetime of honeyed things, surrendered to a starving court beneath a sleeping mountain. But prices were her trade, and she knew a fair exchange when she signed one. She had given up sweetness the way you give up a coin you were only carrying because you hadn't yet found the right pocket for it. What she'd bought in return was a table that expected her. A name spoken in a child's clear voice. A hall that smelled of bread and sounded like argument and had a hook by the door with her coat on it.

She had spent thirty-one years proving, dish by tasted dish, ledger line by ledger line, that she needed no one. She had been very good at it. She had won completely.

It had been the loneliest victory imaginable, and she was so glad to have lost.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when Fen had fallen asleep mid-sentence and Odile had drifted up the stairs humming something in a language older than kingdoms (and wasn't *that* a thought, that Odile Vane might actually speak the Hollow Tongue, might have always spoken it, might explain that someday over wine) — Yara sat at the long table with the ledgers open before her and the last candle burning low, and she did the books.

Old habit. Oldest habit. But it felt different now, balancing the impossible columns: wishes granted, debts honored, doors opened both ways. Before, the ledger had been her secret companion, the only witness to her double life, the confidant of a woman who trusted paper more than people. Now the people were asleep upstairs and the paper was just paper, honest work, a record of a family's trade. She wrote in the new entries in her neat taster's hand:

*Item: one child's soul, sold falsely, reclaimed wholly. Status: home.*
*Item: one food-taster's taste for sweetness, surrendered freely. Status: no loss worth mentioning.*
*Item: sixty-one souls, hoarded, released. Status: scattered home on the wind like starlings.*
*Item: one contract, voided. One mountain, sleeping. One Regent, fed at last on honesty instead of hunger. Status: at rest.*

She paused, quill hovering, and added one more line — the smallest entry, the one that mattered:

*Item: one Yara Coldharbour, formerly belonging nowhere. Status: belonging here.*

She blotted it. She closed the book. And that was when she heard it.

A knock.

At the door. At *this* hour, at *this* door, in weather that would freeze the tears on a stranger's face — a knock, three times, soft but certain, the knock of someone who has walked a long way on the strength of a rumor that says there is a guild that trades in impossible things.

The candle guttered. Upstairs, a floorboard creaked — Odile, awake already, because of course she was awake; Odile had been waiting two hundred years for knocks like this. In the corner, the ledger, closed, seemed to lean toward the door the way a dog leans toward a scent.

Wren's voice came sleepily down the stairs, thick with dreams: "Somebody's there. Somebody cold."

Yara looked at the door for a long moment. Then she took the candle, and opened the ledger to a fresh page, and went to answer it — unhurried, unafraid, her hand steady on the latch, the way you open a door when you have finally, truly, come home.

Behind her, on the stairs, soft footsteps gathered: Odile first, then Bramble with her shawl, then the others, waking, gathering, the whole improbable family assembling itself in the dark like a constellation deciding to shine.

Yara lifted the bar and pulled the door open onto the snow, and the wind came in carrying the smell of woodsmoke and far places and one more impossible thing.
