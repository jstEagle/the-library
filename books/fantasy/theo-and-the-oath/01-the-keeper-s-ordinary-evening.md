## 1. The Keeper's Ordinary Evening

The valley did its forgetting gently, the way a mother smooths a child's hair.

Ada Okonkwo stood at the kitchen window of her grandmother's house and watched the last light drain out of the western hills, and she thought—not for the first time, but tonight with an ache that felt almost physical—*I could walk out of this valley and no one would ever know I'd been its keeper.* That was not quite true. The valley would know. The gate would know. But the world beyond, the wide sunlit ordinary world where people bought bread and argued about football and went on dates—that world would look at her and see only a tall woman in her late twenties with careful hands and a habit of standing too long in doorways.

She turned back to the small chaos she had made of the bathroom mirror. Lipstick. She owned lipstick. It had been a gift from a cousin years ago, still wearing its cellophane like a chastity belt, and now she peeled it off and applied the color with the grim concentration of someone disarming a trap. Her grandmother, Nneka, would have laughed at her. *You dress like you're going to a funeral, Ada. Let the man see your mouth move.*

"Grandmother," Ada said to the empty house, "is going to pretend she doesn't approve, and then she's going to interrogate him within an inch of his life."

But Nneka was not downstairs. Nneka was three miles east, sitting with her bad hip propped on cushions beside the Hollow Gate, keeping the evening watch as she had kept it for fifty years, as her own mother had kept it before her, back through a line of women whose names were written in a book Ada was not yet allowed to read. The book sat upstairs on a shelf in the locked room. Ada had dusted it every week of her life without once being permitted to open it.

Tonight, though, tonight was different. Tonight Ada had lied—gently, lovingly, with a plate of jollof rice and a story about needing rest—and convinced her grandmother to let her take one evening. One. The first in twenty-two years of watching.

*One evening,* she told herself, smoothing down the front of her green dress. *The gate has held for four hundred years. It will hold for four hours.*

The trouble with the Hollow Gate was that it looked like nothing. That was the genius of it, if you could call four centuries of vigilance genius: a seam in a hillside of grey limestone, so narrow you could slide your hand into it and feel nothing but cold stone and colder air, so unremarkable that hikers walked past it every summer without a glance. There was no arch, no door, no hinge. Just a crack in the world, thin as a knife cut in cloth, running from the hill's crown down into its roots. On the valley side, sheep grazed. On the other side—on the *other* side—the rules of the valley simply stopped applying.

Magic could not cross into the valley. That was the covenant, the whole reason for the Okonkwo line, the entire architecture of Ada's life: whatever lived beyond the seam stayed beyond the seam, and the keepers watched to make sure nothing came looking for the way through. Nothing had come looking in living memory. Nothing had come looking since her great-great-grandmother's time, when something *had* come looking—a story Nneka told only after two glasses of palm wine, about a winter when the crack had widened by a finger's width and the sheep on the near pasture had gone silent, all of them, all night, facing the hill like a congregation facing an altar.

Ada had grown up inside that story the way other children grew up inside their family names. She knew which stones along the seam were warm on cold nights (none should be). She knew that the moss growing in the crack was a species found nowhere else in the county and nowhere else in any book, and that it leaned, all of it, toward the hill's interior, like grass bending under wind that wasn't there. She knew how to read the gate's moods in the behavior of birds: jackdaws were fine; a barn owl hunting at noon meant check the wards; silence from the whole hillside meant run for grandmother and don't ask questions on the way.

And she knew, with the bone-deep certainty of someone raised on duty the way other children are raised on milk, that she would never leave. Not really. Not the way other people left. Other people moved away for university and came home at Christmas. Ada had gone to university forty minutes' drive from the gate and had driven back twice a week to walk the seam with a lantern, checking stones. Her friends had scattered to London, to Lagos, to lives with weather in them. Ada had stayed because the book upstairs said the keeper stays, and because Nneka's hips were failing, and because love, in her family, wore the shape of obligation and fit perfectly.

Until Theo.

She said his name to herself in the mirror and immediately felt ridiculous, twenty-eight years old and mouthing a man's name at her reflection like a girl. But there it was. *Theo.* Theodore something—he'd said the surname twice and she'd lost it both times because she'd been too busy noticing the way he pushed his glasses up with one knuckle, an archivist's gesture, precise and absent, as if even his face were cataloguing itself. He worked in the county records office. He had emailed her about a donation of nineteenth-century farming ledgers—her family's, deeded to the archive by Nneka in one of her occasional fits of modernity—and he had written such a strange, careful, funny note about marginalia (*someone named E. Okonkwo appears to have been keeping score of debts in 1887 and also, I suspect, of insults*) that Ada had read it four times and then answered it against every rule she'd set herself about strangers and attention and the cost of being known.

Three months of emails. Two phone calls. One date, tonight, at the Rose & Crown in the market town outside the valley, where the fish was mediocre and the lighting was kind and no one, absolutely no one, would know what she was.

That was the part that made her hands shake as she fastened the small silver clasp at her throat—the sigil, the keeper's mark, a stylized key worn smooth by six generations of thumbs. She never wore it out. It was the one law she'd never tested: *the sigil does not leave the valley.* Because the sigil was not jewelry. It was a key that happened to be shaped like a thing you could wear, and keys, her grandmother said, have a way of finding locks.

She looked at it in the mirror, lying cool against her collarbone.

Then she took it off, wrapped it in flannel, and put it in the drawer of her grandmother's sewing table, beneath the pincushion, exactly where Nneka kept it during her own rare absences. The clasp left a faint pale groove on her skin that she covered with her hair.

"There," she whispered to the mirror. "Just a woman. For four hours, just a woman."

Her reflection looked skeptical.

* * *

The bus out of the valley ran twice a day, once at dawn and once at dusk, and Ada caught the dusk one, which meant she stood at the stop while the light died behind the eastern hills—the hills that hid the Hollow Gate—and felt the guilt arrive precisely on schedule, the way the bus arrived precisely on schedule, both of them punctual servants of her life.

It wasn't a dramatic guilt. That was the worst of it. It didn't come as thunder or warning; it came as arithmetic. *Nneka is alone at the watch. Nneka is seventy-nine. Nneka's hands shake when the weather turns.* And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, older, a voice with her grandmother's cadence: *Four hundred years, Ada. Four hundred years of women who did not go on dates.*

She made herself breathe. She made herself look at the valley instead—at the low gold windows coming alight in the village, at the smell of woodsmoke and cut hay, at the rooks going home over the church tower—and she let the valley be beautiful, which was easier than letting it be a cage. This was the trick she had learned and hated having learned: you could bear anything if you kept renaming it.

On the bus she sat across from Mrs. Prewitt, who kept bees and gossip in equal quantities, and Mrs. Prewitt looked at Ada's dress and her uncovered neck and said, with the delicacy of a bomb-disposal expert, "Off somewhere nice?"

"The Rose and Crown. Meeting a friend."

"A *man* friend." Mrs. Prewitt's eyebrows performed a small interpretive dance. "Well. About time. Your gran must be pleased."

"She doesn't know."

Mrs. Prewitt's expression shifted through several registers and settled on something unexpectedly kind. "First one in a while?"

Ada looked out the window. The valley was sliding away behind them, folding itself into the dark, and she felt the peculiar sensation she always felt leaving it—a faint pressure behind the sternum, like the moment after a door closes, like the air remembering you were there. "A while," she agreed.

"Well." Mrs. Prewitt patted her knee with a hand that smelled of smoke and honey. "The valley keeps. It'll keep one more evening."

*The valley keeps.* Ada smiled and thanked her and looked out at the road unspooling toward the market town and its ordinary lights, and she did not say what she was thinking, which was: *That's exactly what frightens me. That is exactly what I am afraid of—that I will keep until there is nothing left of me but keeping.*

* * *

The Rose and Crown had sawdust on the floor and a fire that was mostly ceremony, and Theo was already there, in the corner booth, with two menus and the anxious posture of a man who had arrived twenty minutes early and rearranged the salt twice. When he saw her his whole face did something uncomplicated and delighted, and Ada felt the last knot of guilt loosen—not break, but loosen, enough to breathe through.

"You found it," he said, standing too quickly and half-knocking over the vinegar. "Sorry—I've been—yes. Hello. You look—" He stopped, visibly refusing to finish the sentence with anything generic, and finally landed on: "You look like someone who reads footnotes."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me in years."

"It's true, though. I can tell. You have footnote energy." He pushed the menu at her. "Also I ordered you a lime soda because you said you don't drink much, and if that's presumptuous you're allowed to hate me."

"I could never hate a man who defends my marginalia." She slid into the booth. The seat was cracked vinyl, warm from the fire, and the whole moment was so aggressively ordinary that she wanted to laugh or cry or possibly both. A pub. A menu. A man pushing his glasses up his nose with one knuckle. No wards, no watches, no seam in a hillside humming under the moon. Just this.

They talked for three hours. Or rather, they talked for what the clock claimed was three hours and what Ada experienced as roughly eleven minutes. He told her about the archives—the smell of them, the vellum that survived centuries while paper ate itself, the letter he'd once found in a box of tax records from a farmer in 1743 apologizing to his wife for a fence dispute and asking forgiveness in terms so tender that Theo had had to sit down. She told him things she shouldn't have found easy to tell: about her grandmother, about the hills, about how she'd studied history and geography and never once used either degree. She did not tell him about the gate. She told him everything *around* the gate, a portrait of her life with one figure carefully painted out, and he listened the way archivists listen—completely, as if she were a document worth reading properly.

At some point he said, "Can I confess something odd?" and Ada, who had spent her life around oddness, said, "Please."

"When I was reading through your family's ledgers, there was a book mixed in that isn't a ledger. Old binding, hand-sewn. Someone catalogued it wrong—it's filed under agricultural accounts, which is madness. I haven't opened it properly, just the first page. There's a drawing in it." He turned his glass a quarter-turn, frowning. "An old woman wrote on the flyleaf that it was a family record. Prophecies, maybe? Folk stuff. Anyway—the drawing. It's a sketch of a young man's face, pencil, very fine work. And Ada, it sounds mad, but it looked—" He laughed at himself, embarrassed. "It looked a bit like me. Isn't that strange? Probably just one of those faces. Generic faces. Everyone looks like someone in an old book."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind the bar a glass was set down with a small clean chime, and in the space of that chime Ada felt the evening tilt, very slightly, like a floor testing its own level.

"A century old, you said?"

"At least. The paper's older than the binding." He shook his head, smiling. "Forget it. Occupational disease—we archivists see our own faces in everything. Digits of old census takers, portraits on deeds. You spend enough years among dead people's handwriting, you start hearing your name in it."

"Hearing your name," Ada repeated.

"In the ink. It's a thing that happens." He said it lightly, and then, because he was observant under all that mildness, he added, "Sorry. Grim dinner conversation. Tell me something cheerful about the valley. Tell me about the sheep."

So she told him about the sheep, and the evening righted itself, or seemed to. They closed the pub down. He walked her to the bus stop under a sky rinsed with stars, and they stood in the cold arguing amiably about whether the archive should digitize everything or preserve the sacred friction of paper, and when the bus's headlights swung up the road he said, suddenly and sincerely, "This was the best evening I've had in years. Can I see you again? Properly. I'll bring the strange book. You can tell me if the face is mine or if I'm losing my mind."

"Yes," Ada said, before the word could pass through any committee of caution. "Yes. Bring it."

He grinned, stepped back, nearly tripped over the kerb, recovered with dignity, and walked away glancing back twice, and Ada stood in the starlight feeling like a woman in a story where nothing terrible happens, and let herself feel it fully, greedily, for the length of one breath.

Then the bus came, and she got on, and the guilt climbed aboard behind her and took the window seat.

* * *

She noticed it before the bus even reached the valley turn: a light on the eastern hills, where no light should be.

Not firelight. Not a torch. A glow, low and steady, seeping out of the hillside itself, from the place her body knew without looking—the seam, the crack, the Hollow Gate—and it pulsed once, softly, the way a throat pulses under skin, and then again.

Mrs. Prewitt had fallen asleep against the window. The driver hummed to the radio. Nobody else in the world was looking at that hillside, and Ada was out of her seat before the bus finished rounding the bend, shouting to the driver that this was her stop, this was actually her stop, please, and stepping down into a darkness that smelled of frost and cut hay and, faintly, underneath everything, of ozone.

The valley where magic cannot follow, and yet something on the far side of the limestone was shining through the seam like lamplight under a door.

Ada ran. Past the sleeping houses, past the church, up the lane with her heels in her fist and her breath tearing white, past the last field wall where the ground began to climb toward the hill—and all the while the glow strengthened, and the rooks, absurdly, impossibly, rose from the trees at midnight in one silent black sheet, every bird in the valley awake and wheeling and facing the hill.

She crested the ridge above the gate and stopped, lungs burning, and looked down into the hollow where the crack ran through the limestone.

The moss in the seam was glowing. All of it, every strand of that species found in no book anywhere, leaning inward as it always leaned—but now lit from within, a pale gold-green light, breathing in slow rhythm, brighter, dimmer, brighter. And the crack itself had widened. Not a finger's width, as in her great-great-grandmother's winter. A hand's width. Enough to slide an arm through into air that moved on the other side, air with a wind in it, though the night on the valley side lay utterly still.

And standing at the edge of the hollow, wrapped in a shawl, leaning hard on her stick, her face lifted and lit gold-green from below—stood Nneka, who should have been home hours ago, who had taken the evening watch after all, who had known somehow, who had come alone.

"Grandmother!" Ada scrambled down the slope, stones rolling under her feet. "What's happening—what's *wrong*—"

Nneka didn't turn. Her voice, when it came, was calm in the way of deep water, and it was not directed at Ada at all. It was directed at the seam.

"He's coming through," her grandmother said. "After four hundred years, child. *He's coming through.*"

And the light in the crack flared, and the wind that wasn't there sighed out of the hill, carrying a scent Ada had never smelled and somehow recognized—old paper, and ink, and something like a name.
