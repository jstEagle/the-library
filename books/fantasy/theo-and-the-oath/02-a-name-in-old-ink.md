## 2. A Name in Old Ink

Theo arrived eleven minutes early, which was nine minutes more than he'd intended, and stood across the lane from the gatekeeper's cottage working up the courage to be a person who arrived on time rather than early.

The valley in the last light of evening did something to him he couldn't name. He had taken the long bus route out of the city on purpose—three hours of hedgerows and stone walls and hills that folded over one another like sleeping animals—and somewhere along the way his shoulders had come down from around his ears for the first time in months. The air smelled of cut hay and woodsmoke and, beneath it, something mineral and cold, as though the ground itself were holding its breath.

The cottage sat where the valley narrowed, tucked against a rise of grey rock that might once have been a wall and might simply have been rock. It was smaller than he'd imagined, whitewashed, with a slate roof furred green at the edges and a garden gone pleasantly wild with rosemary and something blue-flowered he didn't recognize. And it was the door that stopped him: old, iron-banded, painted a deep oxblood red, with a lintel carved in patterns that looked almost like letters and almost like roots.

He had catalogued four thousand books in eight years of archival work. He knew what old carving looked like. That lintel was old the way riverbeds were old.

*You're here for a date,* he reminded himself. *A first date. With a woman you met at a book fair because she argued with a dealer about the provenance of a forged map and won.* He had fallen a little in love with her voice before he ever saw her face. Then he'd seen her face, dark-eyed and unamused and lit from within by the particular joy of being right, and he'd asked her to dinner before his better judgment could intervene.

His better judgment had spent the intervening two weeks filing objections. *She lives three hours away. She guards some kind of family landmark she wouldn't explain. She looked at you like she was deciding something.* But judgment had never been the organ Theo trusted most. Curiosity was. Curiosity had gotten him into archives and out of them at odd hours; curiosity had made him an archivist instead of an accountant, a man whose hands smelled faintly of old paper and whose idea of luxury was an uncatalogued box.

He crossed the lane and knocked.

Ada opened the door already dressed for town—a deep green dress, boots made for walking, a coat over her arm—and for a moment she just looked at him, and he had the strange, vertiginous sense that she was checking him against something. A list. An expectation.

"You're early," she said.

"I'm always early. It's a character flaw. I catalogue things; lateness feels like misfiling."

Something in her face eased. "You'd better come in, then. I'm not ready, and if you're going to stand around being punctual at me, you can do it where there's tea."

* * *

Inside, the cottage smelled of beeswax and dried herbs and, again, that cold mineral undertone, stronger here, as though the house itself were built on the lip of a well. The main room was low-ceilinged and warm, dominated by a hearth, a table scarred by generations of knives, and—Theo's eyes went to it the way water goes downhill—a shelf.

Not a shelf. A wall of shelving, floor to ceiling, crammed into the room's deepest corner away from the window, away from the light. The books on it were not arranged by any system he recognized. No alphabet, no chronology, no size order. They were arranged, he realized after a moment of professional disorientation, the way one arranges things one is *guarding*: spines inward for the oldest volumes, chains on two of them, a locked case of dark glass over a third, and every single book wrapped or boxed or sleeved as though each one were a wound being kept clean.

"That's the family shelf," Ada said from the kitchen doorway, and there was a new note in her voice. Not embarrassment exactly. Wariness. "You can look, but don't touch anything without asking. Some of them are... sensitive."

"Sensitive how?"

"Sensitive." She said it the way people say *classified*, and then smiled, and the smile was so genuine and so deliberately placed that Theo understood it was meant to end the conversation. "Tea's on. Don't judge my mugs."

He judged the mug silently—it was chipped and printed with a cartoon sheep—and said nothing, because he was no longer entirely listening. His attention had snagged on the shelf the way a sleeve snags on a nail, and the more he tried to be a polite guest making pleasant conversation about the drive out, the more firmly it held him.

There was a system after all, he saw now. The newest-looking books were nearest the door-side of the shelf, and the further left one looked, the older the bindings grew—calfskin, vellum, boards worn round at the corners, one spine that seemed to be tanned leather with tooling he refused to let himself think too hard about. And at the far end, half-shadowed, wrapped in linen cloth tied with a knot he didn't know, was a book that was neither old nor new but somehow both: its binding fresh, its proportions archaic. As though someone had recently rebound something ancient and hadn't quite decided how to feel about it.

"Is that one yours?" he asked.

Ada followed his gaze, and he watched her stillness arrive all at once, like a bird landing. "That one's been rebound twice," she said. "My grandmother did the last one. Why?"

"Professional interest. May I?"

She hesitated. He watched her weigh something—he would think about this later, much later, how plainly the weighing showed on her face—and then she nodded once, sharply, as if conceding a point in an argument he hadn't known they were having. "Cloth first. There's oil on your hands."

"There is always oil on my hands. It's a condition of employment."

He untied the linen knot with fingers that were steadier than he expected them to be. The cloth fell open. The book beneath was bound in grey leather, plain except for a stamped device on the cover: a circle bisected by a vertical line, and within the line, a shape like a keyhole or an eye.

The cold mineral smell of the room gathered suddenly close, like a hand cupped behind his neck.

"The Hollow Gate," he read aloud. The title was stamped along the spine in lettering that predated modern typefaces by centuries. "Your family keeps records."

"We keep *records*," Ada said, from very far away, or so it sounded.

* * *

He opened it expecting a ledger. What he found was a chronicle—or the bones of one, because the book was written in many hands across what felt like many lifetimes, entries layered over entries, margins colonized by later annotations, whole pages struck through by furious lines and then, sometimes, patiently restored by yet another hand. It was, he thought with a lurch of professional longing, the single most important-looking document he had ever been allowed to touch. It was also, unmistakably, private. Family-private. Sacred-private.

"Ada," he said carefully, "I don't think I should be reading this."

"You haven't read anything yet. You've looked at a page." She had stopped pretending to make tea. She stood by the hearth with her arms folded, watching him the way one watches weather. "Turn the pages if you want. You'll understand why I don't lend it out."

He turned the pages.

Mostly they were lists—the word recurred, list after list, names and dates in hands that slanted rightward, leftward, pressed hard, faded soft. Names of keepers, he guessed. Names of gates? Some entries were only a single line: *Nothing stirred.* Others ran to pages of cramped, urgent script he couldn't parse. Twice he found drawings: the gate itself rendered over and over—an arch of grey stone set into a hillside, its opening black, its lintel carved with those root-letters—and beside it, in one drawing, a small figure standing watch, hood up, staff in hand. In every drawing, the figure's face was left blank.

"Why is the face empty?" he asked.

"Because it isn't one face," Ada said quietly. "It's whoever's keeping it."

He turned another page and stopped.

The page was a full drawing, larger than the others, done in ink gone brown with age, and it was not the gate. It was a man. Standing, three-quarter view, one hand extended palm-outward as if warding something off—or greeting it; the pose balanced between the two in a way that made Theo's chest hurt without his knowing why. The artist had given the figure a long coat, a scarf whipping in a wind suggested by a few fierce strokes, hair pushed back from the forehead. And the face—

The face was finished. Fully, lovingly finished, in a book where every other human face had been left blank.

Theo knew the face. It took him a long, silent, ringing moment to admit it even to himself, because admitting it felt like stepping off a ledge. He knew it from the mirror. From the photograph on his mother's sideboard that his aunt called *your grandfather's chin*. From the way his own reflection caught him unaware in train windows, a stranger wearing his expression. It was his jaw, heavier in the drawing, drawn younger. His brows, his mouth, the slight asymmetry of his eyes that he'd never mentioned to anyone because who mentions that. The artist had even caught the small scar through his left eyebrow—which he'd had since a fall at six years old—with a single deft interruption of hatching.

"Ada," he said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. Underneath the levelness, something was moving fast, the way water moves under ice. "Who is this?"

She didn't answer immediately. When she did, her voice was careful, the voice of someone stepping onto a bridge they aren't sure will hold. "Read what's written under it."

Beneath the figure, in a formal, ceremonial hand unlike the rest of the book, ran a passage. Theo read it once at speed, then again slowly, forcing each word through the sudden thickness of the air:

*Here is written the likeness of the Oathbearer, who shall come when the covenant frays. He has lived before and will know nothing of it. He will carry no mark, bear no sign, and give no warning. The keeper of the gate shall know him by his face alone, which does not change, though everything else does.*

*When he comes, do not tell him what he was. Tell him what he must do.*

Below that, a date. Theo stared at it. He was good with dates—it was half his profession—and this one resolved with horrible clarity into a year a hundred years past. A century before his birth. Ink brown with age, paper foxed at the edges, the whole leaf smelling of dust and attics and time.

And beneath the date, smaller, in a different hand—newer, sharper, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper—two words:

*He's here.*

"What does 'Oathbearer' mean?" he asked. His mouth was dry. Somewhere outside, a bird called, absurd and ordinary, and the ordinary sound made the room stranger, the way a familiar word repeated too often becomes noise. "What covenant? Ada, this is dated a hundred years ago. This is—" He laughed, one short syllable with no humor in it, reaching for the rational handles a sensible man reaches for. "This is a family tradition, isn't it? Some kind of inherited folklore. Your grandmother wrote that last line recently, or someone did, and the sketch is of an ancestor who happens to look like me, because faces recur in families, it's documented, it's—"

"Look at me," Ada said.

He looked at her. Her arms were no longer folded. Her hands hung at her sides, and they were trembling—not with fear, he thought, or not only fear, but with the effort of holding very still around something enormous.

"When you walked up the lane tonight," she said, "I watched you from the window for a full minute before I opened the door. Do you know why I waited?"

"Because you weren't ready. You said—"

"Because I wanted to be wrong." Her voice stayed steady, but barely, a wire under tension. "I have looked at that drawing my whole life. My mother looked at it. Her mother looked at it. We are taught the face before we're taught our letters, Theo. I could draw it blindfolded. I told myself for years it was a story we told ourselves, a duty dressed up as a destiny, and when you messaged me about the book fair I thought—I actually thought—*what are the odds.* The odds are nothing. The odds are a coincidence. People look alike. I came anyway. I put on a dress and I came anyway, because I wanted one evening of being a woman having dinner with a man instead of a keeper meeting a prophecy."

The fire ticked in the hearth. The tea steamed forgotten on the table between them, and the steam rose straight up in the still air, a thin grey thread.

"So which is it?" Theo whispered. "Am I a coincidence or am I—"

"I don't know." For the first time she sounded young, and scared, and honest all at once, and it undid something in him that the uncanny drawing had not. "I have never once known. That's the truth of keeping a gate: you spend your life preparing for something and praying it stays a story."

"A gate." He heard himself say it as though from a distance. "You said you guard something. On the hill. Behind the cottage. That carving on your door—that's the same as the drawings, isn't it. There's an actual gate."

"It's called the Hollow Gate," Ada said. "And it doesn't open. It hasn't opened in a hundred years. Not since the night my great-great-grandmother wrote *he's here* and then—" She stopped. Her eyes went, involuntarily, to the shelf, to the linen-wrapped shape of the book in his hands, and then past him, through the wall, toward the darkening hill.

Then she stopped breathing.

Theo turned.

He would try, afterward, to describe what he saw in the doorway, and fail, because language kept insisting on the wrong nouns. The evening stood in the doorway—the actual dusk, violet and cool—but the dusk had *depth* now, and in that depth something had changed. The mineral smell he'd been noticing all evening, the held-breath coldness of the ground, had risen and filled the room like water filling a hull. And beneath the floorboards, beneath the foundations, beneath the bedrock of the valley itself, he felt—not heard, *felt*—a sound like a vast lock turning over. Once. Slowly. The way a sleeping animal turns toward warmth.

The lamp flames leaned sideways, all of them, toward the hill.

On the table, the prophecy book lay open between their cooling cups of tea, and as Theo watched, unable to move, unable to look away, the blank margin beneath the words *he's here* began—there was no other word for it—to darken. Not ink rising. Not a stain spreading. Something *writing*, from underneath the world, in letters that surfaced through the paper the way fish rise through dark water, and Theo's own name assembled itself there stroke by stroke, in a hand he recognized, because it was his own.

Behind him, Ada said, very quietly, the way people speak in churches and emergencies:

"Theo. Step away from the shelf."

He did not step away from the shelf. He stepped forward—toward the book, toward his own name surfacing through the page—and the floor of the cottage exhaled, and the gate on the hill, a hundred years closed, remembered how to open.
