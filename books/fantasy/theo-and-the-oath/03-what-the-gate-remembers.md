## 3. What the Gate Remembers

*Ada*

The kiss had barely ended when the light in the hallway changed.

Ada felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her sternum, like a hand pressing flat against her ribs from the inside. She had lived her whole life within earshot of the Hollow Gate, and she had learned its moods the way a sailor learns weather: not with instruments but with the body. The gate was never supposed to have moods. It was supposed to be stone, and silence, and the long patient nothing of a door that no one opened.

It was singing.

Not aloud. Nothing so kind. It was singing in the way that frost sings across a window, in the way that a wire hums before it snaps—something felt in the teeth, in the fillings, in the wet architecture of the inner ear. Ada pulled back from Theo so sharply that he made a small wounded sound, and she was already moving, already grabbing her coat off the hook by the door.

"Wait—" Theo said. "Did I—was it something I said?"

"Stay here." Her voice came out wrong, clipped, the voice she used on trespassers. "Lock the door behind me. Don't follow."

But even as she said it, she knew. She knew the way you know a thing in a dream, without evidence, without logic: *it wants him.* The gate had stirred for the first time in a hundred years, and it had stirred because an archivist with ink-stained fingers and a book of impossible prophecies was standing in her sitting room smelling of chamomile tea.

She took the stairs to the cellar two at a time, and the whole way down the hum grew, until it wasn't a hum at all but a chord, low and vast, the sound a cathedral might make if a cathedral could grieve.

The Hollow Gate stood where it had always stood, in the oldest room of the oldest house, behind a door her grandmother had triple-locked every night of her life. It was not a grand thing. That was the first lesson every keeper learned: the seams between worlds are never grand. It was a hollow in the fieldstone wall, roughly the shape of a doorway, filled with a darkness that did not reflect lamplight. The darkness breathed. Tonight, it billowed.

Tonight, it reached.

Ada watched, frozen on the last step, as a tendril of that unlight stretched out of the hollow like smoke poured backward into a bottle, sliding along the floor, up the stairs, through the house she had defended with her whole life—and found him.

She heard Theo's footsteps above her. Heard him disobey her, exactly as she'd known he would. Heard his startled cry as the dark took him under, gentle as a wave taking a swimmer, and then the silence after, which was worse than any sound.

Every rule she had ever been given assembled itself in her mind like a jury. *Never cross. Never pursue. What goes through stays through. The valley is yours to guard, not yours to leave.* Her grandmother's voice, her mother's voice, the voices of a hundred dead keepers pressed into the margins of the family books: *the gate opens one way for the world and no ways for us.*

Theo's coat was still draped over the chair in the sitting room. His tea was still warm.

"Oh, hell," Ada said, to the empty house, to the jury, to the whole gray valley that had raised her. "I'm sorry, Gran."

She walked down the remaining stairs, put her hand into the breathing dark, and stepped through her own law as though it were a bead curtain.

* * *

*Theo*

Falling, Theo thought, should have a direction.

This did not. He fell upward and inward at once, through a blackness that was less like night and more like the inside of a closed book—the pressed-flower smell of old paper, the hush of pages that had not been turned in centuries. He tried to scream and the dark drank the sound. He tried to remember the archivist's trick for panic, *name five things you can perceive,* and could only name one: the cold. A clean, ancient cold, like holding a key that has wintered outdoors.

Then light, all at once, and ground, hard against his knees and palms, and air that hit his lungs like a first breath after nearly drowning—sweet, impossibly sweet, sweet the way water is sweet when you've been sick.

He was kneeling in a road. A village spread around him under a sky the color of a bruise healing into dawn, except that the sun sat wrong in it, too large and too low, and its light came down in visible shafts, solid almost, like the pillars of some ruined temple. The houses were crooked in a way houses aren't crooked in the valley—not leaning from age but *grown*, walls of woven living willow, roofs of moss that glowed faintly gold where the light touched it. A well at the village center drew up water that shone like liquid mercury. Somewhere a goat was bleating, and the bleat echoed twice, three times, each echo slightly out of sync, so that it sounded like a choir of goats.

"Okay," Theo whispered, because naming things helped, this was a thing he could name: *I am somewhere else.* "Okay. Okay."

Magic. He understood that much, in the dumb way one understands a language one doesn't speak. This place was soaked in it the way his world was soaked in electricity—invisible until it sparked, and sparking everywhere now, small wonders leaking from every seam. A woman hanging laundry paused to watch a shirt mend its own torn sleeve. Two children chased something bright and wingless that flitted between them like a dragonfly made of stained glass. An old man sat on a doorstep whittling, and the wood curls fell as tiny birds, fluttered once, and stilled.

None of them looked surprised to see him fall out of nowhere. That frightened him more than anything else.

"Theo!"

He turned, and relief hit him so hard it buckled something in his chest. Ada came stumbling up the road out of a shimmer in the air—a shimmer he realized, watching it seal itself shut like water closing over a stone, must be the other side of the hollow in her cellar. Her hair was loose, her coat buttoned wrong, and she looked, for the first time since he'd met her, entirely unguarded: terrified, furious, alive.

"You followed me," he said stupidly.

"You *followed* you," she snapped, reaching him, gripping his arms, checking him over with quick clinical hands as if he might have come apart in transit. "Are you hurt? Anywhere? Does anything feel—wrong? Wronger?"

"I don't know what right feels like yet." He looked past her at the impossible village, the doubled goat-choir, the sun in the wrong part of the sky. "Ada. Where is this?"

"This is Thornhollow. Border country. First settlement past the gate." Her hands hadn't stopped moving—his pulse, his pupils, the backs of his hands where the cold had touched longest. "And I am in *so much trouble*, and so are you, and I need you to walk slowly and touch nothing while I figure out how much."

"Touch nothing," Theo repeated. "Because—"

"Because magic doesn't live here, Theo. It can't cross. That's the whole covenant, the whole point of the gate, the whole reason my family has stood in a cellar for four generations like a row of very tired scarecrows. Magic is supposed to stay on this side. And you—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone careful, the way people speak near a sleeping animal. "The gate doesn't open from this side. It hasn't moved in a hundred years. It doesn't reach. And it has certainly never, ever reached *through* into the valley and taken someone."

"So why me?"

"That," Ada said, "is the question I intend to survive long enough to answer."

* * *

*Ada*

The innkeeper of the Waymeet House was a broad woman named Sera with silver threading one braid and eyes the amber of strong tea, and she took one look at Ada and set down three bowls of stew without being asked.

"Eat," she said. "You crossed wild. Both of you. Wild-crossing hollows out the bones; the stew puts them back."

"We haven't any coin from here," Ada began, and stopped, because Sera was staring at Theo with an expression Ada couldn't parse—pity? recognition?—and Theo, oblivious, was trying to eat stew while also staring at everything: the hearthfire that burned blue at its heart, the cat on the windowsill whose shadow moved independently of it, washing itself with a paw that cast no reflection in the polished table.

"You're the keeper's girl," Sera said to Ada at last. "From the valley side. Marta's granddaughter."

"You knew my grandmother?"

"Everyone on the border knew your grandmother. She came through more than anyone liked to admit, back when the gate still let her." Sera's gaze slid to Theo again and stayed there, and Ada saw her swallow. "And who's this, then? Because there's talk already come up the road ahead of you, girl. There's always talk on the border faster than feet. Talk says the gate stood up tonight for the first time in living memory, and it stood up *for him*."

"I'm nobody," Theo said quickly. "I'm an archivist. I catalogue things. Marginalia, mostly."

Sera laughed, short and without humor. "Marginalia. That's what they called the last one too, before—" She caught herself, pressed her lips flat, and turned to pull a fresh loaf from the oven that needed no fire. "Eat your stew."

"*Before what?*" Ada asked.

But Sera only shook her head, and outside, the doubled daylight began to dim, and the villagers in the lane below began, quietly and all together, to bar their doors.

It was the child who made Ada understand how far from home she truly was. The little girl from the lane—the one who'd chased the glass dragonfly—appeared at their table as dusk fell, barefoot, fearless, carrying a wooden bowl of berries she clearly intended as tribute or trade. She planted herself in front of Theo and stared up at him with the frank appraisal of the very young.

"You're loud," she announced.

"I'm sorry?"

"Not with your mouth." The girl tapped her own chest, then pointed at Theo's. "In here. You're loud in here. Like a bell that got rung a long time ago and never finished." She held up the berries. "These help with that. Mam says."

Theo took the bowl as though it might detonate. "Thank you," he managed. "What's your name?"

"Wren." She leaned closer, dropped her voice to a conspirator's whisper, and said words that dropped through the floor of the world: "The river told me you were coming. It talks when the gate's awake. It said *the quiet man is coming back to finish what he started,* and Mam said rivers lie, but rivers don't, they only repeat."

She scampered off before either of them could speak, and for a long moment the only sounds were the blue-hearted fire and Theo's breathing, quick and shallow beside her.

"Ada," he said finally, very quietly. "When we were in your sitting room. Before the gate took me. You asked about the sketch in the book. My face, a century old." He turned the bowl of berries in his hands, not eating. "I need you to tell me honestly. Do you think I'm—do you think I've *been* here before?"

Ada wanted to lie. She heard the lie assembling itself—*of course not, coincidences happen, prophecies are just old poetry*—and she heard, underneath it, the memory of the gate's song, and the way the tendril of dark had moved through her house not like a predator but like a dog greeting its master at the end of a long absence.

She was a keeper. Keepers didn't lie about gates.

"I think," she said, "that whatever you were, whoever you were—you're not that person anymore. People change. Whatever the prophecy says, whatever the river repeats, you get to choose who you are now. But Theo—" she covered his hand with hers, and his fingers were cold as the inside of a closed book, "—we're going to have to find out what you started. Because I don't think it's finished. And I don't think it waited politely for a hundred years just to stay polite now."

Outside, far off toward the mountains, a horn sounded—once, twice—and every door in Thornhollow slammed shut at the same instant, as if the whole village had drawn a single breath and held it.

Sera appeared at their table with a shutter in her hands and fear written plain across her face.

"Eat fast," she said. "Riders coming down the pass. Veyra's colors."
