## 5. Road of Small Mercies

Theo had never been a man who slept well in strange beds, and the border village of Hollowmere offered him not even that much—only a hayloft above a smithy, where the smell of hot iron lingered like a memory of safety and the wind through the thatch sounded, at intervals, exactly like his own name.

He lay awake most of the night cataloguing what he knew, because cataloguing was what he did. It was how he'd made a life: names of dead archivists, shelf numbers of burned libraries, the precise weight of paper against the palm. Facts were handholds on a cliff face, and he was clinging.

Fact: three days ago he had been Theo Marsh, junior archivist at the Vale Street Records Annex, a man whose greatest adventure was a first date with a woman who smelled faintly of woodsmoke and iron and looked at him as though he were a puzzle she hadn't decided to solve.

Fact: there was now a book in his mind's eye—a book he'd held for perhaps ninety seconds before the gate took them—that sketched his own face in ink gone brown with age, beneath the words *the Oathbearer*, dated one hundred years before his birth.

Fact: magic did not work here the way it worked anywhere he had read about, because he had never read about it working at all, and yet yesterday morning he had watched Ada press her palm to a splintered doorpost and murmur something in a language that predated every language he could name, and the wood had knit itself whole like flesh closing over a wound.

And fact: somewhere north of them, an army marched behind a woman named Veyra, and a dying soldier had looked at Theo with eyes full of a century's worth of hate and called him *Betrayer*.

He turned the last fact over and over, the way you worry a loose tooth. The soldier had been feverish, half-blind with blood loss. People said things. People mistook strangers for the dead all the time; grief wore masks easily. That was the rational explanation, and Theo had built his entire self on being a rational man.

But when he finally slept, he dreamed of standing in a hall of white stone while someone knelt before him weeping, and in the dream he knew—he knew with the terrible certainty dreams grant—that he had done this to her. He had put her knees on that marble. And he woke with salt on his own lips and no idea whether it was hers or his.

* * *

They left Hollowmere two hours past dawn, walking south along a road that couldn't decide what century it belonged to. The village had given them bread, hard as slate, and directions delivered with the particular flatness of people who had stopped expecting good news: follow the river until it bent west, then look for dust. Dust meant the caravan. Dust meant Brannoc.

"The smuggler," Theo said, testing the word. "You're certain about him."

"I'm certain he moves people out of Veyra's path for coin, and that he hasn't sold anyone to her yet." Ada walked with the easy stride of someone who'd spent her life covering ground alone. "In this country, 'yet' is the best warranty available."

The land itself unsettled him more than the war did. Back through the gate, the valley had been ordinary—the kind of place where the strangest thing on any given street was a misdelivered parcel. Here, everything was *adjacent* to something else. Hedgerows grew in spirals. A crow had watched them for an entire mile with eyes the color of hammered copper, and when it finally flew, it flew in a straight line that bent slightly wrong, as if the air remembered a different shape. Wild magic, Ada called it—the covenant broken, the old restraints dissolved, power seeping up through the soil like water through a cracked cellar floor.

"Does it hurt anything?" he'd asked.

"It does whatever it likes," she'd said, and her jaw had done the thing it did when she was refusing to feel something. "That's rather the point."

By midday they found their dust.

It rose off the river road in a long brown banner: carts, wagons, handcarts, people on foot with bundles on their backs and children tied to them with rope, because a rope was cheaper than losing them. Theo counted forty-some vehicles before counting became unbearable. There was a smell to a refugee column that he would carry forever after—woodsmoke and wet wool and animal fear and under it all, faintly, milk, because someone was still feeding a baby, because life is obscene in its persistence.

A man detached from the column's head and came toward them at a rolling walk, thumbs hooked in his belt. He was broad without being tall, weathered to the color of old rope, with a beard trimmed short enough to suggest someone had once tried to cut his throat and he'd taken the lesson. His eyes went from Ada's staff to Theo's soft hands and back, doing arithmetic.

"Ada," she said. "Keeper of the Hollow Gate. This is Theo. We need passage south."

"Brannoc." He didn't offer a hand; he offered a long, assessing silence instead. "Gatekeeper. Haven't seen your sigil since I was a boy, and that one was older than my grandmother. Thought your line died out."

"Not quite."

"Shame it picked now to come back." He spat thoughtfully into the roadside grass. "Passage costs. Coin if you've got it. Labor if you haven't. The archivist looks like he can read, which means he can count, which means he can check wheel-bearings. The lady—" his gaze flicked to her staff again, and this time Theo saw him swallow, just barely, "—the lady walks rear guard."

"Done," Ada said, before Theo could open his mouth.

"Brannoc," Theo said quietly, as the smuggler turned away, "why do you do this? The tolls can't cover the risk."

Brannoc stopped. He didn't turn all the way around—just his head, cocked, one eye finding Theo down the line of his own nose.

"My sister's girls are on the fourth cart," he said. "Veyra burned Tarn Ford on a Tuesday. Nobody gave *her* terms." He resumed walking. "Wheel-bearings. Third axle from the front squeaks like a dying cat. Fix it or listen to it for sixty miles."

* * *

The caravan moved at the speed of its slowest ox, which gave Theo ample time to learn wheels.

He learned that a hub squeals when the grease has cooked out of it, that a cracked felloe sings a different note than a sound one, that children in transit will tell you their entire biographies within ten minutes if you let them hold a hammer. He learned that a girl named Wren, seven years old, missing two teeth, believed with absolute conviction that he was a wizard because he could make letters stand still on a page, and that no amount of explanation would dislodge this theory. She appointed herself his shadow and narrated the caravan to him in a whisper that carried across three fields.

"That's Marisol. She lost her house but kept her chickens. Chickens ride in the blue cart. That's Old Tam, he's not actually old, he just looks like a boot. That's the rear guard lady. She watches the road behind us like the road owes her money."

Theo glanced back. Ada walked twenty yards astern, staff across her shoulders, scanning the way they'd come with the patience of someone who expected the horizon to betray her eventually. Their eyes met. Something passed between them—not comfort, exactly. Acknowledgment. Two people standing in the same storm, agreeing silently not to pretend it was weather.

At dusk they made camp in the lee of a ruined tithe barn, and Brannoc posted watches, and Theo sat by the fire repairing Wren's shoe with an awl borrowed from the farrier, feeling—for the first time since the gate swallowed him—almost like a person performing a task rather than a person waiting for a prophecy to finish with him.

"You're getting better at that," Ada said, settling beside him.

"Lying about competence?"

"Sitting with people. You had archive-hunch when I met you."

"I still have archive-hunch. I'm just wearing it in the mud." He tied off the thread and turned the shoe in the firelight. "Ada. The soldier at Hollowmere. What he said."

"Betrayer." She said it plainly, which he loved and feared about her. "Yes."

"Do you believe him?"

She was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. "I believe he wasn't talking about you. Not the you I met on Thursday, eating olives like he was defusing them." A ghost of a smile crossed her face and left. "But this country remembers things, Theo. Names have weight here. If someone wore your face a hundred years ago and broke something enormous, then some part of that is going to be true about you whether you consented to it or not. That's what a covenant is. It doesn't ask."

"That's horrifying."

"Yes." She reached over and steadied his hand, the one holding the awl, which had begun to tremble. "It is. But you're not doing it alone, so eat something and sleep."

He ate something. He did not sleep—or he slept the way a fence post sleeps—and it was in that gray shallowness between watches that the dream came back.

The hall of white stone. The kneeling woman. But this time the dream did not stop where it always stopped. This time he heard himself speak, in a voice like his own run through cold water: *The covenant holds only while all bear its cost equally. You knew the terms.* And the woman lifted her face and said, *I knew the terms. I did not know you would enjoy reading them aloud.*

Theo woke with his heart slamming and his hands pressed over his mouth, because he had almost answered her. Out loud. In the dark. In a voice that was not entirely his own.

Across the dead fire, Wren stirred in her blanket and murmured about chickens, and Theo sat up until dawn watching the stars move in their subtly wrong arcs and understood, with the clarity of the sleepless, that he was not haunted. He was *inhabited*. Somewhere behind his eyes, a hundred years deep, someone else was waking up—and that someone had been cruel in ways Theo could not yet see, and patient in ways that frightened him more.

* * *

The ambush came on the fourth day, at a bend in the river where the cliffs pinched the road to a cart's width.

Later, Theo would try to reconstruct the order of events and fail. There was the moment the birdsong stopped, which Ada caught first—she simply raised her fist and the whole column halted, forty drivers and a hundred souls going silent as though she'd cut a string holding them upright. Then the rocks came down from the ridge, and the screaming started, and men in gray leathers with white chevrons swarmed up from the riverbank where they'd lain flat among the stones like lizards.

Veyra's outriders. Not an army—an appetite. They wanted the carts, the goods, and, rumor said, able-bodied prisoners for the works in the north.

What Theo remembered clearly was this:

He remembered Ada moving—fast, impossibly fast, her staff sweeping a rider's legs and the man going down hard, her free hand sketching a sign in the air that made the air itself go thick as honey around three charging soldiers so they floundered as if wading upstream. He remembered Brannoc roaring orders, getting the lead carts turned, putting wagon-flesh between the raiders and the children. He remembered Wren, who should have been in the third cart, standing frozen in the open beside a toppled basket of chickens, white feathers exploding everywhere, and a raider turning toward her with a knife and the bored expression of a man doing chores.

And then Theo was moving, and he was not thinking, and the thing that happened next did not happen in the world he had grown up in.

Words came out of him.

Not English. Not any language, and yet he knew precisely what each syllable weighed. He spoke a word that meant *stop* the way a mountain means *stay put*, and the word left his mouth and hit the air and the air obeyed. The raider stopped—not stumbled, *stopped*, mid-stride, like a fly in amber, his knife hand frozen six feet from a child's throat. Theo heard himself continue, calm, resonant, in that cold-water voice: naming the man's blade, unmaking its edge; naming the man's grip, loosening it finger by finger; and then leaning close, close enough to see the whites of the raider's wide, terrified eyes, and speaking a sentence he did not understand and understood completely:

*"You are known. Go home and be small."*

The man fled. So did two others near him, dropping weapons, stumbling over each other toward the river. Whatever they had heard in those words, it had reached into them and found something already afraid.

Then the world snapped back to its ordinary speed, the last raider was fleeing or dead or both, and Theo was on his knees in chicken feathers with blood in his mouth—he'd bitten his tongue, when?—and everyone in reach of his voice was looking at him the way you look at a door that has begun to open by itself.

Wren threw herself at him first, arms around his neck, fearless, chattering about how he'd *stopped him, he just stopped, did you see*—and Theo held onto her because holding her was the only thing keeping him anchored to the body he was in.

Because here was the terror, arriving on schedule, right on time, thorough as a tax collector: he had not learned those words. He had never heard them. They had come up out of him the way water comes up through a cracked floor, wild and old and fluent, and they had fit his mouth like they'd been fitted there a century ago. For eleven seconds he had been someone else wearing Theo's skin, and the someone else had been magnificent, and magnificent was the worst thing he had ever been.

Ada found him there. She knelt in the feathers beside him, one hand hovering near his shoulder without landing, giving him the choice.

"Theo."

"I know what you're going to say."

"Then I'll say the other thing." Her voice was low, steady, iron wrapped in wool. "Whatever that was—it saved her. It saved maybe a dozen lives today. Fear later. Right now, breathe."

He breathed. He counted the caravan's survivors because counting was what he did, handholds on the cliff face: thirty-eight carts, four wounded, none dying, one child alive who should not have been. Brannoc limped past and looked down at him for a long moment with an expression Theo couldn't parse—gratitude braided with something colder, older, the look of a man revising a map.

"Well," Brannoc said at last. "Archivist."

"I don't know what that was."

"No," Brannoc agreed. "But I do. My grandmother told stories." He shifted his weight off the bad leg. "There's a place south where words like yours still have roots. The Wellspring, the old folk called it. Where the covenant was spoken first." He looked east, toward the smoke smudging the horizon, then back at Theo, and his voice dropped to gravel. "If half of what my grandmother said is true, then you're either the only thing that can fix this country, or the reason it broke. Either way—you and me and the lady need to talk tonight. Because Veyra's outriders know your face now. And by week's end, so will she."
