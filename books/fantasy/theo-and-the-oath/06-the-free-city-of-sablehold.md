## 6. The Free City of Sablehold

They saw Sablehold before they heard it—a smudge of grey and gold on the horizon where the riverlands gave way to stone country, its nine towers rising like fingers pressed together in prayer. Or in blame, Ada thought, watching them grow through three days of walking. She couldn't decide which.

The road thickened as they approached. Carts drawn by mules and by stranger beasts—six-legged things with hides like wet slate that Brannoc swore were perfectly tame—jostled for position among farmers hauling autumn stores, merchants with sealed wagons, and refugees, always refugees, their possessions bundled into shapes that told you everything about what a person had thought to save when the world ended. A woman carried only a birdcage. The bird was long dead; Ada could see that from twenty paces. The woman carried it anyway.

"They'll queue at the Ash Gate for hours," Brannoc said, spitting into the dust with the affection of a man describing his own mother's cooking. "Sablehold doesn't open its gates so much as deign to notice you exist. But I know the weaver's postern, and I know the toll-keeper's weakness for riverbrandy." He grinned at Theo, who had been quiet all morning, his hands wrapped around a strip of dried apple he hadn't eaten. "Cheer up, archivist. You're about to see the last free city on this side of the mountains pretend very hard that nothing is wrong."

Ada felt Theo's silence like weather. Since the ambush on the caravan road—since the moment his hands had moved with a soldier's certainty and fire had answered him out of empty air—he had spoken less each day. He walked slightly apart now, not from them but from himself, as though he were escorting a stranger and didn't trust it to keep pace.

"You should eat," she said quietly, falling back beside him.

"I'm not hungry."

"You haven't been hungry in four days. That's not how bodies work." She took the apple strip from his hand, tore off a piece, and held it up until he opened his mouth like a sulky child. He chewed. Something almost like a smile crossed his face and drowned again. "Good. Whatever's living in your head, it doesn't get to starve the rest of you. That's the deal."

"And if the thing in my head is *me*?" His voice was low enough that only she caught it over the creak of cart wheels. "Ada, I knew the name of that mercenary captain before he drew steel. Knew which knee was stiff from an old break. I'd never seen him. And when the fire came—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "When the fire came, I wasn't frightened of it. That's what frightens me."

She wanted to tell him it would be fine. She wanted to say: whatever you were before, whoever carved your face into a book a century ago, that person made his choices, and you are allowed to make different ones. She had said something like it two nights ago, by the fire, and he had nodded and not slept. Words like that were true and useless at once, like telling a drowning man the sea was beautiful.

Instead she said, "Then we find out what the Council knows about Oathbearers. They have archives older than anything I ever kept. If anyone can tell you who's living in your head—and whether he can be evicted—it's them."

Ahead, Sablehold's walls rose out of the morning haze, black granite veined with quartz that caught the light and threw it back in threads of silver. Even from here Ada could see the wrongness: the watchtowers manned, yes, but thinly; the great banners of the Nine hanging motionless in windless air, each one stitched with a sigil of one of the founding trades—the loom, the anvil, the ledger, the lamp. Prosperity banners. Peace banners. No war banners anywhere.

"They don't know yet," she murmured.

"Brannoc says they know," Theo said. "He says they just don't believe."

"Those aren't always different things," Ada said, "in cities."

* * *

The weaver's postern admitted them through a door barely wide enough for two, into a tangle of streets that smelled of dye-vats and hot bread and horse. Brannoc led them with the unerring confidence of a man who had been thrown out of every district in the city and remembered each expulsion fondly. Up through the Dyers' Quarter, where cloth hung between buildings like captured skies; across the Ledgerbridge, where money-changers shouted prices for goods whose origins had burned weeks ago; and finally up the Processional, the great avenue that climbed toward the Council Spire at the city's heart.

It was there that Ada first understood the shape of Sablehold's disbelief.

A herald stood on the steps of the Chancery of Records, reading from a scroll to a crowd that had gathered not out of alarm but out of appetite for spectacle. "...and the Council wishes to reassure all citizens that reports of hostilities in the northern holds remain *unconfirmed*," the herald intoned. "The Council further notes that no envoy from the Holds has petitioned this body, which any reasonable person will recognize as evidence against claims of their destruction. Citizens are advised to continue their commerce, observe the festival preparations—"

"Festival," Theo repeated flatly. Beside him, a refugee family with ash still ground into the creases of their faces stared up at the steps, and the woman's laugh came out cracked down the middle.

"The Harvest of Nine Lights," the herald continued serenely. "Which will proceed as scheduled."

Ada watched the crowd. Half of them had come up the same roads she had, past the same burned waystations, carrying the same stories, and she could see the belief and the disbelief fighting openly in their expressions—not because they were fools, but because believing cost something, and the city was offering, for one more day, not to charge them. She knew that arithmetic intimately. She had spent her whole life doing it at the Hollow Gate: the cost of knowing against the comfort of the ordinary evening.

"The Nine aren't cowards," Brannoc said, watching her watch the crowd. "Before you decide they are. Old Merrow—the Speaker for the Loom—fought at Red Ford when she was younger than you. It's worse than cowardice. It's accounting. War means the granaries open, the levies come due, the festival stops paying for itself. As long as Veyra is a rumor, she's cheaper than a rumor's remedy." He shrugged. "I've smuggled a lot of things. Denial ships best of all. Keeps its shape, travels well, spoils slow."

"Then we make denial expensive," Ada said.

Brannoc looked at her with new interest. "And how does a gatekeeper from a valley nobody's heard of do that?"

"She shows them the gatekeeper's sigil," Theo said quietly. Both of them turned. He hadn't spoken since the bridge. His eyes were fixed on the Spire above them, where the Council chamber sat behind glass older than the city's walls. "You told me what it means. Keeper of a covenant-seam. The Nine were founded under the old oath, weren't they? Their charters name it." He glanced at Ada, and for a moment the lost look burned away and something sharper looked out—something that catalogued, cross-referenced, *knew*. "Make them read their own founding documents against their own heralds."

Ada's skin prickled. There he was, then: not the archivist, not quite the Betrayer, but some third thing being assembled out of both, piece by piece, on the road. "Theo," she said carefully, "when did you read the Sablehold charters?"

"I didn't." He frowned, genuinely puzzled, and she watched the sharpness gutter out, leaving only the tired young man who had once asked her, over bad wine, whether she found her work lonely. "I—why did I say that? How do I know their charters name the oath?"

No one answered him. Above them, the herald rolled up his scroll and the crowd began to dissolve into the ordinary business of a city pretending to be a city, and Ada took Theo's arm and felt, beneath her fingers, a fine tremor that had nothing to do with cold.

* * *

They waited six hours in the antechamber of the Council Spire, because even the end of the world required a petition form in triplicate.

The room was beautiful in the way that institutions are beautiful: rib-vaulted ceilings painted with the Nine Trades, benches of polished heartwood worn smooth by generations of waiting citizens, clerks gliding past with armloads of documents that mattered enormously to someone. Ada filled out the forms. Under *nature of emergency*, after a long pause, she wrote: *The covenant between the realms is broken. The northern holds have fallen. The usurper Veyra marches south.* The clerk read it, stamped it, and assigned them a hearing slot for the following spring.

Brannoc got them heard anyway, though Ada never learned precisely what he traded or to whom; she suspected the toll-keeper's brandy was only the beginning of a long and colorful chain. By late afternoon a door opened, a page in livery beckoned with an expression of profound inconvenience, and they climbed the inner stair of the Spire while light poured through windows of blown glass, casting colored ghosts of themselves on the stone ahead—red Ada, green Theo, blue Brannoc, walking toward the Nine.

The council chamber was a ring. Nine seats curved around a floor of white marble, and in the marble's center, inlaid in metals Ada didn't recognize, was the old sigil of the covenant: two interlocking circles, one open, one closed, bound by a line of gold. She stopped when she saw it. Her grandmother had worn that mark on a cord around her neck every day of her life, and Ada wore it now, beneath her shirt, warm from her own skin. She had assumed it was a family thing. A valley thing. Seeing it set into the floor of a city council a hundred leagues from home rearranged something in her chest.

Nine figures occupied the seats. Some old, some not; robed variously in the colors of their trades. At the center sat the eldest, a woman with a spine like an oar handle and hair the white of frost—Speaker Merrow, presumably, the Loom. Her eyes were the pale grey of undyed thread, and they went straight past Brannoc's swaggering introduction and settled on Ada with the weight of a full loom beam.

"A gatekeeper," Merrow said. Not a question. "We had begun to think your kind was a story. Like the covenant itself." Around the ring, several of the Nine shifted; one—a fat man wearing the ledger's chain of office—made a sound of polite dismissal that died quickly under Merrow's glance. "Well, Keeper? You've paid good coin to waste our evening. Spend it."

Ada had rehearsed this on the road. She had rehearsed it in the antechamber. She had rehearsed versions that were diplomatic, versions that were urgent, versions that flattered and versions that shamed. What came out of her mouth, looking at that sigil in the floor, was none of them.

"The northern holds have fallen. Correnvale. Ashford Deep. The river towns are burning—you'll hear it confirmed within the month, and you'll wish you'd spent less time on festival logistics when you do. Veyra has shattered the covenant that your charters—" she pointed at the marble, at the interlocked circles, "—are sworn upon. Your walls were built in balance with the other realm. Your quarries cut stone that obeys the old binding. When the covenant broke, everything downstream of it began to break too. Ask your farmers why the wells taste of iron. Ask your dyers why the mordant's gone strange this season. Magic is leaking through seams that used to hold, and it is coming through *angry*."

"Alleged leaks," said the Ledger speaker smoothly. "Unverified phenomena. The Council has received forty-seven petitions of this general character since midsummer, Keeper. Crop blights blamed on curses. Weather blamed on sorcery. Fear is a crop that grows itself; we needn't water it."

"Forty-seven petitions," Ada repeated. "How many of those petitioners crossed from beyond the boundary to stand in this room?"

That landed. She saw it land—in the small stillness that passed around the ring, in the way the Lamp speaker leaned forward. Because they knew, all of them knew, that crossing was supposed to be impossible. That the gate kept. That no petitioner from the far side had ever reached this chamber in the lifetime of anyone alive.

"I crossed," Ada said. "Through the Hollow Gate, the seam your founders bound, the seam my family has kept for nine generations without pay or thanks or, apparently, a single letter asking whether we still existed. I broke my own law to come here. I broke the one law my grandmother died holding. So believe me when I tell you what it costs me to say the rest of it." She pulled the cord from beneath her shirt.

The keeper's sigil swung into the lamplight: two interlocking circles in old gold, the closed circle dark, the open one—she had never noticed this before, had never been able to see it clearly until this exact angle of light—shot through with a hairline crack.

The effect on the chamber was not dramatic. It was worse than dramatic. It was *quiet*. Nine people who had spent the day refusing to believe leaned, one by one, toward that small swinging disc, and Ada watched certainty die in their faces the way frost dies on glass—not all at once but in spreading patches, from the edges inward.

"That mark," Merrow said. Her voice had changed entirely; the oar-handle spine bent forward. "Where is your seal-stone, Keeper? Every sigil was struck from a seal-stone. Where is yours?"

"Burned," Ada said. "With my grandmother's house. With the eastern watch-post. Three weeks ago, the night the gate first stirred on its own." She let that sit. "Your charter vaults will have records of the seal-stones. Nine of them, once, for nine seams. Ask your archivists how many are accounted for."

An archivist. Of course. She heard herself say it and felt Theo go rigid beside her.

"We will convene a full inquiry," began the Ledger speaker, and the word *inquiry* was still leaving his mouth when Merrow raised one hand, and the room obeyed her the way water obeys a channel.

"Inquiry later." The old Speaker rose. She was shorter than Ada had guessed, and it didn't matter at all. "Keeper. You said the gate stirred on its own. Gates don't stir. Gates are doors; doors wait. Unless something on either side is pulling." Her grey eyes moved—slowly, deliberately—from Ada to the young man standing half a step behind her shoulder, the one who had not spoken, who had been trying very hard all afternoon to be furniture.

"What did you bring us, Keeper?"

And Ada, who had promised him she wouldn't do this without asking, who had sworn it on the road outside Correnvale with his hand cold in hers—Ada looked at Theo, and Theo looked back at her, and gave her the smallest nod, the nod of a man stepping off a ledge because the ledge was going to crumble anyway and better to choose the falling.

"He came through the gate," she said. "Not over it. Through it. It opened for him. It hasn't done that in a thousand years, and the last time it did, the histories call the man it opened for by another name." She drew a breath. "The prophecy books call him the Oathbearer. The soldiers from the north—" her voice roughened, remembering the dying man's grip, the word spat like a curse, "—the soldiers call him the Betrayer. And he remembers none of it, and it's happening to him anyway, and he is either the reason your city survives what's coming or the reason it falls, and I don't know which, and neither does he, and *someone in this room had better*."

Silence. The lamps hissed. Somewhere below, muffled through floors of stone, the city went on preparing a festival.

Merrow descended from her seat, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and crossed the white marble to stand before Theo. She studied his face the way one studies a page written in a language half-remembered.

"Show me your left wrist," she said.

Theo hesitated, then pushed back his sleeve. Beneath it, faint as watermarks, lines circled his forearm—an old scar-pattern none of them had remarked upon, because scars are easy not to see. But Ada saw Merrow's expression, and Merrow's expression saw the pattern, and the pattern was a script.

"The binding-cipher," the Speaker said softly. "Struck into the flesh of every Oathbearer at their swearing. We have one other record of it." She turned to her fellow councillors, and her voice, when it came again, carried to every corner of the ring without effort at all. "In the deep archive. On the tomb-effigy we recovered from the Weeping Pass two centuries ago. The effigy of the First Oathbearer." She looked back at Theo. "Whose face, Master Archivist, matches yours exactly—and whose name, according to our records, was given away at the Wellspring, and cannot be spoken by any living tongue, because it no longer exists to be spoken."

She let the silence ring like a struck bell.

"So," Merrow said, "before we debate belief any further—I suggest someone tells me what he *is* called now. Because whatever name he carries, I suspect it isn't his. And in my experience, Councilor—" her gaze swept the ring, daring any of the Nine to object, "—a man wearing a borrowed name is either the most dangerous thing in this room, or the only thing in it worth saving. Possibly both."

Outside the high windows, far to the north, above the mountains, the sky had begun—very faintly, very early—to glow the color of a distant burning.

No one in the chamber mentioned it. Everyone in the chamber saw it.
