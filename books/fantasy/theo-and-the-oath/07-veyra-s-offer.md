## 7. Veyra's Offer

The letter arrived on the third morning after the Council's dismissal, folded inside a slab of slate no larger than a playing card and sealed with wax the color of old blood.

Theo found it on his pillow.

He stood in the doorway of the small guest chamber Sablehold had given them—given him, though Ada slept in the chair by the window more often than not, as if proximity to him were a duty she hadn't yet decided whether to keep or abandon—and watched the thing sit there in the gray light, ordinary as a bill of sale. The room smelled of tallow and river damp and the cold stone breath of a city bracing itself for war. Somewhere below, a smith was already working, the rhythm of his hammer arriving through the floor like a slow second heartbeat.

No one had entered. He had heard no one enter. And the door had been barred from within—he'd checked twice, an archivist's habit of securing rare things translating, without his permission, into an exile's habit of checking locks.

He crossed the room and picked up the slate. It was heavier than it should have been, the way certain books were heavier than their pages could account for, the way objects became when they had been *meant*. The seal broke with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

*To the man called Theo,* the letter began, in a hand so precise it looked printed rather than written, each letter a small act of architecture.

*You do not know who you are. I do.*

*I know what you were before you were an archivist shelving other people's histories. I know what you swore, and to whom, and what you took in payment, and what it cost—not you; you were never the one who paid.*

*I am offering you the same bargain you once accepted, under better terms. Come to me. Renounce the title the prophecy has pinned to you like a thief's brand, and you will be given amnesty: your life, your freedom, your anonymity. No one will hunt you. No one will remember you. You may return to your valley and your quiet work and your keeper, and live out a small, unwatched life among people who will never know what they almost asked of you.*

*You have made this choice before. You know how it ends. Come and make it again, and this time it can end differently—for you.*

*—Veyra, who remembers everything*

There was no threat in it. That was what unsettled him most, turning the slate over twice, holding it to the light as if the true message might be written in some invisible medium between the lines. There was no threat because there didn't need to be. The offer understood something about him that he himself had only begun to suspect: that he could be bought. That somewhere beneath the archivist's mild surface, beneath the man who catalogued marginalia and apologized to furniture when he bumped into it, there was a self that had already, once, taken exactly this deal.

And that self had doomed the world.

He sat down on the edge of the bed with the letter still in his hands and did not move for a long time.

* * *

Ada found him at dusk on the wall above the River Gate, where the city's stones gave way to sky and the riverlands unrolled southward in bands of bronze and smoke. She climbed the stairs quietly, but he heard her anyway—had been hearing her approach for three days now with an accuracy that frightened him, the way one hears a familiar piece of music from several rooms away. Her footsteps had a particular weight on the left, a half-beat hesitation before the right. He could have identified her blind, in a crowd of thousands.

"You haven't eaten," she said.

"I ate."

"You moved food around a plate." She came to stand beside him, forearms on the parapet, close enough that her shoulder almost touched his. Below them, the evening fires of Sablehold were kindling one by one, and beyond the walls the refugee camps spread like lichen over the fields, ten thousand small flames where people who had run from Veyra's armies cooked whatever they had. "The Council's quartermaster says you've been down there every day. Helping with the ledgers."

"Someone has to count the grain."

"Someone has to," she agreed. "That's not why you do it."

He looked out at the camps rather than at her. Counting was a kind of prayer for him, he was beginning to understand—or a kind of hiding. Numbers stayed where you put them. A ledger balanced or it didn't, and either answer was final, and nothing in a ledger ever turned out to have been someone else all along. "I got a letter," he said.

"I know. Brannoc told me. He says a sealed message appearing inside a locked room is either a very good sign or a very bad one, and he's placing his money on bad."

"It's an offer." He handed her the slate. "Amnesty. My life back—the small one, the valley one—if I renounce any claim to being the Oathbearer. If I walk away."

Ada read it twice. He watched her face by firelight, the way her jaw set at *your keeper*, the way her eyes narrowed—not at Veyra's words but at their shape, reading between them the way she read between the lines of old documents. "She knows things about you," she said finally. "Things you don't know yourself."

"She says she does."

"And do you believe her?"

He wanted to say no. What came out instead was the truth, which had a texture like river water, cold and impossible to hold: "Every word of it sounds like something I would have done."

They stood together in silence while the last light drained out of the west. Then Ada said, carefully, the way she stepped onto ice: "In the pass, you heard your echo too. Everyone did. But yours—" She stopped. Started again. "You went white. You wouldn't tell me what it said."

"It said, *you always choose the door that opens.*"

"That's not so terrible."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. That's what makes it terrible. It wasn't a curse, Ada. It was a description."

Below them a child in the camps was singing something tuneless and repetitive, and the sound rose up the wall thin as thread. Theo found his hands had gone cold around the parapet stone, and he realized he was going to tell her—was deciding to tell her, in the same instant that the telling began, as if some older part of him had already made the choice and the newer parts were only catching up.

"The memory came last night," he said. "After the letter. Or maybe the letter pulled it up—I don't know how it works. Like dredging. Something heavy moving along the bottom of me, and the letter's weight passing overhead snagged it." He breathed out. "I remembered making a bargain."

Ada turned to face him fully. In her eyes the camp-fires burned small and doubled.

"There was a gate," he said slowly, feeling for the memory the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "Not our gate. One of the deep ones, in the north—a crossing place so old it had grown its own weather. And there was a woman standing before it, and the covenant was failing, and everyone knew it was failing, and the whole weight of it had come down to a single question that only I could answer." His voice thinned. "Only the first Oathbearer could answer. Only—him. Me."

"What was the question?"

"Whether to give up a name." The words came easier now, horribly, the memory opening like a well. "The covenant ran on names—that's what the Wellspring is for, that's why it matters. Every binding is a name given freely into the keeping of the oath. And the name the covenant needed was mine. Not *Theo*. The other one. The name I was before I was anyone's archivist, before I was anyone at all. If I surrendered it, the covenant held for another age, and I—" He faltered. "I don't think I would have died. I think something worse. I think I would have become no one. Unwritten. Every record of me erased, every mind that held me emptied of the shape of me. I would never have existed enough even to be mourned."

"And you refused."

"I bargained." The self-loathing arrived then, right on schedule, right on cue, like a performer who had waited a century in the wings. "That's the part that's mine, Ada. Not the refusal—anyone refuses. The bargaining. I looked at the price and I went looking for change. I found another name. Someone whose name mattered less, whose sacrifice the covenant would accept in substitution, because the old oath was built hungry and it wasn't particular about what it ate." His hands had begun to shake, and he gripped the parapet. "It was a ward of the covenant itself. A person raised inside it, fed to its upkeep, kept like a lamp keeps oil. I told myself her name was already spent, already owed, that using it was arithmetic and not murder. And the covenant accepted. And it held."

"For a hundred years," Ada said quietly.

"For a hundred years. Long enough for my betrayal to be forgotten by everyone except the ones it hurt. Long enough for a valley to grow up around a gate and call its watching an honor. Long enough for a girl to be born into a family of watchers who were really just—" His throat closed. "Waiting for me. Keeping my seat warm."

"Theo—"

"Don't." He said it gently, but he said it. "Don't soften it. I need you to understand what Veyra understands. When she writes to me, she isn't guessing at my character. She's *citing* it. I traded one life for millions once, and the trade was rotten, and I dressed the rot up as mercy, and it worked, and the realms got a century of borrowed time that we are living in the last days of right now. And now she's offering me the same door again, polished up, with better terms. Walk away. Be no one. Let someone else pay." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you know the worst part? Standing up here, tired, afraid, with her letter in my pocket—I *want* to take it. Some part of me is already reaching for it. The same part that reached last time."

The child below had stopped singing. In its absence the river sounded loud, patient, indifferent.

Ada was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, she did not touch him, though he felt the decision not to like a hand laid flat against his back. "What was her name?" she asked. "The ward's."

"I don't know." He frowned, groping. "That's the strange thing. I remember everything else—the gate, the frost on the stones, the exact color of the light, the smell of the air like snow about to fall. Everything but her face and her name. As if the memory has been censored. Or as if—" He stopped.

"As if she took it with her," Ada finished.

"Or as if I gave it back." He stared out at the darkening camps. "When the covenant broke—when she broke it, if the soldier in the village was right, if Veyra and the ward are the same—I wonder what happens to a name that was fed into an oath. Does it die? Or does it come back changed?"

"You're asking whether she hates you specifically."

"I'm asking whether I would hate me specifically." He turned to look at her at last, and let her see all of it, the cowardice included, because he was so tired of curating himself in front of her. "She offered me amnesty, Ada. Not punishment. Amnesty. As if the debt between us runs the other way—as if she thinks she owes *me*, or wants me to think so, or wants the world to watch her spare the Betrayer and call it justice. Every version of it is a trap and I can't find the jaws."

"Then don't go alone," Ada said simply.

"It's not an invitation I can accept. It's a summons. Look at the slate again—there's no place, no time. Which means the meeting chooses itself. Which means—"

The light went out.

Not the fires below—those burned on, ten thousand points of stubborn orange—but the light in his hands. The slate, which he had tucked inside his coat, woke through the fabric with a cold that bit through wool and linen and skin, and when he dragged it out it was glowing from within, blood-colored, and letters were surfacing across its face like drowned bodies rising.

*You remember now,* Veyra had written, in that architectural hand. *Good. Then you know the terms are fair. The road east of the river, where the willows grow white. Come at moonrise. Come alone—or come with the keeper, and show us both which promise matters more to you.*

"She's watching," Ada said. "She's watching right now."

"I know."

"How?"

"Because I can feel her attention." He said it with the calm of a man reporting symptoms, and the calm terrified him more than the words. "Like sun through a window. Like being read." He looked down at the letter, at the last line that was even now writing itself, ink blooming wet and fresh:

*One more thing, Oathbearer. Ask your keeper what her grandmother buried under the hearthstone. Ask her why the women of her line never marry outside the valley. Ask her what she was raised for—and whether anyone asked her consent.*

*We are not so different, she and I. Both of us kept.*

Theo read it aloud, because he could not not, and watched the color leave Ada's face by degrees, watched her hand come up and press flat against the parapet as if the wall might be persuaded to hold her up.

"My grandmother's records," she whispered. "The ones I went home for. I couldn't find them. I thought the valley forgetting me had—" She stopped. Her eyes came up to his, and in them he saw the exact moment grief hardened into something with an edge. "She knew. She's known this whole time. About my family. About what we were *for*."

"Ada—"

"She wants to drive a wedge." Ada's voice steadied itself with visible effort, the keeper's discipline reasserting itself over the girl. "Divide the Oathbearer from the one person who—"

"Or she's telling the truth," Theo said softly. "That's what she does. That's what *I* did. The best traps aren't lies, Ada. They're true things arranged in the shape of a lie."

Far to the east, past the black line of the river, lightning flickered inside a cloudless sky—once, twice, rhythmic as a signal lamp. On the wall around them, sentries began to shout. Down in the camps, dogs started up in a ragged chorus, and the child who had been singing earlier began, very quietly, to cry.

Ada straightened. Whatever war was happening behind her eyes, she filed it, unopened, and looked at him with the expression he was coming to think of as her gatekeeper's face—the one that had watched a boundary alone for years, the one that expected nothing from the world except the next thing it would have to survive.

"Moonrise is two hours off," she said. "So we have two hours to decide what we're walking into. Together." She held out her hand for the slate. "And Theo—when we get there, whatever she offers you, whatever she shows you, whatever true thing she arranges in the shape of a lie—you look at *me* first. Not her. Promise me that much."

He gave her his hand, and the slate passed between them, still faintly glowing, still warm as a held secret.

"One more thing," he said, as the sentries' torches came running along the wall toward them and the eastern lightning blinked its patient count. "The willows east of the river. White willows grow over graves."
