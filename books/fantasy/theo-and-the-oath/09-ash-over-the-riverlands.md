## 9. Ash Over the Riverlands

The river came to them before they saw it — a smell on the wind, sweet and wrong, like sugar burning in a closed room.

Theo had learned to read Brannoc's silences over the days since the ambush. The smuggler talked constantly when things were fine: jokes about Ada's cooking, complaints about his mule's politics, long digressions about the tariff wars of his youth. But when danger came, he went quiet as a held breath, and it was that quiet that made Theo rein in his horse now, on the ridge above the Riverlands, and look south.

The sky was the color of a bruise gone bad. Not cloud-dark but ash-dark, a brownish murk that swallowed the sun whole, so that the light falling across the valley below was dim and sourceless, like the last hour of dusk stretched into eternity. And beneath that murk, where the maps in Theo's memory — his real memory, his archivist's memory — said three towns should stand along the bend of the Aldenwater, there were fires.

Not one fire. Dozens. Hundreds. They strung along the river like a necklace of embers, and the smoke rose from them in columns so wide they leaned against each other, merging into that single suffocating ceiling overhead.

"Marlow's Ford," Brannoc said at last. His voice was flat in a way Theo had never heard from him. "That's Marlow's Ford burning. And Caster's Reach beyond it. And—" He stopped. Swallowed. "The Weir, maybe. I have cousins at the Weir."

"How long ago?" Ada asked. She had ridden up beside them without a sound, the way she did everything, and her eyes were fixed on the horizon with an expression Theo was beginning to know: not fear exactly, but a kind of terrible accounting, as if she were measuring the distance between what was and what could still be saved.

"Hours," Brannoc said. "A day, for the furthest." He spat, and the spit seemed gray before it even left his mouth. "Veyra doesn't garrison towns anymore. She doesn't need to. She makes an example of one stretch of river and every other town opens its gates on its knees."

Theo found he was gripping the reins hard enough that his knuckles ached. *She makes an example.* The phrase slid through him with a strange, horrible familiarity, the way certain phrases had been sliding through him since the pass — not memories, exactly, but grooves worn so deep that new words fell into them perfectly. Somewhere in another life, another mind had heard those words spoken and understood them, and that understanding lived on in him like a scar lives on skin that has forgotten the wound.

"We can't go through," Ada said. It wasn't a question. She looked at Brannoc. "Is there another crossing?"

"There's always another crossing. That's my entire profession." Brannoc's humor tried to surface and drowned. "But there'll be people coming out of there too. There always are. The ones who waited too long, or hid too well, or thought the stories were exaggerated." He turned his mule off the ridgeline road onto a sheep track heading downriver, away from the worst of the burn. "We go parallel. Stay ahead of the panic if we can. And we keep our faces out of it, because the caravan's got enough trouble carrying its own."

Theo understood then, with a cold settling in his stomach, that Brannoc meant it — meant to ride past whatever they found on the roads. And he understood, just as clearly, that neither Brannoc nor anyone else could say whether Theo would obey.

* * *

They met the first refugees at midafternoon, though the ash made it feel like evening.

A family, or what remained of the shape of one: a woman pulling a handcart with a broken wheel, two old men walking beside it like pilings holding something up, and a cluster of children moving in the tight orbit children form when the world has taught them suddenly that orbits are safety. They had crossed the ford at Marlow's Ford the night before, the woman said, when the soldiers were still burning the granaries and hadn't yet turned to the houses. Her voice had the peculiar calm of someone who had used up all their screaming and found nothing left on the other side.

"They wore no colors," one of the old men kept saying, to no one, to everyone. "No colors at all. An army ought to wear colors. How do you know who's killing you?"

"The ash knows," said one of the children, matter-of-factly, and went back to leading a goat that had no rope.

By nightfall the sheep track had become a river of its own, flowing the opposite direction from the Aldenwater: hundreds of them, then thousands, a slow dark current of carts and livestock and people carrying what they could and leaving what they couldn't. Brannoc's caravan swelled around them the way a stream swells toward a floodplain. No one decided it. It simply happened, the way water happens. Refugees attach themselves to anyone who looks like they know where they're going, and Brannoc, with his maps and his mule and his endless loud opinions about roads, looked like he knew.

Theo walked most of that night rather than ride. The horses needed easing under the extra weight, and besides, he could not bear the height of the saddle; from horseback the exodus was spectacle, and he wanted desperately for it not to be spectacle. On foot he was inside it: the coughing of the old men breathing ash, the thin persistent crying of infants too exhausted to cry properly, the smell of scorched grain clinging to everything like a second skin. A boy of perhaps six marched beside him for a mile solemnly carrying a single boot. When Theo asked about the other boot, the boy considered the question with great seriousness and said, "It's at home."

Ada found him near midnight, when they paused to let a wagon with a shattered axle sort itself out.

"You're doing it again," she said quietly.

"Doing what?"

"Carrying it." She nodded at the column of humanity stretching back into the dark as far as the eye could follow, lit here and there by cookfires that looked, from this angle, horribly like the distant burns they fled. "You walk like you're trying to hold all of them up by yourself. Like you think it's yours to fix."

"Isn't it?" The words came out of him before he could weigh them, and once they were out he could not call them back. Ada looked at him — really looked, the gatekeeper's look, the one that saw straight through to the load-bearing walls of a person — and Theo realized he had asked her a question he didn't know the answer to himself.

"It's yours to *try*," she said finally. "There's a difference. Try is human. Fix is prophecy, and prophecy is how Veyra thinks." She touched his arm, brief and warm. "Sleep when you can. Tomorrow will ask more of us than tonight."

* * *

Tomorrow asked.

It began with the sound — a low, grinding roar from downriver, like the world clearing its throat — and then the crowd itself changed, the way water changes before it goes over a falls. A ripple of panic ran backward through the exodus, word passing mouth to mouth faster than any rider: *the bridge at Caster's Reach is gone, they've cut the crossing, the ones on the far bank—*

Theo pushed forward through the press with Ada and Brannoc behind him, and when they reached the rise above the river he stopped understanding the world for a moment.

Caster's Reach had been a bridge town; he knew it from the trade atlases, a graceful span of white stone five hundred years old. Now the bridge was a row of broken teeth rising from brown water, and the far bank was black with people. Hundreds. Maybe more. They had reached the crossing after the demolition, and now they lined the shore of the flooded spring river while on the near bank — the bank Theo stood on — the crowd was too dense, too frightened, too pressed against the water's edge for anyone to organize boats fast enough. The river ran high with snowmelt, fast and muscular, tearing branches off trees and spinning them downstream like toys.

And in the middle of the near-shore chaos, wedged on a rock shelf at the waterline where the crowd's crush had forced her, was a child. A girl, small, seven or eight, in a soaked wool coat, stranded as the current climbed. Every wave of the panicked crowd shoved someone new toward the edge; every shove sent spray over the rock; and the girl stood with her arms wrapped around herself, too terrified to move either direction, while the Aldenwater ate the rock beneath her inch by inch.

Her mother was somewhere in the crowd — Theo could hear the screaming, a woman's voice shredded raw, repeating the child's name: *Nell. Nell. NELL.*

Brannoc was already shouting orders, already throwing ropes, already being the competent man he was paid to be. Ada had waded hip-deep into the shallows with her keeper's sigil raised, using its authority like a blade to cut lanes through the mob — *by the covenant, MOVE, in the old law's name, MOVE* — and it worked, partly, the crowd parting around her like iron filings around a lodestone.

None of it would be fast enough. Theo could see that with perfect, crystalline clarity, the way you see things in dreams: the rock giving way, the small dark shape of the girl going into the brown water, gone before any rope reached her.

He moved without deciding to move.

Later — much later, when he tried to reconstruct it — he would remember only fragments. Going down the bank. Cold water to his knees. The girl's face, ash-streaked, eyes enormous. And then the strange sensation, the one that would haunt him longest: a feeling like a door opening inside his chest, not violently but *easily*, the way a lock turns when the right key finally arrives.

The river stopped.

Not all of it. Just there — just before the rock, just before the girl — the Aldenwater stood up. That was the only word his mind would accept afterward. It stood up, a wall of churning brown water ten feet high, hanging impossibly in the air with branches and debris suspended in it like flies in amber, and behind that wall the current roared on, but before it there was only silence, and wet air, and a path of exposed riverbed gleaming like a road.

The crowd went silent. Thousands of people, and not one voice.

Theo walked across the dry riverbed and lifted the girl off the rock. She weighed almost nothing. She smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, and her fists closed in his collar with desperate strength, and he carried her up the bank through that vast astonished quiet, and set her down in her mother's arms, and heard the screaming change pitch — grief turning inside out into joy — and felt nothing at all.

Because all of him, every scrap of attention he owned, was fixed on the wall of water.

It stood there, patient, obedient, waiting for him. And he could feel it — this was the part that curdled the awe into something colder — he could feel it the way you feel your own hand. Not commanding it. *Containing* it. As if the river's weight had been set down inside him, in some room in his chest that had apparently been built for exactly this purpose, furnished and waiting, empty for a hundred years.

*I know this,* he thought, and the thought arrived with the gentle certainty of something long rehearsed. *I have done this before. Many times. It is easy.*

That was the moment fear arrived — not fear of the water, but fear of the ease.

He let go. He didn't know the technique of letting go; it simply happened, the door in his chest swinging open and the river pouring back into the space it had abandoned, crashing down into the emptied riverbed with a concussion that knocked people off their feet fifty yards away. Spray rained over the crowd. Someone laughed, high and hysterical. The Aldenwater resumed its ancient argument with its banks, and the bridge of standing water was gone as if it had never been.

Except that it had been. Everyone had seen it.

* * *

They camped that night on a hillside above the ford, and Theo sat apart from the fires, and the caravan looked at him the way villages look at churches: with reverence, and with the careful distance reverence requires.

Ada came and sat beside him in the dark. She didn't speak for a long time. Below them the refugee fires spread along the hills like fallen stars, and beyond them the southern sky still glowed faintly over the burned towns.

"You saved her," Ada said at last.

"I don't know what I did." His voice sounded foreign to himself. "I didn't decide anything. It was like — like reaching for something and finding your hand already knows the shape of it. Ada, I *held a river*. I stood in a dry riverbed. Rivers don't do that. People can't do that. Whatever I am, whatever I was—"

"Stop." Her hand found his in the dark. "Listen to me. What I felt today, watching you — it wasn't fear of you. It was awe, and then worry, and then—" she hesitated, "—grief. Because you looked at that little girl's mother like you'd committed a crime instead of a miracle."

"Didn't I?" Theo stared at the water-glow of the distant river. "Power like that isn't free. Nothing in this world is free — your grandmother's laws, the covenant, all of it is built on things costing what they cost. If I can stop a river with a thought, then somewhere there's a bill. And I don't know whose hands the bill lands in." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Or maybe worse — maybe there's no bill at all. Maybe it's just *easy*, the way it felt, and the person who finds it easy is the person who starts finding everything else easy too. Burning towns. Making examples. Veyra was somebody's ward once, Ada. Somebody sacrificed. She didn't start wanting the world to burn."

"No," Ada agreed quietly. "But she chose it. That's the difference. The power doesn't choose." She squeezed his hand. "You walked into that river for a stranger's child when you could have stayed on the hill. Whatever else you were, that's who showed up today. Hold onto that when everything else gets loud."

He wanted to believe her. He held her hand and watched the stars come out through gaps in the ash-cloud, and he tried.

But sleep, when it finally took him, brought dreams — and the dreams were not his own. He stood again in a dry riverbed, but the water-wall was miles high and the color of night, and beyond it waited a city under siege, and a voice that was his own voice and yet a hundred years older said, gently, reasonably, the way all betrayals are reasoned: *It's easy. You know how easy it is. One name, and everyone you love walks free.*

He woke before dawn with Ada shaking his shoulder and Brannoc looming over them both, and for the first time since Theo had known him, the smuggler looked afraid.

"Wake up," Brannoc said. "Riders on the north road. Sablehold colors — and they're carrying a summons." He glanced down at Theo, and his voice dropped. "For *you*. The Council wants its Oathbearer."
