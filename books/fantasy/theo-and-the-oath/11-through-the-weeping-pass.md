## 11. Through the Weeping Pass

* * *

The pass began where the map ended.

Brannoc had said it plainly that morning, tapping the worn vellum with one scarred finger: "Everything past this line is guesswork and grief. The Weeping Pass doesn't appear on charts because charts are made by people who came back." He had laughed then, the way he laughed at everything—too loudly, a little too long—and Ada had noticed his hand trembling against the table's edge.

Now she stood at the mouth of it as dusk drained the color from the world, and she understood the trembling.

The pass split the mountain like an old wound that had never learned to close. Its walls rose sheer on either side, black stone veined with something pale and glistening—not ice, not quartz, but water that moved too slowly, weeping down the rock in threads so fine they seemed less like falling than like remembering. The sound filled the gorge: a thousand small voices of dripping, overlapping, until it became not a sound at all but a hush, the way a crowded room goes quiet just before someone speaks.

Ada adjusted her pack and felt her grandmother's sigil warm against her sternum, the keeper's iron disc that had hung from gatekeeper necks for six generations. It had never warmed before the crossing. Now it did so constantly, a low fever beneath her skin, as if the metal knew something she didn't.

"Rules," Brannoc said, turning to face the eleven souls who made up what remained of their company—three Sablehold soldiers, two river-folk sisters, a mason whose town was ash, old Wenna the herbwife, Brannoc himself, and Ada and Theo. "The pass is haunted, but not in the way you're fearing. No ghosts with knives. No wailing dead. Worse than that." He let the silence stretch. "It repeats you. Every oath you ever swore and broke, every promise you let rot—it finds them and says them back to you. Out loud. In your own voice."

The mason, Hesk, shifted his tools. "That's all? Words?"

Brannoc looked at him with something close to pity. "You tell me, after. Which would you rather face—a blade, or the thing you said to your wife the night before the fires came?"

Hesk went gray. He did not answer.

Ada scanned the group until she found Theo at the rear, half-shadowed against the last light. Since the tomb—the frozen shrine where they had found his own name carved into stone above a grave dated a century before his birth—he had been quiet in a way that frightened her more than any noise could have. Not brooding. Hollowed. A man listening to a sound inside himself that only he could hear.

She crossed to him. Up close, in the blue hour, she could see the new lines around his eyes, the way his hands wouldn't quite stay still.

"You don't have to do this part," she said quietly. "If the pass speaks back—"

"It will speak to me loudest of anyone." He said it without drama, which made it worse. "I broke the biggest promise there ever was. Whatever echoes live in these rocks, they've been waiting a hundred years for me to walk through."

"Theo—"

"Ada." He almost smiled. It was a terrible, gentle smile. "There's no version of the next three days where I get to hide from what I did. Maybe that's why the road goes through here. Maybe the pass isn't a punishment. Maybe it's the only honest country left."

She wanted to argue. Instead she did the braver thing: she reached out and took his hand, cold fingers closing around colder ones, and held on.

"Then whatever it says to you," she said, "you won't hear it alone."

They stepped into the pass together, and the mountain closed behind them like a throat swallowing.

* * *

The first night, they camped in a hollow where the walls leaned apart, and the weeping was softer there—almost tender, rain-on-a-roof soft. They built no fire. Brannoc forbade it; light drew the echoes, he said, the way blood draws fish. So they sat wrapped in blankets in the dark, eating cold oats, and listened to the mountain breathe.

It began with Wenna.

The old woman had dozed off against her pack when her own voice woke her—not from her mouth, from the rock wall behind her, seeping out of the stone the way the water did:

*"I'll come back for you, love. I swear it on my mother's grave. Wait for me at the bridge."*

Wenna came upright as if struck. In the darkness Ada heard her breathing change—quick, shallow, the breathing of someone fifty years younger and running.

"That's not—" the herbwife whispered. "That's not possible. I said that in the plague year. I said it to my daughter. There were carts, they were taking the sick east, and I told her—" Her voice cracked. "I went east. I got better. By the time I came back the bridge was gone. The flood took the whole valley. She waited for me at a bridge that wasn't there."

From the wall, gently, pitilessly, her own young voice repeated: *"Wait for me at the bridge."*

"No one heard that," Wenna said wildly. "No living soul heard those words but her, and she's dust—"

"The mountain hears everything," Brannoc murmured. "Or it keeps everything. I told you. It doesn't haunt you with its dead. It haunts you with yours."

Wenna wept then, quietly, facing away from the group, and no one pretended not to hear, and no one intruded. It was the strange etiquette of the pass: your shame belonged to everyone and to no one. You could not hide it and you could not share it. The mountain did the sharing for you, and all you could do was survive being known.

Ada lay awake long after the others slept, watching the faint phosphorescence of the wet stone, listening to the water count its endless beads. And the sigil against her chest grew warmer, warmer, until at last she understood it was not warning her.

It was waiting for its turn.

* * *

* * *

Theo dreamed of fire, and woke to find the dream still standing.

Not the tomb-fire, not Veyra's burning of the riverlands—this was older, baked into him deeper than memory, a heat behind his eyes. He lay in his blanket and stared at the ceiling of black stone, and the stone stared back, and somewhere in its slow bleeding depths a voice gathered itself.

He tried to stay awake. That was the trick, he thought—if he simply refused to sleep, refused the silence, the pass would have nothing to work with. But exhaustion is a tide, and near dawn it pulled him under for just a moment, just long enough—

*"I give it willingly. I swear the covenant whole, my name upon the Wellspring, my life bound to the balance of both realms. This I swear, and nothing—no love, no fear, no bargain—shall turn me from it."*

His own voice. His older voice, the first Oathbearer's voice, ringing off the stone in cadences that belonged in temples, in chronicles, in the founding songs of free cities. The oath he had sworn at the Wellspring a hundred years before he was born.

And then the second voice, quieter, cracked, human—the same throat, the same self, later:

*"Give me the name. Give me the true name of the covenant and I'll seal it away forever. She'll live. I swear it—I swear anything—just let her live."*

Theo sat up so fast he knocked his head against the low overhang. The pain was bright and grounding. Around him the camp slept on—but Ada's eyes were open. Of course they were. She'd been awake through all of it; he could see it in her face, the careful blankness of someone trying to make their stillness a shelter for another person.

"How much did you hear?" His voice came out sanded raw.

"All of it." She didn't look away. "Both parts."

"Then you know what I am. What I traded." He laughed, and hated the sound. "A hundred million lives, weighed against one girl. And I chose. I stood at the Wellspring with the name of the covenant in my keeping and I gave it to a stranger in a mask because he promised—"

"He promised to save someone you loved."

"He lied." Theo pressed his palms against his eyes. "Or maybe he didn't. Maybe she lived a full happy life while the realms burned. That's the joke of it, Ada. Betrayal doesn't even buy what it promises. You pay everything and receive a receipt."

Ada moved closer, close enough that in the dark he could feel the warmth of her, stubborn and real. "The pass showed me something too," she said. "Last night. While you slept."

"What did it say?"

"My grandmother's voice. From when I was nine." A pause; he heard her swallow. "She caught me practicing gate-magic. Child's stuff—pushing a little wonder through the seam to make the valley flowers bloom early. Forbidden. And she grabbed my wrist hard enough to bruise and she said, *'Never cross. Never follow anyone through, no matter who they are, no matter how much you love them. Swear it.'* And I swore it." Her breath shuddered. "I've broken that oath every day since the night you arrived. And the mountain played her voice back to me saying it, and do you know what I felt?"

He shook his head.

"Relief," she whispered. "For the first time since I crossed, I felt relieved. Because the oath was a lie dressed as duty. Because some promises deserve breaking." She took his hand again, the same fierce grip from the pass mouth. "So when you say you're the man who doomed the realms—I believe you. I believe every word the mountain says about you. And I'm still here. That has to mean something."

In the wall beside them, faint as breath, the old voice stirred once more—*nothing shall turn me from it*—and Theo found, to his astonishment, that hearing it hurt less with her hand in his.

Shame, he thought, might be the loneliest thing alive. Unless someone stands inside it with you.

* * *

They climbed for two more days, and the pass took each of them in turn.

Hesk the mason was heard arguing with the stone at midnight—*"I built the door, I built it strong, I told them to run, I TOLD THEM"*—and came to breakfast hollow-eyed but walking. The river-sisters heard their father's dying request split between two walls, each daughter accused in her own voice of being the one who wasn't there at the end; they emerged from the night holding hands, having finally spoken aloud the quarrel they'd carried across half a burned country.

Even Brannoc had his hour. Ada woke to the smuggler's voice, stripped of all its laughter, coming flat and young from the rock: *"One more run, Bren. One more run pays Mother's debt and then I'm done, I swear on the salt, I'm done."* Forty years of one-more-runs, the mountain counted them all.

But it was on the third morning, in the highest throat of the pass where the walls narrowed to a shoulder's width and the weeping ran thick as tears, that the mountain saved its deepest cut—for Ada.

She was walking point when her own voice stopped her mid-stride. Not her child-voice this time. Her present voice, yesterday's voice, from the dark camp:

*"Whatever it says to you, you won't hear it alone."*

The echo repeated it, and repeated it, filling the narrow gorge with the promise, letting it bounce and multiply until a dozen Adas spoke together down the length of the pass: *alone, alone, alone.* And beneath it, woven through the dripping water like a thread of acid, came the question the mountain had been saving:

*"Will you? Will you hear it alone, keeper? When he gives up everything—his memories, his past, his very name—to seal your covenant, will you stand beside a man who no longer knows why you love him?"*

Ada froze. The company stilled behind her. Even the water seemed to lean in.

Because she hadn't spoken that thought aloud. She had barely dared think it. But the pass didn't traffic in words spoken; it trafficked in truths buried, and hers had been buried shallow.

"Yes," she said out loud, to the stone, to the echo, to herself. Her voice rang off the walls. "Yes. I will. That's the difference between an oath and a promise, you listening rock—an oath is a debt, and a promise is a choice. I choose it daily. Write that in your walls."

The water wept on. But the multiplied voices faded, one by one, as if satisfied—or as if filing her answer away for later judgment.

Behind her, Theo had heard every word. When she turned, he was looking at her with an expression she had no name for yet—something between terror and gratitude, the face of a man watching someone walk voluntarily into the fire meant for him.

"Ada," he started.

"Keep walking," she said, and her voice broke only a little. "We're nearly through. Save your strength for whatever's waiting on the other side."

* * *

What waited on the other side was worse than echoes.

They emerged at noon into blinding white light and a wind that smelled of smoke—real smoke, fresh smoke, rolling up from the southern valleys in long gray banners. Below them, spread across the foothills like spilled ink, lay a host: tents, siege engines, the black-and-crimson standards of Veyra, ten thousand strong and marching on Sablehold.

And at the fore of the column, riding a pale horse along the army's edge, a scout raised a spyglass toward the pass mouth—and found them standing in it.

"There," the echo of the mountains whispered, in Theo's own voice, one last time. *"Nothing shall turn me from it."*

Down in the valley, horns began to blow.
