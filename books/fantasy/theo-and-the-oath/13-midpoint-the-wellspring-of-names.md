## 13. Midpoint: The Wellspring of Names

The last mile, the mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain.

Ada felt it first as a wrongness in her teeth—a hum beneath the enamel, the way the Hollow Gate used to feel on storm nights back in the valley. Then the ground itself changed. The scree and frost-bitten stone of the Weeping Pass gave way to something smoother, paler, veined with threads of silver that caught the dying light like fish scales. Brannoc's mules refused it outright, planting their hooves and baring their teeth at the path ahead, and in the end they had to leave them hobbled among the pines with the smuggler's boy, Kess, muttering promises of apples into their twitching ears.

"It knows we're coming," Brannoc said. He'd gone quiet over the past two days, quieter than Ada had ever seen him, and his hands—those quick, clever hands that could pick a lock or gut a man between heartbeats—kept finding each other and wringing. "My grandmother told me about this place once. Said you didn't walk to the Wellspring. You were *received*."

"Your grandmother was a smuggler," Theo said.

"My grandmother was a liar about most things," Brannoc agreed, "which is how I know when she wasn't."

They came around the shoulder of the ridge as the sun went down behind them, and the valley of the Wellspring opened below like a held breath.

It was small—that was the first shock. Ada had imagined, without ever admitting it, something vast: a cathedral of water, a chasm ringing with voices. Instead there was only a bowl of green no wider than her grandmother's orchard, walled by cliffs on three sides, open to the west where the sky burned orange and gold. A stream fed it from somewhere above, falling in threads so fine they seemed less like water than like light deciding, at the last moment, to be liquid. And at the center, in a basin of white stone worn smooth as an old coin, lay the spring itself.

It did not move. That was the second shock, the one that made Ada's skin crawl. The water stood perfectly still—not frozen, not stagnant, but *attentive*, the way a person goes still when they are listening hard. The surface reflected the darkening sky with such precision that for a moment she couldn't tell which way was up, whether they stood above the spring or hung above some inverted heaven.

And the names. She heard them before she understood she was hearing anything: a murmur rising from the basin like heat-shimmer given voice. Not words she knew. Names, hundreds of them, thousands, spoken in every register of the human throat—the bright chirp of a mother calling a child home, the cracked gravity of a burial rite, the flat recitation of a tax ledger. Each one sounded exactly once and then folded back into the whole, a river made of being called.

"The covenant was written here," Theo said beside her. His voice came out strange, flattened, as though he were reading from a page he couldn't see. "Every true name of every oath-sworn soul, spoken into the water and bound. When someone swore to keep the balance, the spring held their name so the oath couldn't be broken by forgetting. Or by lying."

"You remember that?"

"No." He pressed his knuckles against his sternum, against the place where the memories lived now, surfacing more often, arriving less gently. "It remembers itself through me. There's a difference. There's a difference," he said again, softer, as if trying to convince the words themselves.

Kess made a small sound and sat down abruptly on a rock, hands over his ears. "It's too loud," he whispered. "How is it loud and quiet at the same time?"

Brannoc crouched beside the boy, murmuring something Ada didn't catch, but his eyes came up to hers over the boy's head, and what she saw in them frightened her more than Veyra's armies had: hope. Raw, unguarded, ruinous hope, aimed at her and Theo like an arrow already loosed.

She looked away and made herself study the spring instead. Study, measure, catalog—the gatekeeper's discipline, the one thing her grandmother had drilled into her that still felt like her own. The basin was ringed by twelve stones, each taller than a man, each carved with script so ancient the letters had half-sunken back into the rock, the way names sink back into silence. Eleven of the stones were dark. One, at the western edge facing the open sky, still held a faint ember-glow deep in its grooves, like coals banked under ash.

"That one," she said. "That one's still alive."

"The Oathbearer's stone." Theo had drifted forward without seeming to decide to, his boots whispering on the silver-veined ground. He stopped at the basin's rim and looked down into the still water, and his reflection looked back up at him—and then, Ada would swear until her dying day, kept looking after he blinked. "I'm supposed to speak here. I can feel the shape of it. Like a word stuck behind my teeth that's too big to come out."

"Then say it," Brannoc said, rising. "Say the name, reforge the oath, and we get to go home. All of us." His voice cracked on *home*, just slightly, and Ada watched him hate himself for it.

Theo knelt at the rim of the basin. The murmuring of the names swelled—or perhaps it only seemed to, the way a room seems to lean in. He cupped his hands. Dipped them toward the water.

And stopped.

Ada saw it happen: the exact moment the word reached his teeth and found nothing behind it. His face didn't crumple—it did something worse, something she'd seen once on the face of a man who'd walked to his own front door and forgotten why he'd come. It went *blank*. Polite. Lost.

"I don't know it," he said.

"What do you mean, you don't know it?" Brannoc's hope curdled visibly, souring into anger because anger was easier. "You're the Oathbearer. It's your name. People don't not know their own—"

"I gave it away." Theo's hands were still cupped, still empty, trembling above the water that did not ripple. "A hundred years ago. In the tomb, the echo showed me—I traded it. To save one person. The price of the bargain was my true name, and the bargain took it, and everything since then—" He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "Everything since then has been me living on credit. Borrowing a self. Theo Thackeray, archivist, first date, quiet man who likes old books. None of it was built on anything. The foundation's been hollow the whole time, and now I've finally walked far enough to look down into it."

The names murmured on. Somewhere in that patient chorus, Ada thought, was her own true name, held since before her birth, waiting for an oath she hadn't sworn yet. She had spent her whole life guarding other people's names. It had never once occurred to her that a person could lose their own.

"There has to be another way," she said. "There's always—my grandmother kept records. Failures, workarounds. Gatekeepers have been patching this covenant for a century—"

"And look how well that went." Brannoc kicked a pebble into the basin; the water swallowed it without a ripple, without a splash, without any acknowledgment whatsoever, and Kess whimpered. "Sorry. Sorry. I'm not angry at you." He dragged both hands down his face. "I'm angry at a hundred years of dead people who let it come to this."

"The name isn't lost," Theo said slowly. He was staring into the water, and the water stared back, and when he spoke again his voice had that doubled quality it got when the other life pressed close. "Lost things can't be found. But given things can be *returned*. It was surrendered freely—that's what gave it power. Which means..." He trailed off. Ada watched him reach for the thought and flinch away from it, the way you flinch from a stove you've burned yourself on.

"Means what?" Brannoc demanded.

"Means nothing tonight." Ada stepped between them, gatekeeper's voice, the tone that stopped arguments at her threshold. "We've walked eleven days. Kess hasn't slept warm since the pass. Nobody makes bargains with a listening spring on an empty stomach. We camp above the tree line, we eat, and at dawn we think clearly."

Nobody argued. That, more than anything, told her how exhausted they all were.

* * *

They camped on the western lip of the valley, where the last light lingered longest. Brannoc rigged a shelter for Kess from oilcloth and rope with a speed that suggested long practice at making small spaces survivable, and the boy was asleep almost before the knots were tied, exhaustion finally outrunning fear.

Ada took first watch. She sat with her back against a boulder, the valley spread below her, the spring a pale coin in the blue dusk. The names had softened as darkness fell—no, not softened. *Deepened.* The night-names were different: lullabies, deathbed whispers, confessions. Once, unmistakably, she heard a woman's voice say *Ada* in her grandmother's cadence, and her whole body turned to ice before reason caught up. The spring held names that had been spoken into oaths. Her grandmother had sworn hers here? Had come here, walked this same ground—

No. Impossible. Grandmother had never left the valley. Had she?

*Her lineage exists to watch for his return.* The cartographer's daughter. Generations of gatekeepers' failures hidden away. She had thought she'd finished grieving that revelation, back in Sablehold, but grief apparently kept reserves, and it ambushed her now in the dark with fresh force: her whole life, every choice dressed up as choice, had been a watch-post. Even this journey—even love, if that's what this was becoming—had ancestors.

Behind her, gravel shifted. Theo came and sat a careful arm's length away, close enough for warmth, far enough for deniability. For a while neither of them spoke. Below, the spring glimmered, holding ten thousand souls in suspension.

"When I was eight," Theo said at last, "I lost a tooth. My mother told me to throw it in the river so the new tooth would grow straight. And I wouldn't do it. I kept it in a tin for a year because I was afraid that if I let it go, I'd be giving away a piece of myself I couldn't get back." He laughed softly. "Eight years old, and already I knew something was missing. Some instinct of the debt."

"Do you think it can be gotten back?" Ada asked. "The name. Honestly."

He was quiet long enough that she turned to look at him. In the starlight his face was young and old at once, the archivist and the Betrayer layered like pages pressed together.

"Honestly? I think there's a version where I find it, and it's the worst thing that ever happens to me. Because the man who gave it away—he did it to save someone he loved. And to take the name back, I'd have to become him completely. All of him. Including the part that chose one life over millions." His jaw worked. "And I think there's a version where I can't find it, and Veyra wins, and everyone in every refugee caravan from here to the coast pays for my hundred-year-old bargain. So." He spread his hands. "Honestly."

"You could lie to me, you know," Ada said. "Most people would. Most people, kneeling at a magic spring with the world ending, would say something brave and vague."

"Would you believe me?"

"I'd want to." She pulled her cloak tighter. "That's worse, somehow."

Below them, the spring's murmuring changed key. Ada heard it before Theo did—a new thread entering the chorus, thin and wrong, a name being spoken somewhere far off with force and hatred, a name being *used*. The water's surface shivered, the first movement it had made, and the reflection of the stars fractured into rings.

Theo sat up sharply. "What is that? What am I hearing?"

"Someone swearing," Ada said slowly, gatekeeper's instinct rising like water in her chest. "Or unswearing. Someone's speaking a true name into power, and this place hears it." She was on her feet before she finished the sentence, every lesson of her childhood firing at once. "That's what the covenant was *for*. This spring doesn't just hold names. It holds the balance between them. If someone's bending a name hard enough to reach here—"

The rings on the water widened. From the depths of the basin, beneath ten thousand murmured names, something answered the far-off speaker—one low pulse, like a great heart turning over in its sleep. The eleven dark stones flickered. The twelfth, the Oathbearer's stone, flared bright enough to throw their shadows long across the grass.

And in the light of it, Ada saw the water clearly for the first time: not empty, not merely reflective. Shapes moved beneath the surface. Figures, faint as breath on glass, standing in ranks upon ranks, faceless, patient, each one a held name, each one waiting for the word that would call it back into the world.

One figure, nearest the surface, nearest the rim where Theo had knelt, lifted its head.

Though it had no face, Ada knew—with the absolute, sick certainty of the gatekeeper's blood—that it was looking at Theo. That it had been waiting a hundred years. That it knew the way home.

"Theo," she said quietly. "I don't think your name wants to stay lost."

Down in the valley, the spring began, very softly, to sing his name.
