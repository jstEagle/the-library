## 14. Veyra's Version

They took his hands first.

Not with violence—worse, with courtesy. Two soldiers in ash-grey lacquered armor bound Theo's wrists in silk cord dyed the color of dried blood, and they did it gently, the way one handles a relic that might still be holy. Ada fought. He heard her before he saw her, a sound like a struck bell tearing through the rain, and then he didn't hear her at all, and the not-hearing was the loudest thing that had ever happened to him.

*She's alive,* he told himself as they hooded him. *If they wanted her dead, she'd be dead.*

The march lasted three days, or perhaps four; time went strange under the hood, measured only by the ache in his shoulders and the smell of the world passing—wet stone, woodsmoke, pine resin, and beneath it all a faint sweetness like fruit rotting in a cellar, which he came to understand was Veyra's magic itself, perfuming her army's road. The soldiers spoke around him in low voices, never to him. Once, a young one stumbled against him in the dark and whispered, so quietly it might have been a prayer: *"Forgive us, Oathbearer."* Then boots hurried away, and no one else spoke of it.

On the last night the ground changed underfoot. Gravel gave way to something smoother, older, fitted stone worn into shallow rivers by centuries of feet. The air warmed. The sweet rot-smell thickened until he could taste it on his tongue, honeyed and wrong. And through the hood he began to see light—not because the cloth thinned, but because the light did not care about cloth. It came through wool and skin and bone alike, a deep amber glow, as though he were being carried into the heart of a lamp.

The hood came off in a hall of black glass.

Theo had imagined a throne room from the stories the refugees told: skulls, banners of flayed covenant-sigils, Veyra enthroned on the bones of the old order. What he found instead was quiet. The walls rose in seamless obsidian veined with gold, and the veins moved—slowly, patiently, like sap rising in trees—and the ceiling was open to a sky he did not recognize, stars arranged in constellations that belonged to no map of any realm he'd walked. A long table stood at the center, set for two. Candles burned without flickering. Steam curled from two cups.

Veyra sat with her back to him, watching the impossible stars.

She was smaller than he had imagined. That was the first betrayal of expectation—the usurper whose name emptied villages was a narrow-shouldered woman in plain grey wool, hair cropped short and going silver early, hands folded on the table like a scholar between chapters. When she turned, her face was ordinary too: tired around the eyes, a scar bisecting her left eyebrow, the kind of face you'd pass in a market and forget by the next stall.

Except for the eyes. They were amber, lit from within, the same amber as the veins in the walls, and looking into them was like looking down a well and seeing your own reflection very far below, waving.

"The archivist," she said. Her voice was low and unhurried, an instrument tuned to a frequency just below comfort. "Or should I say the other thing? Sit, please. The tea is from the southern terraces. It's the last honest crop in either realm."

"I don't want your tea."

"No." She nodded as if he'd said something wise. "You want your friend back, your freedom, and your innocence. I can offer none of those. So we will begin with what I can offer." She gestured to the chair opposite hers. "The truth. You've been fed a story since you fell through your gate, Theo of the valley. I would like to tell you mine. Not to win you—I'm told the Oathbearer can't be won, only chosen. But because you are about to make a decision that will outlive both of us, and I refuse to let you make it believing a lie."

His legs decided before his mind did. He sat. The tea steamed between them, smelling of cardamom and smoke.

"You killed the river towns," he said.

"Yes."

"You're marching on Sablehold."

"Yes."

"Children died at Ashford Crossing. I carried one of them. She weighed nothing."

Something moved behind the amber—grief, or its well-preserved corpse. "I know," Veyra said. "Her name was Mirei. She liked to catch frogs by the levee. Her mother worked the ferry." She said it flatly, without performance, and somehow that was worse than any gloating. "You want me to be a monster. Monsters are easy. Monsters let you sleep. I am going to take that from you tonight, and I am sorry for it, because I know exactly what it costs. I paid it myself, a hundred years ago, when someone took my monsters away and left me a person."

* * *

She told it plainly, without music, and that plainness was the most terrible eloquence Theo had ever heard.

"The covenant you're walking to the Wellspring to reforge," she began, "was written by nine names and sealed with a tenth. Your name—or the name of the man who wore your face before you. Do you know what the covenant actually *did*, Theo? Not the songs' version. The songs say it balanced the realms, kept magic from bleeding into the mundane valley, kept war from the borderlands. All true. Songs always tell you the *what*. Never the *how*."

She turned her cup slowly on the table, once, twice.

"Balance requires a fulcrum. Every scale does. The old covenant made its fulcrum out of people. Wards, they called them—living anchors, bound at birth to the covenant's roots, their true names written into the oath so their lives could hold the seal shut. There were thirteen of us. We could not leave our stations. We could not age past our binding. We could not die, even when we begged to, because our deaths would crack the seal, and so the covenant forbade us death the way it forbade the valley magic. We were the price of peace. Peace is never free, Theo. Someone is always the coin."

The candle flames leaned toward her, listening.

"I was the youngest ward. Bound at six years old, when the covenant's readers found my name fit the ninth root. I spent eighty years in a chamber beneath the Wellspring, watching the water remember things. I watched three of my siblings go mad and be replaced by children. I watched the fourth beg the first Oathbearer—you—to unbind her, and watch him refuse, because the seal needed all thirteen and mercy was arithmetic he couldn't afford." Her voice did not rise. It never rose. "And then the fifth ward was a girl named Sefa, who was dying anyway, slowly, the binding eating her from inside, and she asked you for the only kindness left. She asked you to give her name away. To trade it out of the covenant so she could die."

Theo's mouth had gone dry. "I don't—"

"You traded it," Veyra said. "You traded her name to save her. One act of mercy. And the covenant, missing its name, began to starve—and a starving covenant feeds on whatever is nearest. It ate the wards next. It ate me last, or tried to. I was in the roots when it turned, Theo. I felt it decide that people were cheaper than balance. I felt it reach up through the earth toward the free cities and start to drink." She lifted her eyes to his. "Your betrayal wasn't greed. It wasn't ambition. It was love, misapplied, and it doomed millions. Mine was the same. I broke the covenant to stop it from eating anyone else. The difference is that history will call me monster and call you savior, and I have had a hundred years to make my peace with that arithmetic."

The tea had gone cold between his hands. He hadn't touched it.

"Why show me this?" His voice came out smaller than he intended. "Why capture us at all? If you know what I'm walking toward—if you know the Wellspring could reforge everything—why not kill me on the road?"

"Because reforging the old covenant means rebuilding the wards." For the first time, heat entered her voice—not anger, but something older and more exhausted. "Thirteen children, Theo. Thirteen lives nailed into the roots of the world so everyone else can pretend the peace is free. Your Council of Nine knows. Your keeper's line knows—it's why they watch the gate, generation after generation, keeping the seam shut so no one ever has to look at the cost. Everyone who tells you to restore the covenant is telling you to feed it again. I have simply decided to stop asking politely."

"And the river towns? Ashford? Mirei?" He heard his own voice crack on the child's name. "Was that stopping politely?"

Veyra closed her eyes. When she opened them, the amber had dimmed to something almost human.

"The river towns held the covenant's secondary anchors. Old binding-stones under the levees, still live, still drawing. I burned them out." She said it the way a surgeon describes amputation. "It was not clean. War is never clean, and I have stopped pretending I can make it so. I will carry Mirei's name until I end, alongside the others. Add it to the ledger if it helps you hate me. Hate keeps people upright. I've used it myself."

* * *

He walked the halls afterward because they let him—because there was nowhere to run, and perhaps because she wanted him to see.

The fortress was not what the refugees described. There were no trophies, no screaming dungeons. There were wards—former wards—people who had followed her out of the covenant's roots when she shattered it, moving through the corridors with the slow, wondering gait of the long-imprisoned. An old man pressed his palm to a window, feeling weather for the first time in ninety years, and wept without sound. A girl of maybe fifteen practiced writing her own name over and over on scrap paper, because until last season it had belonged to the covenant, and she wanted to be sure it stayed hers.

"They aren't prisoners," said a voice behind him. Veyra, again, hands folded, shadowed by no guard. "None of them may leave if they wish to stay—but none wish to leave. Can you guess why?"

Theo looked at the girl with her paper and her pen and her name, and hated how much he understood.

"Because outside, they're fuel," he said.

"Because outside, they're fuel," she agreed. "You've seen the free cities, Oathbearer. Good people. Brave people. People worth saving. And every one of them, from the Council of Nine down to the fishwives of Sablehold, has built their safety on a foundation of thirteen buried children, and none of them know, and none of them *want* to know, because knowing would mean the peace was never innocent, and innocence is the one luxury no realm can survive losing." She stepped beside him at the window. Below, her army drilled in the rain, grey ranks moving like water finding its level. "So here is my version, Theo, and then I'll let you sleep. You are going to the Wellspring to reforge an oath. Ask yourself, before you speak your name into that water: *whose children will hold it shut this time?* Ask yourself whether Ada Okonkwo knows what her grandmother's silence purchased. Ask yourself whether there is any version of the covenant that doesn't require someone at the bottom of the scale—and if there isn't, ask whether you are a savior or simply the covenant's next kindly jailer."

She left him there. The old man at the window was still touching the glass. The girl was still writing her name, filling page after page, as if she feared it might be taken back.

Theo stood a long time in the amber light, and the ground beneath his certainty shifted like riverbank in flood. He thought of Ada's fury in the tomb, of Brannoc's grave under the waystation stones, of Mirei weighing nothing in his arms. He thought of Sefa, dying, asking for mercy, and of the man with his face granting it and damning the world—and of Veyra, six years old, being written into the roots while the realms above slept safe and sang.

Both stories could not be true. Both stories were.

Near dawn—for dawn came strangely in that starless hall—a soldier brought him bread and water and, with it, a single sheet of paper. On it, in Veyra's small precise hand:

*The Wellspring will accept any name spoken truly. Choose yours carefully. Some names are cages. — V.*

And beneath it, as if she could not help herself, as if a hundred years of silence had taught her nothing about restraint:

*P.S. Your keeper lives. She is being kept comfortably, and she is angrier than you are. I mention this only because I suspect you'll do something foolish otherwise, and foolish men die before they finish choosing.*

Theo read it until the words blurred. Then he folded the paper into his coat, over his heart, where the ring from the tomb lay cold against his chest—and far to the north, through stone and rain and the sleeping weight of armies, he felt the Hollow Gate stir like something turning over in its sleep, and knew that whatever he chose at the Wellspring, he would not choose it alone.
