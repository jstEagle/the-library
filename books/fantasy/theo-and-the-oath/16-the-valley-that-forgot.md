## 16. The Valley That Forgot

The Hollow Gate did not want to let her go home.

Ada felt it the moment she laid her palm against the seam—the stone's reluctance, a slow pulse beneath her hand like a heart deciding whether to beat. For eleven years she had opened and closed this gate without ceremony, her grandmother's sigil warm on her wrist, and never once had the gate argued with her. Now it pressed back against her touch like a door swollen in its frame.

"You know me," she whispered to it. "You've always known me."

But the gate had been broken open once already—by Theo's arrival, by Veyra's war, by everything the old covenant had held shut—and perhaps a gate that had been forced could no longer be gentle. Perhaps it simply did not recognize her anymore, now that she carried the other world's dust on her boots and Brannoc's death in her chest like a stone swallowed whole.

She pushed anyway. The seam parted with a sound like tearing linen, and the valley breathed out at her: cut hay, woodsmoke from the far farms, the mineral cold of the river at dusk. Home. It should have undone her. Instead she stood on the threshold with her hand still raised, feeling how thin the air was here, how quiet, as though she had stepped into a photograph of the place rather than the place itself.

Behind her, Theo slept in the shrine's shadow, exhausted past dreaming by the echo of his own confession. She had not woken him to say goodbye. She wasn't sure goodbye was what it would be. *I'm coming back,* she had told him instead, an hour ago, kneeling beside him while the tomb's ring still hummed faintly overhead. He had nodded without opening his eyes, and his hand had found hers briefly, and neither of them said anything about how long *coming back* might take, or whether the woman who returned would be entirely the woman who left.

She stepped through. The gate sealed behind her with a click so soft it might have been imagined.

* * *

The path down from the hollow was wrong in small ways first.

A fence post she remembered leaning against as a girl—splintered at shoulder height where lightning had struck it the summer she turned nine—stood smooth and unmarked. She stopped, touched the clean wood, and felt the first cold thread of dread slide under her ribs. Lightning scars didn't heal. Wood remembered.

Farther down, the orchard on Harrow's hill was planted with plums instead of apples. She stood among the young trees for a full minute, certain and uncertain at once, because surely she had picked apples here every October, had filled baskets until her arms ached—but the ground said plums, the spacing of the rows said plums, the pale blossoms gone over said plums, and memory, she realized with a lurch, was only a story she'd been telling herself, and stories could be contradicted by anything that stood still long enough to argue.

By the time she reached the village proper, the sun had dropped behind the western ridge and the windows were lighting up yellow one by one, and Ada Okonkwo walked down the high street of her own home like a stranger casing it.

Mrs. Ellery was sweeping her step. Mrs. Ellery, who had taught her to braid sweetgrass, who kept peppermints in a tin shaped like a fish, who had cried at Ada's mother's funeral and slipped a five-pound note into Ada's coat pocket at the reception because grief, in the valley, was something you fed people along with the sandwiches.

"Evening," Ada said.

Mrs. Ellery looked up. Blinked. Her gaze slid off Ada's face the way rain slides off oiled canvas—not rudeness, not fear, just a kind of polite failure to land—and then went back to her broom.

"Evening," she agreed, to no one in particular, and swept.

Ada stood there until her throat hurt. Then she crossed the street to the shop, where Dev Achari restocked shelves until ten most nights, and pulled the door open, and the bell above it rang exactly the way it had rung all her life, bright and tinny and familiar, and Dev glanced up from a crate of tinned tomatoes and looked directly through her.

"Can I help—" he began, and lost the sentence halfway, frowning at the space where she stood as though the question had been addressed to someone who'd just stepped out.

Ada bought nothing. There was nothing anyone could sell her.

She understood then, standing in the doorway with the bell still swaying, what the crossing had cost. Not the gate's price—she had paid that knowingly, or thought she had—but the valley's. Every hour she had spent on the other side, the valley had been quietly letting go of her. Names were how this place held things; she had always known that, had grown up on her grandmother's lessons about names and seams and the weight of remembering. And the valley was forgetting her name. Not all at once—that would have been mercy, clean and comprehensible—but in pieces, the way frost takes a window. First the edges. Then the middle. Mrs. Ellery's eyes sliding off her face. Dev's unfinished question. The plum trees standing where her apple trees ought to be, as if even the land were redrafting its records and finding no entry for her.

She was homesick, she realized, for a home that was forgetting her while she stood inside it. It was the loneliest feeling she had ever known, and she had once sat alone at the Hollow Gate through an entire winter of storms.

Her grandmother's house was at the end of Mill Lane, where the lane gave up on cobbles and became dirt, and she made herself walk there slowly, cataloguing everything, memorizing deliberately, the way Theo must have memorized manuscripts before the archive took him in—because if the valley was going to erase her, she intended to keep a copy. The crooked weathervane on the chapel. The smell of yeast from the bakery's evening batch. Three cats on the wall outside the schoolhouse, arranged like notes on a stave. The exact blue of dusk between the ridges, which no other sky in any world had ever quite managed.

*Remember me,* she told the valley silently, walking. *I lived here. I swept this lane. I kissed a boy behind the chapel when I was fifteen and regretted it immediately. I kept your gate.*

The valley said nothing. But it also, she noticed, walking on, did not entirely refuse her. The cats watched her go. One of them blinked, slow and deliberate—a cat's courtesy, which is nearly indistinguishable from love.

* * *

The house was locked, but locks had never meant much to a keeper. She came around the back where the kitchen window stuck, lifted it the way she had since childhood, and slid through into a room that hit her like a fist made entirely of tenderness.

Everything was exactly as her grandmother had left it. The kettle on the hob, black-bottomed from decades of use. The dish towel folded over the chair back. The jar of barley on the third shelf, label gone soft with age. Her grandmother had been dead two years and the room still smelled faintly of her—camphor and strong tea and the particular dry warmth of old wool—and Ada stood just inside the window and let it happen to her, the grief rising fresh as if no time at all had passed, as if she could turn around and find the old woman at the table with her spectacles pushed up into gray curls, saying, *Well? Are you going to stand there letting the night in, or are you going to put the kettle on?*

"I need your papers, Gran," Ada said aloud, because the silence needed breaking and because talking to dead women in empty kitchens was apparently something she did now. "The ones you hid. The ones about him."

Because chapter by chapter, mile by mile, the truth had assembled itself on the road: that the Okonkwo line had not merely kept the gate. They had waited. Generations of grandmothers, each teaching the next, each watching the valley road for a young man with ink-stained fingers and an archivist's stoop, each dying without him ever coming, and passing the vigil on like a candle flame cupped between palms. *The Oathbearer will return*, the prophecy book said, and Ada's family had built four hundred years of quiet, patient lives around that single sentence. Her grandmother hadn't been guarding a gate. She'd been guarding a hope—or a threat—and had died without telling Ada which.

There had to be records. There were always records; her grandmother wrote down everything, receipts and weather and the migratory habits of herons, in a slanted hand that leaned into the future like a person walking into wind.

The front rooms gave up nothing. Ada searched the way she'd been trained to search—for absence rather than presence, for the gap in a pattern. Books shelved by height except one gap on the study shelf, precisely book-width, dustless. A floorboard in the parlor that sounded different underfoot, though it yielded only beetles and old coin. It was in the bedroom, finally, behind the panel above the wardrobe—her grandmother's wardrobe, dark oak, smelling of cedar—that her fingers found the seam.

The cache was shallow but deep enough: a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the same sweetgrass braid Mrs. Ellery had taught her to make, as if even concealment in that house had been an act of continuity. Inside lay three ledgers, a packet of letters bound in ribbon gone brittle, and a single sheet of heavy paper covered in her grandmother's handwriting, dated the week before her death, as though she had known, as the old always seem to know, that time was running short.

Ada sat on the floor with her back against the wardrobe and read by the light of a candle she lit with hands that were not quite steady.

*If you are reading this, then either you are Ada and I am dead, or you are someone Ada sent, in which case tell her she has terrible taste in messengers.* Despite everything, Ada laughed—a wet, startled sound in the empty house. *I will write plainly, because plainness was never my gift and I haven't time to learn it now.*

*We do not keep the gate, child. We keep the account. Four hundred years ago the first Oathbearer broke the covenant, and the covenant could not be mended by force, only by return. So we watched the valley road. My grandmother watched it, and hers before her. We fed travelers. We mended fences for strangers. We learned to love the ordinary world ferociously, because that was our half of the bargain: to make the mundane side worth returning to. And every generation we wrote down what we knew of him, so that when he came, someone would know his face.*

*He came. You found him. I am sorry I did not live to see it, and sorrier for what it will cost you, because I know the cost. Read the ledgers. Count the names of the women who crossed and did not come wholly back. The valley forgets its daughters, Ada. That is the toll. Magic cannot cross, but memory can leak, and the gate takes its fee from both sides of the ledger. Your neighbors will lose you piece by piece. The land will revise itself around your absence-in-presence. By the time you finish what you've begun, you may walk these lanes as a ghost walks a house she built.*

*But hear me. Hear the one thing I never told you, because I was a coward and loved you too much to burden you early.* The handwriting tightened here, the pen pressing hard enough to emboss the page. *The forgetting is not only a toll. It is a door. What the valley releases, the Wellspring receives. Every memory they surrender of you becomes material—becomes name-substance—on the other side. Our family's exile is the reason the reforging is possible at all. We are not victims of the covenant, my darling. We are its collateral, and its currency, and its keepers. All three at once. I chose that for you before you were born, and I will burn in whatever hell accounts for such choices, and I would make the same choice again tomorrow, because someone must, and because I watched you as a baby and saw that you were stubborn enough to survive it.*

*Go back to him. Finish it. And when the valley has forgotten you entirely, remember this: forgotten is not erased. The gate opens from your side alone now—I felt it change the year before I died, and I did not understand it, and now perhaps you do. Whatever you become, you will be the one who decides who crosses. No prophecy owns that. No book owns that. Only you.*

*Put the kettle on before you leave. Houses notice.*

Ada read it twice. On the second reading she did not laugh at the joke, and by the end she had her grandmother's letter pressed flat against her sternum with both hands, sitting on the floor of the darkening room, crying the way she had not cried at the funeral—ugly, doubled-over, unkeeperly crying, grief and fury braided together so tightly she couldn't tell which was which. Four hundred years. Four hundred years of women feeding travelers and mending fences and loving the ordinary world *ferociously* as payment for a debt none of them owed, and her grandmother had known—had known the valley would eat her granddaughter alive, had written it down in her last week of life and hidden it above the wardrobe rather than say it aloud across a kitchen table, because love, in the Okonkwo family, was something you filed away for the person who came after.

She cried until the candle guttered, and then she wiped her face on the dish towel from the kitchen—her grandmother's dish towel—and put the kettle on.

* * *

While the water heated, she read the ledgers.

They were worse than the letter. Page after page of her foremothers' hands: *Crossed in the spring. Returned in autumn. Mother did not know her; called her "the visitor." She stayed anyway.* Another: *Marta crossed for the second time in '61. The village remembers Marta only as "the one who drowned," though she lives yet beyond the gate. We have agreed to let the drowning stand. It is kinder than the truth.* Another, in a hand shaky with age: *I have begun to forget myself, which the others warned me of. I keep this ledger to remind the page, since the page cannot remind me.* And in the margin beside that entry, in fresher ink, her grandmother's addition: *This is why we teach the next one early. Memory must be handed off before it fails.*

Ada understood, turning the pages by firelight, why the valley forgot. It was arithmetic, horrible and elegant. The covenant ran on names; the Wellspring fed on what was surrendered; and every keeper who crossed had paid in the only coin the other side truly valued. Her family had been spending themselves, generation by generation, to keep the account solvent. To buy, in the end, this exact moment: an Oathbearer on the road to the Wellspring, and a keeper who could open the gate from her side alone.

She was not the victim of a prophecy. She was the interest on a four-hundred-year investment, come due.

And God help her, sitting in the kitchen where she'd learned to bake and bleed and braid sweetgrass, drinking tea in her grandmother's chipped brown mug while the house she grew up in failed to notice her—the kettle's whistle hadn't startled anyone because there was no one left inside to startle—God help her, she was not sorry. That was the thing that frightened her most. Underneath the grief, underneath the fury, some stubborn root of her was *relieved*. Relieved to know the shape of the cost. Relieved that it could be named, because Ada Okonkwo had spent her whole life guarding a boundary she'd never been allowed to understand, and understanding, even terrible understanding, felt like being handed back a piece of herself.

Outside, the valley slept. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it. The ridge lines held their old shapes against the stars, patient as ledgers, and Ada looked at them and memorized them again, deliberately, furiously, knowing it wouldn't hold—knowing that tomorrow the orchard might grow walnuts, and Mrs. Ellery's step might sweep itself, and the chapel weathervane might point a direction that had never existed.

"Keep what you can of me," she told the valley softly, at the door, mug in hand. "And I'll keep what I can of you. Fair trade."

The valley, characteristically, did not answer. But as she climbed the hill toward the hollow with the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth against her chest and her grandmother's letter tucked inside her coat, she passed the three cats on the schoolhouse wall—still there, impossibly, in the dark—and all three of them watched her go, and one of them stood, and stretched, and followed her to the edge of the lane before stopping, tail curled, like a sentinel relieved of duty.

Small mercies. Brannoc would have called them omens, and charged her for the reading.

At the top of the hollow, the Hollow Gate waited in the starlight, and beside it, wrapped in her spare cloak, Theo stirred in his sleep as she approached—turned toward her before his eyes opened, the way a compass needle finds north, and said, hoarse and half-dreaming:

"You found it. What they hid."

"Yes," Ada said, kneeling, laying the oilcloth bundle between them like something sworn on. "And I found something else." She looked back once, down the hill, at the valley that was learning to live without her while she still breathed its air. "The valley forgets its daughters, Theo. But it seems the gate doesn't forget its keeper."

Beneath her palm, warm as a waking pulse, the stone had begun—very softly—to sing.
