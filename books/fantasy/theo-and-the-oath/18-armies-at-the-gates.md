## 18. Armies at the Gates

The drums began at dawn, and by midmorning Sablehold's towers were weeping ash.

Ada stood on the eastern rampart and watched the smoke rise from the outlying farms—slow black columns that leaned east in the wind like a row of mourners bowing toward the city. Beyond them, across the grey sweep of the Sorrel Flats, Veyra's army had made its camp: a city of tents so vast it seemed the land itself had grown a second skin, dark and scaled and patient. Banners of iron-grey hung motionless over it. Even the wind seemed afraid to touch them.

"They came faster than the Council said," Brannoc would have said. But Brannoc was three weeks dead beneath a collapsed waystation in the Weeping Pass, and Ada still caught herself turning to tell him things.

Theo stood beside her at the wall, his hands resting on the stone as though he could feel the army through it. He had been doing that more lately—touching things, listening to them, as if some buried sense were waking in him one finger at a time. The sigil-ring from the First Oathbearer's tomb hung on a cord around his neck. Ada had seen him holding it at night without putting it on, the way a man holds a key to a door he dreads opening.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"Brannoc's cousin counts forty thousand." She didn't soften it. They had stopped softening things somewhere on the road, somewhere between the pass and the river towns, between the child Theo had pulled from burning water and the grave they'd left behind them. "And that's not counting what's in the sky."

Above the enemy camp, something circled. At this distance it might have been birds. It was not birds. The shapes wheeled in patterns no flock followed, and when the light caught them they flashed like wet slate. Wardens, the refugees called them. The covenant's sacrificed ward—the phrase kept surfacing in Theo's memory, half-drowned—and Veyra had learned to make soldiers of the drowned.

Below them, the streets of Sablehold churned. The city held perhaps sixty thousand souls, swollen now to twice that with refugees from the northern holds, and every one of them seemed to be moving at once: carters hauling stone to the walls, children passing buckets in chains up the watchtowers, priests of nine different temples ringing bells that did not agree with each other. The Council of Nine had finally stopped arguing about whether the war was real. That was the only thing they had stopped arguing about.

"The envoys arrive today," Theo said.

"I know."

"From all six free cities."

"I know, Theo." She looked at him then—at the hollows under his eyes, at the way his jaw was set like a man biting down on something that wanted to escape. "You don't have to have an answer ready. You know that, don't you? You're not required to be everyone's answer."

He smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen on him—not because it was bitter, but because it was gentle. "Ada. I gave away my true name once already. Do you know what that bought?" He touched the ring through his shirt. "A hundred years of silence, an army at the gate, and a world where a woman who was fed to the old covenant decided the only fair thing was to feed it everyone else. Whatever I say to those envoys today, it will either save us or damn us, and I won't get to choose which by staying quiet."

The drums rolled again, long and low, felt in the stones under their feet more than heard.

* * *

They received the envoys in the Hall of Nine Lanterns, where the great round table of Sablehold's council sat beneath lamps said to burn with light older than the covenant itself. The lamps burned thin and blue today. Ada had never seen them anything but gold before, and neither, judging by their faces, had any of the councilors. The whole room kept glancing upward, the way people do in a house when they suspect the foundations have shifted.

Six cities. Six delegations, travel-worn and desperate, arriving within hours of each other as though summoned—which, in a way, they had been, by the same news carried on the same winds: *the Oathbearer lives, the Oathbearer walks, the Oathbearer has reached Sablehold.*

From Meridian-by-the-Sea: a silver-haired admiral whose fleet was already burning in her harbor. From Kestrelmark: a young duke with his arm in a sling and his father's signet on his thumb because his father's hand had not survived the retreat from the northern holds. From the salt-canyon cities of the south, two merchant-princes who kept looking at each other as though deciding which of them would do the begging. From the highlands, a woman in wolf-fur who introduced herself simply as Speaker for the Clans and did not sit. And last, alone and unannounced, an old man in a plain brown robe who claimed to carry word from the Temple of the Unwritten, far beyond the eastern desert—a temple most of the council had believed was legend.

Theo stood at the center of the floor, and Ada watched him bear it.

She had imagined, in the nights since the Wellspring, a hundred versions of how this day might go. In none of them had she anticipated this: the sheer physical weight of six cities' hope pressing down on one tired archivist who could not remember being anyone else. Because that was the cruelty of it—these people needed the first Oathbearer, the legendary one, the name in the prophecy book. What they had gotten was a man who read too much and slept badly and flinched at the sound of certain bells, carrying a dead man's memories like stones in his pockets.

"Lady Veyra's vanguard reaches your outer fields tomorrow," the admiral said. Her voice was steady the way a rope is steady—taut because it is about to break. "Her main force the day after. The Wardens fly ahead of everything. Your walls are strong, Lord Oathbearer, but walls have never stopped her. She doesn't take cities. She unmakes them. Corveth had eleven thousand people. Corveth has a lake now."

"We ask you to fight," the young duke said bluntly. "Whatever power the old oath gives you—take it. Take all of it. We'll pay any price—"

"You don't know what you're offering," Theo said, and the room went quiet, because his voice had changed—had deepened into something with a hundred years of dust in it, and then he blinked and it was his own voice again, frightened and ordinary. "Forgive me. What I mean is—I've seen the ledger. There is no version of taking the old power that doesn't cost more than it buys. I've paid that bill before. So has she."

"Then what?" It was the merchant-prince from Saltcress, and there was no diplomacy left in him at all. "You stand there, you wear the ring, you carry the name, and you tell us there's nothing? Forty thousand soldiers are going to eat my city, and the answer is *nothing*?"

"Not nothing," Theo said. "Something else."

Ada saw it happen—the moment the idea left him and entered the room. He had told her first, two nights ago, in the dark of the archive tower where they'd been sleeping among other people's records: *a new covenant. Not restored. Rewritten. With her in it.* He had spoken it like a man confessing to a crime, and she had lain awake after, staring at the ceiling, hating how much sense it made and how little chance it had.

"A new covenant," he said now, to the Hall of Nine Lanterns, to six cities' worth of terror. "Not the old bargain that fed realms on sacrificed wards. Not her war of unmaking. A third thing, written fresh, with terms that bind *everyone*—including me. Including her. But to write it, I need her to come to the table. Or I need to bring the table to her."

"And if she refuses?" the wolf-fur speaker asked.

"Then I'll need to be standing somewhere she can't afford not to hear me." Theo's hand found the ring at his throat. "The Hollow Gate. The Wellspring speaks through it. If I open the gate fully—if I make myself the door—there isn't a power in either realm that can ignore it."

The hall erupted. Councilors shouting about security, envoys shouting about madness, the blue lamps guttering as though the arguments were drawing on them for fuel. Ada moved to Theo's side through the noise and took his hand, and his fingers closed around hers hard enough to hurt, and she understood that the grip wasn't for the crowd. It was for himself. An anchor-line thrown inward.

Later—much later, after the envoys had been given wine and beds and the illusion of having been heard—an aide pressed a folded scrap into Ada's palm in the corridor. No seal. Just a single line in a clerk's careful hand:

*Veyra's herald requests parley at the south gate at moonrise. She asks for the Betrayer by name.*

Ada read it twice, and her blood went cold, and warm, and cold again.

"She knows he's here," she whispered to the empty corridor. "Of course she knows. She's always known."

* * *

Theo could not sleep, so he went down into the archive.

It was habit now—or perhaps it had always been habit, in whichever life. Books were the one country where he never felt like a stranger. The Sablehold archives sprawled beneath the citadel in nine descending levels, and the deepest levels were older than the covenant, older than names, shelves carved from the living rock of the hill the city stood upon. He walked the aisles with a lamp and let his fingers trail along the spines, and the spines hummed faintly against his skin, the way they always did now. Everything hummed. Everything remembered him. That was the problem with being a man the world refused to forget.

He had told the envoys the truth, and the truth was insufficient, and he had known it while saying it. A new covenant required Veyra's consent, and Veyra did not consent; she collected. It required the Wellspring, and the Wellspring was days away through country the enemy owned. And it required his true name—the one thing he had traded away a century ago to save a single life, the coin of his original betrayal—and though the echo in the tomb's ring had shown him *everything else*, the name itself stayed sealed, a door inside him with no handle on this side.

Except.

He stopped before a shelf near the lowest gallery, where the oldest books slept in lead-lined cradles against the touch of magic. His hand had stopped without consulting him. He had learned to trust that.

The book was small, bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, and it bore no title. When he opened it, the pages were blank—except they weren't blank, exactly. They were *expectant*. He had felt this once before, at the Wellspring of Names, where the water waited to be told what it was. This book was the same substance. A vessel shaped like a story, waiting for someone to pour.

On the first page, in ink that appeared as he watched—bloomed up through the parchment like frost forming in reverse—two words wrote themselves:

*Ask her.*

Theo sat down on the archive floor, in the oldest room in the free world, and laughed until the laugh turned into something else entirely, and the lamp burned low beside him, and far above, through nine floors of stone, the drums of the besieging army went on beating like a heart that would not be reasoned with.

*Ask her.* As if the answer had been hers to give all along. As if the name he'd sold a century ago had been written down somewhere, and the somewhere was a woman who had been sacrificed to the old covenant and had spent a hundred years learning every word of it.

Moonrise was coming. The herald was waiting at the south gate. And for the first time since the tomb, since the Wellspring, since the dying soldier's whisper of *Betrayer* in the mud of a refugee camp—Theo knew exactly what he had to do.

He climbed the stairs two at a time, the grey book against his chest, feeling it pulse like a second heart.

He had to go and meet Veyra. Alone.
