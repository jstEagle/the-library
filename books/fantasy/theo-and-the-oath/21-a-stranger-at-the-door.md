## 21. A Stranger at the Door

The valley woke the way it always had—mist unspooling from the river like breath held too long, the first light catching the thatch of the low roofs, a cock crowing somewhere with the confidence of a creature who had never known war. Ada stood at her kitchen window with a cup of tea gone cold in her hands and watched it all as if she had been away for years instead of months.

In a way, she had.

The Hollow Gate was quiet behind her house. She could feel it now the way she felt her own heartbeat—not a thing she listened for but a thing she simply was. It no longer pulled at her. It no longer sang. Since the Reforging it sat in its hollow like a sleeping animal, sealed from the far side, open only when *she* chose to open it. Her grandmother's sigil, which had once been a chain, was now a key. The burden and the freedom had arrived in the same hand, wrapped in the same cloth, and Ada still did not know which one weighed more.

She drank the cold tea anyway. Wastefulness was a habit of the other world, and she was trying very hard to be a woman of this one again.

Three days ago she had carried him through the valley paths to the cottage on the hill—the old keeper's house, hers by blood if not by choice—and laid him in the bed beneath the sloped ceiling where her grandmother had once slept. He had breathed. That was all she had asked of the world: let him breathe. The covenant took his memories as payment; it had not promised to take anything else, though Ada had spent three nights watching his chest rise and fall, counting, terrified that the ledger would find one more thing owed.

On the third morning, he opened his eyes.

She had been sitting in the chair beside the bed with the prophecy book closed on her knees—*the book*, the one whose last page now bore a single blank line—and she heard the change in his breathing before she saw it. A waking breath is different from a sleeping one. Sharper. Present.

His eyes found the ceiling first. Then the beam. Then, slowly, the window, where morning lay across the floor in a long gold bar. Then her.

He looked at her the way a man looks at weather. Politely. With interest, even—but nothing underneath. No recognition. No flicker of the road they had walked together, the pass full of echoes, the waystation falling around them while he said her name like it was a word he'd only just learned and already loved.

"Hello," he said. His voice was rough with sleep. "I'm sorry—do I know you?"

Ada had prepared for this. She had rehearsed it on the walk down from the gate, in the voiceless hours, in the space between one step and the next. She had prepared answers about fever, about an accident, about the kindness of strangers. What came out of her mouth instead was silence—a long, terrible silence in which every mile of the Riverlands and every ash-choked town and every night under Brannoc's patched sails passed behind her eyes while he watched her with those clean, unburdened eyes and waited.

"No," she said finally. "Not yet."

He frowned, gentle and confused, and she stood up too fast and made an excuse about tea, and fled to the kitchen, and there she had stood for a full minute with her hands braced on the counter, breathing the way Brannoc had taught her to breathe before a fight—in through the nose, out through the teeth—except there was no enemy here, only the absence of one. Only a man in the next room who did not know her name.

That had been yesterday. Or perhaps two days ago now. The days had gone soft at the edges since the Reforging, blurring into each other like watercolors left in the rain.

Now she rinsed the cup, set it in the basin, and went out into the morning to do what she had decided, sometime in the dark, that she would do.

* * *

He was in the garden.

Of course he was. Some things survived the loss of everything else—an instinct for ink, an instinct for growing things. She found him crouched at the edge of her vegetable beds, sleeves rolled, turning over a clod of earth with fingers that remembered how even if the mind behind them did not. There were smudges of soil on his jaw where he'd pushed back his hair. His boots were wrong—one of hers, borrowed, too small—and the sight of it cracked something in her chest that she had to press flat with both hands.

"You'll want the beans further from the wall," she said. "They climb. They'll take the whole south face by midsummer."

He looked up, startled, then smiled—an easy smile, unguarded, the smile of a man with no history to hide. "I didn't know I knew that."

"You probably don't. Your hands do." She crossed the grass and crouched beside him at a careful distance. Close enough to talk. Far enough not to touch. "Do you remember your name?"

"Theo." He said it carefully, testing it, the way one tests a plank bridge. "It was written inside my collar. And on—" he gestured vaguely toward the cottage— "there's paper everywhere in there. Lists. Notes in different hands. All addressed to me, or about me, or..." He trailed off. His brow creased. "There are notes in my handwriting that I don't remember writing. That's the strangest part. Not the missing memories. The evidence of them."

Ada nodded slowly. She had read those notes herself, late last night, by candlelight. *If he wakes and asks about the pass, tell him the truth or nothing.* In her own hand. *Don't lie to him. He paid enough.* Also her hand. And beneath them, older pages, brittle and brown: generations of keepers writing to a man who hadn't been born yet, instructions folded across a century like letters posted to the future. *Watch for him. Teach him gently. Do not let him learn it all at once.* Her grandmother's grandmother's hand. Her grandmother's. All of them writing to Theo. All of them writing, in the end, about a gate.

"Your name is Theo," she said. "You were an archivist. You came to this valley—" her throat tightened; she swallowed past it— "you came here some months ago, and you fell ill. A fever. It took your memory. You've been recovering here, in the keeper's cottage, because the village has no physician and I have some skill with herbs."

He studied her. Not suspiciously—that was the worst part. Suspicion she could have borne. He studied her the way scholars study sources: openly, generously, wanting to believe.

"And you're the keeper," he said. "Of what? There was a door in the hill behind the house. Stone, no handle. I tried it this morning."

Heat crawled up her neck. "Did you."

"It wouldn't open. It felt like it was deciding whether to." He laughed a little, embarrassed. "Which is nonsense. Doors don't decide."

"This one does." She brushed soil from her knees and stood. "Come walk with me. If I'm going to lie to you—or half-lie—I'd rather do it moving."

* * *

They walked the valley paths she had walked her entire life, and she showed them to him as if for the first time, because for him it was. The millpond with its patient ducks. The orchard where the apples had gone over sweet and soft, dropping their fruit into the grass like coins nobody bothered to gather. The crooked lane where Mrs. Halloran sold bread from a window box and called good mornings to everyone whether they answered or not.

And everyone greeted him as a stranger. That was the mercy of it, Ada realized—the valley had forgotten her crossing, but it had never known him, so his blankness fit seamlessly into their world. They smiled at him the way villagers smile at newcomers: warmly, briefly, already forgetting. Only old Mrs. Halloran squinted at him a moment too long, flour on her forearms, and said, "Have we met?" and Ada steered him onward with a hand at his elbow before he could answer.

"I keep feeling like I've been here," he said quietly, as they climbed toward the ridge above the village. "Not remembering. More like... a room you've dreamed. The shapes are right but the light is wrong."

The wind came up off the fields, smelling of cut hay and rain somewhere far off. Below them the valley lay green and complete, self-satisfied, safe behind mountains that had kept out everything except, once, a quiet archivist on a first date. Ada looked at it and felt the old ache—the one from before, the evening she'd dressed for dinner and left the gate unattended, the longing for a life larger than duty. She had gotten her larger life. It had cost a smuggler with a laugh like gravel and a city of refugees and a man's entire self, and here she stood on the ridge of the ordinary world, homesick for places that had tried to kill her.

"How much do you want to know?" she asked.

He stopped walking. The grass bent around his boots.

"The notes say different things," he said. "Some say *tell him everything*. One says *never*. They're all signed the same way—just a symbol, like a knot or a key. Is that you?"

"It's the office. Keepers sign with the sigil, not the name. It passes down." She turned to face him. The wind pulled at her hair. "Theo. I'm going to offer you something, and I need you to understand that it's a real choice, not a test. I can tell you everything. The fever story is a kindness, mostly true, and you could live inside it comfortably—you'd be an archivist who fell ill, in a valley you like, tended by a woman who—" her voice caught; she steadied it— "who is fond of you. Or I can tell you the truth, which is large and strange and will hurt, and after which you cannot unknow it. But it will be yours either way. That's the only rule. Whatever I give you becomes yours to carry."

He was silent for a long moment. Below, the valley went on being beautiful, indifferent, oblivious.

"When I woke up," he said slowly, "before I opened my eyes, there was a moment—half a second—where I knew exactly where I was. Not this room. Somewhere else. Cold. Ice on stone. Someone was holding my hand and saying my name over and over, and I couldn't see her face, but I knew—" he pressed his knuckles against his sternum— "I knew her. Here. Then it was gone, and I was just a man in a strange bed, and you were in the chair looking at me like—" he paused— "like someone watching a fire to see if it would go out."

Ada said nothing. Her eyes were burning. She kept them on the middle distance, on the blue line of the far hills.

"That was you," he said. Not a question.

"There was ice," she admitted. "There was a tomb. I held your hand for most of a day while you—" *died*, her mind supplied; she declined the word— "while you slept. After something happened that I won't describe until you ask."

He looked out over the valley. When he spoke again his voice had changed—lower, more careful, the archivist surfacing through the amnesia like a fish rising. "An archivist knows better than anyone: a record destroyed is not the same as a record never made. The information can be gone and the shape of it remains. Cataloguers call them ghosts. Shelves that hold the memory of a book long removed." He turned back to her. "I think I'm full of ghosts. And I think you're the only person who can tell me what books used to be there."

"So you want the truth."

"I want the truth." He smiled then, wry, and for one vertiginous second it was the smile from the waystation, from the pass, from the night before Sablehold when they'd shared a blanket and he'd told her that cataloguing was just loving things by naming them. "Though I notice you haven't told me your name. I've been calling you *the keeper* in my head, and it feels rude."

The laugh that broke out of her was wet and surprised, half sob, and she let it, because there was no one in this whole green valley to see her come apart except a man with no past, who would not remember by tomorrow that she had.

"Ada," she said. "My name is Ada Okonkwo. My family has kept the door in the hill for nine generations. And three days ago, you gave up everything you were—including every memory of me—to save two worlds, and I have been trying to work out ever since whether introducing myself to you again is the saddest thing I will ever do or the best."

He took that in without flinching. She had forgotten, in these strange days, how steady he was—had always been, in both lives, she suspected; it was carved into the bone beneath whatever the covenant had scraped away.

"Both, probably," he said. "Most true things are two things." He extended his hand, formal, absurd, soil-stained. "Then hello, Ada Okonkwo. I'm Theo. Apparently we've met, and apparently it mattered. I hope you'll tell me about it. Slowly. I'd like to take notes."

* * *

They shook hands. His grip was warm and careful, and Ada held on one heartbeat longer than a stranger would, and he noticed—he saw her notice herself noticing—and neither of them mentioned it.

They walked back down as the afternoon slanted gold across the fields, and she began, as he had asked, slowly. She told him about the valley and the door that decided. She told him about a book of prophecies on a family shelf, and a sketch in old ink, and a date that predated his birth. She did not tell him about Veyra, or the Wellspring, or the man named Brannoc who died so that they could walk free—those stories would keep; grief needed a foundation before you could build it on someone. But when they reached the gate in the hill, she put her palm flat against the cool stone, felt it stir and settle beneath her hand like a great animal recognizing its keeper, and said, "This part is true, at least: none of it happens unless you came through here. Whatever you were before—whatever you gave up—you're the reason both worlds are still standing. Start there. Everything else is footnotes."

He touched the stone too, tentatively, the way one touches a sleeping dog. Something passed over his face—there and gone, a ghost of a ghost. "It feels familiar," he whispered. "Like a word on the tip of the tongue."

"It will," Ada said. "Or it won't. Either way, you get to choose what it means now. That's the whole point of what you did."

They stood there a moment longer, two people at a sealed door, one holding the only key in the world.

Then, from the direction of the cottage, a sound reached them—small, impossible, utterly out of place in a valley where magic could not follow. The chime of the prophecy book's clasp, snapping open on its own, the way it only did when a page had changed.

Ada's head came up. Down the hill, through the cottage's open window, she could see it: the old book lying on the table, its covers spread wide, its final page—her final page, the blank line—no longer blank.

Fresh ink was drying in the afternoon light, and it was writing itself.
