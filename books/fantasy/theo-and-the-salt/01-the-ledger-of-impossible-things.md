## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There are forty-three ways to say *this map is a lie*, and I have written most of them.

The Guild prefers euphemism. We don't call a chart fraudulent; we call it *apocryphal*. We don't call it cursed; we call it *conditionally accurate*. And when a map describes a place that cannot exist—cannot, will not, has been surveyed by three separate expeditions and found to be nothing but open water—we enter it into the Ledger of Impossible Things with a small notation in the margin: *disputed provenance; see also: hope*.

That last phrase was mine. Master Corvane let me keep it because it made the junior archivists laugh, and because he understood something I was twenty years understanding: that people who catalog impossible things for a living need to believe the impossibilities are somebody else's.

My name is Ada Joubert. Ten years a ledger-keeper in the Cartographers' Guild, third archive, sub-basement two. If you asked anyone in the Guild above stairs who I was, they would think hard, squint, and eventually say something like *the quiet one with the ink stains?* This is not an accident. It is a career.

It was a quarter past eleven on a Tuesday in late autumn, and I was cataloguing a lie about a mountain range that walked.

* * *

The third archive smells of tallow, dust, and old salt—the salt being a mystery nobody below the rank of Guildmaster has ever solved. Sub-basements shouldn't smell of the sea. Ours does, faintly, on damp nights, as if the building were dreaming of somewhere it had been and wasn't supposed to remember. I had learned to ignore it the way you learn to ignore your own heartbeat, which is to say completely, until some night when you can't.

That night I couldn't.

I sat at my desk under the lamp with the good wick—the one I'd traded three hours of inventory work to old Pemmy for—and before me lay accession slip 4471: *Chart, cloth-backed, depicting "the Walking Mountains of Vell," provenance unknown, acquired from estate sale, condition fair.* The mountains in question were drawn striding across the northern ice like pilgrims, each peak crowned with what the anonymous cartographer had insisted were eyes.

I wrote in the ledger, in my careful hand: *Apocryphal. No survey corroborates. Note: artist's rendering of geological process? Recommend sealed storage, dry stack, no handling.*

And then, because it was late and the lamp was warm and nobody living knew where I was, I added, very lightly, in pencil, in the margin where only I would ever look: *I would give a year of my life to see them anyway.*

Then I erased it. Not all of it. Just enough that even I could pretend I hadn't meant it.

This is what ten years in the Guild teaches you. The maps aren't dangerous. The wanting is.

* * *

Let me explain how the Guild works, or at least how it works at my altitude, which is roughly the altitude of sediment.

The Cartographers' Guild maps realms. Not countries—realms. Places that sit sideways to the world, reachable through passes that are only passable on certain nights, doors that open onto coastlines no ship has sailed, forests that appear on no chart but have their own weather. Nobody remembers finding the first of them. The oldest ledgers simply begin mid-sentence, as if the world had always been writing itself and we'd only recently learned to take dictation.

We trade in them too. That's the part they don't put in the apprentice oath. A merchant prince pays handsomely for a route through a realm where his caravans can cross a continent in nine days instead of ninety. A duke pays more for a door his enemies can't find. And sometimes—rarely, quietly, always off the books—someone pays for a realm to be *unmapped*, its approaches erased, its existence reduced to a notation in a ledger that lives in a sub-basement and is read by exactly one man with ink-stained cuffs.

I am that man for roughly four thousand entries. I try not to think about what that makes me.

* * *

The bell over the stairwell rang at half past midnight, which meant someone was coming down without an appointment, which meant trouble, which meant—it took my heart a full three seconds to sort itself out—that it was Wren.

Wren Halloway comes down stairs the way she does everything, which is like the stairs should be grateful. She's a surveyor, first circle, which means she's one of the eleven people alive allowed to walk into unmapped country with a chain, a compass, and the Guild's blessing, and walk out again with the country in her satchel. She had chalk dust on one sleeve and river mud on both boots and she was holding a paper parcel that smelled gloriously of fried dough.

"Joubert," she said.

"Halloway," I said, because we do this. Ten years of this. Surnames, like soldiers, like the whole thing between us was official business, which it has never once been, and never once failed to be.

"You're here late."

"You're here at all."

"I'm always here." She dropped into the chair across from my desk, the one with the broken rung that visiting clerks get and she refuses to let me fix. "Corvane's got the second circle drafting until dawn. Something's coming down the pipe from the coast. Nobody'll say what." She set the parcel on my blotter. "You've eaten nothing. You never eat anything. You eat like a man trying to leave less evidence of himself than a moth."

"That's absurd," I said. "Moths eat quite visibly."

"Eat the dough, Ada."

She used my name maybe twice a year. I ate the dough so I wouldn't have to decide what my face was doing.

* * *

Here is the thing about Wren Halloway, and I have had ten years to compose this sentence and still can't make it honest in fewer than three clauses: she is the most alive person I know, I am the least, and between those two facts there is a distance of exactly one unspoken sentence wide, which I have measured nightly for a decade and never crossed.

We met when I was nineteen and newly apprenticed, carrying a folio up from the stacks, and she was seventeen and newly sworn, arguing with a senior surveyor in the corridor about whether a river could be said to *exist* if it only flowed on Sundays. The senior man said no, that was superstition. Wren said that was a failure of nerve dressed up as rigor. I stopped walking. I remember thinking: *she is going to be trouble for someone, and I hope it's me.*

It took her eight years to learn my name properly. It took me approximately four seconds to be lost, and I have had eight years to get out, and I haven't, and I don't intend to, and this is the entire architecture of my private life. A man builds his house on not-being-noticed, and then he leaves one window open forever, facing the one direction he can't afford to look at.

"Corvane," I said, to have something safe to say. "What kind of coastal business?"

"The kind where he asks questions he already knows the answers to." She stretched her legs out and knocked her boot against my desk leg, a habit, a drumbeat. "He had the courier logs out today. All of them. Even the sealed ones."

"Courier logs are duller than mine."

"Nothing's duller than yours, Joubert, that's why you're trusted with them." She grinned. I looked at the lamp. The lamp was very interesting. "But sealed courier logs getting pulled means money moving somewhere it shouldn't. Or information. He's counting things twice."

"When Corvane counts things twice," I said, "somebody's about to lose their position."

"Somebody's about to lose more than that." She said it lightly, but she watched me when she said it, the way she watches a coastline for the rip current that doesn't show. Then she shrugged it off, because Wren shrugs things off the way other people file them permanently. "Anyway. I came down for two reasons. Dough. And—" she fished in her coat and tossed something onto my desk.

A brass fitting. A surveyor's sight-pin, snapped clean, the thread stripped.

"Broke on the last run," she said. "Can your workshop people—"

"It's not a workshop, it's a bench and a bad attitude. But yes. Give me a week."

"Three days."

"A week, Halloway, I'm not the one who bends metal, I just write down what happened to it afterward."

"That's what I like about you," she said, standing, gathering herself toward the stairs. "You're the record. Everything else moves and you stay put." She said it like a compliment. I heard it like a diagnosis.

At the bottom of the stairwell she paused, hand on the rail, and looked back—not at me, at the shelves behind me, the long dark ranks of them going back farther than the lamplight reached.

"What's down there?" she asked. "Past the impossible things. The really old stuff. I've never been past the third gallery."

"Sealed access," I said. "Guildmaster only."

"What's in it?"

"Wren." I used her name deliberately, spending one of my two annual allowances. "If I told you, I'd have to catalogue you."

She laughed, and the sound went up the stairwell ahead of her, and then the bell rang again and she was gone, and I sat in the warm ring of the lamp with the smell of fried dough and the taste of a sentence I hadn't said, again, and I went back to the walking mountains.

Under the pencil ghost where I'd written and half-erased *a year of my life*, I noticed the salt smell was stronger tonight. Strong enough that I got up and checked the drainage channel along the east wall, which was dry. Strong enough that when I finally banked the lamp and climbed the stairs at two in the morning, I locked the archive door behind me and stood a moment in the cold corridor listening to the building breathe.

Buildings don't breathe. I know that better than anyone. I keep the ledgers.

* * *

Wednesday passed in inventory. Thursday, Corvane sent down a requisition for the sealed courier logs' index—which I copied, because copying is what I'm for, and noted, because noticing is what I'm secretly for, that every entry flagged related to the southern coast. Ports that had been ports for two hundred years. Nothing there but herring, customs houses, and the drowned kingdom.

Everyone knows about the drowned kingdom the way everyone knows about a scar on a relative's face: you don't mention it, you don't look directly at it, and you certainly don't ask how it got there. Mereth, the charts used to call it, back when charts called it anything. Two centuries ago the sea came in—or the land went out, the histories disagree, conveniently—and where a kingdom had been there was open water, and where the Guild had once maintained a survey office there was now a note in a ledger: *realm dissolved; entry closed.*

Entry closed. As if realms were accounts.

Thursday night I worked late again. I'd stopped pretending it was diligence. The truth is that after Wren leaves, the archive is the only conversation I'm any good at, and I am not, when you come down to it, a man who sleeps well in a life this carefully small. So I sat with the lamp and the ledgers, and I did the day's entries, and then I did the thing I do on the bad nights, which is read backward.

Every archivist reads forward—new acquisitions, new lies, new notations. But the ledgers go back further than the Guild admits, and on bad nights I follow them down like a man descending a staircase he knows better than to descend. Entry 1 of the third archive is a shipping chart of a harbor that appears on no other map, dated in a hand older than the Guild's founding charter. Somebody drew that harbor. Somebody walked its quays. Somebody loved it, probably, in the way people love places, stupidly and specifically.

And then somebody closed the entry.

I was deep in the fourth volume—entries so old they predate our numbering system, catalogued by whoever held my desk before me, a line of quiet invisible people stretching back like the ledgers themselves—when I heard the knock.

Not the stairwell bell. A knock. On the *archive door*. From inside.

Which is impossible. Which is, if you want the technical term, apocryphal.

I sat absolutely still for the length of ten heartbeats. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the stacks, a draft moved paper the way drafts do, a dry whisper of vellum against vellum, settling, settling—

And then, faintly, from the direction of the sealed galleries, the ones Wren had asked about, the ones with Guildmaster-only locks—I heard water. A long, low pull of it, like a tide drawing back over shingle, inside a sub-basement two hundred feet above the water table, inside a night with no rain.

I told myself: drainage. Old pipes. The building settling. I am very good at telling myself these things; I've had practice; it is essentially my profession. I banked the lamp, gathered my coat, and left, locking the door behind me, and if my hands were not entirely steady on the key, well, the corridor was dark and no one saw, and no one seeing is, as I have said, the entire architecture of my life.

I did not sleep. I lay in my rooms above the chandler's shop on Tallow Lane and listened to the real tide in the real harbor, and around three in the morning I caught myself doing something I had never done before: wishing the night would end faster. Wishing myself into tomorrow. Me, who hoards time like misers hoard coin, who has spent ten years making each day identical to the last precisely so that none of them would stand out, none would be missed, none would be *examined*—

The chandler's dog barked once, down in the yard, at nothing.

And then, much later, near dawn, boots on the cobbles. Running. Not passing—stopping. Below my window. A wet, ragged sound of breathing, and the unmistakable smell, rising up through the floorboards of a city that has never once in my ten careful years smelled of anything but tar and bread—

Salt.

Someone was standing outside my door, bleeding seawater, and I lay in the dark doing the arithmetic of my own cowardice, and losing.
