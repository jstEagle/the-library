## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Door

There are three rules to surviving a decade in the Cartographers' Guild, and I had written all three of them myself, in the margin of my own mind, where no auditor would ever think to look.

One: never volunteer.

Two: never be interesting.

Three: if you must be noticed, be noticed for your competence and nothing else. Competence is the greyest color in the world. Nobody interrogates grey.

I was thinking about these rules that night because it was inventory season, which meant the archive smelled of lamp oil and old vellum and the particular sourness of clerks who have been awake too long, and because Master Ondrel had just told me—Master Ondrel, who had not spoken to me in four years except through notes—that my cataloguing of the Umber Reaches was "adequate."

Adequate. From Ondrel. In the Guild's private language of praise, that was practically a sonnet. I filed the compliment away next to the memory of my mother's voice and the taste of honey-cake from a festival nine years gone, in the little drawer where I kept things that were true but rarely opened.

The night shift in Sublevel Three is mine by seniority, which is to say nobody else wanted it. That suited me fine. The deep stacks hold the maps that shouldn't exist—the realms surveyed once and then struck from every public index, the coastlines that appear on charts for a single printing and vanish, the islands that sailors swear by and cartographers swear at. Cataloguing them is like being the librarian for other people's dreams: quiet work, careful work, work that leaves you alone.

I had reached tray 114—a chart of a river delta that, according to its own legend, flowed uphill twice daily—and I was arguing with it in the polite way cataloguers argue, when the salt door groaned.

You should understand something about the salt door. It is called that because of what happened during the Founding, when the first Guildmaster ordered the threshold packed with sea-salt as a ward against damp and against worse things. Every apprentice learns the story: nothing from the water passes the salt uninvited. It is the one piece of folklore the Guild maintains without irony, mostly because the salt genuinely does need replacing every year and the budget line has to go somewhere.

In eleven years, I had heard that door open perhaps forty times, and always with the slow ceremony of authorized persons.

Tonight it banged open like a debt collector's fist.

I did what any sensible man does when alone in a sub-basement after midnight and the door slams: I stood very still and pretended to be furniture. This is an underrated skill. I have been mistaken for furniture by two Guildmasters, one visiting dignitary, and a drunk surveyor named Pell who once tried to hang his coat on me.

Footsteps. Wrong footsteps—not the measured tread of enforcers or the shuffling gait of a late clerk, but a lurching rhythm, weight going down wrong, like someone learning to walk on a deck that isn't there. And under the lamp oil smell came another smell, sharp and mineral and wrong for this far underground.

Salt. Fresh sea-salt, wet, on a person.

"Light," said a voice, ragged. "Where's—there should be light."

Rule two says never be interesting. Rule one says never volunteer. But there is a fourth rule, unwritten, older than the others, the one they beat into you at apprenticeship before they teach you the clever ones: *a keeper of records does not leave a man to die in the dark among the records.* Old Archivist Maren said it at my induction, back when I still believed the Guild meant things like that.

I lifted my lamp.

He was standing between the stacks with one hand pressed flat against a shelf for balance, and he was bleeding from somewhere high on his left side, the blood so dark in the lamplight it looked like ink, which even now—I know how this sounds—even now my first coherent thought was *that will stain the vellum.* He wore a courier's oilskin, Guild-grey, with the winged compass stamped at the shoulder, and he was soaked through with seawater, though we were four levels below the water table and half a mile from the harbor.

"You're the night keeper," he said. Not a question. He'd looked at my desk, my lamp, my name-plate, and made his assessment the way couriers do: fast, final.

"I'm the night keeper," I admitted. It felt like confessing to something.

"Good." He pushed off the shelf and came toward me, and I want to be honest here: I stepped back. Any decent man would want to say he stepped forward, arms open. I stepped back and knocked over my stool, and the clatter of it sounded enormous in the stacks, and the stranger laughed—one short, cracked note, like a bell with a bullet hole.

"Don't worry," he said. "I'm not going to hurt you. I'm going to give you something, and then I'm going to be extremely inconvenient."

"Sir, I can fetch a physiker—"

"No." The word came out flat and absolute, a man closing a ledger. "No physikers. No masters. No one with a title. You're the night keeper. You're exactly right. Do you understand? You're exactly the right amount of nobody."

I have thought about that sentence many times since. *Exactly the right amount of nobody.* Most of my life I'd assumed there wasn't a right amount, that nobody was simply nobody, a quantity of zero. He spoke as though it were a measurement. As though he'd been looking for a specific weight of invisibility and had finally found it, bleeding out in Sublevel Three.

He reached inside his oilskin.

Here is the moment I keep returning to, the moment I would take back if the world allowed revisions the way maps allow them. He reached inside his oilskin and I saw his hand—white-knuckled, shaking, nails split—and every rule I owned told me to put my hands behind my back and say *sir, I'll call someone* in my best boring voice. And I didn't. I watched his hand come out holding a leather tube wrapped in oilcloth, and I watched him stagger the last step toward me, and I did not move away.

Maybe that's all it ever takes. One second of curiosity. One second where you forget to be furniture.

He pressed the tube into my hands.

It was heavier than it looked. Cold, too, cold as harbor water, and through the wrapping I could feel the ridged edges of something rolled tight. My fingers closed around it before my brain signed off on the transaction, and by the time my brain caught up—*you are now holding an object of unknown provenance handed to you by a dying man in a restricted archive, congratulations*—his grip had already loosened, and there was no polite way to hand it back.

"The map," he said. His voice had dropped, gone intimate, conspiratorial, a man sharing the last secret in the world because there was no one left to share it with. "It's not a record. Whatever they told you maps are—they lied to you at your induction, boy. A map is a sentence. Some maps are orders. This one—" he coughed, wet and deep, and red flecked his lips, "—this one is a key."

"I don't—sir, I catalogue things. I don't open things."

"Better and better." He smiled. It was a terrible smile, the kind you make when laughing would cost blood you need. "Keep it dry. Keep it *hidden*. Don't let them inventory it. Corvane will inventory it."

"Guildmaster Corvane?" I heard my own voice go thin. "The Guildmaster doesn't inventory anything. He signs things other people inventoried."

"He'll come himself." The stranger's eyes had a shine to them now, feverish and certain. "For this, he'll come down into the dark himself. He burned—" Another cough folded him nearly in half. When he straightened, he grabbed my wrist, and his grip was shockingly strong for a dying man, the grip of someone who had decided to stay upright out of pure spite. "Listen. Listen to me. The gates remember. Whatever the ledgers say, whatever oath they wrote afterward—the gates remember what was done. Do you hear me?"

"The gates remember," I repeated, because when a dying man grips your wrist in a basement full of impossible maps, you repeat what he says. It's in no rulebook, but it should be.

"Yes." He exhaled, long, and some of the tension went out of him, and I understood with a lurch that he had been holding himself together by will and handrail, and that the handrail portion was over. "Yes. Good. Then it's—" He looked past me, up at the ceiling, as if he could see through four floors of stone to the harbor above, and his expression did something complicated: grief and relief and a kind of bitter homesickness, all at once, like a man hearing his mother tongue spoken in a foreign port. "Then it's delivered."

He slid down the front of the shelf.

I caught him—or rather, I broke his fall, which is different; catching implies you succeed. We ended up on the floor together among the dust motes and the ghost of spilled salt, and I did everything the physikers teach and a few things I invented, pressing the wound through his oilskin, shouting for help in a voice that cracked embarrassingly on the second syllable, telling him—he was Vess, he said, just Vess, no house-name, which in the Guild means born outside the walls, same as me—telling him help was coming, that he'd be fine, all the lies you tell the dying because the truth is unspeakable.

Vess was not interested in lies. "Don't report it yet," he whispered. "Not until morning. Let the night shift end clean. Promise."

"Why does it matter—"

"Promise, keeper."

And because he was dying, and because dying men get the last word in every culture I know of, I promised. I promised him I'd wait until morning. I have kept promises to dead men before—everyone in the Guild has, we keep promises to dead men professionally—but this was the first time I'd promised one while he was still warm.

He died between one breath and the next, mid-word, and the word might have been *remember* or might have been *Mereth*—I've gone over it ten thousand times and I honestly cannot tell you. His hand slid off my wrist. The lamp flame steadied. Somewhere in the stacks, the ventilation sighed, the building breathing around its secrets.

I knelt there for what the clock on my desk later told me was six minutes, holding a dead stranger's shoulder, holding a leather tube in my other hand, doing absolutely nothing, which was the thing I was best at in the world.

Then I heard the boots.

* * *

Enforcer boots have a sound. Heavy-soled, evenly spaced, several pairs moving in step without needing to discuss it. You learn it in your first month, the way you learn the fire alarm. It is the sound of the Guild remembering you exist, and it has never once preceded good news.

They came through the salt door four abreast, lamps high, and their leader was a woman I knew slightly by reputation—Sergeant Ilvet, square-faced, patient-looking, the kind of enforcer who interviews you the way a surgeon palpates: thorough, impersonal, hoping not to find anything. Behind her, two more spread out into the stacks with the smooth choreography of people who have done this in many rooms.

"Keeper Joubert." She read my name off my plate without slowing. "Report."

"A man came through the door," I said. "Courier's badge. Wounded. He gave me this—" I gestured at Vess's body with the hand that was not holding the tube, and then, with a small internal scream, realized I was still holding the tube, in plain view, in front of enforcers, like an idiot holding evidence. "He asked for a physiker. I sent word up. He died."

All true. All perfectly, beautifully true. I recommend truth whenever possible; it requires no maintenance.

Ilvet crouched by the body. Her people checked the stacks—fast, professional, checking sight lines and shadows, and I watched one of them pause at my desk and I felt my heart perform a maneuver usually reserved for falling from heights. But the tube was in my hand, not on the desk. Small mercies. Small, damning mercies.

"When?" Ilvet asked.

"Quarter past the hour. Perhaps twenty past when he—" I nodded at the floor.

"And the message he carried?"

"He didn't say. He was—" I hesitated, and here I must record, for the honesty of this account, that the hesitation was real. I had not yet decided to lie. I was standing at the edge of my entire life, toes over the drop, and my body was stalling while my mind caught up. "He was mostly concerned about the salt door being closed behind him."

Something flickered across Ilvet's face. "Was it closed?"

"It closes itself. It's weighted." Everyone knows this. Everyone also knows the enforcers check it anyway.

She looked at the door. Looked at Vess. Looked at my empty hands—because somewhere in the last thirty seconds, without deciding to, without any memory of the motion, I had moved the tube. Down the front of my coat, into the space between shirt and belt where the ledger-clerk's paunch goes to live. I cannot describe to you the sensation of realizing your hands have committed treason ahead of schedule. It is like reading your own handwriting in a book you never bought.

"Word went up about the physiker?" she asked.

"To the duty desk. Standard channel."

"Mm." She stood. "We passed the duty desk. There's no record of your message."

The temperature of the room changed. Or I imagined it did. Both are equally dangerous.

"Then the runner didn't log it," I said, keeping my voice at the pitch of a man mildly inconvenienced by bureaucracy, a pitch I have practiced in mirrors without knowing why. "Or logged it late. Or logged it under the wrong sublevel—it happens, Third and Fourth get swapped constantly, the plates are nearly identical—"

"They're different colors."

"They're *nearly* identical colors."

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—Ilvet looked at me the way auditors look at a column that sums correctly but feels wrong. Then she shrugged, and the moment passed, and I learned that night the single most valuable lesson of my new career: boredom is armor. Be the most tedious object in the room and blades slide off you.

"We'll take the body," she said. "You'll file your incident report through the normal channels in the morning. Full detail. Names, times, condition of the door."

"Condition of the door," I repeated gravely, as if this were the crux of the matter.

"One more thing." She paused at the salt door, silhouetted against the corridor light. "Did he give you anything?"

And here is where I tell you what I did, and you may judge me, and I will deserve it.

I looked her in the eye—her actual eye, steady and tired and human—and I said, "He tried to. He grabbed at my sleeve at the end, said something about a delivery, but he was past making sense. I couldn't tell you what he meant. If he was carrying something, it's on him."

It was a good lie. That's the worst part. It came out smooth and sad and detailed, with just enough failure in it to be believable, and I recognized the craft of it even as I used it, the way you recognize your own handwriting on a forged document. Ten years of watching the Guild lie in triplicate had apparently taught me something.

Ilvet studied me a moment longer. "If you recall anything else, keeper, you'll bring it forward."

"Immediately," I said. "First thing."

Her people bagged Vess—swiftly, without ceremony, the way you handle damaged goods—and carried him out. The salt door boomed shut behind them. The lamps settled. The ventilation sighed. Tray 114 sat where I'd left it, the uphill river patiently waiting to be described.

I stood alone in Sublevel Three with a stolen map against my stomach and a dead man's promise in my mouth, and I waited until my hands stopped shaking enough to count, which took longer than I'm proud of.

* * *

Here is the accounting, since I am a ledger-keeper and ledgers are how I pray.

Item: one map, provenance unknown, described by its dying bearer as a key. Currently concealed upon my person, destination unknown, purpose unknown.

Item: one broken promise—no, not broken; *deferred*. Vess asked me to wait until morning. Fine. Morning it is. Except that morning, per Guild procedure, is when I file my incident report, and the incident report asks, in question fourteen of section two, whether the deceased transferred any item to the reporting party. I have filled out that form. I know its teeth.

Item: one promise, newly made, to Sergeant Ilvet, to bring forward anything I recall. Which is technically compatible with Vess's request only if my memory fails overnight, and my memory has never once failed. Ask anyone. Ask the trays. Ask the uphill river.

So the two promises collide at sunrise, and I am standing on the collision point, and there is exactly one way to satisfy both, and it involves the phrase *he gave me nothing*, delivered again, in daylight, to people whose job is detecting precisely the thing I am doing.

Unless.

Unless the map stops existing as an item. Unless, between now and dawn, the tube in my coat ceases to be *an object given to Keeper Joubert by the deceased* and becomes something else—something already catalogued, already shelved, already part of the great grey body of the collection where a single misfiled roll can sleep for decades. I know those shelves the way other men know their mothers' faces. I know which trays haven't been audited since the old Guildmaster died. I know that in this building, a thing properly hidden in plain sight is safer than a thing locked in a vault, because vaults get inventoried and apathy does not.

This is what I told myself, anyway, standing there in the lamp-smell and the salt-smell, heart hammering out a tempo no competent clerk would approve. This is the story I needed: that hiding it was prudence, was procedure, was simply good custodial practice applied under unusual circumstances.

But underneath the story, in the margin where I write the true things, another entry was drying, and it said this:

*I took it because he offered, and because for one moment—one stupid, blazing moment—I wanted to be the kind of man things are entrusted to.*

The gates remember, he said.

That night, so did I. Every detail. The split nails. The winged compass on his shoulder, and beneath it, half worn away, a stamp I didn't recognize: a circle broken by seven lines, like a wheel with the spokes cut out. The exact weight of the tube in my hands, cold as drowned iron. The word I couldn't parse—*remember* or *Mereth*—except that when I finally unrolled the map an hour later, hands still trembling, in the yellow pool of my desk lamp with the door barred and the salt undisturbed, I found the name written across the bottom margin in faded brown ink, in a script older than the Guild, older than the city:

*M E R E T H*

And below it, smaller, in a newer hand, almost fresh:

*Deliver to the one who keeps the night.*

I sat down on my broken stool, in my invisible life, at my invisible desk, and understood that I was no longer either of those things—and that whatever came through that salt door tomorrow would be coming for me.
