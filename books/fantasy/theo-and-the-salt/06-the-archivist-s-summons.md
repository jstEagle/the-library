## 6. The Archivist's Summons

The summons came the way all Guild summons come: on cream paper, in a clerk's immaculate hand, with no hint of menace whatsoever.

*Master Joubert — The Guildmaster requests your presence at the fourth bell, Thursday next, regarding matters of inventory.*

*Matters of inventory.* I read it four times standing at my desk in the archive, and each time my stomach performed a slightly different maneuver. The first time it dropped. The second time it climbed into my throat and stayed there like a swallowed coin. By the third reading I had achieved something approaching calm, which is to say I had stopped being able to feel my hands. The fourth time I noticed that *inventory* had been underlined. Not by the clerk. The underline was in a different ink entirely — dark, almost black, pressed hard enough to emboss the page.

Guildmaster Corvane underlined things himself. Everyone knew this. It was one of those facts about the Guild that everyone knew and no one said aloud, the way everyone knew the third-floor lavatory window didn't lock and no one ever mentioned it to the Provosts.

I put the summons down. I picked it up again. I held it close to the lamp and looked for anything else — a watermark, a smell, a secret message written in lemon juice the way apprentices used to do before they learned that the Guild could see through everything except its own ledgers. Nothing. Just the word *inventory*, underlined in ink the color of deep water.

Thursday was two days away.

Two days is either an eternity or nothing at all, depending entirely on what you're waiting for. If you're waiting for a lover, it's nothing. If you're waiting for a verdict, it's forever. If you're waiting to find out whether the most powerful man in the Cartographers' Guild has decided you're a problem to be solved or merely a name to be checked off — well. I spent those two days doing the only thing I have ever been genuinely good at, which is being unremarkable on purpose.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about being invisible, because it took me ten years to learn and I might as well pass it on.

Being invisible isn't about hiding. Hiding draws attention — a closed door, an empty chair, a man who suddenly stops coming to the canteen. Being invisible is about *predictability*. It's about being so consistent, so reliably dull, that the human mind simply files you away under *background* and never looks again. The watchman who nods at me every evening has never once seen my face; he sees the nod. The woman who shares my table at the Lamplighter sees a thin man who orders the same stew and reads survey reports over his supper. She could not describe me under oath. I've tested this. Not formally. But I've sat across from her while she described her sister's wedding to someone else, and when the sister walked past our table, the woman looked straight through me to greet her.

So for two days I was Ada Joubert, ledger-keeper, third class, exactly as advertised. I catalogued. I cross-referenced. I complained mildly about the damp in Sub-basement Four to anyone who would listen, which was no one, because complaining about damp in Sub-basement Four is like complaining about gravity. I ate the stew. I read survey reports. At night I went home to my rooms above the chandler's and did not sleep, but I lay in bed with the lamp off in the posture of a sleeping man, because even insomnia should be conducted responsibly.

And underneath all of it, folded into the false bottom of my desk among forty years of misfiled correspondence, lay a map crusted with salt that whispered of drowned kingdoms, and a dead man's last words sitting in my memory like a stone in a boot: *the gates remember.*

I had not told anyone. That was the whole of my strategy, really. Tell no one, be no one, survive until Thursday.

* * *

Corvane's office occupied the top floor of the Guildhall, which meant it had windows, which meant it had light, which meant it was the single most luxurious space I had entered in a decade. Sunlight fell through leaded glass onto a carpet so deep my boots made no sound crossing it. Bookshelves lined every wall, but these were not working shelves — no salt-stained folios, no cracked spines, no maps rolled so tight they'd never lie flat again. These were beautiful books, uniformly bound, their titles stamped in gold. A room decorated with the *idea* of knowledge rather than knowledge itself.

Corvane stood at the window with his back to me, hands clasped behind him, looking out over the city rooftops toward the harbor. He was smaller than I'd expected. I don't know why I'd expected him large. Perhaps because he cast such a long shadow across the Guild. In person he was a compact, silvering man in plain grey wool, the kind of figure who would be described in a witness statement as *unremarkable, sir, honestly, I couldn't swear to him.*

"Master Joubert." He didn't turn. "Thank you for coming."

"You summoned me, Guildmaster. I came." Which was true, and safe, and stupid-sounding enough to be perfect.

He turned then, and smiled, and gestured to a chair. "Sit, please. May I offer you tea? My clerk makes it far too strong, but it's honest."

"No thank you, Guildmaster."

"Suit yourself." He poured his own cup, unhurried, and settled behind his desk with the ease of a man who had done this ten thousand times and enjoyed perhaps nine thousand of them. "Do you know how long you've been with us, Master Joubert?"

"Eleven years this autumn, Guildmaster."

"Eleven years." He sipped. "And in eleven years, how many errors have appeared in your work?"

I blinked. This was not the opening I had rehearsed against. I had rehearsed against questions about Tuesday night, about the courier, about the east door. I had not rehearsed against my own competence. "I — I couldn't say, Guildmaster. Some, certainly. The Provosts correct them."

"The Provosts correct them," he agreed. "Fourteen, by my count. Fourteen errors in eleven years, and every one of them small, and every one of them caught, and every one of them corrected without fuss." He set down his cup. "Do you know what that tells me?"

"That I'm careless fourteen times a decade?" The joke escaped before I could stop it, dry and flat, and Corvane laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised, as if I'd genuinely pleased him.

"It tells me you're the most reliable man in my archive," he said. "Errors are interesting, Master Joubert. Errors are where the mind wanders. Your mind doesn't wander. You are, by every measure this Guild possesses, exactly what you appear to be." He leaned back. "Which is precisely why you're here."

My heart was performing some kind of drum solo. I kept my face in the mild, faintly puzzled configuration I use for budget meetings. "I'm afraid I don't follow, Guildmaster."

"No," he said pleasantly. "I imagine you don't."

* * *

Here is what I learned in the next hour, and I learned it the way a man learns the depth of a river — by walking in and feeling the bottom drop away.

There had been a death. A courier, found in the canal by the Salt Door, three nights ago. Drowned, officially. The Guild's enforcers had recovered the body, searched the lodging, found nothing of note, and filed a report that Corvane now slid across his enormous desk toward me, open, face-up, trusting me to read it.

I read it. It was thorough and bland and wrong in ways I could only guess at, because I knew something the report didn't: the courier had died inside the archive, bleeding, with his hand around a map, whispering to a ledger-keeper he'd never met. Everything else in the report might be true. That part wasn't there at all.

"Tragic," I said. "Was he Guild?"

"He was." Corvane watched me with an expression of gentle, paternal interest. "A nephew of mine, as it happens. Distant. The family connection is why I'm handling this personally rather than leaving it to the Provosts." He paused. "You'll understand I want to be careful. Grief makes people clumsy, and I can't afford clumsiness right now."

"I'm sorry for your loss, Guildmaster."

"Thank you." And here was the terrible thing: he sounded like he meant it. There was real grief somewhere in the man, banked down under the layers of office like a coal under ash. "Now. The reason I've asked for you." He laced his fingers together on the desktop. "The Salt Door. Who has access?"

"Archive staff, Guildmaster. Senior staff. The Provosts. Yourself."

"And on the night in question — three nights ago — were you working late?"

And there it was. The question, arriving at last, wearing its little hat of casualness. Three nights ago I had been at my desk in Sub-basement Four, cataloguing a shipment of coastal charts, when a stranger had come through the Salt Door trailing blood and seawater and fallen against my desk pressing a dead kingdom into my hands.

"Yes, Guildmaster," I said. "I often work late. The damp makes daytime cataloguing difficult — the ink behaves badly in the afternoons. I find the evenings more reliable."

"Naturally." He nodded along, sympathetic, a fellow sufferer of humidity. "Did you see anyone enter?"

This was the moment. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that night. There is a moment in every lie — the first one, anyway; I'm told they get easier, though I hope never to verify — where the truth and the falsehood sit side by side in your mouth like two coins, and you must choose which one to spend, and you know that whichever you spend, you cannot take it back. The truth was: *yes, a dying man came through the door and gave me a map of Mereth and told me the gates remember and I have it hidden in my desk beneath a letter from 1841 disputing a delivery of squid ink.*

The falsehood was: "No one, Guildmaster. It was quiet. I heard the door once or twice — it groans, you know, in cold weather — but I assumed it was staff passing through. I keep my head down when I work. Bad habit. I'd likely not notice the Provost himself unless he stepped on my foot."

Corvane smiled. "A common ailment among archivists. Selective deafness. Half my senior staff have it."

"Occupational," I agreed. "We spend our lives among things that aren't talking to us."

Another laugh, shorter this time. He was testing me, I realized — throwing out little hooks of humor to see if I'd bite naturally or flinch. And I was biting, God help me, biting like I'd been doing this all my life, when in fact I had been doing it for approximately ninety seconds.

"Tell me about the collection in Sub-basement Four," he said. "What were you working on?"

"Coastal charts. The Harrow Bay shipment — forty-one items, mostly soundings, two with damage from salt exposure. I flagged both for the conservators." All true. Every word verifiable. This is the first rule of lying, which I learned that afternoon and have never forgotten since: build your lie out of true things, and let it stand on as much solid ground as possible. Only the load-bearing wall needs to be false.

"Salt exposure," Corvane repeated. He turned his cup slowly on its saucer, a quarter-turn at a time. "Interesting. Salt keeps appearing in this business. My nephew's effects — his boots, his coat lining — salt. The canal water is brackish, of course, so that proves nothing, but the quantity was..." He trailed off, watching me. "You'd know about salt damage, I suppose. In the collections."

"Some, Guildmaster. Mostly we get it on charts carried through coastal weather. It crystallizes in the fibers. Very distinctive."

"Mm." Quarter-turn. "And if a document came in heavily salt-damaged — recently, I mean, not archival damage — what would the procedure be?"

"Flag it for conservation. Log it in the intake ledger. If it were valuable, notify the senior archivist." I shrugged, a small shrug, the shrug of a man discussing procedure. "Why? Has something turned up?"

"Not yet." He said it lightly, but the word *yet* landed on the carpet between us and stayed there, visible, like a dropped pin. Then he stood, abruptly, and the audience was apparently over. "Thank you, Master Joubert. You've been very helpful. More helpful than you know."

"I'm glad, Guildmaster." I rose. My knees worked, which I considered a personal triumph.

"One more thing." He came around the desk and walked me to the door himself, hand briefly on my shoulder, a gesture of warmth that I felt in my spine like frost. "If anything unusual crossed your desk — anything at all, any item out of place, any visitor unaccounted for — you'd bring it to me directly. Wouldn't you? Not to the Provosts. To me. The chain of command exists to protect the people in it, and you're very near the bottom of ours."

"Directly to you, Guildmaster," I said. "Of course."

"Wonderful." He opened the door. "Oh — and Master Joubert? The fourteen errors. Don't let it trouble you. Perfection is suspicious. I trust men who make small mistakes." His smile widened by a degree. "It's the ones who never err that I worry about."

The door closed behind me with a click soft as a key turning.

* * *

I walked down six flights of stairs because my legs refused the ladder to the records lift, and every step I replayed the conversation, hunting for the wound I knew must be there — the place where I'd bled, the tell, the thing he'd seen. I found nothing. That was somehow worse than finding something.

Because here is what I understood, standing in the archive's grey afternoon light with my heart still going like a trapped bird: I had lied to the Guildmaster of the Cartographers' Guild, to his face, in his own office, and he had believed me. Or — and this thought arrived a moment later and sat down beside the first one like an unwelcome relative — he had believed me *exactly as much as he wished to*, and the entire interview had been theater, and somewhere in a drawer of that beautiful office lay a list of names, and mine was on it, underlined in ink the color of deep water.

I couldn't tell which. That was the genius of the man. Corvane had built an institution where terror and reassurance were indistinguishable, where a compliment and a threat used the same grammar. *More helpful than you know.* What did I know? What did he think I knew?

And underneath the fear, shamefully, unmistakably, there was something else. Something warm and rotten and thrilling. I had lied — I, Ada Joubert, whose entire identity was built on the accurate recording of what is — and the lie had *held*. The world had accepted my falsehood as readily as it had always accepted my truths. Eleven years of being exactly what I appeared to be, and it turned out the skill transferred. Boringness, it developed, was not just armor. It was camouflage. And camouflage, worn long enough, becomes a weapon.

I sat down at my desk in Sub-basement Four. I pulled out the false-bottomed drawer. I lifted out the salt-crusted map and held it under the lamp, and for the first time I looked at it not as evidence to hide but as a question to answer — because Corvane had asked me about salt, had asked me twice, had asked me the way a man asks when he already knows the answer and wants to watch your face while you deny it.

The courier had been carrying this. Corvane knew what the courier carried. Which meant Corvane knew this existed, and wanted it, and had asked *me* — the most reliable man in his archive, the man who notices nothing — because either he truly suspected nothing, or he suspected everything, and wanted to see whether I'd sweat.

I hadn't sweated. I'd made a joke about humidity.

I spread the map flat between two weights and began, properly and methodically, to study it — the annotations in the margin, the script I couldn't read, the coastline that matched no chart in the Guild's possession. Somewhere above me, Corvane was turning his teacup in quarter-measures and thinking about salt. Somewhere across the city, whoever had killed the courier was wondering what had become of the cargo.

And in my hands, for the first time, I felt it — not saw, *felt* — a faint pull from the parchment, like tide against an anchor chain. As if the map were not a record of a place at all.

As if it were waiting to be used.
