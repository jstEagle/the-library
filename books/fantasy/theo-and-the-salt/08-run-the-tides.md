## 8. Run the Tides

I have always been a light sleeper, which is the sort of thing people say about themselves as though it were a virtue rather than a symptom. Ten years of cataloguing other people's secrets will do that to you. You learn that the Guild's enforcers come at night not because they are cowards but because night is efficient. No witnesses, no questions until morning, and by then whatever needed doing has been done and filed.

So when I heard the third stair from the landing creak — the one I'd reported as needing repair twice in my first year, in writing, because even my complaints went into ledgers — I did not think *a cat*. I thought: *they came at night after all.*

I had spent three days telling myself the courier's death was a coincidence. That strangers bled out in archives all the time. That Corvane's gentle questions over tea had been exactly what they appeared to be: the idle curiosity of a busy man. I was very good at telling myself things. It was, in its way, the same skill as cataloguing. You take something enormous and frightening and you write it down small enough to fit on a shelf.

The creak came again, closer now, and heavier than any cat.

I got up without lighting a lamp. This is another thing ten years in an archive teaches you: where everything is in the dark. My room above the east stacks held a bed, a desk, two drawers of clothing, and a window that opened onto the drainage channel behind the Guild — a view so ugly that no one had ever once looked at it, which made it the most valuable view I owned. I'd oiled the hinges two winters ago for no reason I could name at the time. I suppose some part of me had been rehearsing this night for a decade without admitting it.

The door handle turned as I reached the window. Not forced — tried, first, politely, the way you test whether prey is asleep. Then the polite attempt became a shoulder against old wood, and I went through the window feet-first into the cold, dropped the four feet to the towpath, and ran.

Behind me, my door gave up with a sound like a ledger spine cracking.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about terror: it is extremely organized. My legs did their work while the rest of me stood very still inside my own skull and took notes. *They waited until the third watch. They knew my room. The stair creak means they didn't come in through the front, or they did and didn't care who heard. If they knew my room, they know my habits, which means they'll check the canal gate first, because everyone knows I walk home along the canal.* Panic wrote all this down in a neat little column while my body scrambled along the towpath with my heart trying to escape through my teeth.

The salt-stained map was against my chest, sewn into the lining of my coat. Three nights ago I had taken it out of the false ledger where I'd hidden it — the copy of Merle's *Inventories, Vol. IX* that no one had opened since before I was apprenticed — because hiding it there meant leaving it in the building, and I had finally understood that the building itself was the trap. I had sewn it into my coat with the clumsy stitches of a man whose hands were better with pens than needles, and every hour since, I had felt it like a coal against my ribs. *The gates remember,* the dying man had said. Lately everything remembered. The map remembered. The Guild remembered. And someone, tonight, had come to make sure I forgot.

I did not go along the canal. I cut inland, through the tanner's yard, over the wall into the chandler's stores, and out onto Cooper's Lane, and I did all of it in the dark without a lantern, and here is the humiliating truth: I have never been prouder of anything in my life. Because every step of that route existed in my head not as memory but as *catalogue entry*. Tanner's yard, gate latch broken since the spring flood, entry 4471-C, noted during a survey requisition I processed in my fourth year. Chandler's stores, second wall, mortar crumbled at shoulder height, entry 5102-A, flagged for repair, repair never funded, because nothing in this city gets repaired unless it can be invoiced to someone who matters.

Ten years of being invisible, and it turned out invisibility was not emptiness. It was a map. I was carrying the most detailed survey of this city that had ever existed, and it was drawn nowhere but inside me, in the margins of thousands of boring requisitions, and every boring line of it was suddenly worth more than my life, because my life currently depended on knowing which walls were crumbling.

I want to be clear that this discovery did not feel like triumph. It felt like finding out that the disease you've lived with all these years has a use. Like learning your limp knows a secret door.

* * *

They found me anyway. Of course they did. I was one man running through a city that the Guild has surveyed down to the individual roof slates; I was a smudge moving across their own masterpiece.

It happened at Saltbridge — I would learn to hate that bridge later, for reasons I couldn't yet imagine — when I came down off Cooper's Lane toward the river and there was a figure at the far end of the span, standing too still, wearing the flat grey of Guild work-clothes and holding something long and low that caught the lamplight wrong.

I stopped. He started walking. That was worse than running. Enforcers run when they're chasing someone they might lose. They walk when the outcome is already filed.

There was a moment — I want to report it honestly, because I am, whatever else has become of me, an honest recorder — a moment in which I genuinely considered simply handing it over. The map. Walking forward, unbuttoning my coat, saying *take it, I'm a ledger-keeper, this was never my story*, and going back to my room and my ink-stained fingers and my safe, suffocating, invisible life. I stood on Saltbridge in the dark and I weighed my life against a dead stranger's last breath, and I am ashamed of how close the scales came. Not ashamed of choosing. Ashamed of how narrow the margin was. A decade of careful living narrows everything.

But the courier had pressed the map into my hands specifically. Out of everyone in that archive, he had chosen the man at the salt-door desk, the one who worked nights, the one nobody watched. Which meant he had known the archive well enough to know who was invisible — and if he knew that, he had probably been Guild himself. And if the Guild kills its own couriers to keep a secret, then the secret wasn't a treasure. Treasures get locked up. Secrets that require killing get *buried*, and things get buried only when digging them up would change the ledger.

And I am, before anything else — before coward, before fool, before the man I would become — a keeper of accounts. Someone had cooked the books. Two centuries ago, by the look of the annotations, and had been killing auditors ever since.

I turned and ran back the way I came, and the walking man broke into a run behind me, which told me I'd guessed right: I was not yet filed under *inevitable*.

* * *

The unmapped stacks.

Every archive has them. Ours had eleven floors of catalogued collection and, beneath and between them, the warrens — the older shelves, pre-reorganization, pre-fire, pre-everything, where the classification systems had died mid-sentence generations ago and no one had ever finished the job because finishing it would cost more than the contents were worth. Officially the warrens were closed for restoration. Unofficially they were where the Guild sent problems it preferred to forget, and where apprentices like the one I had been went looking for quiet, and found it, and also found things like the survey I had catalogued in my second year and never been able to stop thinking about.

Entry 8819-F. Anonymous survey, undated, of the Guild building itself — not the public floors, the *whole* structure, including the warrens, including the spaces between the spaces, including, and this is the part that had kept fourteen-year-old Ada Joubert awake with delicious horror, several rooms that do not appear on any architectural plan and one staircase that appears on the plan but not, according to the anonymous surveyor's dry marginal note, *in fact*.

I had catalogued it, shelved it in Warren Section K, and reported it to my supervisor as *probably a joke by a bored surveyor*, which was the correct professional judgment and also completely wrong. I had gone back to it perhaps thirty times over the years. Not to study it. Just to visit. The way lonely people visit places.

Now I went back to it to live.

I came over the chandlers' wall again, doubled through the cooperage yard, and came at the Guild from the delivery side, where the loading dock has a cellar ramp and the cellar connects to the warrens through a door that officially hasn't opened since the fire. Entry 8819-F said otherwise. Entry 8819-F said the door hadn't opened since the fire *on paper*, and that the surveyor had gone through it on a Tuesday.

The bar was heavy and my arms shook getting it loose, and the whole time my ears were full of my own blood and somewhere behind it the sound of boots on Cooper's Lane. The door swung inward onto black air that smelled the way old paper smells when it's been left alone a very long time — dust and glue and something faintly mineral underneath, like the memory of sea water. I slipped through and pulled it shut and put the bar back, and then I stood in the dark listening to my own breathing and waiting for fists on wood.

They never came. Not there. Later I understood why: the enforcers had my room, my habits, my canal route. What they did not have — what *nobody* had, because I had filed it under *probably a joke* nine years ago and buried it in Warren Section K where the classification dies — was a complete map of the building's underside in my head. They would search the lit floors. They would search the catalogued stacks. They would stand in the restored corridors of the Guild's official self and find nothing, because I was not in the official self anymore.

I felt my way along shelf-ends in the dark, counting turns. Left past the drowned section — the actual flooded sub-basement, entry 6602-A, *do not shelve below row twelve* — right at the collapsed gallery, down the staircase that exists only in one anonymous surveyor's handwriting. My hands knew the way better than my eyes ever had. Somewhere in the black above me, lanterns moved and voices called to each other in the low, procedural tones of men completing a checklist. *Archive, floor four, clear. Archive, floor five, clear.* They would report a clean sweep by dawn, and Master Corvane would read the report over his morning tea, and somewhere in his immaculate mind a line item would close.

Except.

I sat down in the dark on a step that does not exist, in a passage that appears on no plan, holding a map sewn into my coat that the greatest archive in the world had failed to notice for two hundred years, and I started to laugh. Quietly. Hysterically. The laugh of a man who has just realized he has spent his whole life mistaking the cage for the building.

Because here is what panic had burned off and left behind, gleaming, like ore after a smelting: *the Guild does not know everything.* It doesn't even know its own house. It knows what it has catalogued, and cataloguing is a choice, and every choice leaves something out, and I — dull, punctual, invisible Ada Joubert, keeper of impossible things — had spent ten years becoming fluent in exactly what gets left out.

My invisible knowledge was not a consolation prize. It was the only kind of power in the world that the Guild could not seize, because it had never been written down anywhere they could reach. It lived in a clerk's skull, in the negative space of ten thousand tedious entries, and it had just saved my life, and it could do it again.

I stopped laughing. In the dark I took out the map — I could not see it, but I knew its shape against my palms now the way I knew the staircase — and I thought about the dying man's whisper. *The gates remember.* And I thought about the anonymous surveyor of entry 8819-F, who had mapped the unmappable and signed nothing, and wondered, for the first time, with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold rising off the drowned section below, whether that surveyor had also once sat in a hidden place in the dark, hunted, holding something the Guild wanted buried.

Whether I was the first. Or merely the latest in a very old, very quiet tradition.

The lamps upstairs moved away toward the front of the building. Dawn was hours off. I had a decision to make and a city full of crumbling walls to cross it in.

Entry 8819-F, I recalled, marked one more feature: an old drainage culvert beneath the west wing, leading to the river, wide enough for a man, unwatched since before the fire. The surveyor had noted, in that dry hand, *exit viable; recommend discretion.*

"Recommendation noted," I whispered to the dark, to the dead surveyor, to whoever might be listening in the deep shelves — and began the long crawl out of the Guild that had raised me, toward a river, a stolen boat, and a life that would never fit in a ledger again.
