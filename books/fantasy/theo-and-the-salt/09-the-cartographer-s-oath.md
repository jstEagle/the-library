## 9. The Cartographer's Oath

The knife wound in my side had opinions about the stairs.

I learned this at the third step, when my body informed me, in the language of white fire, that climbing was a negotiation I was losing. The archive's back stairwell is narrow — designed, I'd always assumed, for moving ledgers. It turns out it also works for moving a bleeding man who has spent ten years perfecting the art of being furniture.

Behind me, above me, somewhere in the dark of the stacks, the assassin was still listening. That was the worst part. Not pursuit — *patience*. He hadn't followed me down. He was waiting to hear which direction grief would take me, and every board I stepped on answered for me.

So I stopped stepping on boards.

There is a trick to old buildings that no one tells you because no one who knows it has ever needed to explain it: floors lie. The archive's ground floor had settled unevenly over two hundred years, and along the east wall ran a seam where the original foundation met an addition nobody had ever properly mapped — because the addition had never been *added*, only absorbed. A gap of perhaps eight inches between stone skins. I had catalogued its existence four years ago in a ledger nobody read, filed under *Structural Irregularities, Miscellaneous, Sub-basement East*.

Ledger 114-C. I could recite its call number the way other men recite prayers.

I went down through the wall.

It should be noted, for the record I would later be erased from, that I am not brave. What I did that night required no courage whatsoever. It required only the specific, humiliating knowledge of a man who has spent a decade learning where everything is kept, including the things his employers hoped he wouldn't notice. The Guild believes invisibility is a virtue in its clerks. They are correct. It is also a weapon, and they should be more careful about arming their furniture.

The gap let out behind the bindery, into the alley where the paper-waste carts stood. I took nothing but what I'd already taken: the map, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest like a second heartbeat; and from the surveyor's stores, on my way down, a field kit — calipers, a brass rule, three bottles of ink, a compass whose needle didn't point north so much as point at *things*, and a blank ledger with good binding, because if I was going to ruin my life I intended to keep records of it.

Habit. Ten years of habit. You can burn a man's home and he will still fold his hands before he screams.

* * *

The river docks at Greyharbor don't ask questions. This is not because the dockmen are generous; it is because questions cost money and answers cost more. I paid a barge-master named Osk with coins counted out in the particular rhythm of a man who does not wish to be remembered as generous either, and by the second bell after midnight I was lying under tarpaulin among crates of dyewood, watching the city slide away behind us.

Greyharbor went first. Then the Guild spire, its lamp still burning — Corvane works late, or pretends to. Then the rooftops where I had lived eleven years without ever once looking down from them. Then the walls. Then nothing but water and the smell of tar.

I want to tell you I wept for it. I didn't. Wounds are efficient things; they take all your moisture and spend it on themselves. What I felt instead was stranger: the specific grief of a man reading his own obituary and finding it accurate. Somewhere back there, in the archive, there was a ledger page with my name on it — *Joubert, Ada, Ledger-Keeper, Third Class, reliable, unremarkable*. And that man was dead now, or as good as, and the remarkable thing was how little of him there seemed to be worth mourning. Eleven years of careful living, and the whole edifice fit inside a single line of cataloguing.

Wren.

No. Not yet. If I thought about her yet I would turn around, and turning around meant dying in a stairwell for a map I hadn't even read.

The barge rocked. The dyewood smelled of iron and rain. Under the tarpaulin, in the dark, I unwrapped the map for the hundredth time and told myself, as I had told myself since the salt door, the thing that had become less a plan than a creed:

*I will understand this thing before anyone uses it.*

Anyone. Including me. Especially me. Because I had already seen what wanting did to people — the courier had died pressing it into my hands, the enforcers burned books for it, and some small honest part of me knew that if I opened the drowned gates tomorrow I would tell myself it was for knowledge, the way the Guild burns archives and calls it inventory.

So: the oath. Not sworn aloud — I've never trusted vows made in the dark, they're mostly just fear wearing ceremony — but written instead, that night, on the first page of the stolen ledger, in my smallest hand, the one I use for corrections:

*Let this book hold what I learn and not what I want. Let me read before I act. Let me be wrong in ink rather than right in blood.*

Then, underneath, because I am still myself even now:

*Item: find out why a needle points at things.*

* * *

We went upriver for two days. I stayed hidden while the barge worked its locks, coming out only at night to sit at the stern and watch the country I was fleeing become, gradually, the country I was entering. The wound closed slowly. Osk's cook — a silent woman called Maren who fed me stew and asked nothing, which in my experience means she had her own reasons for knowing what fugitives look like — changed the dressing with hands that had clearly done it before, and once, tightening the bandage, said: "Guild?"

I said, "Former."

She said, "They don't have former," and tightened another turn, and that was the entire conversation, and it was more honesty than I'd gotten from anyone in eleven years.

On the third night I began the work.

Not the dramatic work — no revelations, no gate-opening. Cartography, real cartography, is ninety percent boredom performed on behalf of the remaining ten. I started the way any surveyor starts a new country: with the edges. I measured the map. Its dimensions, its materials — skin of some animal I couldn't identify, salted until the grain itself seemed crystalline — and the pattern of stains that the dying courier's blood had half-obscured.

And then I found the thing that made me stop eating.

The stains weren't random.

I laid the map flat in the stern-light and held my brass rule over the brown blooms of salt-crusted blood, and they fell into lines. Deliberate lines. Marginalia, of a kind — a second layer of information hidden in what any inspector would dismiss as damage. Whoever had carried this map before the courier had known it might fall into Guild hands, and had written over it in a code made of wounds.

I spent six hours copying the stain-lines onto a fresh sheet before I admitted what they were: soundings. Depth-markings, in the old Merethi convention I'd seen referenced exactly twice, both times in ledgers marked *Restricted — Historical, Do Not Catalogue*. Numbers running down from the coastline of the drowned kingdom toward the gates, recording not the sea's depth but something else. Each number paired with a word in that dead script, and by lantern light, cross-referencing a grammar fragment I had once catalogued and been reprimanded for lingering over, I translated the first pair.

Depth forty-one fathoms: *the choir remembers*.

Depth sixty: *the doors do not*.

I sat back. The river moved. Somewhere upstream a lock groaned, patient as everything else in the world except me.

A map that recorded memory. A key whose teeth were cut to the shape of what the gates had forgotten — or refused to forget. And annotations written in blood by someone who had understood this and hidden the understanding where only a fool or a clerk would think to look, because everyone hunts treasure and nobody reads margins.

Everyone, I corrected myself, except the Guild. Which is why they burn books.

That was when I heard the splash.

Soft. Ahead, near the bow. Not cargo settling — I knew cargo's vocabulary by then. A body entering water and thinking better of it.

I put out the lantern. In the dark, the map went against my chest, the ledger into my coat, and the survey kit's sharpest instrument — a scribing needle, meant for engraving plates — into my fist, which was absurd and we both knew it.

Footsteps on the deck above. Two sets. One heavy, one trying to be.

Osk's voice, mild as milk: "Toll station ahead. They'll want manifests."

"They'll want," said the other voice, and I knew it — one of the enforcers from the archive, the tall one, the one who'd held the burning ledger open with his boot, "a list of names."

"Passengers aren't cargo."

"Everything's cargo. That's rather the point of the Guild."

The barge slid on. Through a gap in the tarpaulin I watched the toll-lanterns grow, twin orange eyes on the black water, and I did arithmetic with my life: the enforcer had boarded at the last lock, meaning the Guild had watchers on the river, meaning they knew my face and possibly my destination, meaning —

Meaning Maren appeared beside me in the dark, silent as her stew, and pressed something into my free hand. A coil of rope. She pointed at the water, then at the far bank — reeds, black, close enough to touch if the barge drifted a boat's length sideways — and looked at me with the expression of a woman performing one final inventory.

"Former," she said.

The toll-station hailed us. Osk answered. Voices overlapped in bureaucratic liturgy, and beneath the tarpaulin I tied the rope to a cleat with the shaking efficiency of a man whose hands had finally agreed on something, and lowered myself into water cold enough to rewrite my priorities.

The current took me. The reeds took the current. And the last thing I saw of that barge, of that kindness, was the lantern swinging away downstream, carrying my false trail north while I dragged myself south through the mud, wounded, hunted, nameless in every way that mattered —

— and, for the first time since the salt door, certain.

Not certain of safety. Never that again. Certain of the work. The map lay against my heart, its blood-written soundings damp but legible, its gates waiting somewhere ahead of me in the flooded dark, and I crawled up the bank into a country I had never catalogued carrying the only oath I had ever kept:

Read first. Act last. Be wrong in ink, never in blood.

Somewhere south of the river, in the hills, someone was waiting who recognized Merethi script — the carter Brannic, if the exile rumors I'd catalogued were worth anything, if the tide roads I'd memorized from Ledger 88-F still ran where the old maps said they ran.

I had a long walk, a bad wound, and a dead language in my pockets.

I got moving.
