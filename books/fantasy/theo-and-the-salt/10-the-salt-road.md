## 10. The Salt Road

I had been a ledger-keeper for ten years, which meant I knew exactly how much a man could carry before his shoulders complained and exactly how little he needed before his conscience did.

The answer to the first was about forty pounds. The answer to the second was one salt-stained map and a dead man's whisper, and I would have traded both for forty pounds of boring inventory any day of the week.

Three days out of Corvane's Reach, following the river road south because south felt less likely than north and east was where the Guild's influence pooled like spilled ink, I had learned several things about being hunted that no archive had ever taught me.

One: you cannot outwalk your own habits. I caught myself twice checking an inn's registry the way I'd check a ledger, running my finger down the column of names, and both times my own face looked back at me from some clerk's memory like a debt coming due.

Two: fear is expensive. Not in coin—in attention. Every hour I spent listening for hoofbeats behind me was an hour I wasn't reading the map, and the map, I was beginning to understand, did not reward casual readers.

And three: the world is full of people who will help you if you look pathetic enough, and I have never once in my life failed to look pathetic.

* * *

Brannic found me at a crossroads shrine on the fourth morning, or possibly I found him; the distinction mattered more to him than to me. He had a cart, a mule with the moral gravity of a tax assessor, and one eye. The other socket was covered by a leather patch stitched with a pattern I didn't recognize—interlocking waves, or teeth, depending on how you squinted.

"You're bleeding through your shirt," he said, by way of greeting.

"It's old blood."

"Blood doesn't age well." He climbed down off the cart seat with the deliberate care of a man whose body had filed complaints he'd learned to ignore. "You've been walking since the river. I passed you yesterday. You were walking then, too."

"Is that a crime?"

"On this road? Everything's a crime. Walking is just the slowest way to commit it." He unhitched the mule, poured water into a bucket, and watched the animal drink with an expression of genuine tenderness that sat strangely on a man built like a doorframe. "There's a town six miles on. Guild post, Guild toll, Guild clerk who writes down faces. You want to go around it, you take the drover's track past the standing stone. Or you sit on my cart under the sacking and we go through, because I owe them nothing and they know it."

Every instinct I owned—the ones the Guild had spent a decade cultivating, the small cautious instincts of a man whose survival strategy was *be beneath notice*—told me to say thank you, decline politely, and walk alone into the hills.

But my shoulder was infected, I now realize, though at the time I called it "sore," and my coin was nearly gone, and there is a particular loneliness to being hunted by an institution you served faithfully for ten years that makes even a stranger's suspicion feel like company.

"The sacking," I said. "Please."

He grunted, which I would learn was his version of a warm welcome.

* * *

Under the sacking, in the dark that smelled of turnips and axle grease, I did what I always did when I couldn't sleep: I read.

The map had stopped behaving like paper somewhere outside the city. That sounds like madness, so let me be precise. The map behaved like paper in every measurable way—it creased when folded, it tore along the grain, it weighed what vellum weighs. But it did not behave like a *record*. Records are passive. Records sit still and let you look at them. This map looked back.

Not literally. Mostly not literally. But the annotations—the ones in the dead language I'd spent three feverish nights trying to parse—shifted their emphasis depending on when I read them. At dawn, the script along the eastern coast seemed to speak of tides. At dusk, the same lines spoke of doors. I had begun keeping a log of my readings, cross-referencing times against interpretations, because if I was going to be mad I intended to be mad *methodically*, and methodical madness is nine-tenths of scholarship anyway.

The word that recurred—I was sure of this much, even with half the alphabet still opaque to me—was *Mereth*. It appeared in the annotations the way a name appears in an old family Bible: worn soft from touching.

The cart jolted over a rut. The sacking shifted, and a slice of daylight fell across the map, and for half a heartbeat I saw Brannic's eye in the gap between sacks—one eye, fixed downward, reading.

He didn't say anything. Neither did I. The sacking settled back into darkness, and we rolled on toward a Guild toll neither of us wanted to pay, two liars sharing a bed of turnips.

* * *

The toll clerk at Marrowford was seventeen years old and had the eyes of a man who would grow up to be either a great investigator or a terrible drunkard, and I write that with affection because I recognized myself. He looked at me too long. He wrote my description down too carefully. Brannic distracted him with a complaint about road maintenance that was apparently a decade old and personally offensive, and I stood at the cart's tail doing my finest impression of a carter's hired hand, which is to say: slouching, mute, and faintly resentful of authority in a general rather than specific way.

It worked. Of course it worked. Being overlooked is the one craft I have truly mastered.

It was only after the town, on the open road with the salt marshes beginning to show themselves gray and breathing on our left, that Brannic said:

"That book you're hiding. It's not a book."

"No?"

"Books don't make men read them under sacking at noon." He flicked the reins. The mule, unmoved, maintained her pace. "My grandmother had papers like that. She hid them in the flour bin. My father said she was mad. Mad people don't hide things in flour bins, boy. Only frightened people do, and frightened people are usually right to be."

"You saw the map," I said. "At the shrine. And again just now."

"I saw enough." He turned his head, and his single eye had a flatness to it that I distrusted deeply, the way you distrust deep water. "Show me the writing. Just the writing."

"Brannic—"

"My cart, my terms." A pause, and then, quieter: "Or walk. I'll not force you. I've been forced before. It's poor practice."

I should have walked. Let the record show that every sensible part of me voted to walk, and that the vote was overruled by the exhausted, infected, lonely remainder—which is to say, most of me. I drew the map out and held it open between us as the cart swayed, and I watched his face while he read a language that has been dead for two hundred years.

His face did not do what I expected. I expected greed, or fear, or the blank politeness of a man humoring a lunatic. What happened instead was something older and quieter. His jaw worked. His hand came off the reins and hovered over the page without touching, hovering the way you hover over a sleeping child you don't want to wake.

"*Sael meretha,*" he said.

The syllables went through me like cold water. They matched, exactly, the phrase I'd spent three nights failing to pronounce, the phrase that opened the longest annotation, the one I'd privately translated as *the gates remember* because the dying courier had given me no better translation and grief makes a poor dictionary.

"What does it mean?" I asked.

"'The house below still counts its own.'" He said it the way you say a prayer you learned before you understood it. "That's how my grandmother ended it. Every time. Like signing a letter." He took a breath that shook slightly at the edges, and I understood I was looking at something I had not known existed: a living thread back through the drowning. "Her name was Maren ost Vellin. She came from under the water, boy. Not near it. Under."

* * *

We made camp that night in a drover's shelter off the road, a low stone roof with a fire pit and a view of nothing but marsh, and Brannic cooked a stew that tasted mainly of salt and patience, and he told me about his grandmother.

Not all at once. He told it the way careful men tell dangerous stories, in pieces, testing each one before offering the next. Maren ost Vellin had come ashore as a girl of eight, one of eleven children smuggled out through channels that no longer appeared on any chart, in the years after the Guild sealed the gates and the sea took the rest. She had been raised inland by a fishing family paid to forget her. She married a carter. She kept a flour bin full of papers in a language nobody could read, and once a year, on a night she never explained, she walked down to the tide line and stood there until dawn.

"She'd come back soaked to the knees and wouldn't say why," Brannic said. "My father thought she was mourning. I think—" He stopped, worked his jaw again. "I think she was reporting in. Whatever that means. Whoever to."

"And the patch?" I asked, because I am sometimes tactless when I'm fascinated.

He touched the leather at his eye almost fondly. "Different story. Cart accident, if you ask the tax assessors. Knife fight, if you ask anyone else. Truth is I lost it to a man in Saltbridge who wanted my grandmother's papers, twenty years back. I gave him the eye instead. Kept the papers." A grim little smile. "Burned them after, God forgive me. Couldn't read them. Couldn't risk them. Some nights I think that was wisdom. Most nights I think it was cowardice wearing wisdom's coat."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you're carrying her language under sacking on a road where the Guild's riders pass twice a day, and you flinch at the word 'inventory,' and your left arm isn't moving right, and you looked at me like a drowning man looks at driftwood and then pretended you hadn't." He poked the fire. Sparks went up and died young. "You're Guild. Don't insult us both by denying it—you've got archive hands, soft as a clerk's conscience, and you catalogued your own escape route the moment you sat down. But somebody's hunting you, and it isn't the exiles, because exiles don't send riders in Guild colors."

The fire crackled. Out in the marsh, something cried—a bird, probably. Probably.

"They killed a courier," I said finally. "In the archive. In front of me, effectively. He gave me this and died saying the gates remember, and I've spent four days telling myself I stole it out of cowardice or curiosity or shock, and none of those are true. I took it because someone had to, and everyone else in that room was holding a torch."

Brannic nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something he'd already entered in a ledger of his own. "The courier. What did he look like?"

"Tall. Gray at the temples. Guild-issue boots resoled twice—he was careful with money. A scar through his eyebrow."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the mule to complain about it.

"Coleman Vane," Brannic said quietly. "That's Coleman Vane's description. He used to come through the tide market every spring. Brought medicine, mostly. Took letters." His single eye found mine across the fire, and there was grief in it, old and fresh at once. "He was Merethi, boy. Third generation, hidden deep inside the Guild itself. If he carried that map to *you*—"

"He didn't carry it to me. He was dying. I was nearest."

"Weren't nothing nearest about it." Brannic leaned forward, and the firelight moved in his face like weather. "Coleman Vane didn't do anything by accident in thirty years of not doing anything by accident. If he put that map in your hands with his last breath, then he chose you, and I'll wager my good eye—and I value it, believe me—that he chose you *because* you're Guild. Because you'd know what it was. Because you'd know what it cost." He sat back. "Question is whether you know yet."

"I know it's a key."

"Do you." Not a question, really. An invitation to keep going, delivered with the gentleness of a man setting a trap for rabbits he intends to feed.

"It opens something. Gates. The ones the Guild sealed when they drowned—" I stopped, because I heard it as I said it, the shape of the sentence, the way it refused to be passive. The Guild hadn't drowned anything. The sea had. Everyone knew that. It was in the histories. It was in the Guild's own founding records, which I had catalogued myself, twice.

Except I had also catalogued, three nights ago by candlelight, an annotation that translated roughly as *they broke it first*, and my translation had been uncertain, and I had told myself the uncertainty was the fault of my alphabet.

"The kingdom under the water," I finished, carefully. "Mereth."

"Maren's word for it was home," Brannic said. "Same thing, maybe. Different weight." He lay back against the cart wheel, one knee up, and stared at the stars with his one eye as though auditing them. "Eleven children came ashore the year of the sealing. Maren was the last alive by the time I was born. She used to say the water keeps accounts too, boy. Slow ones. Patient ones. Two hundred years is nothing to a tide." He closed the eye. "Get some sleep. We reach the market day after tomorrow, if the roads hold."

"What market?"

"The one that isn't on your maps." A pause, and then, with the first real humor I'd heard from him, dry as old rope: "Which, given your profession, ought to worry you considerably."

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat by the embers with the map open on my knees and Brannic's grandmother's phrase turning over in my mind like a coin being tested for counterfeit: *the house below still counts its own.*

Here is what I understood, sitting in the dark with my bad arm cradled and my good instincts long since exhausted: I had believed, until tonight, that the map was mine to protect. That I was its guardian, its reluctant custodian, a decent man dragged into history by accident. Brannic had dismantled that comfort in the space of a stew. There was a whole submerged nation of flour bins and annual vigils and burned letters, generations of people keeping faith with a country at the bottom of the sea, waiting—not hoping, *waiting*, with the terrible discipline of the patient—for someone to bring the key home.

And a dying man had handed it to me. To *me*. The least remarkable man in the archive. The one who checked registries with his finger and slouched convincingly and had never once, in ten years, been worth noticing.

Unless, of course, that had been the qualification all along.

Out in the marsh the tide was coming in, filling the channels with a sound like slow applause, and I thought: somewhere under that applause there is a house, and the house is counting, and I am very afraid that I have just been added to the ledger.

"Boy," Brannic said, without opening his eye. "Whatever you're deciding over there—decide it walking. We move at first light, and after tomorrow night, there's no road back that doesn't go through people who want that map. So hold onto it, and hold onto your wits, and remember one thing about the tide market."

"What's that?"

"Everybody there wants something. Including me." He turned over, settling. "Sleep."

I didn't. But I watched the horizon go gray, and I held the map, and when the light came I was ready—or as ready as a man can be who has just discovered that his accidental theft was perhaps an appointment, and that the road he'd chosen at random out of a dying man's arms had been leading, all along, to a market that wasn't on any map, where a whole drowned nation had been waiting two hundred years for someone exactly as unremarkable as me.

The mule, for her part, remained entirely unimpressed. Someone had to be.
