## 11. What Corvane Keeps

There are three locks on my office door, and I have never once used them.

This is not carelessness. A locked door tells a visitor *I have something to hide*, and a man who has something to hide is a man who can be blackmailed. An open door, on the other hand—well-lit, tidy, smelling faintly of cedar and good ink—tells the world that Guildmaster Aldous Corvane has nothing in his possession worth stealing. It is the most expensive illusion I maintain, and I maintain it carefully. The ledgers on my shelves are real. The correspondence is genuine. The decanter of amberwine on the sideboard is there to be drunk by anyone who asks.

What I keep is elsewhere.

But we will come to that.

* * *

The inner circle meets on the last Thursday of every month, in the map room beneath the Hall of Surveyors, because the acoustics are excellent and because everyone who matters already knows where it is. There are six of us: myself; Vess, who runs our shipping interests; the twins Oren and Olla Marchetti, who handle acquisitions of the more delicate variety—you would call them theft, I call them *provenance correction*; Master Quill, our treasurer, whose name is actually Barnaby Foss and who has answered to Quill so long he flinches at his baptismal record; and Dame Hesperine Vail, who oversees the archive and therefore oversees everything anyone in this city knows about the shape of the world.

Dame Vail is the only one I do not entirely trust, which is why she is in the room. Trust the people who can hurt you enough to keep them honest. Or keep them frightened. In Hesperine's case, both.

We had finished the quarterly figures—the Guild's holdings now exceed the crown's, though no crown has ever been permitted to learn this—and Quill was gathering his papers when I said, "Before you go. A matter of inventory."

Olla stopped fidgeting. She is the twin who watches faces. Her brother watches hands. Between them they miss very little, which is why I employ both and trust neither, and why the arrangement works beautifully for all three of us.

"The courier," I said. "Recovered from the canal outside the archive. You'll want to know what he carried."

"Salt damage," Oren said, consulting his notes. "Extensive. The map itself was waterlogged beyond recovery. We burned what remained."

"Yes," I said. "You burned what remained."

Hesperine looked up. She has eyes like a hawk that has read your correspondence. "And what did *he* carry?"

I let the pause sit. Pacing, in these matters, is everything. A secret surrendered too quickly seems worthless; hoarded too long, it breeds suspicion. One counts out a revelation like coin, one piece at a time, and always makes the listener feel they have gotten change.

"A copy," I said. "Of a chart we sealed two hundred years ago. The Merethi coast."

Nobody moved. Good. Movement means surprise, and surprise means someone hasn't done their reading. Six people in this room, and every one of them had already guessed—I could see it in the way none of them looked at each other—but hearing it aloud is different. A thing suspected is a splinter. A thing spoken is a wound.

"That's impossible," said Vess, who says that about anything inconvenient.

"It's improbable," I corrected. "Which is worse. The originals were destroyed. I destroyed two of them personally, thirty-one years ago, in this room, with Dame Vail as witness." I nodded to her. "There were supposed to be five."

"And now?" Hesperine asked quietly.

"Now there may be another. Water-damaged, possibly incomplete, possibly a forgery—and currently in the possession of someone inside this building, or it was, before our courier died trying to deliver it to someone else."

* * *

Let me tell you about Mereth, since no one else will, and since even I will only tell you once.

You have heard the official version, because we wrote it and it is a fine piece of work. Two centuries ago, so the story goes, the sea-kingdom of Mereth grew proud and hungry. Its gates could raise tides and drown coastlines, and its king grew covetous of dry land. The Cartographers' Guild—then young, then idealistic—joined the coastal kingdoms in a war of survival, and at terrible cost sealed the drowned gates forever. The sea took the kingdom. Grief, tragedy, necessary sorrow. Every schoolchild in the coast cities learns it with a shiver and a candle lit for the dead.

Here is the true version, which fits on a single ledger line: *we owed them.*

Mereth traded with the coast for four hundred years. Salt, pearls, tide-glass, deep-iron—the wealth of the undersea came up through the gates and made six port cities rich, and the Guild grew fat on the charting fees, because every ship that crossed the shallows needed a Guild map and paid a Guild price. And then the coastal crowns looked at the flow of treasure going down and asked themselves why it should continue going down, and the Guild looked at its own ledgers and agreed. The war was not a defense. It was an audit. We decided the account was unbalanced—in their favor—and we closed it.

The gates didn't fail. They were bound, with blood and ink, by cartographers who knew exactly what they were doing, and who wrote into the treaty-map a clause so vicious I still admire it: the binding could never be undone by force, only renegotiated, and the renegotiation required the original key—a master chart of the drowned coast—which the victors then spent fifty years systematically destroying.

We built everything on top of that. The Guild's charter, its monopoly, its vaults, this room. Every coin in Quill's ledgers descends from a theft so old it has been laundered into history. And here is the part I want you to hold onto, the part that keeps me awake some nights—not from guilt; guilt is for people with time to waste, but from arithmetic: *the theft worked.* The coast has two hundred years of cities, children, harvests, songs. Unseal those gates, and the tide-storm that returns Mereth will take it all back, and the sea-king's heirs will call it justice, and they will be correct, and it will still be the death of a million people who never signed the original contract.

That is why the maps must stay destroyed. Not because the truth shames me. Because the truth, loose in the world, kills.

I have made my peace with that. My peace is very orderly. It has columns.

* * *

"So," Vess said, when I had finished telling them about the courier. "A copy survives. Who forged it?"

"Perhaps no one. Perhaps it's genuine." I poured wine, unhurried. "Consider: five originals. We confirmed destruction of four. The fifth was reported burned with the surveyor's wing during the Sack—that fire, incidentally, was set by our own people, a detail the histories omit. But fires leave gaps, and gaps leave rooms, and rooms leave things. If a copy existed, it existed in Merethi exile hands for two centuries, passed down like a relic. Which means our dead courier wasn't carrying paper. He was carrying a *claim*. A birthright."

"And he brought it *here*?" Olla frowned. "To the Guild? Why would an exile hand the key back to us?"

"Because the courier was not an exile."

I watched them work through it. Oren got there first—he always gets there before his sister with facts, and after her with feelings.

"He was Guild," Oren said.

"He was Guild. Third-rank courier, eleven years of service, clean record, mediocre performance reviews. Tam Wexley." I said the name evenly, and gave them a moment, because the next part deserved a moment. "He was also my sister's son. My nephew. I sponsored his apprenticeship myself, twenty years ago, under a name he never knew to connect to mine. Corvane keeps its family business quiet."

Silence. Even Hesperine's quill had stopped.

"You knew him," Vess said. "You knew what he was carrying."

"I knew he'd gone missing eight days ago with a delivery pouch and no explanation. I knew he turned up floating past the salt door with saltwater burns on a body pulled from a *freshwater* canal, which should interest someone in this room with an eye for inconsistencies—" I looked at Oren, who wrote something down, "—and I knew that whatever he carried, he chose to run *toward* the Guild archive rather than away from it. Dying men go home. Ask yourself what he thought he was bringing us, and what he feared we had become."

"You could have told us," Vess began.

"I could have told you the moment I confirmed it. Instead I waited until the quarterly close, because grief makes poor bookkeeping and I refuse to mix the accounts." I set down my glass. "Tam was the best of the next generation of couriers, which is a low bar and yet he cleared it. He believed in the Guild. He believed in the maps. Somewhere in the last year, something showed him a page we had hidden, and instead of burning it or selling it, he tried to bring it to me. To his uncle. To the Guildmaster." My voice, I noted with professional satisfaction, did not waver. "He died in a canal for it. Whatever else we decide tonight, understand that I intend to find who killed him, and I intend to find what he carried, and neither objective is sentimental. Both are housekeeping."

"Housekeeping," Hesperine repeated softly. It was not a question, but I answered it anyway, because she had earned an answer, and because watching her decide how much of her suspicion to spend is one of the few entertainments left to me.

"Dame Vail. Your archive processed forty-one volumes for destruction last month under standard decay protocols. Three of them were Merethi-era. Coincidence is a currency I don't accept, so: either someone is using your burn schedule to hide a search—or someone is using a search to hide a theft. I want to know which. Quietly."

Her expression did not change, which is how I knew I'd struck near the mark. "And if the thief is inside this room?"

"Then they'll appreciate the discretion," I said, "because so am I."

* * *

After they went—Vess first, as always; the twins together, speaking to each other without words the way they do, which I pretend not to find unsettling; Quill last, pausing at the door with a look that meant *your nephew's funeral costs are handled, off-book*—Hesperine stayed.

She does that. It is her single most effective quality. Everyone else leaves when a meeting ends; Hesperine understands that meetings end when *I* say they end.

"You loved him," she said.

"I liked him. Love requires proximity, and I arranged his whole career from a distance precisely so that I would never have the opportunity." I straightened the ledgers on my desk, aligning their edges. "My sister married a fisherman against the family's wishes and died birthing her second child, and I did what I do with liabilities: I absorbed the asset and managed it discreetly. Tam was clever and honest. Do you know what happens to clever, honest people in this Guild, Hesperine? They become surveyors, archivists, couriers. The backbone. The ones who believe the maps mean something. And the ones who believe the maps mean something are the ones who eventually find a margin note, a suppressed chart, a discrepancy between the story and the numbers—and then they either become cynics like us or corpses like him. There is no third door."

"There's a third door," she said.

"Oh?"

"They become him." She nodded toward the window, toward the city, toward the archive across the square. "Whoever has that map now. Whoever Tam trusted with it. Someone down there is holding the most dangerous document in the world and doesn't yet know what it costs. That's a third door."

I considered her. Thirty years she has served this Guild, cataloguing impossible realms with steady hands, and in thirty years she has never once told me something I wanted to hear. It is why she is dangerous. It is why she is useful. The day those two qualities separate, I will have her retired to a coastal property with a generous pension and a short remaining life expectancy, and I will grieve her with complete sincerity, because I am capable of that. I am capable of nearly everything. That is the job.

"Find the third door," I said. "And Hesperine—if it's a ledger-keeper, if it's some gray little man who has spent ten years being invisible—do not underestimate him. Invisible people know every crack in the walls. I was one, once."

"Were you," she said, and it was not a question either, and she left.

* * *

Alone, I unlocked the third drawer of my desk—the ordinary drawer, the boring one, because no burglar alive opens the boring drawer—and took out the thing I keep.

It is not the map. I do not have the map; if I did, half this story would already be over. What I have is smaller. A page, torn from a surveyor's field journal, two centuries old, recovered from the Sack of the Surveyor's Wing by my predecessor's predecessor and passed down through four Guildmasters, each of whom kept it the way I keep it: hidden in plain sight, among receipts.

It is a receipt. That is the joke, if you want one. The founding document of the Cartographers' Guild's modern fortune is an itemized invoice.

*Received of the Provisional Coastal Compact: 40,000 crowns, in consideration of services rendered in the matter of the Merethi Gates, viz.: surveying of approaches; drafting of the binding-charts; and destruction of same, five (5) originals, per attached schedule.*

Five originals. Per attached schedule. I have read that line ten thousand times. Somewhere in my twenties I recited it nightly like a prayer until the words lost their meaning, and then, around my fortieth year, they gained it back, heavier than before. Five charts. We destroyed four and lied about the fifth, because the lie was cheaper than certainty, and every Guildmaster since has inherited the lie the way one inherits a mortgage: you service it monthly, and you pray you die before the principal comes due.

The principal, I judged tonight, has come due.

I put the receipt away and sat for a while in the lamplight, doing sums I already knew the answers to. Tam had found something—perhaps not the fifth chart itself, perhaps only proof it survived, a copy, a tracing, a rumor with salt on it—and he had run it home to uncle Aldous, because in his honest heart he believed the Guild would do the right thing with it. Burn it again. Keep the peace. Keep the lie serviced.

Or perhaps—and this is the thought I turned over longest, the one with teeth—perhaps Tam had learned what the receipt says. Perhaps my nephew, clever and honest, dead in a canal, had spent his last months discovering that his family's fortune and his Guild's soul rested on a broken oath and an unpaid debt, and had decided, in the way of clever honest men, that the debt should be paid.

In which case he hadn't betrayed the Guild at all. He had audited it.

I snuffed the lamp. Outside, rain was starting, and somewhere across the square, in my archive, someone was hiding a map from me, and tomorrow I would begin finding out who, and how much of my Guild they intended to take with them when they ran.

I am not an angry man. Anger is imprecise. But I will tell you what I felt, standing in the dark with two hundred years of stolen quiet around me:

I felt an entry appearing in a ledger. Debit: one nephew. Credit: pending.

And I intended, whatever it cost, to balance the account.
