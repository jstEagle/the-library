## 12. The Tide Market

*Ada*

There is a particular smell to a place that does not officially exist, and I had catalogued it for years without knowing what it was: wet rope, tallow, old iron, and something faintly sweet like fruit rotting in seawater. The Guild's archive of "unverified realms" smelled the same way, and I had always assumed that was mildew. It was not mildew. It was exile.

Brannic led me down through the marsh at low tide, along a channel that was, according to every chart I had ever filed, a sandbar. My feet knew better by the third mile. The ground beneath the mud had been paved once—cut stone, slick with weed, laid in a pattern that repeated every nine paces. Nine. Merethi numerology, if the annotations on the map were anything to go by. They built in nines the way Guild architects build in sevens, and they built to last, which was more than could be said for us.

"Keep your eyes down," Brannic said. His one eye was fixed ahead, doing the work of two. "And your hands out of your coat."

"The map stays in my coat."

"Aye, and that's why your hands stay out of it. A man reaching into his coat in the Tide Market is a man about to sell something or stab someone, and either way folk will want a look." He glanced back at me. "You're selling."

"I'm not."

"You're carrying the one thing everyone under this water has prayed for since their granddams drowned. You're selling whether you mean to or not." He said it mildly, the way he said most things, a man who hauled freight for a living and had learned that weight was just weight until you fought it. "So decide what price you're asking before somebody else decides for you."

I wanted to argue. I had spent ten years being the man who argued gently and lost gracefully, and some habits are load-bearing. But there was nothing to say. I touched the coat's inner pocket—the paper crackled faintly, salt-stiff—and took my hands out again.

The market announced itself before we saw it: light. Not lantern-light, which falls, but light that rose, a green-gold glow coming up out of the marsh ahead of us like dawn arriving from the wrong direction. Then the sound. A market's sound is the same everywhere—I have heard recordings from eleven mapped worlds, and a market is always the same chord struck by different instruments—but this one had a bass note underneath, a slow pulse, rhythmic as surf.

"That'll be the tide-bells," Brannic said, catching my look. "Salvaged off the palace. They ring when the real tide turns, even here, even underground. Folk set their clocks by them." He paused. "Set their *hopes* by them, more like."

We came around a stand of black alders and the Tide Market opened below us in a basin the sea had left behind—a drowned harbor inverted, its basin dry, its quays rising on all sides, and filling the whole bowl of it, stalls and fires and banners and people. Hundreds of people. More than hundreds. Tarpaulins strung between broken pilings, braziers burning driftwood and something bluer than fire should burn, tables laid out with goods I recognized from the impossible ledgers and goods I did not recognize at all. A woman selling bottled voices. A man whose stall was entirely keys, thousands of them, hanging in curtains, none of them labeled. Children running between the legs of adults who were, every single one of them, watching the approaches we had just come down.

Nobody watched the goods. That was my first professional observation, and it was the one that frightened me. In a normal market, eyes go where value is. Here the value was standing in the crowd, looking at itself, waiting.

"They know we're coming," I said.

"They've known someone was coming for two hundred years," Brannic said. "That's different, but it looks the same from up here. Walk easy."

* * *

*Brannic*

I'd been to the Tide Market four times before, and three of those times nobody had tried to kill me, so I figured our odds were fair.

The trouble with Ada Joubert—which is to say, the trouble with soft men from big institutions—is that they think danger announces itself. They think it wears a uniform. Ada came down that quay straightening his coat and keeping his chin at the angle of a man trying to look unremarkable, which is the single most remarkable thing a man can do in a crowd that lives by noticing. I put a hand on his shoulder and steered him toward the fish-stalls, where the smoke is thick and faces get forgiven.

"What are we doing?" he whispered.

"Becoming customers," I said. "Buy a fish. Haggle badly. Nothing says harmless like a man overpaying for mackerel."

He bought a fish. He haggled beautifully, actually, which worried me more—if I'm honest—and the fishwife laughed and gave him a second one free and asked him, casual as you please, where a landsman had learned to count like that.

"Guild ledger-school," Ada said, before his brain could catch his mouth. I felt him realize it a half-second later. To his credit, he kept counting coins. To his detriment, that was all he did.

The fishwife's laugh went away. She didn't shout. She just looked up—not at Ada, past him, at a big fellow by the bell-pole with a scarred jaw and an officer's way of standing still—and made a small gesture with her thumb, like she was flicking water off it.

"Fool," I told Ada, quiet. "Walk. Slow. Toward the far quay."

"What did I—"

"*Slow.*"

They came for us between the smoke and the bell-pole. Four of them, no blades drawn yet, which was worse than blades because it meant they were confident. The scarred-jaw officer stepped into our path with the pleasant blankness of a customs gate.

"Friend of the market?" he said.

"I'm a carter," I said. "This is my fare. We're delivering survey tools to—" and I named a family upcoast that owed me money and wouldn't miss the lie.

"Your fare," he said, "has Guild ink on his fingers." And that was true, God help us; ten years of iron-gall leaves a stain in the whorls that never washes out, and Merethi exiles know Guild ink the way farmers know weather. He reached for Ada's collar, almost gentle. "Just a look at your face, friend. Faces get remembered."

Here's what I know about crowds, having moved cargo through a dozen of them: a crowd is a thing with one mind and no patience, and the mind is mostly fear wearing hope's clothes. When the officer turned Ada's face up to the brazier-light, the crowd didn't need to be told what Guild ink meant. Their grandmothers' stories told them. Somebody shouted "*Cartographer*" the way you'd shout *fire*, and the whole market turned at once, hundreds of faces, and the space around us emptied like water finding a drain.

Then the hands started.

I got two men off Ada by the simple method of being large and unbothered, and I'll not pretend it was skill. But there were too many, and they weren't angry the way mobs are angry—they were *hungry*, which is a colder thing—and a hungry crowd doesn't stop when blood starts. Ada went down against a stall of bottled voices, and glass broke, and a voice spilled out across the cobbles, some dead woman singing in Merethi, high and clear, and the crowd flinched from it like it was an omen, which I suppose it was.

"THE MAP," Ada screamed. "You want the map? I HAVE THE MAP!"

Everything stopped. Even the dead woman's song seemed to hang.

A lane opened through the crowd, and down that lane came a girl—young, barefoot on cold stone in winter, silver-haired, with the kind of stillness that costs more than movement—and the crowd bowed to her. All of them, all at once, like grass under wind. She looked down at Ada, flat on his back among broken bottles, and she did not help him up.

"Show me," she said.

Ada's hands shook getting the map out. I watched him fight the coat pocket like a man fighting his own heartbeat. And when the salt-crusted paper caught the green-gold light, the crowd made a sound I will hear until I die—not a cheer, nothing so clean. A sound like a hunger finally given permission.

The silver-haired girl knelt, took the map from his slack hands, and read it. Her lips moved. Whatever she read, it undid something in her face, and what replaced it was worse than anger.

"He carries it," she said, to the crowd, in the tone of a priest confirming a relic. "A Guild scribe carries the road home." Then, down to Ada, quietly, in trade-tongue: "Get up, little clerk. You're going to meet the last heir of Mereth, and you're going to pray he's merciful, because I am not."

* * *

*Ada*

Let me record, in the spirit of accurate documentation, exactly how terrified I was: I had wet myself slightly, my left hand was bleeding from the bottles, and I was following a barefoot girl through a hostile nation while holding a fish I had paid too much for. Brannic took the fish from me. It seemed important to him. Later I understood—he was giving my hands something to do that wasn't shaking.

They took us down past the bell-pole to a structure that had no business surviving two centuries underwater and then two centuries above it: a customs house, Merethi-built, nines in every arch. Inside, the walls were lined with niches, and the niches held names—thousands of small clay tablets, each inscribed, each dusted and cared for.

"The rolls of the drowned," my guide said, catching me looking. "Every soul the sea took when your Guild pulled the gates shut. Every soul since, waiting. We add to them every year." She smiled without warmth. "There is room."

"I didn't pull any gates shut."

"No. You wrote the ledgers *about* it." She tapped my inked fingers. "Ink drowns too, clerk. Just slower."

At the end of the hall, doors opened onto a chamber lit by a single brazier, and beside the brazier stood a man talking quietly with an old woman, and when he turned, I understood the crowd's hunger completely, because I caught it too.

Sarel. I didn't know his name yet—that came later—but I knew the type from the Guild's own halls: the ones born to believe things, raised on certainty the way other children are raised on bread. He was young-ish, handsome in a worn way, and he looked at me not with hate but with *recognition*, which was infinitely more frightening. Corvane looks at useful people that way.

"You're the courier's successor," Sarel said. "Tam Vessal carried our road home for six months with Guild knives at his back, and he died getting it to the one man in the world boring enough to keep it safe." He stepped closer. "Do you know what you're holding, Ada Joubert?"

My name in his mouth. Of course he had my name; Tam must have told them everything before the end. Still, hearing it spoken aloud in a drowned kingdom's customs house made my skin prickle, because names, in my trade, are addresses. Say a name and you can find a man. Find a man and you can move him.

"A map," I said.

"A *key*. A door. Two hundred years of doors, locked from the landward side, and you walked in here holding the hinge." His voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "The Guild took our kingdom, drowned our dead, and built its fortune on the salvage. Your masters are hunting you with everything they have. And you ask me—what? What do you want, Ada Joubert?"

What did I want? I wanted to be back at my desk with the lamp turned low and Wren arguing with me about cataloguing conventions. I wanted the map to be a record instead of a key. I wanted, most of all, for the answer to this question to be *nothing*, for wanting to be someone else's job.

But I looked past Sarel, through the doorway, at the crowd that hadn't dispersed—still gathered outside in the dark, silent now, hundreds of faces turned toward the customs house, toward me, waiting the way water waits. Not angry anymore. *Certain.* And I understood what Brannic had been telling me on the road, and what Tam Vessal had died not understanding:

The Guild terrified me because it might kill me. This terrified me because it might *need* me, and need is a thing that does not let go.

"I want to understand it first," I said. "Before anyone uses it. Including me."

Sarel studied me for a long moment. Behind him, the old woman murmured something in Merethi, sharp and short, and I would have given a great deal to know what.

"Understand," he repeated, tasting the word. Then he smiled, and it was a good smile, a warm one, and that was the most frightening thing of all. "Good. Because tomorrow, little clerk, we begin your education—and the first lesson is what your Guild buried at Saltbridge, and why my grandmother's bones are still knocking on a door your masters welded shut."

Outside, the tide-bells began to ring.
