## 13. The Sea-King's Heir

They blindfolded me on the boat, which I thought was a bit theatrical until I realized it was also practical. You can't describe a route you didn't see. The Cartographers' Guild teaches its apprentices that in the first year: *the eye is the first instrument of the map, and the first thing to be confiscated.* I'd catalogued four hundred treatises on the ethics of surveying before I ever saw open water. It turns out the ethics are simpler in practice. Somebody puts a sack over your head and says, don't.

The boat smelled of tar and old fish and, underneath both, salt that was too clean — sea-salt without harbor in it. Brannic sat beside me for most of the crossing and said nothing, which was his way of being kind. His one eye had done a lot of talking back at the market, all of it apologetic, none of it reassuring.

"They'll want to look at ye," he'd said, as the exiles closed around us with knives they hadn't quite put away. "Lookin's not killin'. Usually."

*I usually* is not a phrase that soothes a man whose last three days have included an assassin, a river barge, and a roadside lesson in Merethi script from a carter who learned it from a grandmother who claimed to come from under the water. But Brannic had fed me, hidden me, lied to armed men about my name with a straight face, and bled a little doing it. So when he said *usually*, I decided to believe him, the way you decide to believe a rope bridge will hold: not because of evidence, but because the alternative requires more energy than you have left.

The boat grounded. Hands took my arms — not rough, exactly, but businesslike, the grip of people who move prisoners often enough to have stopped thinking about it as anything unusual — and walked me up what felt like a shingle beach, then stone, then stairs cut so deep into rock that each step echoed differently. Cold air came down around us like water finding its level.

Then the blindfold came off, and I understood why they bothered with boats at all.

We were inside a sea cave that someone had spent generations turning into a hall. Not built — *carved*, or perhaps grown; the walls had the flowing, layered look of things the water itself had made before anyone thought to live inside them. Lamps burned in niches, and their light was green-gold, filtered through some mechanism I couldn't see, so that the whole hall moved gently, the way light moves in shallow water over sand. Banners hung from the ceiling, heavy with damp. They were blue-black, and embroidered on them in silver thread was a device I knew from the map's margins: a gate, half-open, with a wave passing through it.

Mereth. The drowned kingdom. Not a story in a dead language. A waiting room.

"You're staring," Brannic murmured.

"I'm cataloguing," I said. "It's how I stare."

He snorted. Then the hall noticed us, and there wasn't room in the air for anything else.

They came out of side passages and alcoves, maybe forty of them, maybe sixty — exiles, descendants of exiles, people who had waited two hundred years for this night without knowing if it would come. And every one of them looked at the satchel at my hip where the map rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and every one of them looked at *me*, and I have never in my life been so thoroughly seen. Ten years in the Guild archive, invisible, unremarked, one ledger-keeper among ninety. Now sixty pairs of eyes, each pair containing a drowned grandmother, fixed on me with a hunger I could feel physically, like heat off a forge.

I want to say I stood up under it. What I actually did was think, very clearly: *these people are going to get me killed, and the worst part is they'll feel entirely justified.*

A woman in a grey coat stepped forward and held out her hand for the satchel. She had the bearing of someone accustomed to obedience and the patience of someone accustomed to waiting for it.

"The heir will see you," she said. "The map stays with you until she asks for it. Those are her orders." Something flickered across her face — distaste, maybe, at her own orders. "She wants to see whether you flinch when we ask."

"And do I flinch?"

"We're about to find out."

* * *

Sarel received me in a smaller chamber behind the great hall, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe her accurately, because everything I might write about her will sound like either propaganda or poetry, and she was neither.

She was younger than I expected. Late twenties, maybe. Dark hair cut short and practical, hands stained with the same green-gold lamp-oil as everyone else's, wearing a coat that had been mended more than once and well. She was sitting on a bench sharpening a knife when I was brought in, and she finished the stroke she was on before she looked up, and when she looked up, she smiled, and the smile was the trap.

Not a false smile. That's what made it a trap. It was warm and tired and genuinely glad to see me, and I felt it land somewhere under my sternum and begin working, and some cold professional corner of my mind — the part trained by ten years of Guild ledgers, where every generous figure comes with a footnote — said: *watch this.*

"Ada Joubert," she said. My name, spoken correctly, with the accent of someone who'd practiced it. "Ledger-keeper, third rank, Cartographers' Guild. Eleven years' service. Catalogued six thousand four hundred items, flagged nine hundred and twelve anomalies nobody above you ever read your reports on." She set down the knife. "You wrote to the Surveyor-General twice suggesting the Guild re-examine the drowned coast charts. Both times you were thanked and ignored."

"How did you—" I stopped, because the answer was obvious and awful. "The courier."

"Maren." Sarel's voice changed on the name, just slightly, a door opening onto a colder room. "He read everything he carried. He was supposed to. We chose him partly because he remembered what passed through his hands. Your reports were among the few things in that archive worth remembering." She rose. Up close she was shorter than me, broader through the shoulders, with the calloused hands of a working sailor rather than a court. "You found things, Ada Joubert. In the margins. Things the Guild buried. You found them and you *wrote them down* and sent them up the chain, knowing nothing would happen, because writing them down was the only honest act available to you."

"That's a very flattering reading of a very boring career."

"It's the reading Maren gave me the week before he died." She crossed to a table where a single lamp burned and gestured for me to sit opposite her, and I did, because refusing seemed like more effort than sitting. "So. Show me."

My hand went to the satchel strap before my mind agreed to anything. I made myself stop. Made myself breathe. Somewhere in the last three days I had crossed from a man who hides maps into a man who decides who sees them, and I still hadn't held a ceremony for the transition.

"Why?" I said. "Before I show you anything — tell me what you intend to do with it. Because I've read the annotations. I know what the map opens. And I've spent three days listening to your people talk about gates and tides and debts, and every conversation has had the same shape as a Guild requisition form, and those forms end with somebody's village on the wrong side of the ledger."

For a moment something moved behind her face — pain, or its well-managed cousin — and then she did the thing that I will never fully forgive her for, because it worked.

She laughed. Short, genuine, surprised out of her.

"A requisition form," she said. "Yes. All right. You want the truth in plain figures? Here it is."

She turned the lamp toward herself, and in its light I saw what I'd missed: a thin scar running from her jaw down beneath her collar, and older ones on her knuckles, and eyes that were not hungry the way her followers were hungry. Hers were worse. Hers were *grieving*.

"Two hundred years ago my family ruled a kingdom of three million people," she said. "Fishers, farmers, weavers, children. The Guild came with treaties and surveys, and my ancestor signed, because the treaties promised protection and the surveys promised accuracy, and both promises were lies written by the best liars money can train. When the Guild sealed the gates, they sealed them from *their* side. Do you understand what that means? Not a wall against invasion. A lid on a pot. The tide-storm that drowned Mereth wasn't an accident of nature. It was arithmetic. They calculated how much water, through which channels, and they let it fall."

"I've read the Guild version," I said quietly. "The war of the deep succession. The tragic necessity."

"The Guild version cost them nothing and paid for everything. Every chart house in the coastal cities, every survey monopoly, every guildhall with marble in it — paid for out of a kingdom's drowning." Her voice stayed level. That was somehow the worst part. She had rehearsed this grief until it could be delivered calmly, which meant she had needed it calm, which meant she had been delivering it to rooms full of people for years. "And the survivors washed ashore with nothing but stories, and the stories thinned with every generation, and now there are perhaps a few thousand of us left who know the words, and we are dying in exile on coasts that were built on our grandparents' roofs. That is the account, Ada Joubert. That is the sum. And you hold the document that proves it and unlocks the remedy, and you ask me what I intend to do with it."

"Yes," I said. "That is precisely what I'm asking. Because the remedy drowns Grayharbor. And Tarnmouth. And the fishing villages along the whole eastern coast. Three million people, give or take. I did the sums on the barge." I kept my voice flat too. Two could play at rehearsed calm. "Your account balances only if their side of the ledger is blank."

Something hardened in her face then, and I thought: *there it is,* the fanatic under the grief, the moment the knife comes out. I braced.

Instead she said, "You think I don't know that?"

And the hardness wasn't fanaticism. It was exhaustion. Deep, structural, load-bearing exhaustion, the kind you find in bridge pylons.

"You think I lie awake counting lanterns?" she went on. "Grayharbor at night, from a hillside, two years ago. I stood there and counted the lights and tried to make them into numbers I could carry. I couldn't. There were too many. Children's windows. Night-fishermen. A baker, probably, up early, because bakers are always up early." Her jaw tightened. "I know exactly what opening the gates costs. I am the only person in either camp who has done the arithmetic honestly, and I will tell you the conclusion I reached, and then you can decide what kind of fool you think I am."

The lamp hissed. Water dripped somewhere far off in the rock, keeping time.

"My people demand the gates open," Sarel said. "They have demanded it for two centuries, and they have earned the right to demand it, and if I told them no, they would find someone else to lead them, someone angrier, someone whose grandmothers' stories are fresher and whose arithmetic is worse. So I have spent six years walking a line between a debt that must be paid and a payment that must not be made in innocent blood. And I have concluded this: the gates should open. Slowly. Under control. On terms the coastal cities agree to, with warning, with evacuation, with treaty — a new treaty, a real one, signed by both sides this time instead of forged by one. The sea takes back what was taken, yes. But taken *back*, not taken *again*. Nobody drowns. Mereth rises into a world that knows what was done to it and has agreed, in ink, to make it right."

I stared at her.

Because here is the terrible thing, the thing I have not told Wren, have not written in any margin: it was a good plan. It was a *reasonable* plan. It was the plan I would have drafted myself if someone had put a blank page in front of me and asked what justice looks like when it has to share a coastline with mercy. And she spoke it plainly, without performance, in a lamplit cave to a frightened clerk with salt water still drying in his boots, and every word of it landed in me like rain on ground that had been dry for eleven years.

I wanted to believe her. God help me, I wanted it the way the drowning want a rope — instantly, totally, without checking the knots. Ten years of invisible careful life, ten years of flagging anomalies nobody read, and here at last was a cause with my own handwriting already in it: *the Guild buried this; someone should have said; here, finally, is someone saying.* Sarel of Mereth looked at me across a lamp and offered me the one thing Corvane's Guild never had: a use for my honesty that didn't end in a furnace.

"You're doing it again," she said softly.

"What?"

"The cataloguing face. You've gone somewhere in your head and you're filing me." She leaned forward. "File this, then. I don't need your belief, Ada Joubert. Belief is cheap; my camps are full of it. I need your *craft*. That map isn't a key the way a crowbar is a key. It's a key the way a legal seal is a key — it works through precision, and precision is a trade, and you're the first person in two hundred years with the trade and the object in the same room who isn't sworn to Corvane." She held out her hand, palm up. Open. Asking, not taking. "So. Show me."

And my hand went to the satchel again, and this time my mind went with it, and I got the buckle half-open before the cold corner spoke up — late, always late, but speaking:

*Nobody offers a clerk this much respect for free. Ask what she isn't saying.*

I stopped. Looked up at her. She watched me with that patient, load-bearing expression, and I realized I was seeing it clearly for the first time: the exhaustion wasn't only grief. Part of it was the specific exhaustion of a person holding two truths apart by main strength, and I had no way of knowing how far apart, or how long her arms would last.

"One condition," I said.

Her eyebrow rose. Around us, I sensed rather than saw, guards shifted.

"I show you the map," I said. "But I keep it. And before anything opens — anything, ever — you sit down with me and we go through the annotations together, every line, including the parts in the old script that even your scholars may have softened in the retelling. Because I've read them, Sarel. All of them. And there's a passage about the binding of the gates that your people quote as a promise, and when I translate it literally, it isn't a promise. It's a warning with the grammar of one."

For the first time since I'd entered, the Sea-King's heir went completely still.

"What does it say," she said, "literally?"

I unbuckled the satchel the rest of the way, drew out the salt-crusted roll of a dead man's final delivery, and laid it on the table between us, between the lamp and the knife.

"'That which was bound with blood,'" I quoted, "'can only be loosed by blood — and the blood it remembers is not yours.'"

Outside, somewhere up through the layers of rock, the tide turned, and the whole cave seemed to exhale.

Sarel looked at the map for a long moment. Then she reached past it, took the lamp, and turned the wick low, and in the sudden half-dark her voice came quiet and stripped of everything I'd heard in it before — no charisma, no grief, just a young woman asking a question she suddenly badly wanted the answer to.

"Then before anything else," she said, "you'd better teach me to read it properly. And Ada Joubert — start with the part about what the gates remember. Because if that blood isn't mine—"

She stopped. Even in the dimness I saw her arrive at it, the same place I'd arrived on the barge, alone, at midnight, with the script swimming in front of my eyes.

"—then it's the Guild's," she finished. "It's always been the Guild's. And they know it. Don't they. That's what Maren died carrying."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the tunnel behind her, and from the direction of the great hall, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of horns — the exile signal I'd been taught on the boat, the one that meant *company on the water.*
