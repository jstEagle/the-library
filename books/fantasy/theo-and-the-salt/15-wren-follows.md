## 15. Wren Follows

The Guild taught me that every hunt begins with paper. Corvane says it too, in his soft voice, to every new surveyor: *You will not find the world by walking it. You will find it in the ledgers.* He's right, of course. He's always right, which is the most exhausting thing about him.

So I began with paper.

Three days after Ada Joubert vanished from the city—and I want it noted, even if only in my own head, that "vanished" is a word I have never once used for a Guild man before—three days after he vanished, I sat in the archive with a lamp and a cup of cold tea and did what I do best. I read.

The courier who died on the archive floor had lodged at the Salt Door, a flophouse by the river where the beds were cheap and the questions cheaper. I'd already walked that room. Salt rings on the floorboards, drowned-man carvings scratched into the sill, a Merethi exile token under the mattress slats—brass gone green, stamped with the coiled gate-sigil that half the coastal pawnshops wouldn't touch and the other half sold to collectors who didn't know what they were buying. I reported all of it to Corvane. I reported everything except the one thing that mattered: whose hands had been shaking when I found him crouched over the courier's body like a man caught praying over something he'd stolen.

Ada Joubert. Ledger-keeper. Ten years of service. A man so unremarkable that when I tried to remember his face without looking at the personnel rolls, I got a blur with careful eyes.

That was the lie I was telling myself, anyway. I remembered his face fine. That was the problem.

* * *

Here is what the official record said about Ada Joubert, and here is how I knew it was wrong:

Item one: his requisition history. Ten years, and the man had requested exactly four things beyond standard issue. A magnifying loupe, second grade. A lamp with an adjustable wick—the kind archivists use for reading water-damaged vellum. A copy of Hessel's *Margins and Marginalia*, which is a boring book unless you are the sort of person who thinks about what people write in the edges of maps. And a river chart of the Vael basin, dated nine years ago, marked *catalogued, no further interest.*

No further interest. Except he'd kept it. Except when the enforcers inventoried his desk they found the chart folded into his coat lining, worn soft at the creases, with pencil marks along the river route that nobody makes unless they've already imagined leaving.

Item two: the false ledger. The enforcers burned three volumes the night he disappeared, hunting the courier's map. They burned the wrong ones—I'm certain of it now, though I wasn't certain then. The gap in the shelf where the ledger should have been was dusted clean around its edges, the way a space stays clean when the thing that filled it was removed recently and carefully, not seized in panic. Someone took that ledger out before the enforcers arrived. Someone hid it well enough that men who burn books for a living couldn't find it.

I knew Ada Joubert's hand. Every surveyor knows the archivists' hands; we live off their cataloguing. His was small, exact, faintly fussy about descenders. And in the margin of the requisition roll, next to the entry for the river chart, there was a tiny notation I'd never noticed until I went looking for it: a single salt-ring symbol, drawn so lightly it might have been an ink blot.

Might have been. Wasn't.

* * *

Corvane gave me the assignment over tea, because Corvane does everything over tea, as if the whole world were a meeting that could be adjourned.

"The Joubert matter," he said, pouring for me himself, which he only does when he wants something he can't order. "You knew him."

"I catalogued against his ledgers for years." True. Carefully true. "He was quiet."

"They usually are, the ones who turn." He handed me the cup. "There's a current of old Merethi money moving downriver. Exile tokens changing hands at three times their weight. Someone is organizing, and someone took our map to organize them with. I want you on the river road. Quietly. You have a talent for being where you're supposed to be, Wren."

"Thank you, Guildmaster."

"And Wren—" He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't. "If you find him, don't be gentle. Gentle is how the map gets burned or worse, sold. Bring back the map and the man, in whatever order preserves them best."

*Whatever order preserves them best.* I turned that phrase over the whole ride downriver, and it never once stopped tasting like a threat dressed in a kindness.

Because here is the truth I carried out of that office and could not put down: I wasn't going to find Ada for the Guild. I told myself I was—that duty was duty, that the Guild raised me from nothing, fed me, trained me, made me the woman who could walk into any room in any mapped realm and be believed. I owed it everything. But debts are supposed to feel like debts, and this one felt like a leash, and I have never once in my life confused the two.

Officially, Surveyor Wren Halloway was hunting a traitor who stole Guild property.

Unofficially, I checked my pack four times before I left, and three of those checks were making sure the little tin of salve was still there. Ada Joubert used to get ink-cuts on his fingers from turning vellum too fast, and he never once treated them, and I used to leave the tin on his desk without a note because a note would have been a conversation and neither of us was ready for one. Nine years of leaving salve on a desk. If that isn't love, it's at least the shape of it, and shapes can be followed.

* * *

It took me six days to find the tide market, and I'll say plainly that it took me six days because it wanted to take longer.

I won't describe the route—not in my head, not anywhere—because some knowledge is a debt you pay by keeping it. What I'll say is this: the last stretch runs through salt marsh at low water, along a channel that appears on no Guild chart I've ever seen, and the reason it appears on no chart is that the marsh itself disagrees with charts. I passed three survey markers I'd set myself two years ago on a coastal commission, and all three were standing in the wrong places, moved just enough to send a follower into the channel at rising tide. Someone had edited my work. Someone had looked at my careful lines and decided the truth needed adjusting, and they were right, and that frightened me more than any ambush would have.

The market sits in a drowned quarry that fills and empties with the tide, which means it exists roughly half of every day and negotiates about the rest. When I came down the ramp at evening slack water, lanterns were lighting themselves along the quarry walls—or being lit, by hands I couldn't see—and boats I had no names for were tying up at stairs that surfaced only for them. And everywhere, everywhere, salt. Salt in rings, salt in lines, salt ground into the stone so deeply it wasn't decoration anymore but geology.

I had a cover story. I always have a cover story. I was a broker out of Greyharbor, interested in pre-Drowning artifacts, carrying letters of introduction I'd forged myself with a Merethi script hand I'd practiced for two days off a rubbing of the exile token. It was good work. It would have held.

It didn't need to hold, because I'd been recognized within ten minutes.

Not by the guards—they nodded me through, squinting at my forged letters with the bored suspicion of professionals. By a carter. One eye, gray stubble, shoulders like a dock crane, sitting on an upturned crate near the fish-stalls eating something out of a cloth with the total commitment of a man who has known real hunger. He looked up as I passed, and his single eye went over my boots, my hands, the way I stood with my weight balanced for a draw, and then he laughed.

"Well," he said. "They sent a proper one after all."

Every instinct I had said keep walking. I stopped.

"Do I know you?"

"No." He wiped his fingers on his shirt, unhurried. "But I know the Guild when it walks past me in a forged coat. No offense meant. It's the boots. Guild-issue, resoled twice, left heel worn outside. You people walk like you're following a line even when there isn't one." He patted the crate beside him. "Sit down, surveyor. Or don't, and I'll whistle, and then you'll find out how many folk in this market owe the Guild a dead relative. Your choice. But sitting's free."

I sat. It seemed important to establish, early, that I could follow instructions without panicking.

"Brannic," he said, offering a hand like a knuckle-wrapped plank. "Carter. Grandmother came from under the water, if you believe the stories, and I do, because she told them herself."

"Wren."

"I know." He grinned, and it made his ruined face almost handsome. "Wren Halloway. The little bird what follows. You've a reputation downriver already—came ahead of you, the way reputations do. Folk said the Guild's best hound was on the scent." He leaned in, and the grin dropped away like a mask slipping. "So here's my question, and answer it honest, because I'll know: are you here for the map? Or are you here for the man?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling for eleven days, asked flat-out by a stranger over fish guts, and I heard myself answer before I'd decided to:

"The man."

Brannic studied me for a long moment. Then he sighed, wrapped up his cloth, and stood.

"Aye," he said. "Thought so. He talks about you, you know. Badly. Like a man describing weather he doesn't understand." He started down toward the water stairs, and I followed, because there was never any chance I wouldn't. "Come on, then. Bargaining time. And fair warning, surveyor—I drive a hard bargain, and tonight the price is trust."

* * *

We talked on the middle stair, where the water lapped a hand's width below our boots and the lanterns couldn't quite reach, which suited us both. Brannic laid it out the way carters lay out freight: plainly, with the weight marked.

"He's here. Has been four days. Sleeping in Sarel's household, eating exile bread, asking questions till folk want to drown him just for quiet." He watched my face carefully as he said it. "He came to us half-dead with a knife-cut and a stolen survey kit, and the first thing he asked wasn't for help. It was whether we'd read the map yet. Whether anyone had *used* it. Man's been guarding that thing like it bites."

"It does," I said. "Probably."

"Aye, well. Sarel wants it opened. You know that much?"

"I know what the map is. I've read Marginalia. I can do arithmetic."

"Then you know opening it drowns Greyharbor, and Vaelmouth, and maybe the town your Guild raised you in." Brannic shrugged, enormous and unbothered. "Sarel says the debt comes due. My gran said the sea keeps accounts too, but she also said accounts aren't the same as justice. Two different ledgers, she called them. Never trusted a man who mixed them up."

"So what's the bargain?" Because he hadn't brought me here to reminisce, and we both knew it.

Brannic turned and looked at me full-on, and his one eye was very steady.

"You go back to your Guildmaster and you report what you saw. Tide market, exiles, Merethi tokens, all of it true—you give him true things, because a lie from Wren Halloway he'd smell in a heartbeat. But you don't report the route in. You don't report the numbers. And you don't report where Joubert sleeps." He held up a hand before I could speak. "Before you tell me you can't—think on what you already done. You had his name in your first report, weeks back, and you left it out. Corvane knows you left it out. He let you come down here anyway, which means either he trusts you more than he trusts anyone, which I doubt, or you're the leash he's using to pull the dog home. Either way, surveyor, you're already halfway to treason. Cheapest thing now is to pick which side of the line you're standing on."

The water lapped. Somewhere above us, the market noise swelled and fell like breathing.

"And what do you give me?" I asked.

"Proof," Brannic said simply. "When the time comes—and it's coming, the Guild's not going to sit on this forever—you'll need to stand in front of someone, maybe the whole rotten Guild, and show them why the gates stay shut. Joubert's found something in the old archives. Something about an oath. I don't understand the half of it—gran's script, not mine—but he showed me enough to make an old carter sick to his stomach about what his Guild really did two hundred years back." He tapped his chest. "I carry the proof of that. Copies. Gran's journal besides. And when you're ready to choose—not now, I'm not asking now, but when you're ready—you come to me, and I'll put it in your hands."

"And if I report everything instead?"

"Then I'll have misjudged you," Brannic said, "and I'll be sorry about it, and so will you. That's the bargain. That's all bargains ever are—two folk betting on each other." He spat thoughtfully into the black water. "Joubert bet on you too, you know. First night he was here, feverish, knife in his ribs, and Sarel's people asking him who to watch for from the Guild, and do you know what he said?" Brannic shook his head slowly. "Said, 'Wren Halloway. Watch her. She's the only one who'll do the right thing.' Couldn't tell us if the right thing was catching him or saving him. Said you'd know."

I stood there on the wet stair with my Guild-issue boots in Guild-forged cover and felt something in my chest come loose that I'd been nailing down for eleven days.

"When does the tide come in?" I asked, because I needed to ask something practical before I did anything stupid.

"Two hours," Brannic said. "Market floods. Everyone clears out or goes under." He paused. "He'll be at the eastern wall tonight. Sarel's showing him the gate-charts. If you was the type to break a bargain before the ink dried—which you ain't, or you'd have whistled my guards down already—that's where you'd go."

* * *

I climbed the eastern wall as the market below began its nightly negotiation with the sea, and I told myself the whole way that this was reconnaissance. Official Guild business. Locating the asset, assessing the asset, preparing to retrieve the asset in *whatever order preserved them best.*

Lantern-light spilled up from the gate-house below, and there were voices—one low and certain, Sarel's, doing what leaders do to doubters; and one answering, hesitant, precise, arguing points of cartographic principle with the heir of a drowned kingdom while the tide crawled up the walls.

I knew that voice. I'd spent years listening to it across catalogue desks, correcting its grammar in reports, hearing it mutter over margins. I'd have known it underwater. I think I'd have known it drowned.

I stood in the dark above them with my hand nowhere near my weapon, and I did the arithmetic Brannic had left me. Go down there, and I'm a deserter by dawn. Report back, and I'm the leash. Stay here, and I'm a coward, and cowards are just traitors who waited.

Below me, Ada said something I couldn't catch, and Sarel laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and I thought: *she trusts him. They all do. The invisible man walked into a kingdom of exiles and became visible in four days.*

The first wave broke white against the seaward rocks, and the market lights began going out one by one below, doused for the flood.

I turned back toward the mainland and started walking, because I had a report to deliver, and it was going to be true, and it was going to omit exactly one thing, and I already knew—with the terrible clarity of a woman reading her own coastline for the first time—that Corvane would see the omission the way I'd seen the salt-ring in Ada's margin.

Small. Light. There, if you went looking.

And everyone worth fearing goes looking.
