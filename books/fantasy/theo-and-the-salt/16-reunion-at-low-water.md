## 16. Reunion at Low Water

*Ada*

The tide market kept its own hours. When the sea went out past the drowned pilings, the stalls opened; when it came back in, everyone pretended they'd never been there at all. I had learned to tell time by the smell of exposed mud—brine and rot and something older underneath, like a cellar that had been flooded for two centuries and never quite dried out.

I was counting crates of bottled fog for Brannic's cousin when I saw her.

Wren Halloway stood at the mouth of the market lane with her hood down, which was either a courtesy or a message: *I am not hiding from you.* She wore traveling leathers instead of Guild grey, and she carried no visible weapon, which meant she carried at least three invisible ones. Ten years of cataloguing maps teaches you to read terrain. Wren was terrain. I knew every contour of her face, every ridge and valley, and right now all of it was drawn carefully blank—the way surveyors draw coastlines they haven't been permitted to chart.

Every sensible part of me said run. The mudflats were wide, the fog was thick, and I'd escaped one Guild assassin already by knowing a staircase that wasn't supposed to exist. But my feet didn't listen. My feet, apparently, had opinions of their own, and their opinion was Wren.

"You're supposed to be in Corvane's office," I said, when I got close enough. "Ink-stained. Boring. Safe."

"And you're supposed to be dead in the archive," she said. "We've both disappointed people."

Her voice was level. Her eyes weren't. They moved over me like hands checking for wounds—and they found them. The burn on my forearm from the night the assassin came. The bruise along my jaw from Saltbridge, though she couldn't know about Saltbridge yet, could she? No. From the barge. From falling off a barge like an idiot.

"You've been eating?" she asked.

"Is this an arrest?"

"It's a question about your diet, Ada."

"Wren." I said her name and heard everything in it I hadn't meant to say. "If you're here under orders—"

"I'm here because I walked away from my orders four days ago and told myself it was a reconnaissance." A ghost of her old smile, the one she used when a map disagreed with itself. "I'm very good at lying to myself. You may have noticed. It took me ten years to notice you."

Around us the market went about its business with the studied indifference of people who survived by not seeing things. A woman sold eels that glowed faintly. A boy hawked charms against drowning, guaranteed effective or your money refunded posthumously. Nobody looked at us twice. That should have comforted me. It didn't. Comfort required believing the world was still shaped the way I remembered it, and I'd stopped believing that somewhere around the moment a dying man pressed salt into my palms.

"Come with me," Wren said. "There's a place up the shingle line. An old pilot's hut. We can talk without an audience."

"Talk," I repeated. "That's what we're calling it."

"What would you call it?"

"I don't know yet. I've spent the last month being hunted by my own employer. My vocabulary has shrunk considerably."

She turned and walked, trusting me to follow, and that—that was the cruelest thing she could have done. Because I followed. Of course I followed. I had followed her for ten years in smaller ways: her route through the archive, her taste in terrible tavern wine, the way she tapped a pen twice before committing ink to paper. Running after her across a tidal flat was only the most literal version yet.

* * *

*Wren*

The pilot's hut smelled of tar and old rope and someone else's cigarettes, but the door shut, and there was a lamp, and there was Ada Joubert standing inside it looking like a man who had been chased through his own life.

I had imagined this meeting perhaps forty times since leaving the city. In some versions I arrested him. In some I warned him and left before he woke. In one particularly humiliating version I burst into tears, which had happened exactly once in my career, during a cartographic dispute over the Ferrow Estuary, and I did not intend to repeat it.

What I had not imagined was how quiet he'd be. Ada had always been quiet—it was his defining feature, the way stillness defines deep water—but this was different. He'd been quiet like a man keeping his head down. Now he was quiet like a man who had decided noise was expensive.

"The courier," I said. "Tell me what happened. All of it. Not the archive's version."

"Why?"

"Because I've spent three weeks pulling threads, and every thread ends either in your silence or in a grave. The courier's lodging had salt rings burned into the floorboards. Merethi carvings. An exile token stamped with a tide-crown. And then the enforcers burned three ledgers from our own archive—ledgers I helped catalogue, ledgers that had nothing wrong with them except age." I pulled off my gloves finger by finger. "Ada. The Guild is killing its own people over whatever you're carrying. I need to know if you're guilty or if you're next."

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the tide was turning; I could hear it filling the channels, patient as arithmetic.

"You reported the lodging," he said. "To Corvane."

"I reported the lodging. I did not report what else I found there." My voice came out lower than I intended. "There was a ledger page in the hearth, half-burned. Inventory notation. Archive hand. Yours."

His face did something complicated and then settled into something worse, which was resignation.

"So you've known since before you left the city."

"I've known since before you ran." I set the gloves down. "And I sat on it. Do you understand what that means? Sitting on evidence is treason, Ada. Small-t treason, the kind you can maybe walk back, but treason. I committed it because—" I stopped, because finishing that sentence honestly required a second sentence, and the second sentence was *because I love you,* and I had planned to say it at a better moment, ideally one involving wine and neither of us wanted by two armies.

"Because you wanted to be sure first," he offered.

"Because I wanted to be sure first," I agreed, gratefully cowardly.

He laughed—a short, cracked sound, nothing like his real laugh, which I had spent years mining for in the archive's dusty corners like water in a dry country. Then he reached into his coat, and my whole body tensed, hand going to the knife I'd promised myself I wouldn't need, but what came out was wrapped in oilcloth, rectangular, and heavy with the particular gravity of a thing that changes everything near it.

He unwrapped it on the table between us.

The map of the drowned kingdom. Salt-stained, annotated in a script I recognized from exactly one place: the sealed case in the archive's deepest vault, the one apprentices were told contained plague records. Merethi. The language of a kingdom the Guild had helped drown two hundred years ago, and had spent those same two centuries pretending had never existed.

"There it is," I said quietly.

"There it is."

"May I?"

He hesitated. I watched him hesitate—I watched him weigh me, this man who had trusted me with his terrible handwriting and his worse tea and his whole unremarkable heart, weighing whether ten years outweighed one month of running—and then he turned it so it faced me and stepped back.

I bent over it the way I bent over any document, professionally, thoroughly, and it defeated me within a minute, because it wasn't a record. The coastline moved when I looked away and came back slightly wrong. The annotations weren't describing the kingdom. They were *instructing* it. And threaded through the ink, fine as hair, ran lines of salt that caught the lamplight and seemed, briefly, to move.

"This isn't cartography," I said. "This is a key wearing a map's clothes."

"Now you understand why everyone wants it."

"Everyone wants lots of things. I want to know what it opens."

"The gates of Mereth." He said it flatly, the way you read a casualty list. "And opening them brings the kingdom back. And bringing the kingdom back drowns every coastal city built on its ruins. Greyharbor. Tarnmouth. Half the salt trade. Thousands of people who have never heard of Mereth and wouldn't care if they had."

I looked up at him. "And you've had this in your coat for a month."

"I've had it in my coat for a month," he agreed, "and I have slept approximately none."

Something in my chest loosened and hurt at the same time. Relief is a strange wound. He hadn't opened them. He hadn't sold them. He'd run, badly, bleeding, and carried the thing anyway, because burning it apparently hadn't occurred to him or had occurred and been refused, and either answer told me more about him than the last decade of shared workrooms had.

"Corvane knows I'm here," I said. "Not where. But he knows I went dark, and he knows why I'd go dark, because Corvane knows everything about everyone. It's his only hobby." I braced my hands on the table, over the impossible map, close enough that our fingers nearly touched. "Which means I have days. Maybe less. And when I go back, Ada, he will ask me where you are. And I will have to lie to the best liar I have ever met, or give you to him."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I don't think you do. I think you've decided I'll choose you, and you've let that decide for you, and that is the most dangerous arithmetic in the world." My voice shook. I hated it. I kept going. "I love you. I have loved you since the year you reorganized the estuary folios by salinity instead of date and I realized I was the only person alive who understood why that was beautiful. So believe me when I say love is not a plan. Love is a weight, and right now it's on the wrong side of the scale."

The lamp guttered. Outside, full dark, the tide coming in over the flats with that long low breath it makes, like the sea turning over in its sleep.

"What's on the other side?" he asked softly.

"Duty. The Guild. Everything I was raised to be." I straightened. "And the truth, which is that I looked into that lodging and into those burned ledgers, and I did the sums, and the Guild isn't protecting the coast from Mereth anymore. The Guild is protecting its books. Two hundred years of profit built on a drowned kingdom, and Corvane will burn anyone who threatens the balance sheet. Including you. Including me, now."

"That's treason talk."

"That's arithmetic talk. One of us had to learn it."

For a moment neither of us spoke, and the hut felt very small and very safe and doomed, all three at once, like a candle in a high wind that hasn't gone out yet.

Then Ada did the last thing I expected. He picked up the map, wrapped it again—and handed it to me.

I stared at it. At him. "No."

"Take it. Hold it. Feel the weight of what choosing means." His eyes were steady and terrified and certain, all at once. "You said love isn't a plan. Fine. Then decide with your hands, not your heart. If you take it to Corvane, thousands live and a kingdom stays drowned and I lose you forever. If you bring it back to me, we find another way—there has to be another way, Wren, a treaty, a binding, something between seal and storm—and we both probably die. I'm not asking you to betray the Guild tonight. I'm asking you to hold the choice until you're sure."

My hands closed on the oilcloth before my mind finished objecting. It was warm. Maps aren't warm. It was warm like a held stone, like a sleeping animal, like—

Like a promise.

"If Corvane's people come," I said, giving it back, because I was sure already and he deserved to know it, "we run together. And Ada—if you ever make me choose again with theatrics, I will drown you myself and save the sea the trouble."

"That," he said, and this time the laugh was almost real, "is the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

It lasted eleven seconds. I counted them later, the way you count everything you failed to protect.

Because through the wall, from the shingle below the hut, came a sound I knew from a dozen drills and had prayed never to hear aimed at me: the click of a Guild signal-lamp shuttering open. Once. Twice. Three times.

*Found them.*

Wren's knife was in her hand before I finished standing. "Back door," she said. "Now. And Ada—whatever you were planning to do with that map, do it faster."

* * *

*Ada*

We went out through the back of the pilot's hut into the dark, and the dark was full of lamps.

They'd spread along the shingle line in a loose crescent—six, seven shapes moving with the unhurried coordination of people who had done this many times and enjoyed it moderately. Guild field pattern, third formation. I'd catalogued the training manual once. It had seemed academic at the time.

Wren's hand found mine in the dark and squeezed once—not tenderness, tactics, *follow me*—and pulled me down toward the flats, away from the crescent's center, toward the one direction they'd left thin because nobody sane runs onto a tidal flat at flood tide.

"Nobody sane," she murmured, reading my thought, "which is why they won't expect it."

The water was around our ankles, then our knees, black and shockingly cold, and behind us a voice called out—one I knew, the enforcer Vask, the one who'd inventoried the archive while it burned—and ahead of us the sea kept rising with the patience of a creditor.

Halfway across the channel, Wren stopped, chest heaving, and looked back at the lamps, and then at me, and made her face into the expression I'd seen her wear over disputed coastlines: the look of a woman about to redraw something.

"They'll follow us onto the flats," she said. "But they won't follow us past the old breakwater. There's a current there the pilots call the Undertow's Hand. It doesn't kill you. It just moves you somewhere else." She gripped the map through my coat, feeling its warmth, its weight, its terrible patience. "Ada. Wherever it takes us—we finish this together. Whatever *this* turns out to be."

The water rose past our waists. Behind us, the lamps came on, one by one, like eyes opening.

And beneath my feet, faint as a heartbeat, the map began to pull.
