## 17. Corvane's Net

There is a particular pleasure in negotiating with desperate people, and I will not pretend I don't enjoy it. Desperation makes people honest. It strips away the tedious theater of strength and shows you exactly what a man wants, which is the only thing about him worth knowing. A calm negotiator is a liar with good posture. A drowning man tells you where the shore is.

The heir of Mereth was not drowning. That was the first surprise of the evening, and I confess it unsettled me more than I showed.

I had expected Sarel of the Drowned Court to arrive at the parley like a knife—angry, grieving, hungry for restitution, easy to bend. Grief is the most workable material in the world. Instead she came to my tent on the salt flats above the tide market like an auditor: three retainers, no weapons displayed, a ledger-scribe of her own taking notes in that beautiful drowned script that looks like rain running down a window. She sat across from me, folded her hands, and said, "Guildmaster Corvane. You've come to offer me something you'd rather die than give. Let's not waste the tide."

I liked her immediately, which is a professional hazard I have learned to guard against.

"Your reputation for directness is earned," I said.

"My reputation is all I have left," she said. "Mereth is a story now. Stories can be edited. So edit carefully."

Her scribe wrote that down too. I wondered whose archive it would end up in.

* * *

Let me be precise about what I brought to that table, because precision is the whole of my craft, whatever my enemies call it.

The Cartographers' Guild holds eleven thousand sealed charts of the Merethi coast, surveyed before the Drowning by surveyors whose descendants still draw our borders. We hold the tide-tables of two centuries. We hold the gate-anchors—the old binding stones pried from the sea-wall and sold, over the years, to fund this Guild's admirable libraries—and we know where every one of them sits today, in private collections, in vaults, in the foundations of three cathedrals built by men who did not ask what they were standing on. We hold, in short, everything needed to open the gates of the drowned kingdom, scattered so widely and buried so deeply that only the Guild could ever gather it again.

We also hold the reason it must never be gathered. But reasons are negotiable. That is what distinguishes a reason from a law.

"You want the gates open," I told Sarel. "You've wanted them open for two hundred years. Your people have bled for it in alleys and salt-flats while the coast grew fat on your kingdom's bones. I am offering to do in one winter what your exiles couldn't do in ten generations."

"And in exchange?"

"The Guild takes salvage rights. Exclusive. Everything raised from Mereth—art, anchor-stone, archive, coin—passes through Guild hands. Your people get their kingdom back. The Guild gets fair compensation for its assistance."

She was quiet for a moment. Her scribe's pen scratched. Outside, the tide hissed against the flats, patient as arithmetic.

"Fair compensation," Sarel repeated. "Say the number."

"There is no number. There is a percentage. Forty percent of assessed value, in perpetuity."

"Forty percent of a drowned nation," she said, "and you call it fair."

"I call it forty percent of nothing, unless I act," I said. "That is what fair means when the alternative is zero. You've spent your life learning what the coast took from you, Heir. I've spent mine learning what it costs to take things back. They are not small numbers on either side."

I watched her hands. People think I watch eyes. Eyes are trained liars; hands are amateurs. Her hands did not move at all, which meant they had been trained too, or that she had already decided and was letting me finish for the courtesy of it.

* * *

Here is what I did not say, and what I will set down here because these pages are the only place I am ever entirely honest, which is itself a kind of corruption but one I can live with:

I did not tell her that opening the gates kills the coast.

Oh, I'm sure she knows. The exiles have sung about the tide-storm since before my grandmother's grandmother; it is their lullaby and their war-hymn both. Open the gates, wake the sea-king, and the water comes for Grayharbor, for Saltbridge, for the fishing towns and the customs houses and the quarter-million souls who live between the old high-tide line and the new one. The exiles call it the Reckoning. They have named their children after it.

But here is the thing Sarel does not know, the thing I had come to trade on: neither side has ever said it aloud to the other. For two centuries the exiles have dreamed of return and told themselves the storm was a rumor, a metaphor, a price someone else had calculated as acceptable. And the Guild has told the coastal cities that the exiles were thieves and madmen, never mentioning that the Guild's own founding charter contains a page describing exactly how high the water would rise. I have read that page. I have read it many times. The number is specific. The cartographers who drew it were very good at their work.

So there we were, the heir of the drowned and the master of the mapmakers, each waiting for the other to say the unsayable, each prepared to hear it and proceed anyway.

That is the true horror of power, if you want my confession, and I suppose these pages exist because some part of me wants to give it: it is never the villains who frighten me. Villains are rare and mostly fictional. What frightens me is sitting across a table from a young woman whose people were murdered by my predecessors, watching her do the same arithmetic I am doing, and realizing that we will both arrive at the same answer, and that the answer is yes.

* * *

"You know what happens to the coast," Sarel said at last.

It was not a question. I gave it the respect of not pretending otherwise.

"I know what the founding surveys say the storm will do," I answered. "I also know that the Guild's own hydrographers believe the modern seawalls change those figures. Two hundred years of engineering. The storm may drown less than the old maps promise." I paused, letting the word *may* do its work. "Or more. Uncertainty cuts both ways, Heir. Certainty is expensive. I'm offering you a way to buy it down."

"How?" she said. "By opening the gates slowly? By drowning Grayharbor politely, district by district, so everyone has time to file complaints?"

"By evacuation," I said. "By warning. By the greatest coordinated withdrawal in coastal history—conducted by the Guild, which controls every harbor chart, every pilot's license, every shipping route on this seaboard. When the gates open, the cities will not be surprised. They will be *moved*. And Mereth rises into an emptied, prepared coastline instead of a massacre." I spread my hands. "That is what I am actually selling you, Heir. Not treasure. Not even the gates. Foreknowledge. The Guild has spent two centuries being the only institution that knows where everything is. Let me spend it once for your benefit."

It was a magnificent lie, and I want it recorded that it was only half a lie, which in my experience is the most durable kind.

The half that was true: the Guild could do it. Evacuation on that scale, guided by our charts, would save hundreds of thousands of lives. No one else could attempt it. In a just world, that capacity would belong to no one—it would simply exist—but the world is not just, it is mapped, and mapped territory belongs to whoever holds the plates.

The half that was false: I would never spend that capacity for her benefit. I would spend it for ours. If the gates opened, the Guild would extract its forty percent from the rising water with the same efficiency we bring to everything, and if the evacuation saved lives it would be because dead customers buy no salvage rights. I am not ashamed of this. Shame is for people with the luxury of principles. I have ledgers instead, and the ledgers balance.

What I felt—not believed, *felt*, in the brief unguarded space behind my sternum—was something worse than either honesty or deceit. Watching her consider it, watching hope move through her face like weather, I felt the vertigo of a man who realizes the bridge he is building is real. Somewhere in my cold little heart, a voice observed: *you could actually do this. You could open the gates and empty the cities and restore a nation and profit handsomely, and history would call it the Corvane Settlement, and children would learn it as a mercy.*

That is the trap I build for others and fell into myself: a deal so well-constructed that doing the monstrous thing becomes indistinguishable from doing the right thing.

I have been in this trade thirty years. I should have known better than to construct a net I couldn't see the edges of.

* * *

Sarel asked to examine the terms. Of course she did. Her scribe laid out our draft agreement and she read it the way I read enemy accounts—twice forward, once backward, hunting the sentence that isn't there. She found three. I had planted seven. A negotiation where the other party finds none of your traps is not a negotiation; it is a funeral.

"Clause nine," she said. "Salvage assessment is conducted by Guild appraisers alone. Who audits the appraisers?"

"The treaty court, once established."

"Which convenes when?"

"After transfer of assets."

"So we surrender the kingdom's bones first and receive justice afterward, from a court your Guild staffs." She tapped clause fourteen. "And here: 'the Guild retains sole custody of all pre-Drowning archives recovered.' My ancestors' letters. Their names. Their graves' locations. You keep the dead and sell me the furniture."

"Archives require climate control, Heir. Conservation. The Guild has facilities—"

"The Guild has *vaults*," she said, "and a two-hundred-year habit of putting Merethi things in them and losing the keys." She looked up, and for the first time I saw the king's blood in her face, the old drowned fury banked down to coals. "Do you know what my people whisper in the tide market, Guildmaster? Not 'open the gates.' Not 'give us back our land.' They whisper *let us bury our grandmothers properly*. That is the whole of it. That is what two centuries of exile reduces to. And your contract prices even that."

I let the silence sit, because silence was the only honest thing left on the table.

Then she did something I had not planned for. She closed the contract, pushed it aside, and said, "Tell me about the courier."

I kept my face pleasant and my pulse disinterested. "Which courier?"

"The one who died in Grayharbor carrying a salt map. My people say a Guild man. Yours say a traitor. Someone is lying, and it is usually both." Her eyes were quite steady. "Was he yours, Corvane?"

There are questions you answer, questions you deflect, and questions that mark the exact boundary of the room you're standing in. This was the third kind. Because the truth—that the courier was Tam Vessin, my sister's son, whom I had sent to verify the map's existence and who had decided, somewhere on the road, that it belonged to the exiles and not to me—is the one fact in this entire affair that cannot survive daylight. Every other crime I have committed or contemplated has a justification with decent posture. That one has only grief, and grief is not load-bearing.

"He was a courier," I said. "He carried things. Now he is dead, and the thing he carried is loose, and both our causes are bleeding because of it. Which suggests, Heir, that whoever holds that map holds leverage over us both. Another reason to conclude our business quickly."

I saw her decide not to believe me. I respected that too. Believing me would have required her to ignore the shape of the lie, and she was, as I have said, an auditor.

"We will need time," she said. "My council must weigh it."

"Of course." I stood, and offered the courtesy of my hand, which she did not take, which was correct. "But weigh it against the tides, Heir. Winter is coming, and with it the storm-season swells. If the gates are to be opened gently—if they are to be opened at all—it must happen before the equinox, or not for another year. And I am told your hardliners grow impatient. Blood rituals make poor diplomacy."

Something flickered across her face at that—quick, quickly mastered, but I am paid to notice flickers. Her own faction was slipping its leash. Good. A negotiator's best ally is the other party's internal chaos.

"I'll send word within the week," she said.

"Do." And then, because I am not entirely made of ledger-paper, I added: "For what it is worth—heir to heir, in the only sense I have anything to be heir to—I am sorry about your kingdom. My predecessors did it. I profit from it daily. Both facts can be true, and neither excuses the other."

She stopped at the tent flap. For a moment she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with age, the tiredness of a woman carrying a nation's ghost on her back and discovering the ghost has opinions about interest rates.

"Keep your sorry," she said. "Send me my dead."

* * *

After she left I sat a long while with the lamp turned low, doing what I always do when the day's performances are over: reading the account of myself.

The deal is sound. If Sarel signs, the Guild gains the largest acquisition in its history, Mereth gains a resurrection, and the coast gains—possibly—a managed retreat instead of a drowning. Three winners out of three parties. On paper, I am a statesman.

On paper. Paper is my native country; I know its customs better than anyone alive, and I know that paper lies by omission. Here are the omissions:

If Sarel signs, the exiles become clients of the Guild forever, tenants in their own resurrection, paying rent on their ancestors' graves until the sun burns out. I have built that arrangement before, with smaller nations. It holds. That is the horror of it—it *holds*.

And if Sarel refuses—if her hardliners force the gates by blood ritual without us—then the storm comes unwarned, the cities drown unwarned, and the Guild's failure to close the deal becomes, retroactively, a choice. I will have stood in a tent with the one person who could prevent a catastrophe, haggling over percentages, and the water will remember.

Either way, the ledger balances. Either way, I come out ahead. That is what I told myself as I snuffed the lamp, and I almost believed it, right up until the moment I remembered the other loose thread. The courier's map is still missing. The ledger-keeper who took it is still running. And somewhere between my tent and the tide market, my enforcers reported that Wren Halloway—my finest surveyor, my most reliable instrument, the woman I would have trusted to chart the edge of the world—has gone silent for six days.

Instruments do not go silent. They break, or they turn.

I called for my secretary and dictated two orders before I slept. The first: find Ada Joubert, and bring me the map, and this time do not fail. The second concerned Wren Halloway, and it was gentler, and gentleness from me should frighten anyone who understands what it is for.

*Bring her in,* I said. *Unharmed. She has been out in the cold too long.*

Outside the tent, the tide came in over the flats, quiet as a closing book.
