## 20. Wren's Report

The trouble with telling the truth, I've found, is that it takes practice. Lies come easy—they're just maps of the world you wish existed, and I was trained to draw those before I could write my own name properly. But a truth with a hole in it, a true report shaped like a lie? That's cartography of a much finer order. One wrong line and everything downstream is wrong too.

I had three days to get it right.

Corvane's enforcer found me at the Saltbridge survey station, which told me two things before he said a word: first, that Corvane knew where I'd be, because someone had read my requisition logs; second, that he wanted me to know he knew. The Guild didn't send people places by accident. Every assignment in the Cartographers' Guild was a sentence in a larger document, and Vess Tallow—that was the enforcer's name, all elbows and patience, a man built like a folded chart—had been sent to deliver his particular clause in person.

"Wren Halloway." He said it like he was confirming a coordinate. "The Guildmaster would like your report on the courier matter."

"The courier matter," I repeated, and made my face do what faces do when a phrase means nothing yet. "The dead man at the archive door?"

"You've been making inquiries."

"I'm a surveyor. Inquiring is the job."

He smiled. It was a good smile, well-mapped, deployed exactly where a smile goes. "You visited the Harrow Street lodging. You spoke to the landlady. You spent forty minutes in the Saltbridge records office asking after tide schedules from two hundred years ago. And then—" he paused, letting the silence carry its cargo, "—you filed nothing. No interim report. No memo. Nothing for eleven days."

"Surveyors file when there's something worth filing."

"And is there?"

There it was. The whole conversation balanced on that question like a bridge over a gorge, and I stood on it with my hands very still.

* * *

Here's what I actually had, laid out in my head the way I'd lay out a survey before drawing it fair:

Item one: the courier's room on Harrow Street had salt rings on the floorboards, seven of them, arranged in a pattern I later found echoed in Guild records from the Mereth suppression—a pattern the old texts called a *listening mark*, used by people who wanted to know if their rooms were entered while they were out. Someone had taught him to watch for us. Which meant the courier had known the Guild would come, which meant he hadn't trusted us, which meant—

Item two: under a loose floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth, a carved token. Bone or whale-ivory, worn smooth by generations of thumbs. A drowned-man figure with the crown knocked off. I'd seen its twin once, years ago, in a confiscated-effects ledger: *Merethi exile token, common grade.* The exiles were real. They weren't stories we told new apprentices to keep them off the deep-water shelves.

Item three: the courier himself. I'd gotten the landlady talking—landladies will talk if you admire their geraniums—and she described a young man who'd stayed five weeks, paid in old coin, kept odd hours, and once, in fever, cried out names in a language she couldn't repeat but mimicked beautifully: sounds like water going backward. Old Merethi. Living speech, not liturgy.

Item four—the item that mattered, the item that sat in my chest like swallowed ballast: none of it pointed away from Ada Joubert, and all of it pointed toward what I'd seen with my own eyes the morning they carried the courier's body out. His shaking hands. The gap in the access ledger, four lines razored out so cleanly it might have been an error, except nothing in our archive was ever an error. And the way Ada had looked at me when I asked him, casually, over lunch, whether he'd slept—like a man checking the depth of water under his keel.

I knew. I had known for eleven days, and I had said nothing, and every day I said nothing the saying-nothing got heavier and more deliberate until it wasn't omission anymore. It was a choice you make again each morning, like getting up, like breathing.

And now Vess Tallow was standing in my survey station asking me to breathe on command.

* * *

"Sit," I said, because if he sat he'd stop looming, and loomed men ask harder questions. "Tea?"

"No."

"Suit yourself." I put the kettle on anyway. Motion helps. My mother used to say a liar's hands betray her first, so keep them busy, and though my mother was many things I don't recommend becoming, she understood performance the way I understood coastlines: professionally.

Vess sat. He did not relax. Men like him never sit; they place themselves.

"The courier matter," I said, pouring. "Let me give it to you properly, since apparently my filing habits have offended the Guildmaster. Subject: unidentified male, mid-twenties, died of blood loss and exposure outside the archive's salt door, night of the ninth. Wounds consistent with a hooked blade. He was carrying no papers—Guild inventory confirmed this within the hour, and Master Corvane has the inventory himself, so I won't insult you by reciting it."

"Go on."

"I followed standard procedure for unattributed deaths on Guild ground." Standard procedure. The phrase is beautiful; it's load-bearing. "I canvassed the district. The body was found near Harrow Street, so I worked outward from there. The landlady at number nine remembered him. Five weeks tenancy, paid irregularly, no visitors, left each night after dark. She thought he was a fishmonger's clerk. He was not a fishmonger's clerk."

"What was he?"

"That," I said, "is the interesting part." I let myself enjoy it, a little, because enjoyment is credible and dread is not. "His room had listening marks cut into the floor. Salt-charged. That's pre-Concord work—it hasn't been standard practice since the Mereth suppression. Whoever trained him learned from the old methods, or from someone who learned from them. And under his floorboard I found this."

I set the token on the table between us.

I want to be honest about that moment, because honesty in retrospect is also a kind of map, and mine should be accurate: I gave him the token deliberately. It was real evidence. It cost me nothing that mattered, and it bought credibility the way good coin buys bread. A report made entirely of gaps is a report that gets audited. A report with one solid find in it gets believed. I watched Vess look at the drowned-man carving and I felt—there's no cleaner word for it—complicit. Not guilty. Guilt is what you feel before you decide. This was after.

"Merethi," he said.

"Exile-grade. Common issue, if the confiscation ledgers are to be believed. Which suggests our courier wasn't a lone fanatic. He had a community. Possibly a network."

"Did you trace the network?"

"I traced the coin." Another true thing, chosen like a stepping-stone. "Old minting, coastal marks, circulated heavily in the south quarter markets. The landlady said he sold something at the Tide Market stalls—"

"There is no Tide Market."

"—the fish stalls along the south quay," I corrected smoothly, "where he bought lamp oil and, per the landlady, 'talked to the wrong sort.' Her phrase. She disapproved of his company without being able to describe it, which is the most useful thing a witness can do."

Vess turned the token over once, twice. His thumbnail found the worn channel where a thumb had rubbed it ten thousand times, and something crossed his face—not recognition, exactly. Recognition's cousin. The look of a man who has seen a coastline from the sea and now sees it from inland, and understands they are the same coast.

"You've held this back eleven days," he said.

"I've been verifying it for eleven days. There's a difference. If I'd filed a rumor, you'd have burned a rumor. Now you have a direction." I leaned forward, and here came the part I'd rehearsed until it lived in my mouth like a native tongue. "My recommendation, which you're free to pass up the chain however you like: the exiles are moving. Something spooked them enough to send a courier into the heart of the archive, and whatever he was carrying, someone killed him for it. We should assume they'll try again. I'd suggest tightening the salt-door watches and pulling the access ledgers for the last month."

I watched that last suggestion land. Pulling the ledgers. Suggesting it myself, so that when the gap in the access book surfaced—and it would surface; Corvane missed nothing—it would surface as my recommendation, already explained, already digested, already old news. You don't hide a reef from a hydrographer. You chart it yourself, name it, and print it before anyone else does.

Vess looked at me for a long time.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, gulls were conducting their usual argument about jurisdiction over the harbor refuse, and I thought, absurdly, of Ada cataloguing impossible realms in his quiet lamplit corner, and how he'd once told me that the difference between a good map and a great one was knowing what to leave off. *A map is a decision,* he'd said, with that shy ferocity he only showed about ink. *Every map is a decision about what deserves to exist.*

I was deciding things. Right now, across this table, I was deciding them.

"You're thorough," Vess said finally.

"I'm a Halloway. The Guild raised me."

"Yes." He rose, pocketed the token, and paused at the door with his hand flat against the frame—the gesture of a man checking a wall for hollows. "The Guildmaster will want the full detail. Verbally. Tonight, I expect. He finds written reports on sensitive matters..." He smiled his mapped smile again. "...incomplete."

"I'll come at eight."

"Do." And then, with his hand still on the frame, without turning around: "The access ledgers. When you pull them—if anything strikes you as irregular—you'll bring that to me directly. Not to the archive staff. To me."

"Of course."

He left. The door clicked shut with the softness of a well-made thing, and I stood in my little station surrounded by my honest charts of honest coasts, and poured the untouched tea down the sink, and put my hands flat on the table to stop them doing what my mother always said they'd do.

* * *

Eight o'clock found me walking the long corridor to Corvane's office, and I will record for the sake of whoever audits this memory that I considered, seriously and at length, turning around.

Not running. Running was for people with something to flee to. But turning around, walking out the salt doors, going down to the harbor, finding a boat, finding Ada, finding Brannic—I'd pieced together that much, the carter with one eye who'd signed a barge manifest two days after Ada vanished, and the manifest's destination was a port that appeared on no chart the Guild maintained, which meant either a clerical ghost or a whole hidden geography, and I am a surveyor, and hidden geographies are the only kind I've ever truly loved—

But Corvane would see through a missing surveyor faster than he'd see through an incomplete report. And worse: if I ran, I confirmed everything, and confirmation would fall on Ada like a verdict. The only protection I could offer him from inside the machine was the machine's own trust in me. So long as Corvane believed Wren Halloway was his instrument, every piece of me that reached Ada arrived disguised.

So I walked in, and I lied by telling the truth, and it went almost perfectly.

Almost.

Corvane received me the way he receives everyone: seated behind a desk of pale wood with nothing on it but a single lamp and a single closed folio, as though the entire Guild's business fit in one folder and he'd already finished reading it. He's not a large man. He doesn't need to be. Corvane occupies space the way a watermark occupies paper—everywhere at once, visible only when you hold him up to the light.

"Halloway." He gestured at the chair. "Tallow speaks highly of your diligence. Eleven days of quiet fieldwork. Unusual initiative for a surveyor."

"Unusual times." I gave him the report again—the dead courier, the listening marks, the token, the coin, the south-quay contacts, my recommendations. I gave it fluently. I gave it with the small imperfections that make testimony believable: one hesitation before the word *exile*, a glance at the window when describing the harbor, a dry note about the landlady's geraniums that earned, God help me, the ghost of a smile from him.

He listened with his fingers steepled, and when I finished he was silent for exactly as long as it took me to notice the silence.

"You found the token under the floorboard," he said.

"On the fourth day of canvassing. Yes."

"And in eleven days of investigation, in a case involving a murdered man, exile networks, and pre-Concord salt-work inside my own archive—" his voice stayed gentle, which was how you knew, "—you formed no hypothesis about how the courier intended to enter the building. What he intended to do once inside. Who he expected to meet."

"He died at the door, Guildmaster. Whatever he intended died with him."

"Mm." Corvane opened the folio. Inside it, I saw with a lurch I kept off my face, lay pages I recognized. Access ledger extracts. The month's entries, pulled already—pulled before I suggested pulling them, which meant my recommendation had been not a shield but a mirror, showing him only what he'd already decided to look at. "Curious thing about ledgers, Halloway. They're like people. You learn the most from what's missing."

"Sir?"

"Four lines removed from the night of the ninth. Clean work. Archive razor, archive technique." He turned the page toward me, and there it was: the gap, photographed in his neat annotation hand. "Whoever took those lines out wanted to erase a visit. Or a visitor." His eyes came up to mine, mild as weather. "You were in the archive that night. Inventory duty."

"I was."

"Did you see anyone?"

The room was very quiet. Outside, far below, the harbor lapped at the seawall with the sound of a patient thing counting.

"A dying man bled out on our threshold," I said. "Half the night staff saw him. Enforcers, archivists. I was cataloguing the east stacks."

"Cataloguing," Corvane repeated softly. "Of course you were. You've always been exactly where your duties place you, haven't you, Halloway. Since you were six years old and I signed your apprentice papers myself."

He closed the folio.

"Your report is accepted," he said. "All of it. Every true thing you brought me." And then, with the gentleness of a man setting down something fragile that he intends, eventually, to break: "Bring me the rest tonight, whenever you're ready. I know you're holding something back. You've been holding it beautifully—but I built the ledgers you learned to read, child, and I know a redacted map when I hold one."

He returned to some paper before me. Dismissal and invitation in the same motion.

I walked out past the salt door, past the archive where Ada's empty chair still sat pushed in too neatly, down to my little station where my honest charts hung in their honest rows. And I sat in the dark without lighting the lamp, and I understood, with the clarity that comes after the point of no return—which is not a point at all, really, but a country you realize you crossed days ago, whose border sign you can only read in the rearview mirror—

I understood that Corvane had just done me a strange kindness. He had shown me his hand: he knew about the gap, he suspected me, he was offering me one last road back. Which meant I was free. The performance was over; both audiences had seen through it. All that remained was choosing what I'd actually done, owning it, and acting like it.

So I lit the lamp, pulled out a clean sheet of drafting paper, and began to draw—not a coast, not a chart, but the layout of Corvane's office, from memory, marking the folio, the lamp, the sightlines, the door. Because tomorrow I would go back through that door, and this time I wouldn't be carrying a report.

I'd be carrying a survey.

And somewhere south, on a river that appeared on no Guild map, a man who erased four lines of ledger was heading toward gates that remembered, and I intended to reach him before either the Guild or the sea did.

I just had to survive one more evening in Corvane's office first.

At dawn, Vess Tallow was waiting outside my station, leaning against the doorframe, holding out a sealed envelope marked with the Guildmaster's personal cipher.

"He wants you early," Vess said. "Before the archive opens." A pause, and then, mildly, terribly: "He says to tell you—bring everything you haven't brought yet."
