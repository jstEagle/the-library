## 21. The Interrogation of Wren Halloway

They took my knife at the door.

That was the first insult. The second was the tea.

Guildmaster Corvane's private office sat at the top of the Cartographers' Guild like a lighthouse that had decided it was too good for the sea: high windows, brass instruments polished to a shine they'd never earn in the field, and shelves of ledgers bound in leather so fine it looked embarrassed to be near paper. I'd been up here exactly twice in eleven years of service. Once to be sworn in. Once to receive a commendation for surveying the Glasswater Reaches without losing a single member of my crew, which everyone agreed was a record and which I privately attributed to the fact that I hadn't brought a crew, because crews were how you lost people.

Now there was a third visit, and no one was handing me anything except a cup of tea I did not want.

"Sit down, Halloway," Corvane said. "You look like a woman who has been standing at attention her whole life and is tired of it."

"I'm not tired of it."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But you will be."

He was not what people expected. People expected the Guildmaster of the Cartographers' Guild to be some vast ceremonial figure, all robes and rings, a man who creaked when he turned. Corvane was small. Neat. Grey at the temples in a way that suggested he'd ordered it from a catalogue. His hands were an archivist's hands — ink-stained at the cuticles, precise, clean — and he poured the tea himself rather than letting a servant do it, which I understood later was a technique. You cannot hit a man who pours your tea. It disarms something in you. Some animal part of the brain files *he made me a drink* under *friend* and refuses to update the file no matter what happens next.

I sat. I did not drink.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"You received my report." Two days ago. Delivered in writing, as procedure required. "Regarding the courier's lodging on Tanner's Lane."

"Mm." He settled into his chair across from me, and here was the terrible thing, the thing I would think about for years afterward: he looked *pleased*. Not smug. Pleased, the way a surveyor looks at a coastline that finally matches the chart. "Your report was excellent. Thorough. Precise. Salt rings on the floorboards consistent with repeated flooding — or with someone simulating flooding, which is more interesting. Drowned-man carvings in the window frame, Merethi workmanship, pre-Exile. An exile token hidden under a loose brick, which you catalogued but did not remove." He paused. "Why didn't you remove it?"

"Procedure says document in place. Removal contaminates the site."

"Procedure," Corvane said softly, "says a great many things. Most of them are suggestions wearing uniforms." He sipped his own tea. "You omitted something from your report, Halloway."

My heart did not speed up. I'd trained it not to. Eleven years of fieldwork teaches you that panic is just another instrument reading, and instruments are for consulting, not obeying. "The report covered everything material."

"Did it." He set down his cup. It made no sound; the saucer was felt-lined, and I understood that too, later. This room was built so that nothing in it ever announced itself. No sudden noises. No hard edges. A room designed the way you'd design a snare if the snare had a budget. "Then let's review. A courier dies in our archive. Stabbed through the lung, which takes minutes to kill — long enough to talk, short enough that he chose carefully what to say. He pressed a map into the hands of—" Corvane consulted a ledger open on his desk, though I'd have bet my pension he knew every line of it by heart, "—a junior ledger-keeper named Ada Joubert. Within the hour, enforcers arrive. Within the day, the archive is inventoried and three volumes are burned. Within the week, Joubert flees the city with a stolen survey kit and a river barge ticket purchased under a false name that is only slightly false, because Ada Joubert has never had an imagination in his life and couldn't build a lie any more than he could build a boat."

"He was frightened," I said. "People run when they're frightened."

"Frightened men run toward things. Watchmen. Family. The Guild, if they're ours." He tilted his head. "Joubert ran *away*. Along the salt road. Toward the tide market. Do you know what's at the tide market, Halloway?"

"Smugglers. Exiles. Contraband cartography."

"Merethi exiles," Corvane said. "Who have been waiting two hundred years for a key. And a frightened little ledger-keeper ran straight to them, carrying the one object in this world they want most — unless, of course, he wasn't frightened at all. Unless he was *sent*." He smiled at me, gently, terribly. "Unless the whole thing was arranged, and the man who died on our archive floor died as part of the arrangement."

"The courier was your nephew," I said, before I could stop myself.

The silence that followed lasted four seconds. I counted. Counting is what I do instead of panicking.

Corvane refilled his own cup. "Yes," he said. "Tomas. My sister's boy. Clever, restless, sentimental — a bad combination in a courier, and I told her so at his induction, and she cried, and I signed the papers anyway, because the Guild does not run on sentiment and neither, officially, do I." He looked at me over the rim of the cup. "You learned that from the Tanner's Lane token, I assume. The exile token was Tomas's. He'd been running messages between us and the exiles for three years. Did you know that? Of course you didn't. Nobody knew. That was the point of couriers."

"So you killed him."

"I did no such thing." And here his voice changed — dropped its pleasantness like a coat sliding off a shoulder, and underneath it was something older and colder, the voice of a man reading figures aloud in a room where everyone already knows the sum. "Tomas stole from me. He took the Merethi map out of the deep vault — a vault whose existence is itself a classified fact — and he took it to give to the exiles, because Tomas believed in their cause the way young men believe in causes, which is to say completely and without doing the arithmetic. He got as far as the archive. Someone caught him there. I don't yet know who. I have theories." His eyes rested on me with the weight of a thumb on a scale. "I am developing theories at a rate I find professionally embarrassing."

"You think I killed him."

"I think," Corvane said, "that you found his body's aftermath before my enforcers did, which means you were in the archive within the hour of his death. I think you then spent a week conducting a private investigation into his lodgings while reporting none of it. I think you located the tide market — a market that has evaded Guild surveillance for two centuries — in under six days. I think you interviewed a one-eyed carter named Brannic and then, according to my informant, struck a bargain with him, the terms of which you have not disclosed." He folded his hands. "And I think that when Joubert fled the city, someone paid his barge fare in Guild scrip, and the transaction record shows the scrip came from a disbursement account that only three people can access, and one of them is sitting in my chair, and the other two are dead."

I hadn't known about the scrip. That was Brannic — the old smuggler had his own channels, his own money, his own ways. But Corvane didn't know that, and I couldn't prove it, and proving things to Corvane was not going to be the shape of this conversation anyway. I understood that now. This wasn't an interrogation in the sense of questions seeking answers. This was an audit. He had already done the sums. He was showing me the ledger.

"And yet," Corvane continued, "your written reports have been flawless. Every detail accurate. Every omission surgical. You reported the token but not where you found it. You reported the carvings but not the second set of carvings inside the wardrobe, which my people found because you'd left chalk marks beside them — surveyor's chalk, Halloway, you tagged the evidence like a woman building a case for someone. Yourself? Or for him?" He leaned back. "You've been playing both sides of this board since the night Tomas died, and you've done it well enough that I've spent a week genuinely uncertain whether you're the most loyal officer I have or the most dangerous traitor in the history of this Guild. I want you to appreciate what a compliment that is. I am never uncertain."

The tea steamed between us. Somewhere below, the city went on with its morning — carts, gulls, the bell of the Tide Chapel ringing the half-hour, indifferent as water.

"What do you want from me?" I asked.

"Ah." He brightened. "The direct question. Good. I find indirection exhausting, don't you? All this careful reporting, all these omissions shaped like truths. Let's be plain." He stood and went to the window, hands clasped behind his back, a small neat man silhouetted against a large grey sea. "Ada Joubert has the Merethi gate-map. The original. The key. With it, the drowned gates can be opened, and Mereth rises, and everything the coast has built on two centuries of forgetting — every harbor, every city, every ledger in this building — goes under the water with the tide-storm that comes through those gates. Or the map can be destroyed, and the exiles' hope with it, and we return to the comfortable lie. Or—" he turned, "—the map can be *used properly*. Bound. Controlled. By the institution that has kept this coast safe, such as it is, for two hundred years. That is what I want. That is what I have always wanted. Everything else — Tomas, the exiles, the burned volumes, poor dead Brannic-to-be — is bookkeeping."

"You sound like Sarel."

Something flickered across his face — fast, gone. "The heir of Mereth and I want the same object. We differ on disposition. She wants her kingdom back. I want my coast intact. Both of us are willing to spend other people to get it, and the difference between us is that I keep records." He came back to the desk. "Which brings us to you."

"To me."

"Wren Halloway. Orphaned at seven. Raised in the Guild crèche on the fourth floor. Sworn at sixteen. Commended at twenty-three. The Guild fed you, taught you, gave you a trade and a name and a place to stand. In eleven years you have filed four hundred and six reports and every single one of them has been true." He said it like a eulogy. "Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what it costs, being a person who tells the truth in an institution that runs on managed falsehood? It costs you friends. It costs you promotions. It has cost you, I suspect, a certain kind of life — the soft one, the one with someone in it. Because you can't lie, and love requires lying, small ones, daily ones, the lies that make closeness survivable." He sat down again. "You are the most honest person I employ. And you have spent the last month lying to me in every report you file, and the reason you're bad at it — the reason I caught you — is that you've only ever lied for one thing in your entire life."

The room was very quiet. Felt-lined. Edgeless.

"His name is Ada Joubert," Corvane said. "Isn't it."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't confirm it, and silence confirmed it too, and we both knew that, and he let the silence sit there like a stone he'd placed on the table between us.

"I'm not going to threaten him," Corvane said. "Threats are for people who respond to threats, and Joubert responds to exactly two things: guilt and duty. I could drown him in either, but it would take time, and time is the one resource the tides won't wait on. Winter is coming, Halloway. The storm season. When the winter tides come, the gates thin. Sarel's hardliners will force them open by blood ritual if they must — they've begun preparing, my informants say, and my informants are better than yours, because mine aren't hampered by honesty. If the gates open unbound, the coast drowns. Calbridge first. Then the harbors. Then, given a week, everything your Guild oath exists to protect." He spread his hands. "So. Here is your old life, offered back to you with both hands."

"Name it."

"Deliver Ada Joubert and the map to me. Alive, unharmed, and willing — I don't need him broken, I need him *convinced*, and I have documents that will convince him, including the treaty his precious archives contain, including proof of what the Guild did two hundred years ago, which I will show him myself, because a man like Ada can't be threatened but he can be *shown*, and once he sees the full ledger he'll understand that binding the gates under Guild authority is the only outcome in which anyone survives. You bring him in. You stand beside him while I make the offer. And in exchange—" he ticked it off on ink-stained fingers, "—your reports are expunged. Your omissions are forgotten. Your treason — and it is treason, Halloway, let's not pretty it — is never entered into any ledger. Brannic's bargain dies with Brannic. The tide market burns on schedule and you are nowhere near it. And Wren Halloway continues her career as the finest surveyor this Guild has produced in a generation, with a clear path to Master rank and, eventually, this chair." He smiled. "I'm old. Chairs become available."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you're a traitor, and traitors go in ledgers, and I will write you into one myself, in my own hand, and it will be accurate, because I am always accurate. And Ada Joubert will be taken anyway — by enforcers, at the market, during the burning, with casualties — and the last thing he'll learn about you is that you had one chance to stand between him and that, and you chose your principles over his pulse." He picked up his tea. "Principles are lovely, Halloway. I collect them. They look very fine on shelves. But they don't hold a coastline."

I looked at him for a long moment. Small, neat, reasonable. A man who had murdered — or caused to be murdered, the distinction mattered to him, I could see it mattering — his own nephew, and had described it to me as inventory shrinkage. A man who spoke of drowning cities and saving them in the same breath and meant both. A man who was offering me everything I'd ever earned, wrapped around the one thing I'd never let myself want.

And the horrible thing — the thing I carried out of that room and would carry for a long time after — was that I believed him. Not his goodness. I believed his arithmetic. The gates were real. The storm was real. Winter was coming, and Sarel's zealots were real, and somewhere out on the salt road a gentle, hopeless man was carrying a key that every hungry faction in the world wanted to turn. Somebody was going to decide what happened at those gates. Corvane wanted it to be him. I had spent a month pretending there was a fourth option in which nobody decided anything and everyone behaved well, and Corvane had just read that option aloud to me, line by line, and shown me where it ended: in floodwater.

"Loyalty," he said, as I stood, "isn't a feeling. It's an account. You've been drawing on this Guild your whole life, Halloway. Today I'm calling the balance." He slid a folded paper across the desk. Coordinates. A rendezvous. A route into the tide market that avoided his own patrols — he was giving me the road in, which meant he trusted me, or meant he thought he owned me, and I no longer knew the difference and suspected he didn't either. "Three days. Bring him to Saltbridge. Come alone, or as alone as you ever are."

At the door, I stopped. One question left, the honest one, the one that had been sitting in my chest since Tanner's Lane.

"Tomas," I said. "Did you order his death?"

Corvane didn't look up from his ledger. "I ordered the map recovered," he said. "What recovery cost was never itemized." He turned a page. "That's the trouble with loyalty, Halloway. You never see the itemization until it's your name on the line."

I walked down four flights of stairs with my spine straight and my hands steady and my whole understanding of my life quietly rearranging itself behind my ribs, shelf by shelf, like a library being re-catalogued overnight. I had thought I served the Guild. I had thought the Guild was maps, and truth, and the long patient work of knowing the world. Standing in that office, I finally saw what the Guild actually was: a ledger with a roof on it. And Corvane was not its corrupter. Corvane was its purest expression — every entry balanced, every debt called, every human being a figure in a column that always, always summed.

He thought he'd bought me. Three days. Saltbridge. Ada's face when he saw me coming down the road with Guild colors in my pocket.

He was right that I'd come. He was wrong about everything else.

There were three days to prepare, and I intended to use every one of them — because I had read his coordinates closely, the way you read a chart before sailing, and I had seen the thing he thought I wouldn't see: Saltbridge was a chokepoint, narrow, walled, one road in and one road out, and the disbursement orders for the garrison he was stationing there had already been drawn. Whatever Corvane intended for Ada at Saltbridge, it was not a conversation.

He'd handed me the ambush and called it mercy.

Fine. I'd take the road to Saltbridge. I'd even take Ada toward it.

What happened when we got there — who stood where, whose colors showed, who walked away — that part of the map, I was redrawing myself.

* * *

That night I packed in the dark: rope, chalk, the surveyor's kit I'd carried since I was sixteen, and one extra item that had no official purpose and would cost me my rank if found and save all our lives if used. I wrote no report. For the first time in four hundred and seven entries, Wren Halloway's account of her movements existed nowhere but in her own head.

Unrecorded. Unmapped. Off the chart entirely.

It should have felt like falling.

It felt like flying.
