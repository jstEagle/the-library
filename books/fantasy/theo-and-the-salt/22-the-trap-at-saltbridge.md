## 22. The Trap at Saltbridge

**Wren**

The road to Saltbridge was the kind of road the Guild loved: mapped, measured, and utterly predictable. I had walked it a dozen times on survey duty, and I knew every milestone, every culvert, every place where the alder trees thinned enough for a crossbowman to have a clean line. That was rather the point.

Corvane had given me the plan in his gentle voice, the one he used when he was being kind, which was how I knew someone had recently suffered for it. *Bring him to the bridge, Wren. The enforcers will handle the rest. You'll have your old life back by supper.* He had even smiled. Corvane's smiles were like his ledgers — balanced to the last entry, and never once about you.

I had said yes. Out loud. With my face doing whatever my face does when it agrees with the Guildmaster of the Cartographers' Guild.

What I had not said was that I'd spent the previous night memorizing the positions of the eight men he intended to hide along the river, because Corvane — meticulous, thorough, corrupt down to his inkwells — had made me review the deployment myself. He trusted me with the details. It had been four days since anyone had trusted me with anything, and it had worked on me like wine.

So I knew: two archers in the alders on the north bank, three enforcers behind the tollhouse wall, two mounted on the south approach, and Vess Marlow — Corvane's favorite, all shoulders and silence — standing openly on the bridge itself, because a trap works best when part of it is visible. The visible part is what makes the prey stop thinking.

Ada would see Vess. Ada would panic or freeze. And then the invisible parts would close.

Unless the prey had already read the map.

I told myself, walking beside him in the gray morning light, that I still hadn't decided. That this was reconnaissance. That I would know what to do when the moment came. All of which was true in the way that most lies are true: technically, from certain angles, if you squinted.

"You're quiet," Ada said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *surveyor* quiet right now. There's a difference. Surveyor-quiet means you're counting things."

He knew me too well. Ten years of shared corridors and borrowed pens and one disastrous evening involving mulled wine and an argument about projection methods — and he could hear the difference between my silences. I had missed him so much it was a physical ailment, and I was about to either save his life or end it, and possibly both, and I could not say any of this out loud because we were being watched from three directions.

"Counting what?" he asked.

"Trees," I said. "Milestones. Enforcers."

The pause before he answered was exactly as long as it needed to be for him to understand me and no longer.

"Ah," said Ada Joubert, who had survived ten years at the Guild by noticing everything and admitting nothing. "How many?"

"Eight. Plus Vess."

"Vess I can see." He didn't look around. He was better at not-looking than I was; I'd taught him the basics years ago, back when not-looking was just a game we played to amuse ourselves during long cataloguing shifts. "Where are the ones I can't?"

"Two in the alders, north bank. Three behind the tollhouse. Two mounted, south approach. They'll let us get halfway across before—" I stopped.

Before they took him. Before I handed over the man I loved to be interrogated by people who burned their own couriers, and got my name back in the ledgers as payment.

"Before," I finished.

We walked another hundred paces. The bridge came into view through the mist off the river — old stone, seven arches, the middle span rebuilt after the flood year, slightly paler than the rest like a scar. Brannic's cart was already there, parked at the near end, the mule dozing. Brannic himself sat on the tailboard eating an apple with the unbothered air of a man who had driven this road forty years and considered rivers, bridges, empires, and weather to be roughly equivalent inconveniences.

He tipped the apple at me. "Morning, surveyor. Morning, ledger-man. Lovely day for a crossing."

*Brannic,* I thought, *you wonderful, doomed one-eyed ox, why are you here.* Because of course I hadn't told him. Neither had Ada. We had both, apparently independently, decided to protect the carter by leaving him out of it, and the carter had responded by parking his cart directly in front of the ambush, because that was what Brannic did with plans that didn't include him. He climbed over them.

"Brannic," Ada said carefully, "you should take the ford downstream today. Ice on the bridge stones."

"Ice?" Brannic looked at the clear morning sky with deep suspicion, as though it might be lying to him. "There's no ice."

"There's going to be trouble," Ada said, and I admired him for it. Honesty, from Ada, was practically a religious experience.

Brannic chewed. Swallowed. Looked at me — the one eye doing more work than most faces manage with two — and then at the alders, where I knew perfectly well two crossbows were aimed at our general vicinity, and gave them a slow, friendly nod, the way you'd nod at neighbors.

"Aye," he said. "There usually is, this time of year." He hopped down off the tailboard and unhitched the mule, casual as Sunday. "Tell you what. I'll stand on the bridge and admire the view while you two sort out whatever it is you're sorting out. Old habit. My grandmother used to say the water keeps honest accounts."

"Your grandmother," I said, "was Merethi."

"She was that. Come on, then." And the impossible old man started walking onto the bridge, whistling, with his mule following like a large skeptical dog.

Ada caught my sleeve. His hand was shaking. So, I discovered, was mine.

"Wren," he said quietly. "Whatever you're planning — is it the thing where you betray me, or the thing where you betray them?"

"That depends entirely," I said, "on the next thirty seconds," and stepped onto the bridge.

* * *

**Ada**

Here is what nobody tells you about walking into a trap you already know about: it doesn't feel brave. It feels like the dream where you're late for work and naked, except you've decided to arrive anyway, on principle.

Vess Marlow stood at the center of the span with his hands folded, a big calm man shaped like a door. Behind him, the mist on the river had that particular quality mist gets when men are breathing under it. Ahead, Brannic's boots rang on the stone, cheerful as bells.

"Master Joubert." Vess's voice carried pleasantly over the water. "Guildmaster Corvane extends his regards. He asks that you surrender the map and accompany us. No one needs to be hurt."

"No one," I repeated, "has been hurt so far? In this whole business?"

Vess considered this honestly, which I grudgingly respected. "The courier fell," he said. "Down stairs. Twice."

"He fell down stairs twice on the same night."

"The stairs were determined."

Behind the tollhouse, I saw the shadow of movement against the frosted glass. In the alders, a branch shifted wrong. Wren stood a pace behind me and half to the left, exactly where she'd positioned herself, and I realized — with the strange clarity that arrives when your body understands things before your mind consents to — that she had placed herself between me and the archers' sightline.

She wasn't handing me over. She had never been handing me over. She'd walked me straight into Corvane's net carrying the knife to cut me out of it, and she'd done it without telling me, because telling me would have meant trusting my face, and my face, as she has often observed, "broadcasts like a town crier."

"Master Joubert," Vess said again, taking a step forward, and that was the signal, I understood that now, the step was the hinge, and behind the tollhouse the door was opening—

Wren moved.

Later, everyone who survived would tell the story differently, and all of them would be wrong except hers and mine. Here is what actually happened, in order:

Wren drew her chalk line.

Every Guild surveyor carries chalk, and every Guild surveyor learns, in the second year, the trick the Guild officially denies teaching: a line drawn with intent on ground that has been surveyed is a *boundary*, briefly. Not magic, exactly. More like the world briefly remembering that maps are arguments, and agreeing, for a moment, with yours.

Her chalk flashed white across the bridge stones in a single stroke, and the world flinched. The mist pulled back like a curtain grabbed by the hem. The two archers in the alders loosed — I heard the twin snaps of the strings — and both bolts died midair over the water, dropping like exhausted birds, because from where they stood the bridge was suddenly, briefly, *not there*, and arrows cannot fly toward a thing that isn't.

Then she turned and put a bolt of her own into the tollhouse doorframe, close enough to the first enforcer through it to shave paint, and shouted, in her official surveyor's voice, the voice that had made junior cartographers weep in three provinces:

"GUILD SURVEY ORDER SEVEN-SEVEN! This crossing is under measurement dispute! All parties stand down until arbitration!"

It was brilliant. It was insane. A measurement dispute was the only legal instrument that could freeze an enforcement action, and it expired the moment anyone checked whether she actually had authority to declare one, which she did not, which meant we had perhaps ninety seconds of bureaucratic paralysis before Vess decided paperwork was optional.

"Ninety seconds," Wren said to me, already moving. "Run."

I ran. I am not proud of how fast I ran, but pride is for people with time.

And then Brannic ruined everything, in the manner of heroes everywhere, by not running.

* * *

**Wren**

He planted himself at the far end of the bridge like a tollgate, and he picked up the tollbar — a length of oak as thick as my waist — and set it across the roadway, and behind it he stood with his arms crossed and his mule at his side, and when Vess Marlow came onto the span at a walk that was pretending not to be a run, Brannic said, cheerfully:

"Bridge is closed. Measurement dispute. Surveyor's orders."

"Stand aside, carter."

"Funny thing," Brannic said. "My grandmother came from under the water. And she used to say — hold on, it'll come to me — ah. She said: *the water keeps honest accounts.* Meaning, son, that sooner or later everybody pays what they owe. And you lot" — he nodded past Vess, at the tollhouse, at the alders, at the whole tidy machine of Corvane's ambition — "owe a drowning."

Vess drew his blade. To his credit, he tried to go around first. To Brannic's credit, the tollbar caught him across the shoulder and nearly put him in the river, and Vess Marlow was not a small man, and the crack of it echoed off the water like the bridge itself objecting.

Everything after that happened fast and badly, which is how battles go when the good plan lasts ninety seconds and the battle takes longer.

The enforcers came out of the tollhouse in a wedge. One of the alder archers found his nerve and his angle and shot at Ada on the south bank, and I threw myself off the bridge railing into the shallows rather than watch it land, and came up soaked and furious and alive because Ada had ducked behind the mule, of all things, and the mule — I swear this — leaned into it.

I killed no one that day, which I count among my life's small achievements. I broke a wrist, disarmed a man named Osk whom I'd trained myself and who had the grace to look apologetic about the whole affair, and held the bridge steps for the ninety seconds that mattered, and then the ninety seconds were up and Vess, bleeding from the scalp and out of patience, ordered the wedge forward, and the arithmetic stopped working.

Four of them. Me. A carter with an oak bar.

"Go," I screamed at Ada. "GO, you idiot, RUN—"

"I'm not," Brannic called back, conversational, swinging, "leaving my mule."

The wedge hit the tollbar line and Brannic met it, and for maybe ten seconds — I counted, I was a surveyor, counting is what I do — a one-eyed carter in a leather apron held five armed enforcers on a narrow stone bridge, and it was the finest piece of defensive positioning I have ever witnessed, and it cost him everything, and he knew it would when he set the bar down, and he set it down anyway.

They say the water keeps honest accounts.

The last thing he said — I was close enough to hear it, God help me, I'll always be close enough to hear it — was to Vess, whose sword was through him, and Brannic gripped the blade with both hands to keep it there, holding the man in place, holding the line, and he grinned with his blood in his teeth and said:

"There. Paid in full. Tell them... tell them the common folk remember too..."

And then the wedge pushed through, and Ada was screaming my name from the south bank, and I did the hardest thing I have ever done in a career of hard things, which was to leave him.

I went over the railing into the river with Brannic's killer's attention fixed elsewhere, and I swam, and the current took me downstream past the pilings, past the fight, past the sound of it, and the last image I have of Saltbridge is the mule standing over the body on the stones, ears flat, refusing to move, while the enforcers tried to shift it and couldn't, because some animals understand loyalty better than guilds do.

* * *

**Ada**

I ran. Brannic bought it, and I ran, and I will carry that equation until I die: one old man's life weighed against my feet hitting the road south, and the scale tipping the way scales tip when the alternative is that everyone dies and nothing is saved.

Wren caught up with me two miles on, soaked to the ribs, gray-faced, with river mud in her hair and something in her eyes I'd only seen once before — the night the courier died on the archive floor.

She didn't say anything. Neither did I. We walked. Somewhere behind us, faintly, smoke rose from the direction of the bridge, because the enforcers had fired the tollhouse, because Corvane tidied up after himself; that was always his way. Balanced ledgers. No loose entries.

At the third mile marker she finally spoke.

"He knew," she said. "Didn't he. He knew it was a trap and he stood on it anyway."

"He recognized your chalk work," I said. "From the tide market. He said you'd come."

A sound came out of her that was not quite a laugh. "He *recognized* me. From a market I reported to the Guildmaster. Wonderful. I'm known in exile communities now. Corvane will love that."

"Wren."

"Don't." She kept walking. "Don't do the voice where you're kind at me. I led you onto that bridge. Whatever my plan was, you walked it believing I might sell you, and you came anyway, and that's—" Her breath hitched, once, badly, and she mastered it the way she mastered everything, by force. "That's more than I deserve, and we don't have time for deserving things."

"We have time to bury him," I said.

"We don't have a body."

"We have a bridge. We can go back for—"

"And walk into the net again? Ada." She stopped, and turned, and took both my shoulders, and her hands were shaking now, properly, the surveyor's control gone, and what was underneath it was so much worse than control. "They'll have taken him to the Guild. Corvane will want to know who he was, who sent him, what he knew. He'll be a ledger entry by tonight. An *inventory item*. And I—" She closed her eye — the dry one; the other had gone bright. "I reviewed the deployment. I helped design the trap. If I'd drawn the positions differently, if I'd insisted the mounted pair covered the south approach instead of the north, Brannic would be alive. I did the geometry of it. Me."

I had never seen Wren Halloway cry, and I understood, watching her fight it, that she had built her entire self out of not-crying, and that the structure was failing, and that she would hate me forever if I acknowledged it.

So I didn't acknowledge it. I did the other thing. The Ada thing. The only thing I'm genuinely good at.

"Give me your chalk," I said.

She blinked. "...What?"

"Your chalk. And your kit. Right now."

She handed them over, frowning, tears suspended, grateful for the interruption and furious at being grateful. I knelt at the mile marker, scraped the frost off the stone, and drew — quickly, badly, with none of her training — the bridge. Seven arches. The scar of the middle span. A figure at the north end, holding a bar. And beneath it, in the margins, in the little cramped hand I'd used for ten years of cataloguing other people's wonders, I wrote the only epitaph I knew how to write:

*B. Held the line. The water keeps honest accounts. Account settled.*

Wren read it twice. When she spoke, her voice was wrecked and steady at the same time, which should not be possible and yet is a specialty of hers.

"That's not how memorial markers work. That's not official notation. It won't be entered anywhere. It won't survive."

"It survives," I said, "because you and I saw it. Some records live in heads, Wren. You taught me that four nights ago, when you wrote nothing down."

She looked at the little drawing for a long moment. Then she took the chalk back from my hand, and beneath my clumsy lines she added, in perfect Guild surveyor's notation, precise as scripture, the elevation, the span measurements, the load-bearing figures of the middle arch — the technical record of a bridge that now mattered more than every bridge in the atlas — and signed it.

*Measured and verified: W. Halloway.*

"There," she said. "Now it's real. Now he's in the map." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, decisively, done. "South road. There's a ferry at Tarn's Reach and Corvane doesn't know about it because it isn't on any chart he trusts. We make the coast in three days."

"The coast," I said. "Mereth."

"The drowned archive." She said it flatly, daring me to flinch. "You and I are going diving, Master Joubert. And then we're going to finish whatever it is you've been not-telling-me since the night a man died on your floor. Understood?"

"Understood."

We walked south, away from the smoke, two traitors of the Cartographers' Guild, carrying a dead man's account between us.

And neither of us noticed — how could we, grief-blind, hurrying — the rider on the ridge behind us, who watched us go for a long moment, and then turned his horse not south after us, but north, toward the city, toward Corvane, carrying the one report that could undo everything:

that the surveyor Wren Halloway had turned, and that the man she turned for was walking, freely, toward the gates.
