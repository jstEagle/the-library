## 23. What Brannic Carried

They buried Brannic under an alder tree on the north bank of the Saltbridge river, because Wren said he'd have hated a formal funeral and I said nothing at all, because if I opened my mouth I was fairly sure the sound that came out would not be words.

Sarel's people wanted to burn him. "It's the old way," said the one with the eel-scarred neck, whose name I never learned and now never would. "Salt-fire, so the sea can find him."

"He came from the sea," Wren said quietly. "Let him rest in the dirt for once."

I don't know which argument would have won. It didn't matter, because there wasn't time for either. Corvane's enforcers had retreated across the bridge but they hadn't gone far—we could see their cookfires downstream like a string of orange beads—and staying anywhere longer than it took to bleed and bind was a luxury none of us could afford.

So: an alder tree, a shallow hole dug with two survey spades from the stolen Guild kit—Brannic would have found that funny, being buried by Guild equipment; he'd have made some joke about being the first man ever mapped posthumously—and Wren saying a few words in Merethi that I didn't understand but that made the eel-scarred man stop glaring and look at his feet instead.

I put Brannic's good eye-cloth in the ground with him. The empty socket had wept salt-water at the end, which I am choosing to believe was a hallucination brought on by blood loss, because the alternative is a whole theology I don't have room for.

Then we ran. Because that's what we did now. We ran, and we bled, and we left our dead under trees with borrowed spades, and somewhere in the middle of all of it I stopped thinking of myself as a ledger-keeper who had stumbled out of his depth, and started thinking of myself as something else. Something with fewer options.

* * *

We holed up that night in a charcoal-burner's hut three miles into the hills—a squat dome of turf and char-smell that hadn't seen its owner since autumn, if the mouse droppings were anything to go by. Wren cleaned the graze along my ribs with spirits and didn't say a word while she did it, which was somehow worse than if she'd yelled. Sarel took his people—the four who'd survived the bridge, out of eleven—up the slope to keep watch. The eel-scarred man looked at me the whole way up, and the look said, plainly, *you're the reason he's dead,* and the worst part was that the look was right.

The map rode against my chest in its oilcloth wrap, where it had ridden through the ambush, through the river, through everything. I could feel its edges even through the cloth, the way you can feel a splinter you've stopped looking at.

Wren finished with my ribs, packed the wound with moss because we were down to moss, and finally spoke.

"You should sleep."

"I'll inventory the kit first." Old habit. When the world goes sideways, count what's left of it. It's how I've stayed sane—or whatever this is—for ten years in the archive. "Two spades, both accounted for. One sextant, cracked lens. Ink, three vials, one broken. Compass—"

"Ada."

"—compass intact. Calipers intact. Blotting paper, most of a roll. And—" My hand found the bundle at the bottom of the kit, wrapped in waxed canvas, heavier than it should have been. "And this."

"What is it?"

"I don't know. Brannic's. He kept it lashed inside the survey pack the whole road from the Tide Market. I asked him once and he said it was cheese."

"Was it cheese?"

"It was not cheese."

Wren sat back on her heels. In the light of the little spirit-lamp she looked hollowed out, cheekbones like a drawn map of herself, and I realized she'd been crying earlier—not now, earlier, silently, sometime between the bridge and here—and had decided I didn't need to know about it.

"Open it," she said.

* * *

Inside the waxed canvas was a book.

Not a fine book. Not a map, not a ledger, nothing with gilt or seals. Just a journal bound in grey fish-leather gone stiff with age, its spine stitched twice where it had split, the whole thing swollen with damp so it wouldn't close flat. A brass clasp, green with corrosion. Someone had loved this book for a long time and then carried it a very long way.

There was writing on the first page. Merethi script—but not the formal, flowing hand I'd spent weeks decoding in the drowned margins of the courier's map. This was cramped, slanting, practical. A working hand. A market hand.

"Can you read it?" Wren asked.

"Some." I'd been teaching myself from the annotations for a month now, and the month had taught me humility along with vocabulary. "Slowly. Give me—"

I started at the top of the page, and I want to tell you that I read it steadily, with scholarly composure, taking notes as befitted a cartographer of the Cartographers' Guild. That is not what happened. What happened is that I got six lines in and my hands began to shake, and Wren had to take the lamp closer and hold it steady for me, and I read the rest aloud in a voice I barely recognized, because the voice belonged to someone else entirely.

It belonged to a woman named Maren Voss. Brannic's grandmother.

* * *

She had been, the early pages established, a fishwife. Not a priestess, not a courtier, not anyone the histories would bother drowning. She sold herring off a barrow on the Tidewalk in the lower city of Mereth, and she was proud of her herring, and she was proud of her arithmetic, and she wrote her accounts in this journal every single day because, as she put it in the third entry I could fully parse:

*A person who does not write down what happens will believe any story they are told afterward.*

I laughed when I translated that line. It came out of me wrong, a bark, and Wren looked up sharply, and I said, "Sorry. Sorry. It's just—" I tapped the page. "That's a ledger-keeper's creed. That's the first thing they teach you at the Guild. Write it down or lose it. She arrived at it selling fish."

"She arrived at it," Wren said, "before your Guild stole it."

Which was fair.

I read on. The entries were ordinary for a long time—prices, weather, a quarrel with her sister over a net, a wedding, a stillbirth recorded in four flat words that hit me harder than anything else in the book. *The boy did not stay.* Ordinary life, in other words, which is to say the only kind there is, the kind that never gets mapped because maps are made by and for people who own things.

And then, sixty pages in, the handwriting changed. It got faster. The dates stopped lining up neatly at the top of each entry and started crowding the margins, sideways, upside-down, wherever there was room.

The year the ships came.

* * *

Here is what I knew before tonight, and I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of myself: I knew that two centuries ago, the kingdom of Mereth had fallen to the sea in a catastrophe the Guild's official history called *the Subsidence*. Natural, regrettable, geologically fascinating. I knew that the coastal cities—my city included—had risen on the trade routes that bloomed in the drowned kingdom's absence. I knew that the exiles called it the Drowning and spat when they said the Guild's name, and I had always privately filed their grief under *understandable sentiment, historically inevitable, no actionable content*, the same drawer where I filed everything that might cost me something to feel.

I knew, as of the drowned archive and the treaty I'd found there, that the Subsidence was not natural. I knew the Guild had broken an oath. I thought I understood what that meant.

I did not understand what that meant until I read Maren Voss's account of the eleventh day of the siege, written in a margin, sideways, in ink she must have known was running out:

*They did not come for the king. I want that written plainly, so that whoever reads this understands. The king's towers burned on the first day and he fled upriver with his guard, and the ships let him go—I saw them let him go, I stood on the Tidewalk and watched the sails open to let him pass. They came for the gates. The gates under the city. Everything above was noise. Everything below was the point.*

*They burned the granaries on the third day. Why burn grain? You cannot carry grain away. But a hungry city opens its own doors, and ours opened on the fifth night, and the men who walked in wore no colors at all, which meant they had been paid by everyone.*

*I took my sister's children down to the water-stairs on the eighth day. The tide-masters said the deep roads would hold. The tide-masters lied, or they believed, and I do not know which is worse. I sent the children up instead, to the hills, with our neighbor's son, and I stayed to carry what I could carry, and what I could carry was this book and forty years of accounts, because if I did not write down what happened, then afterward everyone would be told a story, and the story would be theirs.*

*On the last day the water did not rise like a flood rises. It rose like a door closing. I have thought about how to write that for two days and that is the truest sentence I can make. The sea came up the streets politely, street by street, as though it had been invited. As though it had been instructed.*

*Instructed. By whom? By whom do you think?*

*Whoever finds this: we were not a tragedy. A tragedy is an act of the gods. This was an invoice.*

* * *

I stopped reading aloud somewhere around *the water-stairs*. Wren didn't ask me to continue. She sat beside me in the charcoal-burner's dark with the lamp between us and listened while I read the rest silently, moving my lips like a boy at his letters, and when I finally looked up her eyes were wet and furious and fixed on the middle distance, which is where I imagine you look when you learn that the institution that raised you committed a crime so large it required its own geology.

"The treaty," I said. My voice sounded like gravel in a tin cup. "The oath the Guild broke. I found the treaty text in the sunken archive. I thought—that was the heart of it. The proof."

"It is," Wren said.

"It isn't." I turned the journal gently, the way you touch a wound. "The treaty tells us what they did. This tells us who they did it to. Do you see the difference? The treaty is a legal document. It has clauses. It can be renegotiated, reinterpreted, argued over by lawyers for a hundred years. Corvane could stand in front of the whole Guild with the treaty in his hand and call it an unfortunate breach of terms, historical context, regrettable exigencies of the period." I heard my own voice climb and didn't rein it in. "But he cannot argue with a woman counting her herring. He cannot lawyer a fishwife. Nobody gave Maren Voss a side in this. She wasn't the king's faction or the Guild's faction. She was a woman with a barrow, and they closed the sea on her like a door, and she wrote it down because she knew—she *knew*—that afterward everyone would be told a story, and the story would be theirs."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Brannic carried this the whole road. Through the market. Through the ambush." Her jaw worked. "He never told us what it was."

"He told me it was cheese."

"Yes, well. He told me his one eye could see tides coming three days off, so I'd stopped fact-checking him." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way you do when crying is a scheduling problem. Then she looked at me, and what was in her face frightened me a little, because it was the same thing that had been building in my chest since the first page: not grief anymore. Something with edges.

"So what do we do with it?" she asked.

* * *

Here is the question I have been circling for a month now, the question underneath every other question, and I'm going to write it down plainly, in the manner of Maren Voss, so that whoever ends up reading this account understands exactly where I stood.

What is the map *for*?

The courier died whispering that the gates remember. Sarel wants it because it opens the gates and gives him back his grandfather's throne, and he has convinced himself that drowning five cities is a debt the living owe the dead. Corvane wants it because it is the last unclaimed asset of the most profitable crime in commercial history, and Corvane's soul is a balance sheet that always balances. Burn it, and the truth dies with the dead, and the Guild's story stands forever—*natural subsidence, regrettable, geologically fascinating*. Open the gates, and the dead get a kingdom built on top of other people's corpses, which is just the original crime performed in reverse, with better music.

Every option serves somebody powerful. Kings, guilds, heirs, masters.

Nobody had proposed serving Maren Voss.

I sat in that hut with the journal open on my knees and I understood, finally, with a clarity that felt less like revelation than like arithmetic, why the binding ritual in the gate-ledger required a cartographer's name. I'd read the requirement weeks ago and filed it under *cost, unknown, terrifying, defer*. But sitting there with a dead carter's grandmother counting her herring in my lap, I finally understood what the requirement *meant*. The gates couldn't be bound by royal blood or Guild authority, because those were the two currencies that had caused the problem. The binding needed someone with no faction. No title. No story told about them afterward. Someone whose only credential was that they wrote things down and refused to lie about them.

Someone boring. Someone invisible. Someone whose entire life had been an apprenticeship in not being noticed.

I looked at the lamp. I looked at Wren, who was watching me with the particular stillness of a surveyor who has spotted movement at the edge of the frame and hasn't decided whether to report it. I looked at the journal, at Maren Voss's cramped, slanting, practical hand, and I made her a promise, silently, in the privacy of my own skull where promises are cheap:

Your book will not serve a king. It will not serve a guild. Whatever I do with the map, it will be done in your name and Brannic's and every ordinary drowned soul the histories skipped, and when it's done, the story everyone is told afterward will be yours.

Outside, boots on frost. Sarel, coming down the slope fast, and behind him the eel-scarred man's voice pitched high and urgent, and beneath both, far off toward the coast, a sound I had hoped not to hear for another month:

Thunder. Out of season. Out of a clear winter sky.

The storm tides had come early.

And Sarel stood in the doorway of the hut with the lamplight behind him and said, in a voice stripped of all his courtly grace: "They're forcing the gates. Tonight. Without us."
