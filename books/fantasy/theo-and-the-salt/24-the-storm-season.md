## 24. The Storm Season

There is a particular kind of silence that a room full of people makes when they have all agreed on something without you. I grew up hearing it. It was the silence of my mother's council when they decided I would be the heir and stopped asking what I wanted. It was the silence of the exile camps when the elders decided a child's rations would go to a warrior instead. And it was the silence that met me now, in the low hall beneath the tide market, where forty faces turned toward me with the patient look of people who have already done their arithmetic.

I had called the meeting to talk about grain stores.

"We have counted," said Veyra Ashfall, who spoke for the salt-priests, and whose voice always sounded like she was reading from a ledger of grievances older than my line. "The winter tides come in nineteen days. After that, the deep channels silt for a season. If we do not open the gates before the storm season, we wait until spring."

"Then we wait until spring," I said.

"Spring," Veyra repeated, tasting the word like bad fish. "Another spring in which our old ones die above water. Another spring of children born who have never seen Mereth. Another spring of *waiting*, heir."

"I am told waiting is a specialty of exiles."

That got me nothing but stares. Somewhere in the last month — I could not name the day; these things happen invisibly, like rot — I had lost the room. Not to Corvane, not to Ada Joubert and his inconvenient map, but to something simpler and more dangerous: hope with a deadline attached. The tide market had spent two hundred years learning patience, and patience is a skill you can lose in a single season if someone tells you the door is unlocked.

"The map exists," said Old Maren from the back. She was ninety-one years old and had been a girl in the last city to burn. "The key exists. The Guild man carries it in his own two hands, and he is sitting in our camp eating our bread while we argue about *grain*."

"He is recovering from a wound taken running from the Guild that your impatience helped create," I said. "And the map is not a key. It is a treaty-document. He found proof of what was done to us —"

"Yes." Veyra again, smooth as oil on stone. "Proof. Wonderful. A paper to wave at the descendants of the men who drowned us. Shall we frame it? Hang it in the market square? 'Here is the oath they broke' — and then what, heir? They will say: how interesting, and continue collecting tariffs on our misery."

I wanted to tell her she was wrong. I opened my mouth to do so, and discovered I could not, because I had asked myself the same question at three o'clock that morning, staring at the gate-ledger's pages by lamplight, and had no better answer than a framed document and a very long sigh.

"The gates can be bound," I said instead. "Joubert proved it. Sealed, opened, or bound into a treaty-line. There is a third way that does not drown the coast."

"A third way," Veyra said, "that requires some cartographer to erase himself from existence. We have read his marginalia too, heir. Did you think the Guild man was the only one who could read?" She let that sit, then leaned forward. "One name, gone forever. One life, unwritten. Against that: the return of a kingdom. Two hundred years of exile ended. The dead given back their graves and the living given back their sea." Her eyes were kind, which was the worst part. "You are asking an entire people to accept one stranger's sacrifice as the price of everything, and calling the alternative *impatience*."

"That stranger has not offered," I said. "That is rather the point."

"He will. Or he won't, and then we do it ourselves, with blood, as our grandmothers did before the sealing. The rites exist. The bloodlines exist. Nineteen days, heir. The tide decides, not you."

*The tide decides, not you.* I heard the sentence land in the hall and be received — not with cheers, because these people were too old and too tired for cheers, but with nods. Small, grave nods, the nods of people confirming a transaction already made. My mother's silence again. My whole inheritance in one soundless moment.

I am told that drowning feels calm at the end. So, apparently, does being devoured by your own cause.

* * *

Afterward I went up out of the halls to the seawall, because I needed sky, and because there is a stretch of broken masonry where you can stand with the winter wind coming straight off the open water and feel appropriately small. Ada Joubert found me there. Of course he did. The man has an unerring instinct for finding people at their least composed; I suspect he catalogued it somewhere, filed under *unfortunate talents*.

"You missed the meeting," I said.

"I didn't miss anything. I stood by the door and listened until Veyra started listing the dead, and then I left, because I've been listed at enough meetings this year." He settled against the wall beside me, careful of his bandaged arm, and looked out at the grey water with the expression of a man doing inventory. "They want to kill the coast, don't they. Before the storms close the channels."

"They want their home back."

"So did the people in the coastal cities, once. On top of whatever was under it." He said it without heat. That was the thing about Joubert — even bleeding, even hunted, he carried his bitterness the way a clerk carries files: organized, labeled, available for reference. "Sarel. Can I ask you something impertinent?"

"You ask things impertinently or not at all. It's why I haven't killed you."

"If the ritual needs royal blood — and the old rites always do, bloodline is the lock and the key both, your grandmother's journal was very clear —" He turned to look at me. "Whose blood opens the gates?"

The wind filled the pause for me, which was considerate of it.

"Mine," I said. "Or enough of mine. Diluted across volunteers from the old families, if the purists insist on tradition over efficiency. There are still Hallow-tide lines, salt-bonded lines, half the market claims descent from somebody." I smiled, and felt it come out crooked. "We are a nation of pretenders, Joubert. It's practically our coat of arms."

"And you'd do it. Pour your own blood into a working that drowns thirty thousand people who never signed anything."

"I'd do it to bring back a kingdom that never signed its own death, either."

"There it is," he said softly. "The arithmetic."

"It isn't arithmetic. It's justice."

"No," he said, with the mildness of a man correcting a column sum, "justice would be making the Guild pay. This is making the coast pay on the Guild's behalf. They're not the same page, Sarel. They aren't even the same book." He pushed off the wall, wincing. "For what it's worth — and I know what my opinion is worth, I'm a fugitive ledger-keeper with one good arm — Veyra's right about one thing. Nineteen days. Whatever anyone chooses, it gets chosen fast."

He left me alone with the wind, which is the cruelest thing a careful man can do.

* * *

That night I went down into the archive vaults beneath the market — the real ones, the Merethi ones, the shelves of waterproofed cedar that my grandmother's generation had carried out of the drowning piece by piece at the cost of eleven lives per shelf, though we don't put that number in the orientation speech. I took a lamp and the copy of Brannic's grandmother's journal that Ada had let me read, and I sat among the records of the dead and tried to find the version of myself who would not do the terrible thing.

I looked for her for a long time.

Here is what nobody tells heirs of lost kingdoms: you are raised as a vessel. Everything — the language lessons, the genealogies, the grief drilled into you in orderly seasonal remembrances — everything is poured into you so that one day you can be poured out. My mother died believing I would open the gates. Half the elders believe it is the only reason the bloodline was preserved at all through two centuries of thin soup and thinner mercy. I did not choose to be the argument my people make against their own erasure. I was *appointed*.

And appointed things break along the lines other people drew.

I found the passage I was looking for near dawn. The grandmother — Ilset, her name was Ilset, a fish-salter's daughter with no title at all — wrote about the last night above water. Not the king's last night. The commoner's. She wrote about her neighbor refusing to leave his shop. About the boats that came too late and the boats that never came at all. About standing on a hill with three hundred others, watching the water take the roofs, and an old priest beside her saying, quietly, not to her, not to anyone: *the king bargained for us and lost, and now we are the coin.*

She survived. She walked inland. She raised children who raised children who raised Brannic, who carried a cart and a grudge and a one-eyed view of the world, and who died on a bridge holding it against the Guild so that a map could keep moving. I have done the arithmetic of that bridge more times than I can stand. Every path I choose walks across Brannic.

And in the margin of that final page, Ilset had written one line, small, in the cramped hand of a woman writing by moonlight because daylight belonged to hiding:

*If ever the sea comes back, let it ask us first.*

Not the king. Us. Let it ask *us* first.

I sat in the vault until my lamp burned low, and I understood two things with perfect clarity. The first was that Veyra's faction would force the gates within nineteen days whether I blessed it or not — they had the rites, the lines, the numbers, and my objection had become, in their telling, not conscience but hesitation, and hesitation in a leader is just cowardice wearing a crown.

The second was that I could stop them. There was exactly one way for an heir to stop a blood rite, and it was the way my grandmother's journal had been trying to teach me since I was six years old, if I had ever been listening.

An heir can abdicate.

But an abdication, in the old law, must be witnessed, recorded, and entered into the archive in the heir's own hand — and the moment my renunciation touched those pages, the bloodline claim passed to the next in succession. Which was Veyra. Who was not royal, but was *salt-bonded*, which the purists had spent thirty years arguing was purer anyway, and who would perform the rite before the first storm tide with a clear heart and my family's blessing, literally, in writing.

I could refuse the drowning of the coast. All it cost was handing my people to someone who wouldn't hesitate.

I laughed, alone in the vault, and it echoed wrong among the cedar shelves. My mother had warned me about this exact trap. *A cause is a current, Sarel,* she said, when I was twelve and full of fire about raiding Guild shipping. *It moves you whether you swim or not. Your only choice is which shore you're aimed at.* I had nodded. Twelve-year-olds nod beautifully.

Somewhere overhead, through sixty feet of stone and market noise, the tide was turning. I could feel it in the walls the way the old bloodlines always claimed they could — a long slow exhale under everything, the sea remembering its schedule. Nineteen days. Eighteen, now. The channels would hold a little longer. The storm season would close them after that, and every option except the blood rite would freeze solid until spring, and spring was a country Veyra refused to believe in.

I closed Ilset's journal. I snuffed the lamp. And I made the decision that I would spend the rest of whatever life I had either redeeming or regretting:

I would not abdicate. Abdication solved nothing and surrendered everything. Instead I would do the thing my line had never once tried in two hundred years of exile.

I would lead.

Not ahead of them — they had followed heirs ahead of them into ruin often enough. Behind them, for once. Into the vaults, into the records, into the truth that Ada Joubert kept carrying up out of the dark like a man bailing a boat with a teacup: that the gates could be *bound*, that there existed a third shore between drowning and sealing, and that the price was one name — and that maybe, if I put my own name forward first, loudly, publicly, irreversibly, before Veyra could marshal her rites, the arithmetic would finally have a denominator everyone could see.

My blood for the binding. My name out of every ledger. My mother's vessel, poured out at last on something other than vengeance.

It was a mad plan. It required Ada's cooperation, Wren Halloway's silence, the archive's cooperation, and a miracle regarding the tides, and I had perhaps eighteen days to arrange all four.

I was climbing back toward the market stairs when the sentry found me, breathless, lantern swinging.

"Heir — the Guild man — Joubert. He's gone. His cot's cold, his kit's gone, and Wren Halloway's missing with him." The sentry swallowed. "And Veyra's called the blood-lines to the gate shrine. Tonight. She says the storm season doesn't wait for the heir's permission."

The sea, far below, turned over in its sleep.

So much for leading from behind.
