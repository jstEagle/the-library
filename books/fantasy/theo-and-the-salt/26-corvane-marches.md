## 26. Corvane Marches

I have burned a great many things in my life, and I want to be clear from the outset that I have never once enjoyed it.

People assume otherwise. They look at a man who has ordered the destruction of three archives, two villages' worth of heretical charts, and one very stubborn lighthouse keeper's collection of tide-almanacs, and they imagine he must feel *something*—fury, or hunger, or the dark thrill of a god playing with fire. I have read the popular novels. In them, men like me stroke our chins and smile at flames.

The truth is considerably more administrative. Fire is a tool with an excellent cost-benefit profile and a terrible public reputation. You do not enjoy fire. You *schedule* it.

On the morning I marched on the Tide Market, I woke at my usual hour, ate my usual breakfast of black bread and salted herring—the irony was not lost on me; I have always appreciated a good irony the way other men appreciate wine—and reviewed the operational ledger twice. Then I put on my grey coat, the unremarkable one, because a Guildmaster who dresses for conquest invites commentary, and commentary is the first symptom of disorder.

Let no one say Aldous Corvane does not understand theatre. I simply prefer theatre where the audience learns nothing until the curtain has already fallen.

* * *

The Tide Market sat in the drowned estuary of the Vell, half a day's ride south of Saltbridge, in a basin the old charts marked as *unsound ground* and the new ones—I had seen to this personally—marked as nothing at all. A blank space. Cartography's oldest lie: that which is not drawn cannot be found, and that which cannot be found need not be governed.

For two centuries the exiles had lived in that blank space like ink living in the margin. They traded there—salt-charms, drowned glass, relics fished up from Mereth's shallows, and worse things whose provenance I did not care to certify. Smugglers called it free ground. The coastal cities called it a nuisance. My predecessors in the Guildmaster's chair had called it *tolerable*, on the theory that a market you can see is a market you can watch.

My predecessors were accountants. I am an auditor. The distinction matters more than either of us would have liked.

Here is what my ledgers told me, and I ask you to follow the arithmetic, because everyone else involved in this affair refused to:

Item: the courier Tavin Corvane—my sister's son, though that relationship appears in no ledger I maintain—had carried a map of Mereth out of the Guild's own archive, or into it, depending on which witness you believed, and had died for it in a stairwell smelling of lamp-oil and old paper.

Item: a ledger-keeper named Ada Joubert had been present at the death, and had since vanished from his lodgings along with a survey kit, a river barge passage, and—this detail pleased me enormously—a copy of the Guild's own fugitive-mapping protocols, which he had evidently studied well enough to make himself briefly difficult to find. Briefly. I want that recorded too. He was difficult the way a knot is difficult. Knots yield to patience and a knife.

Item: Sarel of Mereth, heir to a kingdom that had been underwater for two hundred years and would remain so regardless of anyone's feelings on the subject, was gathering exiles at the Tide Market with the declared intention of opening the gates.

Item, and attend to this one, because it is the whole of my case: the gates, when opened, raise the tide-storm. The tide-storm drowns the coast. On the coast live four hundred thousand people, give or take a census season, none of whom had done anything to Mereth except be born on land that someone else remembered owning.

That was the entire argument. I did not need to believe the exiles wicked. I did not need to believe their cause unjust. I needed only to believe that four hundred thousand lives outweighed the grievances of the dead, and I did believe it, the way I believe water is wet and ledgers balance. Some truths are not convictions. They are simply the figures, added correctly.

Everything else—the burning of records, the seizure of goods, the regrettable necessity of force—followed from the arithmetic as night follows the closing of accounts.

You may call it ruthlessness if it comforts you. I call it addition.

* * *

We came down the Vell road at dawn in three columns: forty enforcers in grey, two wagons of lamp-oil and lime, and a survey team under Master Ellery Vance, who had drawn the approach maps himself and wept quietly about it the whole way, because Vance is the sort of cartographer who believes every place he draws becomes somehow less real, and he had drawn the Tide Market six times in preparation for erasing it.

"The residents will scatter," Vance said, as the basin came into view below us—the crooked rooftops, the salt-white banners, the smell of brine and frying fish rising up even at this distance. "They always scatter. Markets are fluid populations."

"Markets are *inventories*," I said, "that have learned to walk. We will inventory them."

He gave me a look I have received ten thousand times in my career, the look that says *how can you speak of people this way*, and I gave him back the look I always give, the one that says *because no one else will do the arithmetic honestly*. Between those two looks, the Guild has functioned for two centuries.

Below us, the market was waking. I could hear it—gulls, haggling, a bell rung by some exile tradition I had never bothered to learn. And beneath it, faintly, something else: a low harmonic off the water, the sound the estuary made at certain tides, which the exiles swore was singing and which our acousticians had long ago established was wind through drowned chimneys.

Both explanations were true. That is the trouble with this whole business, if you want my private opinion, which no one has asked for in thirty years: both explanations are always true. Wind through chimneys. A kingdom singing under the water. Add the columns together and you still have to decide which total governs your actions, and I decided mine a long time ago, standing in this same office over this same coast, listening to survivors of the last small flood describe how the water had come up their streets like a landlord coming for rent.

We went down.

* * *

I will pass quickly over the fighting, not because it shames me but because it was brief and undistinguished, which is the best one can hope for. The exiles fought. I had expected them to fight and had budgeted accordingly. What I had not fully anticipated—though I should have; I had read the same histories everyone else had—was the *records*.

Every stall, every strongbox, every wall-niche in the Tide Market held paper. Family rolls written in Merethi script. Tide-calendars kept by hand for generations, each grandmother correcting her mother's errors in a different ink. Ships' logs of the crossing-exodus, when the last boats left the drowning city. Marriage registers, debt books, children's primers with the alphabet of a dead language copied out in a child's wobbling hand.

They had spent two hundred years becoming archivists. Of course they had. When your country exists only in documents, documents become the country. I understood this better than any of them knew, and understanding it changed nothing, which is the most instructive thing I can tell you about power.

Master Vance stood in the market square with the burning-list in his hands and looked at me across a stack of tide-calendars.

"Aldous." He used my given name, which meant he was frightened. "These are—" He turned a page with one careful finger. "This is a continuous record. Two hundred years, unbroken. There are perhaps nine such records in the world. This is a *primary source*."

"It is also a recruitment document," I said. "Every name in it is proof to the next generation that Mereth existed, that Mereth was taken, and that the taking can be mourned loudly enough to raise an army. I have read the tide-market sermons, Ellery. They quote these calendars. Verse by verse."

"They're *almanacs*."

"They are memory, and memory is munitions. Burn them."

He burned them. Vance is a good servant of the Guild precisely because he hates the work, because a man who loves burning will eventually burn the wrong things, whereas a man who grieves each volume will weigh it properly before consigning it. I watched him feed the calendars into the fire one at a time, with the tenderness of a man burying relatives, and I thought—not for the first time—that the Guild's conscience had not died when we took Mereth. It had simply been assigned to other people, so that the rest of us could keep the totals.

Somewhere in the smoke a woman was screaming a name over and over, and I noted, in the part of my mind that never stops working, that grief is also an inventory: she was counting what she had lost, item by item, the way I count everything. We were doing the same mathematics on opposite sides of a bonfire, and only one of us had the soldiers.

This is what victory is. I have never lied to myself about it, whatever else I may have lied about.

* * *

By noon the market was ours. The stalls stood empty, the fires had been raked, and the exiles themselves were being processed in the fish-curing sheds at the basin's edge—names taken, tokens confiscated, families sorted by threat assessment, which sounds monstrous in the telling and consists, in practice, of asking each person whether they intended to open the gates and writing down the answer, true or otherwise, because the answer itself mattered far less than having asked.

Sarel was not among them. This did not surprise me. An heir keeps away from markets; markets are for hands, and heirs must remain heads. But I confess the absence itched at me, and I do not tolerate itching.

What troubled me more was the second absence.

"Joubert," said Captain Ferro, handing me the morning's interrogation summary as we walked the emptied square. "None of them will admit to seeing him. Which is itself remarkable—they'll admit to everything else. Sedition, hoarding, the lot. But the ledger-keeper's name comes up and they go quiet as oysters."

"Because he saved their map," I said, "or stole it, depending on whose story you credit, and either act makes him theirs now. Loyalty is not gratitude, Captain. It is investment. They think he will pay dividends."

"And will he?"

"He will pay *costs*," I said. "Everyone pays costs. The only question is who collects." I stopped at the edge of the square, where the ground fell away toward the drowned quarter—the true drowned quarter, the streets of old Mereth visible at low tide like a bruise under the water's skin. Gulls wheeled over it. Somewhere beneath, gates that my predecessors had sealed and I had sworn to keep sealed waited with what I have always suspected was patience rather than dormancy.

Ferro followed my gaze. "You believe he means to open them."

"I believe Ada Joubert is a man who has discovered, late in life and badly, that he is capable of mattering," I said. "Such men are the most dangerous creatures in creation, because they have no practice at weighing consequences. A man who has always mattered hesitates. A man who has never mattered leaps." I turned from the water. "Find him. And find Wren Halloway, who is, I am quite sure, already finding him, in defiance of her orders and her training and her own best interests. Love makes the finest tracking hound ever bred, Captain. It wants so badly to be right about its object that it will follow any trail."

"You speak as though you've had experience."

"I speak as though I've read the ledgers," I said. "Experience merely confirms them."

* * *

That evening I sat in the commandeered customs house above the emptying basin and wrote the official account of the day, which history will receive if history receives anything from me, and which read as follows:

*On this date, acting upon evidence of armed sedition and in fulfillment of the Guild's founding obligation to protect the mapped world from unmapped powers, the Cartographers' Guild secured the Tide Market against the forces of the Merethi pretender. Records of insurgent propaganda were destroyed in accordance with protocol. No civilian casualties resulted from Guild action. The coastal cities stand protected.*

Every word of it true. That is the trick, if you want to know it, the one secret I will carry to whatever grave is allotted me: you need not lie. Lying is for amateurs and novelists. You need only select. The truth is an enormous warehouse, and somewhere in it, on some shelf, there is always a version of events that serves. I have never told a lie in my professional life. I have merely been an exceptionally thorough shopper.

And here is the thing I will admit, here in the privacy of my own head, where I keep the accounts no one audits:

I believe all of it. That is the part no one will ever credit. They will paint me with lust for the drowned treasures, with fear of losing my chair, with some ancient guilt about Mereth gnawing at my soul. None of it. I have seen the storm-models. I helped commission them, twenty years ago, after the Harrow flood took eleven villages and the Guild's response was to *reclassify the coastline*. Four hundred thousand people. I have walked those streets. I have eaten their bread and signed their harbor charters and buried, yes, one sister, one nephew, and one version of myself that once believed maps should serve truth rather than survival.

If the gates open, they die. Not abstractly. By name, by street, by parish register. I have read the projected lists; I keep them in the drawer beneath my seal, and I have read them so often I could recite parishes like psalms.

So let the novels give me a chin to stroke and flames to smile at. Let them call me the butcher of the Tide Market, if the word ever gets out, which it will; words always get out; it is why we burn paper. Let them hate me thoroughly and inaccurately.

I am saving the coast. That is the whole of it. That is the immaculate sum.

The only flaw in the arithmetic—one flaw, and I record it because I record everything—is a woman named Wren Halloway, who was raised on these very ledgers and taught by my own hand that a map is a promise, and who is at this hour, I am certain of it, walking toward the one man alive who might redraw everything I have just defended.

I have decided to let her walk.

Not sentiment. Arithmetic. Because Halloway will reach Joubert, and Joubert will lead her to the map, and wherever the map goes, the gates remember—and a man who knows where his enemies must gather needs never chase them again.

I poured the evening's last cup, set the storm-lists back in their drawer, and wrote a single instruction for the morning watch:

*Follow the surveyor.*
