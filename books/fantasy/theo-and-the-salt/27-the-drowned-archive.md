## 27. The Drowned Archive

*Ada*

The tide gave us one hour. That's what Brannic's grandmother's journal said, in her cramped Merethi hand: *the sea pulls back its hem at the turn of the moon, and for one hour the drowned streets remember being streets.*

I had read that line a dozen times since Saltbridge, and each time I'd told myself it was poetry. Grief-poetry, written by a woman who'd watched her city go under and needed to believe the water was only borrowing it.

It was not poetry. I was standing on it.

The bay at Vell's Mouth had retreated maybe two hundred paces past its low-water mark, exposing a seabed of black sand and weed-slick stone and—rising out of it like the ribs of something enormous—a street. Cobbled. Gridded. Lined with doorways whose doors had rotted away centuries ago, leaving gaps like missing teeth. Streetlamps of green glass still stood at the corners, their iron posts furred with barnacle, and when the low sun caught them they glowed faintly, some alchemical ember inside refusing, after two hundred years, to admit the war was over.

"Well," Wren said beside me. She was already shrugging out of her coat, already checking the straps of the diving rig we'd bought from a smuggler who hadn't asked questions because we paid him not to have any. "You catalogued this?"

"I catalogued a survey of it," I said. "Entry eleven thousand four hundred and six. *Coastal chart, unreliable; surveyor reports submerged structures inconsistent with known geography.* The Guild filed it under Anomalies, Third Class."

"Which means?"

"Which means too strange to map, too boring to investigate." I looked down the long drowned avenue, at the green lamps burning their patient underwater dusk. "The Guild's filing system has killed more truths than any censor. It just buries them politely."

Wren snorted—the short, unimpressed sound she'd been making at me since we were nineteen—and shouldered the coil of rope. "Then let's go annoy an archivist."

She meant me. I chose to be flattered.

We went down the street while the water was still holding its breath around our ankles, following the journal's instructions: *past the lamp with three panes, left at the fishmonger's arch, down to where the road gives up.* The fishmonger's arch was easy to find—it had carved fish leaping along its curve, their scales worn to ghosts but their mouths still open, still mid-leap, frozen in the moment before the water came. Someone had loved this city enough to put leaping things on its doorways. Leaping things, on a city built below the tide-line. I tried not to think about what that optimism had cost them.

At the third lamp—one pane, cracked—I stopped, because the journal said *left*, and there was no left. There was a wall of collapsed masonry where the street should have continued, tumbled blocks furred white with coral, sealing off whatever lay beyond as thoroughly as any Guild vault.

"The journal's two hundred years old," Wren said. "Buildings fall."

"Buildings fall," I agreed. "But—" And here is the thing I will defend to my grave, the thing that ten years of shelving other people's maps had ground into me: "But collapse isn't random. Look at the scatter pattern. Those blocks didn't tumble, they were *placed*. Loose ones on top, load-bearing ones underneath. Someone roofed over this street on purpose."

Wren crouched, ran her hand along a block's edge, and I watched the surveyor in her take over from everything else—the set of her shoulders changing, her eyes going flat and technical. "Mortar's fresh-ish. Relatively. Last fifty years, maybe less." She glanced up at me, and there was salt-light in her expression, the particular fierce joy she got from a problem with edges. "Someone's been maintaining a door at the bottom of the sea."

"Exiles come back for relics," I said. "Sarel's people dive these outskirts all the time."

"Not all the time." She stood. "Not *here*. This isn't salvage, Ada. Salvage leaves holes. This is a sealed archive with a doorman."

I wanted to point out that we were about to burgle a library guarded by someone competent, located underwater, during a tidal window measured in minutes, while both the Guild and Sarel's faction hunted us. I wanted very much to say all of that.

What I said was: "Help me move the top course."

Because that's the truth about Wren Halloway that I'd spent ten years trying to file away and failing: I would follow her into anything, and she, apparently, into anywhere. Some entries don't cross-reference. They just agree.

The top blocks came away easier than they had any right to—we levered them with a wrecking bar and the whole course shifted like a lid designed to be lifted by two people who knew what they were doing. Behind them: darkness, and dry air exhaling up out of it, cold and mineral-smelling, tasting of ink.

Dry. Air. Under the bay.

"Air pocket," Wren breathed. "The whole quarter must be domed under the collapse—old construction trick, the Merethi built against flood, they—" She stopped. We both heard it, from somewhere down in the dark: a soft rhythmic sound. Like pages turning.

Or like breathing.

We looked at each other. In the grey light off the wet stones, her face was pale and set and utterly certain, and I understood that whatever was down there, we were both going to walk toward it.

"After you," I said, because I'm a coward with good manners.

"No," Wren said, taking the lamp. "Together."

* * *

*Wren*

Here is what nobody tells you about treason: it gets easier by increments, and then all at once, and then you're swimming through a flooded street in a dead kingdom holding a lamp for a man the Guild wants dead, and you realize you can't remember which side of the line you crossed or when, only that the water's fine.

I'd reported the courier's lodging. True report. I'd omitted Ada. True report. I'd stood in Corvane's office while he smiled at me with his ledger-keeper's smile and known he saw every omission like a watermark, and I'd held my voice steady anyway. Each step had felt survivable. This one—this one felt different. This one felt like the bottom of the stairs.

Ada went through the gap first anyway, because he was lying about being a coward the way he lied about everything: completely, and so consistently that it had become a kind of honesty. I followed him into the dark with the lamp held high.

The air pocket was real and vast. The lamp found walls—stone, dressed, carved with the same leaping fish—and a floor of the black sand, dry as ash. And it found shelves.

Rows of them. Stone shelves cut into the walls, receding back further than the light could follow, and on them, wrapped in waxed cloth and oiled leather against two centuries of damp: books. Scrolls. Tablets. Bundles of reed-pens tied with cord gone brittle. A civilization's paperwork, filed and shelved and waiting, in a room at the bottom of the sea that somebody had kept *dry*.

"Oh," Ada said. It wasn't a word so much as a wound.

He walked forward like a man in a dream, trailing his fingers above the nearest shelf without touching, reading spines in that half-aloud murmur he did when a catalogue was writing itself behind his eyes. "*Registry of Gate-Tolls. Annals of the Tide Wardens. Correspondence, Office of Treaties—*" His voice caught. "Wren. This is the administrative quarter. This is where they *kept the records.*"

"And the Guild burned theirs," I said. I'd seen the ledgers Corvane's people fed into stoves across thirty years of service. I'd always assumed age and rot. Now I assumed neither.

"Of course they burned theirs." He wasn't even bitter about it; that was the frightening part. He sounded like a man confirming arithmetic. "You don't falsify a history and leave the receipts."

The breathing sound came again—closer now, and I had my knife out before I'd decided to draw it, and Ada had stepped *in front of me*, which was so absurd I almost laughed. Him. In front of me. Ten years of cataloguing had given him spine if nothing else.

"Who's there?" he called, in Merethi. Badly. His accent was a crime scene.

Something moved between the far shelves, and resolved into a person: old, small, wrapped in oilcloth layers, face brown and seamed as driftwood, eyes pale as fish-belly. She carried a lamp of her own and a hooked blade that she held with the ease of long habit, and she looked us over the way you look at weather.

"You're not Sarel's," she said in accented Trade. "And you're not Guild, or you'd have brought more of you." Her gaze snagged on Ada. "You're the one from the market. The map-man."

"We're looking for the gate-ledger," I said, because Ada had gone stiff and strange, and one of us needed to function. "The original treaty records. From before the sealing."

The old woman laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as the room. "Everyone comes for the treaty. Kings want the clauses, exiles want the apology, the Guild wants the parts that flatter them." She turned, unhurried, and beckoned with the hooked blade. "Nobody ever asks what the wardens wrote *after*. Come. My name is Maren. I keep the archive. My mother kept it, and hers, and we are the reason your Guild never found it, so mind your manners and touch nothing without asking."

* * *

*Ada*

Maren led us deep into the shelves, past annals and toll-registries and the mundane immortal paperwork of a drowned state, to a case of stone at the room's heart, and opened it with a key she wore on a cord around her neck, and took out a book.

It was nothing. That was my first thought—two hundred years of legend, and the object itself was a ledger like ten thousand I'd shelved: boards rubbed bare, spine stitched twice-repaired, corners bumped by hands long dead. Its cover read, in formal Merethi script, *Office of the Gates: Master Register of Bindings.*

My hands shook opening it. I noted, distantly, that they were shaking, and that I hated them for it, and that they ignored me.

The early pages were exactly what I expected: survey records, oath-texts, the signatures of the first Guild cartographers alongside Merethi wardens, binding the gates by mutual compact. I knew this history. Everyone knew this history. The Guild came, the Guild made peace, the Guild sealed the gates to end the tide-wars, and then—tragedy, betrayal, drowning, the whole litany Brannic had grown up on.

But ledgers are honest in a way histories aren't. Ledgers have margins. And in the margins of the Master Register, in a later hand, in ink that had gone brown, someone had written:

*Binding renewed, year 12. Binding renewed, year 19. Binding renewed, year 31 — note: the Guild's envoys have ceased to attend. Renewal performed by Merethi wardens alone.*

Year 31 was the year of the drowning.

"Wren," I said. My voice came out wrong. "Look at the renewal dates."

She leaned in, and I watched her read the way she read charts—fast, total, no second pass. "They stopped coming. Before the end." Her finger traced the margin notes downward, entry by thinning entry. "The binding needs renewal. Periodic. Both parties. And the Guild just... walked away from the table."

"And the wardens kept paying the toll alone." I turned pages. Year 31. Year 32—the handwriting deteriorating, the renewals scrawled by lamplight, by desperate people. Year 33: *performed under siege conditions.* Year 34: a single line, in a shaking hand: *Last warden alive performs the rite alone. It holds. Barely. Whoever finds this: the gates are not sealed. They are UNMAINTAINED.*

The room was very quiet. Even Maren's lamp seemed to burn more softly, listening.

"That's why the tide-storm builds," Wren said slowly. "Every winter, worse. It's not the gates failing open. It's the binding decaying. There's no failure-state where nothing happens. Either someone renews the treaty or the gates rot through."

"Yes," Maren said. "Two hundred years, and the coastal cities have been living downstream of an unmaintained lock. Your Guild calls the storms acts of the drowned king. They are acts of *neglect*." She spat the last word with two centuries of inheritance behind it.

I sat down on the floor. I don't remember deciding to; my legs simply resigned. Because I had held the map, the courier's map, the key everyone wanted to turn—and I had thought the choice was binary, open or closed, drown the coast or starve the exiles, fire or water. Every faction had taught me the same lesson: there are two doors.

But a ledger is a third document. A ledger says: *this was a contract, and contracts have terms, and terms can be renegotiated.*

"It can be bound," I said aloud, testing the shape of it. "Not sealed—not broken shut the way the stories say. *Bound.* Renewed. Rewritten. If the original register survives—" I was talking faster now, the old archive-fever rising, the joy that had made a boring life bearable for ten years suddenly the most dangerous thing in the world. "If the register survives, then the map isn't a key. Keys only lock or unlock. It's a *page*. Pages get amended."

Wren crouched in front of me. Close. Lamp-lit. Her eyes were bright and terrified and hopeful all at once, and I thought: I have missed her face across a decade of careful distance, and I am done pretending otherwise, and also we are probably about to die.

"Amended by whom?" she asked quietly.

That was the question, wasn't it. Not the Guild—they'd amend it into a monopoly. Not Sarel—he'd amend it into vengeance with better paperwork. It needed a neutral hand. It needed someone whose entire talent was making themselves invisible in the record. It needed—

Later. I filed it. Even then, with the answer already forming in the back of my skull like a watermark, I filed it, because Wren was close and warm and alive and the tide was turning, and some readings you make one column at a time.

"Maren," I said instead. "How long do we have this air?"

"Tide turns within the hour. You'll swim out or stay forever; those are the options; they're always the options here." She watched me wrap the register in its oilcloth with hands that had finally stopped shaking, because they had work now. "You'll take it to your Sarel? To your Guildmaster?"

"No," Wren and I said, together, in perfect unison—and then we looked at each other, and despite everything, despite the drowned city and the dying clock and the war assembling itself above us, we both started laughing. Helpless, exhausted, salt-stung laughter, echoing down two hundred years of shelved silence.

"To whom, then?" Maren demanded.

"To whoever's willing to read the whole page," I said, standing, register under my arm, Wren's shoulder solid against mine. "All the columns. Including the margins."

Above us, faint through the stone, the sea began to pull its hem back on.
