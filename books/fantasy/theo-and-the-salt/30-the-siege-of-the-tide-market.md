## 30. The Siege of the Tide Market

### Ada

I have catalogued the destruction of cities. I've read the surveys, the casualty ledgers, the neat columns where a Guild clerk reduced ten thousand lives to a figure and a footnote. I always assumed that when a city died, it made a sound.

It doesn't. It makes many sounds, all at once, none of them agreeing with each other.

The tide market came apart around me like a book dropped from a great height—spines cracking, pages flying, everything that had been ordered suddenly everywhere. The first firework of Guild flare-light went up over the eastern arches and turned the whole cavern of the market red, and in that red light I saw what I had spent three weeks refusing to see: that every person here had been waiting for this. Not fearing it. Waiting for it. The fishwives who sold drowned-pearls, the carvers of whalebone saints, the children running messages through the stalls—they didn't scream when the horns sounded. They picked up weapons they'd hidden under their goods for generations, and their faces were not afraid. They were *relieved*.

That frightened me more than the enforcers did.

"Joubert!" Brannic would have shouted it, but Brannic was dead on a bridge two days south, and there was no one left to shout my name except people who wanted me for what I carried. So I shouted it to myself, inside my own skull, like an inventory clerk confirming stock: *Ada Joubert, one map, one life, both compromised.*

I ran.

The market was built in the ribs of a beached leviathan some cartographer had once measured at four hundred feet and then, sensibly, given up on. Its spine was the main thoroughfare; its ribs were the covered lanes; its heart—a hollow chamber near the stern where the exiles kept their oldest records—was where Sarel had gone to arm his people. And beyond it, past the market's broken tail, down through the sea-caves the exiles called the Throat, lay the gates.

Everyone wanted the same thing tonight. That's the thing no ledger prepares you for. Corvane wanted the gates opened so he could tax what came through them. Sarel wanted them opened so he could drown what stood before them. Both of them were certain. Certainty, I have come to believe, is just hunger wearing a good coat.

I threaded between the ribs of the dead beast while the world burned behind me.

* * *

### Wren

The Guild does not teach you how to fight in a burning market. The Guild teaches you how to *inventory* a burning market, which is a different skill entirely and one I discovered I possessed at the worst possible moment of my life.

"Third quarter, unsecured," I heard myself say, crouched behind an overturned stall while crossbow bolts stitched the air above me. "Exiles holding the rib-lanes. Flare reserves low. Recommend—" and then I stopped, because I heard my own voice and it was Corvane's voice, calm and tidy, sorting the end of a people into columns.

Enforcer Dane crouched beside me, reloading. "Recommend what, Halloway?"

"Nothing." I put a bolt into the lane and hit nothing, because I was aiming at nothing. "Push on."

Here is what I had learned in the last six days, and learned too late to be useful: you can commit treason in small pieces, each one defensible, each one survivable, until you look down and find you've built a staircase out of them leading somewhere you never chose to go. Omitting Ada's name from my first report. Omitting the gate-ledger from my second. Telling Dane the Throat caves were flooded when I knew they weren't. Each omission had felt like mercy. Together they had delivered me here, to the siege of the only free place the exiles had left, carrying a Guild warrant with my signature on the manifest.

And somewhere in this smoke, Ada Joubert was running toward the gates with a map that could end or save everything, and my orders—the ones I'd signed—were to take him alive if convenient.

"If convenient," I said aloud.

"What?" said Dane.

"Nothing. Left lane. Move."

We moved. And in the doorway of the record-chamber, silhouetted against his own burning history, I saw Sarel of Mereth standing very still with a sword he clearly hoped not to use, watching his people's archives go up in Guild fire, and I understood—for one clean, horrible second—that I was looking at what Ada must have looked like in the archive the night the courier died. A keeper of records watching records burn.

I raised my crossbow and aimed it, deliberately, at a beam instead.

* * *

### Corvane

There is a moment in every operation—I have presided over forty-one—when the plan stops being a plan and becomes weather. You cannot direct weather. You can only dress for it.

I stood on the cliff above the sea-caves with my survey-glass and watched the tide market burn, and I will record honestly, since honesty is the luxury of the victor: I had not intended the fires. My writ authorized seizure of contraband maps and detention of ringleaders. But Sergeant Vell had interpreted "secure the archive" as "burn the archive," on the reasonable grounds that a burned archive is the most secure archive there is, and by the time the error reached me it had become a fact, and facts are the raw material of policy.

"The exiles are falling back to the Throat," Vell reported. "Sarel is unaccounted for."

"He'll be at the gates." I lowered the glass. "He has always been going to be at the gates. Everything else tonight was furniture."

Vell hesitated. He is a good sergeant, which is to say he asks questions exactly once. "Guildmaster. The civilians in the lower lanes. If we press—"

"Civilians." I considered the word. "Sergeant, do you know what the coastal cities were before Mereth fell? Fishing villages. Mud and herring. Two hundred years of peace, of trade, of children who have never seen a tide-storm—that is what stands on the ruins down there. Tonight we decide whether it continues." I handed him back the glass. "Every death in those lanes is on the conscience of whoever sealed the gates badly the first time. That wasn't us. We're the correction."

This is true. I believe it the way I believe in compound interest: completely, and with full awareness of what the belief costs other people.

"Halloway's team has the rib-lanes," Vell said. "She reports progress."

"Of course she does." Wren Halloway had been reporting progress for six days, each report precise, each report hollow, each one missing exactly the thing I needed it to contain. I had let her run, because a huntress who thinks she is hunting freely comes back to the trap on her own. But the market was burning now, and patience is a currency, and I was spending the last of mine.

"Send word to Halloway," I said. "The traitor Joubert is making for the gates. She is to stop him. By any means." I paused. "Tell her the means matter to me personally."

* * *

### Ada

The Throat was exactly as Brannic had described it, which was the last gift he gave me: *"When the time comes, don't think. The water goes out, you go in, you count two hundred steps wet stone, then the walls start singing. If the walls sing wrong, you've taken someone else's path, and the sea takes its toll."*

The water had gone out. I went in.

Two hundred steps, counting through the boom of distant fire and the slap of my own boots. At step one-sixty the walls began to hum—not singing, really, more like the sound a tuning fork remembers—and I pressed my palm against the stone and felt it answer, faintly, the way a struck bell answers a bell you can't hear yet.

"I'm coming," I told the walls, and hated myself for talking to architecture, and kept moving.

Behind me, the market burned. Ahead of me, the gates waited. And between those two facts, I finally admitted the thing I had been circling for weeks: I did not know whose side I was on, because there were no sides. There were hungers. Corvane hungered for monopoly. Sarel hungered for restoration, which is vengeance with better tailoring. The exiles in the burning lanes hungered for anything at all after two hundred years of salt and waiting. Even Wren—

Even Wren hungered for something, and I was afraid it was me, and more afraid it was her Guild, and most afraid that she couldn't tell the difference anymore either.

The map rode against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from my body. Everyone wanted it. Every faction's certainty pointed at it like a compass needle. And the terrible joke—the one I'd been carrying since the night a dying man bled on my ledger—was that the map didn't care. Maps never do. They wait. They are the most patient form of hunger there is.

Step two hundred. The passage opened.

* * *

### Sarel

I had imagined this night a thousand times, and in none of those imaginings was I standing in ankle-deep seawater arguing with my own aunt.

"The ritual needs a tide-storm, not a battle," Meris said. She held the blood-knife the way our grandmothers held bread knives, practically, without reverence. "Give me the hour and the chamber and I will open the gates myself."

"You will open them onto a battlefield," I said. "Half the coast's fleet is in the sound. If the storm rises now—"

"Good," said Meris, and the word came out of her smooth and simple as water, and that simplicity was the most frightening thing in the whole burning night. "Let it rise. Let it take the fleet. Two hundred years ago they took everything of ours. The debt doesn't shrink because the debtors' grandchildren look sad about it."

"They didn't borrow anything," I said. "Their grandparents did. Or thought they did. Ada found the treaty, Meris. The Guild broke the oath first. Which means—" I stopped, because saying it aloud in front of her, in front of the armed faithful crowding the gate-chamber anteroom, was itself a kind of treason. "Which means the debt is complicated."

Meris smiled at me with real affection, which was worse than anger. "You sound like your father at the end. Complications. Conditions. *Perhaps we should ask what the ordinary people want.*" She turned to the crowd. "Your heir hesitates over arithmetic while the Guild burns your homes. Will you wait for him to finish calculating?"

The crowd did not cheer. That was the thing I would remember afterward, when I had time to remember anything: they didn't cheer. They just moved toward her, quietly, the way water moves toward a breach, and I understood that a cause can devour its heir without ever raising its voice, simply by letting everyone else agree.

Then the far wall of the anteroom rang like a struck bell, and a voice I knew—tired, sarcastic, terrified, mine to protect whether he liked it or not—shouted from the tunnel mouth:

"Don't!" Ada Joubert, soaked to the waist, oilcloth bundle clutched to his chest, looking like a drowned clerk, which in fairness was roughly what he was. "Don't open them tonight. Because if you do it by blood and storm, you'll never learn what I found in the deep archive. The gates can be *bound*. Not sealed. Not opened. Bound—to terms. To a treaty the map itself enforces." He held up the bundle. "But only if nobody opens them in the next hour. So. For once in all our lives, could everyone please just—wait?"

The silence lasted four seconds. Then the doors at the seaward end blew inward, and Guild enforcers came through with Wren Halloway at their head, and waiting stopped being an option for anyone.

* * *

### Wren

Later—if there was a later—I would try to reconstruct the next ten minutes and fail. Battles don't keep records. That's why the Guild writes them afterward, and why the Guild's versions are always lies.

What I have: fragments. Ada shouting about binding and treaties while exiles surged around him like he was a relic. Meris of Mereth raising the blood-knife and Sarel striking it from her hand—his own faction, his own blood, and he chose the stranger's arithmetic. Enforcers pouring through the seaward doors. Firelight. Water. The bell-hum of the gates themselves rising under everything like a second heartbeat.

And Dane, beside me, leveling his crossbow at Ada Joubert across the chamber.

My body decided before I did. That's the truth I'll keep, whatever else gets rewritten. I didn't weigh duty against love. I didn't choose. My arm simply came up and my bolt took Dane in the shoulder and he spun, and the shot that would have killed Ada went into the ceiling instead, and every eye in the gate-chamber turned to me—Guild colors, Guild weapon, Guild treason, all of it visible at once.

"Wren," Ada said. Just my name. As if the whole burning world were a ledger and I were the only entry that mattered.

"Hold the doors!" I shouted at the exiles, in Guild cant, and they were so surprised they obeyed. "Ada—the tunnel! Go!"

"And you?"

"I'm right behind you." I reloaded, turned, and put a bolt into the doorframe an inch from Sergeant Vell's ear, which bought us perhaps ten seconds and cost me everything else. "Go *now*."

He went. I followed. Behind us the gate-chamber filled with fire and shouting and the rising, ringing song of stone that remembered being a door, and ahead of us the tunnel ran down into the dark toward the one room in the world where everything would be decided.

I caught him at the junction where the passages met, both of us gasping, black water at our knees.

"The binding," I said. "The treaty. Tell me the price, Ada. All of it. Don't you dare soften it."

He looked at me in the dark, and I saw that he already knew I knew, and that he had known for days, and had planned to die with it unspoken.

"You'll hate it," he said.

"I've hated plenty tonight. One more won't sink me."

Above us, muffled by stone and water, the gates began to sing.
