## 31. Sarel's Choice

My grandmother used to say that vengeance is a debt you collect with interest you pay yourself.

She said it the way she said everything—half-drowned, half-laughing, salt crusted in her eyebrows, while she mended nets in a house built on a cliff that had once, three generations before her birth, been a street in Mereth. She was born above the water. Her mother had been born below it. That single fact organized our entire family like a spine organizes a body: everything bent around it, everything ached along it.

I thought of her now because I was about to commit an act she would have recognized instantly and, I think, ultimately refused. And I wanted—I wanted so badly it felt like drowning—for her to be wrong about me. For her to be standing behind my shoulder saying *no, child, not you too.*

The gate chamber smelled the way all deep places smell: stone and cold water and old iron, with something underneath that I have never found a word for except *waiting.* The gates themselves rose out of the black pool at the chamber's heart, twin doors of green-dark metal taller than a ship's mast, covered in script that predated every alphabet the dry world had ever invented. Two centuries they had stood shut. Two centuries my people had told stories about what lay behind them—the drowned halls, the pearl courts, the throne of my grandfather where the eels kept the lamps burning out of habit.

And tonight, if my faction had their way, those stories would end, one way or another.

*One way or another.* That was always the problem with us Merethi exiles. We never learned to want just one thing.

The blood ritual was laid out on the stones before me in the pattern the old texts demanded—a spiral of nine basins, each filled to its brim with seawater carried by hand from nine different shores, because the ritual required the sea's consent in installments, which should have told us something about the sea's opinion of the whole enterprise. My aunt Vess knelt at the outermost basin, her knife already drawn across her palm, her blood threading down into the water like red smoke. Behind her knelt forty more of ours, men and women who had lost everything twice over—once to the drowning, once to the two hundred years of being refugees in cities that tolerated them the way a wall tolerates damp.

Forty open hands. Forty debts. And mine, when I drew my own blade, would make forty-one, and the arithmetic would be complete, and the gates would open.

That was the plan. That had been the only plan for as long as I could remember having plans.

"Sarel." Vess looked up at me, and there was such naked hope in her face that I nearly couldn't bear it. "Say the words. The tide is turning. The storm is with us."

The storm was indeed with us. I could feel it even down here, through sixty feet of rock—a pressure in the water, a low groan in the stone, the whole coast holding its breath before a wave. The Guild had burned the tide market three days ago. They had marched openly, banners flying, and Corvane's justification speeches were already circulating in printed form, immaculate as his ledgers. Our people were scattered, dead, captured. Every argument for opening the gates *now*, by force, by blood, before the moment passed forever—that argument had never been louder.

So why did my hand shake?

I told myself it was the cold. The chamber was cold; the water was cold; grief is cold and I was full of it. I told myself it was the weight of the knife, though the knife weighed nothing. I told myself a great many things in the space of perhaps four seconds, which is a thing I suspect all leaders learn to do, and which none of us ever admit to.

Then I did the thing I had been refusing to do for three days, because I knew what it would cost me.

I listened to the water.

You have to understand—this is not poetry. This is the inheritance I actually received, the one useful thing left to a prince of a drowned country: we hear the sea the way hearing people hear a room. And the sea was not singing the song of Mereth rising. The sea was full of other sounds. Small ones. Human ones.

Lantern-light sounds.

I turned from the basin, from Vess's waiting face, and walked—not ran, walked, though every instinct screamed at me to keep moving toward destiny—to the high slit of a window in the chamber's eastern wall, the one the original architects had cut for exactly this purpose, though I doubt they imagined who would stand at it or why.

Beyond the headland, across five miles of black, heaving water, the coastal cities burned with light.

Not fire. Lanterns. Thousands upon thousands of them—street lanterns and window lanterns and harbor lanterns and the little bobbing lights of fishing boats that had gone out anyway, because fishermen go out anyway, that is the whole contract between fishermen and the sea. Saltbridge glowed gold along its river. The lamp district of Corvane's city made a constellation against the hills. Even the small towns, the ones whose names never made it into anyone's chronicles, had lit themselves up against the coming storm like beads strung along the dark.

Cities full of people.

People who had not drowned Mereth. People who had been born two hundred years after the drowning, on land that had risen from the drowned kingdom's ruins without their knowledge or consent, the way children are born into the consequences of their grandparents' crimes. Bakers. Net-menders. Children. A girl somewhere out there right now, I thought, lighting a lantern against the storm, and I did not know her name, and that was precisely the point. My grandfather's war had names in it—names carved in the gates, names in the treaty the Guild broke. But this? This was a nameless multitude, and I was being asked to drown them to balance a ledger written before any of them existed.

Vess came up beside me. I heard her breath catch when she saw what I was looking at.

"They know," she said quietly. "What the Guild did. What's owed."

"I know what's owed."

"Then look away from the lights, Sarel. Look at me. Look at your own blood in that basin." Her voice had the gentle terrible patience of someone talking a loved one back from a cliff edge. "Your grandmother watched her mother's city drown from a hillside exactly like this one. Do you think she counted lanterns?"

"No," I said. "She counted bodies."

"Then count ours." Vess took my wrist, gently, turning me from the window. Her eyes were wet and furious and utterly certain, and I loved her, and I understood in that moment that certainty is the most dangerous thing a grieving person can carry, because it feels exactly like clarity. "Forty-one hands, Sarel. Mine opened first. If you turn from this now, you tell every one of those people that their blood was a rehearsal. You tell them the heir of Mereth blinked at the last light."

"The last light," I repeated.

"What?"

"You called them the last light." I pulled free of her grip, not roughly, but firmly, the way you'd pull free of anything you love that is pulling you somewhere you cannot follow. "Vess, listen to yourself. We are not the ones in the dark anymore. That's what nobody in this room will say out loud. The exile ended. Somewhere in the last two hundred years, without anyone announcing it, we stopped being the drowned and started being the avengers, and avengers—" I gestured at the window, at the golden scatter of the living, "—avengers are always the ones holding the torches."

Below us, the ritual waited. Forty open wounds, patient as scripture.

"You're stalling," Vess said.

"I'm thinking. There's a difference, though I grant it's hard to see from where you're standing."

"And how long do we have for thinking?" She swept her arm toward the ceiling, toward the groan in the stones, the pressure building in the water. "Corvane's fleet is in the harbor roads. His enforcers hold the market ruins. The boy—the ledger-keeper—he went into the deep passages hours ago, running for the same gates we're standing under, carrying proof that might change nothing or everything, and either way, Sarel, the storm does not care. The tide-storm comes whether we open the gates or not. All we choose is whether it comes *through* them."

She was right. That was the horror of it. She was completely right, and being right gave her no wisdom whatsoever, and I was beginning to understand that these two things had never been married, in any generation, anywhere.

Because here was the truth underneath all our certainty, the one we had whispered in exile kitchens for two centuries and never said plainly: the gates were not only a door. They were a wound. Opening them would restore Mereth—the halls, the courts, the throne—and it would also loose the tide-storm that had been held back by the sealing, and the storm did not distinguish between the cities built on our graves and the graves themselves. The old texts were clear. My grandfather's court had known it when they bargained with the Guild's founders; it was in the broken treaty; Ada Joubert had found it in the drowned archive and brought the knowledge back like a man carrying a live coal in his bare hands.

We would rise, and they would drown, and then the water would take stock, and find the account still unbalanced.

Unless.

Unless the third thing. Unless the binding. Unless a name written out of every ledger, a price I had only understood fully an hour ago, standing knee-deep in black water, demanding that a tired clerk stop softening the truth for me.

Ada had told me. All of it. And then he had gone on toward the gates anyway, alone, to do the thing that would erase him from the memory of the world, and I had let him walk, because I was a coward, because some part of me had hoped the enforcers would stop him and spare me the choice.

*Sarel,* I said to myself, *you are standing at the door of your ancestors' house deciding whether to burn down your neighbors' home to get the key, when a stranger has offered to give up his own existence to forge a new key entirely. What kind of heir does that make you?*

The answer, I am ashamed to say, arrived slowly.

"Vess," I said. "Empty the basins."

The words dropped into the chamber like a stone into a well, and I heard the silence fall all the way down.

"My prince—"

"Empty them. Bind no one's wounds closed until I say otherwise—but pour the water out. The ritual requires the sea's consent in installments. Consider this installment withdrawn."

"You would shame forty blooded hands." Her voice cracked like ice in spring. "You would shame the dead."

"I would shame myself, gladly, publicly, in front of everyone we have left." I turned to face the kneeling rows, all those upturned faces lit by the gate-metal's faint green shine, and I raised my voice, and it did not shake, which surprised me, because everything else in me was shaking. "Hear me. I am Sarel, heir of drowned Mereth, and I am telling you that I stood at that window and I saw the lanterns, and I could not find the line between our enemies' grandchildren and ours. Not because I am weak. Because I looked. Looking is the whole duty of an heir, and I am telling you that I looked, and what I saw was people. Bakers. Net-menders. Children lighting lamps against a storm we are about to send."

An old man in the second row began, very quietly, to weep. I could not tell whether it was grief or relief. Perhaps there was no longer a difference.

"The gates will not be forced," I said. "They will not be bought. And they will not be opened by blood, because blood is how they were *closed* wrongly, and I will not correct a crime with its mirror. There is a third path. It has a price I would not wish on my worst enemy, and someone has already agreed to pay it, and the least I can do—the very least—is refuse to make his sacrifice a footnote to our massacre."

Vess stared at me. Behind her, the forty hands bled patiently into basins that were about to be emptied, and I understood that whatever happened next, some of them would never forgive me, and some of them would thank me, and both groups would be right.

"And if the binding fails?" she asked. "If the clerk dies at the door and the storm comes anyway and Mereth stays drowned forever—will you stand there and tell the dead their lanterns were worth it?"

"Yes," I said. "Because the dead of Mereth were people too, Vess. Bakers. Net-menders. Children. And I think—if there is anything left of them that watches—they would rather wait another century in the dark than walk out over the bodies of other people's grandchildren." I swallowed. "I think that's what they died trying to teach us."

For a long moment, nothing. Then, from the outermost basin, Vess lifted her bleeding hand, and instead of emptying it, she pressed her palm flat against the cold stone floor—an old gesture, older than the drowning, the sign a Merethi makes when submitting to judgment. Not agreement. Submission to judgment. In our language, they are different words, and the distance between them is where most of our history happens.

"So be it," she said. "But the storm doesn't read treaties, my prince. And neither does Corvane."

As if the sea itself had been waiting for the cue, the chamber shuddered. Dust sifted from the vaulting. The black pool at the gates' feet trembled into rings, and from beyond the eastern wall—far off, but not far enough—came a sound I had heard described in a hundred survivor accounts and never hoped to hear firsthand: the tide-storm, arriving early, dragging its white wall across the shallows.

And beneath it, closer, much closer, from the deep passages that fed into this chamber: footsteps. Running ones. More than one set.

Then a voice, hoarse with salt and exhaustion, echoing up the stairwell ahead of its owner:

"Sarel! *Sarel!* The Guildmaster's inside the mountain—he's minutes behind me—whatever you're going to decide, decide it *now!*"

Ada Joubert. Alive. Still himself, somehow, still unmapped, still carrying the only map in the world that mattered.

I looked once more at the window—at the ten thousand stubborn little lights strung along the coast, holding against the dark—and then at the gates, and I made the choice that would define me longer than my name would survive me.

"To the doors," I said. "All of you. We don't open them. We *defend* him."

Above us, through sixty feet of stone, the storm began to break.
