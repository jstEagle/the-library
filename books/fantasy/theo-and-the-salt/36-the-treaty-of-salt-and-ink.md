## 36. The Treaty of Salt and Ink

### Sarel

I have signed my name to many documents in my life. Most of them were lies.

The writ that declared me heir of Mereth—signed by an uncle who drowned before I could walk. The oaths my faction swore over salt water and old bones, each one a promise to avenge a kingdom none of us had ever seen above the tide. The letter I wrote to the coastal cities, before the storm season, offering them terms they never answered because they never read it. All of it ink on grief, dressed up as governance.

The treaty laid out before me now is different in only one respect: this time, everyone at the table knows exactly what they are losing, and they are signing anyway.

That, I have come to understand, is what peace actually is. Not the absence of loss. The agreement to share it.

The hall they gave us was once a customs house, back when this coast belonged to no one in particular and everyone in general. Salt Gate, the fishing towns call it—a squat stone building with a long table and windows facing the water, as if the architects understood that any negotiation about the sea should be conducted with the sea watching. Through those windows I can see the shallows where Mereth's towers used to break the surface two centuries ago. On clear days, the fishermen say, you can still see the tips of them at extreme low tide. They have names for each one. They have always had names for them. That is something I did not know until this year: that the people who built their cities on my kingdom's grave also built their lullabies on its stories.

It does not absolve them. But it complicates the drowning of them, which I suppose was the point.

Across the table sit the delegates of six coastal cities. Harbormaster Ovelin of Casterhale, a broad woman with rope-scarred hands who lost her fleet's eastern quarter to the tide-storm and has not once mentioned it as leverage, which I find more unnerving than if she had. A thin, precise man from Vellport whose title changes depending on which faction currently runs his city—I have taken to thinking of him as *the current Vellport*. And a dozen others, merchants and magistrates and one elderly priestess of a god whose name I still mispronounce, all of them tired in the particular way of people who spent a winter waiting for a wave to end them.

And beside me, my own delegation: the elders of the exile families, salt-gray and stubborn, and behind them the young ones—the generation born on land, who speak the coastal dialect better than Merethi and who, three months ago, were sharpening knives for a blood ritual that would have killed every soul in this room.

I look at those young faces and I think about how close we came. How close *I* came. There is a version of me standing at the gates with the ritual ready, and in that version, the lanterns across the water are just lights, not windows, and behind every window is nobody at all. I killed that version of myself at the worst possible moment, with Corvane's army burning the market behind me and my own people screaming for the gates. It was the hardest thing I have ever done, and I include in that list the years I spent pretending my grandmother's stories were history rather than homesickness.

Harbormaster Ovelin clears her throat. "If we're all quite finished admiring the document," she says, "some of us would like to sign it before the tide turns."

The document. Fourteen pages, written in three languages, with the treaty-map itself bound into the center like a spine—the map Ada Joubert redrew, the one that enforces what it describes. I still do not fully understand how it works. None of us do, entirely, except perhaps the woman from the Guild sitting at the far end of the table, and even she answers questions about it the way one answers questions about a miracle: gratefully, vaguely, and with her hand resting flat on the paper as though it might get up and walk.

But I know what it does. I have seen it work. Two weeks ago, a Vellport dredger dropped its nets past the treaty-line—past the gates—and the moment the chains crossed into forbidden water, the ship's own charts went wrong. Every map aboard showed the dredger sitting safely in open sea while the helmsman watched the gate-towers rise dead ahead through clear water. The crew turned back on their own. No soldiers. No enforcement. Just the truth, drawn so firmly that reality declined to contradict it.

"That is the clause I want read again," says the current Vellport, tapping page nine. "The shared survey rights. My city will not agree to Merethi cartographers working our harbors without—"

"Your city agreed last month," Ovelin says.

"My city's government has since been reconstituted."

"Your city's government has been reconstituted four times since midwinter," Ovelin says, "and I refuse to renegotiate with each of them personally." She looks down the table at me. "Heir of Mereth. Your position."

"The clause stands as written," I say. "Shared surveys. Shared archives. If your chart-makers can walk our drowned streets, ours can walk your docks. We are asking to be neighbors, Master Vellport. Neighbors see into each other's houses. That is the price of not being strangers."

He sniffs, but he signs. They all sign, eventually. Six cities, one exiled court, and the Guild—whose delegate, Wren Halloway, signs last, after reading every line twice, because she is apparently constitutionally incapable of doing otherwise.

When it is done, when the wax has cooled and the salt has been shaken over the signatures in the old manner—Ovelin's idea, and a good one; the sea should witness these things—we sit for a moment in silence, looking at the thing we have made.

It is not justice. Let no one write, in whatever chronicle survives us, that this treaty was justice. Justice would have been the coast kneeling and giving Mereth back, and Mereth rising without a single coastal family losing their home, and both of those things are impossible, and we have instead built the second-best arrangement available to tired people in a customs house. The exiles get their drowned city back—not raised, but returned, opened, made theirs again, its archives and its streets and its dead given back to their descendants. The coastal cities keep their harbors and their lives, guaranteed by a map that cannot be lied to. And between the two, a line of salt and ink that no fleet may cross and no ledger may erase.

Hard-won. Imperfect. Mine.

My uncle died believing the gates would be forced open by fire and blood, and that his heir would stand on the tower tops of a resurrected kingdom. Instead his heir has signed a customs agreement in a fishing town, and somewhere under the water, the gates remember him anyway. I think he would be furious. I think, given time, he might have understood.

Ovelin extends her rope-scarred hand across the table. I take it.

"To difficult neighbors," she says.

"To impossible maps," I answer.

### Wren

The deposition of Guildmaster Corvane took eleven minutes.

I want that recorded somewhere, because I was there, and because eleven minutes is what two hundred years of plunder and a winter of attempted conquest adds up to when the ledgers finally turn against you. Eleven minutes. Less time than it takes to inventory a single archive shelf. I have spent longer deciding what to eat.

We convened in the Grand Survey Hall—the same chamber where Corvane had stood, four months earlier, and explained to the inner circle why the destruction of Mereth had been, in his words, "a necessary correction to the record." I sat in the same chair. I wore the same coat. I remember noticing that, and remembering that I noticed, and understanding that I was cataloguing my own life the way I catalogue maps, because cataloguing was the only way I knew how to survive a room.

Corvane stood at the central dais while the Council of Curators read the evidence aloud. He did not defend himself. That was the worst part. I had prepared for defense—for the smooth, reasonable voice that could make bookkeeping sound like virtue, that had once made me feel honored to be a line in his accounts. Instead he listened to the gate-ledgers being read out—the original treaty of Mereth, broken oath by broken oath, the courier's death, the burned records of the tide market, the ambush at Saltbridge—and he simply nodded, occasionally, like a man hearing his own inventory confirmed.

When they asked him if he denied any of it, he said, "No. I deny that any of it was wrong."

Then they voted. Forty-one curators present, thirty-four against. He bowed—to the council, precisely and correctly, the bow of a man who respects institutions even as they devour him—and walked out of the hall through doors that two enforcers opened for him, because protocol is protocol, and the Guild has never known any other way to end anything.

Afterward, in the corridor, I found him waiting for me. Of course he did. He had always known where to find people; it was his gift.

"Halloway." He looked older. Not broken—older, the way a building looks older when the scaffolding comes off. "Your report at Saltbridge. The true report that omitted the crucial thing. I want you to know I saw through it immediately."

"I know," I said.

"And yet you gave it anyway. Knowing I would see through it. Knowing what it would cost you." He tilted his head. "Why?"

It was not a trap. That was the strangest thing—he genuinely wanted to know, the way he had once genuinely wanted to know why I reorganized the northern stacks against his filing system. Corvane's curiosity was real. It was everything else about him that was accounting.

"Because a report is a map," I said. "And I had finally decided which parts I was willing to leave blank."

He considered that for a long moment. Then he said, "You'll make a better Guildmaster than I did," and left, and I have decided to believe he meant it, because the alternative—that he meant nothing at all, that it was only another entry in his ledger—is more than I want to carry.

### Sarel

They tell me the rebuilding begins in spring.

Not the raising of Mereth—no one is raising Mereth; the treaty-map holds the city where it lies, restored to its people but not to the air, and there are elders who grieve this and younger ones who think it right, that some things should stay below the waterline, kept rather than recovered. What begins in spring is smaller and stranger: ferry-routes to the drowned outskirts, diving bells at the treaty-line, the first Merethi children in two hundred years learning to swim down the streets their great-great-grandparents fled.

I will not lead it. That decision was made for me, in a sense, by the signing itself—an heir exists to reclaim a kingdom, and the kingdom is reclaimed, and so the office dissolves like salt. The elders have proposed instead a Tide Speaker, chosen by lot among the families, serving seven years. I have put my own name in. So have four hundred others. Whatever happens, it will not be a throne, and that is the whole point.

Tonight I walk the seawall alone, which is a thing I have wanted to do my entire life and could never explain why until this year. Below me, at low tide, a tower tip breaks the silver skin of the water. Fishermen have hung a lamp on it—someone's small treason, technically a treaty violation, tolerated because the harbor-masters pretend not to see. The light bobs on the swell.

An old woman once told me that the drowned do not haunt the living. They wait for the living to finish being afraid, and then they visit. I think the visiting has begun. I think it looks like lamps on tower tips and children learning old songs and a treaty with salt shaken over its signature.

I think it looks like peace, which is not the same as happiness, and never was, and might be enough.

Behind me, up the coast road, a rider approaches with Guild colors and a sealed case strapped to her saddle. Wren Halloway, coming to deliver the first copy of the ratified treaty-map to the Merethi archive itself—by tradition, by protocol, and, I suspect, by a motive she would deny if asked directly.

She is bringing news. I can tell by the way she rides. Nobody carries good news that fast unless they're afraid someone else will deliver it badly first.

### Wren

The Guild is mine, which is a sentence I intend to say aloud as few times as possible for the rest of my life.

Reforming it is slower work than deposing Corvane was. Deposing a man takes eleven minutes; reforming an institution takes a generation, and I am thirty-two, and some nights the arithmetic of that keeps me awake. But the pieces are moving. The gate-ledgers are public now—every apprentice copies them in their first year, learns what the Guild broke and what it owed. The unmapped stacks are being mapped, carefully, with consent, by surveyors trained to ask permission of places before drawing them. And the deep craft—maps that change what they describe—is taught openly, in one classroom, to twelve students, all of whom signed a pledge I wrote myself and mean to enforce.

Twelve students. Corvane had armies. I have a classroom. I believe I will win.

But tonight I am not Guildmaster. Tonight I am a courier, riding the coast road with a treaty-map in a sealed case and a second item wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of my satchel, and I am taking the long route to Salt Gate specifically so that the long route passes a small shop with a new sign over the door.

*THEO'S MAPS & CHARTS.*

Half a name. His choice, not mine—he told me once that a man who gave up his whole name gets to decide what fragment he grows back, like a limb, like a garden. I argued with him about it for a week. Then I stopped arguing, because I understood that the fragment wasn't a wound. It was a root cutting. You plant what you have and you tend it and you wait to see what comes up.

I slow the horse at the edge of town. Lamplight in the shop window. A figure inside, bent over a drafting table, doing what he has always done: making careful marks on honest paper, keeping his head down, invisible, except that now invisibility is a thing he paid for rather than a thing he hid in, and that makes all the difference in the world.

I have a delivery to make at Salt Gate. It can wait until morning. Treaties keep.

I tie the horse at the rail, take the case and the satchel, and knock on the door of Theo's shop with the hand that isn't holding anything, because the other one is full, and because some deliveries you want both arms free for.

Through the glass I watch him look up—and I watch the exact moment he recognizes my silhouette, the way he always did, three rooms away, in a crowded archive, before anyone else in the world knew I'd arrived.

The door opens. The lamplight spills out onto the cobbles like a tide coming in clean.
