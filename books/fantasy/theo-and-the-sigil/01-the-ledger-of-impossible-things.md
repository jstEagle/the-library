## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The ink was the color of a bruise healing, and Rosalind Voss had mixed it themselves.

They ground it from dried gallnut and rainwater caught in a copper basin during the storm of the ninth of Thawmonth—a storm that had, according to the ledger's own entry, been bottled two days later by a guild member named Oskar Fenwick and stored on the third shelf of the cellar, between a jar of last summer's beeswax and a door that opened onto a beach no map had ever confirmed. The entry read, in Rosalind's careful, deliberately unremarkable hand:

*Item 447. One (1) thunderstorm, minor, coastal variety. Bottled intact by O.F. Contains: wind, rain, three (3) lightning strikes, one (1) rainbow (partial). Shelf 3, position 2. Do not shake.*

Rosalind had written *do not shake* because Oskar had shaken one once, early in their acquaintance, to see what would happen. What happened was that the guildhall's back room had experienced a brief and localized monsoon, and the carpet had never quite recovered, and Oskar had spent a month doing everyone else's inventory as penance. The ledger remembered so that people didn't have to.

This was the work Rosalind loved most in the world, though they would have died before admitting it aloud, and possibly before admitting it even in the privacy of their own skull. The Guild of Impossible Things kept books the way other institutions kept secrets—jealously, obsessively, with an eye toward posterity—and Rosalind Voss was the keeper of those books. Not the guildmaster. Not the treasurer. Not any title that might appear on a tax roll or a census or, gods forbid, a royal register. Just the person who wrote things down.

Every impossible thing the guild acquired, contained, witnessed, or accidentally unleashed went into the ledgers. There were eleven of them now, great leather-bound volumes with spines cracked from use, arranged on the shelf behind Rosalind's desk in the order of their acquisition rather than any logical system, because Rosalind believed that chronology was a kind of honesty and alphabetization was a kind of vanity. Volume One began forty years ago with a single line in someone else's handwriting: *Founded this day, by persons who prefer not to be listed.* Even the guild's origin was an exercise in anonymity. Rosalind found this deeply comforting.

They were, by trade and by gift, a cartographer. This was the thing about them that could not be written in any ledger, because it was the thing that made writing ledgers possible at all. Rosalind drew maps, and the maps did not always describe places that already existed. Sometimes—when the light was right, when Rosalind's hand was steady, when something in the paper itself seemed to lean toward the pen—the places came into being. A harbor drawn on a Tuesday appeared by Friday, complete with gulls. A mountain range sketched in the margin of a shipping manifest became, within the season, a matter of territorial dispute between two duchies who each claimed to have always known it was there.

Rosalind did not do this often. Rosalind did this almost never, on purpose, with the discipline of a monk and the terror of a mouse.

Because here was the truth that lived under Rosalind's ribs like a swallowed stone: the world did not forgive what it could not explain, and Rosalind Voss had decided, somewhere around their nineteenth year, to be entirely unexplainable-proof. Dull. Forgettable. The kind of person whose name slid off memory like rain off oiled canvas. They dressed in browns and grays. They spoke quietly and only when necessary. They paid their taxes early. They had cultivated, over fifteen years, a personality so aggressively middling that their own neighbors sometimes introduced them to each other twice.

It worked. It worked beautifully. It worked so well that some mornings Rosalind woke up and could not remember why they had ever wanted anything else.

And some other mornings—the dangerous ones—they woke up aching with a loneliness so specific it felt less like an emotion than a location, a place they'd been exiled to, coordinates fixed and unchanging.

* * *

The guildhall stood behind a chandler's shop on Candlemaker's Row, which was either a joke the founders had intended or a coincidence they'd have enjoyed. From the street it looked like nothing: a narrow brick face, three windows, a door painted the color of old dishwater. The chandler, Mistress Ivetta, who was herself a guild member of long standing and whose candles burned without ever being lit, pretended to employ Rosalind as her bookkeeper, and Rosalind pretended to be employed, and twice a week Ivetta handed over a purse of coins for services rendered so that Rosalind's finances would look boringly legitimate to anyone who cared to look.

Nobody cared to look. That was the whole architecture of Rosalind's life. Nobody cared to look, and Rosalind had built every wall of it themselves, stone by careful stone.

Inside, past the door and down the stairs that smelled of tallow and cold stone, the guildhall unfolded into spaces that should not have fit inside the building above them. This was not magic exactly—or it was, but the structural sort, the sort you stopped noticing the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. The main room had a ceiling that was higher on some days than others, depending on how many members needed to be under it. The cellar held its impossible inventory in a cool dark that kept bottled weather calm and kept the doors quiet. There were seven doors in the guild's collection, and only one of them was attached to a wall; the rest leaned against shelves like retired soldiers, waiting to be needed.

Rosalind passed through the main room each morning before anyone else arrived, because arriving first meant arriving alone, and alone was where Rosalind did their truest work. They lit no lamps they didn't need. They touched nothing on the curiosity tables—the petrified songbird mid-note, the mirror that showed rooms instead of faces, the teacup that refilled itself with whatever the drinker most missed. Rosalind had catalogued all of these. Rosalind had never once used any of them. Using an impossible thing felt, to them, like signing a confession.

At the desk, they opened Volume Nine and took up the pen.

The morning's entries were ordinary, by guild standards. A report from Marisol Quennel, who kept the guild's eastern contacts, regarding a well in the town of Harrowmere that had begun returning memories along with the water—you drank, and remembered your grandmother's kitchen with such precision you could smell the bread. Item 451. Rosalind noted the location, the date of first occurrence, the estimated volume of memory per liter, and a recommendation that the well be left alone until someone could determine whether the memories belonged to the water, the town, or the drinker. Marisol had appended a personal note: *I drank. I saw my mother young again. I am not sorry.* Rosalind entered the note into the record verbatim, because the ledgers recorded everything, including the things that couldn't be quantified. Especially those.

Then a requisition from Oskar: two vials of binding-salt, one spool of thread spun under a waning moon, and permission to attempt bottling fog, which he insisted was different from bottling storms and which Rosalind privately suspected was Oskar simply wanting to try something new before winter closed the roads. Approved, with the annotation *fog is NOT weather, it is weather's ghost — proceed carefully.* Rosalind allowed themselves the small pleasure of a marginal opinion. The margins of the ledgers were the only place in the world where Rosalind said exactly what they thought.

By the time the others drifted down the stairs—Ivetta first, smelling of honey and smoke that wasn't there; then the twins Pell and Perrin Ashgrave, who could hold a conversation with anything that had ever been alive, including furniture; then old Master Corvane, who had founded the guild's second chapter and could walk through walls but preferred doors on principle—the morning's work was done, the books balanced, and Rosalind Voss was once again the least interesting person in the room, which was precisely how they had arranged it.

"Voss," Ivetta said, setting a cup of tea on the corner of the desk without asking whether Rosalind wanted it. She never asked. "You're gray today."

"I'm always gray."

"Grayer." Ivetta squinted at them with the frank appraisal of a woman who had known Rosalind for nine years and respected them enough not to pretend otherwise. "Something's wrong."

"The weather's turning. My shoulder disagrees with it."

"Your shoulder," Ivetta said, "is a liar and so are you, and I say that with affection." She patted the desk twice, the way she did when she'd decided a conversation was over whether or not it was, and went back to her candles.

Rosalind drank the tea. It was chamomile cut with something bitter and medicinal that Ivetta swore was good for the humors, and it tasted like being cared about by someone who expressed care exclusively through mild poisoning. Rosalind finished every drop. They always did. It was one of the small betrayals of their own policy of distance that they permitted themselves, filed away in the mental ledger they kept alongside the paper ones, under the heading *things I will miss if I am ever forced to run.*

That heading had more entries than Rosalind liked to count.

* * *

In the afternoon, the guild gathered for the quarterly accounting, which was less about money than about existence: who had seen what, who had made what, who had lost what, who needed the guild's help and who owed it. Rosalind sat at the end of the table with the open ledgers and read the quarter back to them, item by item, in a voice pitched low and level, and watched the impossible things of the world pass in review like troops before a skeptical general.

Item 443: the singing stone, recovered from a riverbed, which sang only songs its finder's father had loved. Currently residing with the finder, who reported that she could no longer sleep without it, and that this was either a blessing or a warning, and she had not yet determined which.

Item 444: a letter delivered forty years late, postmarked from a city that had burned down thirty-five years ago. The ink was still wet. The recipient had died in the fire. The letter was addressed to her anyway, and had been delivered to her grave, where it was found the next morning opened and answered. The answer was in the guild's possession. Rosalind had read it once, transcribed it, and locked the transcription in the iron box under the floorboards, because some entries were too heavy to leave lying around. The answer had been two words long. Rosalind had never told anyone what they were, and the ledger entry said only: *Contents withheld by Keeper. Contents are kind.*

Item 445: the map.

Rosalind reached this entry and paused, the way they always paused, the pause lasting half a breath longer than professional composure required. Pell Ashgrave noticed. Pell noticed everything; it was his gift, or half of it, the listening half.

"Go on, then," Pell said gently. "Read it."

*Item 445. One (1) map, hand-drawn, unsigned, discovered among donations to the Chantry of Saint Aldhelm. Depicts a coastline that does not exist. Upon close inspection by the Keeper, the coastline does exist—it has merely not gotten around to it yet. The map is accurate to within a finger's width. The Keeper has verified this against surveys taken after the fact. The Keeper notes, for the record, that the handwriting resembles no hand the Keeper knows, and that the Keeper has chosen not to investigate further.*

"You chose not to investigate further," Corvane said, from the head of the table, his voice dry as August. "Our keeper of records declined to investigate a map that predicts geography."

"I investigated," Rosalind said. "I chose not to publish my findings."

"And what did you find?"

Rosalind turned the page of the ledger so the entry faced them and not the room, an old habit, a shield raised out of pure reflex. "I found that whoever drew it knew the coast the way you know your own handwriting. I found that the surveyors' measurements matched mine to the width of a fingernail. And I found that I have no intention of discussing it further in session."

Corvane studied them for a moment with eyes the color of tarnished silver, and then, mercifully, let it go. He was the closest thing the guild had to a leader, and he governed the way a good fence governs a field: firmly, invisibly, and mostly by being there. He understood, perhaps better than anyone, that the guild ran on a fuel of mutual non-inquiry. Everyone there was impossible in some way. Everyone there had survived by hiding it. To ask too directly was to threaten the shelter that made the guild a home.

But Pell was still looking at Rosalind, and Pell's looking had weight to it, the particular gravity of someone who listened to chairs and therefore had learned to listen to silences too.

"That's the third map," Pell said quietly, not accusing, just placing the fact on the table like a stone. "Third one in two years. Coastlines, both times. Water that hasn't happened yet."

"Maps drift," Rosalind said. "All cartography is prophecy if you wait long enough. Rivers move. Coasts erode."

"Not like this."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "Not like this." And then they said nothing else, and the silence sat in the middle of the table like a fourth twin, and eventually the meeting moved on.

* * *

What Rosalind had not told the guild—not because the telling was forbidden but because the telling was unthinkable—was that the handwriting in the margins of those three maps was their own.

Not similar. Their own. Drawn, all evidence suggested, by their own hand, in states they did not remember, on nights they could not account for. Rosalind would wake with ink stains on the edge of the right palm and a finished page on the desk and a hole in the night where the work should have been. The first time, they had burned the page. The second time, they had hidden it. The third time—the coast, the one in the ledger—they had brought it to the guild anonymously, slipped it into the donation pile at Saint Aldhelm's themselves, and then catalogued its arrival as though it had come from a stranger.

It was the most honest dishonesty Rosalind had ever committed. The guild now possessed, officially, a map drawn by no one, predicting a place that did not yet exist, and Rosalind possessed the knowledge that their gift was not a small talent for pretty harbors but something vast and tidal that moved through them while they slept, drawing worlds, and that the person who woke up each morning and called themselves dull and safe was standing on the shore of an ocean they refused to name.

They told themselves it was containment. They told themselves that a gift like theirs, made public, would not stay a gift for long—that it would become a commodity, a weapon, a crime. That the free cities tolerated oddities the way they tolerated beggars, with charity at a distance and law at a nearer one. That a cartographer who could draw a realm into being was not a wonder. A cartographer who could draw a realm into being was a threat to every border ever surveyed, every claim ever staked, every king who believed his kingdom ended where his maps said it did.

Kings, Rosalind knew, did not like their edges redrawn.

So they kept the ledgers. They wore the gray. They let Ivetta poison them with kindness and let Pell's silences wash over them unanswered, and they went home each evening to a narrow room above a cobbler's shop, where they ate bread and cheese and copied charts for the chandler's trade routes in a hand so deliberately plain that their own mother—if Rosalind had had a mother, which was a story for another ledger, another lifetime—would not have recognized it.

And at night, sometimes, they dreamed of coastlines.

* * *

It was on one of those evenings, the twenty-third of Harvestmonth, with the light going amber early and the smell of woodsmoke starting to season the air, that Rosalind climbed the stairs from the guildhall and stepped out onto Candlemaker's Row and stopped, because the street was wrong.

Not magically wrong. Humanly wrong. There were too many people on it, and they were all facing the same direction, toward the market square, and they were all quiet, and quiet crowds are the loudest thing in any city. Rosalind stood at the mouth of the row in their brown coat with their dull face and their forgettable walk, invisible as ever, and watched a knot of townsfolk gather around the base of the market bell tower, where a man in crown livery was nailing a sheet of parchment to the boards.

Rosalind could not read it from where they stood. But they could read the crowd, and the crowd read like a ledger entry written in a hand Rosalind had hoped never to see: fear, entered neatly, dated today, witnessed by all.

A woman beside them clutched her son's shoulder. An old man took off his hat, which was the gesture of a generation that still believed hats mattered to authority. And down the row, at the chandler's window, Ivetta stood with her arms folded and her face gone the color of tallow, and when she caught Rosalind's eye across the distance she did not wave, did not nod, did not signal anything at all except one small, unmistakable thing: she looked away first.

Rosalind walked home slowly, taking the long way, letting the crowd thin behind them, and told themselves the proclamation was about taxes. Proclamations were usually about taxes. The world was mostly taxes, and weather, and the small stubborn business of people getting by.

But that night the dreams came early, and the coastlines came with them, and when Rosalind woke at dawn with ink on their palm, the page on the desk was not a coast at all.

It was a tower. A bell tower, drawn in bruise-colored ink, and beneath it, in their own sleeping hand, a single line of text that Rosalind read twice before their heart began to pound:

*Register, or burn.*
