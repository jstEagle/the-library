## 2. Proclamation at the Market Bell

Rosalind had learned long ago that fear arrived like weather. It did not knock. It did not announce itself at the door with a herald's trumpet and a wax seal. It simply rolled in off the mountains one morning, grey and total, and by the time you noticed it, it was already in your lungs.

The bell began ringing an hour past dawn — not the market bell's usual cheerful clatter that meant fish had come up from the harbor, but the slow, deliberate tolling reserved for coronations and deaths and royal decrees. Rosalind was still standing over the desk in their narrow room above the chandler's shop, still looking at the bruise-colored tower they had drawn in their sleep, when the first toll shivered through the floorboards and up through the soles of their feet like a word spoken into bone.

*Register, or burn.*

They had hoped, in the way one hopes against a headache or a debt collector, that the dream was only indigestion of the mind. But dreams, for Rosalind Voss, were never only anything. That was rather the whole trouble with them.

They dressed quickly and plainly: brown wool, grey cloak, the ink-stains on their fingers scrubbed until only ghosts of them remained. They took down no ledger, packed no satchel, carried nothing that could be called evidence. This was the discipline of twenty years. A person who wanted to stay unremarkable learned to move through the world the way water moves through cloth — present everywhere, conspicuous nowhere.

The streets were already filling as Rosalind descended into Lantern Row. Neighbors stood in doorways with their arms folded, wearing the particular expression of people who have heard a bell toll and are pretending, together, not to know why. Old Marta from the pie stall was there, flour to her elbows. The cobbler's boy, Perin, perched on his father's step with his mouth full of bread. Everyone drifting in the same direction without admitting they were drifting, the way iron filings drift toward a magnet while insisting all the while they are minding their own business.

Rosalind drifted too.

The market square opened before them under a sky the color of dirty pewter. And there, nailed to the great oak board beside the well where proclamations about taxes and curfews and the price of grain had hung for two hundred years, was a sheet of vellum so white it looked like a wound in the morning.

A crown herald read it aloud from the steps of the assize hall, his voice trained flat and official, the voice of a man who has been instructed to feel nothing on behalf of the words he speaks.

*"By decree of His Majesty the King, let it be known throughout the realm: that all persons possessing gifts unnatural, whether of making, of knowing, of walking between, or of any art beyond the common craft of honest folk, shall present themselves at the registrar's office within thirty days, that their names may be entered upon the Crown Register of Gifted Persons."*

A pause. The herald wet his lips.

*"Those who register shall be granted license to practice their arts under supervision of the crown, and protection thereby."*

Another pause. Longer. Rosalind watched the man's throat work.

*"Those who do not register, being discovered thereafter, shall be accounted traitors to the realm, and dealt with according to the law concerning traitors."*

He stepped down. He did not say *burned*. He didn't need to. Everyone in the square had seen what was done to traitors; everyone had smelled the smoke from the executions green behind the tannery and told themselves it was only leaves burning.

The silence afterward was the worst part. Not weeping, not shouting — just three hundred people breathing very carefully, each one privately wondering who else in the crowd was doing sums like theirs. *Do I qualify? Does knowing things I shouldn't count? Does the charm my grandmother taught me? Does the way my daughter's drawings come true?* Every face became a locked door.

Marta crossed herself with a floury hand. Perin's father pulled him off the step and indoors without a word. Somewhere near the fountain a woman began to cry quietly, and someone else shushed her, gently, urgently, as if grief itself might now be reported.

Rosalind stood at the edge of the crowd with their hood up and their heart going like a trapped bird, and read the vellum themselves, twice, because cartographers checked their sources. The registrar's office would open within the week. The seal at the bottom was the true royal seal, sun and stag and crown. The hand that had signed it was the king's own — or something shaped enough like the king's own that no clerk alive would question it.

*Register, or burn.* Their sleeping hand had known. Had drawn the bell tower hours before the bell tolled. Which raised questions Rosalind did not want to look at directly, the way one does not look directly at the sun or at one's own death — questions about how far ahead their gift reached when it stopped asking permission, and about what else it might already know that Rosalind did not.

But those questions could wait. First: the guild.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its headquarters in a building that did not exist, which was the single greatest security measure available to them. To reach it, one walked down Cordwainer Lane, counted eleven doors on the left-hand side, and knocked on the twelfth — a door set between a haberdashery and a house of ill repute, painted the same tired green as both its neighbors, which every passerby's eye slid across without catching because Rosalind had spent four years and considerable ink teaching the street itself to forget it.

Inside, the guildhall was larger than the street could hold, which was the point. Bottled storms glowed along the rafters like captive weather. A door on the far wall led somewhere that was emphatically not the wall behind it. The long table in the center — scarred oak, older than the kingdom — was already crowded when Rosalind arrived, and every impossible thing the guild possessed seemed to have gone quiet at once, listening.

Master Odile Vane presided from the head, a small fierce woman of seventy whose gift was the finding of lost things, and who had found, over a long career, that most lost things did not wish to be found, which had given her her temperament. Beside her sat Hesper Quill, who bottled storms; Brother Anselm, who walked between places and had not been entirely in one place since the year of the floods; Tamsin Bright, whose woven charms could make a lie smell like truth for exactly as long as needed; and old Corvin, who kept moths that ate memories, and who was, at this moment, feeding one from the palm of his hand with the tenderness of a man feeding a songbird.

Eleven of them. Eleven gifted souls around one table, and the twelfth chair empty, as it always was — the chair kept, though no one said so anymore, for the classmate Rosalind had once been at academy with, whose name was spoken in this room only as a cautionary tale.

"You've heard," Odile said. It was not a question. The bell had tolled for the whole city.

"I've heard." Rosalind took the last standing place against the wall, deliberately, because sitting felt too much like settling in. "I also dreamed the proclamation before it was posted."

That got their attention. Eleven faces turned. Corvin's moths lifted off his sleeve in a soft grey cloud and resettled.

"Dreamed it how?" Hesper asked. She was the youngest after Rosalind, all elbows and impatience, storms under her skin.

"The tower. The bell. The words, in my own handwriting, before the herald ever spoke them." Rosalind turned their palm up, showing the ghost-ink stains. "I don't know what it means yet. I know it means the gift is getting louder, which is its own problem. But first—" they nodded toward the window, toward the invisible street outside, "—first we have a vote, I think."

"We have more than a vote." Odile's voice was dry as August. "We have thirty days, a register with our names waiting to fill it, and a crown that has just remembered we exist. I'll hear arguments. Scattering first. Anselm?"

Brother Anselm unfolded himself from his chair — or partially unfolded; some portion of him always remained seated regardless. "Scattering is survival," he said. "It has always been survival. We are eleven. We become eleven whispers in eleven towns, and the hunt finds no den to burn, only stragglers. It is not brave. It is arithmetic." He folded his hands. "I have walked between places for forty years. I can walk us out. All of us, tonight, to eleven different doors."

"And leave the archive?" Tamsin's voice cracked on the word. She was young enough that the guildhall was the only home she'd chosen for herself. "The ledgers? Forty years of records, Rosalind's maps, the doors themselves? You can't walk a building between worlds, Anselm. Or can you?" she added, with sudden dangerous hope.

"No," said Anselm, and the hope died audibly.

"Then scattering means burning the archive ourselves and calling it wisdom." Tamsin pushed back from the table. "I won't vote for that. Everything in those books is someone. Every entry. If we burn the ledgers we burn the proof that any of us existed."

"The proof will exist either way," Odile said quietly. "On the register. In their handwriting instead of ours. That is precisely the horror of it, child — they don't mean to erase us. They mean to *file* us."

Silence. Above, a bottled storm turned slowly on its rafter-hook, lightning flickering inside the glass like thought.

Corvin spoke next, in his moth-soft voice. "Registration is a trap even for those it claims to protect. My order remembers the last register. Under the old king's grandfather. Do you know what a registered gift is worth, once it's written down? It's worth owning. They licensed the rain-callers first, then conscripted them for the fleet wars. Licensed the healers, then bound them to garrison duty till their hands shook. A license is a leash with pretty writing on it." He stroked a moth's wing with one finger. "And those who refused registration then — the record says they were pardoned, eventually. The record lies. My moths have eaten enough soldiers' memories to know what actually happened to the unregistered. There weren't many left to pardon."

"So we scatter, or we file ourselves into leashes, or we burn everything and run." Hesper laughed, short and bitter. "Wonderful options. Has anyone considered option four, wherein we do absolutely nothing, because surely the crown has better things to do than hunt a guild nobody's ever heard of?"

"They've heard of us," Rosalind said.

All eyes came around.

"Not by name. Not as a guild. But someone drew up that decree, and decrees don't spring from nothing — there were reports, informants, lists. Somebody, somewhere, has been counting us." Rosalind heard their own voice go flat and careful, the voice they used for reading coordinates aloud. "And here is what I know about being counted: I have spent twenty years making myself uncountable. Dull papers. Dull face. Dull little life above a chandler's shop. And I believed it. I believed obscurity was armor." They paused, and hated the honesty of what came next. "This morning a neighbor of mine was taken. Before the herald finished reading. Did anyone else see it? The dyer's apprentice, Joss. From the blue house on Tanner's Walk."

Murmurs. Marta's face, in memory, going white.

"He lighted candles with a snap of his fingers. That's all. A parlor trick, a kindness at dinner parties. Someone reported him anyway — someone who'd eaten his bread and drunk his wine. The registrars' men had him before the vellum was fully nailed up. Which tells us two things." Rosalind held up one ink-stained finger. "First: the lists were made *before* the decree. Second: hiding in plain sight works right up until one person decides it doesn't. Obscurity isn't armor. It's luck. And luck," they said, "is weather."

The phrase dropped into the room and sat there. Fear arriving like weather — Rosalind saw several of them recognize it, because it was a guild saying, half joke and half creed, passed around the table for years whenever a job went wrong. *You can't argue with weather, only prepare for it.*

"And your proposal?" Odile asked, watching Rosalind with her finder's eyes, as if Rosalind were a lost thing she'd been hired to locate. "Since you've dismantled everyone else's."

"Hiding in plain sight. Properly. Not as scattered stragglers but as a strategy." Rosalind pushed off from the wall and paced the length of the table, because movement helped thinking, and thinking was the only gift they trusted today. "The decree requires the *gifted* to register. Very well. What is a gift, legally? Whatever the registrar writes down. So we decide what gets written." They tapped the table. "Tamsin's charms become 'household needlework.' Hesper's bottles become 'glassblowing, unusual.' Corvin's moths are 'entomology.'" A few breaths of startled laughter, quickly smothered. "Anselm becomes a courier with an uncanny sense of direction. I am a mapmaker — a dull, licensed, tax-paying mapmaker, which I already am on paper. We register. All of us. Under small names and smaller talents, and the crown files us away as curiosities, harmless, *supervised*, and therefore beneath further attention."

"Register with lies?" Anselm frowned. "The registrar will test us."

"Will he? With what? The crown hasn't the testers for a whole kingdom of the gifted — why do you think they're using fear instead? A register taken by clerks is a list of whatever people confess. And confession—" Rosalind glanced at Tamsin, "—can be woven."

Tamsin's eyes lit with the particular gleam she got when someone suggested a use for her charms that skirted the edge of decency. "A lie that smells like truth. For as long as needed."

"It's dangerous," Odile said. Not objecting. Weighing.

"Everything is dangerous. Scattering is dangerous — eleven strangers alone, no archive, no doors, no each other. Registering honestly is a leash. Refusing is a pyre." Rosalind stopped pacing. "I'm not saying it's safe. I'm saying it keeps us *together*, and keeps the ledgers intact, and keeps the doors — and if the day comes when none of that is enough, at least we'll be standing in the same room when we find out. I have spent twenty years believing that invisible equals safe." They heard their own voice roughen and did not fight it. "This morning I watched Joss get dragged down Tanner's Walk, and he was the most invisible gifted man I knew. So no. I don't believe it anymore. I believe we hide in plain sight, together, and I believe we start tonight."

The silence stretched. Above them the storm-bottles churned. Somewhere in the walls, the guildhall creaked — a building holding its breath.

"Vote, then," Odile said at last. "Hands. Scattering?"

Two hands. Anselm's, and Corvin's — though Corvin's rose slowly, reluctantly, like a man voting for a funeral.

"Registering under false colors?"

One by one: Tamsin. Hesper. Then others, six, seven — and Odile herself, whose hand came up last but came up like a blade coming out of a sheath. Nine.

Rosalind voted tenth. Ten.

Only Anselm's hands stayed folded on the table, and he looked around at them all with his half-here, half-gone eyes and said, "Then I am outnumbered in two places at once, which is a personal first." A rustle of shaky laughter. "Very well. I'll walk you to the registrar myself, if it comes to that. But mark me—" and his voice lost its mildness entirely, "—the day the register stops being a game of paper, I will open a door, and whoever hesitates deserves what hesitation costs."

"Agreed," said Odile. "Meeting adjourned until—"

She never finished. Because that was the moment the knocking started — hard, urgent, irregular, from the invisible green door on Cordwainer Lane, which no one in the city should have been able to find, and through the guildhall's enchanted walls came the sound of a voice Rosalind knew, thin and cracking with terror:

"*Please — please, it's Marta — the pie woman — they're taking everyone on Tanner's Walk —*"

Rosalind's blood went cold, and colder still as the voice added, sobbing:

"—*and they're asking for the mapmaker. By name.*"
