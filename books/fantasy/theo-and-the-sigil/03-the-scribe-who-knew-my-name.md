## 3. The Scribe Who Knew My Name

The inspectors came on a morning so gray it seemed the sky itself had been ordered to keep watch.

Rosalind knew they were coming before anyone told them—had known since dawn, when every bird in the market district went silent at once, as though a hand had been laid over the city's throat. The guild had its own weather-signs. Bottled storms shivered on their shelves. The door to elsewhere sweated condensation down its hinges like a nervous man's brow. And old Magrit, who read tea leaves and claimed to be able to read anything else besides, stood at the window of the counting room with her cup untouched and said, "They're here."

Not *coming*. Here.

Rosalind set down the ledger they had been balancing—the one with the bottled storm of 1189, the one that had taken three days to catalog because the storm kept trying to escape into the margins—and felt their own heartbeat do something strange. Not quicken. Slow. As if the body, wiser than the mind, understood that what was required now was not speed but stillness.

"Everyone knows the drill," Magrit said. Her voice was flat, which meant she was frightened; Magrit was never flat when she was calm. "Papers out. Gifts folded away. If anyone has anything impossible about them this morning, I want it filed under *Tuesday*."

*Tuesday*, in guild parlance, meant *later, or never, whichever saves your life*.

Rosalind took their papers from the false bottom of the ink drawer—papers they had forged themselves, twice over: once to create them, once to age them. The Guild of Impossible Things did not trust any document it could not break and remake itself. Rosalind Voss, registered cartographer, third-tier, no notable talents, employed by the Crown Survey Office for eleven years. A life drawn in careful gray lines. A life designed to be skimmed and forgotten.

They had spent thirty years becoming unremarkable. It was, in its way, the most remarkable thing they had ever done.

Below, through the floorboards, came the sound of boots. Many boots. The measured, unhurried tread of people who owned the ground they walked on because the law said they did.

Magrit looked at Rosalind across the counting room, and for one moment neither of them pretended. Then Magrit straightened her shawl, became an elderly widow with nothing stranger about her than bad knees, and went downstairs to open the door.

* * *

There were six of them in the guildhall's front room: four crown inspectors in slate-gray livery, a clerk with a portable writing desk strapped to his chest like a breastplate, and—standing slightly apart from the others, as though he were not quite one of them—a scribe.

Rosalind knew him before he turned around. Knew him by the way he held his shoulders, squared but not stiff; by the ink stain on his right middle finger that no amount of scrubbing would ever fully lift; by the particular quality of his stillness, which was not the stillness of a soldier but of someone who had learned early that being noticed was dangerous.

Theo Aldery.

Eleven years fell away like paper sliding off a desk. The academy lecture hall, cold in winter because the crown begrudged them coal. Master Ellery droning about the legal forms of land grant. And two rows back, a boy with dark hair and ink-stained fingers who wrote faster than anyone Rosalind had ever seen—not just fast, but *hungry*, as if words were food and he'd gone without.

Rosalind had watched Theo Aldery from a distance for three years and spoken to him perhaps four times. They remembered every one of those conversations with humiliating precision. Once, he'd lent them a pen when theirs split. Once, he'd laughed at something they said—a real laugh, surprised out of him—and looked almost startled by it. Once, they'd both been kept late copying punishment lines, and he'd shown them how to make a capital letter look older than it was, a trick for aging documents.

That trick lived in Rosalind's hands to this day.

And once—the fourth time—Rosalind had watched him stand up in Master Ellery's class, in front of everyone, and say the words that ended Wren Calloway's life as neatly as an executioner's blade.

Wren. Who could make light bloom between her palms like a caught firefly. Who used to show them in the stairwell where no masters walked, laughing, saying *look, look, isn't it beautiful*. Who trusted the wrong classmate with her secret because he seemed kind, seemed lonely, seemed like someone who might understand.

The inspectors had come for her within the week. Registration first—that was the procedure then, gentler years—but registration led to examination, examination to classification, classification to confinement. Wren Calloway had spent the last decade of her life in the Crown Repository for the Gifted, wherever that was, and Rosalind had heard last winter that the Repository had stopped being a repository and started being something with a shorter name.

And now Theo Aldery stood in the guildhall of the Guild of Impossible Things with a list of names in his hand, and Rosalind Voss was fourth on it.

"The survey office," the lead inspector was saying. He was a heavy man with a voice like a door closing. "Voss. Rosalind. Papers."

Rosalind stepped forward and offered the folder with hands that did not shake. They had practiced that too—thirty years of practicing not shaking, not flinching, not looking too long at anything or too briefly at everything. They let their face settle into the mild, faintly bored expression of a person whose greatest concern this morning was whether the rain would spoil the plaster work.

The inspector barely glanced at the documents. He passed them sideways to the scribe.

To Theo.

Rosalind watched Theo Aldery take their forged life in his ink-stained hands and open it, and thought: *this is the moment*. He would recognize the trick he himself had taught them—the aged capitals, the deliberately imperfect wax seal, all the small dishonesties that made a document believable. Or worse, he would simply remember their name. Eleven years was long, but names outlasted faces, and *Voss* was an unusual name, and there had only ever been one Rosalind Voss in Master Ellery's classes, sitting two rows behind the boy who wrote like he was starving.

He looked up.

Their eyes met, and Rosalind saw—there was no mistaking it, not at this distance, not with this history—the flicker of recognition move through his face like a fish passing under ice. There and gone. His expression did not change. But something behind his eyes moved, and Rosalind's whole body went cold and quiet, the way the world goes quiet before lightning.

"These appear to be in order," Theo said.

His voice was different from what Rosalind remembered. Harder at the edges. Or perhaps not harder—*careful*. A voice that had been trained, line by line, the way a hedge is trained against a wall.

"You're certain?" the inspector asked, with the tone of a man who wanted to find a problem and would be pleased to be given an excuse. "Third-tier cartographer, no notable talents. The decree says gifted persons are to be examined regardless of rank. How do we know this one isn't hiding something?"

"We don't," Theo said. "We know nothing except what's written here." He tapped the page with one finger, once. "But the decree also says examination is for those whose gifts are suspected, reported, or evident. This name appears on none of the informant lists. No reports. No complaints. Eleven years of tax records and survey filings, all consistent." He closed the folder and handed it back to Rosalind, and here—he could not have done it more plainly unless he shouted it—he looked directly into Rosalind's eyes and held them for exactly one breath too long.

"The papers are genuine," he said. "Next name."

*Genuine.* Rosalind's own forgery, pronounced genuine by the man best qualified in the building to know otherwise. By the man who had taught them half the tricks in it.

The inspector grunted, dissatisfied but procedurally satisfied, which was the most dangerous kind of satisfaction because it could be revoked at whim. He moved down his list. Rosalind retreated to the wall with their folder held against their chest like a shield, and watched.

The inspection took another hour. The clerk recorded names. The inspectors poked at shelves with the blunt curiosity of men who had been told there was nothing to find and resented the waste of the trip. One of them picked up a jar that contained, according to the ledger, three inches of captured fog, shook it, frowned at the way the fog pressed against the glass like a face, and put it down again.

"That's just condensation," Rosalind heard Magrit say sweetly. "Terrible damp this time of year. You'll want to check your cellars, sir. Damp gets into everything."

At the door, assembling to leave, the scribe paused. The other inspectors were already in the street. For a moment Theo Aldery stood alone in the guildhall doorway with the gray light behind him, and Rosalind, against every instinct honed over thirty years, met his eyes again.

He said, very quietly, in a voice pitched for no one else:

"The survey office keeps its records in the east annex. Tuesdays, the archivist leaves early."

Then he was gone, and the door closed, and the birds began to sing again as though released.

* * *

The guild convened within minutes of the inspectors' boots fading. They gathered in the map room, the safest room in the building because it was the least interesting—walls covered in charts of coastlines and trade routes, nothing impossible anywhere, unless you knew that the chart of the Silverwind Delta was drawn on cloth woven from a spider's patience, or that the map of the northern passes rearranged itself nightly to reflect truths the mountains preferred not to admit in daylight.

"He lied for you," Magrit said. She had her tea again, finally, clutched in both hands. "Straight out. Called your forgery genuine. I've seen inspectors' scribes work, boy—they're trained hounds. They *smell* paper tricks. He smelled yours and called it clean."

"I know," Rosalind said.

"So either he's incompetent—" began Ferro, the locksmith, whose gift was that locks told him their secrets and who had been arguing for scattering since the proclamation.

"He's not incompetent."

"—or he wants something." Ferro spread his hands. "Nobody lies for a stranger. Nobody lies for a *name on a list*. He knew you, Rosalind. I watched his face. He knew you, and he chose to lie, and people who choose to lie to the crown want something badly enough to risk the pyre for it. What does a royal scribe want from a third-tier cartographer?"

Rosalind didn't answer. They were turning it over in their mind, the way they turned over an unfamiliar coastline, sounding the depths, noting the hazards.

Why would a hunter lie?

Because he wasn't hunting. That was the simplest answer, and the most terrifying one. Because the man who had once handed a gifted girl to the crown for the crime of glowing in a stairwell had somehow arrived at the opposite shore of his life. Because the decree had changed the arithmetic—*register or burn* was a different equation than *register or be confined*—and some men, given a choice between serving a fire and defying one, discovered late what they actually believed.

Or because it was a trap. Because the crown had learned that the guild existed—someone had talked, someone always talked eventually—and had constructed an elaborate theater: a sympathetic scribe, a whispered hint, a Tuesday appointment in an east annex, and beneath the trapdoor, the pyre.

Both answers fit the evidence. Rosalind hated evidence like that. Ambiguous evidence was how maps got people killed.

"There's a third possibility," said Odile, who kept the doors. She rarely spoke, and when she spoke, everyone listened, partly out of respect and partly because her voice sounded like it was arriving from somewhere far away. "Possibility three: he's lying to the crown for the same reason we lie to everyone. Because he's one of us, or near enough. Because he's hiding something too, and hiding it so well that nobody ever thought to put *him* on a list."

"A scribe?" Ferro snorted. "What would a scribe hide?"

"What would a cartographer hide?" Odile replied mildly, and looked at Rosalind, and Rosalind felt the look go through them like a pin through paper.

Silence. Rain against the windows. Somewhere below, the bottled storm rustled restlessly on its shelf.

"I'm going," Rosalind said.

"No," said four voices at once.

"It wasn't a question." They heard their own voice come out level, and marveled a little at it. "He knew my name. He looked me in the eye and committed treason on my behalf, in front of four witnesses, and then he gave me a time and a place. Whatever he wants—whatever he *is*—he's spending his own neck to reach mine. I can't learn why from in here." They paused. "And if it's a trap, better it closes on me than on all of us. My papers are clean. My life is boring. I am, officially, nobody. I'm the one person in this room the crown has no reason to burn."

"That was supposed to be the point," Magrit said quietly. "That was the whole design. Invisible. Safe."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "Well. He saw me anyway."

They hadn't meant to say it aloud, and the moment it left their mouth they understood it was truer than they'd intended—in some way they couldn't yet map. Thirty years of careful gray lines, and one pair of eyes had picked them out across a crowded room and a decade of silence, and had *lied* for them. You could forge a document until even its creator half-believed it. You could not forge being seen.

The guild argued for another hour. Rosalind let them. In the end, as they always did in matters of individual risk, they left the choice to the individual, because the guild's one unbreakable rule was that no one was sent anywhere—they only went.

Rosalind went home—to the narrow rooms above the chandler's shop, the ones with the dull furniture and the deliberately mediocre sketches pinned to the walls—and pulled from beneath the loose hearthstone a box they opened perhaps twice a year.

Inside: a dried sprig of rowan from the academy grounds. A student's pen, cheap tin, long empty. And a scrap of paper, yellowed, on which in a young man's quick hand was written the trick of aging a capital letter—written out for them years after the fact, delivered anonymously, as if he had wanted to apologize for something and had no language for apology except instruction.

Rosalind sat by the dead fireplace a long while, holding the paper, watching the rain write and erase its endless rough draft on the window.

Tuesday. East annex.

Whatever waited there—truth, trap, or something stranger that had no name yet—it had started eleven years ago in a cold lecture hall, with a boy who lent pens and betrayed secrets and then, apparently, spent a decade learning to regret it. Rosalind had survived by being unremarkable. But tomorrow they would walk into the crown's own annex and let themselves be remarkable in the one way that mattered: seen, by him, on purpose.

They repacked the box, and paused, and took the note out again, and tucked it inside their coat.

Just in case they needed to ask him why.
