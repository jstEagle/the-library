## 4. What Theo Carries

Theo had rehearsed this confession for eleven years.

He had rehearsed it in the bathhouse steam of the Scribes' Quarter, murmuring the words into the wet air where no one could hear. He had rehearsed it on the road to Greyhollow, composing and discarding openings the way a bad poet discards verses—*I have come about a matter of state; I have come about a matter of treason; I have come because I cannot sleep.* He had rehearsed it standing before the mirror in his rented room while he pinned the inspector's chain at his throat, watching his own mouth shape the truth and finding that even alone, even to glass, it looked like a lie.

And now, standing in the back room of a shop that sold bottled weather to people who could not afford weather, with a guild of impossible persons arranged around him like jury members who had already decided the verdict, Theo found that every rehearsal had been useless. The words did not want to be spoken in order. They wanted to spill.

He made himself begin anyway. Penance was not supposed to be comfortable.

"My name is Theo Aldery," he said. "You know that much from the register. What you don't know—what no one living knows entire—is what I carry under these robes." His hand went, without his permission, to the leather case at his hip. Not the inspector's ledger. The other one. The one he had carried through checkpoints and across three provincial borders sewn into the lining of a coat that smelled of someone else's sweat. "Eleven years ago I took an oath in the Undercroft of the palace, kneeling on stone older than the dynasty, and swore myself not to the crown but to the man beneath it."

The lamplight caught the faces around him. Old Marta with her storm-bottles, her knuckles like walnuts. The twins, Ferren and Isolde, who were either one person split or two people fused—he had never determined which, and suspected the answer would offend them either way. And Rosalind Voss, seated apart from the others on an upturned crate, arms crossed, watching him with the particular stillness of a person who has spent a lifetime learning how to watch without being seen to watch.

Theo had passed over their forged papers four days ago. He had looked directly into those grey eyes and lied—or rather, had declined to tell a truth—and in the days since he had turned the moment over so many times it had worn smooth as river stone. *Why did you do it?* their expression asked now, more eloquently than any interrogation. *What are you?*

"Then let me start further back," he said, because starting further back was easier than starting at all. "Let me start with the king."

* * *

Everyone knew King Edmun IV. Everyone knew the broad shoulders, the voice that carried across a parade ground without effort, the laugh that shook the chandeliers at state dinners. Theo had known him differently: hunched over a map table at two in the morning, ink-stained, asking his youngest scribe—asking *Theo*, specifically, because Theo was nobody, a scholarship boy from the salt marshes with no family name worth flattering—asking him, *"If you drew the borders, boy, where would you put them?"*

That was the trap of Edmun, if it was a trap. He made you feel chosen. He made a nineteen-year-old scribe believe that being noticed by the king was the same thing as being seen by God.

"The binding was done during the Fever Winter," Theo said. "You'll remember it—the plague year, when the court emptied out and half the capital's bells tolled for weeks without stopping. What wasn't public knowledge was that the king himself sickened. Badly. Delirious for nine days. And on the ninth night, in a locked chamber above the Chapel of the Veil, six people did something to him that the histories will never record, because the histories are written by men like me, and men like me were ordered to burn the pages."

Marta shifted. A bottle on the shelf behind her chimed faintly, a trapped thunderstorm turning in its sleep.

"Six conspirators," Theo went on. "Three of them court physicians. One of them the Lord Treasurer. One of them—" he paused, and hated the pause, hated that his voice still flinched around the name— "one of them Chancellor Maurevan. And the sixth was never identified. The sigil they cut into him—I say *cut*, though there was no blood—is called a Sovereign Binding. It doesn't kill the king. It does something worse. It folds him up small and tucks him beneath his own shape, the way you'd fold a letter into an envelope. And then anyone holding the sigil's key can wear the envelope."

"You're saying," Isolde said slowly, "that the man on the throne—"

"—is not the man on the throne." Theo heard his own voice go flat, the way voices do when they've said a true thing too many times to themselves to feel it anymore. "Has not been for eleven years. The usurper—you may call him Maurevan's creature, call him the Wearer, I've heard the true king call it worse through the dream—wears Edmun's face the way you'd wear a glove. It commands his armies. It signs his decrees. It signed yours." He nodded toward the market square beyond the wall, where the proclamation paper still hung, its wax seal glinting like a wound. "*All gifted persons shall present themselves for registry. Those who conceal their nature are enemies of the realm and shall be dealt with accordingly.* That decree was written in my office. In the king's hand. In a hand I taught myself to forge well enough that I know, absolutely, that it is forged—not copied. *Made*. Because the man making it isn't the man whose hand it imitates."

The silence that followed had texture. Theo stood inside it and felt the weight of what he'd carried settle visibly onto his shoulders, as though the confession itself had physical form, a pack of stones he'd been hauling uphill for a decade.

"Why pass our papers, then?" Ferren demanded. The twin's voice always came out slightly doubled, an echo chasing its own mouth. "If you serve the crown—if you hunt for the Wearer—why let us walk?"

"Because I don't hunt for the Wearer." Theo unslung the leather case and set it on the table between them. His fingers were shaking. He let them shake. There was no point hiding it; hiding things was the whole architecture of his life, and he was here to tear that architecture down beam by beam, or die in the attempt, and either way the shaking might as well be seen. "I hunt for the sigil. For eleven years I have served the true king from inside the machine that imprisoned him. I am his only free servant, gentlemen, madam, and—Rosalind. I am possibly the last man in this kingdom who knows what happened to Edmun IV, and I am asking thieves and smugglers and makers of impossible weather to help me commit high treason against a king who does not exist, in order to save one who does."

He opened the case.

Inside, nested in wool gone grey with age, lay a single sheet of parchment. It looked unremarkable—which was, Theo understood now, precisely how dangerous things liked to look. Inked upon it in lines that seemed to shift when observed too long was a diagram: interlocking circles, a lattice of threads, a knot at the center drawn in gold leaf that had dulled to the color of old honey.

"This came to me eight months ago," Theo said. "By ways I will explain if you ask, and which I barely understand myself. It is a rendering of the Sovereign Binding—the closest thing to a map of it that exists outside the king's own skull. It was sent by someone who wants it broken. Someone on the other side of sleep." He looked at Rosalind when he said it, and saw them go very still, and knew—knew with the sudden certainty of a man stepping off a ledge—that they understood before he said another word.

"I need the Guild of Impossible Things," he said quietly, "because breaking this requires impossible things. And I am prepared to pay the only currency I have left, which is the truth. All of it. Including the part you're waiting for me to say, Rosalind Voss. Including Wren."

* * *

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Rosalind didn't move. But everything about their stillness changed—from the stillness of observation to the stillness of a held breath, a door barred from the inside. Marta glanced between them, sensing weather she couldn't read. The twins exchanged a look that was somehow a single look.

"You were at the Academy together," Theo said. His voice stayed level. He had practiced this part most of all, and practice held where rehearsal had failed. "Third-year cartography seminar. Wren Calloway, who could make a map show tomorrow instead of today. You both hid it. You both were careful." He swallowed. "I was careful too. Careful was the only currency a salt-marsh scholarship boy had, and I spent it hoarding other people's secrets, because secrets bought advancement, and advancement bought survival. And in the winter of our third year, an inspector came to the Academy asking questions about irregular students, and I—" 

His throat closed. He made himself breathe past it. Penance, he reminded himself, was not supposed to be comfortable. Comfortable penance was just vanity wearing a hair shirt.

"I gave them Wren," he said. "Not you. Your papers were duller than mine, your gift better hidden, and I told myself afterward that this was mercy—see, I saved one of you—but we both know what that arithmetic was. It was arithmetic. I weighed two lives against my career and paid out the cheaper one. Wren was taken for assessment in the spring. Wren was never returned. The official record says transferred to a facility in the north. I have spent eleven years with access to every archive in this kingdom, and I have never found a facility in the north, and I have never stopped looking, and I have never forgiven myself, and I am not asking you to forgive me." He drew a breath that hurt on the way in. "I'm telling you this because you deserve to know exactly what sort of man is asking for your help. If knowing it makes you turn me away, then knowing it has done its work."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere in the shop front, a bottle of morning fog leaked a thin silver thread along a shelf seam, patient as time.

Rosalind unfolded from the crate. They crossed the small room in four steps and stood over the open case, looking down at the sigil-diagram, and Theo watched their face do something complicated—the muscles around the eyes working against each other, grief and fury and something colder and more professional all contending for the same ground.

"Wren drew maps that moved," Rosalind said at last. Their voice was very quiet. "Little ones. On scraps. Rivers that flowed. We used to—" They stopped. Started again, flatter. "We used to race them. Paper boats down an ink river, on the floor of the seminar room after hours. That's what you handed to the inspectors, Theo. A person who raced paper boats with you."

"Yes."

"And the man in your case. The king. You're telling me he's alive under there. Aware."

"Aware." Theo's jaw tightened. "For eleven years. Watching his hands sign death warrants. Watching his armies march. Sometimes—" and here his voice finally cracked, the practiced seam splitting, and he let it, because the crack was the truest thing he owned— "sometimes, when the binding thins in the hour before dawn, I can reach him. Through dreams. He speaks to me. Do you know what he says? He doesn't rage. He doesn't curse Maurevan. Eleven years in a golden cage, and what he says is: *Tell them I'm sorry I'm late. Tell them I tried to hold the doors.*" Theo pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "He thinks he failed his kingdom. He lies awake inside his own stolen body apologizing to people who think he abandoned them. And every decree the Wearer signs, every pyre they light in the provinces, happens with his face on it, and he has to watch."

No one spoke. The fog-thread reached the edge of the shelf and pooled, soft as breath.

It was Marta who broke it, in her gravel-and-thunder voice: "Say what you need from us, scribe. Plainly. We're thieves, not philosophers."

Theo lowered his hand. Straightened. This was the part he had not rehearsed, because he had not dared—the part where hope became a specific request and could therefore be specifically refused.

"The binding lives in two places at once," he said. "In the king's body—a mark beneath the crown line, at the nape of the neck, invisible to any eye—and in a place that isn't anywhere. The sigil was cut in ritual space, in the space between sleeping and waking, and it is anchored there as much as in flesh. To break it, you must reach both ends simultaneously. The flesh-end requires getting close to the king's body, which sits guarded in a palace I can get us into. But the other end—" He turned the case toward Rosalind fully. "The other end is a dreamscape. A realm made of the king's sleeping mind and the sigil's geometry. It has geography. It has *edges*. I've seen fragments of it in eight months of dream-letters, and I am a scribe, not a seer—I can read it the way you'd read a description of a country. But I cannot chart it. I cannot draw a road through a place that exists only in sleep."

He met Rosalind's eyes.

"But I'm told," he said, "that there exists a cartographer who maps realms that shouldn't exist. Who keeps the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things. Whose gift the Academy assessed once and misfiled, because no one thought to write down what a mapmaker's mapmaker could do." His voice dropped. "I need you to chart the inside of a king's dream, Rosalind Voss. And I need you to believe that I came here first with the truth because it's the only thing I have left that's worth anything. I spent the rest of myself buying survival. This—" he touched the diagram, gently, the way one touches a relic or a wound— "this is what I spent it *on*."

The lamp burned steady. Outside, the market bell tolled the hour, and somewhere in the city a crier was reading the proclamation aloud again, the usurper's beautiful forged sentences rolling over the rooftops.

Rosalind looked at the sigil for a long moment. Then, without touching it, they said:

"There's a vote tomorrow among the guild. Whether we help you, scatter, or bury ourselves deeper." Their eyes lifted, grey and unreadable and anything but dull. "But before that vote, Theo Aldery, you're going to sit in that chair and tell me about the ninth conspirator. The one never identified. Because I've seen that diagram's geometry before—in Wren's last letter to me, the one that arrived the week they disappeared. And I don't believe in six coincidences."

Theo's blood went cold, and then, strangely, warm—as though some frozen part of him had finally been given leave to feel.

"What did the letter say?" he asked.

Rosalind smiled, and there was no kindness in it, only the terrible patience of a person who has kept a ledger of their own.

"That's my price for the chair," they said. "Sit down, scribe. Truth for truth. You wanted penance—let's find out how deep it goes."
