## 5. A Vote Among Thieves

The guild met in the cellar beneath the mapshop, which was not, strictly speaking, a cellar. It had been one once, in some older building that had burned or fallen or simply been forgotten, but when the Guild of Impossible Things needed a meeting place, they had not built one—they had *found* one, the way they found most things. The room existed now in the space between two walls of three different houses, and depending on which door you used, you entered from the chandler's on Copper Lane, the dyer's yard off Tallow Street, or, if you were Maren and insisted on being difficult, through a cupboard in an apartment where no cupboard had previously been.

Rosalind arrived first, as Rosalind always did. Someone had to light the lamps, count the chairs—eleven, always eleven, though they were fourteen members now and three would stand—and make certain the ledger was open to the correct page. Tonight the correct page was blank. That felt appropriate.

They set out the candles next: stubs of tallow, mostly, with one beeswax end saved from Midwinter. The guild's treasury contained a bottled storm, half a door to elsewhere, and four silver marks, so luxury was relative.

By the time the others came down—Brannoc first, filling the stairwell like weather; then the twins, Odile and Odette, moving as a single shadow with two opinions; old Sefa with her cane that was not a cane; young Pim, breathless, late, apologizing before his foot touched the bottom step—the room smelled of smoke and tallow and the particular nervousness of people who had decided to hold a vote they were afraid to lose.

Maren came last, through her cupboard, carrying a covered dish she set down without explanation. Maren always brought food to grim occasions. "Eat while you can," she said, lifting the cloth. Small pies. Rosalind took one and found it still warm and could not taste it at all.

"Is everyone here?" Brannoc asked, though he could see everyone here.

"Everyone who's coming," Sefa said. "Tomas sent word he won't attend. He says voting is for people with something left to lose." She lowered herself into her chair with the care of a woman whose joints had opinions. "He also says we're fools, the vote is unnecessary, and if we do vote, he votes no. So that's twelve voices present and one absent no."

"Twelve," Odile said.

"Thirteen," Odette said, at exactly the same moment, because the twins disagreed about everything on principle, including arithmetic.

Rosalind opened the ledger to the blank page and dipped a pen they did not yet intend to use. This was their role at these gatherings—not leader, the guild had never had one and would have eaten any candidate alive—but keeper. Keeper of minutes, keeper of accounts, keeper of the record that said *this happened, and this is what it cost*. In eleven years they had written down every impossible thing the guild had done, every debt incurred, every favor traded, every member lost to the crown's prisons or to worse things that lived in the between-places. They knew, better than anyone in this room, exactly how much each miracle weighed.

Which was why, when Brannoc turned to them and said, "Voss. You called this meeting. Say what it is," Rosalind's hand went cold around the pen.

"I didn't call it," they said. "Theo Aldery did."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. Pim flinched. Odile and Odette exchanged the look that preceded their arguments by several seconds, giving everyone time to brace.

"The scribe," Sefa said. Not a question. She'd been at the market bell when the proclamation went up; she'd been in the shop when the inspectors came through with their ledgers of names. "The king's own man sat across from you, looked at papers you forged in an afternoon, and passed you clean. And now we're voting on whether to help him."

"He's not the king's man anymore. He burned his credentials."

"Burning paper is easy. I've seen men burn whole churches and remain exactly what they were."

"Maren vouches for him," Rosalind said.

"Maren vouches for everyone," said Odile.

"It's why we keep her," said Odette.

Maren, unbothered, ate one of her own pies. "I vouch for him because I've watched him for three nights, and a man planning betrayal sleeps badly and checks his exits. This one sleeps like a man who has already lost everything worth stealing and is deciding what to build in the space. Also—" she pointed the remaining pie at Rosalind, "—he told Voss the truth. All of it. The sigil, the binding, the usurper. You don't hand your sharpest secret to someone you intend to rob."

"You hand it to someone you intend to *use*," Brannoc rumbled. "That's the oldest trick there is. Give the mark a grand story, watch them run straight at the blade." He planted both fists on the table, and the table, which was made of salvaged ship-timber and had survived worse, creaked anyway. "I've heard the story. King bound under his own crown, golden thread, a dream-realm only our cartographer can enter. It's a fine tale. Tales are free. What it costs us is not."

"Then let us say plainly what it costs," Rosalind said, and heard their own voice come out steadier than they felt. This was the part they had rehearsed all day, alone, over the maps, and it came out now in the flat bookkeeper's tone they used for bad accounting. "If we help him, we help break a binding sigil woven into the king himself. That means entering a realm that exists only inside his sleeping mind, guided by a map I haven't drawn yet, against defenses designed by whoever bound him—and whoever binds a king does not leave the lock easy. If we fail, we fail quietly, and the usurper never knows we tried. If we succeed, we succeed loudly, because a king who wakes will ask who freed him, and a usurper who falls will take names down with him. Either way, the hunt outside those walls doesn't stop. But success makes us visible. And visibility, right now, is death."

Silence. Somewhere above them, through walls that weren't walls, the ordinary city went on: a cart rattling past the chandler's, someone laughing, a dog with strong opinions. The sound of a world that did not know it was governed by a dead man's stolen face.

"And if we don't help?" Pim asked. Young Pim, who could coax seeds to sprout in midwinter and had spent the proclamation week sleeping in a different cellar every night. "We hide. We wait it out. Hunts end. They always end."

"Do they?" Sefa said softly. "I've buried friends from three hunts, boy. The hunts end. The graves keep."

"The armies don't," Rosalind said. "That's the other ledger entry. The usurper isn't hunting us because we're dangerous. He's hunting us because gifted folk are the only ones who might notice what sits on the throne. Every city he takes, every register he burns, every pyre he lights—it's not cruelty for its own sake. It's him clearing the field. When there's no one left who can see magic, no one will be able to see *him*." They turned the ledger around so the blank page faced the room. "We can wait out this hunt. We cannot wait out what comes after it. There won't be anywhere left to be invisible."

Odette leaned forward. "You've thought about this."

"I'm the bookkeeper. Thinking is my gift. It's the least illegal one I have."

That got a laugh, small and startled, the kind of laugh people use to keep from screaming, and Rosalind was grateful for it even as they hated themselves for earning it.

* * *

The arguments went the way arguments go among thieves, which is to say sideways, in circles, and occasionally through the furniture.

Brannoc wanted numbers: how many guards, what garrisons, what roads the usurper's host would march. Rosalind gave him what the maps suggested and admitted freely what they didn't know, and Brannoc grunted at the honesty more than the answers, because Brannoc trusted nothing that pretended to certainty.

The twins fought about whether helping the crown—even a true king held hostage—was loyalty or lunacy. Odile had lost a brother to a royal hanging before the guild existed; Odette argued that a kingdom ruled by a usurper would hang far more than brothers. They argued for forty minutes, sometimes agreeing so completely they had to invent new disagreements to continue, and in the end they voted opposite ways, because they always did, because their votes canceling each other out was itself a kind of ritual, a way of saying *we disagree and remain.*

Sefa told a story. She was old enough that her stories arrived without preamble, dropping into conversation like stones into wells. This one was about a night, thirty years ago, when she had hidden a runaway witch-child in a flour barrel while soldiers drank her kitchen dry, and how the child had grown up to be a healer who had since pulled more people back from death than Sefa could count. "One barrel," she said. "One night of risk. I didn't save a king. I saved a child in a barrel. And I have spent thirty years watching what grew out of that night." She looked around the table. "I know what we're being asked. It's bigger than a barrel. But I am too old to pretend size changes the arithmetic."

Pim wanted to help. Pim always wanted to help; it was his tragedy and his charm. But when the vote came near, he looked at Rosalind with naked, terrible trust—the trust of the young, who have not yet learned that the people they admire are making it up as they go—and Rosalind had to look away.

And through it all, Maren said almost nothing, which was unlike her, and watched Rosalind, which was very much like her.

It was Maren who finally called the question. "Enough. We vote. Those for helping the scribe and the sleeper king, raise hands. Against, keep yours down. Abstentions count as against—we're not running a philosophy salon." She glanced at Rosalind. "Keeper keeps the tally."

Hands rose. Brannoc: no, though his no came slow, with the reluctance of a man who had already begun planning the logistics of a yes. Sefa: yes. Pim: yes, immediately, hand up so fast it nearly left his body. Odile: yes. Odette: no. Tomas, absent: no. Maren herself: yes, and then she folded her arms and looked at Rosalind and waited.

Six yes. Four no. One vote left.

Every eye in the room came around to Rosalind, and Rosalind understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only at the worst moments, that they had known this would happen. That they had perhaps arranged it, in the way a careful person arranges everything without admitting it—that by calling the meeting, laying out the costs, refusing to argue for either side, they had made themselves the balance. The guild trusted the books. The books said the risk was ruinous. The books also said that doing nothing was merely a slower ruin with better manners.

"I want it noted," Rosalind said, pen poised over the blank page, voice dry, "that I hate every person in this room."

"Noted," said Maren.

"I want it further noted that invisibility has kept me alive for eleven years. Eleven years of dull ledgers and forged papers and being nobody, and it worked. It worked right up until it didn't. Up until a neighbor got taken from his own doorstep for mending a broken pot with a word. Up until a scribe looked at me and *saw* me, and instead of reporting me, he handed me the truth like a man handing over a debt he's carried too long." They set the pen down. Their hands were not quite steady, and they let the room see it, because hiding it suddenly seemed like the smaller lie. "I keep saying I stayed safe by being unseen. But I've spent the last two days drawing a king's dream in my head, and worrying about whether Theo Aldery eats, and lying awake counting the pyres, and I have to tell you the truth, which is my job tonight: I stopped being invisible the moment I started caring. Caring is visibility. There's no forging your way out of it. So the question isn't whether voting yes costs me my safety. My safety's already gone. The question is whether I spend what replaced it on something worthwhile."

They picked the pen back up.

"Yes," Rosalind said, and wrote it: *Aye — R. Voss, keeper.* Seven to four. "Motion passes. Gods forgive us all."

No one cheered. That was the guild's way; victories here were just debts with better lighting. Brannoc exhaled like a landslide settling and said, "Then we'll need routes, supplies, and someone watching the doors," and just like that they were planning, because the guild's answer to terror had always been work.

But as the room broke into voices—Pim volunteering for everything, Sefa assigning herself to the archives, the twins arguing anew about who would scout which road—Rosalind sat with the ledger open and understood something they would never write down in any book:

The page wasn't blank anymore. It hadn't been since a scribe had looked them in the eye and lied beautifully to save them. Whatever Rosalind had been before—a keeper of records, a ghost in plain clothes, a carefully maintained nobody—that person had ended somewhere between the forged papers and the vote, and what remained was someone who cared, visibly, accountably, dangerously.

Someone whose name was now written in ink beside a rebellion.

Above them, muffled through walls that belonged to three houses at once, the market bell began to ring the hour, and Rosalind closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a room that can't be un-entered, and went upstairs to find Theo Aldery—because the vote was cast, and the next thing the ledger required was a map of a king's dreams.

And Rosalind already knew, in the place where cartographers know things before they can prove them, that the map would not be content to be drawn.
