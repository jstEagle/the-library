## 7. Ashes on the Register

The summons came on a morning so gray that the ink in Theo's travel desk had begun to glisten, as though even it wanted to weep and could not quite commit.

He was in the provisioning office of the Third Inspection Circuit, a room that smelled of tallow and wet wool, cataloguing confiscated charms with the methodical patience of a man burying something alive one spoonful at a time. A copper hand-warmer that hummed when held. A needle that found its own thread. A child's slate that wrote back. Each item he entered into the register in his beautiful, condemned script—the script that had won him his place at the academy at eleven, the script that had signed away a classmate's life at seventeen, the script that now recorded, item by item, the dismantling of every small wonder in the province of Vaelmoor.

The courier did not knock. Couriers of the Privy Seal never did. He simply stood in the doorway until Theo looked up, held out the folded parchment with its broken wax, and left without a word, as though delivering a death sentence were beneath his dignity.

It was, in a sense. It was beneath everyone's dignity. That was rather the point of it.

Theo broke the seal with a thumbnail and read the summons twice, because the first reading refused to be believed.

*By order of His Majesty's Grace, the following registered persons of the town of Ashfall are commanded to present themselves at the market square on the morning of the third day, there to demonstrate the loyalty of the crown's registered subjects and the mercy of the crown's justice. Attendance is compulsory for all residents. The demonstration will be public.*

*The demonstration will be public.*

Theo had written enough royal prose in his years as a scribe to know what a phrase like that was built to hide. It was a euphemism the way a headsman's block was a piece of furniture. He sat very still for a long moment, the parchment trembling in a way his hands had not trembled since the academy, and then he did what he had trained himself to do: he folded it, tucked it into his coat, and went back to cataloguing a needle that could find its own thread.

Because that was the arrangement. That was the bargain he had struck with himself the day he took the inspectors' commission and told himself it was penance, that he could work from inside, that he could pass the forged papers of the gifted and slow the machine one gear-tooth at a time. The bargain had a price, and the price was this: when the machine ground someone up, he had to stand at the crank and watch.

He thought of Rosalind Voss's face when he'd looked straight through their forged papers—the flicker of something between suspicion and contempt, the way they had held his gaze a half-second too long, daring him to explain himself. He thought of the ledger of impossible things he had glimpsed once, sideways, in the guild's back room: bottled storms, doors to elsewhere. He thought of a girl named Maren Ostrey, seventeen years old, who had once made paper birds fly across the academy lecture hall to make a homesick boy laugh, and who had been expelled in disgrace because that same homesick boy had reported her to save his own skin.

He had been telling himself for eleven years that the report had been necessary. He had gotten very good at it. He was, he realized, cataloguing the slate that wrote back with the same fluency.

* * *

Maren Ostrey had registered.

That was the thing Theo could not stop circling, the way his pen circled a blot it could not lift. When the proclamation came—*all gifted persons must register with the crown or be judged as traitors*—Maren had done the sensible thing. She had walked to the magistrate's office in Ashfall with her head up and her name spoken clearly, and she had signed the register in front of witnesses, and she had paid her fee, and she had gone home to her husband and her infant daughter believing the words the herald had read: *the crown extends its mercy to all who come honestly into the light.*

Theo knew this because he had been the one to process her registration. Third Inspection Circuit, provincial office, Vaelmoor. He had taken her papers from the magistrate's clerk, read her declaration—*gift of mending, minor; can close small tears in cloth and leather*—and entered it into the crown's book in his beautiful, condemned script. She had stood across the desk from him and not recognized him, or had pretended not to, and either way she had thanked him for his courtesy, because he had been courteous. He had been so careful to be courteous. Courtesy was the coin he paid himself so he could pretend he was not paying in something else.

*A gift of mending, minor.* She could close small tears in cloth and leather. In the eleven years since the academy, she had apparently decided that was all she was, or all she could safely be. Theo had looked at her steady hands and her tired eyes and thought: *there but for a signature go I.* And then he had stamped the page, and wished her a good day, and gone to lunch.

On the third day, the market square of Ashfall was full to its edges.

They came because attendance was compulsory, and because the crown had learned that fear travels fastest when it is made communal. Farmers in their good coats. Shopkeepers with their children on their shoulders, the children excited, the parents gray. The square was ringed with soldiers in the king's colors—the true king's colors, Theo thought, though the king had not chosen to wear them in four years—and at the center, on a scaffold that had been built in a single night by men who were paid too well to ask questions, stood a block and a basket and a headsman in a hood, and beside them, a table with a velvet cloth.

On the velvet cloth lay a sword.

It was a beautiful sword. Theo knew its provenance because he had catalogued it, three weeks ago, from a confiscated collection in the house of a hedge-witch who had died of natural causes and left her treasures to no one. It was a sword that had been made to never dull, never rust, never fail—a sword with a small, patient gift worked into the steel by some smith three hundred years dead who had never harmed a soul. It had been entered into the register of confiscated objects as *item 447: blade, enchanted, minor; destruction ordered.*

They were going to break it on the block. And then—

"Good people of Ashfall." The herald's voice was trained, carrying, pleasant. Theo stood at the edge of the square in his inspectors' coat, and hated that the coat got him the good sightline. "You have heard the proclamation of His Majesty's Grace. You have heard that the crown extends mercy to those who register, who come honestly into the light, who place their small gifts at the service of the realm. Today you will see that mercy honored. Today you will see that the crown keeps its word."

Maren Ostrey was brought up onto the scaffold.

She looked smaller than Theo remembered. Her hair was pinned back plainly; her dress was the gray one, the one she'd worn to the registration, and he understood with a lurch that she had worn it on purpose, that she had dressed for this the way one dresses for church, that she had believed—she had actually, honestly believed—that this was a ceremony of the crown's favor, a public affirmation, her name read aloud as a loyal subject of the realm.

She was holding her daughter.

No. She was handing her daughter to a woman at the foot of the steps—her sister, perhaps, or a neighbor—and the child was crying, and Maren kissed her forehead and said something that did not carry, and then she climbed the steps alone.

"The crown's mercy," the herald said, "is extended to the registered. But mercy, good people, must be *demonstrated*. For there are those who say the crown's word is soft. There are those who whisper that a registered gift is a licensed rebellion, that the register is a list the traitors may one day consult. And to those whispers, His Majesty's Grace answers thus."

He unrolled a parchment. Theo knew the handwriting on it. He had written it—or a version of it, a template, one of forty he had prepared in the first month of his commission, back when he still told himself that paperwork was a wall between the gifted and the pyre.

*Maren Ostrey, registered gift of mending, is found to have used her gift outside the terms of her registration. She is found to have mended, without license, the sail of a fishing boat; the shoe of a neighbor's child; the binding of a book belonging to the parish. She is found to have given her gift away freely, without payment to the crown, without record, without light. For this, the mercy of the register is withdrawn.*

She had mended a sail. She had mended a shoe. She had mended a *book*. The crimes were so small that the square itself seemed confused, a murmur running through the crowd like wind through wheat—*that's it? that's the crime?*—and Theo watched the murmur and watched the soldiers watch the murmur, and understood that the confusion was not an accident. The smallness was the message. The smallness said: *there is no floor beneath you. The crime is whatever we say it is, the morning we need it to be.*

Maren did not struggle. That was the worst of it. She looked out at the square—at her daughter, at her neighbors, at the gray sky—and she lifted her chin the way she had lifted it in the lecture hall at sixteen, before the flying paper birds, before everything, and Theo saw that she had decided to give them no spectacle, that her last act of mending would be to mend the fear in the faces watching her, to show them a woman who was not afraid, so that they might be a little less afraid after.

The headsman's hood was dark against the gray sky. The sword that could never fail was not used; it lay on its velvet, a prop, a promise of what happened to objects that outlived their owners' permission.

Theo did not look away. He had looked away once, at seventeen, when the proctor came for Maren the elder—no, that was a different girl, a different year, they blurred now, they had blurred for years, that was what looking away did, it blurred—and he had promised himself, in the private chapel of his own cowardice, that he would never look away again. Looking away was the sin. Looking away was how the machine stayed clean.

So he looked. He looked while the herald read the sentence. He looked while Maren Ostrey knelt at the block that had been built in a night. He looked while she said something to the headsman that made the man's shoulders flinch—something kind, he would have bet his life on it, something kind—and he looked when it was done, and the basket was covered, and the herald's trained voice said, "Thus does the crown keep its word," and the crowd stood in the gray drizzle and did not cheer, and the soldiers watched them not cheering, and wrote down who did not cheer.

Theo wrote down nothing. Theo stood in his inspectors' coat with his hands folded behind his back like a man at a funeral, which was accurate, and felt something in his chest finish breaking—not shatter, not all at once, but *finish*, the way a dam finishes, the way a rope finishes, quietly and all at once and irreversibly.

The child was still crying when the crowd dispersed. Nobody could make her stop. Her mother had mended tears in cloth and leather. There had never been a gift for this.

* * *

He burned his credentials that night in the grate of his lodgings above the provisioning office.

It was not a dramatic decision. That surprised him most, afterward—how undramatic it was. He had imagined, in the years of his slow penance, that rebellion would arrive like a storm, that some great wind would take him, that he would feel the moment of his treason as a thunderclap. Instead he sat on the floor in front of a small fire with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, and he fed the fire his life one document at a time.

His commission, first. The heavy parchment with the Privy Seal, the one that named him *Theodore Aldery, Royal Inspector of Gifts, Third Circuit, with full authority of seizure and judgment.* It caught slowly; the seal wax resisted, then softened, then went up in a bloom of black and gold that smelled like a church burning.

His travel warrants. His letters of authority, which had opened doors and closed them. His salary ledger, which he kept meticulously, because a man who could not be honest with himself about money could not be honest at all—the salary he had told himself was penance-money, blood-money, money he would one day give away, and never had.

And last—because he made himself wait, because he made himself hold it a long time—the academy commendation. *Awarded to Theodore Aldery, first in his year, for exemplary service to the discipline of records.* First in his year. He had been first in his year at everything. First at script. First at cipher. First, though it was never said aloud, never written down, at the thing the academy actually taught, which was not letters but loyalty, which was the fine and disciplined art of knowing which names to write and which names to surrender.

Maren Ostrey, seventeen, expelled. Maren Ostrey, thirty-one, dead of a mended shoe.

He held the commendation over the fire and found that his hands were steady, and this steadiness frightened him more than trembling would have, because it meant the decision had been made long ago, made in a market square or a lecture hall or a provisioning office, made in all the small surrenders he had called survival, and tonight was merely the date on the page.

"Item four hundred forty-eight," he said aloud, to the empty room, in the voice he used for the register. "Credentials of Theodore Aldery, inspector. Destruction ordered."

He let it burn.

The firelight moved on the wall, and in the movement he saw—not a vision, he was not gifted, he was only a tired man with a good memory—the flying paper birds crossing the lecture hall, bright and impossible, and the homesick boy they had been made for, laughing. He had reported her the next day because the proctor asked if anyone had seen anything unusual, and the truth was in his mouth before he had decided to speak it, and the look she gave him across the hall had been not hatred but *disappointment*, which was worse, which had followed him for eleven years like a debt he kept refinancing.

He could not un-report her. He could not un-register Maren Ostrey, could not un-build the scaffold, could not mend the child crying in a house in Ashfall. The register of his own sins was written in his own beautiful script, and there was no gift of mending for paper.

But there was a ledger across town, in a cellar behind an unremarkable door, kept by an unremarkable cartographer with careful eyes and forged papers that Theo had looked straight through and passed. There was a guild of impossible things, with its bottled storms and its doors to elsewhere. There was, if the whispers in his own confiscated files were true, a king who was not a king, a man on a throne who was a mask, a binding sigil wearing the crown like a wound wears a bandage.

And there was a truth Theo had carried so long it had grown into him like a splinter into bone: *the machine does not slow from inside. He had been inside for eleven years, and the machine had only learned to use his hands.*

He knelt at the grate until the last of the commendation was ash, and then he did not stop there. He took out the files—the real ones, the ones he had copied in secret against a day he had pretended not to be preparing for: the routes of the inspection circuits, the names of the crown's informants, the schedules of the seizures, and beneath all of it, in a folder he had built himself, page by page, the evidence he had been assembling like a man building a raft in a cellar: discrepancies in the king's public appearances, the scribe's notes that did not match the king's hand, the strange stillness of the royal signature after a certain date, four years ago, as though the hand that signed had been a copy of a hand.

A binding. A sigil. A usurper wearing the king.

He had not believed it, when the whispers first reached him. He had believed it the way he now believed everything: reluctantly, and then completely, and then with the whole of his life stacked on top of it.

Theo rose, brushed the ash from his knees, and put on his coat. In the pocket, against his heart, he placed the folder. In his other pocket, a single sheet of good paper, on which he had written nothing yet, because some letters you write only after you have burned the person who would have had to sign them.

The rain had stopped. The streets were black and shining. He went out into them without a lantern, a man no longer employed by anything but his own guilt and his own hope, walking not away from the provisioning office but toward the unremarkable door, the cellar, the ledger, the cartographer who had every right to turn him in and every reason not to trust him.

*Let them refuse me,* he thought, knocking softly, twice, in the pattern he had been given and had never used until now. *Let them hate me. Hate is a door too. It opens from the inside.*

Above the lintel, faint as breath, a sigil he had never noticed before stirred at his knock—and the door began to open before he finished knocking, as though someone on the other side had been waiting up, all this time, for exactly this.
