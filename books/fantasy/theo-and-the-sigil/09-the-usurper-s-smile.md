## 9. The Usurper's Smile

The summons came at the hour when the palace lamps were trimmed and the corridors belonged to the mice and the memory of better kings.

Theo Aldery was in the Scriptorium Annexe, third desk from the window that looked out over the dry fountain, correcting a clerk's transcription of grain tariffs. It was honest work, deliberately small work. He had spent four years making himself the sort of man who corrected grain tariffs—competent, unambitious, forgettable—and he had almost convinced himself it was working when the door opened without a knock.

"Theo Aldery." The page was young, perhaps fifteen, with the hollow-cheeked look of someone who had learned to be afraid efficiently. "His Majesty requires you."

"Requires me for what?" Theo asked, though his hands had already gone still on the tariff ledger, already gone careful, the way a man's hands go still when a snake crosses the path between him and the gate.

The boy's eyes flicked up and away. "I'm not told to know things, sir. Only to carry messages."

*Smart child*, Theo thought, and hated that this city had taught its children that particular wisdom so young. He rose, straightened his collar, and followed the page into the corridor where two of the King's Own waited—not the old King's Own, whose livery had been dove-grey with silver thread, but the new guard, whose colors were black shot through with gold, gold like wire, gold like thread.

Gold like a sigil.

He walked between them through halls he had known since he was nine years old, when his father had first brought him to petition for a scribe's apprenticeship, and he catalogued what had changed the way Rosalind would have catalogued it: as a cartographer notes where a river has moved. The portraits in the Long Gallery had been rehung. The old king's portrait—the true one, painted before his hair went grey, showing the laugh lines around his eyes that no one had ever managed to paint out—was gone from its place of honor above the marble hearth. In its place hung a newer image, technically flawless, of a face Theo knew intimately and had never once seen smile correctly.

The usurper had commissioned a portrait of the king's stolen face and gotten it wrong by exactly one expression. The mouth was right. The jaw was right. But the eyes in the painting held a patience that King Edric's eyes had never possessed—a watcher's patience, an appetite's patience.

Someone in this palace had noticed. Someone always noticed. And someone, presumably, had been made an example of, because the noticing had stopped.

* * *

The antechamber outside the royal audience hall smelled of beeswax and, beneath it, faintly, of iron. Not blood—iron, raw and mineral, like a well that had been dug too deep. Theo had been smelling it for months and telling himself it was the plumbing. Tonight, standing among six other summoned officials while a chamberlain read out their names with the cadence of a man reading a butcher's list, Theo let himself admit what the smell was.

It was the sigil. Or rather, it was the edges of the sigil, leaking through the seams of the world the way light leaks under a badly hung door. Rosalind had described it once, three nights ago in the guildhouse, drawing on the back of a supply manifest: *binding magic isn't quiet. People think spells are loud, thunder and flash. Real bindings are worse—they hum. They sit underneath everything like water under a floorboard, and everything above them starts to rot in patterns.*

The rot-patterns were everywhere now, if you knew how to look. The gardeners had replaced the roses in the east court twice; they kept dying in spiral shapes. The clocks in the palace ran slow by different amounts depending on how close they stood to the throne room. The dogs had stopped barking entirely—you never heard a dog in the capital anymore, and no one remarked upon it, because remarking required the capacity for comparison, and comparison had been quietly amputated from the public mind.

"Master Aldery." The chamberlain's voice. "You are called fourth."

Theo bowed the precise degree required of a senior scribe of the second rank and passed through doors tall enough to admit a war-elephant, into the hall where the king of Varethane received petitioners.

The man on the dais was wearing King Edric's body.

That was the thought Theo made himself hold, every time, because holding it was the only thing that kept the horror sharp enough to be useful. Familiarity was the usurper's greatest ally; the eye grew tired of screaming and settled into acceptance. So Theo looked, every time, and made himself see it. The broad shoulders, slightly stooped now—the real king had carried the weight of the northern wars there. The long face, the strong nose broken once and set a half-degree crooked during the siege of Calver's Ford, which the king had joked about for a decade afterward. All of it correct. All of it worn like a coat.

And behind the eyes, looking out through King Edric's face at the room full of kneeling subjects: something else. Something that enjoyed the view.

"Rise," said the usurper, in the king's voice. The voice was perfect too. That had taken Theo the longest time to accept—that even the voice could be counterfeited, even the slight rasp the king had earned shouting orders across frozen rivers. "Lord Chancellor, approach. I have questions about the western levies."

What followed was forty minutes of statecraft performed flawlessly by a thing that did not care about a single soul affected by any of it. Theo knelt among the other officials and watched, and listened, and learned, because learning was the only weapon he had left that didn't require him to survive using it.

The usurper—no one knew its name, or whether it had one; the guild's records called it only *the Wearer*—was good at being a king in the way that a well-made puppet is good at being alive. It had studied. It knew the protocols. It asked after harvest yields and remembered the names of provincial governors' wives and dispensed justice with an efficiency that had, in the early months, genuinely fooled half the court. There were people in this room, Theo understood, who still believed they served King Edric. Who went home each night satisfied that their sovereign was stern but sound, that the purges were regrettable necessities, that the witch-hunt was the king's own grief-driven response to whatever illness had first touched him.

But the mask slipped in the margins. That was what Theo had come to understand about the Wearer across these months of forced proximity: it could perform the king's decisions, but not the king's mercies. When a petitioning widow had wept before the throne last spring, the real Edric would have risen from the dais—he always had, protocol be damned, the guards always wincing—and lifted her up himself. The Wearer had instead watched her cry for a long moment with an expression of frank, unhurried interest, like a scholar observing an interesting specimen, before waving her off with a wordless flick of the king's fingers.

Cruelty, Theo realized, watching tonight's petitions unfold, was not a tool for the Wearer. It was a pleasure. Everything else—governance, war, the machinery of empire—was obligation, performed adequately and set down gratefully. But cruelty it savored. It drew out the punishments. It scheduled executions at hours calculated to inconvenience the families of the condemned. It had recently begun summoning minor officials late at night, one at a time, for audiences that consisted of nothing but silence while the king stared at them—Theo had heard the reports from the survivors, pale men who spoke of the experience the way sailors speak of storms.

Tonight, apparently, it was his turn.

"Master Aldery." The king's voice, saying his name, and Theo felt his whole body go cold and then hot, the way it does before a fall. "Come forward. The rest of you—out. My Lord Chancellor will remain at the door."

The hall emptied with the smooth speed of courtiers fleeing a fire they were too well-bred to acknowledge. Soon there were only three living souls in the vast stone room: the chancellor, rigid by the door; Theo, ascending the steps to the dais because refusing would kill him slower but just as certainly; and the thing in the king's shape, seated in the throne that had been carved for a man two hundred years ago and had fit Edric like a glove.

The usurper did not ask him to kneel.

"You are the scribe who audits the registries," it said. "The loyalty rolls."

"I have that duty among others, Your Majesty."

"Mm." The usurper leaned back, and the throne creaked, and Theo's skin crawled at the small domesticity of the sound. "You have a reputation, Master Aldery. Diligent. Thorough. Unimaginative." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "My inspectors speak highly of you. Especially your work in the Lower Market districts. You found very few irregularities there, I'm told. Very few gifted persons hiding among the honest population."

*Here it is*, Theo thought, and some cold, clear part of him that sounded remarkably like Rosalind said: *Well. You knew this hour would come. Let's see what you're worth.*

"The Lower Markets are quiet districts, Your Majesty. Honest tradesmen, mostly. Fishmongers, chandlers, dyers." He kept his voice level, bored even. "If there were witches there, they keep better secrets than most."

"If." The usurper smiled, and it was the wrong smile again, the portrait-smile, the mouth moving correctly around an emotion the original face had never housed. "You use the word 'if' as though doubt were a neutral position, Master Aldery. As though a man might simply decline to believe in things and be safe from them." It tilted its head, studying him, and Theo had the vertiginous sense of being examined not as a subject but as an object—as a lock being weighed for the quality of its metal. "Tell me. Do you believe in impossible things?"

The question landed in Theo's chest like a dropped anchor. Somewhere across the city, in a cellar full of bottled storms and doors to elsewhere, a cartographer with ink-stained fingers was keeping ledgers of exactly those things, and Theo loved her—loved them, he corrected himself, feeling his heart stumble and reseat itself, because Rosalind had explained it to him once with great patience and he had promised, privately, silently, to get it right every time—and here was the engine of all the world's burning asking him, in his dead king's voice, whether such things existed.

"Your Majesty," Theo said carefully, "I believe in what can be written down and verified. Grain counts. Tax rolls. Signatures against seals. Beyond that, my belief is not required by my office."

"No," the usurper agreed softly. "It isn't, is it? Belief is such an untidy thing. So difficult to audit." It rose from the throne, and Theo fought every instinct screaming at him to retreat, and held his ground as the king's body descended the three steps until they stood close enough that Theo could smell him—or it—under the iron scent: cedar oil, ink, the king's own barber's pomade. A costume complete down to its smells. "Do you know what I have learned, Master Aldery, sitting in this chair? People will forgive their king anything except weakness. They do not need love. They need certainty. Give them a ruler who is never ill, never doubtful, never merciful at the wrong moment, and they will call it strength right up until the moment it eats them."

"That is—" Theo searched for a courtly phrase and found, to his horror, that honesty was rising in him like floodwater. "That is a bleak philosophy of rule, Your Majesty."

Silence.

The chancellor, by the door, had stopped breathing. Theo watched the usurper watch him, and understood suddenly that he had just done something no one in this hall had dared to do in a year: he had disagreed with the king. Openly. To its face.

And then the usurper laughed.

It used Edric's throat to make the sound, and it was a good imitation of Edric's laugh, warm and rolling—but it came a half-second too late, the way thunder comes after lightning, as though the laughter had to travel a long distance to reach the mouth. And Theo saw, in that half-second gap, the thing underneath look at him with naked, delighted curiosity, the way a child looks at an insect that has bitten it and lived.

"Bleak," it repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. You are either very brave or very stupid, scribe, and my experience is that the two wear identical coats." It turned away, ascending back toward the throne, and Theo allowed himself to breathe. "Which is why you will be useful to me. The witch-hunters bring me lists. Lists are dead things—names crossed off, ashes swept away. What I want now is a mind. Someone who reads the way you evidently read, who hears the lie inside a loyal sentence." It sat, arranged the king's hands on the armrests, and regarded him with the king's tired, handsome face. "You are appointed Special Auditor of Irregular Persons, effective tonight. You will report directly to me. Weekly. Here. At this hour."

"Your Majesty honors me," Theo heard himself say, from a great distance, through a ringing in his ears.

"It is not honor," said the Wearer pleasantly. "It is use. Pray you continue to be useful. The last auditor was not."

It waved a dismissal, and Theo bowed, and walked the length of the hall on legs that belonged to someone else, past the chancellor who would not meet his eyes, through the elephant-tall doors, into the antechamber where the iron smell was strongest, and only when he reached the Long Gallery—the gallery with the wrong portrait hanging over the marble hearth—did he permit himself to stop, and press one hand flat against the cold stone wall, and stand there shaking.

They had given him a title. Access. A weekly audience with the thing itself, alone, in a closed room.

They had handed him the knife and pointed him at the heart, assuming—correctly, so far—that he was too frightened to use it. And the terrible arithmetic of it turned over in his mind as he stood in the gallery: every week he sat in that room was a week the guild gained intelligence no spy could buy. Every report he filed was a map of the enemy's thinking. He had spent four years building himself into a dull, forgettable man, and in eleven minutes the usurper had demolished the disguise and rebuilt him as a weapon.

*Rosalind*, he thought, and the thought steadied him the way a fixed star steadies a navigator. *Rosalind will know what this means. Rosalind will see the shape of it.*

He pushed off from the wall and walked on, past the false portrait, and did not look at it again. Behind him, deep in the palace, the clocks ticked at their separate wrong speeds, and somewhere far below the foundations, under stone older than the crown, a bound man slept and dreamed, and dreamed, and dreamed.
