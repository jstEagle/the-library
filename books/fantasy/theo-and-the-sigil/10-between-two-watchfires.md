## 10. Between Two Watchfires

The borderlands of the dream-realm did not have a proper night, but they had something close enough: a slow dimming of the violet sky, like ink settling in water, and a cold that came up through the ground rather than down from the air. Rosalind had charted three kinds of twilight since they'd crossed into this place, and had named none of them, because some things resisted naming and it was better to admit that early.

They made camp in the lee of a hill that was not quite a hill — more a memory of one, its slopes soft-edged, as if someone had described a hill to the realm secondhand and the realm had done its best. The grass whispered in no wind at all. Two fires burned side by side because Rosalind had insisted on two; a single fire meant sitting shoulder to shoulder, and they weren't sure their ribs could survive that yet.

Theo built his fire with a scribe's economy — kindling laid in careful ranks, each stick placed like a word in a well-made sentence. Rosalind's fire was a mess of driftwood and stubbornness, but it caught, and it threw light further, which was the point. Watchfires were not for beauty. They were for telling the dark where the line was drawn.

Beyond the fires, the borderlands went on being strange. Rivers ran uphill in slow silver ribbons. A forest stood in the middle distance with all its leaves turned to show their undersides, pale as fish bellies, waiting for a season that hadn't been invented. Somewhere far off, something sang — not a bird, not a person, but the memory of both, worn smooth by repetition until only the melody remained.

Rosalind checked the map spread across their knees for the fourth time in an hour. Ink moved here. That was the trouble and the wonder of mapping dreamscape: you drew a river and the river took your line as a suggestion, wandering off to try new courses while your original stroke dried into a record of what the river had *once* wanted. Every map Rosalind made in this place was less a document than a diary of a landscape's changing mind.

"The eastern ridge is gone," they said. "It was there this morning."

"Dreams don't keep what nobody's looking at." Theo sat cross-legged near his neat fire, his sword across his knees in a way that managed to look apologetic rather than martial. "The realm forgets itself when it thinks it's alone."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard about geography."

"It's the truest thing I've ever heard about people, too." He said it lightly, but the lightness had a crack in it, and through the crack Rosalind could see the shape of everything he wasn't saying. They had been traveling together long enough now — through doors, across the pooling magic of the mirrored capital, past checkpoints where forged papers had breathed for them — to know that Theo Aldery carried his silences like sealed letters, addressed and stamped and never sent.

The sky finished dimming. The stars came out wrong, constellations rearranging themselves between glances, and Rosalind decided not to chart them. Some nights a cartographer was allowed to simply look up.

* * *

The first watch passed in comfortable silence, or what passed for comfortable in a place where the silence occasionally cleared its throat. Rosalind took the first turn awake while Theo slept — actually slept, deeply, his face slack in a way that made him look years younger and much more breakable. Watching him sleep felt like reading a page someone had left open, and Rosalind looked away from it, mostly.

When the watch changed, Theo woke without groaning, which struck Rosalind as characteristic. He fed his fire, checked the horizon out of habit even though nothing in the borderlands needed guarding except against forgetting, and then said, unexpectedly:

"I should tell you about Wren."

Rosalind kept their eyes on the map. "You don't owe me confessions."

"I know. But we're going to walk into the heart of this realm tomorrow, or the day after, and if I'm going to trust you with my life I'd rather you knew why you might not want to trust me with yours."

The grass whispered. The wrong stars wheeled. Rosalind folded the map, slowly, buying time with small precise movements the way they always did.

"Wren Callis," Theo said. "Academy year above mine. Gifted — she could make ink remember what it had written before, so a copied letter would carry echoes of the original, every hand that held it, every room it had rested in. She used to demonstrate it at festivals. Write a line, burn the paper, and the copy would still smell faintly of smoke. Everyone loved her. She was the sort of person who made giftedness look like generosity instead of danger."

"You reported her." Rosalind's voice came out flat. It wasn't a question. This was the story they had pieced together from fragments across weeks — the reason a royal inspector's badge hung on a man who flinched around inspectors, the debt he kept trying to pay in truth.

"She asked me to hide her register exemption papers. I was a third-year scribe. My father had just lost his position over — it doesn't matter, debts, the usual rot. I was terrified of becoming exactly what my father became. And the proctor offered me a clean ledger and a recommendation in exchange for names." His hands were very still around the stick he was using to stir the fire, as though stillness could hold him together at the seams. "I gave her name. I told myself it would just mean registration. A stamp, a fee, a life slightly smaller than before. Three days later she was taken to the capital for 'assessment,' and I never saw her again. The official record says she was released to family in the provinces. I've spent four years checking provincial records. There is no family. There are no records. There's nothing."

The fire popped. In the borderlands even fire sounded uncertain of itself.

"So when the inspectors came to your guildhall," Theo said, "and I saw forged papers in the hands of a cartographer whose real work I'd admired from a distance for ten years — I recognized the moment. I was standing inside my own worst hour, holding it again, and this time I got to choose differently." He finally looked at Rosalind directly, and his eyes in the firelight were the color of strong tea, ordinary and warm and wrecked. "I didn't pass your papers because I suspected the king was bound. I passed them because I couldn't stand to be the reason another name vanished. Everything else — the sigil, the usurper, all of it — I found after. But that's the truth underneath the mission, and you deserve it without varnish. I am a man who betrayed someone once, and I have been trying to become someone else ever since, and I don't know yet whether I've succeeded."

Rosalind let the quiet sit. Around them the borderlands listened — he was right about that; the realm forgot itself when unobserved, which meant it also remembered, keenly, whatever happened in front of witnesses. Whatever Theo confessed here would stay confessed. The ground itself would hold it.

"You checked provincial records," Rosalind said at last. "For four years."

"Every archive that would let me in. Some that didn't."

"And did you find anything?"

"One thing." His jaw worked. "A shipping ledger, out of Harrowmouth. A manifest of persons transported north, unlisted cargo class. Her initials were in the margin in a hand I recognized. It's how I learned the crown doesn't release the gifted to provinces. It moves them. North, toward the old war-fortresses. Toward—" He stopped. "Toward places that may matter to what we're doing. Which is why I'm telling you now, and not later. If Wren is alive, she's part of the map we haven't drawn yet."

Rosalind felt something shift in their chest, a plate of armor loosening by one rivet. They had prepared, over these weeks, for Theo to be many things: liar, spy, fool, martyr. They had not prepared for him to be a man keeping a vigil over a missing person, armed with initials in a ledger margin.

"Thank you," they said, and found the words cost more than expected. "For the unvarnished version."

"It's the only version worth carrying."

* * *

The realm's false night deepened. The singing thing in the distance changed its tune to something older, lullaby-shaped. Rosalind took the second watch, then, when neither of them could plausibly pretend to sleep, gave up on watches entirely, and the two of them ended up sitting between the fires — the space where both lights overlapped, warm from either side.

"My turn, I suppose," Rosalind said.

"You don't—"

"No. You're right that we should trade. Fair's fair, and besides—" They smiled, thin and self-aware. "I've spent thirty years being unremarkable on purpose. It turns out that once you start, you never stop practicing the speech where you explain yourself. I've just never given it."

Theo waited. He was good at waiting; scribes were trained to let documents speak at their own pace.

"I had a family once," Rosalind said. "Not born-family. Chosen. A mother and father and a sister in all the ways that counted — Marisol, who taught me to draw; her wife, Petra, who taught me that maps are arguments about what matters; and their daughter, Sefa, who was six and believed our house floated on the back of a sleeping turtle, and I never corrected her because I'd seen the turtle. I'd *mapped* the turtle. Underneath the harbor, miles wide, breathing once a century."

The firelight moved on Theo's face. He didn't interrupt.

"When the first purges came — before this purge, the smaller one, the rehearsal — gifted families in the harbor districts were registered first. Marisol drew maps that came true. Petra read the weather in salt. Sefa was six and nobody knew what she was yet, except maybe the turtle." Rosalind's hands had started moving without permission, sketching in the air, tracing coastlines of a place that no longer appeared on any living chart. "I was twenty-two and I had already learned the trick of being dull. Nobody came for me. Nobody came for any of us, at first, because I had done my job so well. I had mapped our neighborhood as unremarkable. Flood-prone, tax-deliquent, dull. The census clerks used my charts. I handed them the erasure myself."

"You were protecting them," Theo said quietly.

"I was protecting them *and* myself, and I told myself those were the same thing. And then the second wave came, and dullness stopped mattering, and they were taken anyway — and do you know what I did?" Rosalind laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I erased them harder. I redrew the harbor district. I removed the house. Removed the street. I mapped it as open water. Because if the house didn't exist on any official chart, then the registers couldn't connect me to the people who'd been taken, and I would live. And it worked. It worked perfectly. I have been living inside that blank water ever since. There is a hole in every map I own, Theo, and I made it, and I keep it maintained, and sometimes at night I take it out and look at it the way you'd look at a scar."

The borderlands had gone very still around them. Even the grass had stopped whispering, as though the realm understood that this was a confession it ought to preserve.

"Sefa would be fourteen now," Rosalind said. "If turtles remember the people who sleep on their backs, then somewhere under the harbor there's a mile-wide creature that remembers a girl who brought it bread crusts. And I chose a blank space over finding out. For nine years. Until you walked into the guildhall with your inspector's face and your guilty eyes, and I realized I'd gotten so good at hiding other people that I'd hidden myself too, and there was nobody left who knew my name was worth saying aloud."

"It's worth saying," Theo said.

Rosalind looked at him.

"Rosalind Voss," he said, deliberately, like setting a seal. "Keeper of ledgers. Drawer of realms. The person who argued the guild shouldn't scatter, who voted last and voted yes, who walks into a dream with ink on their fingers and comes back with maps that argue back. I know your name. I've known it since the academy — you won the Meridian Prize the year Wren demonstrated her ink. I was in the audience. I remember thinking the winner never looked up from their own boots, and wondering what someone that talented was so busy hiding from."

The words landed somewhere deep, in the blank-water country, and Rosalind found that their eyes were burning and did not fight it. In the borderlands, tears behaved strangely — they fell upward for a moment before deciding on down — and Rosalind let them, watching grief and relief share the same salt.

"I erased them to survive," they said. "And surviving turned out to be the same as disappearing."

"Not anymore." Theo shifted closer — not touching, but near enough that the warmth of him joined the warmth of the fires. "You know what I keep thinking? The sigil feeds on identity. That's why it holds the king — it eats the shape of him and hands the shape to someone else. And tomorrow we go in to break it. And the only tools that work against a thing like that are the ones we just traded. Names. Histories. People witnessed, out loud, by someone who'll carry the memory home." He almost smiled. "The usurper built his whole power on making everyone invisible to themselves. We're sitting in the ruins of invisibility right now, Rosalind. Both fires lit. Fully seen. Let him come."

Rosalind laughed — really laughed this time, wet and surprised — and reached out and took his hand. His fingers were ink-stained at the edges, calloused from quill and hilt, and they closed around theirs like an answer to a question Rosalind had stopped asking years ago.

They sat like that as the false night wore toward its false dawn, trading smaller truths after the large ones — favorite foods, childhood terrors, the exact sound the academy bells made in winter. The borderlands leaned in and memorized all of it.

And then, as the violet sky began its slow brightening, the singing in the distance stopped.

Both of them went rigid. Rosalind snatched up the map, and the ink was moving fast now, all of it, rivers and ridges and the ghost of the vanished eastern ridge crawling toward one point on the parchment like iron filings toward a lodestone. At the heart of the dream-realm, the lines were gathering. Something had noticed the fires. Something had noticed *them* — two fully-seen people burning bright in a land built on forgetting.

"What is it?" Theo breathed, hand on his sword.

Rosalind watched the ink settle into a shape they recognized from their deepest researches, and their mouth went dry.

"The road to the king," they said. "It just opened. And it opened *because* of what we said tonight — the realm heard us, and it wants more."
