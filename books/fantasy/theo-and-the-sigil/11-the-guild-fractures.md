## 11. The Guild Fractures

The trouble began, as most troubles do in the Guild of Impossible Things, with something small and domestic: a pot of tea gone cold on the ledger table, because no one had lit the brazier beneath it.

Rosalind noticed first. They always noticed first—it was the curse of the person who kept the books. A cold teapot meant that Wren had forgotten her evening rounds. Wren never forgot her evening rounds; she was a woman who salted doorways against scrying the way other people flossed their teeth, meticulous, unglamorous, utterly reliable. She had been the guild's warden of thresholds for eleven years.

The second sign came an hour later, when the bottled storm on the third shelf—the one they'd christened *Temperance* after it had nearly drowned a bishop in 914—began to rattle against its cork. Bottled weather did not rattle without reason. Storms were barometers of feeling, and somewhere nearby, someone's feelings had turned to lightning.

By midnight, Rosalind was awake in their narrow room above the map-room, listening to the safehouse breathe, and hating themselves for what they were hearing.

Nothing. The house was breathing wrong. It was too quiet, the way a forest goes quiet before wolves.

They dressed by feel, took down the ledger of current members—the slim volume, not the great impossible tome downstairs—and counted names twice, because the arithmetic refused to be believed the first time.

Maren Ostrey had not come home.

* * *

Maren Ostrey. Rosalind turned the name over in the dark like a coin they suspected was clipped. She was the guild's forger of transit papers, a broad-shouldered woman with ink-stained knuckles and a laugh like a cart over cobblestones. She'd joined seven years ago, after the purge of the river-towns, carrying nothing but her tools and her daughter, Sefa—a girl of nine then, quiet as a held breath, who could make flowers open out of season and who had been registered with the crown at birth because her gift showed too early to hide.

That was the thing Rosalind kept circling back to, pacing the cold length of the map-room while the guild slept around them: Sefa was *registered*. Had been since she was small enough to hold in one arm. Her name sat in a royal book in the capital, in a hand Rosalind could probably have identified if they tried, marked with the little stamped rose that said *known gifted, minor, ward of state*. Every year on her birthday, a crown inspector came to the dye-shop where Maren kept her cover trade, and looked at the girl, and wrote in his own ledger that she remained compliant.

And every year, Maren paid him. Not in coin—in favors. Small ones. A forged passage here, a doctored census page there. Rosalind had known about it for four years and had entered it in the ledger under *standing obligations*, because that was what ledgers were for: not judgment, only record. Theo had argued once, early on, that it was a leash waiting to be pulled. Rosalind had argued back that everyone in the guild was leashed to something, and the honest ones wore theirs visibly.

Now the tea was cold, the storm was rattling, and Maren Ostrey had missed her rounds for the first time in eleven years.

Rosalind stood at the window overlooking the tannery lane and watched the first grey light come up over the roofs of Little Corvess, and understood—with the terrible clarity that only comes when you have already known a thing for hours and merely permitted yourself to say it—that the leash had been pulled.

* * *

"We don't know that," Wren said. She had her arms folded and her jaw set, and she was standing between Rosalind and the door to the tunnel network as though her body alone could keep the truth out. "One missed night. The woman has a daughter. Perhaps the girl took sick. Perhaps—"

"Perhaps," Rosalind agreed. "Check the drop-box anyway."

The drop-box was a dead-letter cache in the wall of the chandler's on Bellows Street, where messages from Maren's outside contacts appeared and vanished. Wren went, because Wren would rather walk into a fire than admit she might be needed elsewhere, and came back forty minutes later white as tallow, holding a single sheet of good rag paper.

It was written in inspector's cipher—the crown's, the real crown's, the usurper's—which Theo could read, because Theo had been trained in it before he'd ever heard of a guild of impossible things. He read it aloud in the map-room, his voice flat as a pressed flower:

*"Route confirmed. Safehouse at the old tannery, Little Corvess, entry via dye-shop cellar. Eleven residents plus irregulars. The woman requests extraction of the child Sefa Ostrey prior to action, per agreement. Payment accepted. Action within three days."*

No signature. There didn't need to be one. The paper itself was crown stock; Rosalind knew its watermark the way they knew their own heartbeat.

Nobody spoke. The bottled storms on the shelves had all gone still, which was somehow worse than rattling—weather holding its breath.

"She wouldn't," said Perrin the door-smith, whose hands were shaking so badly he'd given up pretending to oil the lock he was holding. "Not Maren. She carried Sefa through the river purge with fever, she buried—" He stopped. Everyone in the room knew what he'd stopped at: she'd buried a husband on that road. She'd earned her caution in blood.

"She would," Rosalind said quietly, "if they told her the alternative was watching Sefa burn."

Because that was how it worked now. The hunt had stopped being patient. Registration had curdled into conscription or execution, and the inspectors had learned the guild's oldest lesson—that the surest way into a locked heart is through someone you love more than yourself. They hadn't needed to torture Maren. They had only needed to put a warrant on the table next to her daughter's registration page and let her do the arithmetic herself.

Eleven lives against one child. Rosalind could not even swear, honestly, in the privacy of their own skull, that they would have chosen differently. That was the part that made their hands cold. Not anger. Arithmetic.

Theo was staring at the letter as if he could burn holes in it. "Three days," he said. "We can be gone in three days. We take everything portable, collapse the tunnels behind us, and scatter to the secondary sites. She sold them this house, not us. We are not obliged to be home when they call."

"And leave the house wired with everything we couldn't carry?" Perrin said. "Half the archive is bound into the walls. You know that. The doors alone—"

"The doors go with us or they go dark. We've drilled this." Theo's voice was steady, scribe-steady, the voice he used for reading death warrants aloud. Rosalind watched him and thought: *he has done this before, stood in a room full of doomed people and made the numbers behave.* And thought, underneath, less charitably: *and the last time he made the numbers behave, someone I loved went to the pyre.* They pushed the thought down. It wasn't fair. It was also true, and both things could live in a person at once; that was practically the guild's founding principle.

"It's not a drill," Wren said. "It's a rout. There's a difference in the walking away."

"There is," Rosalind said. "But we're going to walk anyway."

They gave the order because no one else would, and because the ledger-keeper's whole life had been training for exactly this: knowing which things must be saved first when the house burns. Names. Then maps. Then doors. Then everything else.

* * *

They had two days, and they spent them the way drowning people spend air.

Rosalind worked the archive with ink to the elbows, triaging eleven years of accumulated impossibility. The bottled storms went into padded crates, sulking. The ledger of members went into the false bottom of a tea chest that would travel with Wren, who swore on three dead gods she'd die before letting it be read. The great book—the Ledger of Impossible Things itself, the founding tome with its doors drawn on vellum and its storms catalogued in weather that hadn't happened yet—was too large to move and too dangerous to abandon, so Rosalind did the thing they had sworn never to do and began cutting it apart, page by page, separating the leaves with a bone folder and their own held breath, distributing the sections among six couriers like a psalm divided among singers.

"You're mutilating it," Perrin said from the doorway, stricken.

"I'm evacuating it. There's a difference in the walking away." They didn't look up. Their throat was tight. The great book had been the closest thing Rosalind had ever had to a constant companion; they had read it to sleep as a runaway apprentice and entered their first impossible thing into it at nineteen. Now they were feeding it to the wind and hoping the wind would bring it back whole.

On the second night, Maren came back.

She came through the dye-shop cellar door at moonrise, alone, smelling of road dust and lamp oil, and she stood in the middle of the half-gutted map-room with her hat in her hands like a penitent, and looked at the empty shelves, and the bare walls, and the pale rectangles on the plaster where the maps had hung, and said, to no one and everyone:

"You knew."

"We guessed," Rosalind said. "Last night. Tonight we knew."

Maren nodded slowly, the way people nod when a blow they braced for lands anyway. "I told myself you'd have time. Three days. I told myself three days was enough, that you lot could vanish faster than smoke, you're the Guild of Impossible Things, vanishing is your whole—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself. "I need you to understand. They had her file on the table. They had the execution order *pre-written*, Rosalind. All it needed was my refusal. One word from me and my daughter becomes ash and an example. One word from me and she lives to be ten."

"I know," Rosalind said. "I've done the sum myself. I'm not here to judge you."

"Aren't you?" Maren laughed, and it was an awful sound, a laugh with broken glass in it. "You're the ledger-keeper. Judging is what you do. Quietly, in ink, forever."

"No," Rosalind said. "Recording is what I do. And I will record this, Maren, because that's my work—but hear what I'll write: *she traded eleven shadows for one living child, and any of us would have signed the same paper with our own hand shaking.* That's the entry. That's all of it."

Maren stared at them. Something in her face came apart—not the guilt, that would stay, guilt always stays, but the terror of being seen, the flinch she'd been carrying since the cellar door. "Why aren't you angrier?"

"Ask me again when we're all alive," Rosalind said. "Or don't. But right now I need your last service, and I need you to give it clean."

"What service? Haven't I given enough?"

"You're going to tell them the house is full," Rosalind said. "When they come tomorrow—you'll be here, because they'll expect you here, because you're their instrument now whether you like it or not. And you're going to tell them we stayed. Eleven residents, plus irregulars, archives intact. You're going to sell them a full house."

Understanding moved across Maren's face like cloud-shadow. "So they come in force. So they commit."

"So they come expecting prey," Rosalind said, "and find a hollow shell rigged with every surprise Perrin ever nailed into a wall, and waste a night, and a company, and their confidence. And by the time they understand they've been sold an empty purse, the true purse is two cities away." Rosalind held her gaze. "You get your daughter either way. They extract Sefa before the raid, per your agreement. You stand in that cellar and watch them kick in our doors and you say nothing and you grieve later, in private, for as long as it takes. Can you do that?"

Maren's eyes were wet and furious. "Can you?" she said. "Watch them take the house?"

"No," Rosalind admitted. "But I won't be here to watch. That's the whole point of the exercise."

* * *

They left before dawn on the third day, in six silent streams through six hidden ways, and Rosalind went last, because ledger-keepers balance the books on the way out the door.

The safehouse had been their home for nine years. Walking its final corridor, Rosalind touched the wall where the great book had lived and felt the absence like a missing tooth. In the tunnel mouth, Perrin had left his masterpiece: the threshold rigged to seal itself into solid stone the moment the last foot crossed, so that the raiders would find not even the memory of a passage. Rosalind paused at the line where stone would become stone, and looked back once at the lamplit map-room, at the ghost-rectangles on the walls, at the cold teapot still sitting on the ledger table because some habits outlast their owners.

Then they stepped through, and the way closed behind them like water closing over a dropped stone.

* * *

They should have been two cities away by nightfall. They were barely one.

The roads out of Little Corvess had sprouted checkpoints like mushrooms after rain—crown soldiers, crown lanterns, crown questions—and the guild's couriers, laden with pages of the dismantled great book disguised as everything from hymnals to laundry lists, had to move slow and singly, which meant Rosalind spent that first fugitive night in a wool-merchant's attic in the town of Harrow Fen, listening to Wren breathe beside them and counting the hours until the raid they could not witness.

It came, they learned later, at moonset. Two companies. Ram and torch. Maren standing in the dye-shop cellar as promised, pointing them down, selling them an empty purse with a straight face and a breaking heart. Perrin's surprises doing their work—the collapsing stair, the alchemical dark, the door that bit. No captures in Little Corvess. On that count, the plan had held.

But the crown had not bought the empty purse after all. They had sent riders to the secondary sites. To Harrow Fen. To the chandler's on Bellows Street. To every place Maren had ever known them to touch, because a woman who sells one address will sell the atlas, and the inspectors knew it better than Rosalind did—they had built her leash for precisely this.

Perrin was taken at the chandler's, defending a crate of bottled weather with a smith's hammer. Old Cass, who kept the doors to elsewhere, was taken on the Harrow road with three sections of the great book in her saddlebags. Half the guild, scattered across four towns, gathered up like spilled beads in a single night, because trust—Rosalind lay awake in the attic dark and made themselves say the word plainly—trust was not a wall. It was a debt, paid forward in advance, with no guarantee the person you trusted it to would not spend it elsewhere. Maren had not betrayed them out of malice. She had betrayed them out of love, which was worse, because love could be leveraged and malice could only be waited out.

In the grey hour before dawn, Wren stirred beside them and whispered, "Where do we go now? Every route we had is in their hands."

"Not every route," Rosalind said. Because there was one map that existed nowhere in ink, nowhere in any cache or courier's pack—one map drawn only in the space behind their eyes, of a realm the crown could not raid because the crown could not dream their way into it. The dreamscape. The king's country. The road they had opened once and sworn never to open again, because it had begun drawing them in return.

Wren heard the silence stretch and understood, and whispered something that might have been a prayer or a protest.

Rosalind got up, found the merchant's stub of pencil, and on the back of a laundry list began to draw the borderlands of sleep from memory—line by line, gate by gate, the geography only they could chart. Outside, the fen fog pressed against the shutters like a hand. Somewhere south, armies were marching on the free cities, wearing a stolen king's colors.

If the crown owned every road, Rosalind would build them a new one—out of the one material no inspector could confiscate.

They just hoped that when they opened that door again, it would let them choose which side of it to stand on.
