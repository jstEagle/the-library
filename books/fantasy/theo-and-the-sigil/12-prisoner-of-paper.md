## 12. Prisoner of Paper

The cell smelled of wet stone and old ink.

Rosalind noticed the ink first. That was, perhaps, a professional failing—lying face-down on a floor that stank of rot while their wrists were bound behind them with cord rough enough to flay skin, and still their mind snagged on the smell, because someone in this fortress had been grinding oak gall not three days past, and the tannin had seeped into the mortar like a stain into vellum. Ink lasted. Ink outlasted the hand that made it, outlasted the words it carried, sometimes outlasted the language itself. Rosalind had built a life on that truth and now, apparently, they would die inside it.

They catalogued the room instead of panicking. It was a discipline older than fear: when the world went wrong, map it. Four walls, granite blocks dressed smooth at the top where hands had worn them for generations. One door, iron-banded, no window. A slot at ankle height where food might be passed—or smoke. A single torch outside the door, its light bleeding under the frame in a thin amber line that shifted when a guard shifted his weight. Two guards, then, maybe three. Boots with hobnails; one man favored his left heel. The drip came from the northeast corner, which meant water behind the wall, which meant this level sat below the cisterns, which meant—

Which meant they were beneath the citadel of Vhal Marenne, three days' hard ride from where the guild's safehouse had burned, and the map Rosalind had been carrying when the inspectors took them was currently folded against their ribs, sewn into the false seam of their coat, where no searcher had thought to look because no searcher ever looked at a cartographer's plain brown coat.

Rosalind breathed slowly through the pain in their shoulders and allowed themselves one small, bitter laugh.

Invisibility, it turned out, was not the same as safety. They had spent thirty-one years learning that lesson and had needed only six hours in a holding wagon to finally understand it.

* * *

They had been taken at the ferry crossing at Sorrel Ford, of all places—a crossing so dull it appeared on no map Rosalind had ever drawn, precisely because appearing on maps was how places got soldiers. The trap had been elegant in its mundanity: a toll, a queue, a bored inspector with a ledger checking names against the crown's register of the gifted. Rosalind had stood in that queue with forged papers identifying them as *Marla Fenwick, itinerant surveyor*, papers good enough to have survived four checkpoints already, and everything might have held if the inspector hadn't asked them to sketch the ford from memory. A test, he'd said. For surveyors. The crown liked to verify credentials.

So Rosalind had knelt in the mud with a borrowed stylus and drawn the Sorrel Ford—the wrong Sorrel Ford, the one that existed nowhere, with an extra channel and a mill that wasn't there—and the inspector had studied it for a long time with an expression Rosalind couldn't read, and then he had said, quietly, "Bind her," to the soldiers, and everything after that was burlap over the head and the jolt of a cart and the long dark ride during which Rosalind counted turnings and timed distances by the ache in their knees and built, piece by piece, a map of the road they traveled in the dark.

That was the thing about being unremarkable. Nobody bothered to blindfold you properly. Nobody bothered to stop talking near you. In the yard at Vhal Marenne, waiting to be processed, Rosalind had heard two quartermasters complain that the usurper's army was stripping the eastern granaries to feed a host moving south—*south*, toward the free cities, faster than anyone had estimated—and they had heard a clerk complain that the interrogators' wing was short of lamp oil because "the witch-cells burn through it," and they had filed both facts away the way they filed away everything: carefully, without expression, in the vast dim warehouse of a mind trained never to look interesting.

Now, alone in the cell, Rosalind unfolded that mental map and examined it. The cisterns above. The granary complaints meaning supply lines stretched thin. The lamp oil meaning the cells below were used often, which meant the interrogators were experienced, which meant the next few hours would hurt.

Fear arrived, as it always did, like weather—pressure first, then wind, then rain. Rosalind let it come. Then, methodically, they began to work.

Because here was the problem, and it was a beautiful, terrible problem: the map sewn into their coat was real. Not the true map—not the dream-realm chart, the corrected routes to where the king slept tangled in golden thread—but real nonetheless. It showed the guild's hidden ways, the doors to elsewhere, the paths ahead of the armies. Half the surviving guild's escape routes were inked on that sheet. If it fell into crown hands, people Rosalind loved would die with military precision.

And the inspectors knew exactly what they'd caught. Not Marla Fenwick, surveyor. A cartographer who drew impossible roads. The interrogator would want that map the way a starving man wants bread, and he would assume—because clever men always assumed their opponents were clever in the same directions—that Rosalind would have hidden it somewhere physical. Seams. Boot heels. Swallowed, God help them. He would search the body thoroughly and find nothing, and then he would do what experienced interrogators did when the body yielded nothing.

He would ask questions. And Rosalind could not survive questions about the true map, because there were answers they could not give even under pain—they had promised the guild that much, had promised Theo, had promised themselves—and so the only way to protect the truth was to make sure the interrogator never ran out of lies to find first.

You didn't hide a treasure by burying it deeper. You hid it by planting a hundred false treasures around it and letting the diggers exhaust themselves on fool's gold.

Rosalind flexed their numb fingers and began, silently, to compose a forgery.

* * *

They came for Rosalind at what felt like midmorning, though without windows the hour was a guess. The door opened onto a corridor of the same wet granite, and two soldiers walked them up three flights of stairs to a room that was trying very hard to be an office and mostly succeeding: a desk, a lamp, ledgers stacked in ranks, a wall map of the southern provinces pinned up with visible corrections—someone had redrawn the coastline twice and neither attempt matched reality. Rosalind looked at that map and felt something almost like pity.

The interrogator was a spare, grey man called Master Corvel, introduced by a soldier with the flat efficiency of routine. He had ink-stained fingers and the patient eyes of someone who had learned to enjoy waiting. He gestured Rosalind into a chair without ropes, which was itself a message: we don't think you'll run, or we don't care if you do.

"Marla Fenwick," Corvel said, opening a folder. "Itinerant surveyor. Registered at—" he paused delicately, "—four separate checkpoints, four separate districts, with four documents bearing four different seals." He looked up. "You've been busy."

"Work is scarce," Rosalind said. Their voice came out steadier than they'd expected. Dull. Carefully dull. "Forgers charge less than hangmen."

Something flickered at the corner of Corvel's mouth. Not quite amusement. Appreciation, maybe, of the shape of the lie even while disbelieving it. "At the ford you drew a map that does not correspond to any surveyed crossing in the province. Extra channel. A mill." He turned the sketch around so Rosalind faced it. "Yet your hand is excellent. Trained. Academy-trained, I'd wager, from the letterforms in your annotations." He tapped the paper. "A woman who can draw a river that doesn't exist, perfectly, on command. Do you know what we call that talent, in my profession?"

Rosalind said nothing.

"Cartomancy," Corvel said softly. "Map-gift. Rarer than fire-telling, rarer than storm-binding, and worth ten of either, because a gifted mapmaker doesn't just record the world." His eyes were very still. "She makes it true. Draw a bridge on a river and gifted hands can raise it. Draw a road through mountains, and roads get found. So." He folded his hands. "Tell me about the roads you've found, Mistress Fenwick."

There it was. The trap laid open on the desk between them like a dissected animal. He believed—or wanted to believe—that Rosalind could draw things into being, and every instinct in Rosalind screamed to deny it, deny everything, be small, be dull, be *Marla Fenwick, tired surveyor, knows nothing*. That had always worked. That had been the whole architecture of their life.

But denial would send him digging. Digging meant the coat. The coat meant the real map. And the real map meant the doors, the routes, the scattered survivors of the guild—all of it delivered into the hands of the machine that had burned Ashes-on-the-Register's friend and hollowed out the king.

No. Denial was shelter, and shelters burned.

Rosalind lifted their chin and let something shift in their face—a loosening, a weariness that looked like surrender. "If I tell you," they said, "will you let me keep my hands?"

Corvel tilted his head. "Hands are negotiable."

"Then listen carefully." Rosalind leaned forward, and poured out the forgery they had spent all night composing in the dark.

They told him about a sigil-workshop hidden in the chalk caves beneath the Grey Downs—false, invented that morning, but built on real geology so it would hold water. They told him about a master cartographer called Odile Vasse—dead eleven years, untraceable—who had taught them to draw realms true. They told him about a working, half-finished, a great binding-diagram in progress, inked on treated calfskin, that could supposedly anchor or sever any sigil within a league of its drawing. And they told him where it was: sealed in a lead case, cached in the flooded crypt of the old monastery at Saint Aldhelm's Step, because lead defeated scrying and monks had understood hiding better than kings ever would.

None of it existed. All of it was verifiable-shaped. The chalk caves were real caves. Saint Aldhelm's Step was a real ruin, remote enough to cost the crown a week's expedition, far enough from every guild route that even a thorough search would find nothing but floodwater and old bones. And threaded through the whole confession were details too specific to be doubted and too trivial to matter: the smell of the treating-oil, the number of folds in the calfskin, the name of the dead woman's dog. Lies are believed not for their grandeur but for their lint.

Corvel wrote for a long time. When he finished, he looked up with those patient eyes and said, "And this diagram. This anchor-diagram. Could it be turned against the king?"

Ah. There it was—the real hunger underneath. Rosalind let their breath catch, just slightly, and said, "Against anything bound. Yes."

Something moved behind Corvel's face, quick and covetous, like a fish turning in dark water. Whoever this grey man served, he dreamed of holding a knife that could cut the throne's own magic—and Rosalind understood, with cold clarity, that she had just given him a reason to keep her alive, valuable, and pointed at a hole in the ground a hundred leagues from anything that mattered.

"You'll draw us a copy of the location," Corvel said. "Accurate. Complete."

"Give me paper," Rosalind said, "and I'll draw you the whole monastery down to the well-sweep."

He almost smiled. "You understand, Mistress Fenwick, that if this proves false—"

"Then you'll know where my hands stand," Rosalind said evenly, "and I'll know where yours don't. Send your expedition. I'll wait."

* * *

They gave her a cell with a desk. That was the astonishing thing—the machine's appetite outrunning its caution. Paper, ink, a stub of candle, a guard at the door, and Rosalind Voss sat down at last before a blank sheet and felt the old familiar tremor in the fingers, the one that preceded every true map.

But this one was a masterpiece of a different kind.

They drew Saint Aldhelm's Step with loving, meticulous accuracy—the drowned crypt, the collapsed stair, the well-sweep rotted to lace, the exact shelf where the imaginary lead case waited. Around it they wove the chalk caves, the approaches, the tide-tables, everything an expedition would need and everything that would waste it. And then, in the lower margin, in letters so small they might have been texture, Rosalink added the flourish that made the forgery sing: a surveyor's dedication line, *Drawn true by M. Fenwick*, with the date and a tiny compass rose whose north needle pointed not north but east-northeast—toward the free cities, toward the guild's scattering points. A message no inspector would ever read, in a code only three living people could crack. *I'm alive. I'm lying. Look for the needle.*

When it was done, Rosalind sat back and examined their own reflection, faint and warped in the ink pot's black surface.

All their life they had believed invisibility was a shell they lived inside—a smallness imposed, endured, mistaken for safety. Sitting in a witch-cell with forged freedom drying on the page, they finally saw it clearly: the dullness had never been a cage. It was training. Thirty-one years of being overlooked had taught them exactly what overlooking eyes wanted to see, and how to feed it, and how to build a fortress out of other people's certainty that you were nothing at all. The gift wasn't the drawing. The gift was the choosing of what got seen.

Somewhere above, a bell rang the changing of some watch, and boots passed in the corridor—Corvel's expedition already being provisioned, perhaps, marching toward a hole in the earth full of nothing.

Rosalind blew out the candle and, in the dark, permitted themselves the smallest smile.

Then the bolt scraped back, and the door swung wide, and the guard said in a voice that cracked strangely on the second word, "Fenwick. You've got a visitor," and stepping into the lamplight from the corridor, in the grey uniform of a royal inspector with his credentials still pinned to his breast, was Theo Aldery—alive, unburned, and looking at her with an expression Rosalind had never once seen on his careful face.

Terror.
