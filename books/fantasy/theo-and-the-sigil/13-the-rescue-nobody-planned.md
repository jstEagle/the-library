## 13. The Rescue Nobody Planned

Theo Aldery had spent his whole life learning that paper was the most dangerous material in the world.

Parchment could condemn a woman to the pyre with three lines of neat script. Vellum could raise an army or dissolve one, depending on whose seal it carried. A single forged signature had once ended a classmate named Wren Calloway, and Theo knew because his own hand had written the report that did it. He was twenty-six years old and he understood, better than any man alive, that ink outlived intention.

So when he stood in the corridor outside the interrogation cells beneath the Inspectorate's Hall, holding a document that would either save Rosalind Voss or end them both, he took no comfort in the fact that this time the ink told the truth.

The truth, after all, had never once protected anybody.

* * *

They were keeping her in Cell Nine.

He'd learned that from the duty roster, which he had rewritten himself two nights ago — a small, careful forgery, moving his own name onto the night inspection list, moving the night inspection to an hour when the corridors emptied. For eleven years he had used that same meticulous hand in service of a crown that wore a dead man's face, and now he used it for this: a cell block, a schedule, a girl made of maps.

Not a girl. He corrected himself as he checked the guard rotation a third time. A cartographer who had looked at him across an inspector's table with forged papers in front of her and dared him, silently, to see through them. And he had seen through them. That was the whole ruin of it. He had recognized her handwriting before he recognized her face — the way she crossed her sevens, the little hook she put on the descender of a *y*, habits he'd catalogued years ago at the academy when they were both nobody, both pretending at ordinariness, both lying about what they were in different directions.

She thought he'd turned her in. Everyone in that guild hall thought so. Let them. The misunderstanding had been the only armor he had, and armor only worked if people believed it.

Tonight he stopped wearing it.

Two figures waited for him in the coal cellar behind the Inspectorate's stables, exactly where his note had promised. He knew one of them by reputation alone: Marisol Quen, the guild's locksmith, a broad-shouldered woman with silver shot through her black braids and hands that could persuade any lock in the capital to reconsider its loyalties. The other was younger, wiry, a boy of perhaps nineteen with burn scars climbing his left forearm like ivy — Fen, the guild's door-opener, the one who carried keys that opened places keys shouldn't go.

They watched him come down the cellar stairs with the flat, unforgiving patience of people who had every reason to kill him.

"You're the scribe," Marisol said. Not a greeting.

"Theo Aldery."

"I know your name." She let it sit there between them like something unpleasant on the floor. "You wrote the report on Wren Calloway."

The cellar was cold enough that his breath showed. He didn't flinch from it. He had rehearsed this moment on the walk over, and every rehearsal ended the same way, because there was only one honest answer.

"Yes. I did. She was gifted and I was frightened and I wanted to be safe more than I wanted to be good, and I have regretted it every day since the day I did it, which is eleven years of regretting, and none of it helps her, and I am not here to be forgiven. I'm here because Rosalind is in Cell Nine and we have perhaps four hours before the transfer order moves her to the Citadel, where no one will ever get her back."

Fen shifted. "How do you know about the transfer?"

"Because I wrote the transfer order." He held up the satchel against his hip. "I also wrote the release chit that says Cell Nine's prisoner is being moved for questioning by a senior inspector. Me. The seal is real. The authority is real. Everything else is mine."

Marisol studied him for a long moment, her eyes the color of wet slate, and Theo had the distinct sensation of being read — not his papers, not his seals, but him, cover to binding.

"Why?" she said finally. "Truly. Not the speech. The reason."

And because she deserved the truth if anyone did, he gave it: "Because eleven years ago I chose survival over a person, and I have been dying slowly in a clean uniform ever since. Because the man on the throne is not the king, and I am one of four people alive who know it. Because Rosalind Voss looked at me across a table with a lie in her hands and I let her keep the lie, and I have been trying to find a way to give it back to her honestly ever since." He swallowed. "Because duty finally pointed somewhere worth walking."

Fen laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered. Marisol didn't laugh. But something in her shoulders came down half an inch, which from her, Theo suspected, was the equivalent of an embrace.

"The lock on Cell Nine is a Corvane triple-tumbler," she said. "State-of-the-art. It will take me six minutes."

"It will take you four," said Fen. "I've seen you do worse sober."

"And the guards?"

"There are two on the cell block tonight," Theo said. "One of them owes me his brother's pardon — I arranged the paperwork myself, and he knows it. He'll be asleep at his post, genuinely or otherwise. The other is Sergeant Hale, who is honest, diligent, and will absolutely raise the alarm." He paused. "Hale drinks. There's a cask of Vellish red in the guard room that arrived this afternoon under my authority, ostensibly confiscated contraband awaiting inventory."

"You're bribing an honest man into dishonesty," Marisol said.

"I'm giving an honest man plausible deniability. There's a difference, and the difference is that tomorrow he can swear he never left his post, and it will even be mostly true." Theo shouldered the satchel. "It isn't elegant. It isn't clever. It's a plan built out of paperwork and wine, and it will hold together for exactly as long as nothing goes wrong."

"And when something goes wrong?" Fen asked.

Theo looked at him. "Then we improvise, and I suspect improvising is a guild specialty."

Something almost like a smile crossed the young man's scarred face. "You might survive us yet, scribe."

* * *

The Inspectorate's Hall at midnight was a cathedral of bureaucracy, all vaulted stone and guttering lamps, its corridors lined with the framed registers of the condemned — thousands of names in beautiful script, each one a person reduced to a line and a date. Theo walked those corridors every day. Tonight, for the first time, he saw them as what they were: a wall of everything he'd helped build.

Marisol went first, moving through the dark like water finding its level. Fen kept to the shadows with a coiled length of pale cord over one shoulder — thread, Theo realized, dream-thread, salvaged from whatever remained of the guild's impossible stores. Neither of them asked why he was bringing it. Perhaps they assumed it was for the locks. It wasn't. He'd explain later, if there was a later.

Cell Nine smelled of damp stone and lamp oil. The corridor guard was where Theo had paid him to be — slumped, snoring gently, a cup tipped on its side beside him. Sergeant Hale was not at his post. Theo's stomach dropped, and then he heard it: laughter, faint and easy, drifting from the guard room down the passage, accompanied by the unmistakable gurgle of a tapped cask.

Plausible deniability. Mostly true. Go.

Marisol knelt at the lock and became, instantly, a different creature — stillness itself, her picks whispering inside the mechanism like insects in a wall. Fen pressed close to the door, listening. Theo stood watch at the corridor's mouth with a lantern shuttered to a coin-slit of light, counting his own heartbeats.

Four minutes. The lock gave a soft, satisfied click.

Inside, Rosalind Voss sat cross-legged on the cot in the grey light of a high slit window, and they did not look surprised to see him. That was somehow worse than screaming would have been. They simply looked at him — thin, bruised along one jaw, eyes steady and terribly clear — and said, quietly:

"How long have you known my handwriting?"

"Since the academy," Theo said. "You cross your sevens. You always did."

Something flickered across their face — not forgiveness, not yet, but the place where forgiveness might someday be built. "Eleven days in this cell," Rosalind said, "and I rewrote their phantom sigil map so many times I started dreaming in it. And the entire time, the man reading my lies at that desk—" They stopped. Started again. "You passed me. At the inspection. You looked straight at my forgery and called it genuine."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I already knew what you were," Theo said, "and I knew what happens to people whose names get written into certain kinds of ledgers. I couldn't undo Wren Calloway. I decided I'd spend whatever I had left making sure I never read another name into that book again." He held out his hand. "Come on. We have maybe ninety minutes before someone checks the wine."

Rosalind looked at his hand for a long moment. Then they took it. Their grip was cold from the cell, and fierce, and it did something to his chest that he had no ledger-entry for.

Marisol pressed a bundle into Rosalind's arms — a plain courier's coat, a cap, a travel pass. "Guild colors," she muttered. "Which is to say, no colors at all. You look forgettable again. Congratulations."

"It's the finest gift I've received all month," Rosalind said, and despite everything — despite the bruise and the cell and the eleven days — there was a ghost of their old dry humor in it, and Theo found himself smiling like a fool in a prison corridor.

That was, of course, when things went wrong.

* * *

The alarm came not as a bell but as a silence — the laughter from the guard room stopping all at once, followed by boots. Sergeant Hale emerged into the lamplight with his sword drawn and his face flushed, and behind him, unsteady but upright, the other guard, and behind *him*, from the stairwell, the heavy tread of men who had not been part of any roster Theo had forged.

"Hale," Theo said, stepping forward, putting himself between the sergeant and the others. "Sergeant. Stand down. This prisoner is remanded to my personal custody by—"

"I know your seal, Inspector," Hale said, and there was no malice in his voice at all, which made it infinitely worse. Only duty, dull and immovable as a millstone. "I drank your wine. I'm sorry for it. But the Underwarden got word an hour ago that a senior scribe named Aldery has been forging documents against the crown. Your arrest warrant came down while you were walking down here." He lifted a paper in his off-hand. Theo saw his own name on it, in someone else's competent script, and understood with terrible clarity that the usurper's inspectors had been watching him far longer than he'd imagined. "I liked you, sir. Genuinely. So I'll say it once: drop it, and come quiet."

Everything after that happened very fast.

Fen moved first — the boy was quick as a struck match, ducking low, going for Hale's knees with the coiled dream-thread flashing out to tangle a wrist. The second guard stumbled backward into the wall. Marisol pulled a slim blade from her boot and put herself between Rosalind and the stairwell, where the first of the Underwarden's men appeared, then the second, lantern-light sliding up their halberds.

Theo did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since the coal cellar. Four of them. Six coming down the stairs. One unlocked corridor and ninety seconds of head start, maybe less.

There was only one direction the numbers pointed.

"Go," he said, and drew his own sword — a scribe's sword, ceremonial, barely sharpened, and the absurdity of it nearly made him laugh. "East passage, past the record rooms, the grate over the drainage cut is already loose. Marisol knows the way. *Go.*"

"No," Rosalind said.

"Rosalind—"

"You are not doing the noble sacrifice thing in a *cellar corridor*, Theo, I refuse—"

The first halberd came down. Marisol caught it on her blade and screamed something profane and magnificent. Fen's thread whipped wide. And Theo stepped into the gap because somebody had to, because eleven years ago he had stepped *back* from exactly this kind of gap and a girl named Wren had burned for it, because duty had finally, finally pointed the same direction as everything else in him —

The blade meant for Rosalind found him instead.

It went in under his ribs, angled, ugly, and the world went white and then very quiet and very detailed. He heard Rosalind scream his name. He felt himself shoved aside — gentle hands, impossibly strong, the hands of someone who mapped realms — and then the corridor filled with chaos and lamplight and Marisol's blade work, and Theo sat down hard against the stone with his hand pressed over his own side and watched, dimly, as Rosalind Voss stopped being invisible.

Later, he would learn the details from Fen, who told it like a legend: how the cartographer had torn the forged map from their own coat and thrown it into the nearest lamp so the burning phantom-sigil flared up in the guards' faces; how Marisol dropped two men with the economy of a woman closing accounts; how Fen's dream-thread, thrown wild in the smoke, wrapped three halberds together into a single useless bundle. What Theo saw, from the floor, through a greying veil, was simpler and worse: Rosalind turning back for him when every sane instinct screamed to run, kneeling in the blood that was leaking out of him, pressing their palms to the wound and saying, over and over, in a voice stripped of all its irony:

"No. No no no — Theo, look at me, you don't get to do this, you don't get to *choose this now* —"

"Couldn't help it," he managed. His teeth were chattering. The stone was very cold and very comfortable. "Duty finally... points somewhere..."

"If you die making a joke I will bring you back specifically to be furious with you."

"That sounds... in character. For both of us."

Marisol hauled him upright, threw his arm across her shoulders, and Fen did the same on the other side, and between them they moved him — stumbling, dripping, alive — down the east passage while Rosalind walked backward ahead of them with a stolen lantern, watching every shadow, refusing to look away from him even to see where they were going.

* * *

The safehouse was a bindery above a shuttered print shop in the Tanners' Quarter, and by the time they laid him on the long worktable among the pots of paste and the stacks of blank folios, Theo had learned several things about his own body: that wounds under the ribs bleed a great deal and kill a great deal slower than they feel like they will; that Marisol Quen stitched a wound the way she picked a lock, with terrifying tenderness; that gin, administered by Fen, was medicine now whether he liked it or not.

Mostly he learned that Rosalind Voss did not leave his side.

They sat on a stool dragged up against the table, one hand resting on his forearm as though confirming he continued to exist, and they talked to him in a low stream — about the escape route Marisol was scouting, about the message they'd sent to what remained of the guild, about nothing, about everything — and Theo drifted in and out of the sound of their voice like a swimmer in warm shallows.

At some hour past dawn, when the stitching was done and the others slept in chairs around the cold stove, Rosalind leaned close and pressed their forehead against his temple. Their voice came very small.

"When you stepped in front of that blade," they said, "I understood something. All my life I survived by mattering to no one. And in that corridor, the worst moment of my life was also the first time I knew — *knew* — that I mattered to someone. You idiot. You brave, guilty, magnificent idiot. Don't you ever make me know it that way again."

"Next time," Theo whispered, "I'll write it down first. Properly filed."

A breath of a laugh against his hair, wet at the edges. "Stay awake. Talk to me. Tell me what we do now."

"Now," he said, and fumbled one-handed at his coat until Fen, waking, helped him draw out the folded page he'd carried since the coal cellar — the dream-thread coiled with it, pale as moonlight, salvaged from the ruins of the guild's stores. He pressed both into Rosalind's hands. "Now we finish it. The map. Where the king dreams. Because the Underwarden has my warrant and the usurper has my name, and there is no version of tomorrow in which I'm a royal inspector anymore." He smiled, and found that it didn't hurt, or that everything hurt equally, which amounted to the same thing. "I'm just a traitor with excellent penmanship. And you're the only cartographer alive who can chart a sleeping king. So we run east, ahead of the armies, and we follow your map to the throne room of threads — before they move him, before the binding deepens, before—"

He stopped. Rosalind had gone very still, staring not at him but past him, toward the window, where the first true light of morning was coming up grey over the rooftops of the Tanners' Quarter.

Beyond the glass, in the streets below, refugees were already moving — a river of them, silent and endless, streaming east with bundles on their backs, away from the smoke rising on the southern horizon where the free cities' beacon-fires had begun, one by one, to answer the usurper's advance.

"They're here," Rosalind said softly. "The armies. They're already at the gates."

Theo closed his hand over theirs, over the thread and the unfinished map, and held on.

"Then we'd better be faster than a war."
