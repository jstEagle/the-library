## 15. The First Unbinding Fails

The dream-realm's heart had a smell to it, Rosalind decided on the third day of preparation: warm wax and old paper and something underneath both, faintly metallic, like the air before lightning. They had been mapping the borderlands for two days now, sketching the approaches to the sleeping king's chamber in the careful shorthand of their trade—*narrow bridge, unstable; memory of a garden, hostile after dusk; thread of gold, do not touch without grounding*. Theo had copied each note into his scribe's hand, twice over, so that if one map burned the other might survive.

Now they stood at the threshold of the chamber itself.

It was not what Rosalind had expected. They had imagined something grander, perhaps—a vaulted hall of dream-stone, guards of woven nightmare. Instead it was small. Almost domestic. A bed like any bed, canopied in grey silk gone soft with age. A window that looked out onto a sky the color of unbleached linen. And in the bed, an old man, sleeping the way only the truly exhausted sleep: flat on his back, mouth slightly open, hands folded on his chest as though he'd been laid out by someone who loved him and wasn't sure when he'd wake.

Golden thread ran from his chest—six strands, no, seven, Rosalind counted twice—out through the window, up into that blank linen sky, taut as harp strings. Each strand hummed faintly when the wind of the dream moved it. Together they made a sound almost too low to hear, a chord held so long it had become silence.

"That's him," Theo said softly. He had come this far despite every argument Rosalind had made against it. "That's King Aldous. I served him for nine years. I know the shape of his hands." His voice caught. "He used to tap his ring against the table when he was thinking. Two taps, pause, three."

Rosalind watched the folded hands. Under the grey skin, one finger twitched—two beats, a hesitation, three. Their throat tightened unexpectedly. It was one thing to chart a realm. It was another to stand at the bedside of a man reduced to a single involuntary gesture, repeating himself to an empty room because it was the last piece of himself he still controlled.

"He's in there," Rosalind said. "Somewhere under all that thread."

"Can you cut it?"

"I don't know yet." Honesty felt dangerous here, where everything they said seemed to sink into the walls and stay. But lying in the dream-realm was worse than dangerous; Rosalind had learned that much already. Lies told here grew. You could watch them sprouting from the floorboards like mold. "I want to study the binding first. Properly. The way I'd study any map before I drew on it."

Theo nodded and stepped back to the doorway, taking up his post there like a soldier. He had insisted on coming inside the chamber, then insisted just as firmly that he would not approach the bed—that his presence near the king might be *known*, somehow, might alert whatever waited at the other end of those golden threads. Rosalind suspected he was right and also suspected he simply could not bear to be closer. Grief has its own cartography. Some distances you keep because crossing them would break you.

Rosalind knelt beside the bed and began to work.

*

The sigil was not drawn *on* the king. That was the first thing they understood, kneeling there in the hush. It was drawn *through* him—the way a needle is threaded through cloth, the way a river is threaded through a valley. The seven strands entered at the sternum and did not stop. Somewhere beneath the old man's ribs they braided together, and the braid went down and down, past the body entirely, into something deeper than flesh.

Rosalind opened their ledger-mind the way they always did, letting the map assemble itself line by line. Ink for ink. Thread for thread. This was their gift's oldest habit: to translate what they saw into what could be written, because what could be written could be understood, and what could be understood could, eventually, be changed.

But the sigil resisted translation.

Every time Rosalind tried to render a strand as a line on paper, the strand changed. It was gold when they looked at it directly; silver when they tried to measure it; black when they tried to name its origin. It moved the way water moves—not flowing, exactly, but *rearranging*, always rearranging, so that no fixed record of it stayed true for longer than a breath.

"It knows I'm writing it down," Rosalind murmured.

From the doorway, Theo's voice, tight: "Is that possible?"

"Everything here is possible. That's rather the problem." Rosalind sat back on their heels. Their knees ached; dream-bodies inherited the complaints of waking ones, or perhaps invented new ones out of spite. "A normal binding is a lock. You find the mechanism, you lift it. But this—" they gestured at the humming threads, at the whole patient architecture of the thing— "this isn't built like a lock. It's built like a *ledger*."

They hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words arrived already shaped, the way insights do in dreams, fully formed and slightly accusatory. And once said, they would not unsay themselves. The chamber heard them. The threads heard them. Rosalind felt the words settle into the room's fabric the way all words settled here, and understood, with a cold slide of certainty, that they had just told the sigil something true about itself.

Ledgers recorded identity. Names, debts, holdings, histories. Rosalind kept the Guild's books; they knew what a ledger was better than anyone alive. And this binding was keeping a ledger of Aldous Vane—every year of his reign, every decision, every promise, every grief—wound onto seven spools of gold thread. The usurper didn't wear the king's shape the way a mask sits on a face. He wore it the way a borrower wears a debt: drawing on the account, spending the king's self a little at a time, paying interest in captivity.

You couldn't cut a ledger. You could only *close* it.

Rosalind should have stopped there. Should have climbed out of the dream-realm, slept on it, returned with a plan measured twice and cut once. That was their discipline. That was the whole architecture of their survival: never act on the first reading; never trust the map until it has survived three revisions.

But the king tapped his finger. Two beats, pause, three. And something in Rosalind—something they had spent thirty years keeping locked in a drawer of dullness and careful anonymity—leaned toward that small stubborn gesture and refused to walk away.

"I'm going to try," they said.

"Try *what*?" Theo turned from the doorway, alarm in his face. "Rosalind, you said yourself we need to understand it first—"

"I understand enough." It was half true, which in the dream-realm was the most dangerous kind of truth. "One strand. Just one. If I sever the outermost thread—the oldest one, the first year of his reign—it should weaken the braid without collapsing it. Like cutting the top stitch of a hem to see how the seam runs."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'll learn I'm wrong very quickly, and we leave, and I revise the map." Rosalind heard how glib that sounded even as they said it, and hated themselves a little for it. Fear makes liars of careful people. They were afraid—not of the sigil, not yet, but of the alternative: climbing back out of this realm with nothing, reporting failure to a guild that had bet its existence on one vote, theirs. They had cast that vote. They had put every impossible person they loved on the line for this. Failure was not an abstraction. Failure had faces.

Theo took their hand. His grip was cold. "Promise me you'll stop the moment it feels wrong. Not the moment it *goes* wrong. The moment it feels wrong. There's a difference, and in this place the difference is everything."

"I promise."

He let go reluctantly, returning to the doorway, and Rosalind turned back to the bed, to the threads, to the work.

*

They chose their tools the way they chose everything: from the kit of their own nature. In the dream-realm, Rosalind's gift manifested as it always had—as ink. When they willed it, lines of dark light rose from their fingertips, obedient as thoughts, sharp as needles. They had drawn doors into being with these lines. They had mapped realms that maps were never meant to hold. Surely, surely, they could cut one thread.

The outermost strand lay across the king's left shoulder, oldest and thinnest, dulled with age to the color of honey. Rosalind grounded themselves first—anchored a line of ink to the chamber floor, to the doorway, to Theo's steady presence, a triple tether, redundancy upon redundancy. Then they reached out and laid the edge of an ink-line against the golden thread the way you'd lay a whetstone against a blade.

And pulled.

For one glorious instant, it worked. The strand parted with a sound like a struck bell, high and clean, and the whole chamber rang with it, and Rosalind felt the braid slacken somewhere deep beneath the king's ribs, and joy came up through them like water through a well shaft—

—and then the thread remembered itself.

It did not break. It *rewrote*. The severed ends reached for each other across the gap, found one another, and knit—except they did not knit back into gold. They knit into something else. Something that looked, with a horror Rosalind felt in their teeth, exactly like handwriting. Dark ink on pale vellum, looping and precise, and the hand was familiar because it was *theirs*, because the cutting line had been made of their gift and the wound had healed in the image of the blade.

The sigil was learning them.

"No," Rosalind said, and began to withdraw, and felt the tether-lines sing taut, and understood one heartbeat too late that the tautness was not the tethers holding them back.

It was the sigil reeling them in.

The strand that they had cut had not merely healed. It had *taken hold*. It wrapped once around their wrist, gentle as a measuring tape, and began to read. Rosalind felt it reading—felt it move along them like a fingertip down a page, and everywhere it touched, it lifted something. Not blood. Not pain. *Facts.*

Their name went first.

Not torn away—*transcribed*. Rosalind felt the syllables of it leave them the way heat leaves a cup of tea, smoothly, without violence, and appear somewhere else, written in gold somewhere along the strand, filed. *Rosalind.* Gone. They reached for it and found the space where it had been, smooth and empty as a page scraped clean with a knife. Panic arrived then, real panic, animal and total, the kind that does not negotiate. They grabbed for the tether-line anchored to the doorway and the line answered—but sluggishly, dimly, because the line was made of their gift and their gift was made of them and *they* were being unwritten, and what is a cartographer with no name? What is ink with no hand?

More followed. Their mother's name, which they had not spoken in eleven years, peeled off some inner shelf and flew to the thread. The name of the village they'd erased from their own maps. The date they'd walked out of the academy and sworn never to be known again. Each loss was painless, which was the worst of it—painless and tidy, an archivist's theft, the sigil filing them away with the same patience it had spent thirty years filing away a king.

Theo saw. From the doorway he saw Rosalind's face going strange, going *blank*, features remaining while something behind them emptied, and he did the thing Rosalind would spend the rest of their life being grateful for: he ignored every rule of caution in the dream-realm and crossed the chamber at a run.

He could not touch the thread—he tried, and it burned him, and he did not let go anyway, which told Rosalind something about Theo Aldery that no confession ever had. He wrapped both hands around the strand where it held their wrist and *pulled*, screaming words that Rosalind's emptying mind could not parse but their body understood as *stay, stay, stay.*

And the sigil paused.

Because the sigil kept a ledger, and ledgers have entries, and Theo Aldery was *in* this one. Nine years of service. A signature on a hundred documents. A betrayal reported in a clerk's neat hand. The thread knew him—knew his guilt, knew his name, knew his relationship to the sleeping king—and for one suspended moment it hesitated between accounts, uncertain whether Rosalind was prey or *correspondence*.

In that moment, Rosalind's tether-line snapped taut and true. Whether Theo's grip had bought the anchor time, or whether some part of Rosalind deeper than their name had remembered itself, they never learned. But the ink-line held, and Rosalind pulled against the pull, and the strand—stretched between the king's shoulder and their wrist, between two identities it was trying to reconcile—did the only thing a ledger cannot abide.

It tore.

The sound was enormous and wrong, a chord breaking mid-note, and the recoil threw Rosalind backward across the chamber into Theo's arms, and the world of the dream-realm shuddered like a table struck from below.

*

They woke on the borderlands, both of them, sprawled in the grass that grew in pale tufts from soil the color of ash. Rosalind's ears rang. Their hands shook. And inside their chest, where their name had lived for thirty years, there was a hollow.

Not empty. Not quite. More like a room after a burglary: the furniture still there, the shapes of things still present, but the valuable pieces gone, and everywhere the sense of having been handled by strangers.

"Rosalind." Theo was kneeling over them, hands hovering, afraid to touch. "Rosalind, look at me. Say something. Anything."

They opened their mouth to say *I'm here* and discovered, with a terror that made the sigil's theft feel gentle by comparison, that they weren't certain it was true. Who was speaking? What remained? They searched inward, frantically, the way you search a house whose locks have failed, cataloguing damage. Their gift—still there, dimmed, like a lamp turned low. Their memories—mostly there, though some had gone strangely thin, translucent, like paper held up to light. Their love for Theo—there, unmistakably, the one thing the thread had not managed to file, perhaps because it had never been written down anywhere, perhaps because it was too new to have an entry.

"Theo," they said, and the name worked, thank all the small gods of small people, the name worked. "I'm—I think I'm mostly here."

"*Mostly?*"

"There's a hole where my name goes." Rosalind sat up slowly. The borderland sky churned overhead, bruise-colored, agitated. The whole dream-realm had felt the tearing; they could see it in the weather, in the way the memory-gardens at the horizon had begun to lean like plants starved for sun. "It'll grow back. Names grow back. I think. I hope." They laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, ragged. "I've spent my whole life making sure no one knew my name. And the first time I offer it to something, it takes it and runs."

Theo sat down beside them in the ash-grass. For a long moment neither spoke. The ringing in Rosalind's ears faded by degrees, replaced by the vast quiet of the borderlands, and under the quiet, very far away, the seven-stranded chord resumed its endless held note—one voice short, now, and straining to compensate.

"We nearly died," Theo said finally. "You nearly—there may not be a word for what nearly happened to you."

"Unwritten," Rosalind offered. "There's a word. I just invented it, or it invented me, I honestly couldn't say."

He huffed something that wanted to be a laugh and couldn't manage it. His hands were blistered where the thread had burned him. Rosalind took one of them, carefully, and turned it over, and found their eyes stinging. "You shouldn't have touched it."

"You were leaving."

"I was being *filed*."

"Yes," Theo said quietly. "So I touched it."

Rosalind bowed their head over his burned hand. Humility, they were discovering, tasted like copper and ash-grass. All their life they had trusted in method—in revision, in caution, in the doctrine that any lock yields to sufficient understanding applied patiently. And the doctrine had not been wrong, exactly. It had been *incomplete*. Because they had approached the sigil as a cartographer approaches a coastline: something external, something to be surveyed, charted, and ultimately mastered. They had never once considered that the sigil might survey *back*. That a thing built to consume identity would regard a cartographer—who dealt in names, boundaries, definitions—as not an intruder but a *meal*. The richest kind. A person who had spent decades compressing themselves into anonymity, hoarding every unspoken truth, every erased relative, every abandoned name, until the whole compressed mass of them was dense as a star.

The binding hadn't beaten them. It had *outbid* them. It wanted what they had more than they wanted to keep it—or rather, it wanted it more than they had ever admitted wanting to *be* it.

"Some locks can't be forced," Rosalind said at last, to the grass, to Theo, to themselves. "I thought understanding was a key. It's not. Understanding is—" they groped for it, reaching into the hole where their name had been, finding the edges of it tender and raw and, impossibly, already beginning to granulate closed, like skin— "understanding is agreeing to be part of what you understand. That's why it rebound. I tried to stand outside the sigil and cut it. There is no outside. There never was."

Theo was silent for a long moment. Then: "The guild will want to call it off. After this. Half of them voted yes by a single mark, and that mark was yours, and you just—" he gestured helplessly at all of them, the shaking hands, the hollowed name, the bruised sky— "you just paid the first installment of a price nobody quoted."

"I know what they'll want."

"What will *you* want?"

Rosalind looked east, toward the distant smudge of the capital, where the usurper's banners were even now crawling toward the free cities like ink spreading through wet paper. They looked west, toward the door home, toward safety, toward the ledger-lined rooms where they had been so carefully, so expensively invisible. And they looked down at their own hands, which had held an instrument of their own making and been read by it like a book, and which were, even now, trembling not with fear but with something adjacent to fear, something with more spine in it.

The hole where their name had been ached. But around the ache, faintly, the way a forest regrows from a burn, Rosalind could feel something assembling. Syllables drifting back. Not the old name—not yet, maybe not ever the same—but a name nonetheless, coalescing out of everything the sigil had failed to take: the love, the vote, the vow, the burned hand held in theirs.

"I want to understand it properly," Rosalind said. "Not as a coastline. As a *counterparty*. It keeps a ledger of the king. Fine. Ledgers can be audited. Ledgers can be *closed*—but only by settling every entry, and its final entry is a person's entire self, paid in full." They turned to Theo, and their voice steadied as they said it, gathering itself like water finding its channel. "Someone has to pay. Someone has to open their whole book to it—every line, no omissions, no erasures—and write themselves into the breaking so completely that the sigil chokes on a self it cannot digest."

"That sounds like dying."

"It sounded like dying the first time, too, when I only lost my name." Rosalind flexed their fingers. The trembling was stopping. Or being stopped. They could no longer tell the difference, and suspected the distinction no longer mattered. "But I was wrong about the lock, Theo. I was wrong about the map. And I intend to stop being wrong before I try again—because next time there won't be a tether strong enough, and we both know it."

Far off, at the horizon where the memory-gardens leaned, the bruised sky split briefly with lightless lightning, and the chord of the seven threads shifted, strained, adapted—rewriting itself around the wound Rosalind had left in it, healing crooked, growing *smarter*.

They both heard it. They both understood, in the same cold instant, what the sound meant.

The sigil was not waiting for them anymore.

It was preparing.
