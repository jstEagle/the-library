## 16. Armies at the Free Cities' Gates

### Rosalind

The bells of Vellharbor began ringing an hour before dawn, and Rosalind knew before they opened their eyes that the sound was wrong.

Vellharbor's bells rang in patterns. The harbor bell spoke in triplets when ships came in; the temple bells answered each other across the rooftops like old friends gossiping; the market bell—*their* bell, the one under which a proclamation had once been nailed and a neighbor taken—rang slow and mournful for funerals. This was none of those. This was every bell in the city at once, no pattern at all, just raw noise hammered out of bronze by hands too frightened to be careful. Panic had its own grammar, and the city was shouting it.

Rosalind sat up in the loft above the chandlery, where what remained of the guild had slept for three nights running on straw pallets and borrowed blankets, and listened to the sound resolve from noise into meaning.

"They're here," said Wren, already awake, already dressed, standing at the window with her back very straight. She was seventy-one years old and could bottle a storm in her cupped palms, and she had not slept more than two hours any night since the betrayal. "Look at the roads."

Rosalind looked. From the window they could see down the long slope of Chandler's Street to the harbor, and past it to the flat salt marshes east of the city, and beyond *that*—where last night there had been darkness and the scattered lamps of fishing villages—there was light.

Not firelight. Not lamplight. A field of small cold lights stretching from one edge of the world to the other, thousands upon thousands of them, arranged in rows so precise that the eye refused them at first, insisting they must be stars fallen into the marsh. The army of the thing that wore the king's face did not camp the way armies camped. It camped the way a ledger balanced. Every tent identical. Every watchfire identical. Even the smoke rose in columns of equal height, as though the wind itself had been instructed to behave.

"Gods below," whispered Tammas from his pallet by the wall. He was the youngest of them, nineteen, with a door folded up inside him like a handkerchief—he could open ways between places, small ones, brief ones—and he'd been crying quietly in the nights, trying not to let anyone hear. He wasn't crying now. He was staring at the lights with his mouth open, and there was something worse than fear in his face. There was understanding.

"How many?" he asked.

"Doesn't matter," Rosalind said, and heard how hollow it sounded even to their own ears.

They went down into the chandlery proper, where the smell of tallow and salt hung thick, and where Sef—the woman who had sold their route to the crown and then, three days ago, come crawling back half-mad with guilt, her daughter smuggled out ahead of the crown's own men because she couldn't bear what she'd done—sat sharpening knives that would never be used as knives. Nobody had spoken to her. Nobody had sent her away either. Rosalind still hadn't decided which of those was mercy and which was cruelty, and suspected the answer depended on which of them you asked.

By midmorning the refugees began.

They came first as rumors: the villages along the Salt Road emptied overnight. Then as streams: carts, handcarts, families walking with everything they owned wrapped in sheets, driving goats, carrying hens. And then as a river, pouring through Vellharbor's eastern gates until the streets were chest-deep in people and the city guards—poor, outnumbered, loyal to nothing but the pay that the usurper now controlled—stood aside and let them come, because the alternative was to be trampled or to start killing, and killing was the usurper's work, not theirs.

The guild's hidden ways ran beneath all of it. That had been the point of them: doors to elsewhere, passages folded through the world's seams, escape routes for people who could not afford to be found. For two days Rosalind had worked the ledger of those doors like a woman bailing a sinking ship, opening ways north to the hill country, west along the coast, into the memory-gardens' borderlands where the crown's inspectors feared to follow. They had moved six hundred people through doors that should have held sixty.

And now the doors were full, and behind the people filling them came ten thousand more, and behind *them*, patient and precise and lit like a constellation brought low to earth, came the army.

"We can't hold the ways open," Tammas said. His voice cracked on *open*. "I tried this morning. The seams are thinning. Too much traffic. The doors—" He made a gesture like something tearing. "If I push much more, I'll fold myself into one of them and never come back out."

"Then we don't push," Rosalind said.

Wren turned from the window. "Then what do we do, cartographer? Draw them a map home?"

"No." Rosalind stood at the head of the long table where the ledgers lay open—the Ledger of Impossible Things, its entries in a hundred different hands across two hundred years, bottled storms and doors to elsewhere and a cartographer who drew realms into being. They had spent their whole life keeping these books carefully dull, carefully invisible, and invisibility had failed them in every way that mattered. "We stop being a way out. We become a way *through*."

Nobody understood. Rosalind didn't blame them; they barely understood it themselves yet. But they put both hands flat on the ledger and felt, beneath the paper, the faint pulse of the dream-realm's borderland—the realm they had mapped, the realm that had begun mapping them back, the realm whose sleeping king lay tangled in golden thread with a sigil growing smarter around him by the day.

"The army isn't marching on Vellharbor," Rosalind said slowly, feeling the thought assemble itself as they spoke. "An army that size doesn't march on cities. It marches on *everything*. On every free place left on the map. And we know something the usurper doesn't want anyone to know—we know where the king is, and we know the binding can be broken. If we run, we run alone, and everyone behind us burns, and the sigil wins by default." They looked around the table, at Wren's weathered defiance, at Tammas's red-rimmed eyes, at Sef who would not look up from her knives. "So we stand. Here. We hold the doors open as long as they hold, and when they close, we hold the streets, and when the streets go, we hold whatever's left. Not because we'll win. Because someone has to be the wall."

Silence. Outside, the bells were still ringing, and somewhere below a child was crying, and far off across the marshes ten thousand identical fires burned with the patience of arithmetic.

It was Sef who broke it. She set down her knife, laid her palms on the table the same way Rosalind had, and said, in a voice scraped hollow by three days of shame: "I sold you once. Let me buy it back."

And Wren, who had every right to curse her, only nodded once and said, "Then get up, girl. Walls don't build themselves."

### Theo

Theo Aldery had worn a scribe's gray for eleven years, and then he had burned it, and since then he had worn whatever the road gave him—a fisherman's oilskin, a dead man's boots, a coat with a guard's insignia cut off at the shoulder leaving a pale ghost of thread. Standing on the seawall at Vellharbor's eastern gate, watching the usurper's host begin its morning advance across the marshes, he felt the absence of the gray like the absence of skin.

He had come up from the capital ahead of the army, riding hard through territory where his face was now worth more dead than alive. The forged evidence he carried against the usurper—Rosalind's phantom-sigil map, wrapped around truths sharp enough to draw blood if the moment ever came—pressed against his ribs like a second heart. But the moment was not now. The moment was later, in a throne room, in front of the thing wearing his sovereign's face, and whether there would *be* a later depended entirely on what happened at this gate today.

Because the gate was not going to hold.

He could see it plainly, the way he had been trained to see plainly since boyhood, when he'd copied dispatches and learned to read a battlefield from a quartermaster's numbers. Ten thousand men, disciplined past the point of humanity, moving in formations so exact they cast shadows in ranks. Against them: a port city of forty thousand souls, half of them refugees, defended by perhaps eight hundred town guards and whatever the guild could improvise. There was no arithmetic in which Vellharbor survived. He had done the sums a dozen times since dawn and they always came out the same, and the doing of them made him think of Rosalind, who did sums like that for a living, who kept ledgers of impossible things, and who was at this moment somewhere below the chandlery doing something with maps that Theo did not fully understand and had stopped being afraid of.

"You're making that face again," said Brannic beside him. The big man had been a guild doorkeeper for thirty years; he could hold a doorway shut against a flood with his shoulders and had once, allegedly, wrestled a storm-bottle closed with his bare hands. He was leaning on the seawall eating an apple with tremendous calm, as though ten thousand advancing soldiers were a kind of weather. "The face where you've already lost and you're just checking your work."

"I'm a scribe," Theo said. "Checking my work is the whole profession."

"Well, check faster. They're coming."

They were. The front rank had reached the causeway across the marsh, and the cold lights of the camp were resolving into torches and spear-points and the banners of a king who was not a king. At this distance the usurper's standard looked like a golden circle, clean and complete. Theo knew what it really was: a sigil, a binding, seven threads woven around a dying man's dreams. He had seen it in Rosalind's maps. He had seen it, once, in a nightmare he refused to describe even to himself.

Below them, in the gate square, the guild was building the wall.

That was the only word for it. Rosalind had drawn it—an actual drawing, ink on butcher-paper, pinned to the chandlery door where everyone could see—and the drawing showed the gate square transformed: barricades of salt-herring barrels and ship's timber; Wren's bottled storms set into the upper windows of the granary like cannon waiting to be uncorked; Tammas's doors opened and shut in sequence to move defenders across the square faster than any army could react; Sef standing at the eastern barricade with her knives and her guilt, teaching refugee fishermen how to hold a line. And threaded through all of it, in Rosalind's precise cartographer's hand, annotations: *here the seam is thin — do not lean on the third door — the storm-bottles break glass, mind the children.*

It was not a plan that won battles. It was a plan that cost the enemy every inch, and made the enemy understand that the inches were paid for, and gave the people inside the walls something to do with their terror besides flee.

Theo watched a grandmother take a position at the western barricade next to a boy of maybe fifteen, and watched her show him how to brace a pike, and watched the boy stop shaking. That, he thought, was the thing the usurper had never understood and never would. You cannot conquer belonging. You can burn the houses that contain it, and it will simply move into the street, and then into the ditch, and then into the song mothers sing over the ruins. The guild had spent centuries being invisible, and invisible things, when they finally stood up, stood up all at once everywhere.

"He's stopping," Brannic said.

Theo looked up. The army *had* stopped—at the edge of bowshot, perfectly, ten thousand men halting on the same heartbeat. And into the silence between the host and the city rode a single figure in gold, unhurried, and the figure raised a hand, and every banner in the army dipped in unison like a field of wheat.

"Herald," Theo said. His mouth had gone dry. "They're sending a herald."

The herald came alone to fifty paces from the gate and called out in a voice amplified past human limits—magic, then, cheap and showy—and the words rolled across the square and over the barricades and into every listening ear:

*"People of Vellharbor! Your king offers clemency! Open your gates, surrender the traitors of the so-called Guild of Impossible Things, and no harm shall come to any soul within these walls! Shelter them, and the king's justice will fall upon you all alike! You have until sunset!"*

And then, because the thing wearing the king's face enjoyed cruelty the way other men enjoyed music, the herald added, almost gently:

*"His majesty grieves that you have forced him to make this request twice."*

The square was silent. Theo looked at the faces below—the grandmother with her pike, the boy, Sef, Wren, Tammas, hundreds of refugees who had fled one burning village to arrive at the edge of another—and he waited for the fear to tip them, for someone to cry out that one guild was not worth ten thousand lives, because it was true, it was arithmetically true, and fear always found the true argument eventually.

What happened instead was this: Wren climbed onto the eastern barricade, all seventy-one years of her, held a bottled storm above her head where the herald could see it, and shouted back in a voice like gravel:

*"Tell your master the Guild of Impossible Things thanks him for his interest, and regrets that we are entirely booked until the end of his reign!"*

The square roared. It was not a battle cry; it was laughter, ragged and defiant and slightly hysterical, and it spread through the refugees like fire through dry grass, and Theo felt something in his chest crack open—not despair, the opposite of despair, some fierce and unreasonable hope that had no business surviving the arithmetic. He looked at Brannic. Brannic grinned around the apple core.

"Sunset, then," Brannic said. "Best get the wall finished."

### Rosalind

Sunset came the color of a bruise.

Rosalind stood in the chandlery loft with the final map spread before them—the true map, the dream-realm's heart, the sleeping king and his golden threads—and did not look at it. They looked instead at the list they had written in the margin of the Ledger, the list of everyone who had chosen to stay. Wren. Tammas. Sef. Brannic. Forty-one names in total, guild members and refugees both, written in their own careful hand. They had asked each person individually, given each one the honest arithmetic and a door out, and every name on the page had looked Rosalind in the eye and stayed.

Forty-one names against ten thousand soldiers. By every law of sense, the page should have read *none*.

A knock, and Theo came up the ladder, oilskin dripping, face lit orange by the burning watchfires outside. He had spent the afternoon on the walls and it showed: ash in his hair, exhaustion carved deep, eyes bright with something that hadn't been there a week ago. He looked at the ledger, and at Rosalind, and said nothing for a long moment.

"I did the sums," he said finally.

"I know. So did I."

"And?"

"And the sums are wrong." Rosalind turned the ledger toward him, to the margin, to the forty-one names. "Ledgers balance debts, Theo. Not people. I've been keeping books my whole life and I only just understood that. The usurper thinks he's collecting on us—surrender the guild, save the city. But that's not the debt. The debt runs the other way. We owe these people a map out of the dark, and we're going to pay it, and no army on this earth holds enough soldiers to repossess what we've already given away."

Theo sat down beside them, slowly, the way a man sits when his legs have finally told him the truth about the day. Their shoulders touched. Neither of them moved away.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Or tonight. They won't wait past the deadline—it's theater, the clemency offer. He wants the city to refuse so he can call the burning a lesson."

"I know." Rosalind looked at the true map, at the golden threads and the sleeping king, at the annotation they had added that morning in ink still faintly wet: *the sigil grows smarter — the unbinding must happen before the army reaches the capital — days, not weeks.* "Which means tonight I finish the last survey, and tomorrow you ride for the throne room with forged papers and a straight spine, and we do the impossible thing on schedule, in the middle of a siege, because the schedule doesn't care that we're busy."

Theo laughed—a short, real laugh, surprised out of him. "You make it sound almost reasonable."

"I'm a cartographer. Reasonable is what I draw."

Outside, the sun went down behind the army's ten thousand fires, and the first horns sounded at the edge of the marsh—not the attack, not yet, but the tuning of it, the orchestra assembling. Down in the square, Wren's voice rose giving orders, and Tammas's doors flickered open and shut, and forty-one people who had every reason to run took up their places along the wall.

Rosalind blew out the lamp, and in the dark, Theo's hand found theirs and held on.

"One more night," Rosalind said.

And far off across the water, in the dream-realm where a king slept tangled in gold, seven threads tightened all at once—and began, softly, patiently, to sing their names.
