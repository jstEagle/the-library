## 17. The Price Written in Ink

The map would not let Rosalind sleep.

It hung from the rafters of the safehouse's back room, pinned at four corners with iron nails borrowed from a door that had once opened onto elsewhere, and it breathed. That was the only word for it. The parchment rose and fell in a rhythm too slow for wind and too patient for accident, and the ink lines shimmered when the candle guttered, as though the dream-realm beneath them were turning over in its sleep.

Rosalind sat before it with cold tea going colder, and for the third night running they did not look away.

*Understand the lock,* Master Ellery had said, decades ago, in the one lesson Rosalind had never been able to put down. *Anyone can force a door. Understanding is harder. Understanding means admitting what the lock was made for.*

They had thought they understood the sigil after the first failure. They had thought wrong.

The rebound had nearly taken their name — not the name on their forged papers, not the name the guild used over supper, but the name underneath all of those, the one that lived where Rosalind kept nothing written down. They had come back from the dream-realm's heart with their own tongue tasting of ash and absence, and for six hours they had not been able to remember which pronouns were theirs, and Theo had sat beside them saying *you are Rosalind, you are Rosalind, you are Rosalind* like a man laying stones against a flood until the shape of them solidified again.

Now, watching the map breathe, Rosalind finally saw it.

The sigil was not a lock. Locks kept things out. This kept something *in* — and it fed the way all bindings feed, on what it holds. It held a king. It drank a king: his memories first, then his judgments, then his face, then his name, siphoned thread by golden thread into the man who wore him. And because it fed on identity, it could only be broken by identity. Not by force. Force was just more material to drink.

It could only be unbound by someone willing to be fully known.

Every line of themselves, written into the breaking. Offered up so completely that the sigil, confronted with a self given freely instead of a self stolen slowly, would choke on the difference. Bindings ran on theft. Freedom ran on gift. Rosalind had spent thirty years learning that equation in reverse and had never once wanted to read it forward.

"Oh," they said aloud, to no one, to the breathing map. "Oh, you absolute bastard."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

* * *

Theo found them at dawn, sitting exactly where he'd left them, ink on their fingers and a look on their face he had learned to dread in the weeks since the rescue — the look that meant Rosalind Voss had understood something and wished to God they hadn't.

"You didn't sleep."

"I slept." It was technically true. There had been perhaps an hour of it, dreamless, which for a cartographer who walked other people's dreams was its own kind of warning. "Theo. Sit down."

He sat. He had gotten good at that — at being summoned into small rooms and told terrible things and staying anyway. His shoulder still moved stiffly where the guard's blade had found him breaking Rosalind out, and he favored it without noticing anymore, settling onto the bench with the careful economy of a body that had stopped expecting comfort.

"Tell me."

Rosalind told him. All of it, laid out in the flat voice they used for inventory: the nature of the binding, the way it drank, the mathematics of its undoing. A self, freely surrendered, whole and unhidden, poured into the breaking like ink into water. Nothing withheld. No forged papers, no dull cartographer, no careful invisibility. Every line.

Theo was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice came out carefully level, the way it did when he was holding something down. "How much of yourself?"

"All of it." Rosalind turned their hands over, ink-stained, ordinary. "That's the price. That's the whole price and there's no haggling. The sigil reads what's offered. If there's a single line missing — one truth held back, one corner of the map left blank — it takes the blank and keeps the rest bound. I'd feed it and free nothing." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "I've spent my entire life leaving corners blank, Theo. I'm very good at it. It might be the only thing I'm better at than mapping."

"And if you give it everything?" His hands were flat on the table now, knuckles pale. "What's left?"

"Me. I think — " Rosalind stopped. Started again, slower, because this part mattered and they refused to say it carelessly. "I think what's left is me without the hiding. Which is either everything or nothing. I genuinely don't know which. Nobody's ever done it. The guild has no ledger entry for this. I checked. Twice."

"The king dies free," Theo said quietly. "That was always the cost we named. We said someone would pay it. We just assumed it would be the king."

"We assumed it would be a stranger." Rosalind looked at the map, breathing above them. "It's easier to spend a stranger."

"Rosalind—"

"Don't." They held up a hand. "Don't do the thing where you argue me out of it. I need you to do the other thing. The harder thing. I need you to sit there while I decide, and let me decide, and not make my deciding into another weight I have to carry."

His jaw worked. She could see the argument building behind his teeth — she knew all its shapes by now, had catalogued them the way she catalogued coastlines — and then, with visible effort, he swallowed it. "All right," he said. "Decide. I'll be here."

"That's the most frightening thing you've ever said to me."

"I know," Theo said. "Believe me. I know."

* * *

Rosalind did not decide that day, or the next. They decided the way they did everything: by drawing.

The old maps came down off the walls, the ones Rosalind had made of themselves across the years — not the guild's official self-portrait, all careful neutrality, but the private ones, the ones no one had ever seen. The map of age seven, when the gift first showed and their mother's face went carefully smooth. The map of the academy, every corridor annotated with where to walk and where not to be noticed. The map made the year after the classmate burned, all gray distances, nothing living anywhere on it. Decades of cartography whose entire art was omission.

Rosalind spread them across the floor of the back room and knelt among them like a person kneeling among graves.

"This is the problem," they told the empty room, because saying it aloud made it real and real things could be worked on. "I can't offer what I've never drawn. The sigil will read the map I bring. And every map I have is a forgery."

So they began a new one.

Not on parchment at first — parchment felt too final, too much like the register the crown kept, the one with names and pyres attached. They began on the back of an old supply ledger, in pencil, light enough to erase, though they both knew and the pencil knew that erasure was a lie they'd tell themselves for comfort.

*Rosalind Voss,* they wrote. Then, underneath, because the sigil would want the truths under the truths: *born Maren Voss's second child; called girl until fourteen and boy until twenty-six and neither, finally, on purpose, which took longer than any of it.*

The pencil hovered. This was the edge of the map. Everything past this line was country they had never surveyed even alone, in the dark, in their own head.

They drew the line anyway.

*Afraid of bells since the neighbor was taken.* *Loved a woman named Sefa at nineteen and never told her and mapped her city three times trying.* *Reported no one, but stood silent while others were reported, and silence is a kind of report.* *Kept the ledgers because keeping records meant mattering without being seen, and I chose that, I chose smallness, it was not done to me, I built it grain by grain like a wall and called it safety.*

Their hand shook. They kept writing.

Somewhere past midnight, the door opened, and Theo came in with bread and cheese and the particular expression of a man who had decided not to ask and was failing. He set the food down. He looked at the pages covering the floor — dozens of them now, the true map assembling itself in fragments, in confessions, in ink that could not be taken back — and Rosalind watched him understand, watch the color leave his face and come back again.

"You're doing it," he said. "You're actually — you've already started."

"I started the moment I understood the lock." Rosalind didn't look up. "Understanding is the beginning of the offering. Ellery taught me that. I hated her for it at the time."

"Can I—" He gestured at the pages.

"It's all going to be read by a golden horror in a dream-realm, Theo. I think you're allowed."

He knelt. He read. Rosalind kept writing while he did, which was its own strange torture — writing truths while the man they loved read them over their shoulder, every secret losing its armor one line at a time. When he reached the page about Sefa, he went still. When he reached the page about the academy — about the day he himself had turned in a gifted classmate to save his own skin, the betrayal that had started everything between them, the guilt he'd carried like a stone in his boot for fifteen years — Rosalind heard his breath catch.

"You wrote me in," he said.

"You're in the map. You've been in the map since the market bell." Rosalind finally looked up. "That's rather the point. I tried to draw a version of myself that didn't include you and it wouldn't hold together. Believe me. I tried last night, around the third hour, when I was looking for a loophole." They smiled, tired and crooked. "There isn't one. Apparently I'm a person who loves you. It's in the record now. Very embarrassing."

Theo laughed — a wet, broken sound — and pressed his forehead to theirs, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, and the map breathed overhead, patient as a tide.

"If it takes you," he said finally, barely a sound, "if the sigil drinks you the way it drank him—"

"It won't. It might. I don't know." Rosalind pulled back far enough to see his face. "Those are the three true answers and I'm giving you all of them, because apparently that's who I am now. But listen to me. Listen." They took his ink-stained hand in their ink-stained hand. "I spent thirty years believing that invisible meant safe. The hunt proved that wrong. The pyres proved it wrong. Being unseen didn't save anyone — it just meant no one knew who was burning." Their voice steadied. "This is the same lesson wearing different clothes. Hiding half of myself didn't keep me safe from the sigil; it would just keep me bound. The only way through is through. Fully known or not at all."

"And you choose it."

"I choose it." And here was the thing Rosalind had expected to feel like duty and did not, the thing that kept surprising them every time they turned it over: it didn't feel like sacrifice. It felt like finishing a map. Like the last coastline closing, like the compass needle finally settling north. "I choose it the way I chose the guild, the way I chose you — badly advised, fully awake, with my eyes open. Love against duty was always a false choice, Theo. I finally see it. The love *is* the duty. The duty is just love with its boots on."

* * *

Three days later, the new map was finished.

It was not large. A single sheet of the good parchment, the kind the guild saved for realms that mattered, covered edge to edge in Rosalind's smallest, truest hand. No omissions. No gray distances. The mother's smooth face. The bells. Sefa. The silence. The smallness, chosen and built and called safety. The academy. The betrayal witnessed. The classmate's name — Aldric Fen, seventeen years dead, restored at last to a map after fifteen years of Rosalind's deliberate forgetting. Theo, in the market bell square, passing forged papers with steady hands. The vote cast last. The door to elsewhere. The wound taken. The night between two watchfires. The name, all the names, the one underneath.

At the bottom, where cartographers signed their work, Rosalind wrote their full name and did not flinch, and then — because the map demanded completeness and completeness had become a discipline they could not set down — they added a second line:

*Drawn by Rosalind Voss, who is loved by Theo Aldery, and knows it, and is no longer afraid to write it down.*

The guild gathered to see them off at dusk. Wren pressed a vial of bottled stormlight into their pocket and pretended it was nothing. Old Marette, who had voted against the whole enterprise and had been proven right about its costs, gripped Rosalind's wrist hard enough to bruise and said only, "Come back and tell me I was wrong." Half the guild was scattered, captured, or dead, and the ones who remained stood in the doorway of the safehouse like the last lit windows in a dark street, and Rosalind memorized them, because memorizing was what they did, and because the sigil would want this too — this, all of this, the faces, the fear, the stubbornness.

Theo walked with them to the threshold of the dream-realm, to the place where the borderlands thinned and the golden threads became visible even to ordinary eyes, coiling up out of the ground like roots of a tree that grew downward into sleep.

"You'll have maybe an hour," Rosalind said, all business, because business was how they kept their hands from shaking. "Distract him with the forged evidence, keep him in the throne room's waking layer, and whatever happens — whatever you hear from inside — do not come in after me. Promise me. If you follow me in, he'll know, and everything I pour into the breaking becomes a door for him instead."

"I promise." Theo said it easily, which is how Rosalind knew he was lying, and they let him lie, because some promises exist to be broken beautifully and this might be one of them, and there was no time to argue.

He caught their hands. The threads glimmered behind him, gold on the dusk.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I read the whole map. Every line. And Rosalind Voss — the real one, the one with no corners left blank —" his voice cracked and he let it, "— is the best person I have ever known. Whatever that thing sees when it reads you, it won't survive the reading."

"That," Rosalind said, "is the plan."

And they stepped past him, past the last lit window of the waking world, into the country of the sleeping king — the map held against their chest like a second heartbeat, every line of themselves written down at last, walking toward the throne room of threads with nothing hidden and everything to lose and, for the first time in thirty years, absolutely nothing left to hide.
