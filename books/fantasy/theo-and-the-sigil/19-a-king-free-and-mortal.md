## 19. A King, Free and Mortal

The doors opened, and Theo's heart did something complicated and painful and wonderful all at once.

Rosalind stood in the doorway with soot on their cheek and a bruise darkening along their jaw like spilled ink, and behind them the corridor was chaos—guards who didn't know yet whom to arrest, guild members bleeding and grinning, the smell of scorched thread still hanging in the air like incense after a funeral. But Rosalind was upright. Rosalind was *there*. They looked at him across the ruined antechamber of the dream-layer's threshold, where the golden threads still drifted in slow dissolution, catching light that had no source, and something in their face said everything their voice couldn't manage.

"You're bleeding," Rosalind said. Their voice cracked on it.

"It's not mine," Theo lied gently. "Well. Some of it is."

He took three steps toward them and his legs nearly failed—he hadn't realized until that moment how much of the last hour he'd spent holding himself together by pure administrative stubbornness, the scribe's oldest trick: keep writing, keep moving, fall apart later. Later had arrived. He caught Rosalind's hands, and they were shaking, and so were his, and for a moment they just stood there in the wreckage of a usurpation, two people holding on like the floor might go out from under them at any moment.

Because in some ways, it had.

Above them, through the high windows of the throne room, dawn was coming up over the capital—the first dawn in eleven years without the usurper wearing the king's face to greet it. Somewhere in the city, bells were starting to ring. Not the market bell, not the alarm bell. All of them. Every bell in the capital, ringing at once, because someone had seen the thing on the throne unravel mid-command, seen its borrowed shape come apart like wet paper, seen an old man wake in a chair of golden threads and weep—and word traveled faster than shame through streets that had spent a decade learning to whisper.

"The king," Theo managed. "I have to—I should be with him."

"I know." Rosalind squeezed his hands once, hard, and let go. "Go. I'll be here when you're done."

"How do you know?"

"Because I'm the one who unbound him." They smiled, tired and crooked and entirely themselves. "You're going to need me later. Everyone always needs the cartographer eventually. I keep the ledgers."

* * *

King Aldric IV was smaller than Theo remembered.

That was the first thing—the sheer diminished fact of him. Eleven years ago, when Theo had been a young scribe copying decrees in the outer offices, the king had been a presence even secondhand: a voice like a landslide, a will that came down through three layers of bureaucracy and still left dents. The man who sat now in the private solar off the throne room, wrapped in a plain wool robe someone had found for him, was thin as winter branches. His hands trembled. His hair had gone fully white, though by Theo's reckoning the king could only be six-and-fifty, maybe seven.

The binding had fed on him. Everyone knew what the sigil did to identity—it had tried to take Rosalind's name in the first attempt—but no one had quite reckoned what eleven years of being slowly *eaten* would leave behind. Aldric had gone into the threads a king. He had woken up something else: a man, mortal, worn, with gaps in his memory like a book with pages torn out.

He looked up when Theo entered, and for one terrible moment his eyes were empty—searching, reaching, finding nothing.

Then they focused. "Aldery," the king said. "The scribe. You used to bring me the eastern dispatches. You had ink on your cuff every morning, boy, every single morning, and you never noticed."

Theo's throat closed. Of all the things the king might remember. "Yes, majesty."

"Don't do that." The old man waved a trembling hand at the title. "Not today. Perhaps never again—we'll see what tomorrow thinks. Sit down. Sit, sit, before you fall down; I can see the blood on you, and I've watched enough men bleed this week to know what it costs." He waited until Theo sat, then studied him for a long moment with eyes that were sunken but sharp underneath, sharp the way a blade is sharp after the rust is scraped away. "They tell me you're the one who kept faith. Through all of it. That you served the usurper's court by day and my cause by night."

"Some days I wasn't sure which was which," Theo said honestly.

"Good," said the king. "That's honesty. I've had eleven years of flattery filtered through a thing that wore my face, and I find I've developed a violent allergy to it." He leaned back. Outside the solar's window, the bells were still ringing, and somewhere below, crowds were gathering in the great square—Theo could hear them, a low ocean-sound, ten thousand voices trying out words they hadn't been allowed to say aloud. "Tell me what happens next. And don't tell me what you think I want to hear, because I genuinely don't know, and neither does anyone else, and that frightens me more than the threads did."

So Theo told him. The truth of it: the free cities already governing themselves in all but name, their councils grown strong during the war; the army fracturing now that the usurper's compulsion had shattered with him, commanders staring at each other across campfires wondering what exactly they'd been fighting for; the guild scattered but alive; the witch-hunt's machinery still standing, its registers still existing, its inspectors suddenly unsure whether they were enforcers or criminals. A kingdom held together by habit and grief, with no clear claimant to hold it.

"There are loyalists who'll want you crowned again by week's end," Theo finished. "There's a ceremony already being drafted. I've seen the drafts."

"Of course there are. Ceremonies are the one thing this kingdom produces faster than orphans." The king was quiet for a while. His trembling hand rested on the arm of his chair, and Theo watched him look at it as if it belonged to a stranger—as perhaps, in some ways, it did. When Aldric spoke again, his voice was very quiet. "Do you know what I dreamed about, in there? In the threads? Not battles. Not glory. I dreamed about my garden at Vellermont. The little one, with the crooked pear tree. I dreamed about pruning it, and I could never reach the high branches, and I woke each time furious." He laughed—a dry, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering its work. "Eleven years, and the gods gave me a pear tree. I think that was mercy. I think they were telling me who I actually was, under all the gold."

"Majesty—"

"Aldric. Just for tonight, Aldric." The old man rose, slowly, one hand braced against the wall, and went to the window. Below, the square was filling with torchlight despite the hour, and the sound of the crowd had changed—no longer a murmur but a chant, shapeless yet, formless, a name being tested by ten thousand mouths. Theo couldn't make out whose name it was. He suspected the king could. "Here is what I know about legitimacy," Aldric said. "Mine was taken from me while I slept, and no one noticed for eleven years. Which means it was never really *mine* to begin with—it lived in other people's belief, and belief can be worn like a coat by any thief with nerve. If I take the throne back on the strength of that same belief—" he turned, and his eyes were fierce and exhausted and utterly clear, "—then I have learned nothing, and the next usurper merely waits for me to sleep. No. No more borrowed crowns. Not on my head."

"Then what?" Theo asked. It came out barely above a whisper. "What do we do?"

"What you're going to write down," the king said, with the ghost of the landslide-voice returning, "is a charge. And you're going to deliver it to the free cities myself, because I intend to travel there and say it standing, on my own feet, before anyone can put me on a throne or in a grave. Tell them the crown is finished—not broken, *finished*, retired, hung on a wall like a sword too heavy for the hand that carried it. Tell them the realm belongs to whoever lives in it, and that they've governed themselves through fire already, so they may as well make it official. Tell them—" and here his voice did break, just slightly, just enough, "—tell them I'm sorry. For the registers. For the pyres. For sleeping through it. A king who cannot protect his people's smallest freedoms has no business asking for their largest loyalty."

Theo found he was crying without having decided to. He reached automatically for his satchel, for pen and paper, the reflex of a lifetime, and the king huffed something that might have been laughter.

"Yes, yes. Write it all down, Aldery. That's what you're *for*." Then, softer, as Theo bent to the page: "And when it's written—what will you be for then? Have you thought about it? A man spends his whole life becoming useful to a purpose, and then the purpose ends, and he discovers he never learned anything else about himself." The king looked out at the crowd, at the bells, at the raw new day. "Take it from someone who just woke up from eleven years of being a symbol. Find out who you are when nobody needs you. It's the hardest map you'll ever draw."

* * *

The pardons came through in a single decree, drafted in Theo's own hand, sealed with the king's signet—which felt, each time he pressed wax, like sealing a letter to himself from a life that no longer existed.

Every gifted person on the register was freed. Every hunted name was struck. The pyres were dismantled publicly in the capital and in four of the free cities, their timbers carted off by cheerful mobs and repurposed within days into everything from market stalls to a frankly magnificent bonfire for the Festival of Unbinding, which sprang into existence spontaneously and showed every sign of becoming annual. The registers themselves were burned in the palace courtyard, and Theo stood at the window watching the ash rise and thought of a friend from academy years—a girl named Maren Corvel, who had bent spoons with a touch and died on a pyre for it seven years ago, reported by a frightened classmate who had told himself ever since that he'd had no choice.

The frightened classmate was pardoned too. It was in the decree. *All collaborators acting under duress are absolved.* Theo read the line he himself had drafted, and understood with perfect clarity that it did not apply to him. Duress was a coat with sleeves of every size; everyone claimed theirs fit.

He burned his own credentials instead—all of them, the letters of rank, the inspector's seal, the commission signed by a dead man wearing a living king's face—in the small brazier in his quarters at the guild safehouse, which was where he was living now, since his own apartments near the chancellery had been ransacked by loyalists looking for evidence of treason against a usurper, an accusation so circular it almost deserved admiration.

Rosalind found him there, feeding the last of it to the flames.

"You missed a corner," they said, nodding at the commission, which was curling obstinately at one edge.

"That corner has my full name on it."

"I know. I checked the register years ago. Theodore Josiah Aldery, royal scribe, third class, commendation for penmanship." They sat down beside him on the floor, shoulder against his shoulder, close enough that he could feel the warmth of them through two layers of travel-worn clothing. "Terrible penmanship, by the way. Your *r*'s are a disgrace."

"They're *economical*."

"They're lazy, and I love them." A pause. The brazier popped. "You've been quiet for two days."

"I'm deciding who I am," Theo said. "It turns out to be a lengthy process. There's paperwork."

Rosalind laughed—that real laugh, the one he'd only heard a handful of times, the one that made them press the back of their hand to their mouth like the sound might escape and cause damage—and then they went quiet too, both of them watching the fire eat the last of the man he'd been.

"Here's what I know," Rosalind said finally, in the tone they used for reading aloud from the ledger, the formal cadence that meant they'd been composing this in their head for hours. "Item: one disgraced scribe, formerly royal, currently unemployed. Item: betrayed a classmate at nineteen and has been paying interest on the debt ever since. Item: chose rebellion over survival, broke a prisoner out of a fortified registry house, took a knife meant for a stranger. Item:" —and here their voice softened, and they turned to look at him, soot still faintly in the creases of their knuckles, eyes catching the firelight— "item: kissed a cartographer on the night the world ended, and the world, notably, did not end. Conclusion: the ledger doesn't have a category for you yet, Theo Aldery. We'll have to invent one."

"And what would it say?"

"'Under consideration.'" They bumped their shoulder against his. "Permanently. Some entries stay open forever. It keeps the ledger honest."

Outside, the bells had stopped, and in the ringing silence after, the city sounded different—younger, somehow, uncertain, like a student on the first day at a new school. Somewhere out there, the free cities were waiting for an old man with a pear tree in his dreams to arrive and hand them a kingdom. Somewhere out there, the guild's scattered members were drifting home, following marks only they could read. Somewhere out there were doors to elsewhere, dozens of them, opening onto realms no one had ever properly surveyed, realms that existed now on no map at all—

Rosalind sat bolt upright.

"Oh no," they said.

"What? What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong. That's the problem." They were staring past him, at nothing, with the expression Theo had learned to recognize as the exact face of a map drawing itself behind their eyes. "I just realized what comes next. For us. Both of us. And it's enormous, and impossible, and I'm going to need a lot more paper."

Theo looked at the ashes of his old life, still glowing in the brazier, and felt—for the first time since he was nineteen years old and made the worst decision of his life—entirely weightless.

"Tell me," he said.
