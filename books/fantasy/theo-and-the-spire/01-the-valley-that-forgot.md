## 1. The Valley That Forgot

The trouble with contracts in the Valley of Hollow is that nothing here is worth killing for, which means the pay is always insulting and the work always personal. Take this morning: I'd been hired by a miller to settle a dispute with his neighbor over three sacks of flour. Not grain—flour. Already ground. Already ruined, frankly, because the neighbor had let his goats into the millhouse overnight, and there's no negotiating with what goats do to a sack of flour.

I stood between two men who had known each other since they were boys, both of them shouting about honor while flour dust hung in the air like the valley's idea of snow, and I thought, as I often do: *somewhere else, this would be beneath me.*

"Three sacks," the miller said, jabbing a finger. "Three! And he says it was an act of Veth."

That name again.

The neighbor shrugged with the magnificent shamelessness of a man whose conscience died decades ago. "Gods punish the proud. Your mill stood too tall."

"We don't have gods," the miller snapped. "That's the whole point of this place."

He was right, mostly. The Valley of Hollow sits in the crook of two mountain ranges like something cupped in a pair of stone hands, and whatever runs through the rest of the world—the magic, the miracles, the whispering gods that people in the lowlands swear by—we don't get any of it. Priests call it godless ground. Pilgrims used to come here to be cured of faith, back when pilgrims came at all. Now we get deserters, debtors, and mercenaries like me, people who came for the silence and stayed because the silence doesn't ask questions.

I settled the flour dispute the way most disputes get settled in Hollow: badly, with compromise, everyone unhappy. The miller paid me six coppers and a heel of bread that tasted like the millhouse smelled. I was walking home along the river road, chewing on my resentment more than the bread, when the courier came through the eastern pass.

* * *

I heard him before I saw him—a horse in pain, and then no horse at all, just the wet sound of something four-legged giving up on a slope of scree. Then the man himself, coming down the trail on foot, one arm clamped against his side like he was holding himself shut.

He was young, maybe twenty-five, dressed for warmer country than ours. His coat had been good once. Now it was mostly blood, some of it dried to a crust, some of it still doing its enthusiastic work. He walked like a man who had decided falling down was optional but only barely, and his eyes swept the valley floor with an expression I couldn't read at first.

Then I could. It was wonder. Raw, unguarded wonder, the way you'd look at a cathedral or a woman you thought you'd lost.

People don't look at Hollow that way. People look at Hollow the way you look at a room you've been locked in.

He saw me and changed course, weaving toward the river road, and I did the arithmetic every mercenary does—threat, reward, trouble—and got *trouble* twice out of three. But there was something in how he moved toward me specifically, out of all the valley, that made me stay put.

"You're real," he said when he reached me. His voice was shredded. "This place. It's real."

"It's a valley," I said. "It has goats."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough brought up enough red that I stopped pretending I wasn't going to be involved in whatever this was. I caught him under the arm and walked him off the road, behind a stand of alders, because a dying man bleeding in plain view draws questions, and questions in Hollow are like magic: unwelcome, and liable to bring the wrong kind of attention.

"I'm Lior," I said, mostly because it seemed rude not to.

"Theo." He sagged against an alder trunk and slid down it, and I knelt in the leaves beside him and pulled his hand away from his side to see how bad it was.

It was bad. A wound below the ribs, deep, wrong-shaped—not a blade. Something with edges that hadn't been made by a smith. The flesh around it was gray, and where the blood touched the ground, the dead leaves curled like they'd been held near a flame.

"That should hurt more than it does," Theo said, watching my face.

"It will. Give it a minute." I tore a strip from his already-ruined coat and pressed it to the wound. "Who did this?"

"Doesn't matter. They're behind me." He gripped my wrist. His fingers were cold and far too strong, the grip of a man spending money he didn't have. "Listen. I need to know if anyone's ever told you what this valley is."

"A hole in the world," I said. "A place where prayers go to die. Everyone's told me. I live here."

"No." He shook his head, slow and deliberate, a man correcting a child. "Not empty. *Sealed.* There's a difference. Empty is nothing behind the wall. Sealed is—" He winced. "Sealed is something behind the door, and someone paid a great deal to keep it shut."

I looked around at the alders, the river, the gray afternoon light. Somewhere downslope, the miller was probably still yelling at his neighbor about flour.

"You've lost a lot of blood," I said gently. "People say strange things."

"People say true things when they're dying. That's the one advantage of it." He fumbled inside his coat, and I tensed—he was armed, apparently, even now—but what he drew out was a leather tube, sealed with wax, and wrapped around it a strip of cloth gone brown and stiff. "They'll find me. Maybe today, maybe tomorrow. When they do, they'll tear this valley open looking for what I carried. You need to be gone before then."

"Gone where? This is my home."

He looked at me then, really looked, the way you look at someone you're trying to memorize or apologize to. "Is it?" he asked. "Has it ever felt like home? Or has it felt like a room you woke up in, waiting for someone to tell you why?"

I didn't answer. That was answer enough, and we both knew it.

There is a thing about me that I keep folded up small, the way you keep a letter you can't bear to reread but can't throw away. All my life—first in the orphan rows of Lowmark, later in mercenary companies across three borders—I have prayed to a god who never answers. Not with the desperate bargaining prayer of the frightened. With the flat, habitual prayer of someone who talks to a person she knows isn't listening, because stopping would mean admitting something. His name is Veth. The Silent God. In the lowlands they say he abandoned the world; priests of louder gods call him a coward, a failure, a myth. I have spent twenty years telling anyone who asked—and mostly myself—that it meant nothing. A habit. A superstition. A scar I liked the shape of.

So when Theo pressed the leather tube into my hands and whispered a name with his last working breath, I want to tell you I was surprised.

I was not surprised. That's the worst part. Some door in me that I'd painted over twenty years ago swung open like it had never been locked at all.

"*Veth,*" he said. "Tell them Veth sent you. Tell them the silence is a sentence, not an absence. Tell them—"

"What sentence? Who's them? Theo—"

But he was done talking. He died the way soldiers do when they finally allow it, all at once, like setting down a weight, and the valley took the sound of him and gave back nothing but the river and the wind and the distant complaint of the miller, still going, God help us all, about flour.

* * *

I sat with him for a while, because you do. I closed his eyes and arranged his arms and tried to think about what to do with a body in a valley where nobody buries strangers without asking why they came.

Then I opened the tube.

Inside was a map. Drawn on something that wasn't paper—thin, pale, faintly warm, like skin that had been tanned by someone very patient. It showed mountains I half-recognized, the twin ranges that cup our valley, and beyond them a route winding north and east through passes marked in careful ink. And at the end of it, drawn with a precision that suggested the artist had either seen it or feared it: a spire. A black needle rising out of a ring of broken country, rendered so fine the lines seemed to vibrate if I stared too long.

Bloodstains covered it. Theo's blood, presumably. Except—

Except the stains were moving.

Slowly, the way frost creeps across a window, the blood was crawling along the ink lines. Filling in gaps. Where it passed, new markings surfaced like bruises rising under skin: annotations in a script I didn't know, and one symbol repeated again and again at every pass, every bridge, every waypoint along the route.

I knew the symbol. Of course I knew it. Every child in the lowlands knows it, usually as a warning that a shrine is empty, a god is gone, a well of grace has run dry.

The mark of Veth. A vertical line, severed in the middle.

The blood reached the center of the map and pooled there, and for one vertiginous moment the pooling stain shaped itself—not quite formed, but suggested—a word, or the ghost of one, in that unknown script. And though I couldn't read it, I understood it the way you understand a shout through a wall: by its shape, its urgency, its fear.

*Come.*

I dropped the map. Actually dropped it, flung it onto the leaves like it had bitten me, and sat there breathing hard while the autumn light went gold and long and ordinary around me. A fox barked somewhere upslope. The river kept on keeping on. The Valley of Hollow, silent, safe, godless, went about its evening.

And I sat in it thinking about what Theo had said. *Sealed, not empty. Something behind the door.* Thinking about how, for as long as I could remember, praying to Veth had felt less like speaking to a distant god and more like pressing my ear to a wall and hearing—nothing. Not absence. Nothing. There's a difference. Anyone who's stood in an empty house knows the difference between no one home and someone holding their breath.

Twenty years I'd called it habit. Superstition. A scar I liked the shape of.

It was none of those things. It was a leash, and I had never noticed the collar because I'd worn it since before I could walk.

I picked the map back up. Because I'm a fool, or a professional, or because some part of me had been standing at that wall my whole life with my ear pressed flat, waiting.

Downriver, torches were being lit in the village. Behind me, Theo lay quiet under the alders, and I owed him a grave I didn't have time to dig. And far off—not in the valley, I'd have sworn it, but somewhere past the eastern pass, out in the loud world where gods still talk—something answered the map's small summons.

Not a voice. Not yet.

Just the feeling, brief and unmistakable, of a held breath being slowly, deliberately released.
