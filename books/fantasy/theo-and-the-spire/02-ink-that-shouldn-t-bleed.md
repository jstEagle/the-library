## 2. Ink That Shouldn't Bleed

The map bled on the third night.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only prayer Veth ever answered. It didn't smudge. It didn't fade. It *bled*—a slow welling of red at the valley's edge, where the courier's blood had soaked deepest into the paper, and it moved the way a wound moves when you press too hard: reluctantly, and then all at once.

I was sitting in the loft above the stable with a cup of watered wine I had no intention of drinking, because sleep and I had come to an arrangement since Theo died: it could visit, but it couldn't stay. The candle was down to its last knuckle of wax. And the map, which I'd pinned flat to the boards under four iron nails because some animal part of me didn't trust it not to walk away, was quietly growing a new road.

I watched it for an hour before I touched it. That's the discipline they beat into you in the company—watch first, touch second, and if it's still moving when you reach for it, touch with something you can afford to lose. So I reached out with the poker.

The ink flinched.

Not recoiled. *Flinched.* The way a dog flinches from a hand that's hit it before. And then, slowly, like a bruise surfacing, a new line darkened across the parchment—a road I hadn't seen before, running north out of the valley's mouth, past a mark shaped like a broken tower, toward a symbol at the top of the page that might have been a spire or might have been a needle or might have been, if you squinted and were feeling generous, a finger pointing straight up at nothing.

"Veth's quiet mercy," I said to the empty loft, which was, as usual, no help whatsoever.

Here's what I knew about the Valley of Hollow, having been born in it and nearly died in it several times on principle: magic doesn't work here. Not fails—*doesn't work*, the way a wheel doesn't turn when there's no road. Charms go dead at the border stones. Hedge-witches who wander in find their hands suddenly just hands. We're a valley of mud and iron and honest labor, and we tell ourselves that's a virtue, because the alternative is admitting we're a hole in the world and nobody's told us why.

Theo had known why. He'd crawled half a mile past the border stone to die in our godless dirt, and the last thing he'd done with his ruined hands was push a map into mine and say a name I'd been pretending not to hear my whole life.

Veth.

My silent god. The one whose shrine in my mother's house was always clean but never lit, because lighting candles felt like asking questions. The one I'd sworn to at fourteen because swearing to nothing felt worse, and because the silence had always felt less like absence and more like—well. Like someone holding their breath.

I took the nails out one by one and unrolled the map across my knees, and I did the stupid thing. The human thing. I pricked my thumb on the poker's rusted tip—not deep, just enough to bead—and held it over the parchment.

The blood fell. The map drank it.

And the road north widened.

* * *

I didn't sleep after that, obviously. I spent the night doing what soldiers do instead of panicking, which is inventory. One map that eats blood. One name I couldn't stop hearing in Theo's dying voice. One valley that had kept me safe for twenty-six years, and one new and unwelcome thought squatting in the corner of my skull like a debt collector:

Safe from what? Or—worse—safe *for* what?

Because here's the thing about being invisible. I'd always thought of the valley's dead air as a blessing. Magic can't find you here. Curses slide off. The Ashen Order, whoever they were—and I knew them now only as the people Theo had been running from—couldn't scry into a place where their art went to die. We were under a blanket, all of us, and the blanket was made of nothing.

But a blanket has two sides. And a cage looks exactly like shelter until you try the door.

By dawn I'd worked out the arithmetic I didn't want to work out. The map responded to my blood. My blood. Which meant either the map was bound to me, or I was bound to it, and given that I hadn't drawn it, hadn't bought it, and hadn't wanted it, I suspected I knew which way that ledger ran. Theo hadn't handed it to me because I was nearest. He'd handed it to me because I was *addressed*. A letter finds its recipient even in a valley with no roads out.

And letters get read.

* * *

They came asking questions on the fifth day.

Two riders, grey-cloaked, ash-pale sigils stitched where a crest should be—the mark of an order that wants you to know they've burned their family colors and expect you to do the same. They stopped at the border stone like men testing ice, and I'll grant them this much: they didn't cross it. Whatever the valley does to magic, it apparently does to their nerve as well, or their orders, or both.

Instead they did something cleverer. They talked.

I was mending a harness rail at the Merrow farm when they rode up the lane, and old Marta Merrow waved them in with the hospitality of a woman who hasn't had news in a decade and would take plague if it came with gossip. I stayed by the fence, arms crossed, close enough to hear and far enough to look bored, which is a skill like any other.

They asked about travelers. They asked about a courier, wounded, carrying documents. They described him—tall, brown coat, a scar through the left eyebrow—and every word of it fit Theo like a glove fits a hand that's no longer in it.

"He came through," Marta said, "and he died, poor soul. Fever. We buried him up on the ridge."

They looked at each other in a way I recognized, because I've looked at people that way myself: the look two professionals exchange when a civilian has just lied to them badly and they're deciding whether it matters. Then the older one turned and scanned the yard, unhurried, the way a man reads a room for exits and weapons, and his gaze passed over me and stopped.

Just for a moment. Just long enough.

"You," he said. "You found him?"

"I found a body," I said. "He was mostly gone by then."

"What did he give you?"

"Nothing. His hands were empty." This was true in the way that matters to gods and liars: his hands had been empty. Everything else had been full.

The rider studied me. He had eyes the color of wet slate, and behind them was a patience I distrusted immediately and completely, because patience in a man hunting something means he already knows where it isn't and is enjoying the process of elimination. "There are stories," he said, "about families in this valley. Old families. Wardens, the stories call them." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd seen that year, warm as a banked fire. "But of course, no one remembers."

"No one remembers," I agreed.

He nodded slowly, tipped two fingers off his brow like a man saluting a colleague he intends to kill someday, and rode out. The younger one lingered a heartbeat longer, looking at the fence post, at the ridge, at me again—and then followed.

Marta clucked her tongue and started in about how strangers these days had no manners, and I let her talk, because her voice covered the sound of my own pulse, which was doing something undignified against my ribs.

Wardens. No one remembers.

Except somebody had remembered. Somebody outside the blanket, outside the cage, had kept a list of names that the valley itself had been made to forget. And those names had led two grey riders to a farmyard where a woman stood holding a harness rail and lying through her teeth.

* * *

That night I burned nothing and told no one, which tells you something about how far I've fallen from the soldier I used to be, because the correct move was to go to Bren. Bren, who'd carried me out of the marsh fight at Corren's Ford with an arrow in his shoulder and never once mentioned it afterward. Bren, who knows my silences better than my words.

But the map had grown again while I was at Marta's. A third road now, and small writing along its margin in a hand that was not Theo's and not mine and not any living hand I knew—thin, precise, patient script that said, in the trade-tongue everyone forgets and no one entirely loses:

*The door opens from either side.*

I sat with that a long time. Outside, the valley lay quiet under a sky with no stars worth naming, the same sky I'd slept under my whole life, and for the first time it didn't feel like home. It felt like a lid.

Invisible. Untouched. Unfound. I'd worn those words like armor for twenty-six years, and now I understood they'd been fitted to me the way a collar is fitted to a throat—by someone else, for someone else's purposes, and the fitting had been paid for by people whose names I'd never been allowed to learn. Wardens. My mother's clean, unlit shrine. My own oath to a god who never answered, sworn in a valley where no god could.

The silence wasn't empty. It had never been empty. It was a held breath, and held breaths end.

I rolled the map, wrapped it in oilcloth, and strapped it inside my coat, against the skin over my heart, which felt less like concealment and more like delivery. Because the door opened from either side, and the Ashen Order had just knocked politely at ours, and I had learned everything I needed to know about polite men with slate-colored eyes.

In the morning I would find Bren. In the morning I would decide whether the truth between us was still load-bearing.

But tonight, sitting alone in the loft with my thumb still stinging and the ink drying somewhere beneath my ribs, I finally admitted the thing I'd been circling since Theo died:

Whatever waited at the top of that map had been waiting for me specifically. And it was tired of waiting.
