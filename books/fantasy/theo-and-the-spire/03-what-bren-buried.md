## 3. What Bren Buried

There are three ways to learn that you've been lying to someone for eleven years. The first is to confess, which requires courage. The second is to be caught, which requires luck. The third is to watch the truth walk into your camp on the boots of a dying man and sit down at your fire like it had always been invited.

I got the third. I don't recommend it.

The courier died two nights ago with a map in his fists and Lior's name in his mouth—well, not her name exactly. He said *Veth*, which is worse, because Veth is the god Lior pretends she doesn't pray to, and I am one of maybe four people alive who knows she prays to him anyway. She thinks she hides it well. She prays the way other people bite their nails: constantly, unconsciously, and with visible shame when caught.

So when the map started doing things maps shouldn't do—and I'll get to that, because I watched it happen, and I still have to drink something stronger than tea to talk about it—I did what any sensible man would do.

I went to my chest. The one under my bunk. The one with the false bottom I built myself, with joinery so fine that even I sometimes forget it's there, which is the entire point of a lie, isn't it? The best ones are the ones you've hidden from yourself.

Inside were papers. Old ones. And the smell of smoke on paper that has already been burned once and regretted ever since.

* * *

Let me back up, because if I tell this in order, I'll lose my nerve by the middle, and Lior deserves the whole of it even if she never forgives me for any part of it.

I was nineteen when I came to the Valley of Hollow. A deserter from the Karst War, half-starved, carrying a sword I'd stolen more than earned. The valley took me in the way valleys take in strays: without questions, because questions have a way of becoming answers, and answers have a way of getting people killed here.

That's the thing about Hollow that every mercenary company learns eventually and no one says out loud. Magic doesn't work here. Not *fizzles*, not *misfires*. Doesn't work. A hedge-witch can stand at the border and throw her strongest charm and it will land in the dirt like a dead bird. I've seen it. Everyone's seen it. We call the valley blessed, or cursed, or simply *hollow*, depending on how much we've drunk, and we move on.

Except the founding families didn't move on. They'd been here before there was an *us* to move on from. Nine names, carved into the lintel of the old meetinghouse: Nakamura among them. Nakamura first, actually. First name on the stone, which should have told me something right away about how much weight that family was built to carry.

I found the records my second winter. A cache behind the meetinghouse wall—a dry-stone cavity, deliberate, sealed with wax and prayer-marks I didn't recognize then and recognize now in my nightmares. Ledgers. Correspondence. A covenant written in nine hands, some of them shaking.

It said, in essence: *We are wardens. The valley is a seal. The seal holds shut a door that must never open. To keep the seal, we forget. Our children will not know. That is the price, and we pay it gladly, and they will hate us if they ever learn, and that too is the price.*

I was nineteen years old, hungover, and holding the reason a girl I'd come to love like a sister had grown up in a cage and called it home.

You want to know what I did with that?

I read everything. Twice. Three times. I sat in that cold meetinghouse until my fingers went numb around the pages, learning the shape of a secret that wasn't mine, and then—

Then I made the worst decision of my life, which takes some doing, because I've made several contenders.

I burned most of it. Kept fragments. Told no one.

* * *

Here's what I told myself, and I want it on the record that I believed it, because self-deception only works when you're sincere:

*She's happier not knowing.*

That was the whole argument. That was the fortress. Lior Nakamura, twenty-two years old, quick with a blade and quicker with a joke, laughing over dice in the common room—what good would it do her to learn that her laughter was purchased? That her grandmother had signed away her granddaughter's right to the truth so that a door would stay shut? That the valley's peace was a lid, and she was born underneath it?

No good. No good at all. That's what I decided, alone, at nineteen, with the arrogance of a boy who thought protection was a gift you could give someone without asking.

And then I spent eleven years watching her press her palm flat against the valley's silence like a woman testing a wall for hollow space behind it, and I told myself she was happier.

She pressed that wall every day. I saw it. I just called it something else. Restlessness, I called it. Wanderlust. The mercenary itch. Anything but the truth, which was that she could feel the edges of her own life and couldn't name them, and I *could* have named them, and I chose the lid instead.

* * *

The night after the courier died, I sat across the fire from Lior and watched the map do its work.

Maps don't bleed. This one bled. The ink moved—not smudged, *moved*, the way a wound weeps—redrawing roads that hadn't been there an hour before, and where the fresh lines ran, the parchment darkened like skin bruising. And Lior held it in both hands with the expression of a woman reading a letter from a dead relative, and I knew—I knew with the certainty of a man watching his own arson come home—that the map knew her bloodline better than I ever had.

She looked up. "Bren. You're staring."

"I'm thinking."

"You look constipated when you think. It's why nobody asks your opinion."

"Charming." I fed the fire a stick I didn't need to feed it. "Lior. What do you know about the founding families?"

And here is the moment I could have done it right. Here is the door I could have opened with my own hands instead of letting the world kick it down. She was looking at me across the firelight, tired and sharp and trusting—trusting me, gods help us both, after eleven years of trusting me—and all I had to say was: *Everything. Sit down. It's going to take a while, and you're going to hate me, but you deserve every word.*

What I said was: "Nothing much. Old names. Why?"

Because the Ashen Order's scouts had been sighted at the valley's edge that morning. Because I thought—if I gave her the history tonight, she'd go looking for the Spire tomorrow, straight into whatever those ash-cloaked fanatics wanted her walking into. Because I was still playing protector, still deciding for her, still doing the thing I'd been doing since I was nineteen and calling it love.

She shrugged. "The courier said Veth. Before he died. Thought maybe the old families kept records."

"The meetinghouse has some genealogies," I said. "Births, deaths. Nothing else."

Which was true, technically. The meetinghouse has nothing else. Everything else is in my chest, charred at the corners, because I burned it.

She went back to the map. I went back to my bunk. And I lay there listening to the valley hold its breath around us—the silence that isn't empty, I know that now, the silence that is a hand pressed over a screaming mouth—and I understood that my lie had a lifespan, and it was ending, and I had maybe days to decide whether Lior would hear the truth from me or from the world.

* * *

She found out about the scouts before I could tell her. Of course she did; she's Lior. By noon she'd interrogated two farmers, bribed a third with whiskey, and stood on the eastern ridge counting Ashen riders through a spyglass with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

"They're not raiding," she said that evening. "Raiders burn and leave. These ones are *asking questions*. About a courier. About a map." She looked at me. "About bloodlines, Bren. One of them used that word. Bloodlines."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, Sef—the orphan boy who'd been orbiting our camp for a week like a stubborn little moon—was scratching chalk marks on a stone, mapping a valley he'd never be allowed to forget the way the rest of us had been allowed to.

"Lior," I said.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Whatever face you're making, save it. I've known you eleven years. You make that face when you're about to lie to me for my own good, and I want you to know I can see it coming from a mile off, and I let you, because usually it doesn't matter." Her voice dropped. "This time it matters. Tell me what you're not telling me."

And there it was. Eleven years of careful masonry, and she'd walked up to the wall and put her hand flat against it, the same gesture, the same hollow-testing touch, except this time the wall was me.

I could have lied again. Gods know I had practice. But the riders were at the border, and the map was bleeding, and a dead man had given her a god's name with his last breath, and I looked at her—really looked, at the woman who'd pulled arrows out of me and taught me to laugh again after the war and never once asked me for anything but honesty—and I understood that the choice I'd made at nineteen hadn't protected her. It had just delayed her. And delay, in the end, is just betrayal with better manners.

"There's a chest under my bunk," I said.

Her eyes didn't widen. That was the worst part. She'd known. Some part of her had always known I kept things, and she'd loved me anyway, the way you love a house with locked rooms.

"Get it," she said.

* * *

I laid the fragments out on the table between us while she watched. Charred covenant-pages. A ledger with the Nakamura name at its head. The prayer-marks copied onto scrap by my nineteen-year-old hand because I couldn't bear to burn the writing itself, only the proof of it.

"This is a covenant," I said. "Written by the nine founding families. Your family first." My throat closed. I pushed through it, because she deserved a clean delivery even if she'd never forgive the contents. "They built the valley as a seal. There's a door—there's a *thing*—and the valley keeps it shut. And the price of keeping it shut was memory. They chose to forget, and to make their children forget, generation after generation. You weren't cursed, Lior. You were *posted*. Like a garrison that's never told there's a war."

Silence. The kind of silence the valley specializes in.

"You knew," she said finally. Not a question. "How long?"

"Eleven years."

Something moved behind her face—anger first, hot and fast, and I braced for it, almost welcomed it—but it passed through and came out the other side as something much worse. Grief. She looked at the charred pages, and then at me, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady.

"I thought the silence was mine," she said. "My whole life, I thought the hollowness was something wrong with *me*. Something I failed at. And it was a job. It was a *posting*." She laughed—one broken note, no humor in it. "Did you decide that too? Whether I got to know? Did you sit down at nineteen and weigh my life in your hands and choose?"

"Yes," I said. Because lying to her now, after all of it, would have been the final cruelty. "Yes. I did. And I'd do parts of it differently, but I won't pretend I didn't do it, and I won't pretend I'm sure I was wrong, because the Ashen Order is at our border and maybe—*maybe*—the forgetting kept you alive long enough to run."

"That's not protection, Bren. That's just a smaller cage with you holding the key."

"I know."

"Do you?" She gathered the fragments slowly, squaring their burnt edges with a care that hurt more than slamming them down would have. "Eleven years. Every time I asked about my grandmother's hands—she had these marks, did you know that? Little scars, ritual scars, and everyone said farming accident, and I *knew*—every time I asked, you changed the subject. Every single time. I thought I was imagining it. I thought I was the mad one." She tucked the papers against her chest, my confession become her inheritance. "You don't get to decide what I carry anymore. Nobody does. Not the founders, not the god, not you."

"No," I said. "Nobody does."

Outside, far off toward the eastern ridge, a light bloomed against the dark. Then another. The farms. The Ashen riders had stopped asking questions.

Lior was moving before I finished standing, buckling her sword with hands that didn't shake, and when she paused at the tent flap she didn't look back at me—or rather, she looked back at everything *except* me, at the fire, at the boy with the chalk, at the bleeding map—and she said, quiet as a closing door:

"Saddle up. And Bren—ride beside me if you want. But you ride beside me. Not ahead. Never ahead again."

* * *

We rode east through falling ash that might have been snow and might have been burning farms, and I spent the whole ride composing apologies I'll never deliver, because some fractures don't set clean. You just learn to walk on them.

Behind us the valley held its breath. Ahead of us, past the border I'd never crossed, the world was humming with something I couldn't feel but Lior clearly could—her spine straightening mile by mile like a bow being drawn.

And in my saddlebag, wrapped in oilcloth, the last fragment I hadn't shown her. The page I'd kept back at nineteen, the one line of the covenant I'd never had the courage to read aloud:

*When the seal fails, a warden's blood will call it whole again. Pray it is not yours.*

Her grandmother's handwriting. Her bloodline. Her name, effectively, signed eleven generations before she was born.

I'd carried it for eleven years hoping I'd never have to give it to her.

By morning, I knew I would.
