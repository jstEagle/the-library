## 4. The Boy with the Chalk Map

The first thing you should know about me is that I did not steal the chalk.

Everyone in Hollowbrook assumes I stole it, because orphans are assumed to steal things the way rivers are assumed to run downhill. But Widow Marsh gave it to me two winters ago, along with a slap on the ear and the words "if you draw on my fence again I'll tan your hide." The chalk was hers. My ear was also hers. Only the drawing was mine.

I drew maps. That was my whole talent, if you could call it one, and in Hollowbrook nobody called it anything, which is the polite way of saying they thought it was useless. Maps of what? There was nowhere to go. The Valley of Hollow is shaped like a cupped hand, and everything worth knowing fit inside the palm: the mill, the tannery, the eastern farms, the shrine to nothing where old people left flowers for a god who had never once answered.

But I drew the valley anyway. I drew it on walls, on fences, on the flat stone behind the tannery where rain couldn't reach it. And because I drew it over and over and over, I learned things about it that nobody else bothered to know. That the millstream ran a finger's width further east than anyone's memory of it. That the pilgrim road didn't end at the western pass—it kept going, worn into the rock, like the road itself remembered somewhere else it used to be welcome.

And that the courier came through on the high path, not the low one.

That last fact is how I ended up standing in a burning farmyard at dawn, holding the only copy of a dead man's route, watching Lior Nakamura decide whether to leave me behind.

* * *

Let me back up three days, to when the dying man came down off the ridge.

I saw him first. Not because I was watchful—because I was supposed to be somewhere else, namely Widow Marsh's woodshed, stacking wood I'd promised to stack, and instead I was up on the high path drawing the bend of the river in chalk dust on a flat rock. He came out of the tree line like a sack of wet grain thrown off a cart. One shoulder lower than the other. An arrow through the meat of his thigh. He looked at me and his eyes did something I'd never seen eyes do before: they went past me, all the way through me, hunting for something behind me that wasn't there.

"Valley," he said. "It's true, then. It's real."

"It's a valley," I said, because I was eleven and clever and had no idea yet that cleverness is just stupidity with better timing.

He laughed, which turned into coughing, which brought up blood, and then he grabbed my wrist with a grip that had no business being in a dying arm and said, "Listen. Listen, boy. Are there soldiers behind me? Ashen. Grey cloaks."

"Not yet."

"*Yet.*" He closed his eyes like I'd told him something worse than the wound. "Then there's no time. Get me to the mercenary woman. Nakamura. The quiet one."

"Why her?"

"Because she's the only person in this whole cursed bowl of a valley who still says a god's name out loud, even if she doesn't know she's doing it."

Which confused me, because everyone knows the Valley of Hollow has no gods. That's the whole point of us. Other places have their temples and their tithe-collectors and their little carved idols that need polishing; we have work, weather, and each other, and we're smug about it in the way poor people get smug about not needing much.

But I got him to Lior anyway. I'll tell you why, though I've never told anyone why: because when he said *Nakamura*, I remembered that Lior's maps—the ones I'd seen her studying at the tavern, rolled and tied, marked with a sigil like a bird with folded wings—had that same name written small in the corner. *Nakamura.* A mapmaker signs her maps. It's the first rule of the trade. You don't put your name on something unless you mean to be found by it.

So I figured the courier hadn't come looking for a mercenary. He'd come looking for a signature.

* * *

He died that night, in the back room of the Wayhouse, with Lior's hand pressed over the wound like she could hold his blood in by will alone. Before he went, he gave her something—a roll of leather—and said one word that I heard clear across the room even though I was pretending to sleep by the fire:

"Veth."

Lior flinched. I want to be honest about this, since honesty is apparently my one redeeming feature: she flinched the way you flinch when someone says your secret out loud in a market. Not surprise. Recognition.

After that, everything happened fast, the way bad things do. The map redrew itself—Lior showed Bren, and Bren went grey as porridge, and the two of them had a fight so quiet it was louder than shouting, all held breath and doors closed too carefully. Riders were seen at the valley's edge wearing grey cloaks and asking after a courier. And then, on the third morning, the eastern farms started burning.

I was on the roof of the tannery when I saw the smoke. Three columns, maybe four, rising off the far fields in the windless cold air, straight as chalk lines. And beneath them, tiny with distance, riders moving in formation—not looting, not raiding, but *burning*, methodical as sowers, row after row.

They weren't trying to take the valley. They were trying to smoke out whatever was hiding in it.

I climbed down and I ran, and I don't remember deciding to run; my legs made that decision without consulting me, which is how most of my best decisions get made. I found them at the west gate—Lior, Bren, two horses, packs already cinched. Leaving. This morning. Without telling anyone, especially me.

"No," I said.

Lior looked at me the way you look at weather that has developed opinions. "Sef. Go home."

"I don't have a home. I have a woodshed, and the widow charges me for it in chores, and there's going to be ash where the farms were by noon, and those riders are coming here next, and you're the only ones leaving, so *no*."

Bren made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Lior didn't laugh. She crouched to my level, which people do to make themselves seem kind and actually makes them seem taller, somehow, looming in miniature.

"Sef. What's behind us is going to be a war. What's ahead of us might be worse. I'm not taking a child into that."

"I memorized the courier's route."

Something changed in her face. Small, like a draft under a door.

"He took the high path," I said, talking fast now, the way you do when you can feel your chance sliding. "Not the low road—everyone thinks the low road, because it's wider, but he came over Sorrow Ridge and down by the split oak, and I know because I watched him from the rocks and I've drawn that path forty times. Forty. I can draw you every ford, every shelter cave, every place the pilgrim road narrows enough for three men to hold off thirty. Can your map do that? Your map redraws itself and doesn't tell you why. Mine tells me *everything*, because I put it there myself."

Silence. Behind us, faintly, the smell of smoke arrived before the wind did, like a messenger running ahead of an army.

"You're eleven," Lior said.

"I'm twelve in the spring."

"That's not—"

"And I'm fast, and I'm quiet, and I'm light, and I don't eat much, and I already follow you around anyway, you just never noticed, which means I'm *good at it*."

She stood up. Looked at Bren. Bren looked at the sky, the smoke, the road—anywhere but at me, which was its own kind of answer—and finally said, "Lior. The farms are burning. There's nothing behind us to send him back *to*."

And that was the moment. I saw it happen. I watched Lior Nakamura, who is famous for having a face like a locked door, open that door about a finger's width, and look through it, and see exactly what I'd been counting on her seeing: that sending me back wasn't mercy. Sending me back was just choosing which fire I died in.

Her jaw did a thing. Her hand did another thing—I think she almost reached for my head and thought better of it, and I have thought about that unfinished gesture more times than I'll admit, including right now.

"Ride behind Bren," she said. "You fall off, we don't stop. You slow us down, I tie you to a fence post. You touch my saddlebags, I sell you to the tanners."

"Yes," I said, glowing like a lantern.

"I mean all of it."

"All of it," I agreed, and swung up behind Bren before she could hear her own good sense calling her back.

* * *

We made six miles before the sun cleared the ridgeline, and that's when we heard them: hooves, many, coming up the valley road behind us. Not chasing us. Chasing *everyone*. Bren pulled up on the rise above Hollowbrook and we all turned, the way you turn even when you shouldn't, and looked back.

The riders had reached the village outskirts. I couldn't see faces at that distance. I could see fire, though. I could see the mill go up—that beautiful, stupid mill whose crooked wheel I'd drawn so many times I could do it blind—and I could see people spilling out of houses like startled birds, and I gripped the back of Bren's coat and felt him go rigid under my hands.

"The shrine," I said, without meaning to say it out loud. "They're burning the shrine first."

Nobody keeps anything in our shrine. No idol, no relic, no god. Just flowers, and names scratched into the stone by people who hoped someone was listening. And the Ashen riders rode straight to it and put it to the torch anyway, like they hated the *silence* itself, like an empty room offended them more than a full one.

"They think something's here," Bren said quietly. "Under the quiet. Something they want."

"Then let them dig," Lior said, and turned her horse west, and there was iron in her voice but her knuckles were white on the reins, and I noticed that, because noticing is what I do.

We rode. The valley fell away behind us, smoking, and I did the only thing I knew how to do with grief, which was to start mapping it: the shape of the smoke, the spacing of the riders, the lie of the land ahead. Somewhere in my coat pocket was my stub of chalk, and somewhere in my head was the growing certainty that the world outside the valley—the world I'd never drawn because I'd never seen it—was about to become the biggest map of my life.

By midday we hit Sorrow Ridge, and Lior reined in at the top, and none of us spoke, because below us the valley lay cupped in its hills like a coin in a palm, and beyond its western edge the land changed. I can't describe how it changed. The colors were the same. The trees were trees. But it looked *loud*. It looked like a room where somebody had just stopped talking.

"What is it?" I asked.

Lior stared down at the border for a long time, and when she answered, she said it mostly to herself, mostly to whatever god she pretended she didn't keep:

"It's breathing."
