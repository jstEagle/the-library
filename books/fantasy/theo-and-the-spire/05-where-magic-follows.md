## 5. Where Magic Follows

The Valley of Hollow ended at a river so narrow I could have jumped it as a girl, which is exactly the kind of joke the world likes to make when it's about to ruin your life.

Three days out from the burning farms, we came down through the last fold of hills and there it was: the Sereth, all of six feet wide in the shallows, chuckling over its stones like nothing had ever been asked of it. On our side, brown grass. On the far side, brown grass. No wall of fire. No line of warning stones. Just water, and the smell of pine coming down from the mountains beyond, and Sef asking for the fourth time whether we could stop soon because his boots were rubbing blisters.

"That's it?" Bren said.

"That's it," I said.

"It doesn't look like the edge of the world."

"Most terrible things don't. That's how they get you."

I crouched at the bank and put my fingers in the water. Cold enough to bite. I'd crossed this river a dozen times in my life—on contracts, on errands, once running from a man in Corren Hollow whose wife I'd made a widow and who wanted to make me one of something. Every time, I'd come back feeling like I'd walked through a curtain of wet wool. Heavy. Dull. Like the world on the other side was louder and I was glad to be deaf.

I'd always blamed the altitude.

Sef sat down beside me and dangled a hand over the water without touching it, the way he did everything—testing, measuring, committing nothing to paper until he'd committed it to memory first. The boy had a chalk stub in his pocket and three maps folded into his shirt that he treated like scripture. One of them was the courier's route, drawn from what he'd watched Theo trace with a dying finger. One was the valley, every farm and fencepost. And the third—

The third was us. Four stick figures and a horse. He hadn't shown it to me directly, but I'd caught him checking it at night by ember-light, touching each figure in turn like a man counting coins he couldn't afford to lose.

"Is it true," he said, "that magic can't follow you across?"

"Can't or won't. Nobody in the valley knows the difference. That's rather the point of the valley."

"And on this side?" He nodded at the far bank.

"On this side, everything follows you." I stood and wiped my hand on my coat. "Whether you invite it or not."

Bren brought the horse down the slope, and we crossed.

* * *

I want to tell you it happened dramatically. That the air split open, that a voice spoke, that the sky did something theatrical. The gods I've met—and I've now met more than most priests, though fewer than most corpses—do not care for drama. They care for timing.

So no. We crossed the Sereth, climbed the far bank, walked maybe forty paces into the pines, and then the world *breathed in.*

That is the only way I can describe it. Imagine standing your whole life inside a bell that never rang. You know the shape of it. You know the weight of the bronze around you. You've stopped noticing because you were born inside it. And then someone strikes it—not hard, just once, a single low tone that goes up through your boot soles and your teeth and the soft meat behind your eyes—and you understand, all at once and forever, that everything before was silence, and that the silence had a *shape*, and that the shape was holding you.

I stopped walking. Sef bumped into my back.

"Lior?" Bren said. "What is it?"

I didn't answer, because answering required language, and language suddenly seemed like a very small tool for a very large job. The forest was humming. Not sound—humming. The trees were full of something moving the way sap moves, slow and green and patient, and the ground under my feet was full of it too, and the air itself was thick with it, a pressure against my skin like walking into deep water. Magic. Actual magic, unhidden, unsealed, everywhere, the way water is everywhere inside a lake. In the valley we had none of it. Out here it wasn't a thing you found. It was a thing you swam in.

And it noticed me.

I don't know how else to say that. It turned, the way a crowd turns when a door opens. All that humming, all that patient green attention, gathered itself toward me like iron filings finding their magnet, and I felt it settle against my spine and the nape of my neck—a touch, cool and enormous, the way you'd rest a hand on something you were deciding whether to keep.

A god's hand on the back of my neck. That's what it felt like. That's what it was.

"Not here," I said, and my voice came out flat and strange, and I started walking again, fast, up the trail, because if I stood still one more second I was going to do something embarrassing like kneel, or scream, or both.

They followed. Of course they followed. That was the whole problem with found family—it comes with opinions and legs.

* * *

We made camp that night in the hollow beneath a granite shelf, and I sat apart, the way I always do, except this time sitting apart meant sitting alone with a god's attention instead of merely alone with myself. Which is worse. I recommend solitude. It has better manners.

The map was warm against my ribs. I'd taken to carrying it inside my coat since the farms burned; some instincts you don't argue with. When I finally pulled it out by ember-light, the lines had moved again. They did that every night now, redrawing themselves in that slow blood-colored ink, and every night the route grew more specific, the Spire at its end drawn taller, more certain. Tonight there was something new: a small mark near where we'd camped. A circle. A point of attention.

It knows where I am, I thought. The map isn't leading me to the Spire. It's leading the Spire's attention to me.

"You felt it too."

Bren. He'd come around the shelf without a sound, which after twenty years together should have stopped surprising me and never had. He sat down heavily, joints cracking, an old man's body wrapped around a soldier's reflexes. He looked at the map in my hands and didn't ask about it, which was its own kind of conversation.

"The hum," he said. "When we crossed. You went white as milk."

"I felt a hand on my neck."

Whose hand, I didn't say. Whose, I didn't have to. Bren had grown up in the valley same as me, raised on the same godless pragmatism, taught to sharpen steel and distrust prophecy in equal measure. But he'd carried something extra all these years—I'd seen it, the flinch whenever anyone said Veth's name, the way he'd gone quiet the night Theo died. There were things Bren knew that Bren hadn't told me, and the map's ink bleeding fresh every night had a way of making old silences feel deliberate.

"In the valley," I said, "there's nothing. No hum, no hand. I used to think that was peace." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Twenty years thinking I was free of gods. Turns out I was living inside a held breath."

"A held breath," Bren repeated.

"Veth's silence." I tapped my own sternum, where the quiet god lived the way a scar lives—present, painless, permanent. "Every prayer I ever sent him hit a ceiling. I thought he wasn't listening. Now I cross a river and the whole world puts its hand on me like I'm something it lost track of, and I understand—the silence wasn't absence. Silence is what you get when something is holding itself shut. Holding *something else* shut. He wasn't ignoring me, Bren. He was gagged. Or busy. Or both."

Bren was silent long enough that the fire popped twice.

"There are things I should tell you," he said finally. "About the valley. About your family."

"Not tonight."

"Lior—"

"Bren." I folded the map away. "Tomorrow I will want every word. Tonight I have a god touching the back of my neck and I would like to pretend, for approximately six hours, that I am still just a mercenary with a bad knee and a drinking problem. Let me keep that."

He let me. It was the last kind thing either of us would manage for a while, though neither of us knew it yet.

* * *

Sef woke me near dawn, which was becoming his role in the family: small alarm, cold hands, terrible news delivered at volume.

"Lior. Lior. The trees."

I came up with a knife already in my hand, which startled him less than it should have—he'd been living with mercenaries for weeks; knives at dawn were practically breakfast. But there was nothing to fight. He grabbed my sleeve and pointed east, through the pines, where the light was coming up grey and wrong.

Frost. Frost in high summer, silvering every branch, breathing off the ground in low white ribbons. And not random frost—it lay in patterns, spirals, great slow whorls like fingerprints pressed into the land by something vast. The humming under the world had changed key overnight. Lower. Strained. Somewhere far north, in the direction of the Spire, the pressure against my neck had shifted from a resting hand to a grip.

"What is it?" Sef whispered. He'd stopped being afraid of me months ago but he still whispered around magic, the way people whisper in churches they don't believe in.

I didn't know. I knew only that the breath Veth was holding had begun to shake—that somewhere ahead, the thing in the dark was pushing against the door, and the door was old, and the door knew my name.

"Pack up," I said. "We walk faster today."

Sef got out his chalk and drew the frost-spirals in his little book before they melted, quick sure strokes, because that's what Sef did: he wrote down the world so it couldn't be taken from him. Then we broke camp, and the three of us and one horse went north along the pilgrim road, toward the Spire, while behind us the frost spread south across the border of the valley like fingers testing a seal—

—and far off, thin as a wire, I heard—for the first time in my life, I *heard*—the sound of Veth beginning to speak.
