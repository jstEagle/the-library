## 6. The Ashen Vigil

They took me at the ford, which was embarrassing, because I'm the one who taught Lior never to cross a ford in the open.

To be fair to myself, I wasn't crossing it. I was doubling back along it, checking our trail like a sensible man, when the net came off the willow branches and the world went upside down. Six of them. Ashen-grey cloaks, ash-white brands on their cheeks, the whole funeral procession. They must have split from the main column days ago and walked the river instead of the road. Clever. I made a mental note to be furious about it later, assuming there was a later.

The tall one with the brand shaped like a broken wheel crouched beside me while I hung there swaying. He studied my face the way a man studies a horse he's thinking of buying.

"Bren," he said.

I hadn't told him my name. That's the part that still itches. "You have me at a disadvantage."

"Corvane. We've been reading your campfires for four nights. You burn green wood on the downwind side so the smoke drifts into the trees. A soldier's habit." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Only someone trained by the Valley companies burns like that."

"Or someone who hates dry wood."

"Take him up," Corvane said, standing. "Carefully. He's worth more unbroken."

Which, I'll say this for the Ashen Order: they kept their word about the carefully part, right up until they didn't.

* * *

Their vigil-camp sat in a hollow below an old waystation, all grey tents pitched in concentric rings like ripples around a stone. Everything about them was quiet in a way that reminded me unpleasantly of home — of the Valley. They moved softly. They spoke little. And they prayed constantly, lips moving over some litany I couldn't catch, hands pressed flat against their own sternums as if holding something in.

They put me in the waystation itself, which had a roof, which I appreciated, and iron staples in the wall for my wrists, which I didn't. The first day they fed me well and asked me nothing. The second day they did the same. By the third I was nearly climbing the walls with the particular madness of being ignored by people you're trying to deceive.

It was Corvane who finally came, on the fourth night, carrying two cups of something steaming and no weapons visible, which in my experience means every weapon is visible.

"You're her shield-arm," he said. "Lior Nakamura. The courier reached her."

"Courier? I mostly remember a man bleeding."

"He reached her." Corvane set one cup within my reach and drank from his own, unhurried, like we were two old friends watching weather come in. "His name was Aldous Vane. He carried the map out of the Spire country because he believed someone in the Hollow would listen." A pause. "He was my brother."

Well. There it was. I'd been turning over a dozen stories in my head for four days and none of them had room for that.

"I'm sorry for your loss," I said, because whatever else was true, a man had died in the mud asking for help, and grief is grief.

"So am I." He said it plainly, without performance, and that frightened me more than any interrogation would have. Zealots perform. This man just hurt. "Tell me what she believes, Bren. Tell me what the Valley told her about the seal."

"The Valley doesn't tell anyone anything. That's rather its whole personality."

"What do *you* believe?"

I looked at the cup. Looked at him. Decided that if I was going to hang by my wrists in a ruin for a man's amusement, I might as well get the whole shape of the thing.

"I believe the seal keeps something shut that should stay shut. I believe your Order wants it open. I believe those two facts make us enemies, and I'd very much like to keep it that simple."

"And if I told you we don't want it open?" Corvane set down his cup. "If I told you we want it *unlocked*?"

"There's a difference?"

"A door locked is a prison. A door opened is an invitation. But a door unlocked—" his voice went soft, reverent, and the whole grey camp seemed to lean in with him, "—a door unlocked is a *return*. The god behind that seal isn't a monster, Bren. It's Veth's prisoner, yes. But it's also Veth's *twin*. The mercy that Veth cut away to make itself silent. The half of the divine that could weep, that could forgive, that could raise the dead. Veth buried its own compassion because compassion grieves, and grief makes gods weak. We don't serve war. We serve the mercy that was thrown away."

I want to tell you I laughed. I want to tell you I gave him the speech Lior would have given, all flint and contempt, and that I slept soundly after.

What I actually did was feel the floor tilt.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about lies: the good ones are built out of true things. And everything he'd said fit. It fit the silence too well. Why would a god go mute for centuries over a mere beast in a box? Beasts don't require sacrifice of *voice*. You muzzle a dog; you don't cut out its tongue and swear an eternal vow of quiet unless what's in the cage shares your blood.

And the courier — Aldous — dying in our valley, whispering *Veth*, not *help*, not *run*. Whispering the jailer's name like a prayer or an apology.

"You believe that," I said finally. "That's the worst part. You actually believe it."

"We watched the Vigil-litany work, Bren. Our wounded pray to the Buried Mercy and their fevers break. Coincidence, once. Twice. Forty years of it." His eyes were bright now, feverish in their own quiet way. "Aldous thought the same as you, once. Then he read what we'd read. He went to see for himself. What he found at the Spire broke him — not because the thing inside was horrible." Corvane leaned close. "Because it was *weeping*."

The fire cracked somewhere outside. My wrists ached in the irons. And I sat with the vertigo of a man whose map has been redrawn overnight — the same roads, the same landmarks, but north and south quietly swapped.

"They'll kill her," I heard myself say. "Your people. If they can't turn her, they'll open the Spire over her body, and whatever's in there—"

"—will be welcomed," Corvane said. "By its faithful. As it always should have been."

* * *

They left me alone after that, which was either mercy or a mistake, and I've built a life on assuming mistakes.

Here's what I know about rope, irons, and old waystations: the irons were new, but the wall wasn't. The Order had reinforced the staples with fresh mortar, and fresh mortar in a frost month is mortar that hasn't finished being stone. I spent two nights working the staple with a rhythm timed to the sentries' rounds, and on the third night the frost got into the joints and the whole bracket sighed out of the wall like it had been waiting for permission.

Getting past the ring of tents was harder. Getting past the *prayer* was hardest — because halfway across the camp, hugging the shadow of the woodpile, I heard the litany clearly for the first time, dozens of voices murmuring it low and constant, and I caught the words.

*Buried Mercy, weeping twin, forgive the silence, forgive the silence, we dig toward your voice.*

Not war-hymns. Not blood-chants. A lullaby. An apology, sung nightly for forty years, to something under the ground.

I ran anyway. Let's be clear about that — I ran, cold sweat and burning lungs, back through the frost-killed fields toward the pass where Lior and Sef would be making their slow, stubborn way east. Because belief, even sincere belief, even *grieving* belief, doesn't change what happens when you unlock a cage to comfort what's inside it.

But I ran with a splinter in my mind I couldn't pull free:

The Order thinks they're digging toward mercy. Lior thinks she's guarding a monster. And both of them can't be right — but both of them can't be wrong, either, and I had seen enough of the world's seams by then to know that the worst truths wear two faces.

There was one more thing. On my way out, past the prayer-tents, I'd seen the Order's maps pinned in a command tent flap left open to the wind. Their route to the Spire. And marked on it, in fresh red ink, a date.

Three weeks. They expected the seal to fail in three weeks.

Either they knew something about the Spire we didn't —

or something in the dark was already counting down for them.
