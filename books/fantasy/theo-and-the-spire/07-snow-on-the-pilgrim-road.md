## 7. Snow on the Pilgrim Road

The snow started on the ninth day out of the valley, which was wrong for two reasons. First, it wasn't winter yet — I knew because I'd been counting days since the farms burned, and counting is the one thing orphans are better at than anybody. Second, it fell upward for a few minutes before it fell down, and Lior said a word in a language she claimed not to speak, and Bren went very quiet in the way he does when he's deciding whether to lie to us.

"That's normal," Bren said.

"It's snowing *up*," Lior said.

"Normal-ish."

I liked the snow at first, I'll admit that. I'd never seen it. In the Valley of Hollow we got rain and mud and one memorable hailstorm that broke the temple roof, but never snow, because the valley kept everything out and apparently weather was included in that. The first flakes landed on my sleeve and held their shape like tiny stars, and I thought: this is what magic looks like. Then the flakes started landing wrong. On the north side of trees. In drifts shaped like spirals. Once, in the shape of a handprint, pressed into the road ahead of us, as if something enormous had walked by in the opposite direction and left its opinion behind.

Lior stopped sleeping well after that. She'd sit up with her sword across her knees and stare at the dark, and when I asked what she was listening for she said, "Nothing," which was a lie, because Veth is the god of nothing, and lately I'd started to understand that "nothing" was the busiest word there was.

I'm getting ahead of myself. Sef's rules for surviving a bad road: keep moving, keep eating, keep your people where you can see them. By day nine my people were a mercenary who didn't talk much, a liar who talked too much, and a map that bled. It wasn't the family I'd have picked. It was the family that had picked me back, which I learned later is rarer and worth more.

* * *

The priestess found us before we found her hut, which should have been the first warning. She stepped out of the treeline with an armful of firewood and no footprints behind her, an old woman wrapped in grey wool, and she looked at Lior the way you look at a door you've been knocking on for years.

"You're late," she said.

"We weren't coming," Lior said.

"Everyone's coming eventually." The woman shifted the wood to her hip and nodded toward the ridge, where the snow was falling in a slow, deliberate spiral, like it was being poured. "Storm's going to eat the pass. You can freeze on principle or you can come inside. Your choice. I've made both."

Her name was Mother Ossa, though she told us not to call her Mother because, as she put it, "I was defrocked so thoroughly they scraped the word off the ledgers." Bren knew of her — of course he did, Bren knows of everyone — and the knowing went badly at first.

"The Ashen Order lists you as a heretic," he said.

"The Order lists anyone who reads as a heretic." She kicked the door open with her heel. "Come in before the snow decides to come in first."

Inside was warm in a way that felt watched rather than earned. A fire that burned without smoking. Herbs hanging from beams, arranged in patterns I recognized from my chalk maps of temple walls — wards, drawn in dried things instead of ink. And on a shelf above the hearth, a row of stones, each one the size of a fist, each one faintly glowing, like coals that had forgotten how to be hot.

"What are those?" I asked.

Ossa followed my eyes. Something crossed her face — grief, maybe, or the memory of it. "Memory-stones. Sermons, if you like. The kind you can't argue with." She took one down and turned it in her hands. "Your Order burns books, boy, because paper tells the truth politely. These tell it rudely."

"I'm not—"

"You draw maps on walls. That's reading. That's writing. Don't let anyone tell you different." She set the stone on the table between us, and looked at Lior. "You especially need to see this one. You came through the border feeling it, didn't you? The hum. Like a hand on your neck."

Lior's hand went to the back of her neck. She didn't answer. She didn't have to.

"Bren," Ossa said, "you can leave the room if you like. You've clearly been practicing not knowing things for years, and I wouldn't want to interrupt the discipline."

Bren stayed. He sat down slowly, the way old soldiers sit, like every chair might be a trap. And Ossa pressed her palms flat against the stone, and it lit.

I don't know how to describe what came out of it except that it wasn't pictures exactly, and it wasn't sound exactly, but it got into you anyway, the way cold gets into boots. We were standing somewhere else. Somewhere enormous. A plain under a black sky, and a figure walking across it — I couldn't see its face, but I could feel it, the way you feel someone watching through a window. It sang while it walked, and the song was beautiful, and the beauty was the worst part, because everything it sang about stopped existing. Fields it passed went grey. Birds mid-flight forgot how wings worked. It wasn't angry. That was the horror of it. It was *hungry*, and hunger doesn't hate what it eats.

And then another figure rose up to meet it. Smaller. Dimmer. Made of quiet the way the first one was made of song.

They met in the middle of the plain, and the quiet figure opened — I don't have a better word — opened like a door, and the singing thing poured into it, and the door closed, and became a tower. A spire. I'd seen its shape before. So had Lior. It was inked on the map that bled, night after night, redrawing itself in her blood.

The vision held one image more: the quiet figure, standing at the base of the tower it had become the prison of, opening its mouth to speak one last word — and no word coming. Silence, taken whole, like a tooth pulled. The price. The god Veth, who had swallowed the devourer, paying with its own voice forever.

Then it was just a stone on a table again, and the fire crackling, and none of us breathing.

"That's the polite version," Ossa said quietly. "The full version takes three days and leaves most people unable to eat for a week."

"The Order says the Spire holds a god of mercy," Bren said. His voice was strange, flattened, like a man walking on ice he didn't trust. "That breaking the seal frees it. That it will heal the world."

"And I say the Order's high priest has never stood where you just stood." Ossa leaned forward. "Or if he has, he's decided a beautiful lie is kinder than the truth. Mercy, war — those are names men give gods. What's in that spire doesn't want worship. It wants *everything*. Worship is just the shape hunger takes when it needs hands."

Lior hadn't moved since the vision ended. Her blood-map lay against her chest under her coat, and I saw her press her palm over it, over the place it lived, the way you hold a wound.

"My whole life," she said, "the valley was silent. No magic. No gods answering. I prayed to Veth for years and got nothing, and I told myself that meant nothing was there."

"It meant everything was there," Ossa said. "Held breath isn't absence, girl. It's the loudest thing in the world. Your god gave up its voice to cage that thing, and then your ancestors gave up their memories to build the seal around it, and then the valley itself was folded shut so no one would ever come looking. Three layers of sacrifice, all so the world could go on not knowing why it sleeps so easily." She tapped the cooling stone. "And now the courier dies in your arms, the map finds your blood, and the Ashen Order marches on a spire they think is a cradle. You're the last warden whether you agreed to the job or not."

"I didn't agree to anything."

"No one ever does. That's what makes it sacred."

I didn't understand all of it, then. I understood enough. I understood that the snow outside was falling wrong because the thing in the spire was pressing against its prison, and the prison was leaking, and leaks are how I lost my parents' house in the flood year — small, at first, ignorable, until the whole wall comes down. I understood that the woman across the fire from me was carrying something in her blood that the world needed, and that the price of it was written on the shelf of stones in front of us, because memory was currency here, and the exchange rate was terrible.

What I did next, I did without thinking, which is either my best quality or my worst; ask me on my deathbed and I'll still be deciding.

I moved around the table and sat down next to Lior. Not touching her. Just close. Close enough that she'd know I was there.

She glanced at me sideways. "You should be scared of me, kid. Everyone in that vision should make you run."

"I ran once already," I said. "From the farms. Didn't help. Things catch you faster when you run alone." I shrugged, because shrugging is how boys our age say important things without dying of it. "Besides. You're the warden. Somebody's got to draw the warden a map."

Something happened to her face — not a smile, exactly. The place a smile goes when it can't quite afford the fare. She reached over and knocked her knuckles gently against mine, once, like a promise tapping on a door.

Bren cleared his throat. "For what it's worth," he said to Ossa, gruff, "I burned the records of the founding families. Fifteen years ago. If the Order learns Lior's line was warden-blood—"

"They'll stop chasing the map," Lior said, "and start chasing me."

"They may already have," Ossa said. She rose and went to the window, and pulled the hide covering aside, and the firelight spilled out onto snow that had gone utterly still. Not falling. Hanging. Thousands of flakes suspended in the air like the world had paused to listen.

"Storm's stopped," she said. "It shouldn't do that. Storms don't stop; they die or they wait." She let the hide fall. "Whatever's leaning on that seal just noticed you, girl. Eat something. Sleep if you can. Because tomorrow we ride for the Spire, and I'm coming with you — defrocked, heretical, and rude — because someone has to teach you what the stones know before you're standing in front of that door with your hand on it."

Lior looked at the stone. At the map against her heart. At me, sitting stubbornly beside her like a burr she couldn't shake loose.

"One more sermon first," she said.

Ossa smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "Oh, I've got a whole shelf."

Outside, the hanging snow began, very softly, to fall upward.
