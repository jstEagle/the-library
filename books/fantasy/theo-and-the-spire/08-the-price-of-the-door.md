## 8. The Price of the Door

The Spire did not look like a prison. That was my first thought, and it felt like an insult to everyone who'd died keeping it shut.

It looked like a needle of black glass driven through the crown of a mountain, thin enough that it should have snapped under its own weight ten thousand years ago. Snow streamed sideways around it in a spiral, as though the weather itself were being wound onto a spindle. No doors. No windows. Just that impossible slenderness, humming at the edge of hearing—the sound I had spent twenty-nine years mistaking for silence.

"Veth," I said, out loud, because it seemed rude not to.

The hum shifted. Not an answer. An acknowledgment. A god clearing its throat after a very long time of holding its breath.

Sef pressed against my side, chalk stub clutched in his fist like a knife. He'd drawn the Spire on three different walls across four hundred miles, and every drawing had been wrong in exactly one way—too short, too wide, too *kind*. He stared up at the real thing and didn't reach for the chalk.

"It's smaller than I drew it," he said finally.

"Everything is, from far away."

"No." His voice was flat, the way it went when he was frightened and refusing to perform fear for anyone's comfort. "I mean it's *smaller*. Like it's holding itself small. Like—" He stopped, jaw working. "Like someone squeezing a scream down to a whistle."

Out of the mouths of orphans. Bren, hauling himself up the last stretch of switchback with his bad knee complaining in a dialect only he understood, shot me a look over the boy's head. *You heard that too,* the look said. *We're all going to die, aren't we.*

*Probably,* my look answered. *But let's be professional about it.*

* * *

The door found us before we found it.

We made camp in the lee of a shattered pilgrim shrine—its statue long since worn faceless by wind, which struck me as either vandalism or mercy—and at moonrise the mountain simply grew a doorway where there had been rock. Black glass thinned into transparency, then into an arch, then into a hall lit by nothing at all, and standing in it, faint as breath on a window, a woman.

She wore robes the color of old ash. Her face was mine.

Not *like* mine. Mine, aged forty years, with lines carved around eyes that had seen whatever I was walking toward and kept walking anyway.

"Lior Nakamura," she said, and her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way Veth's non-voice lived in my skull. "Warden-blood. Last of nine families who agreed to forget."

"Let me guess," I said. My mouth does this thing when I'm terrified; it turns into Bren's mouth, all sarcasm and armor. "You're here to tell me there's paperwork."

"There is always paperwork," she said gravely, and for one absurd heartbeat I liked her. "The founders left a message for whoever came last. I am the shape that message wears. I am what your blood remembers, even when you do not."

Bren stepped forward, hand raised, and she flicked her gaze to him and said, without heat: "Ash-burner. You burned the records to spare her the knowing. It was a kind act done the coward's way, and it bought her eleven good years. Sit down."

He sat down. I'd never seen Bren sit down on command in fifteen years of soldiery. I filed that away for later appreciation.

"The seal is failing," she said, turning back to me. "It was woven from the voices of nine wardens and the voice of Veth. Eight of those threads snapped generations ago. The ninth—that was your grandmother's line. Your line. It holds because you live, and it frays because you do not know you are holding it. A rope cannot bear weight it doesn't believe exists."

"So teach me to hold it."

"That is not how the bargain was written." She folded her hands, and the gesture was so precisely my own mother's—my dead, unremarkable, valley-born mother—that my chest seized. "The original wardens asked Veth: *let us keep the devourer shut, but let us keep our lives.* Veth could grant one fully and one only partially. So they kept their lives and paid in remembering. Each renewal demands the same. Whoever rebinds the seal must give up everything they love. Not their lives. Their loves. The memories go into the weave. Love is the strongest thread we have. It was always the currency. Survival has always been paid for by someone."

The wind screamed past the shrine. Somewhere below us, miles down the pilgrim road, I imagined I could hear hoofbeats, but that might have been my heart learning a new rhythm.

"And if no one pays?"

"Then the door opens, and the devouring god wakes hungry, and the war that Veth ended at the cost of its own voice begins again—with interest." The ghost-that-was-me tilted her head. "You have been paying your whole life already, child. You simply weren't given the receipt."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"You think the Valley of Hollow chose *you* randomly? You think the silent god marked you at birth out of sentiment? Every generation, one child of warden-blood feels Veth most keenly. Hears the silence loudest. Prays to nothing and is somehow answered. We called it faith. It was maintenance. You were the valve. The grief you've carried since childhood, the loneliness you couldn't name—" her smile was terrible in its tenderness, "—that was the leak. You were born already bleeding for this door. The question is only whether you'll bleed *knowingly*."

I sat down next to Bren. My legs had decided the matter without consulting me.

* * *

Sef wouldn't sleep. He sat at the edge of camp sharpening a stick with his knife, making a point of the noise, and eventually I gave up pretending to rest and went to sit beside him.

"Go to bed," he said.

"It's my camp. I'll stay up if I want."

"You're going to do something stupid tomorrow." He didn't look at me. Ten-year-olds are the finest lie detectors God ever failed to invent. "Bren keeps doing his quiet crying, and you keep doing your quiet deciding, and neither of you thinks I can hear it, and I can hear everything."

"Sef—"

"I know about the price." Now he looked at me, and his eyes were wet and furious, furious at being wet. "The lady-in-the-door talks loud when she wants to. Everything you love. That's the toll. And you're going to pay it, because that's the kind of person you are, and I hate it. I hate that the first—" his voice cracked, and he hated that more, "—the first people who wanted me, I'm going to lose to a *door*."

Something in my ribs came loose and would not go back.

"Come here," I said, and he fought it for exactly two seconds and then crashed into me like a small, bony avalanche. I held him while the snow wound its spiral around the Spire and the hum beneath the world held its endless note.

"Listen to me," I said into his hair. "Whatever happens tomorrow. However it goes. You drew me a map once—of the journey, of us, everything. Maps don't need the mapmaker to remember them. They just need someone to carry them. If I forget you, Sef, that's not the same as the family not having happened. The family happened. Nothing un-happens a thing."

"That's a stupid philosophy."

"It's the best one I've got."

"It's still stupid." But his grip tightened, and we stayed like that until his breathing evened out and Bren limped over wordlessly and took the watch I hadn't officially been relieved of.

* * *

They came an hour before dawn. Of course they did. Villains and dawn raids—it's practically a guild requirement.

Bren woke me with a hand over my mouth and his eyes saying what his lips couldn't. Below us, torchlight snaked up the pilgrim road like a burning vein: fifty riders, maybe more, white-and-grey livery gone orange in the firelight. At their head rode a figure on a pale horse whose armor drank the torchlight rather than catching it, and even at this distance I felt the man's certainty like heat off a forge.

The high priest. The courier's brother. The man who believed—or had been taught to believe—that breaking the seal would resurrect a god of mercy.

"He won't listen," Bren murmured. "I got half-truths out of them when they held me, and half-truths is generous. Their whole religion is a misread epitaph. You show them the devouring god's cage and they see a tomb waiting to be opened."

"People see what pays them to see."

"Yeah, well." He passed me my sword. His hands were steady, which meant he was more frightened than he'd ever been in his life. Fifteen years of reading that man. "What's the play, Captain?"

I looked at the door of black glass, standing patient and awful in the pre-dawn dark. I thought about a rope bearing weight it doesn't believe exists. I thought about a boy asleep by a dead fire, clutching a stick he'd been sharpening into a weapon to protect people twice his size.

I thought about my grandmother, whom I had never met, who had carried this same weight without ever being told she carried it, who had died not knowing why her heart ached in the night, and I was suddenly so angry—at the founders, at Veth, at every generation that had chosen ignorance as anesthesia—that the anger burned clean through the fear and left something cold and workable behind.

"The play," I said, "is that I go in alone and pay the bill."

"Lior—"

"Bren." I gripped his forearm, soldier-style, and held it longer than soldierly. "Fifty riders. You and Sef can't stop them. But a door that eats love doesn't care about swords—if I'm inside when they break through, they break through to a finished job. Buy me time. That's all. Buy me time, and get him out, and don't you dare let him watch."

"You're asking me to leave you."

"I'm asking you to do the brave thing instead of the loyal one. For once. Grow into it."

His jaw worked. His eyes shone. And then, because he was Bren, because he was the other half of every hard day I'd ever survived: "Eleven years I kept this from you so you could have a life. One morning to buy you forever. Fine. Fine. But you come out the other side of that door, Nakamura, or I will burn every record in the world looking for a way to make you remember me, and I am *very* thorough."

"First honest threat you've ever made me," I said. "I'm proud."

Below, the torches reached the last switchback. The pale rider raised a hand, and fifty voices began a hymn in a language that hurt to hear, and the Spire's hum deepened, strained, like a held breath approaching its limit.

I walked toward the door that ate love, carrying all of mine, spending none of it yet.

Behind me, steel cleared leather. Ahead, in the dark of the threshold, something ancient turned its attention upon me and waited, courteous as a debt collector, for payment.
