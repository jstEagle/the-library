## 10. The Warden of Hollow

The valley woke up the way you wake from a fever: confused, damp, and furious about the dreams.

I know, because I watched it happen. I was standing on the ridge above Hollow with chalk dust on my fingers and a hole in my chest shaped exactly like a person, watching the mist roll back off the farms and the eastern orchards put out new leaves out of season, because apparently that's what happens when a seal stops dying. The whole valley exhaled. Even the river ran clearer. Old Marta at the mill swore her bread rose better that morning and refused to hear any other explanation for it.

Nobody remembered why.

That was the thing about miracles nobody had paid for yet—they looked like weather.

I'd been on the ridge since before dawn because I couldn't sleep in the Spire anymore, even though they'd given me a bed there, even though the Ashen Order had scattered like ash does when you blow on it, even though the war-god-that-wasn't-a-war-god—or maybe was, depending on which priest you asked—was locked up tight behind a door made of a woman's blood. Bren said I should go home to the orphanage in Millbrook. Bren said a lot of things in the week after we came down off the mountain, most of them gentle, all of them wrong.

"You can't stay up there forever," he told me, packing his gear with the exaggerated calm of a man who'd learned not to look at me directly. "The Order's broken. The valley's safe. There's nothing left to guard."

"There's always something left to guard," I said. "That's kind of the whole job."

He didn't argue. Bren never argues; he just gets quieter until the silence does the arguing for him. But as he shouldered his pack he stopped at the door of the waystation and said, without turning around: "She'd have liked that you stayed. Whoever she was."

"Whoever who was?" I said.

And he looked at me then, finally, and his face did something complicated—the face of a man holding two truths in his hands and knowing one of them would burn him no matter which he set down. "No one," he said. "Forget it."

* * *

Here's what I knew, three weeks after the Spire:

I knew her name the way you know a song stuck in your head—*Lior*, over and over, a word with hooks in it. I knew she'd carried me out of a burning farmyard on her back while Ashen riders shot arrows into the dark. I knew she taught me how to hold a knife, how to read a ridge line, how to swear in four languages, and how to tell when Bren was lying (his ears went red). I knew she hated the taste of river fish and ate them anyway. I knew she once gave me half her cloak in a snowstorm and lied about not being cold.

Except I didn't know those things. That's the part that made me want to scream into the well some nights. I *knew* them—I could feel every single one of them sitting in my chest like swallowed stones—but if you asked me where they came from, my mind came up smooth and blank as fresh snow. There was no face attached. No voice. Just the shape of a person, worn into me like a name carved into a doorframe by somebody who'd moved away.

The priests called it grace. The villagers called it the blessing of the waking. I called it theft, but I couldn't figure out who to be angry at, because whoever I was angry at had done it so I wouldn't have to remember being left behind, and somehow that made it worse.

Bren said the high priest of the Ashen Order had gone into the Spire and never come out. Bren said Lior—that word again, that hooked word—had fought him at the heart of it and won, at a cost the valley would never see and shouldn't. Bren said a lot of things with his ears going red.

But here's the thing nobody else noticed, because nobody else was looking: every morning, before dawn, a woman walked the perimeter of the Valley of Hollow. Tall. Gray-eyed. A sword on her hip that nobody recognized and nobody questioned, because that's the kind of place Hollow is—you don't ask the quiet ones their business. She walked the whole border, every day, rain or shine, touching certain stones as she passed them like an old woman counting beads. And sometimes, when she passed through Millbrook, she'd stop outside the orphanage window and stand there for exactly as long as it took me to notice her, and then she'd walk on.

She never spoke. Not to anyone. And when I tried to approach her once, in the market, she looked at me with gray eyes full of something so vast and so careful that my feet stopped working, and she nodded—just once, like a queen acknowledging a peasant—and bought an apple, and left.

I asked the fruit seller who she was.

"Fiona?" he said. "Warden's daughter, come back from the coast. Keeps to herself. Sad story, probably." He shrugged. "They're all sad stories, in this valley. We just forgot which ones."

* * *

So I started drawing again.

I'd stopped, after. Maps were how Sef the orphan boy had made himself useful, how he'd earned his place at the fire next to Bren and—next to her. Every mile of the road from Hollow to the Spire lived in my hands: the pass where we lost the mule, the frozen shrine with the heretic priestess and her memory-stone, the night we burned our boots to survive. I'd drawn it all, obsessively, on any surface that held still. Chalk on slate, charcoal on board, mud on the waystation wall until Bren threatened to make me scrub it.

After the mountain I couldn't look at those maps. They were full of her—her route choices, her corrections, her handwriting correcting mine in the margins, *no, fool, the ford is south*—and every mark was a hook, and every hook pulled on the stone in my chest.

But grief is a river, and rivers find their level. So in the third week I sat down in the orphanage loft with a sheet of good paper and I drew the journey one last time. All of it. From the burning farm to the foot of the Spire. And in the margins, where her corrections used to go, I drew small things instead: a boot burning in the snow. A fish skeleton. Two stick figures and a tall one with a sword, walking north.

It took me all day. When I was done, I rolled it up, and I knew—stone-in-chest certainty—exactly where it needed to go.

* * *

I found her on the western ridge at dusk, because of course I did. She walked the border at dusk now instead of dawn. I don't know why that felt significant. Everything felt significant that autumn; the whole world had the charged, just-before-thunder quality of a valley breathing under a new seal.

She heard me coming—of course she did—and didn't turn. I stood beside her at the boundary stone, close enough to touch, far enough to be polite, and together we watched the sun go down on the farms, the mill, the little lights coming on in Millbrook like someone scattering embers.

"I brought you something," I said.

Nothing. The wind moved her hair. Somewhere below, old Marta's dog barked at nothing, or at everything.

"It's a map," I went on, because talking to silent people is a skill you learn young in an orphanage. "Of a journey. You wouldn't know it. Nobody knows it. It's—" My voice did something humiliating. I pushed through it. "It's the only record. Of us. Of the people we were. Because somebody decided we weren't allowed to keep it, and I've decided that's garbage, so." I held it out. "Take it. Please. You don't have to know why. You just have to take it."

For a long moment she didn't move.

Then she turned, and looked at the rolled paper in my hands, and her gray eyes did the vast-and-careful thing again—but this time something cracked in the middle of it, a hairline fracture right down the center, and I saw what was behind it, which was everything. Grief with nowhere to live. Love with no address. A person carrying a houseful of furniture with all the doors painted shut.

Her hand came up. Stopped. Hovered over the map like a bird deciding whether to land.

"Do I—" she started, and her voice was rough as an unsharpened blade, like it hadn't been used in weeks. "Do I know you?"

"No," I said honestly.

She flinched like I'd hit her.

"But," I said, fast, before the crack could seal itself, "you knew me. Before. Whatever happened on that mountain, whatever it cost—it took the memory, but it didn't take you. I've watched you walk this border every day for three weeks. Nobody walks a border like that unless they're guarding something. Or saying sorry to it. And you buy apples from Tomas and pay too much, and you fixed the Hendersons' gate last Tuesday when you thought no one was looking, and you—" I had to stop and breathe. "You used to give me half your cloak in the snow. I don't remember it. But my chest remembers it. Does that make sense? It probably doesn't make sense."

She was very still. The kind of still that has a battle in it.

Then her fingers closed on the map.

She unrolled it right there on the ridge, in the failing light, and I watched her face as she read her own journey in a child's handwriting—watched the confusion, and then the recognition of the places if not the events, and then, at the margin drawings, the stick figures and the burning boot and the fish skeleton—

Something broke loose behind her eyes. Not a memory. Just the pressure where a memory used to be, the way an amputated limb aches.

"Sef," she said.

Not a question. Not a memory either—just the word surfacing from somewhere deeper than the theft had reached, hauled up from the same cellar where she kept the sound of my screaming her name. Her hand pressed flat against her sternum, hard, like she was holding a wound shut.

"That's me," I said. My eyes were doing something embarrassing. "Sef. Yeah."

"Lior," I added, quietly. "Your name is Lior. In case nobody ever told you. In case you were waiting for permission to know it."

The wind came up the ridge and moved between us, and far below the valley lights burned on, warm and oblivious, and somewhere under all of it the seal breathed deep and slow in its sleep, dreaming whatever sealed gods dream.

She looked at the map for a long time. Then she folded it—carefully, along my creases, like it was scripture—and tucked it inside her coat, over her heart, which is where I'd hoped she'd put it.

"I don't remember you," she said at last, and her voice was steady now, steady the way ice is steady. "I'm sorry. Whatever I was to you—I don't have it anymore. They took it. I let them take it. And I'd do it again, do you understand? I'd do it again tomorrow."

"I know," I said. "That's why it doesn't hurt as much as it should. You did it *for* us. That comes through. Memory doesn't. But that does."

She almost smiled. It was terrible and wonderful, that almost-smile, like the first green shoot through ash.

"Come to dinner sometime," I said, backing down the ridge, because I'd learned when a moment is finished. "Millbrook orphanage. Thursdays. Bren comes sometimes. He'll pretend he doesn't know why he's crying into the stew, and you'll pretend not to notice, and it'll be great."

"I don't—"

"Thursdays," I said. "You already bought the apple. You're basically family."

* * *

She came on Thursday. Of course she came.

She stood in the doorway of the orphanage kitchen with her sword wrapped in cloth like a courtesy, holding a bag of apples she'd clearly paid too much for, and Bren dropped a ladle, and the little ones swarmed her because children can smell a protector the way dogs smell thunder, and she stood there drowning in orphans with an expression of absolute baffled terror that made me laugh for the first time since the mountain.

She didn't remember any of us. That never changed. Some doors stay painted shut; that's the price of the paint.

But she sat next to me at the long table. She always sat next to me. And afterward, when the little ones were abed, she'd walk me to the edge of town before taking up her rounds, and sometimes—not often, but sometimes—she'd say strange things, like: "You take your eggs scrambled. Don't ask me how I know that." Or: "There's a ford south of the mill. Everyone says cross at the mill. Cross at the ford." Or, once, looking up at the stars over the Spire: "Someone used to draw maps. I dream about chalk. Is that strange?"

"Not strange," I said. "Familiar."

And every Thursday she came back, and every Thursday the hole in my chest ached a little less and filled a little more—not with what we'd lost, because that was gone, sealed away behind a door made of her blood, guarding a sleeping god and a grateful valley—but with something new. Something built on the foundation of the old thing, the way you build a house on old stones. Different rooms. Same ground.

Because here's what I learned, walking the ridges of Hollow with chalk in my pocket and a warden at my back: memory can be taken. Gods can take it, seals can take it, the whole hungry machinery of sacrifice can strip a person down to smooth blank snow.

But love isn't stored in memory. Love is stored in routes. In fords crossed and gates fixed and apples overpaid for and seats kept warm at a Thursday table. Love is a map drawn in margins, corrected in a dead woman's handwriting, folded along its own creases and carried over the heart.

You can steal the remembering.

You can't steal the route.

And on the last Thursday of autumn, when she paused at the edge of town and looked back at the lit windows of the orphanage with an expression I couldn't read, she reached into her coat and pressed something into my hand—a scrap of paper, torn from the margin of my own map, which she'd evidently been carrying everywhere.

On it, in careful, unfamiliar, unmistakably hers letters, she had written:

*Lior. Sef. Bren. Home.*

Three names and a word. The only record she had left.

"Thursdays," she said, and walked out into the dark to walk her borders, guarding the silence, keeping the breath, carrying her map the way I carried mine.

And I stood in the doorway of the only home I'd ever chosen, holding three names and a word, and understood at last what the whole journey had been for.

Some seals are made of blood.

The best ones are made of chalk.
