## 1. The Smuggler's Ledger

The snow came three weeks early that year, which was rude of it, because I had plans for those three weeks and none of them involved freezing.

I knew the storm before I saw it. That's the thing about being raised by storm-callers — even exiled, even stripped of my sigil and my name in the order's ledger, the weather still talked to me. Most people hear wind. I hear *intent*. And the mass of grey rolling down off Thornfell's shoulder that evening had all the intent of a debt collector who knows you're home.

"Storm's coming," I told Dagny, who was already tying down her ferry ropes across the river like a woman pretending she hadn't heard me. "A real one. Not a squall."

"It's midsummer," she said, without looking up.

"I'm aware."

"Then either your senses are broken or the world is." She cinched the knot hard enough to make the rope creak. "Which is it?"

"Both," I said, "historically speaking."

She grunted at that, which from Dagny was practically a sonnet. We'd worked together four years now — me guiding contraband through the passes, her moving it across the Marrow when the water ran high enough to drown questions. She never asked what I'd done to get exiled. I never asked why a ferrywoman needed a smuggler's cut so badly. Between us we had built something rare and precious: an entire friendship with no history in it.

I left her to her ropes and went to count my money.

This is not a metaphor. I literally counted it, every night, in a room above the Waystone inn where the floorboards were honest and the walls were thin enough that I could hear Brannick the innkeeper lying to his wife about how much ale he'd drunk. Forty-one crowns, nine silvers, and a copper some fool had bitten so many times it looked like a moon with teeth. I arranged them on the table in neat rows, then moved two crowns into a separate pile: *exit money.* Enough for a horse, a week's bread, and a false name in a town far enough south that nobody would ask after a tall woman with storm-grey eyes and a habit of watching horizons.

Every smuggler counts her coin. The good ones count their exits too.

I was still good. I intended to stay good.

* * *

The caravan arrived at midnight, which meant they were serious, and they arrived with eleven wagons, which meant they were stupid.

"Eleven," I said, staring down at them from the ridge trail where they'd gathered, lanterns shuttered, mules steaming in the cold. "I said six."

"We got a deal," said the man called Ferro, whose smile had more gold in it than his ledgers probably did. "Opportunity knocked."

"Opportunity knocks. It doesn't bring five extra wagons of glassware through the Goat's Teeth in what's about to be the worst summer snowfall in forty years."

His smile flickered. "You can read snow?"

"I can read *everything*, Ferro. That's why you pay me triple what the last guide cost, and the last guide is dead, if you'll recall. Fell into a ravine because he trusted the pass instead of reading it." I let the silence do a little work. It usually did. "Here's what happens now. You take eight wagons back down to the low camp and cache them under the rock overhang past the split birch. You take three up with me tonight, ahead of the storm front, because in four hours the north col will close and stay closed for days. In four days, if the weather breaks, we come back for the rest. If you argue longer than it takes me to say the word *no*, I take my fee and walk, and you get to explain to whoever's waiting on the other side why eleven wagons of glass are buried in a drift until spring."

He argued anyway, because men like Ferro always do — argument is how they convince themselves they're still in charge of things like weather. But he came around eventually, the way they always do, and by the time the first flakes started falling fat and sideways out of a sky gone the color of dirty wool, we were climbing.

And here is the part I will admit only here, in the privacy of my own skull, where no one can use it against me: I love this mountain.

Not in the way pilgrims love it, all incense and trembling awe. I love it the way you love a language you've spent ten years learning to speak — badly, secretly, alone. Every switchback of the hidden pass had a name I'd given it myself, because no map carried them and no one else would ever use them. The Widow's Elbow, where the trail leaned out over nothing. The Kettle, a bowl of stone where wind spun itself dizzy and lost its temper. The Long Stair, where the mountain had shrugged off an entire cliff-face a century ago and left a slope of shattered granite that sang faintly under boots if the frost was right.

Tonight the frost was right. Tonight the whole pass whispered as we climbed through it, and I walked at the head of three wagons and eleven nervous mules translating the mountain's mood into orders.

"Rope on the left side. Left, Ferro, your *other* left, gods preserve me. Halt at the Elbow — no, halt *now*, before the Elbow, there's ice under the scree and it lies about being solid." I crouched, pressed my bare palm flat to the stone, and listened with the part of me I was supposed to have surrendered along with my sigil. Cold ran up my arm like a rumor. Under it, deeper: pressure. Weight. The slow groan of a cloud carrying more than clouds should carry in the first week of harvest season.

It wasn't natural, this storm. Storms have grammar, and this one had been *written* — assembled with intent, pushed down off the high peaks by something with a purpose. I filed that away in the drawer where I kept everything I didn't want to think about. It was getting crowded in there.

"Move," I said, standing. "Quickly but quietly. If anyone sings, hums, or tells a joke, I feed them to the Kettle."

"You're a charming woman," Ferro muttered.

"I'm expensive," I corrected. "Charming costs extra."

* * *

We made the high shelf by the third hour of the storm, which was better than I'd hoped and worse than I'd lied. The snow was coming down thick enough to erase our back-trail behind us — good for hiding from border wardens, bad for everything else — and the temperature was dropping in long ugly lurches, like the night couldn't decide how cruel to be and kept escalating out of spite. I put the mules in the lee of the split birch, rigged the canvas into a windbreak, and set the men to rotating watch while I stood out in the white and did the one thing exile had never managed to beat out of me.

I listened to the storm.

It's not casting. Casting is what the order does — formal, ritual, hands and words and the great bronze horns they blow from their spire to pull lightning out of a clear sky. What I did was smaller and older and, according to the order, worse: I just *paid attention*. A storm is a crowd of small decisions, each gust a mind changing course. And underneath the noise of this one, beneath the written intent I'd felt earlier, there was something else. Something almost like a voice fraying at the edges. Like a song sung by someone forgetting the words.

I pulled my hand back out of the snow and flexed feeling into my fingers. My heart was doing something I disapproved of.

"Guide." One of Ferro's men, young, chapped lips, the universal look of someone who regrets every choice since leaving home. "There's a light. Down-slope. Moving."

I followed his pointing arm and saw it: a pale glow bobbing through the murk far below, wrong for a lantern — steady, unblinking, the color of moonlight through ice. It moved against the wind. Nothing moves against the wind up here unless it has business with you.

"Ferro," I called, not taking my eyes off it. "How many guides turned you down before me?"

"All of them," he said. "Whole town. Wouldn't touch this run with a barge pole."

"And you didn't wonder why?"

"I figured they were cowards."

"They were *informed*." I turned, pulling my hood forward. "Get your men awake. We move for the hollow now, tonight, storm or no storm. Whatever that light belongs to, I don't want to be introduced."

We broke camp in twelve minutes, which would have been a record if anyone had been keeping records besides me, and I took us down the hidden way — the crack in the mountainside that doesn't look wide enough for a goat, let alone a wagon, but opens into a slot canyon the old fae roads had worn smooth as a throat. The smugglers called it the Hollow. They thought it was just geology.

Geology doesn't hum.

But it hummed tonight, faint and uneasy, as though the mountain itself had noticed the same light I had and didn't care for it. I put my palm against the slot canyon wall as we passed and gave it what the order would have called a profane gesture — a simple touch, no ritual, just acknowledgment. *I know. I feel it too. Keep your stones around us.*

The humming deepened by half a tone, which in mountain is roughly the equivalent of a friend saying *get behind me.*

Three hours later we came out below the tree line, into air that stung less, and I handed Ferro across the boundary stone at the pass mouth with the specific pleasure of a woman concluding a transaction.

"The rest of your cargo stays cached four days," I told him. "Send payment to the Waystone, as agreed. And Ferro—" I caught his sleeve before he could turn away. "Whoever hired you for this run. Did they tell you anything? Anything odd. A reason for the timing. A passenger expected. A book, a package, anything."

Something crossed his face — quick, guilty, gone. "Just freight," he said. "Glass and ironwork. Why?"

Because storms don't get *written* for glass and ironwork. Because lights don't walk uphill against the wind for cargo. Because twice in one month I'd found fresh hoofprints on trails that weren't on any map, and someone in a hooded cloak asking after me in the lowland taverns by description rather than name — *tall woman, grey eyes, keeps to herself* — and I wanted very much to believe these facts were unrelated.

"No reason," I said. "Old habit. Guides worry."

"Cheerful trade you're in." He mounted up, and then, because men like Ferro can't help themselves, added: "There was one thing. Fellow came through two nights back asking after you. Scholarly sort. Bookish. Carrying some old tome wrapped in oilcloth like it was a baby. Wouldn't say what he wanted, only that he needed guiding into the high fells and no one would take his coin."

My hand was still on his sleeve. I made myself let go.

"Name?"

"Didn't give one. Bramble-something, the innkeeper called him. Theo, maybe." Ferro shrugged, wheeled his mule, and clattered off down toward Marrowgate with his three wagons, leaving me standing in the early snow of a midsummer night with the taste of a word in my mouth.

*Bramble.*

I stood there long enough for the snow to settle on my shoulders like a hand. Behind me the mountain hummed its low warning note. Ahead, down in the valley, the lamps of the Waystone burned yellow and ordinary, and somewhere inside one of them sat a scholar with a book, waiting to hire a guide nobody else would take.

I could walk south. Forty-one crowns, nine silvers, and a chewed copper. Exit money was right there in my pocket; the road south was right there under my boots. Ten years I'd stayed invisible by the simple method of never being interesting, and every instinct I owned was screaming that interesting had just ridden into the valley wearing oilcloth.

But the storm overhead was still written. Still deliberate. Still pushing down off the peaks with a purpose I couldn't read, and the mountain was still humming, and I had learned — expensively, bloodily, in ways I didn't let myself remember at night — that ignoring a thing the mountain wanted me to see always cost more than facing it.

"Damn it," I said, to no one, and turned downhill toward the lamplight.

I counted my exits one last time as I walked. There were four from the Waystone yard: the road south, the river path, the goat track east, and the roof route over the stable that I'd used exactly once and never mentioned to anyone.

I memorized all four.

Then I went in out of the snow to meet my future, and asked the innkeeper — casually, I thought, quite casually — whether the bookish fellow was still taking dinner.
