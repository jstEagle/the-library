## 2. A Stranger with a Book

I want it on the record, before anything else, that I did not want to be there.

The inn at Thornfell's edge was called the Drowned Goat, which should have been my first warning, and it smelled of tallow smoke, wet wool, and a century of spilled ale that had seeped into the floorboards and given up on ever leaving. The barkeep was a woman built like a doorframe who looked at me the way farmers look at weather they don't trust. Behind her, mounted above a row of chipped mugs, hung a stuffed pike with one glass eye and an expression I found uncomfortably familiar.

I had been traveling for nineteen days. My boots had given up their argument with my feet somewhere around day eleven and settled into a grudging ceasefire. My coat was ruined. My book—*the* book—was wrapped in oilcloth against my chest like a second heart, and every time I coughed, I checked to make sure neither of us had sprung a leak.

"Ale," I said, because it seemed like the thing to say.

The barkeep poured something the color of pond water and slid it across. "Two coppers. And before you ask: no rooms till the smugglers clear out, no hot food till the stove's fed, and no guides."

"I haven't asked about guides yet."

"You will." She said it with the weary certainty of someone who'd watched this particular play run its act a dozen times that season. "Everyone asks about guides. Answer's still no."

I paid the two coppers and drank the ale, which tasted the way pond water looks, and considered my position with the clarity that only exhaustion provides.

My name is Theo Bramble. I am—or was, before the archive burned—a junior conservator of texts at the Athenaeum of Corvale, which is a fancy way of saying I spent six years learning how to keep old books from falling apart while older men took credit for reading them. I am not brave. I have never been described as brave by anyone who wasn't being polite. My talents are narrow and specific: I can read dead languages, repair water damage, and identify forgery in ink at twenty paces. None of these skills had prepared me for carrying a book across half a kingdom while people tried to kill me for it.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. In the inn at the mountain's foot, all I knew was: the book needed to go into Thornfell, to a place marked in its margins in my grandmother's handwriting, and no guide in the lowlands would take me.

Three had refused me outright. A fourth had quoted a price clearly designed as an insult. A fifth had taken one look at the book when I made the mistake of unwrapping it to show him and left the tavern without finishing his drink, walking quickly, the way men walk when they've remembered an urgent appointment elsewhere.

So I sat in the Drowned Goat, drinking bad ale, watching the door, because Grandmother's letter had said *find the storm-warden's ghost, she'll know the pass*, and I had spent nineteen days failing to understand what that meant until tonight, when the answer walked in wearing a hood.

She came in from the snow—snow, in midsummer, which everyone else in the room ignored with the practiced ease of people who had decided not to think about it—and the cold came in with her like a dog that hadn't been invited but was used to following anyway. She was tall, dark-haired, dressed in travel leathers patched so many times the patches had patches. Her hood stayed up even indoors. She moved through the common room the way water moves around stones: without touching anyone, without being touched.

The smugglers at the corner table—the ones whose presence apparently blocked all available lodging—nodded to her. Not a greeting, exactly. An acknowledgment. The way sailors nod at the tide.

"That's her," I whispered to no one, and immediately felt foolish, because I didn't actually know it was her. I only strongly suspected it, in the way you suspect a storm is coming when the birds go quiet.

The barkeep set down a bowl of stew without being asked. The woman ate standing up, fast, economical, like someone who treated meals as maintenance rather than pleasure. When she finished she counted coins onto the counter—one, two, three—with a little pause after each, as if confirming each one was real and each payment was final.

I should let her eat. I should let her drink. I should approach her calmly, respectfully, like a reasonable man making a business inquiry.

I stood up, knocked my knee on the table hard enough to see colors, and said, louder than intended, "Are you the guide?"

The whole room went quiet in that specific way rooms do when a stranger has just done something tactically stupid. The woman turned. Up close her eyes were gray, the flat gray of sky before hail, and they went over me once, head to boots, cataloguing. I had the distinct feeling of having been weighed, priced, and found to be a poor investment.

"No," she said, and turned back to the bar.

"Someone told me—" I began.

"Someone told you wrong." She picked up her bowl, carried it two stools down, and sat where I could no longer be mistaken for her companion.

It was, I will admit, a masterful refusal. Brief, complete, and requiring no further engagement. A lesser man would have retreated. But nineteen days of wet boots and worse roads had cured me of retreating, mostly because I'd discovered that behind me were considerably more frightening things than in front of me.

I sat back down next to her. She did not look at me. This did not slow me down.

"My name is Theo Bramble," I said. "I need to cross Thornfell. Into the high country. The interior passes, not the trade routes."

"Eat your stew."

"I'm not hungry."

"Then you're stupider than you look, and you look pretty stupid." She tore bread, dipped it, ate. I noticed her hands: scarred across the knuckles, rope-callused, steady. Hands that had done things. "Trade routes are closed. Interior passes are closed. Everything above the treeline is closed. It's summer and it's snowing, scholar. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means the covenant's failing." The words came out of me before I could weigh them, and I watched them land. Her spoon stopped moving. Just for a heartbeat. Then it resumed, slower.

"Where'd you hear that word?"

"In a book."

"What book?"

"That's complicated."

"Everything with scholars is complicated. It's why nobody hires you." She pushed the bowl away, finally turning to face me fully, and studied me with what I can only describe as professional distaste. "Let me save us both time. You're going to offer me money. I'm going to refuse. You're going to offer more. I'm going to refuse again, and then you're going to say something earnest and doomed, and I'm going to leave, and tomorrow you'll be dead or gone, and either way it won't be my problem. Yes?"

"...There might be a step where I show you the book," I offered.

Her expression flickered. Curiosity, maybe. Or recognition of a trap she couldn't quite name yet. "No book," she said. "Whatever it is, whatever it says, whoever died getting it here—I don't want it near me. I've spent nine years being no one, scholar. I'm very good at it. Books with names in them ruin that."

I hadn't told her the book had names in it.

I must have shown something on my face, because her eyes narrowed, and for the first time she looked at me not as an obstacle but as a question. "What's in the book?"

"Prophecies," I said. "Old ones. Written a hundred years ago by—" I stopped. That was the part I'd decided not to tell anyone. "By an anonymous author. It mentions Thornfell. It mentions the covenant. It mentions..." I hesitated again.

"Say it or don't," she said, standing, dropping coppers beside her bowl. "But don't say it slowly."

And then, because I am apparently incapable of learning, I said it.

"It mentions you."

*

Here is what I knew about Ulyana Zhou at that point, which is to say, here is what I'd managed to piece together from three weeks of asking questions in lowland taverns while pretending to be a surveyor:

That she was real, which half the rumors denied. That she guided caravans through Thornfell for smugglers, which the other half confirmed. That she never spoke of her past, never traveled with the same crew twice, never slept under the same roof a full season. That she had once been something else—there was disagreement about what—and had erased herself from the record so thoroughly that even her former colleagues spoke of her in the past tense, like a dead language.

And that the order of storm-callers, up in their cliff-hung monastery, wanted her found.

I learned that last part four days ago, from a dying man named Aldric, who had been the archivist of the Corvale Athenaeum, who had been my mentor, and who had spent his final hours pressing a water-damaged book into my hands and telling me to run.

"They burned the west archive," he'd said, blood on his teeth, firelight on his face. "Not an accident. They were looking for this. Bramble—they were looking for *her*. The name in the book. You take it to Thornfell, you find her, you get her to the spire, and Theo—" he'd gripped my wrist with an archivist's strength, absurd and iron, "—you tell her it wasn't her fault. Whatever they told her. Whatever she believes. It wasn't her fault."

Then he died, and I ran, and now here I was in the Drowned Goat, having just told a woman who'd spent nine years becoming invisible that a hundred-year-old book mentioned her by name, which was possibly the single least helpful thing I could have said to a person actively engaged in not existing.

She stopped halfway to the door.

I watched her decide something. Watched her shoulders settle, watched her turn around and come back, and there was a new quality to her movement—not softer, nothing about her was soft, but *focused*, like a drawn bow.

"Show me," she said.

*

We took the corner table farthest from the fire, and I unwrapped the book with the care it deserved, peeling back the oilcloth layer by layer. Even damaged, even half-drowned, it was beautiful. Calfskin boards tooled with a design I'd spent nineteen nights studying: a mountain, a tree, and between them a figure with arms raised, stitched in silver thread gone black with age. The spine was cracked. The first forty pages were fused into a single water-swollen brick. But the rest—

I opened it to the page. I'd marked it with a thread, because I'd read it so many times I could find it blind.

The script was old High Lowland, the formal hand of a trained scribe, but the marginal notes were different—smaller, sharper, written by a faster mind. Grandmother's hand. I'd know it anywhere; I'd learned my letters tracing it.

Ulyana leaned over the page. Firelight caught the ink.

*"In the hundredth year of the fading, when frost comes in the green month and the border villages dream of ice, the exile who calls storms shall return to the mountain of thorns. She carries no banner and keeps no name. On the eve of the Longest Night she stands at the gate of the drowned chapel, and the covenant shall be reforged in her hands—or buried in winter, both realms together, root and stone and bone."*

And beneath it, in the margin, in the same century-old ink, smaller, almost casual, as if the author had written it with a shrug:

*U. Zhou. Ask for her at the Drowned Goat. She'll refuse twice.*

The date beneath it was tonight's date. One hundred years old.

For a long moment Ulyana didn't move. The common room noise went on around us—the smugglers' dice, the barkeep's rag on the counter, wind worrying the shutters—but our table had become an island of absolute silence.

"That's not possible," she said quietly. It wasn't a denial. Denials are loud. This was the voice of someone doing arithmetic and hating the total.

"The ink's period-correct," I said, because apparently my response to tension was to lecture. "Iron gall, oak-gall base, faded at the rate you'd expect. The binding thread is pre-Imperial style. If it's forged, it was forged a century ago by someone with access to authentic materials and a reason to fake a prophecy about a woman who wouldn't be born for decades." I swallowed. "I'm a conservator. Forgeries are kind of my specialty. This isn't one."

"Refuses twice," Ulyana read aloud. Her voice was strange. Flat, careful, like someone crossing ice and testing each step. "She'll refuse twice."

"And I believe that makes two," I said. "You refused me at the bar, and you refused me at the—"

"I know how to count." She was still staring at the page. At her own name, in ink older than she was. Something moved behind her face—fear, I thought, or its close cousin, grief—and then it was gone, packed away with a speed that told me she'd had a lot of practice packing things away.

"Who wrote this?" she asked.

"An anonymous author."

"Try again, scholar. You flinched just now. Right at 'anonymous.' You've got a tell like a cracked bell."

I looked at the book instead of at her. "The author was my grandmother," I said. "Marisol Bramble. She was a warden of the old covenant. The order calls her a heretic now. They burned everything she wrote. Except this." I put my hand flat on the cover, over the mountain and tree and the small raised figure. "Aldric hid it for sixty years. He died getting it out. Four days ago. There were men with white-and-gray sashes, and he told me to run, and I ran, and I've been running since, and I am—" my voice did something humiliating and uncontrolled, and I steadied it with effort, "—I am extremely tired, and I need to get this book to a place called the spire, at the heart of Thornfell, before the longest night, and you are the only person alive who knows those mountains well enough to get me there. So. Will you guide me?"

She was quiet for a long time. Outside, the freak snow ticked against the window glass. Someone at the dice table swore cheerfully. The stuffed pike stared its glass stare.

"No," said Ulyana Zhou, for the third time.

"But the book says—"

"The book says a dead woman guessed right twice. Guessing isn't destiny. And names in books aren't orders." She stood, wrapped the oilcloth back over the pages with surprising gentleness—she handled it like something living, I noted, something wounded—and pushed it across the table to me. "Find another guide."

"There are no other guides. You know that. Everyone in this valley knows that. The passes are closed, the trade routes are closed, the lowland guides won't touch Thornfell in winter, and the mountain guides won't touch me because someone's paying them not to. You're not my best option. You're my only option, and I think we both know that's not an accident."

Something in that landed. I saw it land. Her jaw tightened.

"Who's paying them not to?" she asked softly.

"The same people who burned the archive, I'd guess. The same people Aldric died running from. The same order that exiled you for a crime you didn't commit."

Her whole body went still—the particular stillness of prey animals and soldiers, of anyone who has survived by knowing exactly when to freeze. "How do you know that?"

"Your grandmother's margin notes," I lied smoothly, and then, because lying to her felt like lying to a blade, I corrected myself: "Aldric's letters. He wrote about you. He called you the exile who calls storms. He said—" and here I gave her the dying man's gift, because it was the only thing of value I owned, "—he said to tell you it wasn't your fault. Whatever they told you happened. Whatever you believe you did. It wasn't your fault. Those were nearly his last words. He spent sixty years keeping that book safe so someone could say them to you."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a board creaked. Ulyana Zhou looked at me for a long, long moment, and I watched nine years of carefully maintained invisibility strain against a hundred-year-old sentence in a dead woman's handwriting.

"I don't want pardon," she said at last. "I don't want vindication. I don't want prophecy. I want to be left alone, and everything you're carrying is the opposite of alone. Do you understand that? Every mile you walk toward that spire, you drag the world's attention with you. The order. Worse than the order. Things in the high country that have been waiting a long time for someone foolish enough to open this door." She tapped the book's cover once, lightly. "I've seen what happens to villages that stand too close to the covenant's cracks. Have you?"

"Marrowgate," I said. "Frost in midsummer. I passed through it. Black fields. Children breathing fog in June."

"Yes." Something crossed her face at the name—Marrowgate—something old and raw. "So you understand why I stay out of it. Small life. Quiet life. Alive life."

"And yet," I said, and turned the book back to the page, and tapped the line beneath her name, the casual, shrugging, hundred-years-dead handwriting: *She'll refuse twice.* "Twice, and then what? Whoever wrote this knew you'd say no three times. Knew it a century before either of us was born. Which means the third no was never really on the table, was it? You're not deciding whether to help me. You're deciding whether you find out what happens next."

"That," she said, "is the most annoying true thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I have others."

"I believe it." She sank back onto the stool, and for a moment she just breathed, staring at the fire, and I watched the war happen behind her eyes—want and dread pulling the same rope in opposite directions. When she spoke again, her voice had changed. Lower. Wary in a new way. "Terms. If I do this—and I haven't agreed—you follow my rules absolutely. We move at night through the order's territory. You speak to no one. You show that book to no one. If I say drop it in a river, you drop it in a river, and you thank me after. And you tell me everything. Who killed Aldric. What the order wants with this. What's really at that spire. No more flinching at 'anonymous.' No fragments. All of it."

"All of it takes time," I said. "Some of it I don't know yet. Some of it I'm afraid of."

"Get in line," she said dryly, and something in my chest loosened for the first time in nineteen days, because that was the first thing she'd said that sounded like a person rather than a fortress. She held out her hand across the table. Hers was callused and cool. Mine was ink-stained and shaking slightly, and she pretended not to notice, which I appreciated more than she would ever know.

"Passage to the spire," she said. "One scholar, one cursed book. Payment negotiable, terms non-negotiable. We leave before dawn, before the inn wakes up. Sleep in your boots."

"Why before dawn?"

"Because," said Ulyana Zhou, glancing once toward the shuttered window, where the midsummer snow fell silent and thick, "the order already knows you reached the valley. I felt the storm change when you crossed the river. And if they know you're here—" her gray eyes came back to mine, and there was no warmth in them, but there was something better: resolve, hard and clean as ice, "—then they'll be here by morning. Drink your ale, scholar. It's the last bad thing you'll taste for a week."

I drank my ale. It was still terrible.

Outside, the snow kept falling on a world out of season, and somewhere in the dark above the treeline, I couldn't help thinking, the mountain was waiting for its exile to come home—and so was everything else.
