## 4. Terms of Passage

I had been thrown out of better rooms than this one, but never by someone who looked so tired doing it.

"You're still here," Ulyana said from the doorway of the inn's common room, morning light cutting through smoke and spilled ale behind her. "Most people take a no the first time."

"Most people have somewhere to be." I kept my hands visible on the table—empty, palms up, the universal posture of *I am not about to do anything clever*. It rarely worked, but it cost nothing. "And nowhere else to go, which is worse."

She crossed the room without hurrying. That was the thing about her I was already learning, in the small hours between her refusals: everything she did was unhurried, as if hurry were a luxury she'd once indulged and found wanting. She sat across from me—not because she wanted to talk, I suspected, but because standing made her look like she cared.

"You want a guide into Thornfell," she said. "In what is either late spring or early winter, depending on how cursed the year's feeling."

"That's roughly the shape of it, yes."

"Alone."

"With company," I said. "The book counts."

Her eyes went to my satchel the way a cat's eyes go to a mousehole. "That book again."

"That book again."

"And when I asked who sent you, you said—a patron." She let the word sit there like a fish on a slab. "A scholar's patron. Wealthy. Discreet."

"I said those words, yes."

"But they weren't true."

"They were *mostly* true," I said. "Wealthy and discreet are accurate. It's the 'patron' part that wobbles."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, perhaps—the kind of expression a person makes when they've decided not to laugh at you and are annoyed about the effort it costs.

"Theo Bramble," she said, tasting the name like she was checking for rot. "Sit down properly. Tell me about your wobbling patron. And then I'm going to say no again, and you're going to sleep in the stable, and we'll both pretend this conversation was about the weather."

* * *

Here is what I told her, in pieces, over the next hour while the common room emptied and the fire burned down to a sullen orange eye:

I told her I was an archivist. True. Third-rate, unlettered, chronically underemployed, but true. I'd spent six years cataloguing the private collections of minor lords who liked the *idea* of scholarship more than its practice—men who bought books by the yard the way they bought tapestries, to cover walls and impress visitors who couldn't read.

I told her the book came from an archive. Also true. The Ashvale Repository, though I didn't give her the name. A century-old collection, mostly fae-lore and covenant law, gathered back when scholars still believed the old pact was worth studying rather than merely mourning.

I did not tell her that when I arrived there last autumn, following a rumor and a letter written in my grandmother's handwriting—my grandmother, dead eleven years—the archive was already burning.

That part could wait. Most things could wait. Waiting was my one genuine talent.

"So a patron hired you to retrieve this book," Ulyana said. She'd commandeered a heel of bread and was eating it with the efficiency of someone for whom meals were logistics. "From a burned archive."

"The burning happened after. Or during. The sequencing was—" I waved a hand. "Fluid."

"Fascinating." She chewed. Swallowed. "And your patron wants this book carried where, exactly? Into the mountains? To whom?"

"That's the wobbling part."

"Theo."

"It's not that I won't tell you," I said, which was a lie, and by the way her eyebrows rose a quarter-inch, she knew it was a lie, and was letting me finish it anyway the way you let a child finish a story about a dragon. "It's that the instructions were... incomplete. My patron left me a list. Deliver the book to a guide in the borderlands. One guide specifically. No name given, only a description."

"A description," she repeated flatly.

"'Storm-grey eyes. Scar through the left brow. Will refuse twice.'"

The bread stopped halfway to her mouth.

I watched her decide whether to be afraid. It took her maybe two seconds, and what impressed me—what has continued to impress me, all these years later—is that instead of fear she chose arithmetic. I could see her running numbers behind those storm-grey eyes: how much coin I might carry, how many exits the inn had, how fast she could be through the back door if I turned out to be bait.

"You've been looking for me," she said.

"Somebody's been looking for you. I'm just the fellow carrying the note."

"There is no note."

"No," I admitted. "There isn't. There's a description and a date. The date's tomorrow, incidentally. Which is why I've been sleeping badly."

* * *

Let me tell you what I hadn't told her yet, since apparently I'm doing this in order:

My grandmother's name was Odile Bramble. To hear my mother tell it, Odile was a difficult woman who wrote tedious monographs on covenant jurisprudence and died of a fever like a sensible person. To hear my grandmother's letters tell it—letters that began arriving three years after her funeral, postmarked from towns she'd never visited, in ink that dried silver—Odile had spent her last decade assembling something, hiding something, and entrusting the final piece of it to a grandson she'd met exactly four times.

The letters stopped eighteen months ago. The last one contained a single line: *The archive will burn. Take the book to the exile before the frost comes early.*

I'm a scholar. I don't believe in prophecy the way farmers believe in weather or priests believe in guilt. But I believe in evidence, and the evidence was: my dead grandmother's handwriting on fresh paper; an archive full of covenant law reduced to ash two months after her warning; a book pulled from that fire with my name inside the binding, stitched into the leather like a label on a jar.

And now a description of a woman who matched every scar and storm in the margins of that book's oldest pages, delivered to me by a smuggler's gossip in a border town, along with the news that the frost had come early to Marrowgate and wasn't leaving.

Someone had killed to send that book with me. Two someones, actually—I'd watched the second man die in an alley in Lowmark with a crossbow bolt through his throat, and I'd taken the bolt meant for my own shoulder as a souvenir scar and a strong hint that whatever I was carrying mattered enough to kill for, which meant it mattered enough to die for, which meant I needed a guide who knew these mountains better than the people hunting me.

Which brought me, cold and out of coin and out of alternatives, to Ulyana Zhou.

* * *

"Say it plainly," she said. We'd moved to the window bench, away from the dying fire and the innkeeper's ears. Outside, the peaks wore their new snow like a threat. "What happens if I say no a third time?"

"Then I hire someone less good, get lost or killed in the Goat's Teeth, and the book ends up in a snowdrift until spring. Whoever wants it badly enough to burn an archive finds it eventually. And you—" I shrugged. "You keep being nobody, very successfully, until somebody reads that page."

Her jaw tightened. "There's no page."

"There's a page."

"In a book that's been water-damaged within an inch of illegibility."

"Water-damaged," I agreed, "except where it matters. You've seen it. You read it by candlelight at midnight and then threw it across the room, which, for the record, is the most honest review any text has ever received."

"I threw it because it's nonsense."

"You threw it because it named you."

The silence stretched long enough for the fire to pop twice. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into something quieter and considerably more dangerous. "Who wrote that book, Theo?"

Here was the fork in the road. I want to be clear about this, because for years afterward Ulyana insisted I'd manipulated her, conned her, played her grief like a lute—and she's not entirely wrong, but she leaves out that I gave her the choice honestly, at every fork, even the ones where lying would have served me better. This was one of them.

"My grandmother," I said. "Odile Bramble. She died eleven years ago. The letters started three years after that."

"Letters from a dead woman."

"Posthumous correspondence. Scholars do it all the time." I smiled. She didn't. "No. Nobody does it ever. It should have been impossible, and I've had a year and a half to construct a rational explanation and the best I've managed is *someone is forging her hand extremely well*, which raises the question of why anyone would forge the handwriting of a footnote of a woman to send a stranger into the mountains after a book of nonsense that names a smuggler who doesn't exist."

"Doesn't exist," she echoed softly. "Good. Keep telling yourself that."

"Ulyana." I leaned forward. "I'm going to tell you something now and then I'm going to stop talking, because I can see you deciding whether to break my nose and I'd like to finish my sentence first. Someone burned an archive to keep this book from being read. Then they tried to kill me to keep it from traveling. They failed twice, which means they'll try a third time, and they'll send professionals. I cannot outrun professionals. I cannot fight professionals. What I can do is walk into Thornfell with the one person in either realm the book says matters, and hope that whoever is hunting this thing hesitates at the edge of her territory." I sat back. "I need you. Not your coin. Not your pass. You."

"You don't know me."

"No," I said. "But the book does. And the book has been right about everything so far—the frost, the date, the description. I hate that as much as you do. Believe me. I had plans. They involved a chair."

* * *

She looked at the mountains for a long time. I've since learned that this is how she thinks—not with her eyes closed, but aimed at the horizon, as if the answer might be hiding behind a ridge.

"Terms," she said finally.

My heart performed a small undignified leap. "Terms."

"First. Half payment now, half at the far side of the range. If you die, I keep the advance and sell your satchel."

"Savory."

"Second. We leave tomorrow, before dawn, and we take the low road past Marrowgate. Don't ask why yet."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

"Third." She turned from the window and fixed me with a look I would come to know intimately—the look she gave people right before she asked them for the truth, and right before she decided not to give hers. "You don't open the book without me present. You don't read from it aloud. And if it starts doing anything—burning, bleeding, whispering—you drop it and step back and let me deal with it."

"Because you're the expert in cursed literature?"

"Because I'm the expert in things that go wrong near me." Something flickered across her face, too fast to name. "Fourth term. Whatever your patron wants—whatever your dead grandmother wants—you tell me before it becomes a problem, not after. I've been lied to by institutions, Theo. I can survive a liar. What I can't survive is being surprised by one."

I considered pointing out that she was withholding plenty herself—the way she flinched at the word *order*, the way the innkeeper's wife watched her like she owed money, the way she counted coins that night like a woman planning an escape route rather than a journey. Two liars, agreeing to need each other. That's what we were, from the very first hour. I think we both knew it. I think it was, in its strange way, the foundation of everything that came after: neither of us trusted the other, but both of us trusted the other's dishonesty to have limits.

"Agreed," I said. "All four terms. And one of mine."

She raised an eyebrow. Negotiation, apparently, permitted this.

"When this is done—if we survive it—you tell me what crime got you exiled. Not for the book. Not for the bargain. Because I've read your file, metaphorically speaking, and the numbers don't add up, and I'm a very annoying person to have noticed that."

For a moment I thought she'd strike me, or laugh, or both. Instead she stood, brushed bread crumbs from her trousers, and looked down at me with an expression I couldn't read at all.

"Term accepted," she said, "on the condition that you don't enjoy being right."

"I never enjoy it," I said. "It's terrible for the digestion."

She paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the grey morning, and for a heartbeat she almost smiled—almost, the way the sun almost breaks through cloud in Thornfell, which is to say you learn not to plan around it.

"Dawn, Bramble. Stable yard. If you're late, I'm gone, and the mountain keeps your secrets."

Then she was gone, up the stairs, and I sat alone in the cooling common room with my hand pressed flat against the satchel, feeling the book beneath the canvas sit there like a sleeping animal.

Two liars. One road.

I paid for the room I'd barely slept in, bought a second blanket against the frost, and tried not to think about the fact that tomorrow we rode toward Marrowgate—the village the smugglers' gossip said had gone white in midsummer, its wells iced, its crops blackened overnight.

Toward the frost. Toward whatever my grandmother had seen coming from beyond her grave.

Dawn came early. Of course it did. Everything in this story came early—winter, death, the future arriving ahead of schedule like a creditor.

I was in the stable yard five minutes before she was.
