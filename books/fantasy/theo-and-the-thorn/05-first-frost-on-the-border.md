## 5. First Frost on the Border

The trouble with lowlands, I'd always said, was that they lied to you. Everything down there was soft edges and easy distances—fields that looked like they went on forever, roads that didn't try to kill you every hundred paces. A person raised in Thornfell learned to read the mountain the way a sailor reads the sea: every shadow a warning, every silence a debt coming due. The lowlands offered no such honesty. They just smiled at you with all that green and pretended the world was kind.

By the time we came down off the last shoulder of the Thornfell massif, I'd stopped saying it, because I wasn't sure the lowlands were smiling anymore.

I smelled Marrowgate before I saw it. That's not poetry. Smoke, yes—villages always smell of smoke—but underneath it something else, something wrong, like a hearth fire trying to burn wet wood. A green smell gone sour. Theo walked beside me with his book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest and his scholar's eyes busy cataloguing everything and understanding nothing, and I found myself shortening my stride without deciding to, the way you do when you're walking someone toward something they're not ready to see.

"You're doing the thing again," Theo said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you look at the horizon like it owes you money."

"Everything owes me money," I said. "That's what being a guide is. But that's not—" I stopped. Below us, in the valley, Marrowgate sat in its bowl of fields, and even from the ridge I could see the color was wrong. Not the gold-green of midsummer. A gray-gold, like old parchment left out in rain. "That's not right."

Theo followed my gaze. I watched him do the scholar thing, the thing where he reached for a reason before he'd let himself feel the thing itself. "Frost damage?" he offered. "A late cold snap could—"

"It's midsummer, Bramble. There hasn't been a cold snap in the lowlands in living memory. The mountain keeps them warm. That's the entire point of the mountain." I was already moving, boots finding the scree path down, and I heard him scramble after me. "Keep your book close. And keep your mouth closed unless someone asks you a question. Villages spook easy, and a stranger with a scholar's accent asking about frost is a stranger they'll remember."

"You say that like being remembered is a bad thing."

I looked back at him. He had that open face, that terrible open face, the kind that made people want to tell him things. It was a wonder he'd survived the archive fire at all. "Up in Thornfell, being remembered is how you end up in a ledger with a price next to your name. Trust me. I've read the ledger."

* * *

The village gate was a joke by mountain standards—a timber arch, a rope, a boy of maybe ten years holding a spear longer than he was tall. But the boy's eyes were the problem. I'd seen that look before, in the high passes, in villages the winter had gotten into. It's a specific look. Not fear, exactly. Fear has a future in it, a *what happens next*. This was something older. This was a boy doing arithmetic with his own village and not liking the sum.

"State your business," he said, voice cracking on *business*.

"Passage," I said. "We're through-travelers, headed for Ferrow's Landing on the far side. We'll buy supplies, pay for a night's lodging, and be gone before your rooster finishes his morning argument."

The boy squinted at me, then at Theo, then—this was the interesting part—up at the sky, as though checking whether the sky had cleared us. "Gran says no one travels anymore. Gran says the frost takes travelers first."

"The frost takes no one first," I said. "Frost doesn't take. It stays." I flipped him a copper, which he caught on reflex, then looked at like it had personally betrayed him. "Where's your reeve?"

"Meeting house. Everyone's at the meeting house." He hesitated, and the spear dipped. "You're not from the Order, are you?"

Something went cold in my chest that had nothing to do with frost. "Why would you ask that?"

"Because the Order came through in spring," he said. "They wore gray and they didn't smile and they asked about the cold, and Gran said when the Order asks about the cold, you tell them it's nothing. You tell them it's nothing even if your crops are dead." He stepped aside, suddenly eager to be done with us. "Meeting house is the big building with the bell. Don't ring the bell. Nobody rings the bell anymore."

I walked through the gate and did not look back at him, because looking back would have meant admitting what his face had done to me.

* * *

Marrowgate in midsummer should have been loud. That's what I knew of lowland villages from the few times I'd guided caravans through them—loud with harvest prep, with children, with dogs, with the particular quarrelsome music of people who believed, deep in their bones, that tomorrow would be more or less like today. Even in the border villages, even the poor ones, there was that hum of assumption. Tomorrow. And tomorrow. And tomorrow.

Marrowgate was quiet the way a sickroom is quiet.

The fields were the worst. I'd seen blackened crops before—blight, fire, flood—but this was different. This was frost-kill in the shape of plenty. The wheat stood in rows, still standing, still in the shape of a harvest, except every stalk was black and brittle and rimed with white, as though someone had taken a healthy field and dipped it in the mountain's winter and left it there. The frost didn't melt. It was midsummer, the sun was out, a fat and friendly sun, and the frost on those black stalks glittered like it was proud of itself.

Theo stopped at the edge of the first field and just—stood there. His hand went to the oilcloth bundle at his chest, an unconscious gesture I was beginning to recognize. Touching the book when the world frightened him. Touching the thing that had frightened him first.

"This is frost," he said, in the tone of a man informing himself.

"Very good."

"No, I mean—" He crouched, and before I could stop him he reached out and touched a blackened stalk. It crumbled at his fingertip, and the frost on it didn't melt against his warm hand; it climbed. A thin white lace crept up one knuckle before he snatched his hand back. "Ulyana."

"I saw."

"Frost doesn't climb."

"No," I said. "It doesn't." I crouched beside him, careful not to touch, and looked at the field properly, the way I'd been taught to look at weather. And there it was, underneath the wrongness, underneath the horror: a pattern. The frost wasn't scattered. It moved in lines, faint as veins under skin, running from the field toward the village, toward the well, toward the meeting house. Running downhill, all of it, as if the cold were water and Marrowgate sat at the bottom of a basin that someone was slowly filling. "This is coming from the mountain," I said quietly. "Not the weather. The *mountain*."

"The covenant," Theo said. He said it the way he said everything about the covenant—carefully, like a man handling someone else's heirloom. "You still think I'm mad about the covenant."

"I think you're a lot of things, Bramble. Mad is the least of my worries right now." I stood. "Come on. Meeting house. I want to hear what they're telling each other, because I promise you it's not what they're telling that boy at the gate."

* * *

We didn't have to find the meeting house. The meeting house found us—or rather, a woman did, broad and weathered, with a ferryman's shoulders and a reeve's chain of office worn like it had gotten heavier every day since spring. She planted herself in our path in the village square, near a well whose bucket rope was furred white with frost, and looked us over with the flat professional gaze of someone who'd spent the season deciding whom to trust and had a very short list.

"Through-travelers," I said, before she could ask. "Supplies, a bed, gone by morning."

"No one's gone by morning in three months," she said. Her voice was rough and level. "Where'd you cross?"

"Thornfell."

"Which pass?"

"The one that doesn't have a name."

Her eyes narrowed. "Smuggler."

"Guide," I said. "The distinction is mostly legal."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one used to be kept. "I'm Dagny. I'm reeve here, and ferry-mistress on the Marrow, when the Marrow isn't frozen. Which it is. In *midsummer*." She said the last word the way you'd spit out a bad tooth. "You came down off Thornfell, so you'll have seen the high snow. Tell me it's normal. Tell me the passes are just having a bad year, and I'll believe you, because I need to believe someone today and you're the first person in a week who isn't from the Order."

"The passes are having a bad decade," I said, and watched her face do the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do. "I'm sorry. I won't lie to you. Lying's for people selling something, and I'm only selling a bed for the night."

Dagny looked at me for a long moment. Then at Theo, and his book, and back. "You'd better come inside," she said. "And you'd better hear it from us, because the Order's version is going around, and the Order's version is that this is quarantine, and quarantine means the Order gets to decide who counts as sick."

* * *

The meeting house was full, though full meant something different now—maybe forty people in a space built for two hundred, and the empty benches not empty the way unused things are empty but the way *left* things are empty. A bell hung in the loft, and I noticed the rope had been taken down and coiled away on a high shelf, out of reach. Nobody rings the bell anymore. I was starting to understand why. A bell calls people together, and a bell also announces, and there were things in Marrowgate that the villagers did not want announced.

Dagny stood at the front and didn't ask for quiet, because there was already quiet, the deep kind, the kind that has weight. "These two crossed from Thornfell today," she said. "The woman's a guide. The man's—" she looked at Theo, at his ink-stained fingers and his scholar's coat and the bundle he held like a child holds a doll in a lightning storm, "—something else. They've seen the mountain. I say we ask them what the mountain's doing."

A man near the front, old, with a farmer's ruined hands, said: "Ask them if it's the long winter. Ask them if it's coming back." And around the room, people flinched—actually flinched—at the words, the way you flinch from a name you've been taught not to say.

"The long winter," Theo repeated softly, beside me. "That's in the book," he whispered. "That's—" He stopped himself, which was a first. Theo Bramble, learning discretion at last, or at least learning it in the presence of forty frightened people.

An old woman spoke from the shadows near the wall, and I guessed she was the boy's gran, the one with the rules about the Order and the frost. "My grandmother saw the last of it," she said, not to us, to the room, the way you tell a story that's been told so many times it's worn smooth as a river stone. "Before the covenant held proper. She said the winter didn't come like winter comes. It didn't build, it didn't blow in, it didn't give you a gray morning to prepare. It just *was*, one day, and the wells froze from the bottom up, and the fire wouldn't take no matter what you fed it, and the frost walked. She said the frost walked in lines, like it knew where it was going." She looked at me. "You came down the mountain. Did you see lines?"

My mouth was dry. "The frost in your fields moves in lines. Toward the well. Toward here."

The room made a sound I'll remember until I die. Not a gasp—gasp is too dramatic, too story-shaped. It was a settling sound, like a house in cold weather, like forty people's spines all going rigid at once. The old woman nodded slowly, unsurprised, and that was somehow the worst part. She'd been waiting for confirmation the way you wait for a doctor.

"Then it's the covenant," she said. "The covenant's failing, and the winter's leaking through, and the Order will come and call it sickness and burn what's sick." She said it flatly, a fact among facts. "They did it at Harrow's Bend. My cousin's daughter married a Harrow's Bend man. There's no Harrow's Bend now. There's a scorched field and a stone that says QUARANTINED, and the Order's records say fever."

"Fever," Dagny said bitterly. "In midsummer. With frost on the wells."

"Fever's what the Order calls anything it doesn't understand and can't tax."

I should have said something. I was the guide; guiding was what I did; I could have told them which passes were open, which valleys would take them, how to move forty people over ground that wanted them dead. Instead I stood there with Theo's shoulder almost touching mine and felt something crack open in my chest that I'd been keeping boarded shut for six years. Because I knew the Order. I'd *been* the Order. I knew exactly how a village like this looked from inside a gray cloak: a line in a ledger, a problem with a solution, a quarantine with a torch attached. I had stood in rooms like this one and told frightened people that surrendering their homes was the same as saving them.

"Where do you go," I heard myself ask, "if it comes?"

Dagny looked at me. "If what comes?"

"The winter. The frost walking. When it reaches the village proper—and it will, it moves downhill and downhill is where you all live—where do you go?"

"Nowhere," said the old woman. "The Marrow's frozen. The ferry's dead. The passes—" she nodded at me, almost a smile, "—the passes belong to smugglers and the Order doesn't know them, but we're farmers. We don't know them. There's nowhere, guide. That's what the long winter is. It's the season with nowhere in it."

* * *

We took a room above the tavern, which was less a room than a corner with a curtain, and I sat on the bed with my ledger of exits open in my head, the way I always did, counting doors and windows and roof angles, and for the first time in six years the arithmetic came up short. Two exits. A village with one road, a frozen river, and a wall of mountain I knew like my own hand—and I could not for the life of me find a third way out that didn't leave forty people behind.

Theo sat across from me with the book unwrapped, drying its water-swollen pages by the candle, not reading, just looking at the cover like it might explain itself.

"Say it," I said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been not-saying since the well. You've got a face like a held breath."

He was quiet a moment. The candle fluttered, though the window was shut. "In the book," he said, "there's a passage about the covenant failing. It's not a warning, exactly. Prophecies aren't warnings, they're—descriptions. It describes frost that walks in lines. It describes a village with a bell no one rings." He looked up. "It describes tonight, Ulyana. The date. It's dated. A hundred years before tonight."

The candle fluttered again. I thought about the boy at the gate with his too-long spear and his arithmetic face. I thought about frost climbing a living man's knuckle. I thought about a scorched field with a stone that said QUARANTINED, and a gray-cloaked order I used to belong to, and how they'd come through in spring asking about the cold, which meant they knew, they'd known for months, and they'd sent no one, done nothing, except prepare the vocabulary for burning.

"My name's not in it," I said. It came out flat. "Whatever your book says, whatever it describes—my name's not in it. I'm a guide. I know the passes. That's the whole of my involvement, and tomorrow we go through Ferrow's Landing and you find your scholars and I find my next caravan, and none of this is mine."

Theo didn't argue. That was the worst of it. He just wrapped the book back up, gentle as bandaging a wound, and said, "Get some sleep. We leave at first light."

But I didn't sleep. I lay in the dark listening to the tavern below—Dagny's low voice, the old woman's, the creak of the frozen well rope—and somewhere past midnight I heard the sound I'd been dreading without knowing I dreaded it. Hooves. Many hooves, coming up the valley road, in the dead hours when nothing honest travels. And under the hooves, faint, from the direction of Thornfell, a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat: the mountain's storms waking, one after another, like dogs answering a whistle.

I was at the window before I'd decided to move. Down the valley road, torches. Gray cloaks. And at their head, on a white horse, straight-backed and terrible, a woman I would have known by her silhouette alone, by the way she sat a horse like a verdict.

Serapha. My mentor. My judge. The woman who'd held the torch at my trial and never once looked me in the eye.

The Order had found me.
