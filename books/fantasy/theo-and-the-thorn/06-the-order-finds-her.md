## 6. The Order Finds Her

There are three things a smuggler's guide learns before she learns anything else: how to read weather, how to read men, and how to disappear.

I had spent seven years perfecting the third. Seven years of gray wool and unremarkable boots, of paying my debts in exact coin so no one remembered owing me anything, of laughing at the right moments in taverns without ever saying anything worth repeating. I had made myself into a rumor with legs. A convenience. The kind of woman you hired through a third party and forgot by spring.

And now Serapha Voss was riding up the valley road toward my door at the head of twelve gray cloaks, and every one of those years felt like a sandcastle built below the tideline.

"Ulyana." Theo's voice behind me, hushed, as if the word itself might carry. He'd seen my face, apparently, and drawn his own conclusions. "Who is that?"

"The past," I said. "With torches."

The book was in my satchel. It had been in my satchel since the night I burned the page and watched the ink crawl back like moss over stone, and I will not pretend I hadn't thought about throwing it in the river. I'd stood at the ford for a full quarter hour, actually, weighing it in my hands. But the words came back when burned, and I didn't much like my odds against whatever brought them back. So the book stayed, and it weighed nothing—leather, paper, water damage—and it weighed everything, all at once, which is the trick of cursed objects and bad debts alike.

"They're stopping," Theo said.

They were. At the wellhead, where the road bent and any rider worth her spurs would want to be seen from the inn's windows. Serapha dismounted with the unhurried precision of a woman who had never once been late to anything she considered important, and handed her reins to a soldier who took them like they were holy relics. Twelve storm-callers. The Order hadn't sent twelve storm-callers to collect overdue library books since the Sundering.

"That's a lot of gray for one scholar," I said.

"I did tell you someone died sending this book to me."

"You mentioned it." I turned from the window. My heart was doing something embarrassing, hammering away like an apprentice smith who'd been left alone with the forge. I put my hand flat on the table until it understood its place. "Here's what happens now. You go upstairs. You take the book."

"The book is yours to—"

"*Take the book,*" I said, and he went pale and quiet, which was at least obedient. "You take the book up those stairs, you find the loose stone behind the third roof beam—the one with the scratch on it—and you sit there with your knees hugged to your chest until either I come get you or the building comes down around us. Those are the options. There isn't a third."

"What are you going to do?"

I looked at him. Water-damaged book, ink-stained fingers, a face built entirely out of good intentions and poor survival instincts. Somewhere behind his eyes, his grandmother was probably screaming.

"I'm going to do what I do best," I said. "Disappear."

* * *

Serapha Voss had taught me everything I knew about storms, and most of what I knew about lying, though she'd have flayed me for calling it that. She preferred *diplomacy*. *Necessary discretion*. The gentle arts of telling people what kept the peace. She had silver in her black hair now—I could see it even across the yard, catching torchlight—and deeper lines around her mouth, but she sat her horse exactly the same, spine like a plumb line, chin level, eyes doing the arithmetic of a room before anyone spoke.

She'd done that arithmetic on me once, eleven years ago, in a trial chamber that smelled of tallow and fear-sweat. I'd been nineteen. I'd stood in the circle with my wrists bound in copper—the metal we use to still our own, because iron is for common criminals and storm-callers are never common—and I'd watched her face while they read out what I'd supposedly done. Called lightning into a sanctioned convoy. Killed four of our own. She had held the torch. She had read the verdict herself, in that clear bell of a voice, and she had never once looked me in the eye.

I used to think the not-looking was shame. Later, in the worst years, I decided it was satisfaction. Now, watching her cross my inn's yard like she owned the deed, I honestly couldn't say which would be worse to learn was true.

The taproom emptied the way taprooms do when gray cloaks come in—not all at once, but in a slow bleed of chairs scraping and mugs abandoned and eyes carefully elsewhere. Dagny wasn't due back from the ferry until morning, thank every indifferent god, and the innkeep, Marta, had the good sense to find urgent business in her cellar. That left me alone in the doorway of my own rented room, arms crossed, wearing the bored expression I kept for tax collectors and other natural disasters.

"Lady Voss." I let my voice flatten into the guide's drawl, lowland vowels, no trace of the order's clipped mountain accent. "We're full. Try the next town."

Serapha stopped three paces away. Up close, the years sat heavier on her. The lines weren't just around her mouth anymore; they'd colonized her whole face like frost claiming a windowpane.

"You know who I am," she said.

"Everyone knows who you are. First Voice of the Stormcaller Order, Warden of the High Reaches, terror of smugglers and small children everywhere." I shrugged. "Doesn't mean I have a bed for you."

"Your name is Ulyana Zhou."

My blood went cold and then hot, in that order, fast. "That's a coincidence of names. Common enough."

"It is not remotely common." Something flickered across her face—something that wasn't triumph, which unsettled me more than triumph would have. Grief, maybe. Or its well-dressed cousin. "You guide contraband through the Thornfell passes. You've done it for six years. You charge double in winter and you've never lost a client. The ferrywoman at Marrowgate speaks of you like a saint with a ledger."

"Dagny talks too much."

"And you were exiled from the order eleven years ago for a crime the records describe in remarkable detail." Serapha's voice dropped, and here was the terrible thing, the thing that made my hands want to shake: she said it gently. Like a mother setting down something breakable. "I have spent eleven years wondering where you went, Ulyana. You were the finest student I ever trained. And then last week, a report crossed my desk. A village saw freak snow in midsummer. A stranger asking after guides. And a name, whispered in a border tavern, attached to a woman with storm-grey eyes who walked through the Goat's Teeth pass in weather that kills sheep."

The silence stretched. In the cellar below, Marta's business had gone very quiet indeed.

"You have something," Serapha said. "A book. Water-marked, old, taken from the Ashvale archive before it burned. The man who carried it out of Ashvale is dead. The man who carried it to you is standing behind a door upstairs pretending he can't hear us, and breathing like a man running in his sleep." Her eyes didn't move to the stairs. That was the discipline of her; she kept them on me, because looking away from a storm-caller, even a disgraced one, was how you ended up wet. "Give it to me. Walk away clean. This doesn't have to end the way the last thing between us ended."

"There's nothing between us," I said. "There hasn't been since you held the torch."

Something moved behind her eyes then—a crack in the plaster, quickly patched—and I seized it, because seven years of smuggling teaches you that cracks are doors.

"Pardon," I repeated, tasting the word. "That's what you're offering? Surrender the book, and the order lifts my exile. Eleven years later. Just like that."

"Just like that."

"And if I don't?"

Serapha was quiet long enough that the fire popped in the hearth. When she spoke again, the gentleness was gone, packed away like a field kit.

"If you don't, I am authorized to retrieve the book by any means necessary, and to treat you as an agent of whatever power moved it into your keeping. The covenant is failing, Ulyana. The winter is real—you've seen Marrowgate's fields, or you will. The council believes the prophecy book is either the cause or the cure, and they have voted, unanimously, that we cannot afford to guess wrong." She stepped closer. Torchlight through the window turned her shadow long and thin across the floorboards, reaching for my feet. "I voted with them. I need you to understand that. Whatever you remember of me, remember that too: I voted for this."

"Then why offer pardon?"

"Because I am tired," she said simply, "of being the person who did that to you. And because I would rather the story end differently the second time."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what I felt in that moment, because memory is a liar and I'd rather catch it early.

I felt the pull. God help me, I felt it—eleven years of exile pressing on a bruise I'd convinced myself had healed, and here was the order's First Voice offering to lift it in a single sentence. Belonging. The bell tower at dawn. The smell of ozone and chalk. My name spoken in the roll call again instead of struck through. I had told myself for seven years that I didn't miss it, the way you tell yourself you don't miss a limb, and some traitorous part of me heard the word *pardon* and started packing.

But underneath the pull, colder and older, sat the memory of copper binding on my wrists, and a trial where the evidence fit my guilt like a glove fitted to a hand—which is to say, perfectly, suspiciously, in the way things only fit when someone has measured them first. And underneath that, the page I'd burned. *The exile who calls storms.* Tonight's date, inked a century before tonight.

They hadn't sent twelve storm-callers for a book. They'd sent twelve storm-callers because the book named me, and somewhere in the order's high chambers, people who had spent eleven years believing I was safely buried in obscurity had opened their ledgers and discovered their mistake.

Pardon wasn't mercy. Pardon was containment, offered politely, with a horse waiting outside.

"No," I said.

Serapha closed her eyes for half a heartbeat. "Ulyana—"

"You'll forgive me, Lady Voss, if the order's change of heart arrives a decade too late to move me. You want the book? Take it from me." I unfolded my arms, slow, letting her see my empty hands, and let a little of the old craft rise under my skin—just a taste, just enough that the lamp flames in the room leaned toward me like they'd missed me. I watched her feel it. Watched her shoulders set. Eleven years, and her body still answered mine the way it had in training yards: recognition first, regret second. "But you should know the last person who tried to take something from me by force was a Kellish bandit with fourteen men and a very confident smile. They found his boots on the ridge the next spring."

"You'd raise a hand against your own order."

"My own order?" The laugh came out of me ragged, with broken edges. "My own order tied me in copper and read my crimes off a page while I stood there innocent. My own order put me out in winter with nothing. My own order—" and here my voice did the thing voices do when you've kept a lid on them too long, cracking sideways, "—my own order never once asked me if I *did* it. Not you. Not anyone. Nineteen years old, and not one soul in that marble tower thought to ask."

For a moment—one raw, unguarded moment—Serapha looked at me the way she'd never looked at me in the trial chamber. Straight on. And what was in her face was worse than satisfaction and worse than shame, because it was *knowledge*. The look of a woman carrying a specific weight, a particular sin, with a date and a name on it.

"Ask you now," I said quietly. "Go on. You're the First Voice. Ask me if I called that lightning."

Her jaw worked. Outside, one of the soldiers shifted, leather creaking.

"I can't," Serapha said. "Not here. Not—" She stopped herself, and I watched her rebuild the wall behind her eyes brick by brick, the discipline sliding home, and oh, I knew that trick intimately, because I'd learned it from her and I'd been doing it for seven years straight. "Give me the book, Ulyana. Please. If you keep it, others will come. Worse than me. Veyra already petitioned the council to declare you a covenant threat and treat you accordingly, and Veyra does not make offers in taprooms."

"Veyra," I repeated. The name landed like a stone in still water. Magister Veyra, who had coached witnesses before my trial. Who had smiled at me afterward with genuine warmth, which I had never understood until this moment, and understanding it now curdled something in my stomach. "So that's who's frightened."

"That's who wins if you force this," Serapha corrected. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" I tilted my head. "From where I stand, the difference keeps getting smaller."

We looked at each other across three paces and eleven years. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, Theo was presumably hugging his knees behind a roof beam with the graceless competence of a man who had never hidden from anything more dangerous than a dissertation deadline.

"I gave you the terms," Serapha said at last. Her voice had gone formal, official, the verdict-voice, and hearing it again dropped ice down my spine. "Surrender the book and walk free. Refuse, and at dawn I execute my writ." She moved to the door, then paused, one hand on the frame, not turning around. "For what it's worth—and I know what it's worth, which is nothing—you were innocent. I have known that for eleven years. I have known it since the night before the trial, when I found the second witness list in Veyra's chambers, and I said nothing, because the alternative was tearing the order apart and I was a coward."

The words hit me like a wave hits a swimmer who thought she was past the breakers. I gripped the table's edge.

"You *knew*," I said.

"Goodnight, Ulyana."

She walked out into the torchlight, mounted her white horse, and sat there a moment in the yard, a silhouette against the peaks, straight-backed and terrible and—God help me—weeping, if the angle of her head meant anything, which I refused, absolutely refused, to let it mean.

Twelve tents bloomed in my yard like gray fungi. Torches were planted. A watch was set.

At dawn, she'd execute her writ.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked—Theo, shifting his weight behind the wall—and I stood alone in the taproom of the only place that had ever almost felt like mine, counting my money and counting my exits and coming up short on both, while outside, the wind began, very softly, to turn against us.

Because that was the other thing about storms. I'd taught Serapha everything I knew about reading them.

But she'd never learned what I knew about *calling* them.
