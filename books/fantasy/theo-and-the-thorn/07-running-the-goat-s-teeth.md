## 7. Running the Goat's Teeth

### Ulyana

The Goat's Teeth earned its name honestly, which is more than I could say for most things in Thornfell. Thirty-odd granite spires jutting from a spine of black rock, each one sharp enough to flay a man who leaned wrong, with gaps between them narrow enough that a loaded mule had to be talked through sideways, like a drunk aunt at a wedding. Smugglers loved it because the order's patrols wouldn't fly it in anything worse than clear weather. Storm-callers, you see, have a professional aversion to places where lightning has nowhere to go but into their ribs.

I was counting on that aversion.

"They're behind us," Theo said, for the fourth time in an hour, each time as though he were breaking news to me.

"I know."

"You *know*? You haven't looked back once."

"Looking back costs time, and time is the only currency we have left." I patted the lead mule's neck. She was called Turnip, and she had the sense of a philosopher and the temperament of one too—unbothered, faintly contemptuous. "Besides, I can hear them."

Theo cocked his head. "I don't hear anything."

"That's because they're being quiet. Quiet is loud, if you know what to listen for." I didn't explain further. Explaining meant talking about how I'd spent eleven years learning the sound of my own order moving through stone—the particular hush of boots wrapped in wool, the way storm-callers unconsciously dampened the wind around themselves, leaving a pocket of dead air that trailed behind them like a scent. Most people couldn't hear it. I'd been most people's teacher.

We were two days out of Marrowgate, three days ahead of Serapha by my best guess, and my best guess had been shrinking all morning. The frost on the southern slopes hadn't melted with the sunrise. It had spread north instead, climbing toward us like something patient. The covenant wasn't failing anymore. It was collapsing, and the order knew it, and somewhere in their ledgers my name sat next to the prophecy book's description in ink that was probably still wet.

"Tell me again why we're taking the ridge," Theo said. "There's a valley route. A perfectly good valley. With a river. Rivers are flat."

"The valley floods when the snowmelt comes off the Teeth, and the snowmelt is coming early this year." I nodded at the white line creeping down the peaks. "Also, the valley has one entrance and one exit, and both of them will have storm-callers standing in them looking smug. The Teeth have forty entrances and no exits, which sounds worse until you realize it means nobody's watching any of them."

"That logic is deranged."

"Thank you. It's kept me alive."

He muttered something that might have been a prayer or an insult—I'd stopped trying to distinguish, with scholars—and went back to clutching his satchel, where the book rode against his chest like a second heartbeat. I tried not to look at it. Every time I did, I saw the page I'd burned in the inn's stove, and the words crawling back up through the char like green shoots through ash: *the exile who calls storms shall stand upon the Teeth.* I'd told myself it was coincidence. I'd told myself a lot of things lately, and my voice was getting hoarse from it.

We made the first saddle by noon. The wind up there came off the icefields in long, sawing gusts, and it carried the smell of snow that shouldn't exist in high summer. Turnip picked her way across the knife-edged traverse with her ears flat, and I walked backward half the time, watching the southern sky.

That was where I saw them.

Three figures on the ridge below us, maybe a mile back, moving fast. Too fast. They weren't climbing; they were *gliding*, riding a cushion of stilled air the way a boat rides calm water, and the sight of it hit me in the sternum like a fist. That was order-craft. That was the technique Serapha herself had invented, the one they only taught to hunters.

"Ulyana?" Theo had gone pale. He'd followed my eyes. "Is that—"

"Yes."

"How fast?"

"Faster than us. Maybe twice as fast on open ground." I turned and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. The Teeth ran six miles east to west. We were a mile in. Behind us, open ridge. Ahead of us, the worst section—the actual teeth, where the spires crowded together and the path became a rumor. If we stayed on the ridge, they'd run us down before we cleared the third spire.

If we dropped off the ridge, there was nothing below but scree fields and a drop that would make a nice epitaph.

"There's a cut," I said slowly, thinking aloud. "Between the second and third spire. A chimney, maybe eight feet wide, drops down through the mountain's shoulder and comes out in a hanging valley on the north side. If we can reach it before they close the distance—"

"If we can't?"

"Then you'll want to make peace with whatever gods scholars make peace with. Quickly. While running."

* * *

### Theo

I want it noted, for the historical record I someday intend to write, that I did not scream. Much.

The chimney was exactly as wide as Ulyana had promised and exactly as terrifying as she had not mentioned. We lowered the mules first—Turnip descended the thing with more dignity than I managed, which I've chosen not to take personally—and then we climbed down after them, hand over hand, while the rock bit my palms and the wind tried to peel me off the wall like old wallpaper. Above us, at the rim of the ridge, the light changed.

Not darkened. *Stilled*. The way air goes when someone with the blood holds it still, pressing it flat like linen under a book. I'd read about it. Reading about it and feeling it settle over your shoulders like a cold hand are different educations entirely.

"They're here," I whispered.

"I know." Ulyana was above me on the last stretch of chimney, wedged into the rock, and her face had gone very still in a way I was learning to fear. Not afraid-still. *Remembering*-still. "They'll rope down. Four minutes, maybe five, before they reach the bottom."

"And then?"

"And then they take the book, and they take you, and they take me back to the Spire for a trial that ended eleven years ago with my name already written in the verdict." She said it flatly, the way you recite something you've rehearsed in the dark. Then she looked down at me, and something moved behind her eyes that I recognized, because I'd seen it in my grandmother's face the night the archive burned: the look of a person deciding what they're willing to become.

"Ulyana," I said carefully, "what are you—"

"Get to the valley floor. Take the mules to the far wall. There's an overhang—a shelf of rock, you'll see it. Get under it and stay there."

"What are you going to do?"

She didn't answer. She was already climbing back up.

I should tell you what I know now, which is more than I knew then. I know that storm-craft is forbidden to exiles—not discouraged, not frowned upon, *forbidden*, written into the oath they made her swear with her hand on the storm-bell: *neither call nor shape nor sing, on pain of the mountain's own judgment.* I know that the oath isn't a formality. The old texts in my grandmother's margins say the oath binds into the blood itself, a knot in the marrow, and that breaking it doesn't just draw the order—it draws the mountain. I know that Ulyana Zhou had kept that knot tied for eleven years, through winters and betrayals and every small humiliation exile invents, because keeping it was the last piece of herself the order hadn't taken.

And I know that at the mouth of that chimney, with three hunters roping down toward her and me crouched under a rock shelf holding a mule's bridle like a coward, she untied it.

I felt it happen before I understood it. The pressure in the air reversed—all that stilled weight above us suddenly *moved*, and the whole hanging valley seemed to inhale. Then Ulyana stepped out onto the scree at the chimney's base, alone, arms loose at her sides, and raised one hand, palm up, the way you'd offer bread to a bird.

The storm answered.

I don't have better words than that, and I'm supposed to be a man of words. Lightning didn't strike so much as *arrive*, threading down out of a cloudless sky in three white veins, and thunder detonated inside the chimney with such force that the whole mountainside rang like a struck bell. The hunters' still-air cushion shattered—they fell the last thirty feet, tumbling, and the wind Ulyana pulled off the icefields hit them before they landed, driving them into the scree and pinning them there like specimens under glass. Snow came down in a horizontal sheet. The temperature dropped so fast my breath froze midair and drifted past me in little glittering ghosts.

And through all of it, Ulyana stood in the center with her hair whipping around her face, and she was *laughing*.

It wasn't a happy laugh. Or rather—it was, and it wasn't. It was the laugh of someone drinking water after years of thirst and finding, underneath the relief, a kind of grief. Eleven years of swallowing her own voice, and the voice came out of her like this. Like weather. Like a god clearing its throat.

Then it stopped. All at once, the way a door closes. The wind died, the snow settled, and Ulyana stood swaying in sudden silence with her hand still raised, staring at it as if it belonged to a stranger.

The hunters were alive. I could see them struggling up out of the drifts, dazed, one of them bleeding from the scalp. She could have killed them. Everyone on that mountainside knew she could have killed them, and she hadn't, and somehow that frightened me more than the alternative.

"Ulyana!" I stumbled out from the overhang. "Are you—are they—"

"Go." Her voice was raw. "They'll recover in minutes. We need to be over the north wall before they can signal."

"Signal what?"

She looked at me, and for the first time since I'd met her, she looked genuinely afraid.

"Everything," she said. "They'll signal everything."

* * *

### Ulyana

Here is what they don't tell you about breaking an oath: it feels wonderful, and then it feels like the worst thing you've ever done, and then those two feelings refuse to resolve, and you carry them both up a mountain at the same time.

My hands were shaking. Not from the power—from the *stopping* of it. When I'd sworn the oath at nineteen, kneeling on the Spire floor with Serapha's hand heavy on my head, they'd told me the binding would feel like a door closing. It had. For eleven years I'd lived on one side of that door, and I'd hated it, and I'd also built my entire life on top of it, the way you build a house on a fault line and pretend the ground is ground.

Now the door was open, and everything I'd buried under it was loose in the house.

"You used the third form," Theo said. We were climbing the north wall of the hanging valley, and he said it quietly, without accusation, which was somehow worse. "The chain-lightning variant. My grandmother's notes describe it. It was banned after the Border Wars."

"You read my order's craft manuals?"

"I read *everything*. It's a disease." He paused on the ledge beside me, breathing hard. "You could have taken the first form. Simple strike. Less... spectacular."

"Spectacular was the point." I didn't look at him. "Serapha taught me that. If you must break rules, break them loudly enough that everyone spends the next week talking about your audacity instead of looking for your pattern." I finally glanced over. "She also taught me that lesson so I'd never use it against the order. There's a certain poetry in that, if you squint."

"You're making jokes."

"I'm always making jokes."

"You're making jokes *faster than usual*. In my experience that means you're terrified."

Gods, I hated how quickly he'd learned to read me. Two weeks. Two weeks of shared roads and guarded conversations, and the scholar had assembled me like a manuscript with missing pages. "Of course I'm terrified," I said, because lying to him had started to cost more than truth. "Do you know what happens now? The moment I called that strike, every warding-stone within fifty miles felt it. Every storm-caller felt it. They've spent eleven years listening for exactly that sound—my sound. I have a signature, Theo. Everyone does. Mine hasn't been heard since my trial, and now it's ringing across three valleys like a dinner bell."

"So they'll come faster."

"They'll come *differently*. Before today, I was a errand. Recover the book, retrieve the exile, tidy the paperwork. Now I'm a precedent. An exile who broke the oath and lived—that's a story, and stories are contagious. If I get away, every disgruntled acolyte with a grudge starts wondering what their oath actually weighs." I pulled myself over the final lip of the wall and stood up into the northern wind, which was clean and cold and mercifully ordinary. "Serapha won't send hunters anymore. She'll come herself. And she won't be offering pardons."

Below us, the north country opened up: grey-green foothills falling away toward the lowlands, a thread of river catching the afternoon sun, and far off, barely visible, the smoke of Marrowgate rising straight and thin into a sky that had no business being that blue in a world where frost grew in midsummer.

Theo came up beside me. He was quiet for a long moment, and when he spoke, he wasn't looking at the view. He was looking at my hands.

"It hurt you," he said. "Using it. Even though it saved us."

"It didn't save us. It bought us a day, maybe two." I flexed my fingers. They'd stopped shaking, mostly. "And yes. It hurt. Not the way you mean. It fit, Theo. That's the problem. Eleven years of pretending I'm just a guide with a good memory for passes, and the moment I let go, it was like—" I searched for it. "Like putting on a coat I forgot I owned. Perfect fit. Warm. And every year I wore the other coat instead, the one that chafed, because wearing it was the price of being allowed to exist somewhere."

"And now?"

"Now the order knows the coat exists." I started down the slope, because standing still felt dangerous, and because if I kept moving I could pretend the tightness in my chest was just the altitude. "Now I have to decide whether I ever put it back on. And the truly insulting part—" I laughed, and it came out bitter, "—the truly insulting part is that your ridiculous book probably has an opinion."

"The book has opinions about everything," Theo said. "That's rather its defining feature."

We walked. Behind us, far back along the ridge, a single point of light flared and died—a signal-lamp, or a warding-stone answering the one before it, a message relayed from peak to peak toward the Spire faster than any rider. I watched it go and counted, the way I always counted: coin, exits, days.

Coin: enough for a month, if we ate poorly. Exits: fewer than yesterday. Days: two, possibly three, before the full weight of the order came over those mountains like winter itself.

And somewhere in my chest, under the guilt and the exhilaration and the dread, a smaller, quieter thing that I refused to examine too closely: the memory of the storm arriving when I called it. Not reluctantly. Not obediently.

*Gladly.*

As if it had been waiting eleven years for permission. As if it had never once considered me exiled at all.

"We should move faster," Theo said.

"No," I said, watching the signal-light die on the farthest peak, and feeling the words arrive with the certainty of weather. "We should move smarter. Because that lamp isn't just calling hunters." I turned to him, and the wind coming down off the Teeth was cold on my neck, colder than it should have been, colder than summer had any right to be. "That's a summons. They're waking the Warden's Vigil, Theo. All of it. Which means somewhere behind us, my old mentor is opening a vault that hasn't been opened since the last covenant crisis—and whatever's in it, the order built it to stop someone exactly like what I just became."

Theo went very still. "What's in the vault?"

"I don't know." I smiled, and it felt like baring teeth. "But I burned a page out of your book once, and the words came back. Let's hope the vault is less persistent."
