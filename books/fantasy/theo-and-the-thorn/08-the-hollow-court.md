## 8. The Hollow Court

Theo's POV

The storm caught us an hour after we left the Goat's Teeth behind, and I learned something about Ulyana that night: she apologizes to the weather.

Not out loud. Not in words. But she walked at the head of our little column with her chin lifted and her eyes half-closed, and when a gust tried to knock Pip off Dagny's shoulders, Ulyana turned into the wind like a woman greeting a rude relative—*I know, I know, I'm sorry, I know*—and the gust, absurdly, gentled.

Ivo saw it too. He said nothing, but he walked closer to her after that, the way you walk near a hearth you're not sure is safe.

We were three days out from Marrowgate's ashes by then. Dagny's arm was splinted where a beam had caught her during the evacuation—not the burning itself; she'd been pulling children out of a smokehouse when it collapsed—and she carried the pain like she carried everything else, silently and with visible contempt for anyone who noticed. Pip had not spoken since the village. The child simply pointed, now, in the direction we should go, and because Pip's directions had thus far been uncannily correct, we followed.

It was Pip who found the caverns.

The child stopped so suddenly that Dagny nearly trod on them, set them down, and stared at the mountainside ahead of us with the particular intensity of someone listening to a conversation we couldn't hear. Then Pip raised one small hand, palm flat, and pushed it toward the rock as though pressing against glass.

"There's a door," Ivo said flatly.

"There's a door," Pip agreed, speaking for the first time in three days, and their voice came out rusty and strange, "and it's sad."

*It's sad.* I wrote that down later, in the margins of my notes, because I have learned that when Pip describes something, the description is more accurate than anything I could measure.

Ulyana stood before the blank cliff face for a long moment. Snow gathered in her hair. I watched her do the thing she does—counting exits, I'd begun to call it; her eyes flicking left, right, up, cataloguing every way this situation could go wrong—before she pressed her palm against the stone exactly where Pip had indicated.

"Hello," she said, to a mountain. As though this were normal.

The mountain, I can only report, answered. A seam of pale light split the rock from crown to ground, widening into an archway tall enough for Dagny to walk through upright, and warm air rolled out over us smelling of loam and cold water and something faintly, achingly sweet, like flowers remembered rather than smelled.

"Well," said Dagny. "That's either shelter or a stomach."

"Only one way to find out," said Ulyana, and stepped through.

* * *

I want to describe the cavern-city properly, because everything that happened afterward happened against its backdrop, and because I suspect I am going to be the last person to see it whole.

Imagine a cathedral built by hands that loved curves more than corners. The cavern opened into a vast hollow maybe four hundred paces across, its ceiling lost in a haze of blue-green luminescence that dripped down from veins of crystal in the rock like light given a liquid form. Terraces had been cut into the walls in spirals, and on the terraces stood houses—but not built houses. *Grown* ones. Pale, smooth-walled structures that curved up out of the living stone like mushrooms the size of cottages, their doorways arched, their windows glowing faintly from within.

Stairways of root and stone wound between the terraces. Bridges of woven silverwood spanned gaps I didn't want to look down. And everywhere, everywhere, there were gardens—or what had been gardens. Beds of white-flowered plants gone grey and brittle. Trees whose leaves hung in curls of ash-color. Fountains that still ran, clear water over carved basins, feeding channels choked with dead reeds.

And people. Or—the word *people* kept snagging in my throat, so let me be precise, because precision was my trade before any of this: there were figures. Tall, most of them, taller than Dagny, willowy, with skin in shades of bark-brown and moss-grey and one startling individual with skin like birch-pale porcelain. Their ears swept back into points. Their eyes—

Their eyes were the worst part. Not because they were frightening. Because they were *familiar*. I've seen that look in the faces of refugees, of famine survivors, of old men in war museums staring at cases of uniforms. It's the look of someone who has lost something so large that the mind has quietly stopped reaching for it, the way a tongue stops probing a missing tooth.

They did not attack us. They did not greet us. They simply... paused, as we came down the great entry ramp, the way a field of wheat pauses when wind passes through it, and then resumed whatever they had been doing—which, I began to realize with growing unease, was very little. One figure sat at a fountain's edge trailing fingers through water that they did not drink. Another stood in a dead garden holding a watering can made of shell. A third lay on a terrace in the attitude of someone watching clouds, except the ceiling held no clouds, only light, and had for what looked like a very long time.

"They're starving," Ulyana said quietly, beside me. Not for food. I understood her meaning anyway.

"Their court," Ivo murmured. His voice had gone rough. "When the covenant broke, the courts were anchored to it. This must be—" he shook his head. "I only ever heard the order's version. That the fae abandoned the lowlands out of spite."

"Spite," Ulyana repeated, very softly, looking at the figure with the watering can. "Yes. Look how spiteful."

Dagny crouched down beside Pip. "You knew," she said. Not unkindly. "That's why you wouldn't talk. You could hear them."

Pip nodded, then shrugged, then did something between the two that managed to convey *yes, and also it's complicated, and also please don't make me explain*. Then the child pointed again—upward, along the spiral terraces, toward the largest grown structure at the cavern's crown, which was less a house than a palace, if a palace could wilt. Its windows were dark. Its doors stood open.

An invitation or an abandonment; with fae architecture, I was beginning to learn, the distinction was often academic.

"She's home," Pip said. "The sad queen. She wants to know why the storm-girl smells like the mountain."

Every head turned to Ulyana.

"I don't know what that means," Ulyana said, in the tone of a woman who suspected exactly what it meant and had decided, provisionally, not to think about it.

* * *

The climb to the palace took us past more of the fading fae, and I'll confess to a scholar's shameful instinct: I wanted to stop, to sketch, to ask questions. My notebook fairly burned in my satchel. But every time I slowed, one of the hollow-eyed figures would turn toward me with that terrible patient attention, as though I were a sound in an empty house, and my questions died of embarrassment.

One of them did speak to me, though. An elderly figure—I say elderly, though for all I know the fae measure age in centuries—seated on a stairway, mending a garment that had no visible tear. They looked up as I passed and said, in Common accented strangely, like wind through a keyhole:

"You carry her book."

My blood went cold in a way the cavern's warmth couldn't touch. "Whose book?"

But the figure had returned to mending the unbroken seam, and I got nothing else. *Her book.* I told myself they meant my grandmother. Of course they meant my grandmother. Who else? The prophecy book was hers, her handwriting, her obsession, her death.

Except that a century had passed since she wrote it, and this figure had spoken as though she were alive, and I found myself climbing the rest of those stairs considerably faster than dignity required.

Ulyana fell into step beside me. She didn't ask what the fae had said—she'd seen my face—but she matched my pace, and once, briefly, her shoulder brushed mine, and stayed there a half-second longer than accident required. Neither of us mentioned it. We were getting quite good, by then, at not mentioning things.

The palace doors opened onto a hall whose ceiling was a single vast crystal, dimming and brightening in slow rhythms, like breathing. Moss carpeted the floor. At the far end, on a throne that was less carved than *grown*—a chair of silverwood roots that had been persuaded, patiently, into the shape of majesty—sat the queen.

She was smaller than I expected. That was my first thought, absurdly: *she's small.* Grey-skinned, fine-boned, dressed in robes that might once have been magnificent and were now merely immaculate, faded the way a photograph fades. Her hair was white as frost. And her eyes, when they fixed on Ulyana, were the hollow eyes of her people—except that in her, the hollowness had a floor to it. Something at the bottom. Something still deciding whether to care.

"So," she said. Her voice filled the hall without effort, the way cold fills a valley. "The storm-child walks into my house wearing my sister's face."

Ulyana went very still beside me. I had catalogued, by then, perhaps forty of her stillnesses, and this one ranked among the top five.

"I don't have a sister," Ulyana said.

"No." The queen rose. Up close she was taller than she'd seemed, the way mountains are taller than they seem. She descended the two steps from her throne and circled Ulyana slowly, and I watched Ulyana endure being examined the way she endured everything: jaw set, hands loose, exits counted. "No, you don't. You have half of one. Half of a face I buried. Half of a bloodline I sealed shut with my own hands and grief enough to drown the lowlands." She stopped in front of Ulyana. "And now you come walking through my door smelling of storms and secrets, asking nothing, and I am supposed to believe that is an accident."

"I wasn't asking anything," Ulyana said evenly. "Your door opened. Pip said you wanted to meet me. If I've intruded on a grave, I'll go."

Something flickered across the queen's face—amusement, or its fossil. "A grave." She looked around her hall, at the dimming crystal ceiling, the grey gardens beyond, and her voice dropped to something almost human in its tiredness. "Yes. That is precisely what this is. Welcome to the Hollow Court, storm-child. We are what remains when a covenant dies and nobody tells the tenants." She turned, gesturing vaguely at the fading splendor. "Three generations ago, this court sang loud enough to crack stone. Now we sit in the dark and forget words. Do you know what it is to *forget words*, child? To reach for your own name some mornings and find the shelf empty?"

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

"You are Theo Bramble," she said then, turning to me, and I started badly enough that Dagny snorted. "Descended of the author. Yes, I can smell the ink on you. Ink and guilt." She studied me with those floorless eyes. "You brought her book into Thornfell. Do you understand what you've done?"

"It's a prophecy," I said. "About the covenant. About restoring—"

"It is a *key*," the queen said, "bound in leather and lies, and every power in these mountains has spent a century waiting for someone foolish enough to carry it through the front door." She smiled, and there was nothing warm in it, only an old, exhausted sharpness. "The Gilded Court knows you're here, by now. So does the order that burned Marrowgate. You have perhaps days. Possibly hours."

"Then help us," Ulyana said. It wasn't a plea. It had the flat pragmatism of all her negotiations: *here are the terms, name your price.*

The queen regarded her for a long moment.

"Help," she repeated. "You stand in a court of ghosts and ask the ghosts for aid." She drifted back toward her throne, trailing one hand along the rootwork, and I saw—for just a moment, before she mastered it—her fingers sink slightly into the wood, as though she were becoming less solid than her own palace. She noticed me noticing. She withdrew her hand. "I will tell you what I can spare, storm-child, which is truth, because truth is the only currency I have left that doesn't crumble. There is a keystone in my keeping. Has been, since the breaking. My court bled for it. We hid it where no gilded thief would think to look."

Hope moved through our little group like current through wire. I felt it in my own chest, ridiculous and immediate.

"And the price?" Ulyana asked. Always the price.

The queen settled onto her throne, and the hollow eyes closed, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet, almost gentle, which was somehow worse than the coldness.

"The price is a promise," she said. "A promise you cannot yet understand, made anyway. Sworn on your storm, sworn on your name—your *true* name, child, the one you don't know you're carrying." She opened her eyes. "Swear to me that when the covenant stands reforged, whatever shape it takes, whatever it costs—you will not let my court be forgotten again. Swear that the Hollow Court will have its place at the new table, or no table at all. And swear it *now*, before you know what it means."

Silence. The crystal ceiling breathed its slow light. Behind me, I heard Ivo shift, and Dagny mutter something that was probably a prayer and probably blasphemous.

Ulyana looked at the queen for a long, long moment. I knew her well enough by then to read the ledger behind her eyes: *help always costs more than coin; never sign what you haven't read; count your exits.*

And I knew, too, what she was weighing it against. The grey gardens. The watering can. The figure mending an unbroken seam. A whole people forgetting their own names in the dark while the world above argued over who owned the mountain.

"All right," Ulyana said finally. "But I want it witnessed. All of you." She turned and addressed the hall, and the handful of hollow-eyed fae who had drifted to its edges to listen. "Hear this. I swear on my storm and my name—whatever that name turns out to be—that when the covenant is remade, the Hollow Court keeps its place. Or there will be no covenant worth the name."

The effect was immediate and unsettling. Every fae in the hall turned their heads toward her at once, in perfect silence, and the dimming crystal overhead flared—a pulse of blue-white light that ran through the veins in the rock and out into the cavern beyond, and somewhere far below us, I heard something answer. Not words. More like a bell rung underwater.

The queen exhaled. For a moment she looked, impossibly, younger—as though the oath had paid her a single coin of strength from some account running dry.

"Vess," she said. "My name is Vess. Queen of what remains. And you, storm-child, will need this."

She drew something from her robes: a sliver of crystal, no longer than a finger, veined with the same blue-green light as the cavern ceiling. She held it out, and hesitated—one heartbeat, two, a miser's hesitation over her last coin—before pressing it into Ulyana's palm.

"A shard of the Bell of Hours," Vess said. "The first keystone sleeps in a drowned chapel, west of here, beneath the Merrowmere. This will wake it. This will also wake what guards it, and I will not lie to you: the guardian does not test your strength. It tests your *choices*. It will show you the worst night of your life and ask you to hold it up to the light." Her gaze lingered on Ulyana. "For you, I suspect, it will show the night you were condemned. And I will tell you now, freely, what I smelled on you the moment you entered my hall, because you'll learn it soon enough and better you hear it from me: the official story and your memory do not agree. They were never meant to."

Ulyana's hand closed around the shard until her knuckles went white. I watched her not ask the question screaming behind her teeth—*what do you mean, what do you know, tell me*—and file it away instead, into that inner vault of hers, next to everything else.

"When do we leave?" she said instead.

"Tomorrow, at first light. My people will guide you to the lake paths." Vess settled back, and the exhaustion reclaimed her, settling over her features like snow. "Rest tonight. Eat what you can—the kitchens still remember how, barely. And storm-child—" I was already turning to follow the others out, but her voice caught Ulyana at the doorway, and mine too. "One more thing. The book your scholar carries. When you sleep tonight, keep it away from the walls. The old ink listens. And the last thing my court needs, in the state we're in, is to overhear a prophecy."

* * *

We were given a guest chamber on the third terrace—a round room grown, not built, with walls of pale silverwood and beds of moss deep enough to swallow a body whole. Dagny claimed the corner nearest the door out of pure professional habit and fell asleep mid-sentence. Ivo took the opposite corner and did not sleep at all; I heard him breathing awake for hours. Pip curled against Ulyana's side without asking, the way cats choose their people, and Ulyana—who I had once seen threaten a smuggler with a lightning strike for touching her pack—went carefully, carefully still, and let the child sleep.

Which left the book, and me, and the wall.

I should explain that I hadn't slept properly in days. I should also explain that Vess's warning had done the precise opposite of its intention, because I am, at bottom, a scholar, and scholars are creatures who touch the stove specifically because they've been told it's hot. I sat cross-legged with the prophecy book open on my knees, drying its water-swollen pages by firelight, telling myself I was performing routine conservation.

I was reading it.

And the walls were listening.

I noticed it gradually, the way you notice someone humming along to a song you thought you were reading silently. The silverwood around us had a grain to it, whorls and lines, and the longer I read, the more I could have sworn the whorls were shifting—subtly, organically, the way clouds move if you watch too long. And the firelight seemed to be catching the ink differently. The letters had a faint sheen, a wetness, and as I read the old verses about the warden *of both bloods*, about the sacrifice clause everyone hoped was metaphorical, I could feel the words paying attention back.

*Keep it away from the walls,* Vess had said.

I looked up. Across the chamber, Ulyana sat watching me, Pip asleep against her ribs, her eyes reflecting firelight.

"You feel it too," she said. It wasn't a question.

"The ink's awake," I said. "Or the room is. Or—" I laughed, and it came out shakier than I intended. "Honestly, at this point I've lost track of which parts of this journey are haunted."

"All of them," Ulyana said. "Probably all of them." She was quiet a moment. Then, without looking at me, in the voice she used for things that cost her: "Theo. What Vess said. About my memory not matching the story." Her thumb moved over the crystal shard in her other hand, back and forth, back and forth. "What if she's right? What if I go to this chapel and the guardian shows me that night, and it's not—what if the thing I remember isn't the thing that happened?"

"Then you'll finally know," I said. "And knowing is worse, usually. But it's also the only place to build anything from." I hesitated. "My grandmother wrote in this book that truth is a foundation, not a comfort. She underlined it. Twice."

"Your grandmother sounds exhausting."

"She was," I said, and meant it fondly. "She was."

Outside the chamber door, somewhere in the deeps of the dying city, the underwater bell rang again—once, soft, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Pip stirred against Ulyana's side and murmured, in a voice thick with dreams, words none of us wanted to examine closely:

"The water remembers her name."

Ulyana looked down at the sleeping child. Then at me. Then at the crystal shard in her palm, glowing faintly now, pulsing in time with the distant bell.

"First light," she said, and her voice was steady, and her knuckles were not. "Let's go meet whatever's been remembering me."
