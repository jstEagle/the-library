## 9. Briar and Bargain

The Hollow Court didn't have doors. It had thresholds, which was worse, because a door you could lock and a threshold only ever meant *you may enter if we let you*, and I had spent thirty-one years building my life around the first kind and avoiding the second.

Theo walked through it like a man walking into a cathedral, head tipped back, mouth slightly open, one hand pressed to his chest as though his heart needed restraining. I walked through it like a woman walking into a debt collector's office, because that's what it was.

The cavern-city spread below us in tiers of pale stone, lit by veins of something that wasn't quite fungus and wasn't quite moonlight — a blue-green glow that pooled in the hollows of carved arches and made every shadow look like it was thinking about moving. The fae who lived there — *lived* being a generous word — stood or sat or drifted along the edges of the great central gallery, watching us with eyes like banked coals. They weren't hostile. That was the terrible part. Hostility would have been simple. What they were was *faded*, the way a tapestry fades when it hangs too long in a window, all their colors gone to gray at the edges, their outlines soft, as if the world had already half-forgotten them and they were politely waiting to be forgotten the rest of the way.

"They're starving," Theo murmured. "Not for food."

"Don't," I said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't *feel* at them. They can tell. And pity is worse than a knife down here." I kept my voice flat and my hands loose at my sides, where the storm in me could reach them fast if it needed to. "You wanted to see what covenant failure looks like when it's had a century to settle in. Look. Then stop looking like that."

He tried. He genuinely tried. But Theo Bramble had been built by some careless god out of curiosity and conscience in roughly equal measure, and neither of those shut off on command.

A child-shaped figure detached itself from a pillar as we passed — a girl, maybe, or something wearing the memory of a girl, her skin the color of birch bark, her hair drifting around her face as though underwater. She reached toward Theo's sleeve. Didn't touch. Just held her hand an inch away from the wool, close enough to feel its warmth, and her whole gray face went briefly, unbearably bright.

"Move along," I said gently, and she did, folding back into the dark between pillars like smoke returning to a doused fire.

Theo's jaw was tight. "How many?"

"Of them? More than the order admits exist. Fewer than there were last winter." I'd been through the Hollow Court twice before, years apart, running cargo for smugglers who paid extra for routes that skirted the gilded court's territory. First time, the galleries had been full enough to echo. Second time, the echo had gotten bigger and the crowd smaller. You do the arithmetic once and then you spend the rest of your life not doing it again.

We were expected. Of course we were expected. Vess had known we were coming before we'd crossed her outermost threshold; the hollow ones felt footsteps in the stone the way I felt pressure changes in the air, and a queen — even a queen of the forgotten — keeps better books than any smuggler.

Her hall was at the bottom of everything, past galleries that got older and quieter the deeper we went, until the carvings stopped being decorative and started being *records*: names, genealogies, treaties, all of them worn smooth as river stones, as if generations of grieving hands had touched them on the way past. At the end of it stood a throne of black briar — actual briar, thorned and living, grown into the shape of a seat no human spine could tolerate — and in it sat the queen.

Vess had been beautiful once. You could see the architecture of it still, the way you can see the bones of a great house behind ivy. She was tall and thin as a winter branch, crowned in the same black briar as her throne, and her eyes were the only part of her that hadn't faded: two points of deep amber light, patient and old and absolutely awake.

"The exile," she said. Her voice was dry leaves and door hinges. "And the book-bearer. Sit."

There were no chairs. There was a ledge of stone that had probably been furniture in some earlier century. We sat on it anyway, because you don't refuse small courtesies from queens, and because my knees had opinions about the day we'd had.

"You came for the Bell of Hours," Vess said.

"We came for shelter," I said. "The Bell is a rumor."

"Everything true starts as a rumor. Everything false starts as a certainty, and your former order is full of certainties." She leaned forward, and the briar throne creaked like a ship. "I will give you the Bell's location. Exactly. Precisely. With the tide-tables and the guardian's name and the three words that open the drowned chapel's door."

Theo went very still beside me. I kept my face doing nothing, which is my best skill, and said, "And in exchange?"

"That," said Queen Vess, "is the interesting part."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about bargains with the faded: they don't want gold. Gold is for people who plan to keep living. They don't want power, or territory, or vengeance — vengeance requires a future, and futures are the first thing the covenant's collapse took from them. What they want, what they hoard in the deep places like dragons hoarding a different kind of treasure, is *promise*. A promise is a shape you cut out of tomorrow and hand to someone else. The hollow ones collect shapes of tomorrow the way misers collect coins, and they can wait a very long time to spend them.

"I offer you a blank promise," Vess said. "One favor, unnamed, uncapped, owed by you to me, callable at a time of my choosing."

"No," I said.

She smiled. It rearranged her whole faded face into something briefly, shockingly lovely, the way lightning rearranges a landscape. "You haven't heard the terms."

"There are no terms. A blank favor to a fae queen isn't a bargain, it's a leash with my name on the collar. I've read enough contracts. I've *been* a contract clause." I stood up, because sitting felt too much like agreeing. "I'll pay in something real. Information. Service. Storm-work. I know passes your scouts haven't found in four hundred years. I can call rain into the upper aquifers and buy your people another season of—"

"Of fading slightly more slowly," Vess said. "Yes. Generous. Insufficient. Do you know what my court was, exile, before the covenant began to rot?"

Theo answered before I could, quietly, from the ledge: "Warden-court. You *were* the covenant. You held the mountain's side of it." He'd been reading in the archives again. Of course he had. The man treated forbidden knowledge like other men treated ale.

Vess turned those amber eyes on him, and something in them flickered — surprise, maybe, or grief recognizing itself. "The scholar reads."

"The scholar reads too much," I muttered.

"My court held the eastern keystones. We were the hinge. When the pact was forged, my grandmother's grandmother set her own name into the stone of this mountain so that the winters would have somewhere to go that wasn't the lowlands. Every storm-warden since has drawn on that sacrifice without knowing whose it was. Your order prays to the mountain, little storm-caller. My family *is* the mountain's patience." She said it without pride. Pride needs an audience that remembers you. "And when the covenant cracked — when your humans broke faith first, whatever your histories claim — the weight of the failing fell on us. We have been absorbing a broken treaty for ninety years. You fade. I fade faster, because I hold the center of it."

The gallery around us had gone silent in the way empty rooms are silent, but this silence had texture. Listening. Hundreds of faded faces turned toward their queen, and I understood suddenly that they weren't listening to the negotiation. They were listening to her say aloud, perhaps for the first time in decades, what had been done to them.

"So the Bell of Hours," Theo said slowly. "It's yours. Your court's keystone."

"It was entrusted to us. Hidden in the drowned chapel beneath the Grey Tarn, with a guardian my house set over it." Vess settled back into the briar. "I could simply tell you. I could simply tell you and watch you walk out of my hall and lose yourself in the mountains and die of the order's hunters or the gilded court's flattery, and the Bell would stay lost, and my people would finish fading, and the winter would finish eating the borderlands, and everyone would be equally dead, just on different schedules. Or—" the smile again, brief and terrible, "—you can owe me."

"What would you even ask?" I demanded. "You're fading. What use is a favor from me in ten years when there's no court left to collect it?"

"Oh, exile." She sounded almost fond. "You think fading means finished. Fading means *concentrated*. Everything unimportant burns off. What remains is purpose, and purpose is very patient, and very precise. I will not need a large favor. I will need exactly one, at exactly the right moment, and I will know the moment when it comes, and you will not enjoy hearing about it, and you will do it anyway, because you will have promised."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then leave. Take the scholar. Climb back into the snow. Find the chapel yourselves, if you can — the tarn moves, the door lies, the guardian tests, and the order's maps are wrong in four places I could name for free, but won't, because free things teach humans nothing." She folded her long gray hands. "I am not threatening you. Threats require wanting something from you. I am simply describing the weather."

I hated her a little, right then, in the specific way you hate someone who is completely correct.

* * *

Theo and I took the argument outside — well, "outside" meaning a side gallery where the glow-veins ran thin and the carved names on the walls gave us something to look at instead of each other.

"We can't take it," I said.

"We can't find it without it."

"Those aren't the only options."

"Name a third one, then." He was doing that thing he did — arms crossed, chin down, arguing like a man defusing a bomb, careful and stubborn and secretly enjoying himself. "Because I've been counting our assets, Ulyana, and the list is short. One disgraced storm-caller with a price on her head. One scholar with a water-damaged book and no field skills whatsoever. Zero money after the waystation bill. Zero allies above ground. And a clock — the frost line moved another mile south while we were walking here. Dagny told me. Marrowgate's wells froze top to bottom."

"Dagny told you?" I raised an eyebrow. "You've been talking to my ferrywoman."

"*Your* ferrywoman. Possessive already. Interesting." He unwound that smug thread before I could pull it. "Point stands. We have nothing to bargain with except ourselves, and Vess knows it, and she's pricing accordingly."

"She's pricing a blank check, Theo. With fae. There's no such thing as a blank check with fae. Whatever she calls in, it'll be shaped exactly like the worst possible moment. She said so herself — she'll pick the time. That's the whole design. You don't sell someone a knife and let them choose the stabbing."

"Unless," he said slowly, "the stabbing is also the thing that saves you."

I glared at him. "Explain that sentence or withdraw it."

He thought about it properly, which I was learning to expect from him and which kept catching me off guard anyway. "Prophecies run on irony. It says you reforge the covenant *or* bury both realms in winter. What if Vess's favor, whenever she calls it, turns out to be load-bearing? What if the promise itself is a keystone — not stone, but oath? The old covenant was made of promises. Maybe the new one needs one more than it needs bells."

"That's either brilliant or the most elaborate rationalization I've ever heard from a man who owns one coat."

"Both things are frequently true." He looked at me then, straight on, no scholar-shuffle, no book between us. "You're afraid of it. Not of her — of promising. You've spent your whole adult life making sure you never owe anyone anything. Coin on the table, exits counted, debts settled by morning. Because the last institution that held your loyalty used it against you."

Well. It's unpleasant when a man you've known nine days reads you like marginalia.

"And you'd know all about family legacies used against you," I shot back, because fair was fair and I keep my ledger balanced.

"Yes," he said simply, and that took the wind out of it, because he never fought with bluster, he just set the truth down on the table between us like a stone and waited to see what I'd do with it.

* * *

I went back alone. Theo offered to come; I told him negotiations went better without an audience of conscience. Really I wanted to look at Vess without him watching me decide, because I've learned that I make my worst choices in front of witnesses and my best ones alone in the dark, and this one mattered too much to get wrong.

The hall was emptier when I returned. Some of the faded had drifted off, or further into themselves; a few remained along the walls, motionless, keeping their queen company the way candles keep a room company. Vess hadn't moved. Queens of dying courts don't fidget. Fidgeting is for people who believe things change.

"One question," I said, "before I answer anything."

"Ask."

"When you call the favor in — will you tell me what it's for?"

She considered that longer than she'd considered anything yet. The glow-veins pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat under stone.

"If it serves you to know, yes. If knowing would make you refuse, no." No apology in it. None asked for, none offered. "That is the nature of the thing, exile. That is why it costs what it costs. A named favor is a purchase. An unnamed one is a trust, and trusts are worth more than purchases precisely because you cannot audit them."

"And if your moment comes and I'm dead?"

"Then the promise dies with me unspent, and my court ends owing *you* a debt instead, and my heir — if any heir remains — will honor it." Something moved behind the amber, brief and cold. "I do not make traps, storm-caller. Traps are for courts that expect to win. I am making an anchor. There is a difference, and you are clever enough to feel it even if you cannot name it."

Maybe that was the sales pitch. Maybe it was true. With fae, and especially with faded fae, those are usually the same document printed twice.

I thought about the child reaching for Theo's sleeve. About the galleries getting quieter every year. About Serapha's face when she'd offered me pardon for the book — *pardon*, as if the crime had been mine, as if the ledger could be balanced with a word from the same mouths that wrote the lie. I had promised myself, on the ridge above Marrowgate, standing in frost that should have been midsummer grass, that I would stop letting institutions define the terms of my life. Vess wasn't an institution. Vess was one old woman on a throne of thorns, holding a door shut against a winter with her body, asking for one hand to help hold it.

Help always costs more than coin. My mother — whoever and whatever she'd been — supposedly said that. I'd heard it secondhand, from a smuggler who'd heard it from a priest, and I'd filed it under peasant wisdom and ignored it for fifteen profitable years.

But coin was the wrong currency down here. Coin was for the world above, where things ended. Down here everything unfinished gathered like water in a cellar.

"Terms," I said, and Vess's head lifted a fraction, and every remaining soul in the gallery turned to watch, and I understood that I was about to do something ceremonial whether I liked it or not, so it might as well be done properly. "One unnamed favor. Callable once. Not transferable to the gilded court or any agent thereof — if your moment comes and you're too far gone to speak it, it lapses, it doesn't pass to your rivals. And it stops short of this: you will never ask me to raise a storm against the lowland villages. Whatever else you ask, that line holds."

Silence. Long enough that I counted my exits twice, out of habit, and found the same three I'd found on the way in.

"Agreed," Vess said. "Spoken and witnessed." And the gallery exhaled — a sound like wind through a forest of dry reeds, hundreds of faded voices assenting at once, and the glow-veins flared from blue-green to a color I had no name for, and I felt the promise leave me. Physically. Like a thread drawn out through the sternum, fine as spider silk, spooling away into the dark toward the throne, and the strange thing — the thing I'd turn over at three in the morning for weeks afterward — was that it didn't feel like losing something. It felt like being tied to something. An anchor line, exactly as she'd said. You throw out an anchor and the rope goes taut and for one horrible moment you think you're the one being pulled.

Then it holds, and you're moored, and the current can't take you wherever it likes anymore.

"The Bell of Hours," Vess said, and her voice had changed — lighter, almost warm, the voice of a queen remembering she'd been young once. "Beneath the Grey Tarn, three days' climb northeast, where the goat track forks at the split stone. The chapel drowns and surfaces with the tarn's mood; it rises fully only when the water is stillest, which happens at dawn on the dark of the moon — five nights from now. The door answers three words in the old tongue, which I will teach your scholar, because scholars remember words and storm-callers remember grudges, and the door must be spoken to kindly." The smile again, quick as a fish. "The guardian's name is Ossian. He was my uncle. Be polite. He has been alone a very long time, and loneliness makes even the gentlest keeper strict about the rules."

"One more thing," I said. "Free, since you value free things so poorly."

"Amuse me."

"The gilded court sent an envoy toward Thornfell two days ago. Silver litter, six bearers, carrying invitations sealed in gold wax. They're not hunting us. They're shopping. For a warden they can own." I watched her face do something complicated. "If they get to you before I get to the Bell—"

"They won't." Flat. Final. "The gilded court has never once descended to the hollow places. Coming down here costs them their shine, and they would rather lose the mountain than the shine." She rose from the briar throne, unfolding to her full height, and up close the fading was worse and the dignity was somehow greater for it. "Go, exile. Five nights. Speak kindly to my uncle. And when the moment comes that I call in my promise — whatever it is, however it arrives — remember this hall. Remember the child who reached for warmth she couldn't hold. Remember what you're anchored to."

I remembered, all right. I'd be remembering it for a long time.

* * *

Theo was waiting at the upper threshold with our packs and a face full of questions he was polite enough to hold until the hollow ones were out of earshot. Mostly. His eyebrows were doing the rest.

"You promised," he said.

"I promised."

"A blank favor. To a fae queen. You, who count exits in every room and settle bar tabs before the barkeep asks."

"People change," I said, shouldering my pack and turning toward the northeast passage, where the tunnel breathed cold air that smelled of ice and stone and, faintly, impossibly, of bells. "Besides — she wasn't selling me a leash. She was throwing me a rope. There's a difference, and I felt it, even if I can't prove it."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then in a few years a very old, very patient voice is going to ask me for something impossible at the worst possible moment." I glanced back at him, and despite everything — the frost on the border, the hunters behind us, the book full of my own name — I found I was almost smiling. "Good thing I'll have a scholar who reads too much, a ferrywoman with a crossbow, and a head start."

"Five nights," Theo said, checking the almanac he'd reconstructed from the burned archive's fragments. "Dark of the moon. Dawn. We can make the fork at the split stone in two days if the weather holds."

I put my hand flat against the tunnel wall, and the mountain's pulse came up through the stone into my palm, slow and enormous, like something sleeping just under the skin of the world.

"The weather," I said, "will hold. I've decided it will. And lately, when I decide things about storms, they've started deciding to agree with me."

Which should have comforted me. Instead, three days out, camped under the split stone with the Grey Tarn lying flat and silver as a coin below us, I woke at midnight to the sound of distant water draining — the tarn exhaling, the chapel rising — and found Theo standing at the cliff edge, pale in the starlight, holding the prophecy book open to a page I'd never seen.

"Ulyana," he said quietly. "There's something written in the margin. In my grandmother's hand. It's dated five nights from now."
