## 10. What Theo Won't Say

There is a particular art to burning letters, and I had, by that point in my life, become something of a master of it.

The trick is the wind. You want a steady breeze, nothing gusty—gusts carry half-burned corners with legible names into the underbrush, and legible names are how men like me end up in ditches. You want dry paper, which sounds obvious until you've tried to burn correspondence that crossed a wet border in a scholar's satchel. And above all, you want privacy, which is why I'd waited until the camp was asleep, until Dagny's snores had settled into their reliable rhythm, until Pip had stopped muttering at the fire in whatever language children use to negotiate with things that aren't there, and until Ivo's watch had carried him to the far side of the hollow.

I crouched behind the lean-to with my little tin of correspondence and my little tin of matches and felt, as I always did, like the world's most pathetic arsonist.

Six letters. That's what remained of what had once been a tidy bundle of twenty-three. I'd been rationing myself—two a night, no more, because a man who burns his whole inheritance in one evening is a man who has made a decision, and I wasn't ready to make decisions. I was ready to delay them. Delay, I have found, is the scholar's native element. We can live in it for years, like fish in water, and die of nothing but the delay itself.

The first letter was from my mother, dated the spring before everything went wrong. *Theo, the university has written again. They want to know if you intend to finish your degree or simply continue to be an embarrassment to the family name in increasingly remote locations. Your father says nothing, which from him is a paragraph.* I read it twice. I always read them twice. Then I touched the match to the corner and watched my mother's handwriting curl and go orange and become smoke, and I felt the specific, complicated grief of a man destroying evidence of being loved.

Because that was the thing I couldn't have explained to anyone, if anyone had asked. My family hadn't cast me out. That was the tragedy of it. They would have taken me back tomorrow, with open arms and a lecture, if only I'd come home without the book. Without the *purpose*. My mother's letters were full of love the way a net is full of rope—all of it functional, all of it designed to pull me somewhere I refused to go.

The second letter was older. My grandmother's hand—small, precise, ink the color of dried blood. I had saved it for last, as I saved all of hers, because her letters were the only ones I read slowly.

*Theo. If you are reading this, you have found the book, which means I have failed to keep it hidden, which means you are in danger, which means I am sorry. I have spent your mother's whole childhood teaching her not to believe in the family stories, and I did it out of love, and I did it out of cowardice, and you should know that both things were true at once. That is the lesson I can give you that no archive will: love and cowardice wear the same coat. Check the pockets anyway.*

I'd had that letter for three years. I knew it by heart. I read it anyway, every time, because some part of me was still a boy sitting in a cold study while his grandmother explained that the family business wasn't bookbinding, whatever she'd told the neighbors, and that the book she kept locked in the floor safe was older than the university, older than the lowland kingdoms, and that someday—she'd said *someday* the way other people say *never*—someone would come asking about it, and when they did, Theo, you must be the one who is boring. Boring men survive.

I had been spectacularly boring for three years. And then a burned archive and a dying courier had put the book in my hands anyway, and here I was in a smuggler's camp in the Thornfells, burning my past one letter at a time and calling it caution.

The match trembled. I told myself it was the cold.

* * *

"You're doing it wrong."

I have been startled by bears with more grace than I managed at the sound of her voice. I dropped the letter. The wind—steady, reliable, the one thing I'd planned for—chose that exact moment to gust, and a corner of my grandmother's handwriting lifted off the fire and went cartwheeling toward the trees like a tiny burning bird.

Ulyana caught it out of the air. Bare-handed. A letter that was actively on fire, caught bare-handed, and she didn't even flinch; she just pinched it out between two fingers and stood there in the dark holding a scrap of my family's secrets, looking at me with an expression I was beginning to know very well. It was the expression of a woman cataloguing evidence.

"Doing what wrong," I said, because my options were that or fainting, and I have a rule about fainting.

"The burn." She crouched, dropped the scrap into my tin, and held out her hand for the remaining letters. When I didn't immediately surrender them—forgive me, I have attachment issues—she sighed and sat down on the cold ground beside me as if we were old friends sharing a drink instead of two liars sharing a fire hazard. "You're reading them first. That's your mistake. You read them, and then you burn them, and the reading is the part that keeps you up at night. Burn first. Read nothing. Grief is a task like any other. You do the task, and you sleep."

"That," I said, "is the bleakest thing anyone has ever said to me, and I once had a professor who opened his lectures with 'existence is a wound.'"

"Was he wrong?"

"He was wrong in the way clever men are wrong, which is loudly."

She almost smiled. I've been keeping count. That was the fourth.

I handed her the letters. All of them. I don't know why. That's not true; I know exactly why. Because she had just told me, without telling me, that she understood this particular ritual, and because a man who has been carrying secrets alone for three years will hand them to almost anyone who looks at him like they already know.

She read them. Every one. Quickly, the way she read everything, the way she seemed to do everything—efficient, unsentimental, thorough—and I sat there in the firelight watching my mother's love and my father's silence and my grandmother's warnings pass through a stranger's hands, and I waited to feel violated, and instead I felt lighter. Which was its own kind of alarming.

"Your grandmother," Ulyana said at last, holding the oldest letter up to the fire. "She wrote the book."

It wasn't a question. I could have lied. I want that on the record: I *could* have lied. I had excellent lies prepared, a whole taxonomy of them, sorted by audience. But she was sitting in the dark with my whole history in her lap and a scar on her knuckles from catching my burning past out of the air, and the lies died in my throat like unwatered plants.

"Yes."

"The prophecy book. The one that names me. Your grandmother wrote it."

"Wrote it down," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the only reason I'm still alive. She didn't invent it. She *recorded* it. She was a—scholar, I suppose, if scholar means a woman who spent forty years interviewing things in the mountains that most people insist don't exist. The fae. The old courts. The covenant. She had sources." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "She had sources the way a spy has sources. She spent her whole life collecting testimony about a treaty that was falling apart, and she wrote it all into one book, and she hid it, and she taught my mother to despise it, and then she died protecting it, and now it's in my satchel, and now it's *your* problem, and I am so sorry about all of it."

Ulyana was quiet for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere across the camp, Dagny rolled over and muttered something that sounded like a threat against a specific fish.

"Tell me how she died," Ulyana said.

* * *

Here is what I told her, and I told it all, because once the dam cracks you may as well let the valley flood.

My grandmother's name was Odile Bramble. To the neighbors she was a widowed bookbinder with poor eyesight and excellent tea. To a very small number of people scattered across two realms, she was the last living archivist of the covenant—the person who had spent decades gathering what everyone else was busy forgetting. She'd started, she told me once, as a young woman with a research grant and a theory, and theories about the fae have a way of becoming careers, and careers have a way of becoming vows.

"She didn't write the prophecy," I said. "I need you to understand that. She didn't make any of it up. She found it—fragments of it, in a dozen places. Court records. A hermit's letters. Songs that the hollow fae still sing, or sang, before they lost the singing. She assembled it the way you assemble anything true: painfully, and from pieces, and with the constant suspicion that you've got it wrong."

"And the part about me? The exile who calls storms? The date?" Ulyana's voice was flat, but I'd learned to hear the flatness for what it was—a door held shut with her shoulder. "Your grandmother assembled *my name*?"

"No. She assembled a *description*, and the description has been accurate for a hundred years, because it wasn't a prediction. That's the thing I should have told you in the inn, the thing I've been circling for weeks." I pulled my knees up. "It's not a prophecy in the way the lowland churches mean it. It's a *ledger*. The covenant was made by people, Ulyana. Real people, with real names, and they wrote down the terms, and they wrote down the conditions of its failure, and they wrote down—because the people who made it were thorough, or cruel, or both—exactly what would happen and who would be involved when the terms came due. Your name isn't in it because someone foresaw you. Your name is in it because the covenant was *built* to need someone like you, and the people who built it were precise."

She stared into the fire. I watched her do the arithmetic I'd watched her do a dozen times since the inn: the dates, the exile, the trial, the order, her whole ruined life laid against a document older than her grandmother. I watched her arrive, again, at the sum that said *none of this was accident*.

"The night she died," Ulyana said. "Tell me that part."

I told it. I've told it so many times in my head that saying it aloud was almost a relief, like setting down a load I'd forgotten I was carrying.

Three years ago, men came to the house. Not soldiers—worse, the kind of men who don't wear uniforms because uniforms can be traced. They said they were collectors. They said the university had an interest in certain rare documents. My grandmother invited them in, made tea, and sent me to the cellar on an errand she invented on the spot, because Odile Bramble could invent an errand the way other people breathe.

I heard it through the floor. Not the fighting—there wasn't any fighting, not really. There was the sound of furniture, and one short cry, and then a long silence, and then the men's voices, angry now, because they'd torn the study apart and the floor safe was empty and my grandmother was dying on her own rug and laughing at them. Laughing. I heard her laugh. She'd hidden the book years earlier—moved it the day she wrote my letter, the day she decided I might someday need to be boring—and she let them break her rather than tell them where, and she laughed while they did it, and I stayed in the cellar because she had raised me to follow instructions and because if I had gone up those stairs I would be dead, and some nights I still can't decide which of those facts is the shame.

The book reached me a year later. A courier—a woman I never learned the name of, half-dead of something that wasn't any wound I could find—delivered it to my lodgings with instructions in my grandmother's hand: *Take it to Thornfell. Find the exile. Don't read the last chapter.*

"Have you read the last chapter?" Ulyana asked.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because she asked." I looked at my hands. "And because I'm a coward, and because I love her, and both things are true at once. Check the pockets."

That landed. I saw it land. Ulyana went still in a way that was different from her usual stillness—her usual stillness is a hunter's; this was something rawer—and for a moment I thought I'd finally said the thing that would make her leave, or make her tell me everything she'd been not-telling me since the inn.

Instead she said, "The order killed her."

"The order, or someone using the order's methods. The collectors knew what they were looking for. They knew the book existed. Only a handful of institutions knew that, and the Storm-Caller order is the largest of them." I hesitated. This was the part I'd been carrying separately, the part that felt like a stone in a boot. "There's more. The courier—the one who brought it to me—she said something before she died. She said, *they're not hunting the book, they're hunting what the book proves.* I didn't understand it then. I've had three years and a lot of walking to think about it, and I think I understand it now, and I think it means the order didn't kill my grandmother to suppress a prophecy. They killed her because the prophecy, if it's true, proves that everything they did—everything they've *done*, for a hundred years, maybe longer—was according to someone's design. And institutions can survive their crimes. They cannot survive their irrelevance."

* * *

We sat there until the fire burned down to a red argument with the dark. Ulyana fed it sticks without being asked. I noticed, in the way you notice things at that hour, that she'd kept two of the letters out of the pile—the oldest one of my grandmother's, and one of my mother's, the angry one, the one that was secretly a paragraph of love.

"You're keeping evidence," I said.

"I'm keeping *your* evidence. You've been so busy burning mine that someone has to keep yours." She folded them into her coat with the same care she used for the prophecy pages, and something in my chest did a complicated maneuver I chose not to examine. "Your grandmother sounds like she was insufferable."

"She was."

"Good. Insufferable women live longest." A pause. The fire cracked. "Mine died when I was six. I don't remember her voice. I remember her hands. She was mending a net, and she had a way of— " Ulyana stopped. Recalibrated. I watched her decide, in real time, how much truth she could afford, and I watched her decide on more than usual, and I understood that this was her version of handing me letters. "She was from the mountains. My grandmother. That's all I know. That's all anyone would ever tell me, and I stopped asking, because asking questions in the order is how you end up assigned to the latrine aqueducts for a decade. But she was from the mountains, and I have spent my whole life being told I don't belong anywhere, and I have never once been told *why*, and lately I've been wondering if those two facts are the same fact."

I thought about the ledger. I thought about the last chapter, the one I hadn't read, the one my grandmother had died to protect and died, possibly, to keep *from me*. I thought about the phrase the courier had used—*what the book proves*—and about the way Ulyana's name had been inked on a page a century before she was born, in a book assembled by a woman whose sources had been fae.

I thought: *I know something. I don't know what, but I know something, and it's about her, and it's in that last chapter.*

And I thought: *if I tell her, she'll read it, and if she reads it, she'll be the one who has to decide what it costs, and I have watched one woman I love die carrying a decision that should have been shared.*

"I'm going to read the last chapter," I said. "Soon. Before the first keystone. Whatever's in it, you'll hear it from me before you hear it from anyone else. That's a promise, and I know my promises have been worth approximately nothing so far, but this one I'm going to keep, because my grandmother died so that the truth would arrive on time, and I'll be damned if it arrives late because I flinched."

Ulyana looked at me across the dying fire for a long moment. Then she stood, brushed the ash from her knees, and said the thing I would spend the next several days turning over like a stone with something under it:

"Your grandmother and my grandmother knew each other, Theo. I don't know how I know that. But I've been looking at her handwriting in your letters, and I've seen it before. On the inside cover of a book in the order's restricted archive, in a file they kept on *me*."

She walked back toward her bedroll, and I sat alone with the ashes of my family, and above the ridge the stars were coming out the way they only come out over Thornfell—thick, cold, and patient as a held breath—and I understood that tomorrow we would walk into a drowned chapel after a keystone, and that the last chapter was burning a hole in my satchel, and that somewhere under that stone there was a truth with both our names on it.
