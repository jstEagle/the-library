## 11. Keystone One: The Bell of Hours

### Ulyana

The chapel had been drowning for a hundred years, and it had not gotten any better at it.

We stood at the lip of the flooded valley where the Bell of Hours had once kept its parish, and the water below was the color of old pewter, still as glass except where Pip's reflection leaned out over it and made faces at itself. Somewhere beneath that surface lay the first keystone of the old covenant, sunk when the river changed its mind and the lowlands let it. The prophecy book—Theo's cursed, waterlogged inheritance—had led us here the way a drunk leads you home: confidently, by routes that made no sense, arriving exactly where it intended.

"It's down there," Theo said, in the tone of a man hoping the sentence would become less true if he said it quietly.

"Chapels sink," I said. "Keystones don't swim."

Dagny spat over the edge, which was her way of contributing to strategic discussions. "I'll ferry ye across to the tower top. After that, ye're on your own. I don't do drowned churches. Bad luck, and worse footing."

Pip did not wait for permission. The child went over the rocks like water finding its level, bare feet on wet stone, and stood at the tower's broken crown pointing down through the floodwater at something only Pip could see. "There," Pip said. "It's ringing. Can't you hear it? It's been ringing the whole time, slow, like it's counting us."

Nobody asked what it was counting us toward. We all knew better than to invite answers.

I checked my gear the way I always did before a descent—rope, knife, flint, the little tin of salt I'd carried since my exile because salt is honest about what it's for—and I did not check my fear, because inventorying fear is how it gets organized. Then I tied off the line, took a breath that tasted of lake and rot, and went down into the dark after a bell.

* * *

Underwater, the chapel was more itself than it had ever been dry.

Light came down in slanted columns, thick as wool, and caught on things suspended in the green: a hymnal gone to pulp, drifting like a jellyfish; a christening gown moving gently on some current I refused to think about; fish schooling through the nave as though the congregation had simply changed species. The bell tower rose above me, its arches furred with weed, and at the bottom of it, half-buried in silt, hung the Bell of Hours—no longer bronze but something darker, veined with silver, humming.

I felt it before I touched it. A note too low for ears, passing up through my ribs, my teeth, the fillings of my bones. It rang slow, like Pip said. Like a clock counting down something enormous.

My hands found the keystone set into the bell's crown—a wedge of stone no bigger than a hymnal, carved with interlocking rings, warm in a way the water was not. Warm like a hand. I worked it loose while my lungs burned and my vision started going soft at the edges, and the moment it came free, the bell *rang*.

Not underwater. Not in the water. In me.

The world folded shut like a book.

* * *

I was standing in the great hall of the storm-caller order, ten years younger, wearing robes I hadn't thought about in a decade, and the hall was full of people who were all looking at me.

I knew this night. Of course I knew this night. I have carried it the way you carry a stone in your boot—always there, always shaping how you walk. The night of the fire. The night Serapha stood silent while they read the charges. The night I stopped being Ulyana Zhou, storm-caller of the third circle, and became a name on a warrant.

But the guardian—the thing the bell had woken, a presence like cold air given opinions—did not show me the trial. It showed me the hours before it. And that was the problem. Because I remembered those hours one way, and the vision showed them another, and the two versions did not fit together, and a person can survive almost anything except arithmetic that won't come out right.

In my memory: I had been on watch that night. Alone, as assigned. The granary fires started at moonrise, three roofs at once, which no natural spark does. I raised wind to smother them—I remember the effort of it, the taste of blood in my throat from calling air that hard—and I failed, or arrived failing, and by dawn three families were homeless and someone had to be guilty, and the guilty someone was the exile-to-be, the outsider, the one whose grandmother had come from over the mountains.

That was the story. That was the story I had built my whole exile on, the way you build a house on whatever ground you're given.

The vision disagreed.

It showed me the granary yard at moonrise, and I was not alone. There were others in the shadows—robed, hooded, moving with the particular economy of people doing something planned. One of them struck flint against steel with a steadiness that made my stomach turn even in memory-of-memory. And I came around the corner of the threshing floor and saw them, saw the fire catch, and I called the wind—

—and the wind did not come.

That was the detail. That was the splinter under the nail of everything. In my memory, my power had simply failed me, the way it sometimes did in those years, the way I'd blamed myself for ever since. But the vision showed it from the outside, the way the guardian sees: my hands rising, my mouth shaping the call, and the air lying flat and dead around me like a held breath—not empty, but *occupied*. Someone else had already claimed the sky. Someone had bound the winds against me before I ever opened my mouth, and they had done it smoothly, expertly, with a signature I knew.

Third-circle binding work. Order craft. My own tradition, turned on me like a lock turned from inside.

And then the vision gave me the face of the one who'd held the winds, and I wish to every god of both realms that it hadn't, because it was a face I loved once, weathered and kind and gray at the temples, and it wore an expression not of hatred but of *regret*, the regret of a surgeon doing necessary work.

Serapha.

The vision ended. The water closed back around me. I surfaced into the drowned nave gasping, keystone clutched to my chest, and Theo was hauling himself up beside me, white-faced and shaking, and neither of us said anything for a long moment because we had each just been read like cheap books and needed time to pretend otherwise.

"Did it show you yours?" he finally managed.

"Yes."

"Was it true?"

"That's the wrong question." I looked down at the keystone in my hands, warm as a living thing. "Ask me again in a day or two. When I've decided which version of my life I'm going to live in."

Above us, faint through the water, Pip's voice drifted down, cheerful and terrible: *"Found the boat! Also found a skull! He says hello!"*

I laughed, because the alternative was screaming, and I had learned long ago that laughing is just screaming with better manners.

### Theo

I want to say, before anything else, that I am a scholar, and scholars are trained to observe their own terror with clinical interest. This training is useless. It is like being trained to catalog a house fire while standing in it. But I mention it because it explains why what follows is written the way it is: precisely, and with shaking hands.

The guardian tested me second. I know this because Ulyana told me afterward, in the flat voice of someone who has decided not to feel something yet, and because when my turn came the water around me went cold in a new way—attentive, like a teacher who has already graded the other students and found them interesting.

I expected it to show me the archive burning. Everyone's worst choice should logically be the archive; I have rehearsed that guilt so many times it has grooves worn in it, like a stair. The night the Repository of Vell went up, and I ran—not away, I have always insisted, *through*, carrying what I could—but the truth is that I ran past people. Past Old Merrow, who had taught me paleography, who was shouting something at me from a doorway full of smoke, and I did not stop, because the book was heavier than a man and I had made my calculation and my calculation did not include him.

He died in that doorway. I have known that for four years. I have arranged my life around not knowing it.

But the guardian did not show me the archive. It showed me my grandmother's kitchen.

I stood in it—the low ceiling, the copper pots, the smell of bread and ink that I had forgotten I remembered—and she was there at the table, writing, and she was alive, and this alone told me the vision was a lie or a mercy or both, because she has been dead for six years and I was not permitted to attend the funeral, on account of being, at the time, a fugitive in all but paperwork.

She looked up at me and said, "You could have come sooner, Theo."

"I didn't know," I said, and heard my own voice crack on the water, absurd, arguing with a vision in a drowned church. "Grandmother, I didn't know about any of it. The book, the covenant, any of it. You never told me."

"No," she agreed, mild as milk. "I didn't. Why do you suppose that was?"

And the guardian, patient as sediment, let the kitchen dissolve and showed me instead a series of small doors I had closed and labeled *unimportant*. The letter I received at university, the one in her handwriting that I never finished reading because it seemed rambling and old-fashioned and I had an examination. The summer she asked me to stay at the house and I chose the fellowship instead. The last conversation, in which she said, "If anything happens to me, the green book goes to you, and Theo—" and I interrupted her, because I was twenty-two and knew everything, and told her nothing would happen to her.

Nothing happened to her for another four years. Then the archive burned, and men came to our family's house asking questions, and my grandmother died protecting a book she had spent her whole life pretending was a recipe collection.

"You chose not to know," the guardian said—or the water said, or the bell said, in a voice like a page turning. "Knowing was heavy. You put it down. Someone else picked it up. That was your worst choice. Not the running past Merrow—that was cowardice, and cowardice is common. This was rarer. You abandoned the truth *before* it cost you anything, on the chance it might someday cost you something. You pre-paid your cowardice."

I would like to report that I argued. Scholars argue. It is our whole profession. But the guardian was correct, and correctness has a silence all its own, and I stood in the cold green dark and let it be correct at me until the vision released me and I surfaced, choking, into the nave where Ulyana floated with the keystone glowing faintly against her chest like a coal refusing to go out.

She looked at me then. Really looked, the way she rarely does, eyes narrowed, reading me the way she reads weather.

"What did it show you?" she asked.

"My grandmother," I said. "And all the times I could have listened to her and didn't."

Something moved behind her face—recognition, maybe, or envy, because at least my worst choice was ordinary. At least mine was only negligence. Hers, whatever it had been, had left her looking like a woman holding something that weighed more than a keystone.

"We should get out of the water," she said.

"We should get out of the country," I said.

"One catastrophe at a time, Bramble."

* * *

Climbing out of the flood valley took until dusk, and by the time Dagny ferried us the last stretch and Pip met us on the bank demanding to hold the keystone ("just to look at it, it's *warm*, it's like a kitten"), the light had gone the color of weak tea and the mountains had begun their nightly performance of looking ominous.

Ulyana sat apart from the fire. She sat apart from fires as a rule—it was one of the hundred small exiles she performed daily, sitting outside circles, keeping exits in view—but tonight the distance had a different texture. Tonight she wasn't performing solitude. She was conducting it, like an orchestra, and the music coming out of it was bad.

I brought her tea because it was the only instrument I had.

"The guardian showed you the night of the fire," I said. Not a question. She had told me enough on the climb, in fragments, the way people tell things they hope will stop being true by the time they finish saying them.

"It showed me the truth of it." She turned the cup without drinking. "Which is worse. Do you know what I've done with ten years, Theo? I built a whole self out of that night. *They* wronged me. *They* forged the evidence, coached the witnesses, cast me out. And I was right! I was innocent! I've been innocent for a decade and it has been the load-bearing wall of my entire personality."

"And now?"

"Now the guardian shows me that yes, they forged the evidence, and yes, the witnesses lied, and yes, Serapha stood silent—all of it true, every injustice I've nursed like a wound I'm secretly proud of. *And* the reason my defense failed that night, the reason I couldn't call the wind to save the granaries, the reason I stood there helpless while three families lost everything—" Her jaw tightened. "Was that Serapha bound the skies against me first. Before the fires. Before the charges. She disabled me, then let me fail, then let me be blamed for failing. It was all one operation. The fire, the failure, the trial. A single machine, and I was the part that got ground up."

"So you *were* framed."

"Oh, completely. Thoroughly. With craftsmanship." She laughed, briefly, horribly. "I was framed by someone I loved, using methods I was trained to admire, for reasons I still don't know. Do you understand what that does to a person? For ten years my story was simple: they were cruel, I was innocent, the world owed me. Now the story is: they were *afraid*, and afraid people do precise terrible things, and the person who did them to me used to braid my hair when storms kept us indoors. And I don't know which version is worse. I only know the simple one is gone, and I built my house on it, and the house is making noises."

Pip appeared at her elbow at that exact moment, as children do, holding the keystone up like an offering. "It hums," Pip reported. "When you hold it near your heart it hums in a different key. Yours is a sad key. Mine is a normal key."

"Pip."

"What? It's true. Ask it yourself." Pip pressed the stone into Ulyana's hands and watched her with the unblinking frankness of the very young and the very strange. "Maybe it's not sad. Maybe it's just loud. Sad things are usually just loud things nobody listened to."

Out of the mouths of feral children. I wrote it down later, in the margin of the prophecy book, because it was truer than anything the century-old ink had managed.

* * *

We broke camp before dawn. Dagny announced she was coming with us—"not for ye, for the ferrywoman's granddaughter who lives past Marrowgate, and if that happens to serve yer purposes, well, coincidences exist"—and Ivo said nothing but was packing before anyone asked him, which was his way of volunteering. Even Pip simply fell in line, carrying a stick, a skull (the skull's, presumably), and an air of having decided something on our behalf.

Ulyana noticed. Of course she noticed; she notices everything, it's her curse and her trade.

"They're following you," I said. "Not me. You're the one with the keystone and the storm in her hands."

"They're following the direction the food is," she said. But she didn't quite believe her own deflection, and neither did I, and I saw her look back at the little camp—Dagny coiling rope, Ivo banking the fire, Pip negotiating with the skull—and I saw her count them, the way she counts money and exits, except this time the number came out differently than she expected.

Then she looked south, toward Marrowgate, where frost was eating the fields in midsummer, and her expression changed into something I had seen only once before, on the ridge during the storm: the face of a woman deciding to use the power everyone told her not to use.

"The order will have felt the bell ring," she said. "A keystone waking carries. They'll come, and they'll come fast, and Serapha will be at the front of them wearing her kind face." She rolled the keystone in her palm, warm as a sleeping bird. "Ten years I wanted to ask her why. Now I'm going to get the chance, and I find I don't want the answer anymore. I want the *accounting*."

"That's a very lawyerly thing to want."

"I've had ten years to prepare the case." She shouldered her pack and started walking, and the keystone's glow leaked between her fingers like a secret badly kept. "Come along, Bramble. History's open. Let's go make some entries."

Behind us, in the drowned valley, faint as memory, the Bell of Hours began to ring again—slow, patient, counting.

I did not ask what it was counting now.

Some questions you save for the chapter where you can't avoid the answer.
