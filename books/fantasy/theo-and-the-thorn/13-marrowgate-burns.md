## 13. Marrowgate Burns

I want to say we saw the smoke from the ridge, that we had time to brace ourselves, that grief arrived like a letter—sealed, addressed, expected.

It didn't. We smelled it first.

Woodsmoke has a particular grammar to it. Hearth-smoke says *someone is home*. Kiln-smoke says *someone is making something*. This smoke said nothing at all. It was the smoke of a thing being erased rather than made, thick and gray and indifferent, rising off Marrowgate in columns so straight they looked drawn with a ruler. The wind carried it to us a full hour before we crested the last hill, and for that entire hour I kept telling myself reasonable things. A festival. A bonfire. An accident at the mill. The mind is a generous liar when it needs to be.

Ulyana didn't lie to herself. I watched her stop walking, nostrils flaring, and go very still in the way she does—the stillness of an animal deciding which direction to bolt. Then she said one word: "No," quietly, like she was correcting someone, and started running.

I ran after her. I'm not built for running. I'm built for libraries and long sedentary afternoons with cheese. My lungs filed a formal complaint somewhere around the second fence line and I ignored it, because she was ignoring hers, and there was a certain solidarity in that.

* * *

We came over the hill above Marrowgate at midday, and midday was black.

The village sat in its little valley like a burnt page in a book—same shape as before, same layout I'd memorized on our way through three days ago, but the meaning had been scraped off it. The mill was gone. Half the row houses along the brook were gone. The rest were burning still, or smoldering, and between them moved figures in storm-gray cloaks moving with the unhurried efficiency of people doing a job. Not looting. Looting has a different posture. These were workers. They had a checklist.

At the center of the square stood a pyre already built and already lit, and around it, held back by a line of cloaked figures, maybe two dozen villagers—some fighting, some weeping, some simply standing with the terrible quiet of people who have stopped expecting anything from the world.

"Quarantine," Ulyana said. She wasn't looking at me. Her voice had a flatness I'd never heard from her, and I've heard her describe avalanches fondly. "That's what they'll call it. That's what they called it at Vell's Ford, forty years ago. I read the order's own records." She laughed, one dry note with no humor anywhere in it. "They keep records, Theo. They're very proud of their records."

"The frost-sickness—" I started.

"Is not a sickness." She was already moving down the slope, fast, low, angling toward the tree line at the valley's edge where the smoke would hide her approach. "The winter gets into people who've been touched by the covenant's collapse. Chills that don't break. Dreams of standing in snow. And then—" her hand cut sideways at the pyre "—that. The order decided a century ago that the touched spread it. There's no proof. There was never any proof. But proof requires you to believe the person you're burning might be innocent, and it's much easier to believe they're not."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I was trained by the people writing it down." She glanced back at me, and her eyes were doing something complicated—rage and grief fighting for the same chair. "Stay behind me. If they see your face, they'll take the book. If they see mine, they'll take everything."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about watching an atrocity from thirty yards away, crouched in a hedge while smoke stings your eyes: it's boring, mostly. Atrocity is administered. There are forms. The gray-cloaks went door to door with a ledger—one of them actually had a ledger, I could see it, a fat bound thing he consulted between houses—and they marked something, and then other gray-cloaks did whatever the marking indicated. Some houses they only searched. Some houses they emptied into the crowd by the pyre. One house they simply fired, without opening the door, and stood outside listening to what was inside, and wrote something down afterward.

I threw up in a hedgerow. Ulyana put a hand on my shoulder—not comforting, exactly. Stabilizing. Like bracing a shelf.

"Don't watch if you can't," she murmured.

"I have to watch," I said, and hated how noble I made it sound, because the truth was uglier: I couldn't look away. My grandmother spent her life writing warnings about this exact thing, died face-down in a river protecting pages that described it, and I had always read those passages as history. As finished. Watching a man in a gray cloak consult his ledger while a house burned behind him, I understood for the first time that my grandmother's book wasn't a record of the past. It was a schedule.

"There." Ulyana's grip tightened on my shoulder hard enough to bruise. "By the well. The woman in the blue shawl."

I found her. Middle-aged, stocky, gray-streaked hair coming loose from its braid, holding a pitchfork with no plan attached to it, shouting at a line of gray-cloaks in a voice cracked raw. Behind her huddled a knot of villagers—including, gods help me, children—and among them I recognized faces from our passage through: the innkeeper's wife who'd fed us barley soup and complained about her husband; the ferryman with the laugh like a cart on gravel; a girl of perhaps ten who had asked me, with the directness of children, whether the book I carried was worth more than a horse.

"That's Maren," Ulyana breathed. "She gave us beds. She told us about the frost in the barley fields. She's—she pulled people out of the cold-houses, Theo, she's been nursing the touched for weeks, which means by the order's arithmetic—"

She didn't finish. Below us, a gray-cloak officer—a woman with silver at her temples and a voice like a closing door—said something we were too far to hear, and the line advanced on Maren and her cluster of survivors, and Maren leveled her pitchfork and did not step back.

"We can't fight twelve of them," I said. My mouth was making plans my body had already declined to fund. "You said yourself, if they see you—"

"I know what I said." Ulyana's hands had curled into fists at her sides, and I swear the air around them went strange, pressure shifting, the hairs on my arms lifting. Storm-craft, gathering. The oath she'd sworn never to break, gathering in her knuckles like weather. "Get to the treeline behind the chapel. There's a drainage culvert, it comes out past the sheep pens. When I make noise—and I am going to make *noise*—you get those people out."

"Ulyana—"

"Theo." She turned and looked at me fully, and I saw that under the rage there was terror, and under the terror something worse: recognition. *This is what I always knew they were*, her face said. *I just hoped I'd be further away when it stopped mattering.* "Whatever you see me do down there, whatever it costs—I need you to understand I'm choosing it. Nobody's forcing me. Write that down in whatever book you're keeping in your head. I chose."

Then she stepped out of the hedge and walked down into Marrowgate like she owned the deed to it, and I scrambled for the chapel.

* * *

I will tell you what happened in the square as best I can, though I only saw pieces of it—glimpses between walls, fragments reflected in a chapel window while I pried at a rotted culvert grate with a belt knife. Fragments are how I remember everything important anyway.

I saw Ulyana walk into the open and throw back her hood, and I saw the officer's face change—the particular change of a professional recognizing an unprofessional problem.

I heard Ulyana's voice carry across the whole valley, clear as a bell across water: *"Storm-Caller Ulyana Zhou, Third Circle, Order of the Veiled Sky. You are conducting an unsanctioned cull inside Thornfell's shadow. Stand down or answer to the mountain."*

It was magnificent. It was also, I realized with a lurch, a bluff stitched together from half-truths—she hadn't been Third Circle in years, the order answered to no mountain, and the mountain, as far as anyone alive knew, had never answered to anyone. But authority is mostly costume, and she wore it beautifully. The line of gray-clocks hesitated. Hesitation is a crack, and Ulyana drove straight into it.

I heard Maren shout something joyful and disbelieving. I heard the officer—Veyra, someone called her, Commander Veyra—reply in that closing-door voice, calm as arithmetic: *"Exile Zhou. The Order pardons no one. Seize her."*

And then Ulyana raised her hand, and the sky over Marrowgate, which had been sullen and gray all day, went *dark*.

I don't have vocabulary for what happened next that doesn't sound like a bard drunk on his own metaphors. Lightning came down—not striking anyone, that was the terrible artistry of it—it struck the well, the ground, the empty street, a wall of thunder and white light boxing the gray-cloaks into a ring of shattered cobblestone, close enough to singe, deliberately missing everyone. Every bolt placed like a word in a sentence whose entire meaning was *no*. The pyre itself she hit last, and the rain came down with it, sudden and torrential, drowning the fire in the square and beating back the fires in the houses until the whole valley steamed and hissed like something scalded.

In the ringing dark afterward, I got the culvert open, and Maren—who missed none of this, who understood in one glance what the lightning was *for*—got her people moving. Through the culvert, past the sheep pens, into the trees. The ferryman carried a boy too young to run. The girl who'd asked about my book grabbed my sleeve and hauled me along when I stumbled, which I intend never to mention to anyone.

Behind us, in the drowned square, Ulyana turned to face Commander Veyra across forty feet of smoking mud, rain streaming off her, and I heard the last of it as we reached the treeline:

*"Tell Serapha the exile is done hiding."*

Then thunder took the rest, and we ran.

* * *

We didn't stop until full dark, eleven miles north into the foothills, to a place Maren knew—an old shepherd bothy tucked under an overhang, the kind of place that exists on no map because maps are made by people who have somewhere to be. We crowded twenty-three people into a space meant for four and a dog, and nobody complained, because complaining requires believing the world owes you comfort, and every person in that bothy had recently revised their expectations downward.

Maren set broken fingers for a boy whose father hadn't made it out of the third row house. The ferryman—he was called Aldous, and his real name for laughing was gone forever now—sat by the fire with his burned hands wrapped in wet cloth and stared at the wall. The girl with the grip like a winch—Pip, someone called her, no surname, no origin offered or requested—sat beside Aldous and silently pushed her share of bread onto his knee until he ate it. Small mercies, administered with the practical ruthlessness of children.

Ulyana stood apart, at the mouth of the bothy, watching the dark. Rain still dripped off the overhang. She hadn't spoken since the square except to count heads—twice, three times, a fourth—like a miser recounting coin.

I brought her tea because I didn't know what else to bring a person, and tea is what I bring.

"They'll report it," she said, before I'd even extended the cup. "A cull disrupted by the exile Zhou, using forbidden craft. By morning every hunter in the order knows I broke oath and location both." She took the tea. Held it. Didn't drink. "There's no going back to invisible after tonight. I spent nine years building a life small enough to disappear into, and I burned it down in ninety seconds to save twenty-three strangers I'd known for three days."

"That sounds like a good trade," I said.

"It was a terrible trade." But her voice cracked on it, just slightly, and I understood she didn't mean it. "Theo, they burned a *ledger*. They had a ledger for the burning. Names checked off. That's what I can't—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I was trained by those people. I loved some of those people. And the whole time, they were keeping books on how best to do *this*, and I never once asked to see the accounting."

"You couldn't have known."

"I could have asked." She lowered her hand and looked at me, and the firelight showed me something I hadn't seen in her before—not the smuggler's wariness, not the storm-caller's edge. Something younger. "That's the difference between us, scholar. You ran toward your family's secrets with a book under your arm. I ran away from mine for nine years and called it freedom."

I didn't have an answer worthy of that, so I gave her the honest one. "Maybe we're both carrying dead women's books now."

Something happened to her face at that—something quick and complicated, grief and almost-laughter wrestling—and she finally drank the tea.

Inside, Pip had fallen asleep against Aldous's good shoulder, and Maren had begun, quietly, to organize. Rations inventoried. Watches assigned. The wounded triaged. She did it the way Ulyana navigated passes—total competence deployed against chaos, no permission sought. And I watched the others orient around her, the way iron filings find a magnet, and I thought: *this is how it starts.* Not with armies. With a burned village, a stolen night, and a handful of people the world discarded discovering they hold together better than the world does.

Ulyana watched them too, over the rim of her cup.

"Dagny," Maren was saying to the ferryman. "Your sister's ferry landing at Greywater—how far?"

"Two days, if the passes hold."

"And your sister?"

Dagny snorted, which I gathered was affection. "Meaner than me and twice as armed. She'll take us."

Ulyana set down her cup. "Twenty-three mouths," she said softly, to me, to the fire, to no one. "No homes. No coin worth having. Hunted by my order, and now—" she flexed her fingers, and faint, at her fingertips, static crackled like distant summer "—now the order knows exactly what I am again."

"Found family," I said. "Chapter fourteen, if my grandmother taught me anything about narratives."

She looked at me like she was deciding whether to hit me or laugh, and settled—magnificently—for neither. "Greywater at dawn," she announced to the bothy, loud enough to turn every head. "Anyone who'd rather take their chances alone, no shame in it. Anyone who's coming—sleep now. We move before the hunters pick up the storm's scent."

Nobody left. Of course nobody left.

But at the bothy's mouth, as the fire settled and the rain thinned to a whisper, Ulyana went rigid—hand up, head cocked, listening to something in the dark that none of us could hear. Listening to the *wind*, I realized. Reading it, the way she reads everything.

"What is it?" I whispered.

Her eyes, when they found mine, were wide and lit with something worse than fear.

"The storm I called," she said. "It's not dissipating. It's circling. Something up in Thornfell caught it, Theo—and it's *answering*."
