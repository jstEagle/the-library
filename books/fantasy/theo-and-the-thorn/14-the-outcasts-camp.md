## 14. The Outcasts' Camp

There is a particular smell to a village that has been burned by its own protectors. Not woodsmoke—woodsmoke is honest, domestic, the smell of supper and hearths. This was something else: wet ash, charred grain, the sour tang of things that had been alive too recently to deserve it. It clung to the back of the throat like an accusation.

We smelled Marrowgate two miles before we saw it.

Theo rode ahead of me on the mule he'd named—unforgivably—Bartholomew, and when the wind shifted he pulled his collar up over his nose and said nothing, which from Theo was its own kind of eloquence. The man had learned in three weeks of travel that some silences are kinder than questions.

The village wasn't burning anymore. That was almost worse. What remained was a black ribcage of roof beams against a grey sky, and snow falling on it in midsummer, and no one left to shovel it.

"Quarantine," I said. The word came out flat as a coin dropped on stone.

"You're sure it was them? The order?"

"I'm sure it was fire lit by people who believed they were right." I swung down from my horse and stood in the slush, listening. "Righteousness smells like this, Theo. Remember it."

He remembered it. I watched him remember it—the way his jaw set, the way his hand went to the satchel where the prophecy book lived, as if the book might need defending from the smoke itself.

That was when we heard the coughing.

* * *

It came from under the ferry dock, of all places—a lean-to of driftwood and stolen tarpaulin wedged between the pilings, half-hidden by snowdrift. Theo found it because Bartholomew refused to walk past it, and Bartholomew, whatever his crimes in the matter of naming, had better survival instincts than either of us.

The woman inside the lean-to had a boat hook leveled at our throats before I'd finished crouching down. She was broad as a door, weathered to the color of old rope, with frostbitten cheeks and eyes like chips off something harder than flint.

"That's far enough," she said. Her voice was gravel dragged through tar. "One step closer and you'll be walking funny for a fortnight."

"We're not with the ones who burned it," Theo said quickly, hands up. "We came to warn them. We were too late."

"Warn them about what?"

"The winter," I said. "The covenant failing. The long winter coming back."

Something moved behind her. A small something, feral, all elbows and matted hair, peering out from a nest of fishing net like a creature deciding whether we were predators or prey.

The woman's grip on the boat hook didn't waver, but her voice did—one hairline crack, quickly plastered over. "You know about the winter."

"I know more than most."

"She's a storm-caller," the small something said, from the dark. Its voice was high and certain and entirely unafraid. "I can smell it on her. Like before lightning."

The boat hook swung toward me. "Is that true?"

"It's complicated," I said.

"Everything worth asking about is," she said. "Complicated or a lie. Which are you?"

I thought about lying. I'd gotten very good at it; it had kept me alive for six years. But there was a burned village at my back and a child who could smell storms and a woman holding a boat hook with the desperation of someone who had already lost everything else worth holding.

"Complicated," I said. "My name is Ulyana Zhou. And if you have survivors to feed, I'd suggest we stop pointing iron at each other and start counting heads."

* * *

Her name was Dagny, and she had been Marrowgate's ferrywoman for nineteen years, which meant she had carried every living soul in the village across the river at one time or another, and she took the burning personally in a way that made grief look like anger and anger look like grief until you couldn't tell them apart.

She had eleven people hidden in the reed beds downstream. Fishermen, mostly, and two grandmothers, and a goatherd who hadn't spoken since the fires. When the order's riders had come through announcing quarantine—"for your protection," Dagny spat, and the word hung in the air like something rotting—she'd loaded her ferry with anyone who could run and poled them into the marshes while the village went up behind her.

"They said the winter-sickness spread," she told us that night, over a fire built low in a hollow so the light wouldn't show. "Said the frost-touched had to be cleansed. Old Marta Voss was ninety-one years old, and her fingers had gone black with cold, and they called that *contagion*." She turned a stick in the fire hard enough to snap it. "Ninety-one. She taught me to read."

Theo sat very still beside me. I could feel him not saying something, the way you feel weather building.

"How many got out?" he asked finally.

"Eleven of two hundred and forty." Dagny said it without inflection, which somehow made it worse. "And now the order's hunting the marshes, so we move tomorrow. Question is where."

"Nowhere's safe," said the child.

Everyone looked over. The feral thing had crept close to the fire's edge, close enough that I could see her properly for the first time: maybe eight years old, maybe ten, all sharp angles and watchfulness, with a shock of white streaking through otherwise black hair—frost-marked, I realized. Frost-touched and alive, which meant the order's definition of contagion was exactly as scientific as I'd suspected.

"Pip doesn't talk much," Dagny said. "Except when Pip talks."

"Pip knows roads," the child said, chin up, addressing the fire rather than any of us. "Secret ones. Fae ones. Grown-ups can't see them but Pip can. Pip could see them even before the frost touched." A pause, and then, quieter, with the terrible directness of children: "Pip's mam couldn't see them. Pip looked back and she didn't follow. So."

Silence around the fire. Theo's hand had gone, unconsciously, to the satchel again.

"Well," I said, because someone had to, and because I recognized the shape of what Pip was doing—I'd done it myself for years, throwing wounds into the open air just to see who'd flinch, so I'd know who to keep at arm's length. "Roads grown-ups can't see sounds useful. We're being hunted by people with maps."

Pip looked at me directly for the first time. The child's eyes were pale grey, almost colorless, and they held the same expression I'd seen in my own reflection once or twice lately: the look of someone doing arithmetic with trust.

"You smell like storms," Pip said. "But like a storm that's been locked in a cellar. That's stupid. Storms shouldn't be in cellars."

"No," I agreed. "They shouldn't."

* * *

The eleventh survivor found us three days later, and he fell out of the sky, more or less.

We'd moved camp twice, following Pip's impossible roads through terrain that shouldn't have connected—over a ridge and suddenly into a valley that lay thirty miles west, according to every map I'd memorized in six years of smuggling. The refugees grumbled about the wrongness of it. Dagny didn't; she just watched Pip with an expression I slowly identified as awe trying to disguise itself as suspicion.

On the third evening, a figure staggered out of the treeline and collapsed at the edge of camp, and every refugee grabbed a weapon, and I nearly did too—until I saw the man's cloak.

Storm-caller grey. Order-issue. But scorched, torn, and—this was the detail that stopped my breath—deliberately defaced. Someone had taken a knife to the order's sigil on his shoulder, hacking the stitched lightning-bolt into meaningless threads.

Dagny reached him first and rolled him over. He was young—not much older than Theo—with a broken nose healing crooked and burn scars crawling up one side of his neck. His eyes opened, found my face, and went wide with something that wasn't fear exactly.

Recognition, maybe. Or recognition's ugly cousin.

"You're Zhou," he rasped.

"Careful," I said pleasantly. "The last person who recognized me handed me to the order."

"I know. I was there." He coughed, wet and bad. "Not at the trial. At the arrest. I held the binding chains." His eyes didn't leave mine. "Ivo Sarn. Journeyman caller. And three days ago I walked out of the order's forward camp with everything I could carry, because they sent me to do to this valley what they did to Marrowgate, and I—" His voice cracked. "I wouldn't. So now I'm here. Doing whatever the opposite of that is."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere in the reeds, one of the grandmothers was singing something low and tuneless to keep a child asleep.

"You expect us to believe that?" Dagny said at last.

"No," Ivo said. "I expect you to tie me up and decide later. That's what I'd do." He managed something that was almost a smile, ruined by the split lip. "For what it's worth, I don't believe me either yet. Give it a week."

I crouched down next to him. Up close I could see the tremor in his hands—the specific tremor of a caller who'd been suppressing their gift too long, power backing up in the channels like floodwater behind a dam. I knew that tremor intimately. I'd worn it for six years.

"Why did you really leave?" I asked quietly. "Not the noble version. The real one."

He held my gaze. It cost him; I could see it costing him.

"Because Serapha Vale ordered the burn at Marrowgate personally," he said. "And she signed the order with the same hand that signed your arrest warrant. I've seen both documents. Same ink, same pressure, same little hitch in the S." His breath whistled. "I used to think you were the traitor, Zhou. Then I spent four years watching what the loyal actually do, and I decided I'd rather be the other thing."

* * *

We tied him up anyway. Dagny insisted, and honestly, I respected the principle. But I sat near him through the night watches, ostensibly guarding him, actually listening to him breathe and thinking about chains.

Here is what nobody tells you about exile: it isn't the loneliness. Loneliness you get used to; loneliness becomes a room you live in, and eventually you learn where the furniture is. What nobody tells you is that exile makes you fluent in other people's abandonment. You develop an ear for it. You hear it in the pause before Dagny says "we" and corrects herself to "me and the others." You see it in how Pip positions herself always at the edge of the firelight, close enough for warmth, far enough to run. You recognize it in Ivo's hands, in the way he flinches from kindness like it's a trap being sprung.

Abandoned people are easy to spot, if you've done the reading.

By the fourth morning, I had stopped pretending I wasn't collecting them.

It started practically, the way these things always lie to you about starting. Dagny knew the river country south of Thornfell better than any smuggler—I needed that knowledge if we were going to reach the drowned chapel where the prophecy said the first keystone waited. Pip's fae roads were the only reliable way to move eleven slow civilians past the order's patrols. And Ivo—

Ivo was the problem. Ivo was also, infuriatingly, the solution.

"You're suppressing," I said to him on the fifth morning, while he sawed through his own bindings with a piece of flint, having apparently decided that if we weren't going to untie him he'd handle it himself. "Badly. How long since you last called?"

"A month." Flint, saw, saw. "Suppression's discipline. Discipline's virtue. That's what they teach."

"What they teach keeps you weak enough to control." I crouched beside him. "Let it breathe. Small working. Nothing anyone will feel past a mile."

He looked at me like I'd offered him wine during a fast. "You're exiled for calling. Why would you tell me to—"

"Because I've been choking on it for six years and I know what it does to you." I heard my own voice come out rougher than intended. "It doesn't make you obedient. It makes you *hungry*. And hungry people do stupid things when someone finally offers them permission. I'd rather you practice permission with me than take it from Serapha."

He stared. Then, slowly, he opened his hand, palm up, and let something loose that he'd been holding for a month.

It was nothing, really. A breath of wind, a static charge, the smell of ozone like the air before rain. The fire flames leaned toward him. Pip, twenty feet away, lifted her head and grinned like a shark.

"There," I said. "Still evil?"

"Devastatingly," Ivo said, and laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through and became something else, and I pretended not to notice, because that was the courtesy abandoned people extend to one another.

* * *

Theo noticed what was happening before I did. Of course he did. The scholar spent his days cataloguing everything—Pip's road-sense, Dagny's river-lore, Ivo's recovery—and somewhere in his notebooks, I later found out, he'd drawn a diagram of our camp with lines connecting everyone to everyone else, and labeled it, in his precise academic hand, *provisional family, handle with care.*

"You smiled today," he said that evening, appearing at my shoulder with two cups of Dagny's appalling pine-needle tea.

"I did not."

"Three times. Once when Pip stole your knife and returned it sharpened, which raises questions. Once when Ivo made it rain on his own head by accident. And once when Dagny called you 'the useful one,' which from her I understand is roughly equivalent to a knighthood."

"I was grimacing."

"You were smiling. I have references." He handed me a cup. Steam curled between us. Below us, the camp had gone quiet in the way camps only go quiet when people feel safe enough to sleep deeply—eleven Marrowgate survivors breathing slow, a ferrywoman snoring like a landslide, a child curled against Dagny's side as though she'd always been there, a defector muttering storm-metrics in his sleep.

I drank the terrible tea and looked at all of it and felt something in my chest that I didn't have a name for, and distrusted accordingly.

"I keep waiting for the trick," I admitted. It was easier to say to Theo than to anyone; he collected confessions the way he collected marginalia. "Six years of keeping moving, keeping unnamed, keeping exits counted. And now there are twelve people who know my face and a child who can smell my power and a defector who knows my case file, and instead of running I'm—" I gestured with the cup. "*Staying.* Teaching suppression theory by firelight. It's tactically indefensible."

"Maybe it's not tactics."

"Everything's tactics."

"Ulyana." Theo turned to look at me, and the firelight caught the scar on his cheekbone, the one he'd never explained. "You spent tonight teaching a man who chained you once how to hold lightning without hurting himself. You spent yesterday letting a grandmother teach you a Marrowgate lullaby because she said your pronunciation was criminal. Whatever you call that, I promise you the word isn't tactics."

I said nothing. The tea was awful. I drank it anyway.

Because here was the truth I wasn't ready to say aloud, the truth that sat in my chest like a coal wrapped in wool: I had spent six years believing that safety was a place you reached alone, that every bond was a leash, that the order had taught me the world's one true lesson—which was that belonging is bait. And now here were twelve people who had lost their village, their order, their mothers, their honor, their faith, each in their own way, huddled together in a marsh hollow under a wrong-colored sky—and none of them, not one, had asked me to be anything other than what I was.

Useful. Complicated. Storm-in-a-cellar.

It was, I was beginning to suspect, the most dangerous thing that had ever happened to me.

* * *

Pip found me at the last watch, as the sky was going from black to bruise.

"Grown-up Ulyana." She planted herself in front of me, arms crossed, in the stance of someone about to negotiate. "Pip has decided something."

"Dare I ask?"

"You're going to the chapel place. The drowned one. For the bell thing." My face must have done something, because she nodded, satisfied. "Pip hears things. Dagny talks loud. The book man talks louder." She scuffed her boot in the ash. "Pip wants to come. All the way. Not just roads. *There.*"

"It's dangerous."

"Everywhere's dangerous. Marrowgate was dangerous and it had *goats*." Her chin came up, and the frost-white streak caught the first grey light. "Pip's the only one who can see the doors. If Pip stays behind, you go the slow way, and the grey-cloaks catch you, and then who teaches Ivo to not be scared of himself? Who calls Dagny 'the useful one' back?" A beat. "Also Pip wants to see a keystone. Pip's seen everything else."

I looked at this scrap of a child—motherless, village-less, marked by the same frost that was eating the borderlands—and felt the arithmetic of trust finish computing at last.

"One condition," I said.

Pip narrowed her eyes suspiciously. "What?"

"When it's dangerous—and it will be—you stay behind me. Not beside. Behind. Storms go in front."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she stuck out one grubby hand.

"Storms go in front," she agreed. "Pip goes in front of *everyone else*, though. Deal?"

I shook it. Her grip was surprisingly strong, like a bird of prey deciding to be gentle.

It was full dawn when the screaming started—from the southern picket, high and cracking, one of the fishermen. I was on my feet with power humming in my palms before my mind caught up, and around me the camp erupted into motion that was already, impossibly, coordinated: Dagny bellowing the refugees into the reeds, Ivo stepping up beside me with ozone rising off his skin, Theo shoving the prophecy book deep into his coat.

Pip appeared at my elbow, pale-eyed and perfectly calm.

"They're not grey-cloaks," she said. "Wrong smell. Wrong *cold*." She pointed south, toward the tree line, where the morning mist was curdling in a way mist should not curdle, folding inward, thickening, taking a shape.

A shape with too many limbs and a crown of black ice.

"Fae," Pip said. "Real ones. And Pip thinks—they're *heralds*."
