## 16. The Gilded Court's Envoy

I have read enough histories to know that when something beautiful arrives unannounced at your campfire, you should count your fingers afterward. The histories agree on this point with unusual unanimity. Beauty is the oldest lure in every ledger—sirens, will-o'-wisps, the smiling tax collector—and yet every generation of scholars insists on learning it again the hard way.

I like to think I was prepared.

I was not prepared.

* * *

It happened on the ninth evening after Marrowgate burned, in the hollow below the Goat's Teeth where we'd made our camp—a lean-to of canvas and pine boughs, a fire that Dagny insisted be kept small because "smoke reads like a signature," and six people who had no business trusting one another but were doing it anyway, tentatively, the way you test ice.

Dagny's fever had broken two days prior. She was up and moving, gray-faced and furious about it, rewrapping the bandage on her side where Serapha's hunters had opened her at the Sorrow Bridge. Pip was teaching Ivo a clapping game that involved cheating. Ulyana sat apart, as she often did, mending a strap with the particular concentration of someone who found silence easier to hold than conversation.

The light changed first.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have to offer anyone. It wasn't that the fire brightened or the sunset deepened. It was that the shadows *leaned*. Every shadow in the camp tilted toward the eastern ridge, as if the dark itself had heard a rumor and turned to look.

Then the envoy stepped out of a birch tree that had not, moments before, contained a doorway.

She was tall in the way that makes architecture seem an afterthought—tall the way a spire is tall, tall on purpose. Her skin held a faint gold shimmer, as though she'd been dipped in candlelight and let dry. Her hair moved in a wind that touched nothing else. And her clothes—I am a scholar, I should describe them properly—her clothes appeared to be woven from late afternoon, the specific golden hour that painters chase and never catch, hemmed in embroidery so fine it might have been frost on a window.

Every blade in the camp came out at once. Dagny moved faster than a woman with a stitched-shut wound has any right to move. Ivo's hands went up in the old storm-caller stance before he caught himself and lowered them, jaw tight. Even Pip stopped cheating at the clapping game.

The envoy regarded the weapons with mild, genuine interest, the way one regards a child's drawing of a dragon.

"Put those away," she said. Her voice was warm honey poured over cold stone. "If I meant harm, you'd already be a very tidy pile of regrets." She smiled, and the smile did something to the air—made the fire burn golder, made the evening feel, briefly, like a memory of a better day. "I'm here on embassy. My lady sends greetings."

"Your lady," Ulyana said. She hadn't stood. She sat with the strap across her knees, needle still, and looked at the envoy the way she looked at weather coming over the pass—assessing, unhurried, already planning three exits. "The Gilded Court."

"The Court of Everbright, if you please. 'Gilded' is what the losers call us." The envoy's smile didn't falter, but something behind it sharpened by a degree. "My name is Liriope. I've come a long way through ugly country to speak with the storm-caller."

"She's busy," Dagny said flatly.

"I can see that. Mending. How admirably practical." Liriope's gaze slid off Dagny as though she were furniture. It went to Ulyana and stayed there, and I watched my guide—the woman who could read a snowfield like a ledger, who trusted nothing and no one—become the object of a full, undivided, radiant attention. "May I sit? Refusing me would be rude, and rudeness has consequences, though I promise none of them are violent. Merely tedious."

"You're sitting whether we say yes or no," Ulyana said.

Liriope laughed—a sound like coins spilled on marble—and settled onto a fallen log that had definitely been too far away a moment ago. "Oh, I like her. Lady Vess said you'd be sharp. The hollow queen's compliments are rare currency; spend them wisely."

At the name *Vess*, Ivo stiffened. He'd been quiet since the Hollow Court, quieter than usual, carrying whatever he'd seen in those fading faces somewhere behind his ribs. Now he said, carefully, "You know Queen Vess?"

"I knew her court when it had a name worth saying." Something flickered across Liriope's beauty then—not quite regret, more like the memory of where regret would go if she'd bothered to install it. "A century ago. Before the covenant rotted. Which brings me, pleasantly, to business."

* * *

Here is what I noticed, as a scholar trained to notice: Liriope never once threatened us. Not explicitly. Every sentence was silk. And yet the whole conversation had the texture of a hand closing slowly around something soft.

"We know what you carry," she said to Ulyana. "Or rather, we know what you're *looking for*, which is nearly the same thing and considerably more flattering to your intelligence. Three keystones. The old pact, shattered. You've found one—the Bell, yes? We felt it wake. The mountain groaned in its sleep and half our court needed a lie-down afterward."

Ulyana said nothing. She'd gotten very good at saying nothing in ways that made people fill the silence themselves. I'd seen smugglers confess to crimes they hadn't committed just to end that silence.

Liriope, to her credit, didn't fill it. She simply continued, patient as erosion. "You're hunting the other two through country that wants you dead, pursued by an order that wants you dead-er, while winter eats villages at both ends of the border. Meanwhile, my lady offers—" she gestured, and the gesture conjured a small golden light that hung in the air between them like a captive star, "—an alternative."

"Which is?" Ulyana asked.

"Sanctuary. Legitimacy. A seat." Liriope leaned forward, and the light between them swelled, and I swear the temperature rose five degrees. "Not a hovel at the edge of exile, storm-caller. A *seat*. In the Court of Everbright, with rank and name and protection that no order of hedge-witches would dare cross. Your record expunged. Your trial overturned—we have influence in human courts older than your order's founding. The men who forged your conviction would find their careers ending in interesting ways."

I saw it land. I saw it land in Ulyana's face before she killed the expression, and I don't think anyone else caught it, because I'd spent weeks learning to read her the way she read weather.

It landed like rain on ground that had been drought-cracked for years.

Because that was the thing about Ulyana Zhou that I understood better than I ever said aloud: she didn't want power. She wanted *belonging*. She wanted it so badly she'd built an entire life out of pretending she didn't—out of counting money and counting exits, out of being useful instead of being known. And here was a creature made of gold and afternoon offering her, in one breath, everything the order had taken and everything the order had refused to give.

"And in exchange," Ulyana said quietly.

"In exchange, you bring the keystones to us when they're gathered. All three. Let the Court of Everbright do the reforging—it's delicate work, covenant-work, hardly suitable for"—the pause was surgical—"amateurs. You'd be our honored guest during the restoration. Honored guest, honored ally, honored *warden-elect*, if the title fits when the time comes. Everything above board. Everything witnessed."

"That's a lot of honor," Dagny muttered from the fire.

"It is indeed," Liriope agreed serenely. "We're very honorable."

I opened my mouth—I'm told I do this at the worst moments—and Ulyana's hand moved a fraction, a small motion that meant *not yet*. So I closed it and did what I do second-best: I watched, and I took notes in my head.

Notes, for the record:

One. Liriope had said *when they're gathered*, not *if*. The Court knew about the keystones' locations—or believed it did.

Two. She had offered Ulyana a title. Warden-*elect*. Which meant the Court expected a warden to exist at the end of this, and expected to choose one.

Three. She had not once mentioned Theo Bramble. Not my bloodline, not the book, not the prophecy whose author sat six feet from her wearing borrowed boots. Either she didn't know, or—

Four. She was looking at the book right now. Had been, in fact, since she sat down. Her gaze kept returning to my satchel the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth, casual and constant, and she thought nobody noticed.

I pulled the satchel closer. Her eyes flicked to mine, and for one unguarded instant the warmth drained out of them entirely, and what was underneath was old and cold and patient as bedrock.

Then the warmth returned, seamless, and she smiled at me like I was a clever nephew.

* * *

Afterward, when Liriope had risen—announcing that she'd leave a token, in case Ulyana wished to send word, and pressing into her palm a small gold coin that hummed faintly like a struck bell—and departed by walking backward into the birch tree until the tree swallowed her, we sat around the fire for a long time without anyone speaking.

Pip broke it, because Pip always broke it. "Her shadow was wrong," the child announced, with the authority of someone reporting a missing sock. "It pointed the wrong way. Away from the fire."

"Thank you, Pip," Dagny said. "That's deeply comforting."

"I'm just saying. Wrong-way shadow."

Ulyana turned the coin over in her fingers. It caught the firelight and threw it back too brightly, too warmly, a little sun in her palm. "Well," she said. "That's the nicest anyone's offered to murder me in years."

"That's not what she offered," I said.

"No?" She glanced up at me. "What would you call it, scholar?"

"Honest question. That's the problem." I crouched by the fire, because pacing would have given away how much the visit had rattled me, and I'd learned from watching her that stillness reads as strength even when it's a lie. "She offered you everything you've wanted since you were—what, seventeen? Pardon, name, home, standing. And the price is handing the only leverage in either realm to the people who most benefit from holding it."

"You don't know they'd misuse it."

"I don't know they wouldn't. Neither do you. That's the entire shape of the offer—it's shaped exactly like trust, and it costs exactly as much as trust, and it isn't trust." I heard my own voice getting sharper and couldn't stop it. "Ulyana. They sent *her*. They sent the most beautiful thing any of us have ever seen to say the most reasonable-sounding words any of us have ever heard. That's not diplomacy. That's a weapon calibrated to each of us individually."

"To *me* individually," she corrected, but softly, and there was no argument in it.

"Yes," I admitted. "Mostly you."

Silence again. Ivo fed the fire a stick. Dagny pretended to check her bandage. Pip had fallen asleep against Dagny's shoulder mid-sentence, which was the only honest thing anyone had done in an hour.

"They knew about the Bell," Ulyana said at last. "They felt it wake. Which means they're watching the keystones, which means when we go for the second one, they'll know that too." She turned the coin once more, then flipped it to me. I caught it, and it was warm—body-warm, hand-warm, warm like something alive. "Hold onto that. If I keep it, I'll use it. Some night when the rain's coming sideways and the order's close and I'm tired enough to believe beautiful things."

"What would using it do?"

"Ask for help." She smiled, and it was the crooked, real smile, the one she spent like a miser. "And I've survived this long by never asking anybody for anything."

"You asked me to guide you," I said.

"I hired you. There's a difference."

"There really isn't," I said, "and we both know it," and she didn't answer, which from Ulyana was practically a sonnet.

* * *

I couldn't sleep. This will surprise no one who has traveled with me; I sleep the way cats permit affection, intermittently and on my own terms. But tonight the restlessness had a specific flavor. I sat up past middle-night with the prophecy book open on my knees, reading by coals, running my thumb along the water-warped pages and thinking about my grandmother's handwriting—thinking about how she'd died for these words, how someone else had died getting them to me, and how a creature woven from golden hour had looked at my satchel with eyes like a frozen lake.

*The exile who calls storms shall stand where the realms wear thin,* the old ink said, in Nana Bramble's careful hand. *And the courts shall make their offers.*

I'd always read that line as filler. Prophecies love their filler—*and lo, there shall be wailing*, etcetera. But sitting there with the coin humming in my pocket and Liriope's smile still hanging in the smoke, I understood that Nana hadn't been filling space. She'd been warning. *The courts shall make their offers.* Plural. Offers, not one offer. Which meant somewhere ahead of us, something else was going to arrive with its own version of sanctuary, its own golden light, its own price dressed as a gift.

And beneath that line, almost invisible unless you tilted the page toward the coals, was a marginal note in even older ink, faded to the color of dried blood:

*Belonging is the oldest chain. Ask what it is chained to.*

I sat with that for a long while.

Across the camp, Ulyana slept sitting up, as she always did, back against a stone, facing the pass. Even unconscious, she counted her exits. And I thought about what Liriope had offered her—not the pardon, not the title, but the thing underneath: *you will never have to be outside again.* I thought about how a person who had been thrown away at seventeen might hear those words. Might ache toward them. Might, on some bad night, with the order close and the rain sideways, reach for a humming gold coin and say *yes* without meaning to mean it.

I got up quietly, crossed the camp, and did something I'd never done: I took the coin from my own pocket and buried it under a rock twenty paces out, beyond the firelight, marking the spot with a cairn of three stones that only I would recognize.

Cowardice, probably. Or scholarship. With me the two are often indistinguishable.

I was walking back when I saw the light.

It came from the east ridge—not firelight, not moonlight, but a thin vertical seam of gold hanging in the darkness where no door should be, wide as a knife-blade, tall as a man. Standing open. Patient.

And beside it, small and barefoot and utterly silent, stood Pip, looking up at the seam of light with her head cocked, the way she'd looked at the wrong-way shadow.

"Pip," I whispered.

She turned. In the gold light her eyes were enormous, and she put one finger to her lips.

"The roads are waking up," she whispered back. "All of them. Everywhere. Something's coming through, and it ain't asking."

The seam of light began, very slowly, to widen.
