## 17. Pip's Map

Here is a thing nobody tells you about grown-ups: they think small means stupid.

I knew that before I ever met any of them. I knew it back when I was still somebody's kid, before the winter ate our village and the soldiers with the grey cloaks came and my mam put me in the root cellar and told me to count to a thousand and not come out till I finished. I got to four hundred and twelve when I heard the screaming stop. I counted to a thousand anyway. Mam always said finish what you start. Then I climbed out through the drain and walked into the mountains because up felt safer than down.

That was two years ago. Maybe three. Winters are hard to count.

Anyway. The point is: grown-ups see what they expect to see. A feral mudlark with no shoes and a habit of biting when grabbed. That's what Ulyana saw the first week, and Dagny saw, and even Theo — who's supposed to be clever — saw it too. Nobody asked why I never got lost. Nobody asked how I found water in dry country, or why I'd walk three fingers' width off the trail sometimes, for no reason anybody could see, and come out ahead of everyone who took the proper way.

Nobody asked because asking costs attention, and attention is expensive when you're running from an order of storm-priests and a court of beautiful liars.

But here's the thing about the seam of gold light standing open on the east ridge at midnight, with something on the other side breathing like a big slow animal:

Somebody had to ask *me*.

* * *

Ulyana came down the slope fast and quiet, which is her way. She moves like water finding the crack in a wall — all purpose, no splash. Behind her, further back by the dead fire, Theo was already up, book clutched against his chest like a shield made of paper, and Dagny was swearing in ferry-language, which has more words for "bloody" than any language ought to need.

"Pip." Ulyana crouched next to me, close enough that I could smell woodsmoke and cold iron on her. She does that — comes down to my level. It's one of maybe four things she does that makes her worth following. "What did you mean, something's coming through?"

"I didn't say coming through," I said. "I said asking. Coming through is what it'll do if nobody answers."

Theo arrived breathless, glasses fogged. "What is that? What *is* that?" He pointed at the seam of gold hanging in the dark like someone had cut the night open with a hot knife. "There's nothing on the east ridge. There's nothing *on* the maps—"

"That's 'cause your maps are for feet," I said.

He blinked at me. "I'm sorry?"

"Your maps are for feet and wheels and boats," I said. "They show where bodies can go. They don't show where everything else goes."

Dagny stomped up, one hand on her axe, which she sleeps holding, which I respect enormously. "The little beast is talking in riddles again."

"I'm not a beast and they're not riddles." I looked at Ulyana, because she was the only one looking at me like I might actually be saying something instead of just making noise. That's another thing worth liking about her. "You've seen them. On the trail. You just don't know you've seen them. Places where the path feels wrong. Where you walk a bit and the birds go quiet and then come back all at once, like you passed through a held breath."

Ulyana went very still. Stillness is her whole personality, mostly. But this was a particular kind of still — the kind where something old is being remembered.

"Dead ground," she said slowly. "We call it dead ground. Storm-callers are taught to route around it. The air doesn't answer there."

"It's not dead," I said. "It's *busy*. You're walking through somebody's hallway and you don't even knock."

Theo made a noise like a kettle deciding whether to boil. He sat down right in the frost-melted grass, which Dagny would've scolded him for any other night, and dragged the wet old prophecy book out of his coat. "The fae roads. The *veins* of Thornfell. There are references — fragmentary, maddeningly fragmentary — accounts of couriers crossing a hundred miles in an afternoon, armies vanishing between hillsides—" He stopped. Looked at the gold seam. Looked at me. "Pip. How long have you been able to see them?"

I shrugged. Shrugging is free.

"All your life?"

Another shrug. Then, because he was looking at me the way Ulyana looks at weather — like something that might be read — I said, "Since the winter ate my village. Before that I saw 'em like everybody else. Not at all."

Nobody said anything for a bit. The seam of gold light hung there, patient as a debt collector. And underneath us, under the mountain, I could feel it again — that big slow turning, like something asleep rolling over. It had been getting stronger every day since the drowned chapel. Since the bell. The roads were waking up because the covenant was dying, and dying things twitch, and the roads were Thornfell's veins, and veins carry blood or they carry nothing at all.

"Something's coming through," I said again, quieter. "Or trying to. It ain't asking nice. We should be gone from here before it finishes waking."

"Through *where*?" Dagny demanded. "Where does it come out?"

"Depends who opens the door," I said.

And then, because I'd been thinking about it since sundown, and because the feeling under the mountain was getting louder, and because I was tired of being the mudlark people fed but didn't consult, I said the other thing. The big thing.

"But there's a road we could take first."

* * *

They all stared at me. Grown-ups are good at staring.

"A road," Ulyana repeated.

"A shortcut." I stood up and brushed off my knees, which were filthy, which is normal. "You want to get to the Rootcrown before the gilded lot or the grey-cloaks or whatever's knocking on that door gets there first. Walking, that's eleven days through the Sorrow country, and we already know Serapha's watching the passes. But there's a way under. Through the hill. Three days. Maybe two, if the road likes us."

"Under the hill," Dagny said flatly.

"There's a door near the old ferry stones. Well. Not a door. More like a place where the hill forgets itself for a minute, if you stand right and don't breathe loud." I looked at Ulyana. "You won't see it. None of you will. That's the problem."

"And you will," Ulyana said.

"I will."

Theo turned the wet book pages carefully, hunting. "In the margins — there are marginalia in a hand I haven't identified, tiny script, notes on *passage* — 'the small ones remember the doors, being closer to the ground where the roots drink'—" He looked up at me over his fogged glasses with an expression I didn't love, because it wasn't *finally, someone useful*, it was more like grief wearing curiosity's clothes. My grandmother might have looked at me like that, probably. If I'd had one longer than four hundred and twelve counts.

"So let me get this straight," Dagny said, in the voice she uses when she's about to agree to something terrible. "The plan is: follow the mudlark into a hole in a hill that isn't a hole, onto a road that isn't a road, through the inside of a haunted mountain, to steal a second piece of a broken pact, while a storm-order hunts us and a golden court wants us dead and something enormous knocks on doors in the night."

"Yes," I said.

"Right," she said. "Just so we're all clear."

Ulyana hadn't stopped watching the gold seam. Now it was wider. Not much. A hand-span, maybe. But wider, and the light coming through it wasn't gold anymore — it had gone the color of the sky right before hail, green-white and wrong, and the air tasted like the inside of a bell.

"We leave now," she said. "Before that finishes opening. Pip — lead."

* * *

Walking at the front of a line of grown-ups is a strange job. For two years I'd walked at the edges, in the blind spots, the way you do when you're small and alone. Now five pairs of boots followed mine, and I could hear them behind me — Dagny's heavy tread, Theo tripping over rocks and pretending he hadn't, Ivo quiet as a held breath (he's the best walker of them, which figures, traitors learn to move soft), and Ulyana last, always last, watching our backs, counting exits even now. Even with me. Especially with me, maybe. I decided not to take it personal. Counting exits is how she loves things.

The old ferry stones sat in a fold of the hillside where a stream used to run before the winter killed it. Black stones in a row, taller than me, older than the village names around here. The locals called them grave-markers. They weren't. They were fence posts. Somebody, a long time ago, had built a fence between worlds, and fences rot, and posts fall, and between two of the fallen ones there was the forgetting-place.

"Stop here," I whispered. "Don't breathe loud."

"You cannot seriously—" Theo began, and then he shut up, because I'd stepped between the fallen posts and the hill forgot itself.

I can't explain it better than that. It's like when you walk into a room and forget why. Except it's a hill doing it, and it forgets it's solid, and for as long as the forgetting lasts there's a doorway where a doorway shouldn't be. To the others it probably looked like I'd walked at a black stone and vanished. From my side, the hill peeled open like a curtain of dark water, and beyond it was the road.

Oh, the road.

I'd been on it twice before. Once fleeing, once just to see if it was real and not a thing my head invented to keep me company. Both times I'd only gone a little way in and backed out, because alone, the road is a bad idea. Alone, the road notices you the way a cat notices a beetle.

But I wasn't alone now. I had a storm-witch, a scholar, a ferrywoman with an axe, a defected priest, and a wet book written by somebody's grandmother. That's practically a parade.

The road ran through the inside of the mountain like a river of silver-grey light through stone that wasn't quite stone. No sky. No walls, exactly — the rock curved overhead far enough away that it might have been clouds, or memory of clouds. And along both sides, at intervals, stood things like trees except they were made of the same silver light, and their branches held lanterns that had gone out, hundreds of them, thousands, a whole avenue of dead lights stretching away farther than my eyes wanted to follow.

"Theo," Ulyana said behind me, very quietly. "Tell me you're seeing this."

"I'm seeing it," he breathed. "I'm seeing it and I will never sleep again."

"Is it supposed to look like a cathedral had a baby with a river?" Dagny asked.

"This is a fae road," Theo said. His voice had gone reverent and cracked at the same time. "These are supposed to be *myths*. Couriers' tales. Grandmother mentioned them only once, in code, in a letter I thought was nonsense — she wrote 'the lamps of the under-way have all gone cold, and that is how you will know the hour.'" He touched one of the dead lanterns with one careful finger. Nothing happened. Nothing warm happened, anyway. But the lantern flickered — once, faintly, like a sleeper swatting at a fly — and Theo snatched his hand back.

"They're not all dead," I said. "Farther in. Some of 'em still burn. Those are the parts of the mountain that haven't given up yet."

I started walking. The road made a sound under our feet, very faint, like a long note held on a glass rim. After a while I realized we were going faster than walking. Not running — the steps felt normal — but the dead lantern-trees slid past quicker than they should, and when I looked back, the entrance had folded shut behind us into seamless hill-dark, and Dagny said a word in ferry-language that I filed away for later use.

"How long?" Ulyana asked, drawing level with me. She kept her hand near her knife. Her eyes kept moving — up at the false clouds, down at the glowing ground, at every shadow between lanterns. Counting exits in a place with none. I almost felt sorry for her.

"Two days, if the road behaves. Three if it sulks."

"Roads sulk?"

"These do. They're alive. Sort of. They're the mountain's veins, and the mountain's sick, and sick things get cranky." I glanced up at her. "Also there's tolls."

She stopped walking. So did everyone else, which shows how well trained they are. "Tolls."

"Not money. Roads like these don't want money. They want—" I waved a hand. "True things. That's what the stories say, anyhow, and the stories I heard from the hobs under the Marrowgate bridge were usually right about the ugly stuff."

"True things," Theo repeated. "Payment in truth. Yes — yes, that's consistent, the old pact-economics, oaths as currency—" He was pacing now, excited, which is what he does instead of being scared. "Then we mustn't lie on this road. At all. Not even small lies. Not even polite ones."

"Wonderful," Dagny muttered. "So no more telling Pip his cooking doesn't taste like boiled boot."

"It does taste like boiled boot," I said.

"See? Now we're all doomed."

Ivo spoke for the first time since the ridge. He'd been staring back the way we'd come, at the sealed dark. "The herald," he said quietly. "The thing opening the door on the east ridge. Could it follow us in here?"

I listened, the way you listen — not with ears. The road hummed its glass note beneath us. Far, far behind, at the edge of hearing, something else hummed too. Slower. Deeper. Like the biggest bell in the world being struck once, and the sound still traveling toward us through the bones of the earth.

"No," I said. Then, honest, because lying on this road was now officially a bad idea: "Not through the door it opened. But it doesn't need that door. It's already *in* the mountain, deep down, where the third keystone sleeps. It's not chasing us." I swallowed. "It's waiting for us. All of us. Everyone who's gathering the pieces. We're not the runners in this story. We're the crumbs leading it to bread."

Nobody said anything. The dead lanterns slid past, and past, and past, and somewhere ahead, I could see it — a single point of living gold, one lamp still burning, growing slowly bigger.

"One lamp," Theo murmured. "Why would one lamp still burn?"

"Because," I said, "somebody left it lit for us. That's how roads work when they like you. Come on. It knows we're coming."

And far below us, deeper than deep, the great bell hummed again — and this time, faint and vast and unmistakable, it hummed *back*.
