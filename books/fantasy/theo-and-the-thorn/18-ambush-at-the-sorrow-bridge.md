## 18. Ambush at the Sorrow Bridge

The Sorrow Bridge earned its name honestly, which in my experience is rare for geography. Most places get their names from some poet's whim or a landlord's flattery—Crystal Falls that's mostly scum, the Vale of Plenty where half the children used to starve. But the Sorrow Bridge was named by the people who crossed it weeping, because it was the last crossing before the deep Thornfell passes, and everyone who took it was leaving something behind.

A hundred feet of weathered planks strung across a gorge so deep the river at its bottom sounded less like water and more like breathing. Fog lived down there permanently, thick and pale as spilled milk. The locals said you could hear your dead calling up out of it if you listened too long. I'd crossed eleven times and never listened. I had enough dead already; I didn't need to go collecting more.

We reached it at dusk with Pip riding Dagny's shoulders like a very small, very feral figurehead, Ivo walking with his hood back for the first time since he'd defected—he said the order tracked him by his shadow now, which was either true or a symptom—and Theo clutching the prophecy book to his chest the way other men clutched their mothers' letters. The keystones rode wrapped in oilcloth in my pack: the Bell of Hours cold against my spine even through two layers of canvas, the Rootcrown warm, a living thing pretending to be wood.

"Bridge looks sound," Dagny announced, stamping a plank with one boot. It boomed like a drum. "Sound-ish."

"You've been a ferrywoman for thirty years," Ivo said. "You know exactly how sound it is."

"That's why I'm giving you a professional estimate. Sound-ish." She set Pip down and rolled her shoulders. "I'll cross first. If it holds me, it'll hold anything short of a siege engine."

"If it doesn't hold you?" Pip asked.

Dagny grinned her gap-toothed grin. "Then you'll all owe me a funeral feast, and knowing this lot, you'll skimp on the wine."

It should have been funny. It was funny. We laughed, or made the shapes of laughing, and I remember thinking afterward—I remember this with terrible clarity—that I catalogued that laugh the way I catalogued exits. Filed it away. Kept it.

I want to say something here about what I felt when I looked back at that bridge. But the truth is I didn't look back. That's the whole of it. You spend years learning not to look back and then one day looking back is the only thing left you'd give anything to do.

* * *

The fog came up out of the gorge just after full dark, and that was wrong.

I knew the Sorrow Bridge's moods. Its fog stayed below, sulking in the gorge like an old dog too tired to climb. When it started rising past the railings in ropes and ribbons, moving against the wind—

"Stop," I said.

Everyone stopped. Even Pip, who stopped for almost no one.

"Fog's climbing," I said. "Fog shouldn't climb."

Ivo went still in the particular way storm-callers go still, listening with their whole skin. His face did something complicated. "Ulyana."

"I know."

"It's not fog."

"I *know*, Ivo."

Because I could feel it now that I let myself feel—the pressure behind the mist, the taste of ozone under the river-smell. Cloudcraft. Someone was spinning cloudcraft, and there were maybe six people alive who could spin it well enough to make it walk uphill, and five of them hated me officially, and the sixth was standing next to me looking sick.

"They found us," Theo whispered.

"No," I said. "They found the bridge. We're a bonus."

The first bolt came out of the mist without any warning flash—a lance of white heat that hit the far anchor post and blew it into splinters and burning rope. The whole span lurched sideways like a living thing in pain. Pip screamed, high and thin. Dagny caught him one-handed without even looking, which tells you everything about Dagny.

"Off the bridge!" I shouted. "Everyone off—"

And then they came out of the fog.

Storm-callers of the Order of the Veiled Sky, in their grey-and-silver, hoods back, faces painted with the ritual lines that meant *no quarter given*. Nine of them. I counted the way you count in a fight, automatically, uselessly, because counting doesn't save you, but nine. Serapha had sent nine for four travelers and a child. Flattering, if you squinted. Or terrified, if you didn't.

I recognized two of them. Corven, who'd taught me to read wind-pressure by the ache in my teeth. Little Maren Ash, who'd been twelve when I left, who'd cried at my trial, who was now painting warlines on her own face and holding lightning like she'd been born to it.

That's the thing about exile nobody warns you about. Your past doesn't stay politely behind you. It learns to hunt.

"Halloran!" Corven's voice cracked across the gorge like a whip. "Surrender the book and the stones. The Warden-Speaker offers mercy."

"She always says that," I called back. "Last time her mercy was a cell with no windows."

"Last time you were guilty." A pause. Even through the fog I could hear him choosing the words carefully, which meant the words mattered, which meant someone had scripted them. "This time, surrender is absolution."

Theo grabbed my sleeve. "Ulyana. What do we do?"

What we do is die, I thought, or run, and running means the bridge, and the bridge is dying under our feet.

The second bolt took the near anchor.

* * *

Here is what happened next, and I will describe it as plainly as I can because I have spent every night since trying not to dress it up.

The bridge sagged. The ropes burned. Pip was on the span, halfway across—he'd bolted toward the far side, child-logic, away from the fire—and the far anchor post was gone, and the whole structure swung out over the gorge like a pendulum with a boy on it.

And Dagny Zhou-of-nowhere, ferrywoman, smuggler's guide, woman who once talked her way past three border wardens with nothing but a barrel of pickled herring and sheer contempt—Dagny went onto that bridge.

Not ran. Walked. Calm as Sunday. She got Pip over her shoulder the way you carry a sack of grain, turned around, and started back while the world tried to shake her off into the dark.

"DAGNY!" Theo's voice, cracking apart.

"Shut up and hold the rope!" she bellowed, and grabbed a stay-line with her free hand, and kept coming, plank by burning plank, with arrows of white light falling around her like the sky had decided to harvest her specifically.

I stood at the bridgehead and I understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst moment of your life, that she wasn't going to make it. Not because she was slow. Because they weren't aiming at her yet—they were aiming ahead of her, herding her, and the moment she reached the near third of the span, close enough to be a real threat or a real hostage, the orders would change and she would stop existing.

I had sworn an oath. Eleven years ago, kneeling on wet stone while Serapha watched with her face folded shut, I had sworn to never again raise a storm, to let my power rot in me like a chained dog, because that was the price of walking away instead of hanging. I had kept it. Through Marrowgate's blackened fields, through the Goat's Teeth, through every night the sky begged me—I had kept it, because keeping it was the last piece of myself the order hadn't taken.

Dagny stumbled. Caught herself. Kept coming.

The order raised their hands together, all nine, and the air above the bridge began to boil.

There are moments when the choice isn't a choice. There's just a gap between who you were and what has to happen, and you fall through it.

I stepped past the burning bridgehead onto the first intact plank, planted my feet, and opened the sky.

* * *

People think calling a storm is shouting. It isn't. It's asking. You ask the air to remember it was once ocean, and the clouds to remember they were once rivers, and the pressure to stop lying about itself—and if you ask correctly, with the right grief in your mouth, everything answers.

I hadn't asked in eleven years. I'd forgotten how much of me had been waiting.

It came down the gorge like something released from prison. Every drop of moisture in that fog turned soldier. The temperature fell so fast the river below cracked and groaned. Lightning didn't fork—it *poured*, a white cataract of it, and I aimed it the way Serapha had taught me before I understood she was teaching me to be a weapon: not at the callers themselves, because some part of me—even then, even now—couldn't quite kill them—but at their craft. At the cloudwork. At the boiling air above the bridge.

Their spell shattered like a dropped mirror. Three of them went down screaming, hands burned by their own backlash. Maren Ash threw up a shield and I broke it in half and saw her face, young and appalled, mouthing something—my name, maybe, or a prayer—and I pulled the next strike wide and it hit the cliff instead, raining stone.

Corven rallied them. Credit where due; he was good. He spun a counter-gale that actually staggered me, actually bent my storm aside for two full seconds, and in those two seconds a bolt got through and hit the bridge twenty feet behind Dagny.

She didn't scream. That's what I keep returning to, the thing I can't put down no matter how many times I pick it up. She didn't scream. She adjusted her grip on Pip, looked down at the smoking ruin where the planks had been, and shouted—not to me, not to the order, to *Pip*—"Don't you dare look down, little man. Look at me. Eyes on me."

I tore the storm open over the hunters' heads and brought the whole gorge's weather down on them at once—hail like slingstones, wind like a slammed door, rain in sheets so hard the fog drowned. They scattered. Corven dragged two of them back into the mist. Maren hesitated—one heartbeat, looking straight at me across all that ruin, her warlines streaked and running—and then she was gone too, swallowed by her own retreat.

And the bridge, freed of everything except physics, finally finished dying.

Dagny was ten feet from the end. Ten feet. I felt the main cable part the way you feel a bone break in your own body, a shudder through the whole span, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do: ten feet, a falling arc, a gorge with a bottom you couldn't see.

I threw the storm downward. All of it. Everything left. An updraft under the collapsing span, crude and desperate, the magical equivalent of catching a falling plate with your face. The bridge didn't stop falling. But it fell *slower*, and it fell *angled*, and Dagny—Dagny, gods keep her, Dagny understood in half a heartbeat what I was doing—leapt.

She cleared the gap. She landed on rock, on solid ground, with Pip still clamped to her shoulder, and she rolled, and came up on one knee, and grinned that gap-toothed grin, and opened her mouth to say something magnificent, I'm certain, something we'd have repeated around campfires for years—

—and folded.

Just folded, like laundry, like the fight had simply left her all at once. And I saw the blood. I hadn't seen it during, couldn't have—there'd been too much rain—but a shard of the blown anchor post, a splinter of bridge-longer-than-memory, had gone into her side somewhere in those last ten feet, and she had carried Pip across anyway, and jumped anyway, and grinned anyway.

"Oh no," Theo said beside me. Just that. Oh no, small and flat, the sound a person makes when language fails.

* * *

We carried her off the rocks to the leeward hollow below the bridgehead, Theo and Ivo and me, Pip running ahead crying and denying he was crying. Ivo worked with hands steadier than mine, which shamed me—he'd been an order healer before he defected; he'd stitched wounds the order's own storms had made, and he said later that was the only reason his hands didn't shake now, because they'd shaken so much already they'd worn out.

The splinter had gone deep. Deeper than deep. Ivo's face told me everything before his mouth could, and I made myself look at that face and not away, because Dagny deserved someone who looked.

"Can you—" Theo started.

"I can keep her comfortable," Ivo said quietly. "I can fight the fever. I can give her time." He tied off another stitch, jaw tight. "Time is not the same as saving."

"How much time?" I asked.

"Ask the mountain. Ask whoever you prayed to before you stopped praying." He glanced up at me, and there was no blame in it, which somehow was worse than blame. "You saved her life tonight, Ulyana. Don't you dare let anyone tell you different. Including you."

Pip sat against Dagny's unwounded side and held her hand and told her, in a fierce whisper, all the things they were going to do when she got up. The hot springs he'd heard about. The pie she'd promised him in Lowmark. He had a whole list. He'd clearly been keeping a list for weeks, the way children do, hoarding futures like coins.

Dagny's eyes opened. Found me.

"There she is," she rasped. "The weather woman. Told you the bridge was sound-ish."

"Don't talk," I said.

"Gonna talk." She coughed, winced, grinned through it. "Listen. The wine. For the funeral feast. You lot will try to skimp. Don't let Theo pick it, he thinks anything over two copper is a scam—"

"Stop it," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle like bad ice, and she heard it, and something in her face gentled.

"Hey. Halloran." Her hand found mine, grip weaker than I wanted it to be, still stronger than it had any right to be. "You broke your oath tonight. Whole sky came down. I saw it." A breath. "Best thing I ever saw. Whatever penance you were planning—spend it different. Spend it on finishing this. Get the boy's book where it goes. Get the kid his pie." Her eyes drifted to Pip, soft as I'd ever seen anything. "Get 'em all home. That's the penance. That's all of it."

"Dagny—"

"And Ulyana." Her grip tightened, one last flare of the old iron. "When they come back—and they'll come back—you don't hold back to spare them. Not one bolt. They chose their side with paint on their faces." She closed her eyes. "Now somebody find me something to drink that isn't river water, and somebody tell this child about the pie."

Theo found the bottle. Of course he did. Cheap plum brandy, the kind he complained cost double what it should, and he held it to her lips like it was communion wine, and she drank, and made a face, and said, "Two copper, maximum," and laughed until it hurt, and stopped laughing, and slept.

* * *

I walked out past the firelight, to the gorge's edge, and I let the storm go.

Not all at once. Storms don't leave you all at once; they leak, they sulk, they drip out of you for hours. So I stood at the rim in the drizzle of my own making and I shook, and I thought about the oath, and about what Dagny had said—*spend it different*—and about Maren Ash's face, thirteen and horrified, mouthing my name through the rain.

Eleven years I'd told myself the oath was wisdom. Discipline. The price of survival. Standing there with my hands still smelling of ozone, I finally admitted the uglier truth: the oath had also been a hiding place. As long as I never raised a storm, I never had to learn what I was willing to do with one. Never had to find out whether the order was right about me. Exile as armor. Powerlessness as a kind of innocence.

Tonight the armor had come off, and the sky had answered me like a loyal dog, and three people lay burned in the fog because of it, and Dagny was bleeding out ten feet from safety, and victory tasted exactly like ash, like cinders on the tongue, like the inside of a burnt page.

Behind me, footsteps. Theo. He stopped at my shoulder and said nothing for a long time, which is one of the thousand reasons I hadn't killed him yet.

"The bell rang," he said finally.

I frowned. "What?"

"The Bell of Hours. In your pack. When you called the storm—it rang. Once." He was quiet a moment. "In the chapel, the guardian said the bells mark what matters. I think..." He exhaled. "I think it counted tonight. Whatever tonight was."

I looked east, where the storm I'd spent was breaking apart over the peaks, ragged and gorgeous and done. Somewhere out in that dark, Serapha was regrouping. Somewhere behind us, Dagny was fighting a fever with plum brandy and a feral child's list of futures. And somewhere under my ribs, where the oath had been, there was a new space, raw and echoing, waiting to be filled with something.

"We move at first light," I said. "Slow stages. She rides, we don't argue about it." I turned back toward the fire, toward the family that was smaller tonight than it had been that morning. "And Theo? Nobody touches my pack. The bell and I need to have a conversation."

Because there was a question sitting in me like a swallowed stone, and I needed the old bronze to answer it:

If the Bell of Hours rings for what matters—

Why, of all the nights of my life, had it chosen the one where I finally became the weapon they always said I was?
