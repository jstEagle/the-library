## 19. Tending Wounds, Trading Truths

Fever has a smell. I learned that the night my grandmother died—willow-bark tea going cold on a table, the sour-sweet reek of a body burning itself from the inside out. I'd hoped never to learn it a second time.

Dagny lay in the bedroll nearest the fire, and she smelled of it now: willow bark, sweat, and river water gone stale in her clothes because she refused to be undressed by anyone but Ulyana, and Ulyana's hands had been needed elsewhere for the last six hours. The ferrywoman's leg was wrapped in a poultice that Ivo had mixed with the grim economy of a man who'd done field medicine for an order that caused most of its own wounds. The bandage had been white at dawn. It was not white anymore.

"Her color's wrong," Pip said. The child sat cross-legged near Dagny's head, holding her hand with both of theirs, as if they could anchor her to the world by grip alone. "She was gray this morning. Now she's got some pink back. Is pink good?"

"Pink is good," Ivo said from across the fire, not looking up from the arrow he wasn't really fletching. "Pink means the fever is arguing with her instead of winning."

"And if it wins the argument?"

Ivo set down the arrow. "It won't."

He said it the way he said everything—as though he were reading it off a list of things that were true whether or not he believed them—but Pip seemed satisfied, and crawled off to sleep curled against a pack like a cat who had decided the household's crisis could proceed without their supervision.

Which left three of us awake: me, Ulyana, and the fire.

We hadn't spoken more than ten words to each other since the bridge. There is a particular silence that follows violence—not the shocked silence of people who have seen something terrible, but the working silence of people who are still inside the terrible thing, carrying pieces of it around, deciding where to put them down. Ulyana had killed four men on the Sorrow Bridge. I had watched her do it. I had watched the sky come down off the mountains to answer her like a dog answering a whistle it had spent years pretending not to hear, and I had understood, in that moment, two things with total clarity.

The first was that everything in the prophecy book was true, and worse than true—it was *incomplete*, the way a map is incomplete when the cartographer has left off the dragon.

The second was that I loved her.

I want to be precise about the order of those revelations, because I've thought about it since. The clarity came first. The love came half a heartbeat later, arriving not as a discovery but as a confirmation—the scholarly pleasure of finding that a hypothesis you've been too cowardly to test turns out to have been correct all along.

Now she sat across the fire from me, mending a strap on her pack with the fierce concentration of someone performing surgery, and I decided that if we were both going to be awake until dawn anyway, we might as well stop being strangers about it.

"You should sleep," she said, without looking up. Of course she knew I was watching. She always knows.

"So should you."

"I don't sleep well after storms."

"After storms you cause, or storms you witness?"

That got her to look up. Her eyes caught the firelight and did something unfair with it. "Is there a difference?"

"There used to be," I said. "Before the bridge."

* * *

Here is what I know about confession: it is easier when the other person goes first, which is why nobody ever goes first, which is why everyone dies with their secrets arranged neatly around them like funeral goods.

Ulyana broke the deadlock, which surprised me. Or perhaps it didn't. Perhaps I had already learned that she does the frightening thing first, always, so that no one can ever accuse her of being afraid.

"Ivo told me what you asked him," she said. "About the sacrifice clause. Whether it was literal."

"He didn't answer."

"No. He answered me, though." She set down the strap. "He says the old covenant required a warden. Someone bound to the mountain. Permanently." She let the word sit there between us like a stone dropped into a well, and I listened for the splash and heard none, because she had already decided how deep it went. "So before you say anything else tonight, Bramble, I want to know why you're really here. Not the book. Not the prophecy. You. Because you've been carrying that book like it weighs a hundred pounds, and paper doesn't weigh a hundred pounds unless it's attached to something heavier."

I fed a stick into the fire and bought myself a moment with it. She waited. She was very good at waiting; it was one of the many things about her that I found myself cataloguing against my will, the way a scholar catalogs specimens, except that every entry came out looking less like research and more like devotion.

"My grandmother's name was Ottoline Bramble," I said. "You may have heard of her, if your order burned libraries as thoroughly as mine did. She was the archivist of the Lowland Concordance. Keeper of the covenant records. And forty years ago—no. Thirty-eight. She wrote a book."

"The book you're carrying."

"A copy of it. The original went into the archive, and the archive—" I shrugged. "Archives burn. That's practically their defining feature. Someone set fire to the Concordance vaults eleven years ago. Officially, it was lamp oil and carelessness. Unofficially, my grandmother spent her last decade making copies of the covenant records and smuggling them out one page at a time, sewn into the bindings of harmless books, and someone in the lowlands government wanted those copies to stop existing."

"And she gave one to you."

"She gave one to a boy who thought his grandmother was just a fussy old woman who smelled of ink and disapproved of his handwriting." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "She died the week after she gave it to me. Fever. Which is why I know the smell, incidentally. She called it her 'retirement project,' the book. Said she'd finally write the family history she'd been threatening us with for years. I read it cover to cover as a child and thought it was fiction. Beautiful fiction. A girl who calls storms, three keystones, a mountain that needs a keeper. I memorized passages because I liked the rhythm of them. I recited them at her bedside while she died, and she held my hand and said, 'Good, Theo. You'll need the words later.' And I thought—grief makes old women strange. I thought she was confused."

"But she wasn't."

"She was the only person in either realm who knew exactly how confused I would need to be." I looked at the fire. "Eleven years I carried that book. Eleven years I told myself I was a scholar studying an interesting text. Then the winter started eating villages, and I looked up the dates in the prophecies against the dates in the gazetteers, and every single one matched, and I stopped being able to pretend it was fiction. So I came to Thornfell to find the exile who calls storms, and everyone I asked refused to tell me where you were, and eventually I realized that was because everyone here loves you enough to protect you from me."

"They don't love me," Ulyana said. "They tolerate me."

"Dagny took an arrow through the thigh for you."

"Dagny took an arrow for the caravan. I was simply standing behind the caravan at the time." But her voice did something on the word *simply*, a small fracture, and I understood that she had been counting her exits again—the habit she probably thought I hadn't noticed—and that somewhere between Marrowgate and the bridge, the count had started including people instead of places.

"My turn," she said, before I could respond. "Since you went first. Properly first, I mean. No fragments."

"All right."

"I told you I was exiled for killing a fellow storm-caller. That part was true. What I didn't tell you is that I don't remember doing it." 

The fire cracked. Somewhere up the slope, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhale.

"There was a night," she went on, "eleven years ago—I've noticed everything in my life happened eleven years ago, isn't that neat? Yours and mine run parallel, Bramble, did you plan that?—a night when the whole order was summoned to raise a ward-storm over the eastern valleys. Forty callers, standing in a ring, pulling the sky down. And somewhere in the middle of it, a girl named Cassia Verrow ended up dead, and I was the only caller whose hands bore storm-signs. Fresh ones. Everyone saw the marks on my fingers. Nobody saw me do anything." She flexed her hands, and I saw her looking at them as if checking for the marks even now. "They tried me within the month. Serapha vouched for evidence I never saw gathered. Witnesses described things happening that I have no memory of. And the worst part—the part I've never said aloud to anyone—is that for years afterward, I *doubted myself*. Because what if I did do it? What if I called the bolt and my mind hid it from me because it couldn't bear it? I built my whole life on top of maybe-murder. Every kindness I've done since, I've done to balance a scale I couldn't see."

"And now?"

"Now the guardian in the drowned chapel showed me that night." Her jaw tightened. "And in the vision, I was standing in the ring, and the storm-signs appeared on my hands, and I never cast anything. Someone *fed* the mark onto me. Someone stood behind me in the circle and put the killing in my hands without my knowledge, and I felt it happen in the vision, Theo. Like a hook going into the back of my neck. I felt where it entered." She turned her palms up. "I just couldn't see who was standing there."

"Serapha."

"I don't know. That's the truth. I don't know, and I've made peace with not knowing, because not knowing is at least honest." She pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them, and for a moment she looked young, which startled me; I kept forgetting she was only a few years older than me, because she wore her exile like armor plate. "So that's my ledger. Falsely convicted, possibly by my own mentor, definitely by someone in my own family of callers. Exiled to keep me small and far away. And I survived it by becoming invisible—by guiding smugglers and learning every goat track in Thornfell and convincing myself that anonymity was freedom." She looked at me across the fire. "It wasn't. It was just a slower cage. And then you walked into the inn with a book that had my name in it, and the cage door opened, and it turned out the cage had been load-bearing. Everything I was stands on that exile. Take it away and I don't know what's underneath."

"I do," I said.

"Don't."

"Someone stubborn," I said. "Someone who counts her money and her exits and the number of people willing to bleed for her, and keeps getting the third number wrong because it keeps growing. Someone whose idea of mercy is standing between an arrow and a ferrywoman. Someone who—" I stopped, because her expression had gone still in a way I recognized as dangerous, or possibly as terrified, and with Ulyana the two were often indistinguishable.

"Don't make me into something, Bramble. People who get made into things get used."

"I'm not making you into anything. I'm telling you what I observed." I hesitated. And then, because apparently we were trading truths tonight and I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a ledger unbalanced: "There's a reason I haven't slept properly since the bridge. It isn't the fighting. It's that when the storm came, I wasn't afraid. Four armed men shooting at us, a woman bleeding out on the stones, the sky tearing open—and I stood there thinking, *she'll handle it.* Complete certainty. Do you understand what that means? I've spent eleven years verifying sources and doubting documents and triple-checking everything, and in one afternoon I developed absolute faith in another human being, and faith terrifies me more than any hunter your order can send."

Ulyana was quiet for a long time.

"That's a stupid thing to have faith in," she said finally. "Storms kill the people who call them. Ask any warden in any prophecy."

"Then I'm stupid."

"Yes," she agreed, and something in her voice had loosened, some knot I hadn't known was there. "You are."

* * *

Dagny's fever broke a little before dawn.

I was dozing upright against a stone when I heard the change in her breathing—the wet rattle smoothing out into something steadier—and opened my eyes to find Ulyana already kneeling beside her, pressing the back of her hand to Dagny's forehead with a gentleness I doubted the ferrywoman would ever be allowed to remember.

"Well?" Ivo asked quietly, from wherever Ivo went when he wasn't visibly present. He had a gift for occupying space without seeming to.

"Cooler," Ulyana said. "She'll live. She'll complain about the scar for thirty years, but she'll live."

"Thirty years," Pip repeated sleepily, from the depths of a blanket. "Good. I promised her I'd learn to swim by then."

I crossed to the fire and found Ulyana standing at its edge, looking east, where the sky was doing the thing that passes for dawn under a covenant gone rotten—a thin graying of the dark, no warmth in it, the light of a world holding its breath. The frost had crept closer to our camp overnight. I could see its edge on the ground, a silver line in the dirt like a tide mark, patient as arithmetic.

"It's moving faster," she said, when I joined her. "The frost line. Two miles since yesterday. Before the bridge, it was half that."

"The keystones."

"Two down." She held up her fingers. "One to go. Except the third one isn't a bell or a rootcrown or anything you can dig up and carry. Ivo says it's the original oath itself, held in the memory of the first warden's ghost. Which means the last keystone is a conversation with a dead person who gets to decide if I'm worthy." She smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "And then whatever's left after that. The binding. The clause nobody wants to read twice."

"We don't have to talk about that now."

"When, then? When we're standing at the spire?" She shook her head. "No. I've spent eleven years not talking about things, Bramble. Look where it got me. Say what you were going to say at the fire. You had a face. You've been wearing it all night."

I took a breath. This was the part I'd rehearsed and abandoned and rehearsed again, the part that had no good version, only versions with different shapes of damage.

"The book's second half," I said. "The pages hidden in the binding. The restoration magic revealed them, and I've been translating them since, slowly, and I found the passage about the warden. Who the mountain accepts." I watched her face. "It requires someone *of both bloods*. Human and fae. That's the requirement, Ulyana. That's the fine print. Only a half-blood can bind the covenant."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for me to count my own heartbeats. Twelve. Fifteen. Twenty.

When she spoke, her voice was perfectly level, which from Ulyana was the loudest alarm there was.

"Why would you wait until now to tell me that?"

"Because I hoped I was misreading it. Because I hoped the translation was wrong. Because telling you felt like handing you a death sentence written in my grandmother's handwriting, and I'm a coward about exactly one thing in this world, and it's you." I made myself go on. "And because there's a second part. The restoration also recovered a marginal note in Ottoline's hand. Just one line, in the margin next to the blood-requirement. It says: *'The order knows what she is. That is why they exiled her—to keep her from the mountain, from the truth, from the crown of storms. If she ever reaches the spire, it will be because they failed, and heaven help us all, because she will be magnificent.'*" I swallowed. "Ulyana. The note doesn't name you. It couldn't have—you weren't born yet. But it describes the exile. It describes *an* exile who calls storms, kept deliberately ignorant of her own blood. My grandmother knew the order was hiding half-bloods. Knew they'd done it before. Maybe—maybe to your mother. Maybe to you."

Ulyana didn't move. The firelight moved on her instead, shadows sliding across a face gone absolutely still, and when she finally turned to look at me, her eyes were wet and furious and more alive than I had ever seen them.

"Say the rest," she said. "Whatever you're still holding back, Bramble. Say all of it. Tonight we trade truths, remember? All of them. Even the ugly ones."

So I told her the last piece—the piece I'd found at the bottom of the restored pages, the marginal note beneath the marginal note, the one that had kept me awake while the camp slept:

*"If my notes survive me: find the Zhou girl. Whatever the order tells the world she did, check the trial record against the weather. Storms remember what courts forget."*

She stared at me. Behind us, Dagny breathed, slow and steady, alive. Above us, past the frost line, past the ridges, the mountain waited with something that might have been patience and might have been hunger.

"Storms remember," Ulyana whispered.

And high overhead, though the air hung windless and cold, thunder answered her—soft, distant, unmistakable—like something vast turning over in its sleep, remembering too.
