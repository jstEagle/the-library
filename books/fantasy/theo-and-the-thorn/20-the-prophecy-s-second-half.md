## 20. The Prophecy's Second Half

There are two kinds of scholars in this world: the kind who treat a book like a relic, and the kind who treat it like a suspect. I was trained as the first kind. The last few months have made me very much the second.

The book had been behaving strangely since the Sorrow Bridge.

Not dramatically — no glowing, no whispering, none of the theatrics you'd expect from an artifact that had already gotten one archivist killed and was working on a second career. It behaved the way a guilty man behaves in a room full of constables. Quietly. Watchfully. When I picked it up, the water-swollen covers felt heavier than they should have, the way a lie feels heavier the longer you carry it.

I noticed it first on the night we buried Dagny's fever under willow-bark and prayer, when I went to transcribe the prophecy for the dozenth time and found the ink had shifted.

Not faded. Shifted. The word *exile* on the opening page sat a hair lower than it had the day before, as if the line had settled, like a house on bad foundations. I told myself I was tired. I told myself a lot of things that week. Dagny's fever was burning through its second day, Pip hadn't slept more than an hour at a stretch since the bridge, and Ulyana had gone somewhere between the campfire and the treeline to be alone, which was her way of saying she needed not to be looked at. I had learned not to follow. Mostly.

But the ink had shifted, and I am my grandmother's grandson, and my grandmother raised me on one commandment above all others: *when a book lies to you, Theo, it is because it is afraid of what you'll find if you make it tell the truth.*

So I did what any reasonable man would do with a century-old prophecy book at midnight in an outlaw camp: I took it apart.

* * *

Restoration magic is not the glamorous branch of the craft. Nobody writes ballads about the menders. The ballads go to the battle-mages who set the sky on fire, and the duelists, and — if the balladeer is feeling generous — the healers. The menders get the unglamorous miracles: the mended bone, the unburned letter, the un-rotted beam that keeps a roof up one more winter. My grandmother's whole philosophy fit in a thimble: *the world is always breaking, Theo; the only interesting question is what you put back together, and what you leave broken on purpose.*

I set the book on the flat stone by the fire, wrapped my hands in the mender's weave — a low, warm working, less a spell than a sustained act of attention — and began, very gently, to ask the binding to remember what it used to be.

Water damage lies. That's the first thing they teach you. Water doesn't destroy a book; it *rearranges* it, presses pages into new shapes, glues secrets shut. A swollen binding is a locked box that got rained on, and everyone assumes the rain was the end of the story. It almost never is. The story is still in there. It's just holding its breath.

The leather gave up its secrets in layers. The outermost layer was a century of damp and one very bad river crossing, and it came away like fog. Under that: smoke. The archive burn had gotten into the grain, a black ghost of the fire that had killed Master Aldous and however many others whose names I'd never know. I worked the smoke out thread by thread, and the leather under it was a different color entirely — not the drab brown of a scholar's commonplace book but a deep oxblood red, dyed, expensive, the kind of cover you put on a book you intend to survive you.

And under *that* — under the spine's stitching, where the thread had been redone in a hurry by someone with competent hands and no time —

Pages.

Not loose leaves tucked in. Pages *bound in*, sewn into the spine itself beneath the visible gathering, folded flat and covered over with a pastedown so old and so thorough that whoever did it had meant it to survive everything short of a determined scholar with a guilty conscience and nothing to do at midnight but take books apart.

I sat back on my heels. The camp was quiet around me. Dagny's breathing rasped slow and even in the big tent — better, better than yesterday, the fever finally bending. Pip was a small bundled shape against Dagny's side, having finally lost the argument with sleep. Somewhere out in the dark, on the ridge above camp, Ulyana was sitting with the wind, doing whatever she did out there, and I found myself, absurdly, wanting to go get her before I read another word. Which was ridiculous. It was a book. I had read this book forty times.

I read the hidden pages anyway.

* * *

The hand was my grandmother's. I'd know it anywhere — the sharp ascenders, the way she crossed her t's like she was striking them through, the little hooked asterisks she used to mark the passages she wasn't sure of. I had grown up watching that hand move across paper by lamplight. Seeing it here, in a book I'd carried across half a mountain range while people shot arrows at me, was like being grabbed by the collar by a dead woman.

She had written it as a letter. That was the first shock. The prophecy — the whole prophecy, the one that had gotten Aldous killed, the one with Ulyana's name and tonight's date inked in a century ago — was not a prophecy at all in this version. It was a *record*. She had written it in past tense.

*The exile who calls storms came to me in the spring of the year the frost came early,* my grandmother wrote, *and she told me what she was, and I did not believe her, and by the time I believed her she was gone, and the covenant was already dying, and there was nothing left to do but write down what she said would happen, so that when it happened, someone would be ready.*

Someone would be ready. I turned the page with hands that had gone unsteady, and the mender's weave flickered and had to be rekindled.

*The warden must be of both bloods.*

There it was. Not buried in verse. Not dressed up in the oblique riddling language the published fragments used — the *child of neither shore*, the *one who walks the veil's edge*. My grandmother had gotten tired of riddles, or had never had patience for them, and had written it out plain, in the voice she used when she was angry:

*Of both bloods. Human and fae, fae and human, and no one else. I have checked the histories. I have checked them twice. Every warden the mountain has ever taken was of both bloods, and every one of them knew it beforehand, and every one of them was given the choice — that is the part the courts will want to erase, so mark it well: the choice. The mountain does not take thieves. The mountain takes volunteers. If anyone tells you otherwise, they are selling something.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, a dry cracked sound in the dark, and Pip stirred, and I held still until the small breathing evened out again. My grandmother, a century dead, still lecturing. Still underlining. Still so certain the world would try to cheat that she'd sewn the correction into the spine of the only copy.

*The exile who calls storms is of both bloods,* the next line read. *She does not know it. She has been made not to know it, and the making was done carefully, by people who loved her, or who told themselves they did. I am writing this down so that when she finds out — and she will find out, because the truth is a patient animal and she is a thorough one — she will know that at least one person, a hundred years before her trouble began, thought she deserved to hear it from a friend.*

The fire popped. Somewhere in the treeline an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

I read the rest of the hidden gathering twice. Most of it was mechanics — the keystones, the spire, the oath in both tongues — things I'd half-guessed and things I hadn't guessed at all, including a passage about the sacrifice clause that I made myself read three times and then made myself stop reading, because Dagny was fevered and Pip was asleep on her and I could not afford to be the kind of man who read the fine print and said nothing. But the passage that mattered, the one that turned my stomach over and left it there, was near the end:

*They will tell her she is human. They will make it a crime to be anything else, and then they will make her a criminal, so that no one will ever look closely at what she is. An exile asks no questions. An exile keeps her head down. An exile is the safest thing in the world to be — for everyone except the exile.*

*If you are reading this, and she is with you, put the book in her hands. Not the fragments. Not the pretty verses. This. She has spent her whole life being told what she is by people who needed her not to be it. Let her hear it from the book, at least, which never wanted anything from her except to be read.*

I closed the covers. The oxblood leather was warm under my hands, warmer than the fire could account for, and for a moment I had the distinct impression — scholar's superstition, I told myself, scholar's superstition — that the book was relieved. That it had been holding its breath for a hundred years and had finally, finally been made to tell the truth.

Then I looked up, and Ulyana was standing at the edge of the firelight.

* * *

She moved quietly for a woman who commanded thunder; I'd stopped being surprised by it somewhere around the second week. She had her arms crossed against the cold and frost in her hair — real frost, in midsummer, the kind that didn't come from weather — and she was looking at the book in my hands with an expression I'd learned to read the way sailors read clouds.

"You took it apart," she said.

"You were watching."

"From the ridge. For the last hour." She came closer, slow, the way you approach a snare you've spotted. "You had your mending-weave out. You looked like a man performing surgery on his own child."

"Your grandmother's craft?"

"My grandmother," I said, "could take a clock apart and put it back together with three extra pieces and make it run better than before. I like to think I inherited something." I turned the book over in my hands. The pastedown was peeled back now; the hidden gathering showed at the spine's root like a second set of ribs. "There were pages sewn into the binding. Under everything. A hundred years under everything."

"More prophecy?"

"No." I heard my own voice go careful, and hated it. "The opposite of prophecy. It's a record. She wrote it after — she met someone, Ulyana. Someone who came to her and told her what she was. A century ago. And my grandmother didn't believe her in time, and then wrote all of this down so that — " I stopped. Started again. "She wrote it down *for you*. She says so. In those exact words. She wrote it so that when you found out, you'd know someone thought you deserved to hear it from a friend."

Ulyana had gone very still. Not her combat stillness — I knew that one, the coiled readiness, the weight shifting to the balls of the feet. This was different. This was the stillness of a woman standing on ice and listening for the crack.

"Found out what," she said.

I could have softened it. I want to be honest with myself, at least, in this account: I considered softening it. I considered the whole long winding road — the fragments first, the riddles, the gentle on-ramp, *of neither shore, what do you suppose that means, hypothetically* — and I looked at her face, at the frost melting in her hair, at the woman who had carried a false conviction for a decade like a stone in her boot and had never once set it down to rest, and I heard my grandmother's voice as clearly as if she were standing behind me with a wooden spoon:

*Put the book in her hands, Theo.*

I put the book in her hands.

"Read it yourself," I said. "All of it. I'll be right here."

She read. I watched her read, which is its own particular torture, because reading is a private act and I was trespassing on it and we both knew it and neither of us moved. Her eyes went down the page the way they went down a mountain trail — fast, thorough, missing nothing, twice over the dangerous parts. I saw her reach the line. *The warden must be of both bloods.* I saw her reach the next one. *The exile who calls storms is of both bloods.*

I saw her stop reading.

That was the thing I would remember afterward, when everything else had blurred together — not the storm that came later, not the shouting or the silence, but this: she stopped reading. Ulyana Zhou, who read terrain and faces and weather and the space between a man's words faster than anyone I had ever known, sat with an open book in her lap and did not turn the page. The fire crackled. Dagny's fever-breath rasped on, slow and even. And Ulyana sat absolutely motionless, the book open, the truth three lines below her eyes, and did not move.

"Ulyana."

"My mother," she said, "died when I was four."

"I know."

"The order told me she died of fever. In the lowlands. They told me my father was a ferryman who drank." Her voice was level. That was the worst part — the levelness, the flat calm of a woman reading her own death warrant and finding it well-drafted. "They had a record. A whole record. I saw it, at my trial. Birth, parentage, the fever, all of it. Ink and seals. A *whole record*."

"Serapha helped forge the evidence at your trial," I said gently. "How hard do you think it was for them to forge a mother?"

That landed. I watched it land, and I would have taken it back if I could, but some things, once said, simply stand there in the firelight with you.

Ulyana turned the page. Then the next. She read the passage about the making — *she has been made not to know it, by people who loved her, or who told themselves they did* — and something in her face closed like a door. She read the passage about the choice — *the mountain takes volunteers* — and her jaw set. She read the last line, my grandmother's last line, the one I'd read four times and still couldn't read aloud without my throat closing:

*Let her hear it from the book, at least, which never wanted anything from her except to be read.*

And then Ulyana Zhou closed the book, set it down on the stone with a care that frightened me more than any violence could have, and went quiet.

Not angry-quiet. Not the tight, banked-coal quiet she wore after Marrowgate burned, which was a *useful* quiet, a quiet with a direction. This was something I hadn't seen from her before. This was a woman discovering that the floor she'd been standing on for thirty years had been painted on, and that beneath it there was nothing — no mother, no father, no crime, no exile, no story at all that belonged to her — and that the only true things left were a dead scholar's handwriting and a mountain that wanted her for a warden.

"Say something," I said, when the silence had gone on long enough to ache. "Anything. Insult my mending. Tell me my grandmother was a fraud. Yell at me for taking the book apart without asking."

"Why would I yell at you?" Her voice came out strange. Distant. "You didn't do this."

"No."

"They did." She looked at the fire. "My whole life. Every piece of it. The trial was just the last page. They wrote the book, Theo. They wrote the whole book, and then they handed it to me and told me it was my life, and I *believed them*, because why wouldn't I, because who forges an entire childhood — "

"Someone with a century of practice," I said. "Someone with a covenant to protect. Someone who was more afraid of what you are than of what they'd have to do to you."

"Half-fae." She said the word the way you'd handle a snake — at arm's length, watching the head. "I've spent ten years being hunted by fae and hated by humans and the whole time I was — " She laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard from her yet, worse than the crying at Marrowgate, because it had no grief in it at all. Only arithmetic. "Do you know what the order's first law is? *Purity of the calling.* No fae blood in a storm-caller, not one drop, because the storm answers fae blood *too well*. I was the best caller they'd produced in a generation. The *best*, Theo. And they couldn't have that, not from me, so they — "

She stopped. Her hands had curled into fists, and frost was creeping out from under her boots across the stone in a slow, glittering tide, and she didn't seem to know it was happening, and I didn't point it out, because there are moments when pointing things out is a cruelty.

"They didn't exile me for a crime," she said at last, very quietly. "They exiled me for existing. The crime was just the paperwork."

The frost reached the edge of the fire and hissed. Pip stirred in the big tent, murmured, settled. Out on the dark ridgeline, the wind changed direction — I felt it, we all felt it, the whole camp felt it — swinging around to the east with a low moan like something waking up.

And Ulyana stood, and picked up the book, and held it against her chest the way you hold the one true thing you own, and said:

"Vess knew."

I blinked. "What?"

"The hollow queen. *Of both bloods* — she looked at me, Theo, in her court, the first night, and she said, *you have her hands.* I thought she meant my mother's. I thought — " Ulyana's voice cracked, just once, and she pressed it flat again. "She *knew*. She's known this whole time. And she traded me a keystone for a promise I couldn't name, and I have been walking around with her bargain in my pocket like a coin I was afraid to spend, and now I know what she was saving it for."

"Ulyana — "

"She's the only one left who knew my mother." The frost-tide was retreating from her boots now, pulling back like a wave, and her eyes had gone bright and terrible in the firelight, and I recognized the look at last, because I'd seen it the night she burned the prophecy page and the words grew back: dread and purpose, welded together into something you could steer a ship by. "She's the only one who can tell me who I am. And tomorrow, Theo, first light — you and I are going back to the Hollow Court, and I am going to collect on that promise."

She turned toward the dark, toward the mountain, toward whatever was waiting for her under it — and paused, just at the edge of the firelight, with the book against her chest and the wind pulling at her hair.

"And Theo," she said, without turning around. "Thank you for taking the book apart."

Then she was gone into the dark, and the fire cracked, and I sat alone with my grandmother's century-old letter and the growing conviction — scholar's certainty, this time, not superstition — that we had just opened the last door, and that everything on the other side of it had been waiting a very long time for us to knock.
