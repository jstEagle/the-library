## 21. Half-Blood

The Hollow Court kept no clocks. I suppose when your whole existence is a slow fade, marking the hours feels like counting your own teeth as they fall out.

I found Vess where I'd left her the night before, in the antechamber of the drowned chapel's echo—a cavern the hollow fae called the Listening Room, though nothing in it listened so much as remembered. Stalactites hung like the ribs of some enormous patient animal, and between them hung the queen, or what passed for hanging when you were mostly memory held together by spite and protocol. Her feet didn't quite touch the ground. I'd stopped pretending not to notice.

"You read it," she said, without turning. Her voice had the quality of wind through a keyhole—thin, high, and going somewhere you didn't want to follow.

"The second half of the prophecy, yes. *Of both bloods.* Theo's restoration magic pulled the hidden pages out of the binding." I kept my arms crossed, which is what I do when I'd rather be holding a weapon. "The warden must be of both bloods. Human and fae. And there is exactly one storm-calling exile running around Thornfell who fits that description, according to a book written a hundred years before I was born."

"Congratulations," Vess said. "You've mastered arithmetic."

"I want the rest of it. From you. Not from ink, not from Theo's scholarly hedging, not from the mountain's cryptic light-show. From the one person in this cave who was alive when the covenant was young enough to break."

She turned then. It was unsettling, the way she moved—like watching a portrait decide to rearrange itself. Her eyes were the worst part. They'd been beautiful once, I could tell. Now they were the color of frost forming on a window, and they looked at me with an expression I recognized from the mirror on my worst days: the look of someone cataloguing exits.

"You won't like it," she said.

"I've been exiled, hunted, lied to by everyone I've ever trusted, and informed that my entire life is a prophecy fulfillment strategy. My standards for *liking* are on the floor."

"Then sit down, exile. This is a long fall."

I didn't sit. Sitting felt like surrender, and I'd done my surrender quota for one lifetime. But I leaned against a stalactite—cold through my coat, cold like a hand—and I waited.

* * *

"Your mother's name was Liriope," Vess said.

The name landed in me like a stone dropped down a well. I waited for the splash. It never came. I had no memory of the name at all, and that absence was somehow louder than any memory could have been.

"I don't—" I started.

"You were three when she left you at the order's gate. You wouldn't remember her voice. But you remember other things." Vess drifted closer, and the frost of her gaze did something almost gentle. "You remember hating thunderstorms as a small child, and then one day, at six, standing in one and feeling it *answer* you. You remember the order's teachers calling it talent. Gift. Discipline." A sound came out of her that might have been a laugh, if laughter could freeze and shatter. "It was none of those things. It was inheritance."

"Say it plainly."

"Your mother was of the Hollow Court. Before it was hollow. A noble of the Thorn line—the old blood, the covenant blood. She was the daughter of the last warden's sister. Which makes you—" she paused, savoring it or suffering it, I couldn't tell— "the last living heir of the house that forged the pact between your lowlanders and our courts."

The cave was very quiet. Somewhere water dripped, patient as a metronome, counting out a time signature for a song I didn't want to hear.

"That's not possible," I said, and heard how thin it came out. "The order tests for fae blood. Every initiate. They test *yearly*. I passed every—"

"You passed because they made sure you passed." Vess's voice hardened, frost thickening to ice. "Do you imagine a century-old prophecy naming a half-blood warden would survive contact with an order of storm-callers whose entire institutional purpose is keeping the realms *separate*? They found her, your mother. Found her hiding in their care, hiding *you*, and they made a choice."

"What choice."

"The merciful one. Or the cruel one. The wording depends on how much you value truth." She drifted to the cave wall, where the bioluminescent lichen threw her shadow long and wrong. "They let her go. Unharmed. In exchange, she surrendered you to their raising, agreed never to return, and consented to the suppression. There are rites, exile. Old ones. The order pretends they don't keep them. Blood-quieting, we called it, back when we still had the standing to name their sins. It doesn't remove fae blood. Nothing removes fae blood. It mutes it. Blurs it. Makes a testing-rite read *human* while the blood sits in you like a banked fire, waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the quieting to fail." She looked back at me. "And it is failing. Isn't it? Your storms come easier now. The mountain speaks to you. The guardian in the chapel didn't show you visions—it showed you *memories*, and some of them weren't yours."

My hand went to my sternum without my permission. Because she was right, and I'd known it, and I'd filed the knowing under *later*. The guardian's visions had bled past the boundaries of my own life. I'd felt a chapel I'd never entered. I'd heard an oath spoken in a language I had never studied and yet understood word for word. I'd told myself it was the keystone's magic, residue, echo.

I'd lied to myself with my usual efficiency.

"How long?" I asked. My voice came out flat. I was proud of that, in a distant, numb way. "How long has it been failing?"

"Since the covenant began to die in earnest. The quieting was anchored to the old pact, exile. It was one of the order's little cruelties that doubled as a safeguard—if the warden's blood ever woke, it would mean the covenant was failing, and the blood would wake *stronger than the quieting could hold*. Your masters built a tripwire and called it mercy."

*Your masters.* The words sat in my stomach like swallowed ice.

* * *

I made her walk me through it. All of it. I have this habit—Theo has commented on it, gently, in the way of a man who has learned that commenting on my habits too firmly results in him carrying the heavier pack—of needing a thing complete before I can decide what I feel about it. Feelings, in my experience, are like storms: manageable if you can see the whole front coming, catastrophic if they arrive in fragments.

So. Fragments, assembled:

My mother, Liriope, of the Thorn line, had fled the Hollow Court's collapse—or its beginning, the two were hard to separate—carrying a child fathered, Vess believed, by a lowland storm-caller. An initiate. A man of the order. Which meant I wasn't just half-blood; I was half of each side of a failing treaty, a walking argument for why the two realms should never, ever fall in love.

She'd come to the order because it was the one place the fae courts would not follow. Sanctuary through institutional pettiness. She'd begged them to raise me as human. To let me be small and safe and blind.

And the order—Serapha's order, Serapha's *predecessors*, though I'd have bet my last coin Serapha knew—had looked at the prophecy book, and at the child, and done their arithmetic.

"They couldn't kill you," Vess said. "The prophecy was explicit enough that killing you risked burying both realms in winter, and even the order flinched at that. But they couldn't let you *know*. A warden who knows what she is might claim the covenant. Might reforge it. Might bind herself to the mountain and take the realms' weather out of the order's hands entirely." Her frost-pale eyes glittered. "You think your exile was punishment for a crime?"

"I know it was. I was there. I stood trial. There was evidence—"

"There was a *story*," Vess said, "and you were the ending they'd written for it before you ever walked into that chamber. Think, exile. Use that clever, cornered mind. What does an institution do with a weapon it cannot destroy and cannot use? It does not keep the weapon loaded and in the armory. It *loses* the weapon. Disgracefully. Publicly. Somewhere far from the mountain's heart, somewhere its own people won't look twice at it, somewhere the courts will dismiss as a broken thing not worth collecting."

The stalactite I was leaning against had gone from cold to burning. Or my skin had. Hard to say where the cave ended and I did, lately.

"They exiled me," I said slowly, "so that I would never come back."

"They exiled you so that you would never come *home*." Vess let the distinction hang there. "There is a difference, and it is the width of a mountain. Every road they cut you off from, every name they made poison in your mouth, every gate closed—ask yourself who benefited. You spent nine years believing you were hiding. Child, you were *herded*."

* * *

I want to tell you I took it well.

I want to tell you I stood there, spine straight, and received the truth about my mother and my blood and my life with the composure of a woman who guides caravans through blizzards for coin.

What I actually did was put my fist through a stalagmite.

It was a small one. It didn't deserve it. The hollow fae watching from the gallery—those fading, translucent witnesses—made no sound, but I felt their attention the way you feel weather change, and I understood, distantly, that I had just struck the floor of their dead court, and that some of them had died for less, and that I was becoming, in their eyes, something worth fearing. The stone cracked along a seam and sheared off, and my knuckles bled, and the blood—my blood, half of it older and stranger than I'd ever known—hissed faintly against the lichen, and the lichen *brightened*.

It had never done that. In three days in the Hollow Court, the lichen had never answered anything.

"Oh," Vess said softly, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard from her before. Not pity. Not triumph. Something older. "Oh, you *are* waking."

"Don't," I said. My voice shook. I let it. There was no one here I needed to perform steadiness for, and I was so tired of performing it for empty rooms. "Don't look at me like I'm the prophecy. I'm a smuggler's guide with a bad temper and a bleeding hand."

"You are a woman who just learned her whole life was a cage with the door painted on," Vess said. "Look at me like *I* am something, then. I knew. I have known since you walked into my court smelling of storm and quieting-spell, and I said nothing, because I wanted the keystones, and a waking warden is a lever, and I have spent a hundred years learning to use what leverage remains to the dying." She inclined her head, and it was the most honest thing she'd done. "I am not your friend, exile. I am your evidence. Everything I've just told you, weigh it accordingly."

"I have been weighing everyone accordingly since I was three years old and someone left me on a step," I said. "It's the one skill they couldn't confiscate."

* * *

I walked the caverns after that. Not toward anywhere. The hollow fae let me, drifting aside like fog making way, and I walked until the Listening Room was far behind and I could hear only water and my own too-loud heart.

Here is what vertigo actually feels like, in case you've been lucky: it isn't falling. It's the sudden, total loss of confidence in the floor. Every step I'd taken for thirty years re-presented itself for audit. The orphanage winters—had they been cold, or *made* cold, to keep me small and grateful? My aptitude for storm-craft, the thing I'd built a self around—inheritance, not achievement. My exile, the defining wound of my life, the wound I'd sharpened myself on like a whetstone—engineering. Containment strategy. A door painted on a wall, and me running at it for nine years, bruising my soul, believing the wall was the world.

And my mother.

I tried to make her a face and couldn't. Three years old is too early to keep a face. But I could remember—God help me, I could suddenly *remember*, memories surfacing like drowned things finally allowed to rise—a voice singing in two languages, one of them the oath-language from the chapel visions. Hands that smelled of rain. A woman who had handed her child to strangers and walked away, and I had spent my whole life reading that as abandonment, and it had been—what? Sacrifice? The only door left open? She'd traded her child's *truth* for her child's *life*, and the order had accepted the trade and then quietly, institutionally, made sure the child never grew up strong enough to ask for a refund.

I sat down on the cavern floor, finally, and laughed until it turned into the other thing. The hollow fae gave me the courtesy of pretending not to notice, which is more than the order ever did.

* * *

Theo found me eventually. Of course he did. The man has a scholar's instinct for where the primary source has gone to have a breakdown.

He didn't say anything at first. He just lowered himself down beside me against the cave wall, in that careful way of his, like a man sitting down next to a horse he hasn't been introduced to, and he opened the prophecy book on his knees. The restored pages glowed faintly in the lichen-light, the hidden ink patient as ever.

"I ran the dating again," he said. "The original hand and the hidden hand are the same. My grandmother wrote both halves. And there's a marginal note I couldn't restore before. It's in the oath-language, but I've been working on the grammar." He turned the book toward me. "I thought you should see it before anyone else does. Before Vess. Before anyone."

I looked. The note was small, cramped, written in the margin the way people write things they need to say but hope no one reads:

*The warden of both bloods will not be found. She will be made—or unmade. Guard the child from those who would guard her too well.*

"Your grandmother knew," I said.

"My grandmother knew *everything*," Theo said. "That was her whole tragedy. She knew, she wrote it down, and the world burned her archive for it and killed her couriers. And now—" he exhaled, and I watched him decide to say the true thing, watched him choose it the way you choose to step off a ledge— "now I understand why my family kept the book for a hundred years and why the order wants it so badly it's willing to burn villages. It's not a prophecy, Ulyana. It's a *record of a crime in progress*. And you're not the criminal. You're the evidence."

I closed the book gently. Gently, because it was his grandmother's blood in it, and because if I touched it roughly I was afraid of what would come out of my hands.

"Serapha," I said.

Theo went still.

"She was my mentor. She was at my trial. She looked me in the eye and said nothing while they read out forged evidence." I stood, and the vertigo was still there, but underneath it now was something with edges. Something I could hold. "She's been hunting us across half the mountain. And if anyone in that order knows *why*—knows what I am, knows what they did to my mother's bargain—"

"You can't be thinking of confronting her."

"I'm thinking," I said, "that I've spent nine years running from my past, and it turns out my past has been running alongside me the whole time, wearing a mentor's face." I picked up my coat. My knuckles had stopped bleeding; the lichen-light followed the scabs like it was reading them. "And I'm thinking that when her hunters catch up to us next—and they will—I'm not running. I want answers. And she's the last person alive who has them."

Theo stood too, book hugged to his chest, looking at me with an expression I was starting to know well—the look of a man who has just realized the document he's been guarding is going to cost more than his life.

"Ulyana," he said. "Her hunters are already here. I heard the horns from the eastern gallery an hour ago. I came to find you because—"

Because the past had teeth, and it was chewing through the Hollow Court's dying gates, and this time I intended to make it talk.

I was already walking.
