## 23. Keystone Two: The Rootcrown

*Ulyana*

The mountain had been trying to tell us something for three days.

I don't mean that in the poetic way Theo means everything—I mean the ground hummed under our bedrolls at night like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing, and Pip woke up twice saying the dirt was talking to her, and Ivo's old storm-sense—the one he swore he'd cut out of himself when he left the order—kept making him flinch at nothing, like a dog hearing a whistle no one else could hear.

"Rootcrown's close," Vess had told us before we parted from her hollow court. "It sleeps where the mountain's heart used to beat." Then she'd smiled her terrible fading smile and added, "Mind you don't wake it angry."

Everything worth waking, in my experience, wakes angry.

We'd left Dagny at the camp with Pip—her arm still splinted from the bridge, her curses still inventive—and come down into Thornfell's roots: me, Theo, and Ivo, carrying rope, lamps, a week of hardtack, and the Bell of Hours wrapped in wool like a sleeping infant nobody wanted to hold. The tunnel mouth was a crack in the mountain's flank that Pip had found by falling into it, which is how most good things are found.

Now we were four days deep, and the world had gone strange.

Not dark—we had lamps for dark. Strange. The stone around us had begun to look less like stone. Veins of pale gold ran through it in branching patterns, thickening as we descended until the walls looked like cross-sections of something living, something with a pulse. And there was warmth. Not firelight warmth. Body warmth. The deeper we went, the more it felt like walking down someone's throat into their chest, and every instinct I owned said turn back.

Every instinct I owned had also kept me alive ten years as a smuggler's guide, and instincts are cowards. So we went down.

"You're quiet," Theo said behind me. His voice did that thing it does in enclosed spaces—went careful, like he was afraid of disturbing something. Which, to be fair, he usually should be.

"I'm counting," I said.

"Counting what?"

"Exits. Steps. Heartbeats." I put my palm flat against the wall, and the gold vein pulsed against my skin, slow and vast. Once every eleven seconds or so. It had been eleven seconds since we entered the tunnel. "The mountain has a heartbeat, Theo. That's new."

"That's not new," Ivo said grimly from the rear. He carried the Bell now—he'd volunteered, and none of us were stupid enough to argue. "That's the covenant. When it was whole, the whole mountain rang like this. My grandmother sang songs about it. Lullabies." He shifted the bundle. "I always thought they were about sheep."

"Sheep lullabies about mountain heartbeats," Theo mused. "Your order's oral tradition needs work."

"My order's oral tradition got my family exiled, so yes, I'd agree it needs work."

They bickered the way they always did, Theo needling gently and Ivo grumbling back, and I let it wash over me because the alternative was listening to the silence between their words, and the silence was full of that hum, that slow patient pulse, and it was starting to sound less like a heartbeat to me.

It was starting to sound like breathing.

Someone asleep. Someone very large, very old, breathing once every eleven seconds, and us three small warm things crawling through its ribs.

"We're close," I said, because the vein ahead had widened into a channel wide enough to swim through, glowing faint gold, and the air smelled green. Green. Four days underground and the air smelled like a meadow after rain, and I hadn't smelled that since—

Since Marrowgate. Since the frost. Since everything started dying.

"Ulyana?" Theo again. He'd learned to read the set of my shoulders; I both appreciated and resented it.

"The air's wrong," I said. "Good-wrong. Come on."

* * *

The chamber opened without warning. One moment the tunnel, the next—a cathedral of root.

That's the only word I have. Roots, hundreds of years thick, arched overhead like the ribs of some buried ship, silver-grey bark shot through with the same gold veins, and between them hung curtains of pale moss that glowed with their own soft light, so we didn't even need the lamps. The floor was not floor exactly but woven root, smooth as a chapel aisle, running forward to the center of the chamber, where—

Where the tree stood.

I say tree, but what stood there was a heart. A trunk wider than Dagny's ferry, barkless, the color of dawn, beating. Actually beating, visibly, the whole massive trunk swelling and easing with each pulse, and from its crown—not branches, but a circlet of root-crowns growing inward like clasped hands, and cradled in the center of those hands, turning slowly in the warm light:

A seed. No. A knot of wood the size of a fist, carved—or grown—into the shape of an interlocking ring, gold veined, pulsing in time with everything else.

The Rootcrown.

Nobody spoke. Even Ivo, who complained the way other men breathed, had gone silent. Theo's lamp hand was shaking. I noticed mine was too, and made a note to be disgusted about it later.

"There should be a guardian," I whispered finally. "The Bell had one. Vess didn't mention—" 

"Vess said mind we don't wake it angry," Ivo said. "She didn't say wake it at all."

"Well," Theo said, in the tone of a man reading a sentence he doesn't like, "the book says the keystone must be taken by the hand of the storm, freely offered, freely received—"

"The book says a lot of things. The book also rhymes."

"Prophecy rhymes, Ulyana. It's part of the horror."

I walked forward. Because someone had to, and because the pulse in the air had changed when I stepped into the chamber—it had quickened, just slightly, just enough that only someone who'd been counting for four days would notice—and I am many bad things, but I've never been able to ignore being noticed.

The root-aisle was warm under my boots. With every step the glow brightened, and the pulse quickened, and somewhere above us—miles of stone above us—I swear I felt snow shift on a peak, the way a sleeper shifts when a door opens downstairs.

Ten steps from the heart, I stopped.

"Hello," I said.

Theo made a small strangled noise behind me. Talking to it. She's talking to it. But what else do you do? You knock.

"I'm Ulyana Zhou," I said. "Exiled storm-caller, smuggler's guide, allegedly a criminal, recently informed half-fae, currently extremely tired. I've come for the keystone. The covenant's dying. Villages are freezing. People I—" my throat caught on it, "—people I know are dead because it's dying. I'm asking. Not taking. Asking."

The pulse paused.

One full second—eleven became twelve, thirteen—and the whole chamber held its breath, moss-light dimming, roots creaking like a house settling, and then the heart beat again, harder, and light rolled outward from the trunk in a wave that passed through me like warm water, and I felt it look at me.

There's no better word. It looked at me. Through me. Every lie I ever told, every coin I ever skimmed, every night I cried into my cloak so the caravan wouldn't hear, all of it read, weighed, filed away in whatever ledger a mountain keeps. I stood in that gaze and did not run, which may be the bravest thing I've done in this entire miserable prophecy, including the storm on the bridge.

Then the root-hands opened.

Slowly, groaning, bark parting like fingers uncurling after a hundred years of sleep, and the Rootcrown drifted down through the glowing air and stopped at the level of my chest, turning, waiting.

Freely offered.

My hands shook when I took it. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything here was warm; it was the first warm thing I'd touched since Marrowgate burned, and I stood there holding a piece of a broken covenant in my cupped palms and felt something crack open in my chest that I'd spent ten years welding shut.

"Ulyana," Theo said softly. "Look at the walls."

I looked.

The gold veins in the roots had brightened—and they were moving. Flowing. Converging inward from every direction, streaming toward the chamber, toward the heart, toward me, and the pulse wasn't eleven seconds anymore. It was nine. Eight. Quickening like a sleeper's heart when the dream turns.

"It's waking up," Ivo breathed. "Saints' teeth, it's actually waking up. After a century—"

The mountain shuddered.

Not a tremor. A stretch. Somewhere far above, I heard stone grind against stone, long and slow and enormous, the sound of something rolling over in a bed it had occupied too long, and dust sifted down through the moss-glow like golden snow, and every root in that cathedral flexed, and I understood—with the calm clarity that arrives precisely when panic would be most useful—that we were standing inside something that had just opened its eyes.

"Take the keystone," I said. "Back up the tunnel. Now. Slowly."

"Why slowly?"

"Because," I said, watching the veins of light converge, watching the heart-beat climb toward seven seconds, six, "we are currently inside the chest of a thing the size of a mountain range, and I would very much prefer not to be mistaken for a splinter."

Theo grabbed my elbow. Ivo had the Bell clutched to his chest like a shield, and his face had gone the grey of old ash. "Ulyana," he said, and his voice cracked on it, "when the mountain fully wakes—the covenant's anchor wakes with it. The wardenship. Whatever's left of the old binding, it'll all start pulling taut, and you're—"

"I'm what?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. Half-blood. Both bloods. The words from the hidden pages sat between us like a third person.

Above us, the grinding grew louder. And beneath it—faint, rising, resonating up through my boot-soles and my bones and the keystone warm in my hands—came a sound I knew.

Wind. Trapped wind, ancient wind, moving through passages no map had ever drawn, and it was singing in a register only storm-callers can hear, and the song had words.

And the first word was my name.

* * *

*Theo*

I want it noted, for the record of whatever book I eventually write about this, that I did not scream.

I came close. There was a moment, roughly forty seconds into the mountain waking up around us, when a root the width of a fishing boat peeled itself off the ceiling and moved aside to let us pass, deliberately, courteously, the way a servant holds open a door, and my knees informed me they were resigning effective immediately, and I overruled them, and history will judge whether that was courage or stupidity. Probably stupidity. Most of my decisions look identical from the outside.

Ulyana walked point. She always walks point, but this time it wasn't habit—it was gravity. The light followed her. The veins of gold bent toward her like iron filings toward a lodestone, and the pulse of the great heart sped whenever she strode faster, and I watched the realization land on her face the way you watch frost form: gradually, then all at once.

"It thinks I'm coming home," she said quietly. She didn't look at either of us. "It thinks I'm coming home, and it's excited."

"That's... sweet?" I offered.

"It's a mountain, Theo. Mountains aren't sweet. Mountains are patient." Her jaw tightened. "It's been waiting a hundred years for someone with my blood to walk down here, and it doesn't care that I never agreed to anything."

Ivo said nothing. Ivo had been saying nothing with increasing volume for some time, which for him was practically a scream.

We climbed. The tunnel had changed on the way back—or we had changed, or the mountain had rearranged itself around our route with the offhand power of something adjusting its blankets—and everywhere the gold light ran ahead of us like lantern-bearers, lighting each bend before we reached it. Helpful. Eager. The eagerness was the worst part. You can brace against malice. Eagerness gets under your guard.

At the fourth junction, the humming resolved fully into song, and I heard my own name in it too.

Bramble. It said. Bramble-bramble-bramble, threaded through the wind-tone, and cold walked up my spine despite the warmth, because my grandmother's name had been Bramble, and she had written the book, and the book had led us here, and the mountain knew it. Knew the line. Knew the debt.

"It's calling you too," Ulyana said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Of course it is. Everyone in this story is related to everyone. At this point I expect to find out Pip's my cousin."

"Pip might be," I said. "Given the fae roads, honestly, we should test it."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of her, thin and startled, and then the tunnel ended and we emerged into grey daylight under a sky the color of dirty pewter, and the mountain rose above us, and it was breathing.

You could see it. Shoulders of stone rising and falling. Snowfields shifting in long slow rhythm. Clouds circling the summit not with the wind but against it, orderly as thoughts, and every bird within miles had gone silent, and the trees at the treeline had all leaned slightly, uniformly, toward the peak—like a congregation. Like witnesses.

Because that's what it was, I understood suddenly. That was the thing the songs had gotten wrong, or the thing I'd gotten wrong reading them. The mountain wasn't a fortress the covenant was kept in. It was a witness the covenant was sworn before. The original oath hadn't been spoken in front of an altar. It had been spoken in front of this—this enormous sleeping presence, older than both courts, older than the lowland kingdoms, asked to remember what mortals and fae alike would inevitably try to forget.

And we had just handed it the second keystone's absence like tearing a page out of its memory, and woken it up, and now it was remembering everything.

Including, apparently, us.

The ground under our feet pulsed once—warm, deliberate, unmistakable—and in the pulse I felt, plain as speech, not words exactly but meaning, the way you understand a tone of voice before the sentence lands:

*Found you.*

Ulyana swayed. I caught her arm. Her face had gone white around the eyes, and her free hand was clenched around the Rootcrown hard enough that the carved edges bit her palm, and she was staring up at the breathing peak with an expression I'd only seen once before—the night she burned the prophecy page and the words came back.

"What is it?" I asked. "What did it say?"

Her voice, when it came, was almost steady. Almost.

"It said it remembers the last time someone with my blood stood at its heart." She swallowed. "It says her name was Wren. And it wants to show me."

The wind picked up around us, warm and gold-flecked, spiraling, insistent, gathering like a hand extended palm-up—an invitation, or a summons, and Ivo took one step backward and said, with tremendous feeling, "Absolutely not, I'll wait here," and Ulyana looked at me.

"We don't get to choose what it shows us," she said. "Do we."

"No," I admitted. "I don't think we ever did."

The wind closed around us both like a closing book, and the mountain began to speak.
